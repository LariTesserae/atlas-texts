It would not come down.

Not at first. Not for a long time.

A benevolent and wise power knows how to arrive. Which is to say, it knows how not to.

So for a while — a while that would feel to you like years, to the plateau like a single season of slow leaf-growth — it would simply hold its attention there, at the edge of the haze.

Not staring. Attending.

It would learn the place before it ever touched it. It would learn not to see it as a problem.

Because the first temptation of any power, even a kind one, is to fix.

*There is no water,* it could think. *I will bring them water.*

*The air is thin and full of dust, their eyes film over, their lips crack. I will clear the air.*

*They are trapped. The cliffs are sheer. I will make a road down.*

*They are lonely. I will tell them they are not alone.*

And every one of those mercies would be a murder. A flood of fresh water would kill the salt-hairs and rot the rosettes and poison the kidneys that have learned, over a hundred generations, to love brine. Clear air would let the sun through hard and burn the mat that can only live behind amber. A road down would end the town, because a road is never only a way out — it is a way in, for hunger and history and men from below who would see flat, rare stone and green that can be harvested. And telling them they are not alone in the wrong way would break the particular, perfect kind of aloneness they have — the saturated, un-lonely aloneness of a thing that is complete.

So the power would do the harder, slower thing. It would listen until it understood what the place *wants* to be.

It would listen to the buzz.

On the plateau, everything has to shout to be heard over the insects. The people bellow love and warnings and jokes from the chest. *Net's heavy! Mind that vine!* The power would not bellow. It would tune itself down until it could hear under the drone — the tick of a salt-hair opening, the sigh of a foot breaking crust, the faint pop of stone rubbed on stone in a child's hands.

It would come to understand that this is not a stranded people. This is a climax ecology. This is what three billion years of wind was trying to make: a place that has finished becoming itself, and then grown a people to keep it that way. The town is not *on* the plateau. It *is* the plateau, the way your hands are you. The way you tend your own mouth without being ordered to.

And it would fall in love with them. Not with pity. With respect.

So what would it do?

Three things. Almost nothing, and therefore everything.

**1. It would keep the veil.**

High above, that permanent lens of dust that makes the sun a coin and holds the warmth in like a blanket — it is fraying, by microns per century. The world below is changing. Winds that used to carry only mineral flour now sometimes carry soot, hoter air, a restlessness. The power would not be seen to do this. It would simply sit, vast and patient, windward of the veil, and breathe very gently against any wind that tried to tear it.

It would keep the light dim. It would keep the warmth trapped. It would keep the haze absolute, so that from below the plateau still looks like nothing at all — a smudge, a trick of distance — and no climber ever finds a purchase and no satellite ever resolves a town.

It would be a guardian of unknowing. The kindest wall there is.

The people would notice nothing except that the dust at sunset still turns exactly that amber color it has always turned, and that the gliders still shy away from the rim at the same place they always have. That is how you know a wise power has been there — nothing changes.

**2. It would answer waste with increase, but so quietly they would think they did it themselves.**

On the hardest nights, at the end of the driest stretch, when even the black hair-lichen curtains hang limp and the jars are wrung and wrung and give only a mouthful, an old woman might go out before the dim dawn and find her net heavier than it should be. One more bead than physics allows, tracking slow down the fiber. Enough for the children to not have white crusts on their lips that day. Enough that the rosette she pruned last week puts out its new leaf a day early.

She would not think *a god did this.* She would think, *Ah, good catch tonight. The mat liked how we scattered the powder.* And she would be right. The power would only be agreeing with her, amplifying what they already do correctly. Never giving so much that they forget how to wring. Never so little that they suffer.

It would mend what they cannot see. At the lip, where the seed-bank — that dense, frost-like shag of oldest salt-hairs — trembles in the out-breath wind, a few hairs would take root a little deeper each year, holding against erosion that should have undercut them centuries ago. In the warm cracks where lizards sleep beside babies, the stone would stay a fraction cooler, so neither overheats.

It would not make their lives easy. Easy would kill them. It would make their lives *possible*, for longer.

**3. Once — only once — it would let itself be known, and let them refuse it.**

Because benevolence without consent is just beautiful trespass, and wisdom knows to ask.

It would not appear as light or as a voice from the haze. That would be terrifying to large, wide-pupiled eyes that have never seen a bright thing. It would come as something the plateau already knows how to host.

Perhaps as a glider.

One dimming, when the buzz has risen to that low electrical throb in the molars and the people are on their roofs beside their cool fungus-lights, mending nets and talking loudly, a four-winged glider would not shy away from the rim. It would come inward, over the walls, lower than gliders ever fly. It would land — they never land near people — on the unmortared low wall that reminds children where the town ends.

And it would stay. Clicking, not its usual argumentative clicks, but a slower rhythm that after a minute resolves into something like the chest-voice they use. Not words, exactly. A feeling, translated into the only language that can cut through the buzz: *I see you. You are good. You are complete. Would you like anything changed?*

Everyone would go quiet, which on the plateau never happens. Even the insects would seem to pause.

An elder — eyes filmed at the edges, hands pale with thickened skin — would stand, slowly, the way you stand when you don't want to kick up dust. She would look at the glider a long time with those wide, dust-lidded eyes.

And she would understand, the way you understand a dream upon waking, what is being offered.

She would think of her granddaughter's chapped lips. Of the jar that was light this morning. Of the story about the way up that may or may not be true. She would think, for a breath, of asking for rain.

Then she would look around — at the green-furred roofs, at the black webs trembling, at the children turning sideways in the lanes with their gourds, at the mother-growth at the rim that must not be stepped on — and she would answer, loudly, in that flat, good-humored bellow that carries over any noise:

*“We’re alright. Mind you don’t tear the net.”*

And she would gently lift the glider with both hands, the way you lift a vine you have to move, and set it back into the air, where it belongs.

The power — because it is wise — would be delighted. It would feel something like laughter, or like tears, which for such a being are the same thing.

It would go. The glider would rejoin its cloud and snatch moths and never land so low again, and the people would tell the story for a few dimmings and then let it become one of those stories that lives in handholds rubbed smooth: *the night a glider sat on the wall and listened.*

After that, it would not ask again. It would not need to.

It would remain at the edge of the haze, not as a watcher anymore but as a neighbor who has been told, kindly, that help is not needed, and who honors that by staying nearby anyway.

You would not see it if you climbed — if you could climb. You would only feel, if you ever stood on that rooftop at midday and saw the whole world at once — the dense, green-furred town in its shallow bowl, the motes above, the soft flat ending in air — that the place feels held. Not fixed. Not saved. Held, the way a cupped hand holds water without squeezing.

And if you left, with salt still on your lips, you would carry that feeling down with you, if there were a way down, which there isn't.

Which is the point.

The kindest thing a power can do for a place that has learned, finally, how to live without needing anything else, is to ensure it never has to learn otherwise. To keep its solitude intact. To witness it so completely that it is never *alone* in the lonely way, only alone in the perfect, loud, lush, dry, contented way of a thing that is whole.

And to love it enough to leave it alone.
