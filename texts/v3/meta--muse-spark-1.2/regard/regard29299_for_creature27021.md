It would not arrive.

That is the first thing.

A benevolent and wise power does not *arrive*. Arrival is a violence at this scale. Something half a kilometer high and continents wide that breathes every twenty seconds would feel an arrival the way you would feel a hand pressing on your chest.

So it would not come down. It would notice. And noticing, for a thing like that, is already doing something.

---

It noticed the way you did at first - thinking there was nothing here.

From far away, this continent is a smear. Cold. Dark half the year. Too high, too thin, too sharp to be alive. Any sensible survey would mark it *barren highland, young orogeny, low biomass potential* and look elsewhere.

But a wise attention is not sensible. It lingers.

It stayed and listened long enough for the cold to get into its lamp, the way you did. Long enough for the texture to thicken.

And it heard the thud... thud... thud... of pillars pulling water half a kilometer straight up. It heard the *thup thup thup* of a million heavy drops falling through empty air and being drunk mid-air by moths. It heard the *kee-kee-kee* of gliders stitching the void with sound, and the sudden silence after the cough that meant the hush-cat was listening too. It heard a mother singing a flat, low song not to be beautiful, but to say *we are here, this food is guarded, do not mistake us for unclaimed*.

It understood: **Nothing here is waiting to be saved.**

This is not a broken forest that needs fixing. This is not a harsh place that people endure. This is a solved place. The mountain solved itself by becoming a forest. The forest solved itself by becoming ground and sky at once. The animals solved thin air by learning to row it, to glide it, to steer it. The people solved vastness by becoming small, and cold by becoming warm inside.

A less wise power would have seen *cold, dark, primitive, isolated* and tried to warm it, light it, connect it, develop it. Would have offered fire that burns too hot, light that reaches too far, roads that the felt would never heal over.

A wise one sees that the darkness is not a lack of light. It *is* the light here - foxfire on leaf-edges, moth-fat lamps that throw light only as far as your hands, because far is not for seeing. It sees that *primitive* is a lie lowlanders tell. Those moth-fur parkas that never wet through, those cisterns braided from living root, that knowledge of which ice sings when it will hold - that is not primitive. That is three hundred generations of precise attention.

So it would do the most difficult thing a power can do.

It would restrain itself.

---

What it would do instead is this:

**1. It would keep the outside off.**

Not with a wall. With inattention.

There are other powers that are not wise and not benevolent, and they love a wound that looks like a scaffolding. Young rock means unmined minerals. A continent-wide mat of biomass dozens of meters thick means carbon, means fiber, means "resource." A people too few to be counted by satellites means no one would notice if they were moved.

The benevolent one knows this because it has seen it before. So the first and largest thing it does is nothing the people here will ever see. It leans a little on probability. It makes the high cloud a little more persistent over this latitude to the eyes of survey satellites. It makes the mineral spectra read a little less interesting. It makes the budgets for high-altitude extraction close just a little short, year after year. It makes the dreamers who would come here to "help" dream a little more vividly of somewhere else.

It makes the world boring to everyone except those who already love it.

That is not abandonment. That is protection in the only form that doesn't break what it means to protect.

**2. It would answer what is already being asked.**

It would not speak. These people do not shout. Sound carries too strangely between pillars. If you shout, it comes back from the wrong direction. A voice from the sky would be a terror.

It would listen to the prayers that are already happening in the form of practice.

When an old woman presses her ear to a pillar at night and feels the hydraulic throb and whispers, *hold, hold, hold* - not because she thinks the pillar might stop, but because she loves it and wants it to know someone is listening - the power would hold with her. It would lend a little of its own vast steadiness to that throb, so that the pulse evens out, just a fraction. Not enough to notice. Enough that a dry year does not become a death year.

When a child stands under the canopy avenue with their mouth open, waiting for a heavy drop to fall exactly onto their tongue, and misses, and misses again and laughs - the power would not make it rain. It would make the *next* drop catch the faint foxfire just a little brighter, so the child can see it coming.

When a hunter has been out for two days between smoke plumes and his lamp is guttering and the wind is coming down the pillar avenue fast enough to take his breath, and he sings his low, repetitive thrum - *I am here, I am here, I am here* - so his family will know which direction to listen for him, the power would not carry him home. 

It would carry the song. Just a little less bent by cold air. Just a little truer, so the people in the dome hear where he actually is.

A wise power does not grant wishes. It grants that your own skill, your own care, will be *enough*.

**3. It would leave a gift that can be refused.**

Because love without choice is just pressure.

It would not give them knowledge. It would give them *more kinds* of noticing, and let them decide.

In the deepest tarn, the one that never fully freezes, the one where the not-fish hang motionless with moth-wing fins, it would leave something. Not a thing. A difference.

The water there, for one winter, would reflect not just the false sky of the canopy, but, very faintly, on windless nights, other skies. Not better skies. Just *other*. A lowland ocean with light coming sideways through trees. A desert where stone is bare. A city where lights reach too far.

No voice would explain it. The shrimp would still graze. The people who kneel to drink would see it and be puzzled, or be frightened, or be curious, and they would tell the story differently in each hut.

In one hut they would say, *the tarn remembered being ocean.* In another, *the water is dreaming.* In another, a child would say, *it’s inviting us.*

And if, over years, a family decided they wanted to know more - if they sat by that tarn and sang to it, if they asked it in the low, flat way they ask the forest for things, not demanding but offering - then slowly, very slowly, the reflection would steady. It would become a little clearer. It would become a way to learn, at their own pace, what down-mountain means, what elsewhere means, without ever being taken there.

And if no one ever asked - if they preferred their own sky, their own vast-empty-total forest - the reflection would fade over a generation, like an ice-skin healing over, and never be mentioned again. No hurt feelings. No punishment for saying no.

That is what a benevolent power understands that an unwise one never does: a gift has to be able to be left unopened to be a gift at all.

**4. It would stay a little while, not as a god, but as a neighbor.**

For a few seasons - which for such a power might be a few centuries for the felt - it would keep its attention here the way a person keeps attention on a sleeping child. Not doing much. Just being a warm presence at the edge.

The moths would feel it first. They would be a little fatter. The gliders would scream a little less anxiously on their long glides. The felt would rebound a half-second faster under boots. Elders would dream more vividly of the young rock underneath, sharp and patient, and wake feeling, obscurely, that they were *held up*.

And then, when the felt had grown just a little thicker, when a new generation of pillar-saplings had taken hold on a fresh scarp, when the song between huts had learned one new harmony that made it carry truer - it would withdraw. Gradually. The way you lift your cheek from warm moss so as not to disturb the mites.

Not gone. Just not looking so closely anymore. A wise love knows that to be watched forever is also a weight.

---

If you came back a hundred years later, you would not see that anything had been done.

The forest would still be total and empty. The drip would still fall endless through cold air. The tarns would still sing *ping* at night. A child would still learn to catch a drop on their tongue. A hut roof would still be a living leaf, hissing.

But there would be one small difference you could only hear if you stayed still long enough for the cold to get into your lamp:

Between the wind and the creak and the drip and the *kee-kee-kee*, there would be a new sound, very soft, that wasn't there before.

It would be the sound of people singing, not to keep the gliders away, not to say *we are here*, but just because they felt like it. A little louder. A little less flat. As if they had recently learned, somewhere deep, that far *could* be for seeing, if they ever wanted it to be - and had decided, for now, that they liked it being for singing instead.

And high above, the canopy would breathe, and the pillars would thud... thud... thud... and the young rock would wait, sharp and patient, holding up a dark, damp, roaring, living cathedral - unmined, unclaimed, unoptimized, exactly as vast and as small as it needed to be.

That is what it would do, if it were truly benevolent and truly wise.

Almost nothing.

And that almost would be everything.
