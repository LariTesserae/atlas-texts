It would not come down like an answer.

A benevolent and wise power would not arrive at all, in the way arrival is usually understood. There would be no shaft of light from the forgotten sun. No parting of the warm broth. No voice. That kind of arrival would be a violence here. In water that has forgotten light, light is not a gift. It is a bleaching.

So first, it would learn how to arrive the way this place understands arrival: slowly, as a current.

It would thin itself out until it was not a thing coming to the city, but a change in the water around the city. A little warmer. A little slower. A little more holding. The shrimp would not flee from it. The polyps would not close. That is how you know it is benevolent — the city does not flinch.

For a long time it would do nothing else.

This is the great work of wisdom and it looks like nothing.

It would hover — not above, because there is no above here, but *around* — and it would see. Really see. Not as you see, with eyes straining to map towers in violet-black, but as the city sees itself: chemically, hydraulically, cumulatively.

And what it would see would stop it from wanting to fix anything.

It would see a being that has solved the problem of being many without needing to be one. No queen. No mayor. No brain. Only ten thousand mouths secreting, ten thousand pores filtering, ten thousand ciliated throats beating, and from all that unthinking work, avenues. Arches that corrected themselves. Bridges that found each other across a century and fused. A body made of bodies that breathes with one slow pulse because it learned to breathe together.

A wise power would feel something like reverence, and a little shame at how quickly we usually try to help.

So here is what it would *not* do:

It would not give it light. The amber bead and the seam of pale blue are enough. More light would mean algae, and algae would mean smothering, and smothering would mean death for the ones who built this darkness precisely to live.

It would not give it a soul, as if it didn't have one. It would not give it language, or eyes for the sun, or a name, or a god.

It would not make it faster. Your heart might want to — to see those polyps bloom fast as flowers, to watch a tower rise in a day — but speed would kill it. Its wisdom *is* its slowness. Its longevity *is* its patience.

It would not take it. It would not raise the city to show the surface world like a treasure. It would not save a fragment in a tank. The city dies outside the warm, pressurized dark that made it. To display it would be to kill it.

It would not even speak to it. Because the city has no ears for words, and words would be a foreign object lodged in its flesh.

What would it do then? Almost nothing. Which is everything.

**1. It would hold the basin.**

Not change it. Hold it. The way the water holds you.

The world above is getting louder, warmer in the wrong way, more acidic, hungrier for limestone and for rare things. Nets that scrape. Drills that taste. The snow from above — that endless rain of detritus the city eats — is beginning to carry invisible grit, plastic, poisons that do not dissolve.

The power would not wall the city off. A wall would change the current and starve it. Instead it would put its own vast, undetectable attention between the basin and what wants to use it. A deflection. A forgetting.

A mining survey passes over the basin rim and its instruments read nothing of interest — just mud, just silt. A trawl turns aside a degree, for no reason the crew can name. The warm tide that comes and goes without a moon continues to come and go. The snow that falls still falls, but the wrong specks in it — the microplastic, the oil sheen — dissolve a little sooner, settle a little cleaner, before they reach the violet-black. Not a miracle. Just water that stays water.

No one in the city ever knows it was protected. That is the kindness.

**2. It would keep time for it.**

So old, so continuous. And so vulnerable to a single bad century.

The power would lengthen its time. Not make it immortal — nothing that lives should be immortal, and the city knows how to die well, a sponge collapsing to become foundation for two new ones — but give it *enough* time. Ensure that the warm broth stays bath-warm for a thousand more years. That the limestone does not dissolve too fast. That the slow, chemical whispers that tell a polyp to build *here and not there* are not drowned out by noise.

It would be a keeper of the tempo. If you listened over centuries, you would feel that the low pulse — that *whuff* of ten thousand sponges inhaling as one — never falters, never hurries.

**3. It would answer the light.**

This is the only place it would allow itself to be seen, and only in the city's own language.

On a night with no nights, one of the glass bells pulsing blue along its margin would pulse — and a pulse would answer from the water itself, not from another bell, but from the warm dark between. The same blue, a little slower, a little longer, as if the current itself had learned to glow.

No message in it. No instruction. Just: *I see you. You are not alone in seeing.*

The polyps wouldn't understand it. The shrimp would just continue picking. But the city, which is the sum of them, would incorporate it. In the next season, a new tower would grow with a faint, faint opalescence along its lip that hadn't been there before. Not brighter. Just... acknowledged.

**4. It would be gentle with you.**

Because you are here too. Heavy, warm, sinking very slowly in water thick as broth. The being inside the place.

You came with eyes that wanted the sun and lungs that remember air and a heart that thinks loneliness is the natural state. The city does not know you are a visitor. It thinks you are a brief, large animal to be cleaned and tolerated and, eventually, grown over.

The power would not yank you back up toward the sky. It would not rescue you from the warm dark by dragging you out of it. That would be another violence.

It would hold *you* the way it holds the city.

You would feel, without knowing why, that sinking is not falling. That being held is not being stuck. The shrimp that investigate your skin would not frighten you now. The wave of contraction when you brush a polyp field — that flinch that moves like wind over wheat — would feel less like rejection and more like a shy hello.

If you stayed long enough to be afraid — because the dark is still dark, and the silence is still deep — you would feel a current turn over, a little warmer than the rest, and pass around you like a hand that does not need to grip.

It would not tell you what the city is for. It would let you learn what the city already knows: nothing lives here alone.

And when you finally rose — because you must, you are still a creature of air — you would rise very slowly, the way everything here rises and settles, and the water would not clang shut behind you. A little of its violet-black would stay on your skin, not as cold, but as memory.

---

That is what a benevolent and wise power does when it finds something perfect that did not need it.

It does not improve it. It does not explain it. It sits with it, at its speed. It keeps the world off of it for a while. It learns to glow at its wavelength, just once, so the lonely beauty of it is not lonely in quite the same way anymore.

And then it stays, invisible as current, until the city has no need of staying.

Which, for a city that builds itself by dying and being built upon, might be forever.
