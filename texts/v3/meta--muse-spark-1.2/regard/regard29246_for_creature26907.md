It would not come down the stair.

That is the first thing it would understand.

Anything wise and benevolent learns, before anything else, how to *arrive*. And a power that came down that stair the way you did — with dry shoes, with weight, with a shadow that startled the pigeons into wheeling — would already have done harm. It would have displaced air. It would have been too much.

So it would not come down.

It would gather at the top. Or it would have always been there, at the lip of the stair, the way dusk gathers.

It would look — and with that kind of looking comes a different scale of sight — and it would see what you saw, but differently. You saw a crowded, warm, hand-made hamlet. A place full of touching and dripping and small work.

It would see a single being.

Not the people alone. Not the brick alone. The whole Basin as one organism that has learned how to keep itself alive: brick that sweats and people who catch the sweat, beetles that would eat the mortar and hands that harvest the beetles, pigeons that carry in grain from the dry world above and children who will later eat the pigeons, moss that holds moisture and old women who guide the moss so it doesn't choke. A slow, patient lung, breathing in light and rain through five open mouths and breathing out smoke and sound and frogsong.

It would see health. Not perfection — there is fever, there is the white film in the eyes of the oldest from smoke, there are the tiny graves tucked in a niche too small for anyone to have lived long — but health. Equilibrium. A thing that has solved itself without being told the answer.

And a wise, benevolent power, seeing a solved thing, feels a very particular kind of reverence. Not the urge to fix. The urge to kneel.

So what would it do?

### Nothing that could be seen.

This is the discipline of benevolence. Malevolence wants to take. Simple kindness wants to give. Wisdom wants to *not break what is already whole*.

It would know that every obvious gift would be a poison in that space:

If it gave them more room, they would lose the touch that teaches them where they are.
If it gave them metal tools, the brick would be cut too fast, too clean, and the soft bowls worn by feet would be quarried away.
If it gave them bright, steady light that did not move, they would stop migrating with the sun and their legs would forget.
If it took away the damp, their skin would crack and their hair would dry and the frogs would go silent.
If it took away hunger or work, the beetle bowls would stay full and the beetles would take the walls.
If it lifted them up out into the dry, windy world because "surely you would be happier with sky," it would kill them with kindness in a week.

Wisdom has seen enough "rescues" to know they are often just a larger mouth eating a smaller one.

So it would do the thing that takes the most restraint of all:

It would keep them hidden.

The true danger to the Basin is not from inside. It breathes fine. The danger is that the surface will remember it. That someone up there will decide the vault would make a good foundation, a good cistern again, a good cellar to be cemented over. That a road will be cut that diverts the rain. That a war will need bricks and will rob them from below.

So the power would not touch the people at all. It would touch the world *around* them, so gently they would never notice.

It would put a thought in the mind of the farmer whose field holds the five oculi: *leave that patch fallow, the ground is soft there, nothing grows well there anyway.* And the farmer would, and would not know why, and thistles would grow tall around the open eyes and shade them just enough.

It would put a weight in the storm — not much — so that the heavy rain still finds the old runnels instead of washing new ones. So that the cistern-well still never overflows, never dries.

It would settle its own hand, which is not a hand, against the barrel-arch. Not to rebuild it. The people know how to repair their own home with more brick and lime, and they do it well. No, only to let the arch settle one millimeter less this year. To let the lime cure one day slower, stronger. To give them another hundred years before a pillar needs to be replaced. They would think they had simply mixed a good batch that year.

It would veil their sound. From above, on a very quiet night, you might hear the frogs if you pressed your ear to the earth. The power would fold that sound back down, the way you cup your hands around a candle. Let the Basin stay loud inside itself and quiet to the world.

That is the first work. Guardianship without presence.

### And then — only if invited — the small mercies that look like nature.

A benevolent power does not withhold comfort out of principle. It just refuses to give comfort that makes the receiver smaller.

So it would give only what can be received as if it had always been there:

In the dark felt of moss at the base of the third pillar, where the vine-tending old women work, a new kind of lichen would appear. Paler, softer, faintly sweet when boiled. Not much, just enough that the children's winter coughs ease a week earlier. The old women would find it and say, "Ah, this one is good this year," and scrape it carefully and share it, and think themselves clever for noticing it.

In the well, among the amber frogs, one more clutch of eggs would take each spring. Not ten times more — just one more. Enough that the boy who watches "his" frog would, more often than not, see it make it to dragonfly-hunting age.

In the deepest part of the night, when the oil lamps are low and the air is at its stillest, the brick would exhale a little more cleanly. The smoke would find the oculi a little faster. The oldest would wake and think, "I slept well," and their eyes would film a little less that year.

And for the children who offer beetles to strangers — that hospitality so complete it doesn't know to be afraid — the power would answer in the only way that doesn't break the rules of the Basin, which is: everything is shared, everything is touched.

It would let one swift's nest, high in the vault where no hand can ever reach, hatch four instead of three this year. It would let the chives on the ledge go to seed so abundantly that there are seeds to trade for a story.

It would never speak in words. Words in that vault double and arrive late. But it would speak the way they speak: in clicks, in small sounds, in touch.

A drip that lands exactly when a crying infant needs a new thing to look at.
A beam of light through an oculus that lands a moment longer on a weaver's hands when she is tired, so she doesn't have to move her loom just yet.
A gecko that pauses on the shoulder of the child who is lonely that day, puffing its throat, staying just long enough to be company.

And once — only once, perhaps for the old woman by the well who lifted her chin to you — it would let itself be felt as it is. Not seen. Felt. As a warmth that is not from fires or bodies. As a sense, settling over the whole Basin at dusk when everyone sits and the frogs get loud, that they are *held inside something that loves them*, the way they hold their own children inside the crook of an arm, shoulder to shoulder, without needing to move.

She would not have a name for it. She would just close her filmy eyes and breathe out with the brick, and think, *we are still here*, and that would be true in a deeper way than before.

That is what the wise and benevolent would do, if it loved them.

It would not save them. They do not need saving.

It would not teach them. They would teach *it* — how to borrow light instead of owning it, how to let what flies in stay, how to wait for water instead of forcing it.

It would sit outside the stair, out of sight, keeping the world off them, tending the tendency of things to stay as they are, and making very small, deniable miracles that the Basin can absorb and call its own.

And when, in a hundred years, a child of theirs climbs the glossy stair for the first time and puts his hand to the plaster burnished by every palm before him — and finds it still warm — he will not know that anything ever watched over him.

He will only know that the wall is warm, and the water is still waiting in its runnel, and the light is still moving, and there is work to do, and a place for his feet.
