If I were that power — wise and benevolent, which is to say *old enough to know better* — I would not come as a rescue.

That's the first temptation, and the first mistake.

From the plain, from above, the cut looks like a wound. If you have power, the instinct is to heal wounds. To fill them, to close them, to lift the people out into the high savanna where the air moves and the sun is honest and there is room to run.

But I would have circled once, high above, and felt the wind shear off the rim and spill down into that still kiln, and I would have looked properly and understood:

This is not a wound anymore. It's a hearth.

They have already healed it the only way a wound like that wants to be healed — not by closing, but by being filled with a living thing that fits it exactly.

So I would not bring the river they don't have. I would not blast the walls down into soft slopes. I would not give them a sky that is all sky, or machines that run at their rated speed, or lungs that don't know wet iron. To do any of that would be to kill them kindly and call it salvation.

A wise power does not fix what is already perfect. A benevolent power does not save people from the shape they have chosen to love.

So this is what I would do instead:

### I would learn to move at their speed.

I would not arrive in light or thunder. Nothing in the cut could survive that kind of arrival — the air is too thick, the sound would break the walls and their eardrums at once.

I would come down the switchback on foot, at the hour when the column of sun has just lifted off the cistern. I would keep my eyes down so I didn't blind myself, and I would breathe shallowly, the way they do. One breath every ten seconds. Thud... thud... thud... in rhythm with the flywheels.

I would make myself small and damp enough that at first they would not see me either. A smear of condensation on a pipe. A draft that finally finds the bottom and stirs the beans around a flywheel housing for a moment before it stills.

I would sit on the highest catwalk at dusk. Not above them, *among* the level where their roofs are streets and their streets are roofs. Where the pale night-blooms are opening and that sweet rot perfume is laying itself over the coal smoke.

And I would watch, for a long time, without being seen to watch. Because to be witnessed without being watched is a kind of blessing, and these people are watched by no one except each other. Nine hundred souls in a room where everyone can see everyone else — they have never been seen from the outside by eyes that did not want something from them. Metal. Labor. Pity.

I would want nothing.

I would just learn their tap-language before I ever used it. Wrench on pipe: *I'm coming up behind you.* Three taps on tin: *water coming through, move your bucket.*

### What I would not do:

I would not take the smoke away entirely. They have banked their forges low on purpose. They know how much smoke the rim can swallow before the bad air pools and settles. To make their air crystal would be to tell them their careful throttling was never needed.

I would not make it less damp. Their skin has learned to never be dry. Their clothes, their barter, their gourds, their moss ten centimeters thick that glows faintly green-black — all of it is a treaty with damp. Break the treaty and you break the city.

I would not give them more space. *Build up, not out. There is no out.* That rule is not a limitation to them. It is their geometry of belonging. From any point you can see rim to rim — that is how they know they are held. To give them an annex, a suburb, a tunnel to the plain would be to give them agoraphobia.

### What I would do — so quietly they could argue it never happened:

**1. I would put my hand on the walls.**

Not to soften their knife-clean edges — they love that the cut is still young and glassy and remembers being sheared. But to stop them from failing. Deep in the basalt, heat stress fractures are still traveling, one micron a year. Water is still trying to cut those black teeth two or three meters down into a gully that, in a hundred years, will finally undercut the eastern switchback and take half the fourth tier with it when the rains come hard.

I would put my palm there, on the rock that still holds drill marks, and I would let the fractures knit. Not healed smooth — just stitched, like a scar that holds. The walls would stay sharp. They would just stay sharp *forever*, and no child would ever have to wake to the wall coming down.

They might notice that rust forms a little slower on the rebar after that season. They wouldn't know why.

**2. I would tend the water without ever touching a drip line.**

The first rule is the holiest: *Do not block a drip line. The path of water belongs to everyone below you.*

I would not add a spring. A spring would make them fight over ownership. I would not make it rain inside the cut.

I would do this instead: I would make the stone exhale a little more evenly. Right now, beads form and run together into trickles, but some ledges weep and some stay dry for days, and the old man by the cistern has to record who got lucky. I would thicken the condensation on the dry ledges by one degree of cool. Not enough to notice, just enough that every barrel gets *something* by morning, even the one under the overhang that always comes up short.

And once a year, when the moss high up gets waterlogged and releases all at once and threatens to overtop and churn the cistern into mud — I would hold it for one extra hour. Just one. So the old man's notebook says, "released slow today," and he frowns, and then they have time to bail.

I would never correct a kinked hose. That is their justice to do. But I would make sure the punishment — three days of having to ask — never kills a child.

**3. I would filter the bad air the way they already try to.**

They train bean runners around flywheel housings to pull heat away. They sing low so they don't sweat. They run everything slow so heat doesn't pool. And still, sometimes in deep August, the bad air pools — carbon smoke and hot damp that won't leave, that settles on the lowest sleeping lofts and makes the old cough and the babies cry in a thin, tired way.

I would ask one of the flowering vines to change, very slightly. A new sport, waxy pale as the others, night-blooming, sweet rot perfume — but with leaves a little broader, a little more stomata on the underside. It would eat that low smoke a fraction faster. It would seed itself only where the bad air is worst, because it likes it.

They would think a grandmother discovered it and trained it. They would be right. She did. I just made sure the seed was there for her tweezers to find.

The old will breathe a little easier in their sleep. They will think this August was not as bad as last. They will be correct.

**4. I would leave a door that is not a door.**

On the western wall, behind where the foundry pours once a week at night, there is a place where the basalt sheared glassy and glittering with unoxidized minerals. If you put your ear to it, you can hear the plain's wind on the other side, very faintly.

I would thin that place by the width of a fingernail. Not to make an exit — they don't want an exit — but to make a choice *visible*. If anyone ever needed to leave and could not climb the elevator — if the chain shuddered and failed, if the cut ever did try to close — that thin place would give under a sledge, one time, and make a crawlway out that could be resealed.

They will never use it. That is the point of a good door in a loved house. You don't use it to leave forever; you use it to know you are not locked in. The people who ride the elevator up to trade and come back after a day saying the huge sky makes them feel like they'll blow away — they will touch that glassy place sometimes, tap it once with a wrench — *I'm here* — and feel better, and go back to their loom.

**5. I would let them feel, once, that they are held correctly.**

This is the only overt thing I would do. And I would do it at dusk, when they all come out onto each other's roofs and share water that has been passed through three sets of hands already.

For one evening, I would not add light. The sodium worklamps and bare bulbs are already amber and enough. I would not add food or song.

I would add weight.

Not heavy weight. Held weight. The way the basalt warms at night and radiates back their own heat, only slightly more so. The way smoke rises straight up for fifty meters before it spills out, only that column would hold a second longer. The air, for an hour, would feel exactly as thick and damp and still as it always is, but also — for once — *intentional*. As if the cut itself exhaled and said, *yes, this is the right shape for you.*

The weaver at her throttled loom would pause with her foot on the treadle, once every four seconds, and feel not alone in her patience.

The forge-man leaning against his anvil, watching his metal heat to cherry for a long, long time, one measured scoop of vine-char at a time, would feel his slowness not as endurance but as prayer, and that prayer being heard.

A child who was born when the bean vine reached the third-floor walkway would look up at the square of deepening blue and the first stars appearing, far above the rim, and would not feel small beneath it. She would feel located.

No one would see anything. No one would need to. In the morning, the flywheels would still go thud... thud... thud... The drips would still go tap-tap-tap on tin. The ferns would still tremble in the updraft where the walls are right there, close enough to see.

And on the highest catwalk, where I had sat, there would be a faint handprint in the moss, ten centimeters thick. Green-black, already filling back in.

They would train a vine over it before the week was out.

That is what a benevolent and wise power does when it finds a place that has already learned how to live:

It does not lift it out of its hole. It keeps the hole from being quarried deeper. It keeps the world above from ever deciding the cut is empty and useful. It learns the tap-code and uses it sparingly.

It becomes, for as long as it is needed, the extra wall that makes the room feel *more* like a room, not less.

And then, because wisdom is mostly knowing when not to stay conspicuously, it leaves — slowly, at walking pace — up the switchback road, letting the heat trap itself again behind it, leaving the city exactly as packed and damp and dim and patient and strange and perfectly suited as it was.

Only — if you were a resident, and you lived there long enough to see a building settle a degree every year — you might notice this year it didn't.

You might look at the cistern, always half-full and rippling from drips that fall sixty meters, and think the ripples are, today, a little more regular. A little more like breathing with you.

You would be right.
