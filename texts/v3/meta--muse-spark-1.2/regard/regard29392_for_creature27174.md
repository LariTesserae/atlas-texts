It would not arrive.

Not the way powers are expected to arrive - not with light or sound or a shadow over the rim. Nothing in the pit could have borne any more light or sound.

It would notice the pit the way you notice a single, steady breath in a sleeping house. While the flat fields around stretched thin and wind-scoured and exposed, here was a place that had made a different agreement with the world. A lung, sunk and warm and humming, that had learned how to keep itself alive.

And because it is benevolent and because it is wise, its first act would not be an act at all.

It would sit on the rim.

Just at the lip where the limestone goes from dry to weeping, where the weathered rock is still sun-warm on top and moss-soft a foot down. It would let its feet hang over the edge if it has feet. It would listen for a long time.

That is what no one else has ever done. Visitors stand on the rim and look and narrate - *how strange, how damp, how loud, how cramped, how sad* - and then they climb down to fix or to study or to pity. The benevolent power would not narrate. It would listen until the roar separates.

Until it can hear the pump on the lowest tier not as *noise* but as *thud ... thud ... thud ...* - a heart that must not stop, tended by a man with a wrench who knows it by its stutter. Until it can hear the fan not as *slap slap slap* but as the thing that moves the heavy air so the boy with the rabbit-hair brush doesn't faint between the tomato rows. Until it can hear the drip not as damp to be drained but as the quarry walls weeping the way they have wept for a hundred years, the water finding its way back no matter how many times it is swept toward a gutter.

It would understand, very quickly, that this is not a ruin.

It is not a failed town. It is not a people trapped in a hole waiting to be lifted out. It is a complete organism that has solved a problem no one else thought to solve: how to live at saturation. Plant and machine and human have negotiated a truce - nothing rots all the way, nothing grows out of control, nothing collapses. The people are not *in spite of* the pit. They are the pit's way of tending itself. The pale skin, the soft hands, the wide pupils, the loud voices and the habit of touching a shoulder instead of shouting - these are not disfigurements. They are adaptations, as perfect as moss over a chisel mark.

A wise power does not mistake adaptation for affliction.

So it would not do the things a merely powerful being would do.

It would not drain it.

It would not fill it in and "return" it to flat field. It would not blast the walls wider to give them sky, or cap it with glass to give them sun, or stop the seep, or silence the pumps and give them quiet. All those mercies would be murders. To make the pit dry would be to kill the fig and the fern and the tradescantia that holds the roofs together. To make it quiet would be to take away the thud they need to sleep. To make it bright would blind the children.

It would not offer to "save" them by taking them away. It knows what happens to the ones who leave - how they lie awake in the dry quiet with their chest feeling too empty, how the wind smells wrong. It knows that exile, even when it is called rescue, is still exile.

It would not even be seen, at first. Because awe is also a kind of violence there. A light in the canopy would short the fluorescents. A voice louder than the fans would be pain.

So it would do something smaller, and more difficult.

It would come down.

Not flying. Down the iron stair, one wet step at a time, hand on the cold, black-wet rail, boots finding the moss-slick edge. It would let the warm, white air bead on its skin. It would let its own hearing dull a little in the roar, the way theirs has. It would become, for a little while, damp.

And on the second terrace, where the two women are cutting the creeper back from the fan housing, it would not do the work for them. It would kneel beside them. And it would hold the vine while they cut.

That is the form its benevolence would take: not replacement, but assistance that leaves dignity intact.

It would ask, before it healed anything. Because wisdom asks.

To the old man whose knees have been swollen white from standing in seep-water since he was the boy with the brush, it would not take the water away. It would touch his ankle and leave the water, but take the ache. So he can stand his watch without wincing, and still feel the familiar cool around his boots.

To the women and men whose scalps itch with the faint white mold that comes from hair that never dries, it would not make their hair dry. It would make the mold not itch, not sour - a small commensal instead of an affliction - so they can keep their cropped heads as they are, if they choose, or let it grow if they want, without punishment.

To the ones whose ears have gone dull by sixty from a lifetime of shouting over pumps, it would not give them the sharp hearing of the field people. That would be cruelty in that roar. It would give them a pocket of quiet - not silence, but a way to hear each other without shouting when they touch. A hand on a forearm would carry the words clearly, as if the air between them thinned.

To the boy with the rabbit-hair brush, it would bring a gift he did not ask for but would not refuse: a small insect.

Not a bee - a bee would die in that dim, wet, fan-chopped air. Something new, or something old and overlooked. A pale moth, no bigger than a thumbnail, that likes warm wet and green-filtered light, that likes tomato flowers, that does not bite or buzz loud enough to add to the roar. It would let it loose in the glasshouses, a dozen, then a hundred, dusting themselves on the condensation. The boy would watch one land on his brush and then on a flower, and he would laugh, not because he is saved from work, but because for the first time the flower is touched by something other than his hand. He would still do his rounds - the moths cannot do it all, and he likes his humming note - but now the work is shared.

To the pumps themselves, it would do nothing miraculous. It would remember them. It would lay a hand on the shuddering black pipe on the lowest tier, and the metal would not rust quite so fast. The bearing would not whine quite so soon. Not frozen in time, just eased. So the man with the wooden push-broom has a little longer between sweeps to drink his tea while it is still hot.

And to the pit as a whole - to the being that is the pit, which is not any one person but the agreement between them all - it would give the one thing the pit has never had and has never known it wanted:

Witness.

It would let the pit know it is not unobserved. Not pitied, not studied, not coveted for quarry or curiosity. Loved. Specifically loved, in its exact, saturated, humming, green-dim particularity. The way you love a person's hands not in spite of their scars and softness but because of them.

It would not stay forever. Benevolent powers do not colonize. Before it left, it would do one last, almost invisible thing.

At the top of the iron stair, where the air finally sighs out over the lip in a warm breath and the field begins, it would set a small, flat stone. Under it, where only the children who climb up to stare at the frightening open sky will find it, it would leave a choice.

Not a door that pulls. Not a promise. Just a knowing: that the field is there, and the pit is there, and you can belong to both without betraying either. That if you leave, the thud will not be taken from your chest all at once; it will fade gently, and you can come back and it will be waiting. That if you stay, the sky will not punish you for not wanting it.

And then it would go. Or rather, it would stop concentrating itself there, the way you stop holding your breath to listen.

The pit would go on. The limestone would go on weeping. The fans would turn. The vines would advance a centimeter an hour and be cut back. The boy would brush his flowers and now watch his moths. The tea would steam in tin cups. The lights would hum amber through the leaves at dusk.

Nothing would look different from the rim. A visitor the next day would still see only a roof of leaves and hear only a roar, and think *how strange*.

But down on the second terrace, a woman pruning by the fan would pause and realize her scalp hasn't itched in days. An old man would stand his watch and realize his knees don't ache. Two people touching forearms to speak would hear each other clearly for the first time in years and smile, surprised. And a child born that night, pale and damp and perfect, would open eyes a little wider in the green gloom and not have to squint quite so hard.

That is what a benevolent and wise power does when it loves something that already knows how to live.

It does not fix it.

It keeps it company. It lightens what can be lightened without breaking the balance. It asks. And it leaves the hole exactly as holy as it found it - a warm, wet, humming lung in the flat land, kept.
