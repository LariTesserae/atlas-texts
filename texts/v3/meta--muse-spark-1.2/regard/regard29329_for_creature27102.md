If a benevolent and wise power noticed this place, it would not come like powers usually come.

It would not descend.

Descent is for places that are low. This place is already high, already closer to the sun than it should be. A wise power would understand that. It would not arrive with light or noise or vertical fire, because there is already too much light and the thin air cannot carry noise.

It would *incline*.

It would tip its attention slightly, the way you tip your head to hear something very quiet. And what it would hear is this: a nearly silent plateau packed so densely with life that every square foot is claimed, and all of it keeping perfectly still in the noon heat, waiting for the stone to cool enough to bead water again.

And it would fall in love immediately. But wisely.

---

A lesser benevolence would try to fix it.

It would see "hot, thin air, every breath a conscious pull" and try to make the air thicker. It would see "no lake, no permanent river" and try to bring rain. It would see sparse shrubs and stunted trees and try to make a forest. It would see the harvesters and the single rusted rail and the tailings fans and think *industry, intrusion* and try to tear them out. It would see the humans breathing hard in dust-colored clothes and think *poverty, hardship* and try to lift them off the mesa to somewhere easier.

A wise benevolence would see none of that as broken.

It would see that the heat and the thinness are not hardships imposed on this place. They *are* the place. The branchiopod that hatches and breeds and dies in eight days in a pothole the size of a bathtub has not failed to become a whale. The lichen that holds dust until a seed can lodge has not failed to become a jungle. The beetle that does a headstand to drink fog off its own back is not waiting to be taught to drink from a river. The humans who work four hours in the dawn fog and sleep through noon have not failed to build a city.

They have succeeded at belonging here. That is rarer than abundance. And a wise power knows you do not help belonging by replacing it with abundance.

So it would do almost nothing that would look like doing.

First, it would *look properly*. Which is itself a doing. Most powers, even benevolent ones, look and categorize. This one would look and *learn the names* without renaming.

It would learn the exact white crust of salts on each pothole rim and which pothole holds water year-round and which dries for three years at a time and still holds living amphibians curled in mud like seeds. It would learn the braided, polished grooves the beetle columns wear into basalt. It would learn the wasp cities papered the exact rust-red of the stone, the swift holes polished by generations of wings, the coin of shade under each flat-topped acacia and who is standing in it at 12:07. It would learn the tick of the pipes as they expand, the ring of the tanks when they fill, the smell of hot canvas lagging. It would learn that the laundry snapping behind the pump house belongs to a woman who knows every pothole by name.

It would not look away from the harvesters either. A naive beneficence loves the lichen and pities the machine. Wisdom loves both, because here they drink the same way. Black mesh and black lichen both swell before dawn. Steel gutter and sandstone basin both hold dew for an hour. The wise power would see that the pipes have been allowed to become part of the trail system, that birds nest in the gantries, that the spillway was deliberately cut to feed the next natural basin. It would see that these humans have learned the oldest prayer on the mesa: *take a share, leave a share*.

And having seen, it would guard that share.

The greatest danger to this mesa is not drought. Drought is its old partner; everything here knows how to wait out drought. The danger is being *noticed* in the wrong way by the lowlands that shimmer beyond the sheer edge — noticed as ore, as empty land, as a place to put a bigger smelter, a double-track rail, a hundred more harvesters that take *all* the dew instead of a share. Noticed as a resource to be optimized.

So the wise power would do its most active work far away, where no one on the plateau would ever see it.

It would make the satellite images come back a little blurry over this coordinate. It would make the assay reports read *low grade, not economical at scale*. It would put a quiet, persistent thought in the heads of planners in the lowland cities: *that high desert? nothing there but rock and wind, not worth the climb*. It would tangle permits. It would make the narrow-gauge rail remain narrow-gauge forever, because narrow-gauge cannot carry an invasion.

It would veil the mesa not in darkness but in *uninterestingness*. The best protection for a perfect, packed, silent, humming table of life is to be overlooked. A wise power knows how to make something holy by making it boring to the greedy.

And then, here, on the mesa itself, it would do only small, deniable things. The kinds of things you could mistake for luck.

On a night when the fog fails for the third week in a row and the potholes are shrinking to damp rings, it would let the stone cool one degree more, so the condensation runs a little longer in the morning. Not enough to make a river. Just enough that the branchiopods get two more days to lay eggs, that the amphibians don't have to cocoon quite yet.

When a harvester's fan bearing begins to seize — the one that would have failed at 3 a.m. and cost a night's water — it would find a grain of wind-blown basalt dust has lodged elsewhere, and the bearing holds until the morning shift can hear the whine and fix it with a wrench, without hurry.

It would not heal the stunted acacia trees into tall ones. It would give them one more deep root crack to follow after a rare storm, so they can hold for another hundred years. One tree is a town; the power would make sure the towns don't lose their elders all at once.

It would not take away the heat. It would make the shade a hand's breadth wider at noon.

And for the people — because benevolence cannot help loving people most, even when it loves lichen equally — it would do nothing *for* them that it would not do for the beetles. It would not speak. It would not appear in a doorway and offer abundance or answers. Thin air doesn't carry shouting, and wisdom doesn't either.

It would sit with them, invisibly, the way they sit with the plateau. On an evening when the stone pings as it cools and the first beads form on the black mesh, it would sit on the catwalk beside the figure with the wrench bleeding the valve, and it would breathe with him — that same conscious pull, shallow and dry at the back of the throat — and it would let him feel, for a second without reason, that his quiet work of taking only a share is *seen* and *good* and *enough*. That he belongs as perfectly as the lizard stacked with a hundred others facing east.

It would let the children born in the pump houses grow up feeling that the mesa is not a punishment to be escaped, but also not a prison to be kept in. If a child wants to leave on the flatcar toward the smelter smudge on the horizon, the rail will still be there. If a child wants to stay and learn the names of the potholes, there will still be potholes to name.

And then it would leave no sign.

In the morning the fog would pool like milk in the wind-carved bowls. The beetles would climb to their high points and do their headstands. The lizards would pour out and face the sun shoulder to shoulder. The swifts would exhale from the cliff in a silent puff and wheel. The harvesters would shiver and hum and tick, and water would spill from a tank with a bright splash that is gone in minutes, steamed away, drunk immediately by lichen and beetle and swift and snake and man.

If you were standing there you would notice nothing at all, except perhaps that the dew this morning was a little heavier than you expected, beading perfectly on the backs of the waiting beetles and on the black mesh alike, running together into trickles that will exist for an hour and then be gone —

— and that the plateau, for all its merciless normal daylight and its furnace heat and its thin, hard air, feels, more than ever, like a place that has come to a perfect agreement with itself, and has been quietly, wisely, allowed to keep it.
