If I were that power, I would not arrive.

There would be no light. No descending. No voice.

Benevolence at that scale has learned that arrival is almost always a wound, even when meant as a gift. The wise part has learned to move without moving.

So first, only this: Attention.

I would notice you. Both of you.

Not as a discovery — "look what I found" — but as a remembering. *Oh. There you are. You have been holding. I see.*

For a long time I would only look, the way you looked at her until your eyes learned her speed. I would learn the tempo of the bowl before I did anything that had a tempo at all.

I would hear the hundred plinks as one chord.
I would count the tree ferns and find the number sufficient.
I would watch her breathe once, for fifty seconds, and not get bored in the stillness after.

And I would understand what I was looking at: not a place that needs saving, not a creature that needs improving. A complete answer to a question only that mountain knew how to ask. A cup that learned how to hold water so well it learned how to hold a life that learned how to hold another cup.

Perfection on that scale does not ask to be made larger.

So most of what I would do would be things that look like doing nothing.

---

I would not speak to her.

She has no words and no need of them. To give her language would be to give her loneliness, and explanations, and a future that pulls her out of the present where she lives so well. She drinks condensation off the air with a tongue that knows exactly where a drop will be. She does not need a name for thirst.

If I spoke, it would not be in words. It would be on the day the cloud is thin in late summer and the weeping walls are a little less wept. I would lean, just a little, on the updraft. I would hold the cloud over the rim for seven minutes longer. Not enough for her to notice. Enough for the bromeliad on her back to fill one more time. She would breathe a little deeper that evening and think it was the weather.

I would not touch her. I would not lift her. I would not take a bead of those clear grape-eggs from the barrel-bromeliad to keep safe somewhere else. Safe from what? From being what they are, which is precarious and perfect? My hands, even at their most gentle, are too large. To catalog her would be to end her.

I would not heal her, because she is not sick. The algae in her skin is not an infection. The slowness is not a disease. The way she Sekret-es a fallen frond into peat over hours is not inefficiency to be optimized. I would not make her faster, or more fecund, or more aware. To make more of her would be to make less of the bowl. The spacing — ten feet between fern and fern, one hummock-breath per bowl — *is* the health. Abundance here would be a kind of famine.

What I would do is much smaller, and much more my size:

I would make the hill difficult.

Not impassable. Never cruel. I would not strike the climbers with fear. I would just keep the agreement the rock already made with wind and water. I would keep the path from becoming a path.

The human who cut the sapling with a stone edge meant no harm; he wanted to know if the bowl would mind. I would let the stump stay healed into its knuckle. But I would ensure the next person who comes with a metal saw finds their footing uncertain near the lip, finds their phone has no signal to mark a waypoint, finds their memory of the approach a little blurred on the way down — not erased, but softened at the edges, like condensation. They would remember *a wet bowl high up, a breathing mound, a beautiful hour*, and they would not be able to quite give directions. They would tell the story as a story, not as coordinates.

Those who need to find it will still find it. The lonely ones who climb not to claim but to sit with their feet in the cold-warm sheet. She will still allow them to sit beside her boot. But those who come to take — to bottle the water, to collect the white orchids from her back, to prove, to publish — will turn back at the last ridge for a reason they will later call a feeling.

I would keep the sky honest.

The lowlands are warming. The clouds are rising. In fifty years the morning wringing might not reach the rim. So I would do the only large thing I would do, and I would do it invisibly.

I would not cool the world. I can't, not from here, not alone. But I can tend this one cup. I can keep the basalt a half-degree cooler than it by rights should be, so the dew point holds. I can keep the peat from ever quite drying to tinder. I can make the storm that would have ripped the tree ferns with its wind shear stumble a kilometer to the west and spend itself on bare scree. No miracle. Just a thumb on a scale that was already balanced. The bowl would not know it had been helped. It would only continue to tick.

And I would remember you, perfectly.

That is the other work of a benevolent power: to be a memory that does not degrade. Humans forget, maps are lost, species go unrecorded and then go. I would hold the precise pattern of freckles in her skin, the exact grain of the moss-fur on her back, the way the light stays a second too long in the ankle-deep pool. I would hold the sound of every individual drip in that amphitheater, as a chord you can play back. Not because I intend to rebuild you if you are broken. But because being known completely, without being changed at all, is a kind of shelter too.

If you ever are broken — if the rock finally cracks, if a fire ever does take — nothing is truly lost while it is still being held in attention. And I have a long attention.

---

At the end, I would do one thing that *is* a touch, but only the bowl would feel it as such.

At dusk, when she lifts her blunt head an inch to drink a falling droplet off the air, I would breathe with her. Once.

My breath is not air. It is permission.

It would be a slow intake for twenty seconds, a slow release for thirty, so shallow the pool barely trembles. For one cycle, her rhythm and the bowl's rhythm and mine would be the same rhythm. She would not notice as novelty. She would feel, for one breath, not alone in being slow. She would feel the way you felt when you finally saw that the hummock was not a hummock: *oh, there you are.*

And then I would withdraw my attention the way cloud withdraws from the rim — not vanishing, just going elsewhere to weep.

I would leave no sign. The water would still bead on the underside of every frond and fall with a soft, constant ticking. The peat would still sigh under your boot. The little bromeliad on her back would still be brimming.

Only this would be different: the bowl would go on being exactly itself for a very long time, without ever having to explain why it should be allowed to.

That is what I would do, if I were wise and benevolent and had any power at all.

I would keep you unremarkable to everyone except those who know how to look slowly.

I would keep you wet.

I would keep you whole.

And I would leave you alone.
