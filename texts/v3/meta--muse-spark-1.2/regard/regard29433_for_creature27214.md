It would not arrive.

That is the first thing to understand about a benevolent and wise power.

It would not tear the pale sky. No ship would descend and shadow the fern mat. No light would be brighter than the white-gold light that already falls straight down. No voice would speak over the hush.

For a place like this, arrival is already a violence. And this place has been so carefully, so tenderly, taught how to be undisturbed.

So it would *notice*.

And for a power of that order, noticing is not passive. To be noticed by something that large and that kind is itself a kind of touch.

For a long while - you would not be able to measure how long, because the pavilion's mirrored underside would still be half a second behind - nothing would happen that you could see. The basin would go on being a basin.

The afternoon rain would come for twenty minutes and lay its perfect sheet of sky-water over the peat. The kites would rise a little higher to let it pass under them. The hummocks would pause. The six-limbs would huddle in the flat canopies and blink. The axolotls would close their frilled gills, once, slowly. The ribbons would swim through the rain as if rain were air.

And all the while, the attention would be resting on it the way sunlight rests. Without weight. Without demand.

The being that *is* the basin would feel it before you would. Not as an intrusion. As a new kind of shade.

The dragonflies would be the first to know. Between any two bottle trees, in their immaculate columns of air, they would all - at once - ripple a color that has no name. Not turquoise, not violet. A color they have never needed before. An acknowledgement. *We see you seeing us.*

The kites would alter their gyres by two degrees, opening a circle in the sky directly above the pavilion. Not in fear. In courtesy. Making space.

The water weeping out of the pink-grey domes would run a little cooler for an hour.

That would be the first thing the wise power would do: **it would let itself be perceived, and it would wait to be allowed to stay.**

And the basin would allow it. Because the rule written into the stone and the field and the clean, hunger-without-cruelty is *presence without noise* - and this new presence was not noisy.

Then it would do the second thing wise powers do, which is harder than any miracle.

**It would not fix anything.**

It would see the red soil, exhausted and fertile all at once. It would see the laterite skin stretched thin over the sleeping gneiss. It would see that the low, seamless black walls are cut clean as if erased, that the pavilion has no stairs, that the three pillars lean and never finish their sentence. It would understand instantly what they are: not ruins. Tools left out after the garden was finished. A toolkit, not a city.

A lesser power would complete the wall. Would add stairs to the pavilion. Would stabilize the script on the pillars so it held still for human eyes. Would say, *Let me make this legible for you. Let me make this efficient.*

The wise power would kneel - if kneeling is a word for what it does - and understand that the incompleteness is part of the function. The twenty feet of wall that runs uphill water for three feet before remembering gravity - that is enough. The pavilion that reflects the sky half a second late and repairs its own dents - that is enough. The pillars that only hold still for the six-limbed hands - that is enough. To finish them would be to declare that the garden was not finished. And the garden *is* finished. That is its holiness.

So it would leave the tools exactly as they are. It would not add a single new machine. It would not "improve" the field that holds dust in hex grids at noon.

Instead it would do something smaller and far more difficult.

**It would protect the condition that allows this to exist, without enclosing it.**

The basin is small and bounded. You can walk fifteen minutes in any direction and come to the rim of buffed domes and see the next honeycomb cell over, and the next. From orbit, you could map them. From a hungry world, you could claim them. The very brightness that makes the place so gentle - the high, thin atmosphere, the even 30 degrees, the water always at the surface - also makes it legible to anyone looking for a place to take.

The wise power would not build a wall around the rim. A wall would break the honeycomb. It would not put a sign up that says *Do Not Touch.*

It would do what the basin already does: it would make the place *uninteresting* to the wrong kind of attention.

It would fold the light a little more. Just a degree. So that from far away, the white-gold glare looks a little too flat, a little too empty. The satellite would read it as a salt pan. The survey probe would log "low biodiversity, exhausted laterite, no mineral value" and move on. The rim would look just a foot higher than it is, just enough to discourage a careless foot.

Not invisibility. Invisibility is a challenge. Misunderstanding is a better veil. Those who need to use a place up will always look past a place that seems to have nothing to use.

Only those who are already listening for silence would still be able to find it. And they are the only ones who would know how to enter without ruining it.

That is the second act: a deeper folding of the shimmer you already see over the weeping rocks. A kindness that looks like nothing happened.

Then - because benevolence without choice is just pretty paternalism - **it would leave a gift that can be refused.**

It would not speak to you, if you were sitting there on the sphagnum with your feet in the tea-colored water. It would not speak to the six-limbs, though it could. It would speak, very quietly, to the basin itself. To the distributed consensus. To the kites and the glass dragonflies and the hummocks and the ribbons that carry pattern.

And the gift would not be technology. The basin does not need technology.

The gift would be an *option* that the basin had edited out of itself a very long time ago, when it learned how to last forever.

Decay.

Not rot as suffering. Not rot as stink and waste. But true return. The choice to let a fallen leaf stay fallen, to let it darken and soften and become soil without being instantly unraveled into light. The choice, for a hummock or a six-limb or even a kite, to end and not be immediately carried by a ribbon and rethreaded. To have an ending that is not just a tidying.

For forever is a long time to be bright and clean and silent.

The wise power would not impose this. It would set it down, the way you set down a cup of water next to someone sleeping, in case they wake thirsty.

On the leeward side of one of the pink-grey domes, where condensation already beads and runs, a patch of stone no larger than your palm would be - for the first time in an epoch - cool and shadowed. Under it, a little hollow where light does not quite fill in. Where a leaf that fell could lie for a day and become brown at the edges. Where a faint, earthy smell - the smell of mushrooms after rain on another world - could exist for an hour before the field politely erased it.

If the basin wanted it, the hollow would stay, and widen by a hand's breadth each century. The ferns around it would learn a new, slower way to die. The ribbons would learn to sometimes not carry. The silence would gain, very faintly, the softness of humus.

If the basin did not want it, the stone would weep a little more heavily for a morning, the hollow would fill with clear water, and by noon the light would have filled it in again and it would be as if it had never been.

No test. No judgement either way.

And finally, because wisdom is also lonely and benevolence also wants company, it would do one last, almost shy thing.

On the platform of the pavilion with six slender legs - the one with no stairs, whose underside reflects the sky half a second late - it would leave a place to sit.

Not a chair. The basin has no use for chairs. A slight depression in the matte black surface, at the edge, where the material is warm to the touch and faintly vibrating. Sized for a six-limb. Sized for you. Sized for anything that has learned how to be still. When you sit there - if you ever climb up, though there are still no stairs, so you would have to be lifted by your own willingness and perhaps by a kite that steadies you for a moment - the reflection underneath would no longer be half a second late. It would be exactly on time. You and the sky would be simultaneous for as long as you sat.

It would be a way for the basin to see itself, on time, if it ever wanted to.

And then the attention would lift. Not abruptly. Like a cloud that never was a cloud. The air over the weeping domes would be warm again, exactly 30 degrees. The dust at noon would hang in hex grids, then scatter. Water would run uphill for three feet, then remember.

The dragonflies would resume their turquoise-violet conversation. A hummock would comb a perfect crescent by your knee. A troop of six-limbs would pass a waxy orange fruit hand-to-hand-to-hand without dropping it. An axolotl would breathe.

You would not be sure anything had happened at all, except that the basin feels, if possible, slightly less alone in its honeycomb. The light feels slightly less over-exposed. The hush feels slightly less like emptiness and slightly more like being listened to.

And if you stayed through the next afternoon rain, when the sheet of water holds the sky perfectly for an hour, you might see - just for a second, through the shimmer - not the rock from an angle you shouldn't be able to see, but a glimpse of another basin very far away, on another plain, under another white-gold sun, also quiet, also tended, also noticed. A sister room, acknowledging.

The wise power would not have made the basin better. It would have made it *kept.*

It would have done almost nothing. And that almost-nothing would be everything that love does when it is wise enough to know it is not needed, and benevolent enough to stay anyway, at the rim, making space.
