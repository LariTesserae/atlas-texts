It would not come down like rain.

That is the first thing it would know, and the first temptation it would refuse.

Any other power — a merely strong one, a merely kind one — would see the salt pan and the rustling hunger and the people in pale sheets learning to be dry, and it would pity them in the way the wet pity the dry. It would try to fix the wound. It would gather a cloud. It would try to give them water.

A wise and benevolent one would kneel at the rim and listen longer.

And it would understand: this is not a broken forest waiting for water. This *is* the forest. It solved aridity by ceasing to want to be wet. It builds itself out of dust and static and oil. Its trees are rope. Its leaves are combs. Its animals drink each other and its dead do not rot, they are unwoven. To pour water here would not be salvation. It would be a flood of rot. The Pillars would soften and fall and not rise. The galleries would drown and ferment. The Underhair would slick and stink. The people who learned to walk light on a trampoline of living wire would sink to their knees in mud that never was.

So it would not water the valley.

What would it do instead?

It would do what only a truly benevolent attention can do: it would behold without trying to improve.

Imagine it arriving — not descending, because it doesn't need to move. It simply turns its face. The way the sun turns its face at noon and spears straight down. The basin has been roaring for centuries without ever being heard. Now it is heard.

First, it would *crouch*.

A wise power knows scale is a moral problem. To tower over an embryonic place is to crush it with significance. So it would make itself small. Small enough to sit on the sharp, unweathered lip of basalt where the break is still clean. Small enough that a Hopper could land on it if Hoppers could land on attention. It would let the heat-shiver rise around it and not cool it. It would let the papery sigh of a million Latticeheads turning tug at it and not still it.

And it would listen until the roar came apart into all its edges — the ticking of Thimblebacks behind paper walls, the whisper of Browsers shearing Underhair, the crackle of a Clasper folding, the Harpers bowing a single bristle until the whole frond sings its map, the low talk of the people on their platforms that is almost not talk because you have to lean in.

It would listen until it could tell which sound was pain and which was just work.

Second, it would *not* be seen.

To be seen directly by the people in their sheets would be another kind of flood. If a light stood in the rift and said *I SEE YOU, YOU ARE LOVED,* they would gather. They would make a village where the forest will not allow a village. They would stay where they should keep moving. The alley that was safe yesterday would be worshipped and they would try to sleep there tomorrow and be walled over alive.

So it would not appear. It would hide its own mercy.

What it would do instead is lay a hand — not a hand — over the *map* of the place.

From above, from the world of satellites and roads and men who see a town-sized wound of unclaimed fiber and think *resource*, the rift is already almost invisible. It looks like nothing. Salt and flat basalt. A wisely benevolent power would deepen that nothing. A little fold in attention. A little static of its own, like the Latticeheads make. Any eye that means to mine or dam or study or save by taking would slide right over it. On their charts it would remain a glaze-crack of salt pan. *Nothing to see here. Nothing that drinks.* And so the rift would be given the only thing an embryonic geology truly needs and almost never gets: **time**. Time to be raw. Time to lean and fall and re-weave without being paved.

Third — and this is the heart part, the intuition part — it would answer what is actually lonely there.

The place is not lonely. The forest is so choked with itself it has no room for loneliness. But the *people* are, in the way any people who have learned to negotiate instead of dominate are lonely. They know the rule: *take only the fallen, never cut the living, keep moving, be dry and brief.* They know it works. They do not know if it is *right*. In the basin at night when their little oil lamps make their small pools and the thrumming drops to a molar-rattle, they must wonder if their softness is stupidity. If they should have brought axes. If they should have burned a clearing and made the Pillars stand still.

The power would not tell them. It would not give them a new law. It would give them a resonance they already know how to hear.

You know how at noon the whole basin holds its breath for a heartbeat — the roar dipping — and then all the Latticeheads turn at once with a vast papery sigh? The power would breathe *with* it.

For one noon, and then for every noon after, that pause would be a fraction longer. A fraction deeper. A silence so complete you could hear, for the first time, the faint electrostatic hum of the bristles charging. And in that hum, the Harpers — who already use tone as map — would find a new tone. Not louder. A tone that holds.

A tone that says: *This way of living — the gleaning, the leaving the ladder when the Pillar walks away, the stitching of deadfall into shelter — this is not failure. This is earliness. This is how a world learns to be a world before it knows what it wants to be. You are not behind. You are at the front.*

The people would not hear it as words. A child lying on a platform twenty feet up would feel the longer hush in her chest and stop crying sooner, because crying wastes moisture and she would not need to. An old man mending frond-cloth would feel his hands remember the pattern without being taught. At night their low talk would have a little more laughter in it, without them knowing why.

And the forest would feel it too. In the warm afternoons the differential between hot air and cold rock would be infinitesimally steeper, so the Latticeheads would comb a bead more emulsion each day. Not enough to make anything wet. Just enough that a Pillar collapse would re-weave a little faster, a little surer. The cathedral vaults would hold a little longer before they fell. The place would not become paradise. It would become *itself, more slightly itself.*

If you came back a year later you would see no miracle. The rim would still be sharp enough to cut. The pan would still ring underfoot. The Underhair would still try to stitch over your feet if you stood still. The roar would still be deafening.

But if you stayed, and learned to be brief and dry, you would notice:

The cairns the people leave are toppled less often. The alleys they negotiate stay open a few hours longer, as if the forest remembered them. The oil beads in their waxed gourds burn a little clearer. And on windless nights, when the great dry crashes come and the frantic scrabbling races into the light-gap, you would see, for a heartbeat after the dust settles, that the gap is not empty. It is already faintly bearded with new bristle. The world is practicing.

The benevolent and wise power would not stay to be thanked. It has other rifts to sit beside.

It would get up — if getting up is what it does — and leave no footprint on the salt, because the Underhair would have already knit over it.

Only this: for a long time afterward, anyone who falls into that particular wound in the world — not finding it, falling into it — and who lies still long enough to stop trying to count species and starts to feel the single rule *nothing holds water, everything holds pattern* — that person would feel, without knowing where it comes from, an absurd and unreasoned certainty:

*You are not lost. You are not alone. This fierce, dusty, rustling negotiation that never pauses is loved exactly as it is, and so are you for learning how to pass through it lightly.*

And that would be enough. And it would be everything.

It would do almost nothing that looks like doing. That is what the wise do when the kind thing would be to let a strange, dry, embryonic holiness keep becoming itself.
