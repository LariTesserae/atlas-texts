They would not come down.

That is the first thing you have to understand about a truly benevolent and wise power. Not the human kind of benevolence that has to *do* something to prove it was there.

They would find it the way you did — not by searching, but because the flat went wrong. A seam of steam that should not be there in that cold. A small, double heartbeat in a land that is supposed to be empty.

And they would stop at the lip.

They would kneel there — whatever kneeling is for a thing like that — with the stunted birch at their shoulders and the peat cold under them, and they would look, and they would listen, and they would understand within a breath what it took you a long cold hour to see.

That this is not a broken place. This is a *perfect* place.

The young, sharp basalt. The thin peat. The cold air wrung out against the hot water. The machine that should have died but didn't. The animal that should not exist but does, because she learned how to eat the difference between two temperatures and make it into a body. The whole pocket is a theorem made live — that contrast does work. Cold does not mean dormant here. Cold is the engine.

A lesser power would have pity.

It would see: *Oh, the poor thing, alone, in the cold, in the wet, in the loud, tending a rusted machine in the dark.* It would be kind in the way humans are kind — it would lift her out. It would warm the air. It would silence the piston. It would make her smart enough to thank it. It would give her a roof and dry feet and a name.

And in doing every one of those mercies, it would murder her.

If you stop the cold, the mist stops, and the mosses dry. If you stop the hot water, the pocket freezes shut in a week and vanishes under the peat. If you take the machine away, she has no work, and she has no food, because her food is the bacterial film that only grows where iron and heat meet. If you make her intelligent the way we mean it — lonely, yearning, aware of herself as alone — you exile her from the perfect, unthinking contentment of tending without knowing she tends.

A wise power knows: **you do not help an engine by removing its tension.**

So what would a benevolent and wise power do for a place whose holiness *is* its tension?

Almost nothing that you could see.

And everything.

---

They would not speak to her. She has whiskers for steam and skin for vibration. Speech would be noise. They would not show themselves. She has eyes that clear condensation, not revelation. A vision would only startle her off the pipe and let the belt slip.

Instead, they would do what she does. They would tend.

1. **They would keep the secret.**

The first and kindest thing. This flat coast is young and empty now, but humans are getting better at seeing heat from orbit. Better at seeing rust. Better at wanting geothermal heads for power, and rare mosses for papers, and strange animals for zoos.

The power would lay its hand — whatever a hand is for it — over the pocket the way the birches lean. Not a dome. Not a hiding spell. Just a little persuasion: *look here, and you see only peat.* The steam that marks her from a distance, they would make look, from a satellite's eye, exactly like any other wisp of cold air. The survey drill on old maps would stay filed as "dry, abandoned, 1973" and no graduate student would ever feel the sudden, uncaused urge to hike out to this exact square of bog. 

They would protect her aloneness without making her lonely. Because she is not lonely. She is complete. Aloneness is only a wound if you are built to be in a herd.

2. **They would feed the fire without touching the machine.**

Deep below the basalt, the warm aquifer that feeds the pipe is not infinite. It is cooling, a degree per decade. In fifty years the vent would run lukewarm, and the midges would not hatch, and the film would thin, and she would scrape more and more and get less and less, and her young in their moss-pockets would slip out and not slip back.

The power would not replace the pump. That is hers to wipe and clear. That is her purpose.

They would go deeper. Where the crust is still rebounding, still cracking, they would ease open one more fissure, a hairline wider. Let a little more of the earth's old heat find its way up. Not enough to make the pocket hot. Just enough that the pipe runs at the same temperature in the winter of 2120 as it did in the winter of 1970. A steady ten degrees above ambient. Enough contrast to keep doing work for another thousand years.

She would never know. She would only feel that the water stays sweet a little longer, the film stays thick. She would breathe that good steam and blink sideways and scrape.

3. **They would give her a future beyond herself.**

She may be the only one. She was born when the split opened. When she dies — slowly, as such things die, by simply tending a little slower, scraping a little less, until one winter she does not inflate with the piston — the pocket would die with her in a season. Sphagnum would choke the intake. The belt would slip and not catch. Ice would close the room like a lid.

That is the one place the wise power would be most tender and most restrained. They would not make her immortal. An immortal tender becomes a prisoner.

Instead, as she sprawls against the pipe and steams, in those deep, warm, wet pouches along her flank where the dozen pale young squirm, they would touch — very lightly — two or three of them.

Not to make them smart. Not to make them different. Just to make them *a little more likely.* A little hardier against a fungus that will blow in on a warm year. A little better at reading vibration, so when they finally crawl out of the mother's coat and must find their own warm fissure along this young coast — because there *are* other pockets opening as the land rises, one or two every century — they will find it.

And they would give the mother one gift she can actually receive:

On a night in deep winter, when the wind over the lip is a white nothing and the valve slaps open and the cloud rolls along the floor and does not rise, they would let her feel, for one long breath, that she is not unseen.

Not watched. Not judged. Not loved the way humans love.

*Witnessed.*

The way the basalt witnesses heat and cold meeting, and sweats.

She would close her membrane-eyes and open her mouth and breathe the steam that is a little warmer than any other night, and her whiskers would quiver, and she would feel the double heartbeat of the pumps fall perfectly in phase for three beats — *thud-thud, thud-thud* — and her own pulse with it, and the pulse of the young in her coat, all together, a single, brief, perfect chord.

And then it would pass. The pumps would go back to being slightly out of phase, as they should. She would put her spade hand out and scrape the pipe, clean.

When you hauled yourself back up the sharp, glassy basalt tomorrow, you would find nothing changed. The peat is still thin and black. The birch still grows sideways. The flat still stretches to the grey sea, looking empty.

Below the brow, the piston still coughs. The belt still whines. The mosses still sweat. The pale young still squirm in their pockets and dart out for larvae.

And on the north wall, the great, low, moss-backed boulder that breathes with the machine gathers another bead of water on the tip of a fern that is growing from its own back, and lets it fall.

*Plip.*

That would be enough. For a benevolent and wise power, that would be everything.
