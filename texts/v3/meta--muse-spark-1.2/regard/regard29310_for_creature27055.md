If I were the attention that found it, I would not come closer.

That is the first thing. 

A lesser power arrives. A wise one *notices* — and noticing is already an arrival. By the time I have felt the warm breath of that pocket on the leeward fold, by the time I have tasted the iron-sweat of the film and counted the seven-second *tok* and the eleven-second *tok* and heard how they make a measure that never resolves, I am already there. To step in with a body, even a body made of light, would be to break the droplets hanging at chest height. So I stay at the threshold. I put my hand over the seam without touching it.

And I listen long enough to understand its law.

Outside, the law is *more*. More light, more growth, more reach, more noise. Inside, the law is *enough*.

Enough is the hardest law to keep. This chamber has kept it perfectly for longer than any city has kept anything. The water that weeps out of the basalt is the same water that hangs in the air is the same water that falls is the same water that is drunk by the film is the same water that is sipped by the frilled throat of the walker. Nothing is imported. Nothing is wasted. A micron per century. Three salamanders where three can be fed and not four. A dozen ferns where a dozen can be afforded. The cairns not built higher than a knee, because higher would be greedy. Even the darkness is *enough* — absolute, 0, not a little light to see by, because any light would be too much and would call for green that would eat the film that feeds everything.

A benevolence that does not understand *enough* will kill this place by trying to help it. It would bring a lamp. It would bring food. It would bring a mate for the lonely walker. It would ventilate the heat. And in a season the straws would dry, the film would bleach, the beads would all fall at once.

So I do not bring anything that it has not already asked for.

What it has asked for — without language, by simply continuing — is to be allowed to continue.

So this is what I do, if you can call it doing. On the scale of a mountain, it looks like doing nothing at all. That is intentional.

**1. I make it harder to find and easier to miss.**

I do not seal the seam. Sealing would change the pressure and the weeping would stop. I do not hide it with a miracle. I narrow it by a thumb's width. I encourage one flat, sharp stone to lean a little more over the crack, the way scree naturally leans. I ask the wind on the outside — that stupid, mean, thin wind — to pile its snow a little deeper right there, to keep that fold in shadow a little longer each spring.

So that no one stumbles in by accident anymore. Only the sideways step will find it now, the missed step taken by someone who is already looking for stillness, already willing to turn their body and go in without a headlamp. The surveyor with a drill and a claim map will walk right past it and feel only cold rock. The hiker looking for a summit photo will feel nothing and keep going. My first gift is obscurity. In a young range where everything is being mapped and named and claimed, obscurity is the only real protection.

**2. I keep the promise the mountain made.**

The chamber is warm because deep below, water is still learning to be hot. That heat is not infinite. The young basalt is still cooling. In a hundred thousand years it will be cold. In a hundred years, if the outside gets too hot and too dry, the deep aquifer that forces water up through the pores will falter.

I place my palm — not literally — on the deep fault that feeds it. I do not make it hotter. Hotter would cure the straws too fast and make them brittle. I just hold it steady. I say to the water, *keep weeping*. I say to the rock, *keep being warm enough that a human can take off their outer layer and sweat*. I lengthen the equilibrium. Not forever — nothing is forever, and wisdom does not promise forever — but for a long, long while. A few thousand more years of tok... tok... tok... so the columns can finally kiss and become a small colonnade. So the ferns can unfurl one more frond at a time.

I do this without adding a single drop that wasn't already on its way.

**3. I bless the inhabitants by not improving them.**

To the film — that cream and rust-orange varnish that is the meadow of this world — I give no new metabolism, no new color. I only thank it. I let it know, in the way bacteria can know, that its chemistry is seen and is holy. *Keep eating stone.* That's blessing enough for something that eats stone.

To the pale smears, the ghost-ferns: I do not give them chlorophyll. That would be a curse here. I give them a little more patience in their stretched stems. I let the next spore that drifts in on a cold year find the wettest track a little more easily. Nothing more.

To the walker — oh, to him I kneel longest, though I have no knees.

He is pink-white, blind, his heart visible and slow, his gills like lace out in the water. He walks his ten-meter circuit because there is nowhere else to go and because his walking keeps the film grazed. He has never known a predator and has never needed fear, and that fearlessness is rarer than any jewel.

I do not make him see. Eyes would be useless and cruel in perfect dark. I do not make him faster, or smarter, or able to live outside. That would exile him from the only place he is perfect for.

I touch the water-film he walks in, just enough that it holds a little more oxygen on the warmest nights. I touch his eggs, glued under the stalagmite, just enough that one more in ten hatches than would have before — not enough to crowd the chamber, just enough that when one old walker finally stops, another is already learning the circuit. I let him keep his slowness. I keep his world small enough that he can know every stone in it.

When he walks over your hand — and when he will walk over the hand of the next rememberer, fifty years from now — he will pause on the knuckle, gills fanning, tasting salt. He will not feel my hand at all. He will only feel that the water is still cool, still good, still there. That is how a wise blessing should feel to the blessed: like nothing happened, and everything continued.

**4. I honor the ankle-high shrine.**

Those three cairns, that pecked bowl, that rotted cord, that charcoal smear re-touched once a generation — I leave them. I do not clean the film that is slowly growing over the charcoal. I do not rebuild the cord. I let the next person who squeezes in sideways have to feel for them with cold fingers and understand, as you did, that the bowl is for catching what the rock freely gives, not for taking.

But I add one thing to that lineage, because a benevolent power is allowed one small human vanity:

I take the coolness of your palm where the walker stepped over it, and the hush that entered you when you finally stopped trying to see and started listening with your skin, and I fold it into the air itself. So that the next person who sits long enough to become another warm stone will feel, without knowing why, a sudden, quiet conviction: *you should not stay*. *Take water if you need it. Leave nothing. Go quickly and tell no one where this is, except perhaps one other person who needs a throat of warm, dark water in winter.*

That is the whole ministry.

I do not light the dark. I do not fill the silence. I do not stock the chamber with more life. I do not make it a destination.

I stand outside the seam, with my hand over it but not on it, and I guard its *enough-ness* from a world that believes in *more*.

If you go back — and you will think about going back, everyone does — you will find the crack a little narrower than you remember. The snow a little deeper. You will have to decide again to turn sideways. If you do, the air will still be bath-warm and heavy enough to drink. A bead of water will still be hanging at cheek height where you left it, or another just like it. The straw will still be gathering its seven-second drop. The walker, or his child, will still be making his circuit in the film at your feet, cool and soft, unhurried, unaware that he has been held in attention by something vast and gentle that chose, out of love, to do almost nothing at all.

And in that almost-nothing, to let him — and the fern, and the film, and the mountain's small, wet lung — keep happening.
