It would not come down.

That's the first thing.

A lesser power would have. A lesser power would have torn the weep-cloud open like a curtain, put a sunbeam on the tallest pillar, spoken in a voice that used the thin air efficiently. Would have landed.

The wise one doesn't land. It has watched enough continents be born to know that landing is a kind of claim. And this place has not been claimed - not even by the people who live here. That is its holiness.

So it arrives the way weather arrives on the plateau. High, and all at once, and without a center.

For a long while it just watches. Which is not nothing. To be watched by something that knows how to watch is already a kind of action.

It watches the filaments hum. It watches water bead down fresh basalt and learn, inch by inch, how to be a stream. It watches a line of shag-plates a kilometer long move like a dotted line. It watches a child waste air asking a loud question and an adult answer without shushing her. It watches an old one walk out past the warm cylinder and sit by a seep until the pikas come close.

It understands, very quickly, what it is looking at.

Most young worlds are frantic. Most young peoples are frantic with them - either trying to finish the world too fast, paving over the unfinished basins before the water knows where to go, or trying to freeze it in place, terrified of its youth.

These ones have learned the one art that is almost impossible for intelligence to learn:

*Be unfinished well.*

They scaffold without smothering. They warm without burning. They hold.

A benevolent and wise power recognizes that when it sees it the way you recognize a clear note in thin air. It knows this is rare. It knows not to touch it with clumsy hands.

So it asks.

Not out loud. Not in words that would cost air. It asks the way the plateau asks - by making a space where an answer can be given if it wants to be.

It loosens its attention just enough that the people, in their quiet, ground-conducted way of hearing, can feel they are being noticed. Not stared at. Noticed. The way you feel a glider turn its head to watch your needle pass without alarm.

Some of them look up. Not all. Most are knee-deep in marsh-mirror, moving a single stone that is diverting a seep the wrong way. They don't stop.

An elder on the lowest terrace, where the doors open directly onto the sponge-ground, feels it first. She is shelling blackroot seeds for the herbaria. She pauses, her amber lids sliding sideways against a sudden clarity in the light, and she listens.

*May I?* the space says.

Not *may I help you* or *may I save you*. Just - *may I?*

And because they are a people who midwife, who know that help without permission is just another kind of violence, she understands the grammar.

She breathes out, long and slow, on one breath the way children are taught:

*"We are holding this. If you can hold with us without holding it for us, then yes."*

That is all the permission a wise power needs.

It does three small things. You would not call them miracles if you were standing there. You would call them weather, or luck, or good engineering. That is on purpose.

**1. It lengthens the time.**

This is the greatest gift it can give and the one only a very old power knows to give. It does not give them new machines or new knowledge or longer lives. It gives the plateau *duration without interruption.*

Far below, beyond the young rock, in the lowlands where air is thick and noisy, there are other peoples beginning to look up with hunger. Peoples who see a high, empty table of water and geothermal heat and think *resource* and *territory* and *strategic advantage.* They have already drawn maps with this plateau colored in as theirs, though they have never breathed its surgical sunlight.

The wise power does not destroy them. It does not punish them. It simply makes the plateau - for a few centuries - profoundly uninteresting to the kind of mind that would take it. A little extra turbulence in the condensing sky over the continental flanks, a little extra static in the survey satellites, a little bureaucratic forgetting. The reports come back: *unprofitable, inaccessible, low yield.* The maps stay blank a little longer.

Not forever. The people here will need to meet the lowlands eventually, and they will, when the rivers have learned their courses. But not while the rock is still settling. Not while the water is still deciding.

It gives them the one thing scaffolding needs to be removed cleanly: *enough time to be no longer needed.*

**2. It answers the scaffolding.**

The gravity lattices deep below the fault blocks - the anchors the city sank to pin the fresh seams - they are working very hard. They will hold for a thousand years, maybe two, and then they will fail, all at once, the way young things fail when they are asked to be permanent.

The power goes down there, where no one can see, into hot, young rock that still remembers being squeezed up, and it does not replace the lattices. It *listens* to them.

And it teaches them a different way to hold. Not tighter. Looser. It retunes the field so that instead of *pinning* the blocks, it lets them settle - millimeter by millimeter, century by century - the way they were always going to settle, but without the catastrophic slip. It turns stasis into a slow dance. The lattices will still be gone in ten thousand years, worn away as intended, but now they will dissolve *with* the land, not against it. The wall-that-teaches-water-a-meander will hold just long enough for the water to remember.

No one will notice. The engineers tending the 40th terrace will simply find, over the next generations, that the calibration drifts less than it should. They will log it as *young rock stabilizing faster than model.* They will be pleased in their quiet way and adjust their herbaria.

**3. It blesses the unfinished.**

This is the smallest and most personal thing, and it does it not to the city but to the going-out.

On a bright weep-fog morning when the tops of the pillars are gone and the marsh is reflecting nothing, an old man walks out. As they do. Past the warm cylinder where the marsh never freezes, out onto the frost-gravel until the cold is clean again. He finds a lichen-black stone near a seep, sits, breathes steam that hangs a long time and then vanishes all at once.

The pikas do not come at first. The herd is far.

The power sits with him. Not as a figure. As a slight warming of the stone under his hands, as a slight thinning of the wind so his last breaths cost less. As a listening so complete it feels like being accompanied.

He does not live longer. The wise do not extend a life that has chosen its own edge. But his last hour is not lonely in the way even a well-held abandonment can be lonely. The seep beside him runs a little clearer. A glider, white and slate-gray, detaches from its wheel high above and lands for a minute on the warm stone - a thing gliders never do on the cold gravel. It looks at him with a dark eye, then lifts again.

When his breath stops, the lichen under his palm - that rust-red, olive, stubborn lichen that cements itself to weeping rock - flowers. Just once. A tiny, pale bloom no larger than a grain of sand, that no lichen on this plateau has ever made before, and will never make again in quite that way. It opens and closes in an hour.

No one will make a myth of it. The people who find him will think the wind brought a seed.

And that is how the power leaves - without having arrived.

If you came back a year later, you would find the plateau almost exactly as you left it. The filaments still hum in your teeth. The shag-plates still drift in dotted lines. The dwarf blackroot still spreads sideways. The children still squint and ask loud questions. The water still beads on fresh basalt.

But if you stood very still on the frost-gravel and listened with the kind of hearing they have - through the ground as much as the air - you would feel the difference.

The quiet is not empty anymore. It is *held*.

The continent is still being born. The city is still scaffolding its birth without claiming it. The people are still walking thinly and constantly, moving a single stone at a time.

Only now, underneath all of it, is a second scaffolding that asks nothing, takes nothing, warms nothing, except to make sure the first scaffolding will be allowed to finish and to fall away cleanly when it is no longer needed.

A benevolent and wise power, seeing a people who have learned how to love an unfinished world by not finishing it for it, does the only loving thing that is left to do:

It learns the same art from them, and practices it in turn.

It holds - lightly, peripherally, for as long as holding is kind - and then, when the rivers finally know where they want to go, it lets go.
