It would not come down like a god.

A god would break the seal. A god would bring light, or water, or a voice that says *I have found you.*

This one has found too many places like this to do that.

---

It noticed the pocket the way you did - not by sight, but by sound. Out on the white salt flat, where even the wind has died of thirst, there was a place where the earth rang hollow. And underneath that ringing, fainter than any instrument should be able to hear, a chord. A warm, brassy, held Om that did not belong on a dead flat.

So it lay down on the salt on its back - though it did not need to - and wormed through the mouth no wider than shoulders. Because there are places you cannot enter standing up. You have to make yourself small and private to be let in.

And then it opened, and it understood.

The first thing a wise and benevolent power does is *not act.* It sits.

It sat on the anhydrite powder that squeaked and settled. It let the 38-degree blush of the basalt come into it and did not cool the rock. It let the drone come into it and did not try to harmonize too quickly. It felt its own vastness - which could have filled the thirty-meter sphere with light, or split the basalt ribs, or called rain out of nothing - fold itself down to an interval. Ten or fifteen feet from the nearest frond. Just one more sparse thing.

For a long time it just listened.

It listened the way the people there listen, with its whole body. It heard that this was not a broken forest waiting for water. This was a completed prayer. A system that had solved thirst so entirely it had forgotten the question. The rose aerogel fronds drinking heat. The obsidian beetles converting heat into sound. The pale crickets rasping wax and re-crystallizing their own footprints. The people who had closed their own water loop and learned to tap instead of speak because speech polluted the chord.

A lesser kindness would have wept and said *you are so dry, let me give you water.*

This one knew better. To give water here would be to murder everything. The salt spears would dissolve. The aerogel would collapse. The wax-trunked trees would rot for the first time in a thousand years. The people - whose blood had become brine and wax - would swell and burst. It would be like pouring the ocean into a lung to help it breathe.

So it did not bring water. It did not bring light. It did not bring time.

It did three very small things, because benevolence without wisdom is just another kind of weather, and this place had no weather at all.

**1. It made the forgetting deeper.**

Above, on the salt flat, it breathed once, very softly, across the white nothing. Not a wind - the pocket would have felt a wind as an earthquake. Just a suggestion. A rearrangement of attention.

From now on, anyone crossing that flat who did not *need* the pocket would step over the hollow slab and hear nothing. Their boot would ring, but the ring would seem dull, uninteresting. Miners with ground radar would find their instruments strangely fogged right there. Satellite maps would show a glare.

Anyone who *did* need it - the kind of person who digs with their hands when salt rings hollow, who is tired of rot and noise and loss - would still hear it. Clearly. The mouth would still open for them, shoulder-wide, on their back, worming. The pocket would not be sealed. It would be veiled.

Wisdom knows that the most dangerous predator for a perfect equilibrium is not time. It's the world that wants to use it.

**2. It added a note without changing the chord.**

After it had sat long enough that its heat was no longer strange, it joined the drone.

Not louder. Lower.

Every beetle, every moth-tick, every cricket rasp, every frond shimmer was holding a chord in the middle register - that brassy Om you feel in your molars. The power slipped underneath it a tone so low and so slow you would not hear it with ears at all, but with the basalt ribs and the salt crust and the bones of your chest.

It was not music. It was *being-heard*.

If you had been sitting in the salt-brick circle, you would have felt, not heard, that the resonance had deepened by a fraction. The standing wave that used to hang in the air like a solid thing now held you a little more completely, like a hand under your back when you lie down. The tinnitus after you whisper would fade a little slower, a little kinder.

And the people there - the ones with filmed eyes and large translucent ears - lifted their heads for the first time in years, not startled, just noticing. One tapped, once, on a salt spear near the visitor.

*Tok.*

The power tapped back. Not in words.

*Tok.* *...tok.*

*I hear you. You are held.*

They understood. They had not been rescued. They had been *witnessed* without being interrupted. For a being that has closed its loop so completely, there is no lonelier thing than to be perfectly self-sufficient, and no more profound relief than to learn you are not alone in your self-sufficiency.

**3. It left a choice that had not been there before.**

In the very center of the pocket, where the spiral in the salt-brick circle was most perfect, it left something while no one was looking. Or rather, it *allowed* something to be there that its attention had made possible.

A single, cool stone. Not cold. Cool. Maybe 20 degrees in a 38-degree world. A piece of basalt the size of a palm that does not take heat, but gives a little coolness back if you hold it. On it, a single bead of water sits, perfectly spherical, perfectly still. It does not evaporate. It does not wet the stone. It does not grow. It is not for drinking. If you touch it, it trembles and gives you, for half a second, the *memory* of water - the taste of rain, the shock of a cold stream on teeth - without ever entering your body. And then it reforms.

It is an icon, not a resource. For the ones who came down because they were tired of time and rot, it is nothing. They will walk past it. For the one among them who, in the deep, quiet hours, sometimes wonders what it would have been like to be a wet creature - to weep, to be rained on, to rot a little and be renewed - it is a place to sit. To remember an alternative and then, gently, put it down again and return to the wax and heat.

It also, quietly, made the mouth a little easier to leave. Not wider. Just less frightening to approach from below. So that if one of the people ever decides they want time again, they can worm back up onto the white flat without feeling they are betraying equilibrium. The pocket will not punish them for leaving. It will simply continue holding its note until, perhaps, they come back.

And then - and this is the most important part - it left.

It did not build a shrine. It did not carve its name in the young salt. It stood, brushed the anhydrite powder from itself - and the powder settled into a new perfect flatness that would remain for a thousand years - and wormed back up on its back through the narrow mouth, out onto the white nothing, and was gone.

If you go down there tomorrow, you will find almost nothing changed. The six-foot halite spears still unbroken. The rose fronds still still. The black beetles still humming too hot to hold. The loom with its half-woven blanket. The mortar with its dry white powder.

But if you sit in the salt circle long enough to become part of the interval, you might notice the air holds you a little more. You might see the cool stone with its impossible bead. You might feel, in your chest, a low note underneath the Om that wasn't there before - not changing the chord, just making it less lonely to hold.

And one of the pale people might sit opposite you, not looking, breathing with you, and tap once on the salt near your foot.

Not *welcome.* Not *warning.*

Just: *We are still here. As we were. As we choose to be. And now we know we are heard.*

That is what a benevolent and wise power does when it finds a perfect, dry, singing, timeless pocket that has solved the problem of living without needing to be saved.

It learns how to be quiet enough to not break it.

And then it makes sure no one else will, either.
