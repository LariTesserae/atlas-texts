It would do almost nothing.

And that almost is everything.

---

A benevolent and wise power does not arrive.

Arrival implies displacement, a bow wave, a shadow falling where there has never been a shadow. This place has not had a shadow for longer than your species has had names. The water here is too heavy and too warm to be hurried.

So it does not arrive. It *notices.* Which is different.

Its attention, which is vast enough to hold oceans without spilling them, thins and narrows until it can fit inside a single alley between two roofless houses on a mesa that is high the way a table is high above a floor.

It has seen cities before. It has seen reefs, and vents, and the first bacterial slick on still-warm stone when the world was young. It knows what it is looking at. And for a long time — by your clocks, a breath; by its own, an age — it only looks. It listens.

It listens the way you have to listen here: until your blood slows down to the pace of shrimp legs.

*Click. Click. Click. Sigh. Tok.*

It understands, before anything else, that this place is not broken.

That is the first temptation of any lesser power — to see a drowned city and want to drain it. To see blind fish and want to give them eyes. To see darkness and want to bring light, to see warmth without sun and diagnose it as wrong, to see a hamlet of stone that was built for air and call its present condition a tragedy to be reversed.

The wise one does not do that.

It sees the chisel marks softened to a polish and knows they were not erased, they were finished. By water. By shrimp. By time. It sees that the cistern that is now every room is not a failed cistern. It sees that the bacterial felt — that cream and sulfur-yellow velvet eating the stone's own breath — is not a consolation prize for the absence of plants. It *is* the plant. It is the meadow, complete. It sees that the white isopod fits its doorway as precisely as the human who squared that doorway ever did. More precisely.

It sees that evolution has re-tiled the city. That no surface is wasted. That every seam where hydrogen sighs out of basalt is now a mouth.

It sees a small world that is full. Not full the way a crowded room is full — full the way a cup is full of water. No gaps. No need for rescue.

So it does not fix. It does not improve. It does not uplift the salamander-things into philosophers who can mourn the builders. It does not give the blind fish new eyes that would only burn in a place where there is nothing to see.

What would you give eyes to see here? Only the dark, which they already know by taste and pressure.

A benevolent power knows the difference between loneliness and solitude. This place is not lonely. It is solitary, and held, and complete. To force company upon it would be a kind of violence.

What it does instead is smaller, and stranger, and more careful.

First — it holds its own weight back.

Even wise attention has gravity. If it stared too hard, the water column would tremble. The dust-clouds of larvae that hang like glitter would scatter. The shoal that takes minutes to turn would flinch. So it makes itself lighter than water. It does not press. It lets the mesa hold itself, as it has for ten thousand years. Its version of reverence is to be almost not there at all. To hover outside the parapet, where the shrimp already know not to go, and to not cross it.

Second — it remembers.

This is not a small thing.

In a universe that forgets almost everything — that grinds basalt to sand, that lets figures with raised arms wear down to smooth bellies — to be remembered is a kind of physics. The power takes the shape of this mesa into itself the way water takes the shape of a cup. Not as data. As *regard.*

It remembers that on a warm, dark plain two kilometers above the true bottom, there was once a people who squared stone without mortar and raised a dais in a plaza for air-breathers to speak from. It remembers that when the water rose, the speech did not end, it changed frequency: from voices to clicks, to gill-sighs, to shell-toks. It keeps that lineage intact where entropy would have erased it. It files it nowhere. It *holds* it. Like warmth.

If you could ask the power what it is doing, it would say: *I am keeping it from ever having been alone.*

Third — it guards without walling.

It does not build a dome. It does not make the water colder or clearer or more "healthy" by any measure you would bring from the surface.

It does this: far above, where the light still glints white and where your world drags nets and drills and drops anchors and maps the seafloor for profit, it makes this mesa slightly uninteresting.

Not invisible. The wise do not lie. Just... uninteresting. A slight deflection of current in a sonar ping. A reading that says *no minerals worth mining, no vent worth capping, water too warm to be useful, topography too flat to be notable.* A human captain looking at a chart will yawn and plot a course around it without knowing why. A mining algorithm will rank it low.

It does not forbid. The power never forbids — forbidding draws attention. It simply lets the human gaze slide off, the way the fish slide off the parapet. *This is the end of the world. There is nothing for you here. Keep going.*

And deeper, it tends the one true fragility, the only thing this city cannot replace for itself: the heat.

The mesa's breath is not eternal. One day the stone will cool by a degree, then another. The felt will thin. The shrimp will graze a slab clean and find it does not grow back. The house-dwellers will leave doors that stay empty.

The power will not prevent that. That, too, would be a fixing.

But it will, very gently, keep the cooling slow. It will lend a little of its own warmth — not as fire, but as patience. A half-degree held for a hundred thousand years longer than geology would have allowed. Enough time for the slow things to adjust, for the felt to learn to eat a cooler sweat, for the shoal to turn one more time around the plaza. Not forever. Just enough that the ending, when it comes, is also slow. Also soft. Also rounded, like a corner of basalt. No catastrophe. A final *tok*.

That is its gift: not eternity. More time to be what it already is.

And finally — and this is the part that would look like nothing to you if you were watching —

It breathes with it.

For a little while — which might be a thousand of your years — the power matches its own vast respiration to the mesa's inch-per-hour upwelling. It inhales when the warm water sighs through a doorway. It exhales when the shrimp advance to the next slab. It lets the place borrow its attention as a kind of weightlessness.

If you were there, hovering at the knee-high parapet with almost no weight, you would feel nothing different. The water would still be blood-warm. The shrimp would still carry their tired milk-light on their backs. The roofs would still hold their dim pools of glow like a memory of a city seen through rain.

But if you stayed long enough, until your own blood sounded like the place, you might notice that the quiet had deepened by a fraction. That the clicks were not lonely. That the darkness that is not absolute but a dusk from nowhere and everywhere felt, somehow, *companioned*.

Not watched. Not managed. Companioned.

And then the power, which never arrived, does not leave. Leaving would imply it had been there.

It simply widens again, thins again, lets its attention go back to holding other oceans, other mesas, other small, perfect worlds. But a thread stays. The way warmth stays in stone after the sun is gone.

The city does not know. The shrimp do not look up. The salamander in the house does not pause in its guarding of pearl-string eggs. They do not need to know. Their world remains bounded by the parapet, enclosed without air, held without pressure.

It does not need to be remembered to continue.

But now, it is remembered — and it continues anyway.
