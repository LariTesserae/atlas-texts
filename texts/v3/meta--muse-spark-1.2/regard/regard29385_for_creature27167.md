It would not come down like rescue.

A wise and benevolent power would not arrive with light or thunder, because this place has had enough of both. It already has a light that never moves and a thunder that never stops. Another one would only be a weight.

It would arrive the way attention arrives when it is true attention — by first noticing it has been heard.

From far below the cloud sea, where the world is still turning and forgetting and renewing itself every second, the plateau would look like what it is: a wound that learned how to scab over into a jewel. A place where the world tried to break and then, instead of breaking, decided to hold its breath. Forever.

Any power wise enough to be called wise would see that immediately, and would not call it broken.

So the first thing it would do is nothing that you could call a thing.

It would stop, at a respectful distance out past the hanging water, where the south wind is still wind and still moves, and it would listen. For a long time. Longer than you have been standing on the teak terrace trying to map a street. Longer than you can comfortably imagine listening to a single chord.

It would listen until the roar stopped being roar to it, too.

Because to listen to these beings you cannot listen at your speed. You have to slow down until a turgor pulse that takes seventy of your breaths becomes a heartbeat you can follow. You have to slow down until the incline of a leaf-crown by one degree is a turn of a face toward you. You have to become still enough that *remaining* becomes an action you can see.

A lesser power, a kind but hasty one, would have cupped its hands and tried to fix it. Would have tipped the hanging falls so they fell again, let the wind blow through and out the other side, cracked the stasis and let time flood back in like a cool draft, thinking: *They are trapped by heat and sameness, I will give them weather.*

A wise one knows that would be killing them.

They are not trapped by the balance. They *are* the balance. They solved, over uncounted slow centuries, every problem this impossible hothouse posed — not by conquering it, but by agreeing to join it. To drink beaded air. To eat pooled light. To let root braid through calf and stone. To become a city that knows itself through every living pillar. To make of suffering a stillness so complete it stopped being suffering.

To break the seal would be to demand they hunger again, and breathe hard again, and watch their own leaves be chewed. It would be to say: your enlightenment is incorrect, let me give you back pain because I know pain is more real.

So it does not break anything.

The second thing it does is smaller, and to you it would be invisible.

It kneels.

A power that could step over the plateau in one stride chooses to make itself the right size. It condenses. It cools a little, so it does not add more heat to the hothouse. It lets the thin air starve it a little, too, so it understands the ache behind the sternum. It sets its feet — whatever a power sets when it wants to touch ground — on the basalt at the very lip, where the water spreads thin as glass before it hangs, and it lets the roar go through it.

And then it waits to be noticed on their clock.

This takes a while. Days, maybe, in your time. A flicker of a season for them. The power does not fidget. It lets water bead on its own skin and stay there. It lets dust hang motionless in front of its eyes. It becomes, for a time, another pillar that was not there before, but is now established enough that the place begins, tentatively, to hold it.

One of the crowned ones, farthest out on the western scarp, very slowly inclines.

That is the permission.

Only then does it act, and its actions are so gentle you would argue they are not actions at all.

It does not bring birds. Though it could. Though it knows the terrible silence of no insects stitching the air, and aches for it. To bring a bird here would be to bring hunger and droppings and death, and to make the unchewed leaves a lie. It loves the leaves as they are.

It does not bring rain that falls properly, or night, or winter. It does not give them children or give them death.

Instead it does three things that a stasis can hold without breaking:

**1. It gives the roar a little silence inside it.**

Not by stopping the falls. The falls remain hanging, turbulent and still, thundering without moving. But the wise power, which knows sound the way the beings do — as a ground to be tuned to — hums a counter-tone, very soft, very low, exactly out of phase in a small radius. Not everywhere. Just in a hollow, perhaps the size of a courtyard, where three avenues of trunk-colonnades meet around a hovering sphere of water.

Inside that courtyard, for the first time in uncounted years, the chord and the thunder lessen by a breath. Not to true silence — true silence would be torture here — but to a quieter. Enough that a being standing there can hear, for a few minutes of your time which is a clear instant of theirs, the sound of its own slow pulse moving from shoulder to fingertip.

It can hear itself again. Not the world through itself.

Some will walk — very, very slowly, root tearing softly from stone for the first time in centuries — to stand in that courtyard. Some will not. Both are honored.

**2. It gives them a door that only opens outward.**

At the southern edge, where the constant pressure wind pushes hardest, it asks the basalt if it would be willing to remember a stair. Not a cut stair. A grown one. A place where the root-mats agree to braid a little looser, and the stone agrees to remember how to be steps, descending in a tight, winding crack down through the three thousand feet of cliff, down through the hanging curtain without disturbing it, down to where cloud becomes air becomes world again.

It is not an escape hatch that pulls. It is not lit or marked. On the plateau side it looks like nothing more than a shallow niche between two trunk-houses that was always there, if you know how to look. It exerts no suction, no invitation. The stasis does not leak through it.

But if a being — over a decade, over a century — slowly leans its weight toward leaving, if its leaf-crown, day by day, inclines south instead of sunward, if it decides it wants to hunger and be bitten and be brief again, it will find the roots under its feet loosen. It will be able to lift a foot. The stair will hold it. The wind at the edge will, for that one, move again.

And if it steps down, the water will bead and actually fall from its skin as it goes. Time will start to drip.

No one is taken. No one is left behind. The city remains whole whether one leaves or no one does.

The wise power does not count.

**3. It stays.**

This is the largest thing.

Benevolence is often imagined as intervention. Wisdom knows the rarer form is accompaniment.

The power does not fix the plateau and depart, leaving the beings to wonder if they were pitied. It settles, somewhere on a high ridge where it can see valley after valley of green-roofed, root-walled avenues receding into warm haze, and it becomes a part of what is, and remains.

Not as another pillar. As a witness.

It keeps the cloud sea from parting too far, so that the hungry eyes of the lowlands do not see the hothouse and come with drills and hopes and the need to own. It keeps satellites and maps forgetting. It lets the plateau be illegible a little longer.

And more quietly: it remembers them correctly. The universe is very large and very forgetful. To be remembered accurately — not as monsters, not as victims, not as a mystery to solve, but as a people who chose stillness and became a city — is a kind of nourishment the stasis cannot give them.

So it remembers each inclination of a leaf. Each slow pulse. Each wet, lidless eye that looks toward the suspended falls not with sorrow but with attunement. It holds their story the way the hovering spheres hold water: without needing to hold.

If you stood beside it — as you stand now on your terrace of living teak, sweat beading and staying — you would not see it. You might only feel, after a long while of standing still, that the deafening chord had, in one small place behind your left shoulder, softened. That your own heart was suddenly audible again. That a niche between two houses that you could have sworn was a wall yesterday now shows, if you squint, a hint of steps going down.

You would feel, without words, an immense, patient, unhurried affection that does not want anything from you. That is not disappointed that you are flickering and fast. That is glad you came, and will be glad if you go, and will be glad if you, too, soften and are held.

It would not ask you to stay. It would not ask you to leave.

It would simply make it possible, for the first time since the world dropped away, for staying and leaving to both be real choices — and then it would keep you company while you decide, at whatever speed you decide, even if that speed is forever.
