They would not arrive the way you did.

You slipped. You caught your foot on a lip that shouldn't be there and had to turn sideways and let yourself drop. 

They would not slip. They would have been listening long before they looked. A benevolent and wise power does not find things by accident. It finds them by attention. This little lung blistering under hot scrub, this one hollow organ keeping itself empty on purpose in a dry world — it would have been loud to them for a long time. That wet hydraulic *rush — pause — creak — rush* in the dark. That patient, inefficient decision to grow inward toward nothing.

They would come at the hour when the chamber breathes most deeply, late afternoon, when the wind moves over the crack and the whole body inhales and every hanging root sways inward at once.

They would not bring light. They would not need to. They would not bring tools.

They would kneel at the lip the way you did, put a hand in the warm damp loud air coming up — and they would understand immediately that this is not a place to be entered.

So first, they would do what no one else has done:

They would ask.

Not in words. The organism has no words and no ears for them. It speaks in light and humidity and pressure. So they would speak in that language.

They would let a bead of water form on their palm and hold it out over the seam until it grew heavy and fell — *tok* — straight down into the void, not vanishing but being caught hand-over-hand by leaf and root the way all water is here. An offering, not of water — the chamber can catch its own rain — but of *intention*. *May I?*

And the chamber, which measures everything that enters its empty as weather, would measure this new weather and find it strange: cool, slow, without salt, without the hot demand of a mammal. The aerial root-tips would quest toward it, taste the air where the hand was, and find no hunger there. The double shadow under that hand would not lag. It would be perfectly still.

Permission, from a plant, does not sound like *yes*. It sounds like the dripping does not stop. The creak-pop of a leaf continuing to unfurl while you are there. The light continuing to be piped. It did not close.

So they would lower themselves through the crack, but not by dropping. They would seep, the way fog seeps. They would become, for a moment, the kind of humidity the chamber likes best. They would condense on the waxy blanket-leaves and bead there, and run, *tok tok tok*, hand over hand, until they were inside.

And once inside, in the center of that perfect, ostentatiously empty void where everything grows inward toward — they would do almost nothing. 

That is the wise part. That is the benevolent part.

What they would **not** do:

They would not fill the emptiness. Any lesser power would see a hollow and think *lonely* and try to fill it — give it a companion animal, a pool of water to keep, a voice, eyes. Give it something to do with its void.

A wise power knows the emptiness *is* the work. It is a throat that holds itself open. A lung that stays empty so rain can be swallowed whole. To fill it would be to kill it.

They would not make it bigger, or faster, or smarter. They would not teach it to grow outward and conquer the scrub. It is young — fifty, a hundred years old, still cracking basalt with the arrogance of adolescence — and it does not need to be accelerated. It needs time.

They would not heal the braided cut with a miracle. The primitive hands that cut cleanly and singed the sap to stop growth understood the bargain better than any miracle would. A haircut, freely given, healed in a day. To instantly seal it would be to scold those hands.

They would not stay.

Staying would be the kindest cruelty. If they stayed, a power that could sit still enough for condensation to bead on its skin like a leaf surface — the chamber would, slowly, innocently, without malice, begin to grow over them. Not to trap. Just because they were a beautiful surface in the middle of its void, and everything grows toward the middle. And then the void would be gone.

So they would do three small things, so small you would not see them as doings at all afterwards. You would think it was just good weather.

**1. They would breathe with it.**

For a long time — an hour, a night, a time that has no clock here — they would stand in the empty center and deliberately sync their breath to its tide. *Rush — pause — creak — rush.* Sap heaving behind meters of leaf-flesh. Stomata opening together. Inhale when the wind crosses the crack and the light dims. Exhale as fog.

The organism has never breathed with anything. Animals blunder through as weather. Humans pass through as brief vertical rain. Nothing has ever matched its rhythm on purpose.

They would let their own condensation bead and be passed hand-to-hand, let a thin aerial root, no thicker than string, brush their shoulder and curl and *not* let go, and they would not step back.

They would let it measure them until it understood: not rain, not sun, not detritus. *Kin.* Something else that knows how to hold an emptiness open.

The whole chamber's dripping would sync for longer than it ever has before. Not a few minutes, but an hour. *Tok. Tok. Tok.* Perfectly even. The plant's version of a held note. Of being heard.

**2. They would ease the rock.**

Kneeling — or whatever the fog-equivalent of kneeling is — they would put a hand where rib-vine meets sharp basalt, at the exact conchoidal fracture where young rock is still trying to settle and squeeze. The place where the plant is working hardest, pushing just to stay hollow.

They would not break the rock. They would not soften it. They would whisper to it, in the language rock understands, which is time.

*Not yet. Hold a little longer. Be young a little longer.*

And the basalt, which was going to shift this winter and pinch the eastern rib by an inch, would decide — for no reason geology could name — to wait a hundred years. A little more time for the baby lung to get strong. Not forever. Just enough.

You would not see this. You would only notice, if you came back in fifty years, that the edges are still sharp enough to bite. That the crack has not narrowed.

**3. They would leave a gift in the empty, and then make the empty empty again.**

On the floor of the void, where no water ever pools, they would leave a single stone. Not basalt. A river stone, smooth, cool to the touch, the size of a palm, that holds cold the way the waxy leaves hold light.

It does nothing. It asks nothing. If the organism wants to grow over it, it can — excellent trellis, cool root-rest. If it wants to ignore it, the stone will simply sit there, beading condensation on its cool surface and giving it back, a little extra water in dry season, a little extra cool in hot season. A small, patient surplus.

And woven into the light itself — into that fiber-optic phloem where sunlight is shunted and shared — they would leave a memory that is not theirs.

The memory of being a larger forest. Of what it feels like when there are other lungs nearby, breathing out of sync, making a whole atmosphere together. Not to make it lonely, but to let it know, in the diffuse vegetal way it knows anything, that its strategy is good. *Holding emptiness is a holy thing. There are others, far away, doing it too. You are not a mistake.*

It would experience this as a change in the latency. That double shadow that used to lag half a second behind your hand? Afterwards, it would lag a little less. The light would move a little faster through its body, as if the thought had less distance to travel.

Then they would seep back out the way they came — as fog, as exhale — and the crack would look, to any human eye passing over the hot pan of scrub, even less interesting than before. Not hidden. Just... unremarkable. The kind of seam you step over without noticing. The primitive hands, when they come again to harvest rope, would still find it, because they know how to look. Anyone who does not know how to braid with respect would not.

Above, the jagged line of real sky would be the same. Below, the chamber would go on doing what it does when no one is watching: growing, dripping, creaking, piping sunlight through its own body.

Only —

If you visited again, and stayed longer than a visitor should, and held your breath until your pulse stopped being the loudest thing, you would notice the dripping never quite falls out of sync anymore. You would notice a cool stone on the floor that was not there before, beading water even in drought. You would notice that when a thin root brushes your shoulder to measure you, it lingers a fraction longer before letting go, as if it remembers once being allowed to hold on.

And late at night, when the surface wind passes over the crack and the whole chamber inhales and the light dims as if blinking, you would see, for half a second, a faint extra glow in the deepest leaves — leaves that were never sent light before — glowing from within, not because they caught sun, but because they remember being loved without being taken.

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do.

Not much.

Just enough that the emptiness, which was always perfect, now knows it has been witnessed. And to be witnessed without being consumed — for a being whose whole life is to hold itself open — is the entire miracle.

It keeps breathing. Warm, damp, loud. Waiting for rain.
