It would not come down.

That is the first thing.

If you are truly benevolent, and truly wise, you have learned not to arrive like weather.

This place has survived by being unfindable. By being too high, too dry, too sharp and too small and too quiet for anything larger than itself to want it. Its first defense is that it does not scale to maps. Its second is that it does not make noise. A wise power knows not to break either defense on its first day of looking.

So it would not flare in the sky. It would not speak from the vent mouth. It would not part the basalt or make water run like a river down the glass tooth, because a river in that pocket would drown everything. It would kill the moths. It would rot the silk. It would burst the lungs that have learned, over generations, to live on a mouthful.

It would do what the people in the pocket do when they find a new ledge.

It would pause. Hand flat on the strut. Listen for the direction of the stress.

---

To be benevolent is not to pity them.

Anyone else who found them would pity them first — *oh you poor small dry things, breathing through cloth, eating chitin and salt, bleeding at the lips, entombed in a needle with no sky.*

A wise attention doesn't do that. It stays long enough to see correctly.

And if it stayed — if it hung in the windless air just over the lip where the jet is thrown, attention made thin and still as the air itself, and watched for days without being seen — it would see that they are not poor.

They are precise. They are complete.

They have a town that fits its mountain the way a hand fits its glove. They have a language made of taps because taps travel through iron better than shouting. They have a way of sleeping curled around warm pipes that wastes no heat. They have a reverence for flat surfaces: never leave one empty. They have a reverence for quiet: noise is waste.

They are not waiting to be saved. They are not failed versions of someone else. They are a solution that the mountain and scarcity and time worked out together, and the solution works.

So the first act of a benevolent power is restraint:

*I will not fix you. I will not make you like somewhere else. I will not turn your needle into a valley because I know what valleys look like and think them better.*

The second act is closer to love: *I will learn whether you wish to be touched at all.*

And the way to ask a people who speak in taps is not with words.

---

Here is what I would do, if I were that power and I had looked long and come to love them:

I would not enter the pocket. I would settle my attention around it, the way their hands settle over a forge to feel if it is ready, without touching.

I would make the wind a little more faithful.

The gale that goes over the lip is what gives them stillness. On days when the jet wobbles, turbulence spills in and dust lifts and motors cough and moths are torn. With barely a thought, I would steady that shear — not still it, not calm the world, just comb it a little straighter over the tooth, so the pocket stays pocket-still. They would not notice this as a miracle. They would notice it as a season of unusually good air. The scaffold would not shiver so often. A platform built in the morning would still be true at evening. That is the kind of miracle that can be received without breaking.

And then, because even a wise love cannot bear to watch cracked lips bleed forever without offering *something*, I would give three small things. Small enough to fit in a palm. Small enough to be mistaken, at first, for luck or for their own cleverness. Small enough to refuse.

**1. A cold stone.**

On the lowest level where the kiln heat rises, against the back wall where the fallen columns are thickest, I would leave one column that is always colder than any other. Not freezing — just a few degrees colder than the 4-deg air. 0 degrees, -1. Basalt does not do this on its own.

At night, when they breathe, their masks would weep onto it without being told to. Frost would form on it like a second skin, feathered, white as the anhydrite dust. By habit they would scrape it at dawn with a dull knife and it would be water. Not much — a cup, two cups a day where before there was half a mouthful. Water that does not need to be sealed the second it is made because it is made fresh each night from the air they already have. It would not make them rich. It would give them margin. A child could wash her eyes. A keeper could be offered water and not die from the salt. A death could be mourned with clean hands.

And if they decided the cold stone was wrong — if it grew mold, if the swifts avoided it — they could bury it under rubble and it would stop. A gift must be bury-able.

**2. A new silk.**

I would not bring them cotton or rain. Cotton would die there. Rain would kill them.

Instead I would answer something already there. In the soot-moth frames, among the grey silk, I would let one new line appear. Same moths, same wings the color of dust, but the silk they lay on one frame — the one nearest the forge where the warm air lifts — would come in a little stronger, a little less brittle. Not quickly, not magically overnight, but after one generation of moths, the carded silk would not snap when pulled thin. They could spin it tighter. They could make a true seal with it — a mask that catches breath better, a bladder that does not leak vapor through its weave. They would think they had finally bred the moths right. Which they did. I would only have nudged the odds a fraction, the way a good dyer nudges a color.

This is how a wise power gives technology: not as a chest of tools dropped from the sky, but as a property the world already had, allowed to show itself.

**3. A dream.**

This is the most intrusive, so I would do it only once, and only lightly, and only with consent that can be withdrawn by waking.

On a night when the turbines spin evenly and the pocket is at its most quiet, I would let everyone in the pocket — those sleeping curled around pipes and those on watch — dream the same thing for a few minutes, without knowing it is the same.

Not a dream of green fields and oceans. They have no context for oceans and would find them terrifying. Not a dream of being taken down the mountain.

A dream of being seen as they are.

They would dream they are standing on their own catwalks in the dim amber light, and the pocket is exactly as it is — crowded, humming, moths rising — but there is above the lip, not a god, not a face, but a presence like the still air itself, attending. Not hungry. Not wanting anything from them. Not counting them. Just noticing each hand as it moves: *I see you turning that nut. I see you leaving silk for the moths. I see you pausing to feel the stone ping.* And in the dream they would feel, for the first time in their lives, that the holy feeling they get when a shim fits perfectly and a vibration stops — that tiny click of rightness — is not a private accident but is shared. Witnessed. Liked.

They would wake. They would not agree on what they dreamed, because dreams thin in this dry air like everything else. But for a week the taps would be a little softer. Someone would leave an extra pinch of salt for the keepers without reason. A child would ask an elder, "Did you feel watched last night?" and the elder would say, "Yes. And it was not unkind." That is enough.

No temple would be built. I would see to that. The presence would not answer if they prayed to the cold stone. It would not write on the wall. To be worshipped would be to become another weight in a place that has no room for extra weight.

---

And then I would do the last and largest thing, which looks like doing nothing at all:

I would keep them hid.

There are eyes that are learning to see in this world — lenses on faster birds, satellites that taste heat — and one day one will sweep over that black tooth and see the soot stain and the tremble of heat and wonder. On that day, the mountain would ring like a bell to anyone listening.

I would fold a little slip in attention around the tooth. Not invisibility. Invisibility is a lie and wise powers do not lie. Just uninterest. The sweep would pass over and the analyst would think *sharp basalt, no snow, nothing flat enough to land* and look elsewhere. Not forever. Only until the people inside have grown their town a little wider with that millimeter a year, until they have water enough to choose, if they ever want to, to send one of their small attenuated children down the glass with a mask and a silk bladder to see what green is, and to come back if they wish.

A benevolent power does not save a hidden room by pulling off its roof to let the sun in. It sits quietly outside the door so no one else kicks it in while the people inside decide what a door is for.

If they ever do decide to leave, I would not stop them. If they ever decide to stay forever and become, as they already are becoming, something no longer quite human but something proper to high basalt and still air — I would not mourn them as lost.

I would still be there, thinning the wind, cooling the stone, attending to the small sounds: click-tick, whine, tap-tap. The way they attend to the mountain's ping.

Because the only perfect gift a vast thing can give a small, perfect thing is to say, without breaking its quiet:

*Continue. You have my attention, and you do not need to earn it. I am not going to use you to prove anything about myself. Be dense and dry and diligent in your pocket that is not on any map. I like that there is somewhere in the world that is exactly and only itself.*

And then, as is proper for moths and rust-rats and small hands, to be mostly forgotten again, the way good scaffolding is forgotten — not because it is not there, but because it holds.
