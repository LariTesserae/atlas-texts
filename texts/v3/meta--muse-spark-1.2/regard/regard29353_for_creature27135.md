It would not come down like a landing.

A benevolent and wise power does not arrive. It notices. That is the first difference.

It would have seen the high table for a long time before anything like attention could be called attention - before you made your two hour climb up the rain-dark stone. It would have felt the place the way you feel a sustained note in another room, held so long you stop hearing it as sound and start hearing it as pressure.

And when it looked more closely, it would understand what you were told on the way up without words:

*Nothing lives here* is not a tragedy here. It is a vocation.

So it would do what almost no visitor does. It would not try to fix it.

---

### What it would not do:

It would not bring soil. Not seed it. Not soften the light or warm the wind or lay a river where there should not be a river.

A lesser power - a kind, but not wise - would look at the weeping stone and the crowded pillars and the cold brightness and feel pity. It would try to make it *habitable.* It would rain real rain, plant the lichen that wanted to be there, send a hawk to circle. It would straighten the bent distance and correct the gravity where water crawls uphill and iron out the doubled echo, because those things look like errors.

The wise one knows the difference between suffering and difference.

This place is not suffering. It is not broken and waiting for life to complete it. It is complete in its own tense way - a steady state maintained for longer than any forest, weathered until even its impossibilities fit together like old teeth. To "correct" it would be to kill it. To plant it would be to bury it.

It would not build anything tall. Any power that understood the builders would know why their walls were three courses high and open to the sky.

It would not stay.

### What it would do:

It would come low.

If you were there that evening, you would not see it arrive, because there would be nothing to see. The light would simply, for once, not press quite so hard. The wind would still be sawing through the hollow pillars, but you would realize you could hear the water underneath it more clearly. The thinness in your lungs would ease a fraction, not because the air got thicker, but because the weight *holding* the plateau so high had been - gently - taken up from underneath, like a hand slipped under a long-held tray.

It would sit. That is the whole first act.

Somewhere among the dense crowd of stone, where the basins are finger-deep and the mineral skin quivers like jelly, where a low wall already sits letting water run through it, it would make itself low and small and porous. Not as metaphor. Really. It would take a shape that could let the weeping run through and around it, the way the old walls did. It would not dam, not divert, not demand silence for its listening.

And for a long time - a time that would feel to you like the hour when all the shadows stretch three times too long and don't quite darken where they cross - it would listen in the plateau's own language.

Not to words, because there are none. To drips. To hums. To the pressure of light pooling under water. To the late echo that comes back doubled from nowhere. It would listen to how hard the place is working to hold its bent rules together.

Because that is the only place where its benevolence is needed. Not to change the rules, but to ease the effort of holding them.

The plateau has been holding that slightly doubled sky, that hesitating gravity, that light-with-mass, by sheer maintenance for so long. You can feel it if you sit still - the faint strain, like a muscle held flexed for centuries. It is not pain. It is fatigue.

So the second thing it would do, so quietly you would think nothing had been done at all, is this:

**It would relieve the strain without removing the strangeness.**

In the two or three places where water hesitates and crawls uphill a hand's breadth before gravity remembers it - it would not *force* the water to flow down. It would give gravity a little more slack there, so the water could hesitate and then decide to flow down on its own, without being wrestled.

Where light pools in the basins like a heavier liquid, it would not take the pooling away. It would make the light a little less heavy to carry, so the stone didn't have to be quite so pressed.

Where distance flexes and a pillar fifty meters away arrives too soon, where the eastern scarp shows you the lowlands both below *and* faintly above you, where a clap comes back too late and doubled - it would not collapse the doubling into one. It would tune it, so the two echoes are no longer out of phase. So the sound, when it returns, returns as a chord instead of a stutter. The plateau would still be double, but double like a harmony is double - not like an error.

If you were there, you would feel it only as a sudden, inexplicable ease in your chest. As if you had been bracing without knowing you were bracing, and could stop.

The third thing it would do is for the builders, and for you.

It would kneel - again, low - at the cairn with the chipped disc of black obsidian, and at the fire ring darkened by fires a thousand years gone, and at the scatter of debitage whose edges have gone dull. It would not rebuild them. It would not make them new. It would do what the mineral skin does, but with memory.

It would set its hand - whatever a power like that uses for a hand - on each low wall and let the precipitated crust there grow *around* the memory of the hand, not over it. So the wall remains a wall, but now it will be remembered correctly. Anyone who comes up the switchback after will see it and understand, without needing to be told, *this was made to be small on purpose, this was love made low to not interrupt.*

And it would veil the way up.

Not hide. Veil. A wise benevolence does not hoard beauty, but it protects integrity.

For the climber who comes with hunger - to quarry, to claim, to explain away, to make the plateau useful - the switchback would be subtly longer. The thinness would feel a little thinner. The wind a little louder. They would turn back, telling themselves *there's nothing up there.* And they would be right.

For the climber who comes as the builders did - to mark, to listen, to leave something small and go home - the path would remain exactly as it is: two hours, rain-dark stone, breath coming short. And the plateau at the top would still be loud and cold and bright and impossible, but it would welcome them by letting them be small without making them feel judged for it.

The last thing it would do is the smallest, and the reason it is benevolent.

Late in that first eased evening, after you had gone back down because no one can sleep long where the air is so thin, it would give the plateau one hour that the plateau has never given itself.

It would let the wind stop.

Not forever. Just one hour, after the sun has gone and the hard white sky in each basin finally goes dark. For one hour the hollow pillars would not moan, the fins would not vibrate, the basins would not hum. The water would still weep - it must - but you would hear it truly for the first time: a thousand separate drips, not drowned.

And in that hour it would let the light lift. The pressed, pooling brightness would rise like mist off the basins and thin, and for the first time in its long, bright life the plateau would see stars - not as a glare, but as points. Reflected once, cleanly, in every finger-deep pool.

It would not be a gift *to* the plateau, because the plateau did not ask. It would be a gift *with* it. A shared hour of stillness, so the place that has maintained so much without rest knows what rest feels like and knows, afterwards, that it was witnessed at rest and found good.

Then the wind would return, because the wind lives there, and the light would press again, but more lightly, and the doubled horizon would hang again, but in tune.

The power would leave no monument. If you came up the next day you would find nothing new to point to, except perhaps that the disc of obsidian on the cairn catches the sun a little more cleanly, and that in one basin, the mineral skin - rust-orange and bone-white - has dried in a pattern that looks, almost, like a handprint open to the sky, letting water run through it.

And you would understand, standing there with the too-white light on your face and the cold on your neck and the chorus of drips rising around you, that you were being allowed to be a visitor again. Not a savior, not a settler, not a solver.

Just low and small enough not to interrupt - and now, for a little while, less tired for it.

The plateau would continue. Weeping and humming and pooling light. Maintaining its crowded absence, its steady state that should not be steady.

Only now it would be held, as well as holding.
