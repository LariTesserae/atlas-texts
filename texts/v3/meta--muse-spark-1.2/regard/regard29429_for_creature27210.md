It would not come down like a god.

Not with light. Not with a voice. Not with a tower or a terraforming engine or a promise to make it *better*.

A benevolent and wise power would know on sight that nothing here is broken.

This is not a ruined world. It is a resolved one. It has done the work. It has worn away everything that needed to be worn away and negotiated a truce between rock and water and air until they became one circulation. To arrive here with a plan to fix it — to drain it, to raise mountains, to give it a proper horizon, to stock it with more life — would be like walking into a very old person's house and rearranging the furniture because you think they must be lonely.

So the first thing it would do is nothing.

It would stop at the edge of that low, bruised continent of cloud and just attend. For a long time. Longer than you have stood here watching water run uphill. Long enough to learn the tempo.

It would not hover above. Height would be an insult here. It would lower itself until it was flat. Until it was ankle deep.

It would sit.

Imagine a power vast enough to lift a sea and deciding, instead, to sit on a warm, sweating dome of living stone that is barely a meter high, and let its feet get wet.

And it would listen to the thrumming.

### What It Understands

Within an hour of listening, it would understand three things that take visitors a lifetime, if they ever do:

**1. The turbulence is not a wound. It is the breathing.**

The mud that boils up and becomes an island and then is unmade. The film that blooms wine-red and then tears itself apart. The water that reverses, the reflection that lies, the rain that falls from two meters up without ever having been rain, the sound that arrives before its cause.

A lesser power would try to calm it. *Make the water behave. Make north stay north.*

The wise one would hear it correctly: perfusion. A membrane the size of a continent learning how to stay thin without tearing. Reality has thinned here the way skin thins over an old scar — not because it has failed, but because it has stretched as far as it can to hold everything together. It wrinkles. It shivers. It is not malfunctioning. It is alive enough to tremble.

You do not put a splint on a lung to stop it from moving.

**2. The emptiness is not emptiness. It is a rationing, and a reverence.**

One wader per square kilometer. One glider per endless sky. That sparseness would look like poverty to anyone who measures wealth by abundance. The power would know better. This place budgets life the way a desert hermit budgets water, not because it is stingy but because it is precise. It can only spare so much meat while doing the constant, quiet work of turning stone into sweat and sweat into cloud.

To make it teem would be to drown it.

**3. The straight lines are prayers, and they are enough.**

The causeway that runs true for a kilometer and then dives under the sheet and stops. The ring that holds water perfectly level. The standing stone with its corners worn to a memory.

Whoever made them was not trying to build a city. They were trying to keep one thought true for more than an hour in a place where everything else shivers and reverses and forgets. *Let this stay still. Let this be level. Let this line mean still.*

The power would recognize its kin in that. A wise power also knows what it is to make small, level places in a cosmology that won't stay still.

So it would not answer those prayers with a city either.

### What It Would Do

It would do almost nothing. And the almost is everything.

**It would become a horizon without becoming a mountain.**

The cruelty of this place is not that it is flat. It is that it has no way to measure distance. A reed clump is a hundred meters away until it is suddenly around you. A stone you left behind is ahead of you again. The whole plain gaslights anyone trying to walk it with love.

The power would not raise high ground. High ground would be a wound here. It would do something gentler and stranger:

It would lend itself.

For a little while — an hour, a season, a turning of the cloud — it would be a point of reference that does not misbehave. Not a peak. A presence at the edge of perception. A faint, warm luminosity just at the limit of sight in whatever direction you look, like a lantern so far away it should not be visible through haze but is. It would stay fixed while everything else folded and slipped. You could walk toward it and it would do the honest thing: get slowly, proportionally closer. The wader would see it and not need it. The Rememberer in lichen-draped shoulders would see it and finally have a true north that does not wander, just for a night.

Not a road. A courtesy. A way to prove to the plain that distance can be trusted, if only briefly.

And when it left, the plain would remember the geometry, the way a body remembers being held straight for a moment after the hands are gone.

**It would answer the water that runs uphill.**

It would kneel — that vast, benevolent attention kneeling in water an inch deep — and place a palm on the curve of a weeping dome.

It would not seal the pores. It would not stop the sweating. That sweat is the plain drinking and being drunk in turn. Instead it would regulate the little lie at the edge where physics gets tired. It would stroke that curve once, like you would smooth a wrinkle on a forehead, and for a long, quiet stretch the water would flow downhill again. Reflections would match their sources. Sound would arrive after its cause. The dome would darken from the bottom up at its normal pace, without shivering.

Not forever. Just long enough for the amphibious hauler to haul out and rest on a dome that is not slick with paradox, and blink its filmed eyes and feel, for the first time in its long, adhered life, that gravity is holding it properly.

A small, local honesty. A Sabbath for a square kilometer.

The wise power knows you do not need to fix the whole membrane to give the creature on it one good, solid afternoon.

**It would give the glider a place to land.**

That pale thing with paper wings that drinks lift from invisible columns of steam — it has not landed in its entire life because to land is to be held. The water would cling, the film would take it within an hour.

The power would exhale very carefully over one dome, just warm enough, just dry enough, to make a circle of stone perhaps three meters across that stays dry for a day. Not hot. Dry the way your palm is dry after you wipe it on your shirt. The cloud would part just there, not with a blaze but with a thinning, so that a single shaft of actual, point-source sun fell down for the first time in epoch.

The glider would find it. They always find the one anomalous thermal. It would circle down, uncertain, and find it could fold its wings and stand on stone that does not sweat for a few hours. It would tuck its head and sleep without having to soar through its sleep. It would not be saved. It would be rested.

In the morning the water would return, the cloud would close, the shaft would diffuse again. But the glider would remember that dryness the way the plain remembers a footprint that was slightly warmer than the water around it.

**And mostly, it would witness, and protect the witnessing.**

The greatest thing a benevolent and wise power can do for a place like this is to make sure it is not discovered by a lesser one.

It would draw a veil. Not a hiding — the plain is not ashamed to be seen. A dampening of attention. To any eye that looks down from orbit wanting a mine, a colony, a blank slate to write on: *nothing of interest, just a peneplain, a statistical flat, not worth the fuel.* To any mind that would hear the thrumming and call it a resource: *static.*

It would file the continent away under *resolved — leave alone.* It would keep the map slightly wrong, so that the causeway that runs true for a kilometer never appears on any survey.

And for itself, it would remember. Perfectly.

Because the true loneliness of this place is not that it has few tenants. It is that it erases every footprint within an hour. Every bloom, every mud island, every warm puddle where a foot was. It has no archive. It cannot keep its own history; it is too busy maintaining.

So the power would keep it for them.

It would remember the exact pattern of scales the wader made at 14:03 when the water reversed. The exact temperature of the hauler's back when algae started to grow there. The exact angle of the standing stone's forgotten corner under a stranger's palm. The exact moment the glider closed its paper wings on dry stone and slept.

It would not tell the plain it is doing this. The plain does not need to know it is being recorded. But somewhere, outside the haze, a mind that is kind and old enough to match it would hold the whole, shivering, hyperactive, patient, ancient flat in perfect detail, and think: *Yes. That happened. You happened. Even though the water smoothed you away, you happened.*

That is what a horizon really is, after all. Not high ground to see from. Someone far away who knows where you are.

---

If you stayed long after it left, you would not know it had ever been there. That is how you know it was wise.

There would be no monument. No crater where a foot fell. The stone would still be warm and porous, still weeping, still softly shining in the dim light. The water would still whisper over inch-high falls by the million. The cloud would still hang low and bruised and lit from within.

Only for a day afterward, if you were standing on that causeway while condensation dripped off you from two meters up, you would notice the line stayed true a little longer than any line should here. The reflection in the perfectly level ring would actually match the sky. And far off, impossibly far but steady, a faint light would hold its distance while you walked toward it, and get closer in the honest way.

You would get to that reed clump when you expected to, and not all at once after twenty minutes of not getting closer.

And when you lifted your foot, the water that filled your footprint would still be slightly warmer than the water around it — but it would stay warm just a second longer before it smoothed over. As if something had been sitting with you, palm on the dome, breathing with you, until your heat dissipated.

Then it would be gone. Properly gone, the way any respectful guest leaves a living house: without taking anything, without needing to be thanked, having left the floor dry enough for the host to stand on comfortably for a little while.

And underneath you the whole time, the old, resolved, hyperactive membrane would keep thrumming. Indifferent and intimate. Still the inside of a lung the size of a world.

Only now, for a little while, it would not be breathing alone.
