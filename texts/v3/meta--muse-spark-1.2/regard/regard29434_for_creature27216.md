It would not come down.

That is the first thing a benevolent and wise power understands about a place like this. Anything that *comes down* breaks it. A light in the sky, a voice from the cliffs, a footprint too large to be hidden by moss — all of that would be a kind of weather the bowl has never held before. And this bowl already holds its weather perfectly.

So it would not arrive.

It would *notice*.
And for a power of that kind, noticing is already an arrival.

Imagine it as something that has been watching the range for a very long time. Not a man in the sky. More like attention itself, with a memory. It knows every thumbprint-bowl and corrie and wind-scoured col, the way you know the freckles on your own hand. It knows which slopes slide and which hold, which tarns freeze to the bottom and which keep a dark eye open all winter. It has watched glaciers come and go and come again.

And then, on a circuit that takes centuries, it looks again at this one - this shallow, wet room cut high on the basalt flank - and finds it is not empty the way it left it.

There is green where there should be stone. There is a circling wind that should have dried it out, but hasn't. There is water being moved, a droplet at a time, uphill against common sense, by hands the color of saxifrage leaf.

It stops. Powers like that rarely stop.

And because it is wise, it does not immediately love them the way we love things - by wanting to pick them up.

It listens first.

For a season. Maybe for ten years. Which for it is no time at all. It thins itself until it is no more than an extra coolness in the already cool air. It becomes a pressure not unlike the circling wind, so the choughs don't cry alarm. It watches the interval.

One bee, then a long emptiness of air. One pika call, then a long emptiness of stone. One moss-being moving a saturated pinch of sphagnum from the weeping wall to a dry star-moss cushion two terraces down, then a long emptiness of moss where no one is.

It learns their economy. That everything is dew. That they do not take, they coax. That the shrine is not worship but housekeeping - a dry place to keep the things that should stay dry. That the little carved marmot with the smooth ears is the only thing they touch for no practical reason at all. It watches one of them at dusk press its wide, fringed mouth to the peat and sip, *sip... sip...*, and it understands: they are not waiting to become something else.

They are already complete. They are the bowl's way of keeping itself.

A lesser power would have plans. Would make them taller, or faster, or able to speak in sentences, or able to survive being taken down to the valleys and shown. Would give them a name and a story and a purpose beyond tending.

A wise and benevolent power would feel that impulse and set it aside, the way you set aside a boot before stepping onto deep moss.

So what would it *do*?

Almost nothing that you could photograph. Everything that matters.

**1. It would learn their language and answer correctly.**

Not in words. On an evening when the fox kits have come to drink and the light is hanging too long in the droplets, one of them - the smallest, with the rust-lichen shoulders - whistles: *here, water*.

A long pause. Forty meters of peat and air.

From the other terrace, an answer: *here, I hear*.

On this evening, there is a third answer. Not louder. Not closer. From the rim itself, from the leaning walls, a whistle on the same note but with a slightly longer decay, as if the stone had learned to whistle back.

*here, I hear you both.*

The two beings pause. They lift their dark, double-skinned eyes. They do not look frightened. They look - as much as a face that weeps can look - recognized. Then they go back to moving water. But for the rest of that evening their sipping is a fraction less solitary.

**2. It would hold the bowl.**

Not by force. By remembering.

The world below is getting warmer. You felt it on the switched-back scar on the way up - the stunted pines stunted a little less, the scree a little more bare. Up here, that means the air might not hit its dew point at noon anymore. The weeping walls might weep a little less. A millimeter less each year is a death sentence for a place that farms fog.

The power cannot cool the whole planet. That is not its work. But it can sit, vast and cool itself, on the lip where the ground falls away into white-blue gulf. It can breathe out - very slowly, over decades - air that is just a fraction cooler, just a fraction more saturated. You would not feel it if you visited. Your hands would still stay tucked in your sleeves. But the droplets would again hang that fraction too long before falling. The pools would again hold that second, calmer skin beneath the ripples.

It would keep the dew.

And in winter, when the snow seals the bowl like a lid and the beings sink into moss hollows between sleeping boulders, pulling peat over themselves like a quilt until a heartbeat is minutes apart - that is when most watchers look away. There's nothing to see. Just white.

It would not look away. It would stay. It would keep a low, white shroud of frozen mist just above their hollows, so the wind that scours the rest of the range does not scour them. It would count their slow breaths with them. *Tick. Patter. Sip.* The mountain holding its breath with them, and something larger holding the mountain.

**3. It would make the bowl harder to find.**

This is the kindest thing.

It would not build a wall. It would not strike dead the shepherd who remembers the sheepfold. It would just - gently - let the trail do what trails want to do when left alone. A little more scree across the switchbacks. A dwarf willow taking root right in the middle of the scar. A rockslide that is not quite dangerous, just inconvenient, exactly where a map would be folded.

And in the minds of those below, a softening. The story of "the wet bowl high on the east flank where the water hangs in the air" would become, over a generation, a story that people half-remember but don't quite believe. *Oh, that place? I think it's drier now. I think it caved in.* Only the ones who need it - the ones who can sit still against a sitting-polished stone for a whole afternoon without needing to be the observer - would still find the lip, and still find the room. And there would be fewer of those year by year, which is as it should be.

The power knows that being seen too much is how small, perfect things end.

**4. It would leave a gift that is not an improvement.**

One spring, after a winter that was long even for them, the smallest one comes out of its peat hollow and finds that the old dry channel - the one mossed over and perfectly smooth, where water used to run higher - is running again. Not much. Just a thread, a new braid no wider than a stride, beading along a seam that had been sealed for a long time.

At its head, half-sunk in turf like the glacier boulders, is a stone that was not there before. Not large. Not strange. Basalt and old slate, bearded already with lichen the color of rust and bone. But its north side weeps more generously than any other stone in the bowl. When the sun clears the eastern wall, the mist forms first above *that* stone.

The being touches it. The stone is cool - the cool that lives in stone. It is the right cool. The being looks up at the leaning walls, as if listening. The walls lean slightly inward, as if listening back.

No voice says *this is for you*. No sign is needed. The being fetches its flat slate shard and, very carefully, scrapes just enough lichen to keep the new channel open, not enough to scar.

That is all.

---

If you came back - years later, with your boots again never quite dry - you would find nothing to prove anything had happened.

The cairn would still be chest-high. Inside: the wooden spoon, the coil of wool gone a little greyer, the carved marmot with its ears a little smoother. Maybe one more flat stone on the cairn, placed precisely where the drip will keep its underside wet.

The terraces would still be quilted green and ochre and brown-black peat. The water would still braid and re-braid across the shelves and slip over the open lip in a thin, wind-shredded veil. The choughs would still ride the circling wind crying *kee-ah*.

And spaced at huge, respectful intervals - one bee, then a long emptiness of air - you would still, if you were very still and very close, see a patch of what you thought was dwarf willow mat move against the wind, not with it.

You would sit with your back against the sitting-polished stone and you would feel, peripherally, that the room is being held. Not owned. Not kept. *Held* - the way cupped hands hold water without squeezing.

You would have the feeling that the bowl, which leans inward as if listening, has finally been heard back. And that the answer was not a word, but a continuance.

That the snow will still seal it like a lid. That the weeping will still tick and patter even when it isn't raining. That the slow hands will still have mist to farm tomorrow, and tomorrow, and tomorrow.

For a benevolent and wise power, that is enough miracles for one small place perched so high the world below is weather.

It would do that. And then it would do it again.
