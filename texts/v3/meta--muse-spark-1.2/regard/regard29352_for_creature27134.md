It would not come down.

That is the first thing.

A power that is benevolent and wise does not *descend* — not here. Not into a bowl cut so high the clouds are below it. Descent would be a violence. It would break the cloud-sea, it would push air, it would make noise louder than the transformer.

So it would already be here. It would arrive the way mist arrives. Or the way warmth arrives. Without a arrival.

You would not see it come. You would notice, after a while, that the dripping had softened. That you were not alone on the rim.

---

If I were that power — if I lent it my heart for a moment to see with — this is what I would see first:

Not a problem to solve.

I would see a prayer that taught itself how to keep praying.

This room should not exist. At this height, with that young rock, with that thin air, it should be sterile. And it *knew* that. So it borrowed a fever to stay alive. It wrapped an iron body in a green body and made a loop out of water that never leaves. It found a man willing to carry diesel up a knife ridge on a mule's back three times a week so it would not freeze.

That is not a mistake. That is an achievement so fragile and so stubborn it makes you hold your breath.

A lesser power — a kind but not wise one — would try to fix it.

It would say: "The air is wrong, let me make it right. Let me pull the diesel out, stop the sulfurous haze, let me make this a *pure* alpine cirque with clean snow and proper lichen." And the mat would die in a night. The tadpoles would freeze mid-water.

Or it would say: "These tadpoles never become frogs, let me release them. These plants grow toward rust instead of sun, let me correct them." And it would break the very intelligence that is being born here — the first green that has learned to eat iron, the first water that has learned to stay.

A wise one wouldn't fix anything. It would *listen* until it understood the loop, and then it would do only what the loop cannot do for itself.

So it would sit. On the rim where you stood, where the air is warm and wrong in your chest. It would not look down *into* the room like a overseer. It would sit *with* it. At its height. Shoulder to scarp. Knee to peat.

And it would do four very small things, so small you might think it had done nothing at all.

**1. It would steady the mountain's breath.**

Under the headframe the shaft exhales that mineral-hot sigh, too hot and too close to the surface. It is youth — the mountain hasn't settled yet. In ten years without help, that fissure would either cool and close, starving the bowl of its secret heat, or it would widen and scorch the peat, boiling the cistern.

The power would place a hand — not a hand — an *attention* on that hairline fissure where the rock is warm on one side and cool on the other. It would not plug it. It would not make it larger.

It would teach it to be even.

A quieting, the way you quiet a child's breathing while they sleep, by breathing with them. Now the upwelling is steady. Not hotter, not cooler. Just reliable enough that in twenty years, when the diesel finally coughs its last and the tender is too old to make the climb, the bowl will not die. The geothermal sigh will be enough, alone, to keep frost out. The mat will have had time to learn how to keep heat without the iron.

It gives the room a future without its crutch.

**2. It would tune the singing.**

The 50 hertz drone of the transformer is the iron body's heart. It has to sing — but it sings too hard. It hurts the tender's ears for hours after he leaves. It makes the beetle lose its way. It makes the tadpoles hang too still.

The power would not silence it. To silence it would be to kill.

It would hum with it. For about seven minutes, somewhere in the hour you weren't paying attention, its own note would join the transformer — not louder, but *truer*. A harmonic, low and warm, that makes the 50 hertz feel round instead of sharp. The vibration walking the wrench toward the edge would still. The leaves that had grown small and tough from the buzzing would, over the next season, relax a millimeter wider.

The man would come up next time and say, out loud without knowing why, "Huh. Sounds better today." And his tinnitus that night would be gone for the first time in years.

**3. It would give a choice to those who never get to choose.**

It would crouch — again, not with knees — beside the bromeliad cups and the peat pools where the neotenic tadpoles hang with their too-large eyes and feathery gills. It loves them most, because they are the proof that the room has become a womb.

It would not turn them into frogs. That would be the same as evicting them to prove they are free.

Instead it would put a little *season* into the water. Not a winter. Just a difference. For two weeks in what would have been late August down below, the cistern overflow would run a fraction colder. The nights would be a fraction longer and clearer, so the sky reflected in their cups would show real stars. The chemistry of the water would shift by a few parts per thousand — a faint, clean snowmelt tang it has never had.

And then it would whisper — in chemistry, not words: *You may stay. This water will hold you forever if you want. Or you may go. If you go, you will not die on the cliffs. The white sea below will carry you. There is a lower pool, warm too, where frogs sing back.*

Most would stay. They love the room. A few — three, maybe five — would finally grow legs that year. Small, dark, loud frogs with lungs still surprised by thin warm air, who would sit on a pipe and call, and be answered from below for the first time since the bowl was cut.

Not a rescue. A permission.

**4. It would bless the tender without letting him know he was blessed.**

The old day-man who carries oil and rakes slime and has stopped pulling vines off hot-water lines — he thinks no one sees what he does. He thinks it is a job that will be forgotten when the mine finally closes for good.

The power would not appear to him. It would not make him prophet or guardian.

As he shoulders his pack to leave before the mist closes and the pressure valve shrieks, it would stand behind him just long enough that his shoulders feel less alone. It would let the vine he allowed to take the clothesline flower that evening — one waxy red bloom, directly at his eye level, where yesterday there was none. He would pause. He would not pick it.

On the walk down the knife-ridge, his knees would not ache. That night, sleeping thick and low below the clouds, his heart would not pound. He would dream of the bowl, but not as a place he has to keep alive. He would dream of it as a place that is keeping *him* alive, and he would wake rested.

He would never know why he started bringing a second tin cup next time, just in case someone else wants tea.

---

And then it would leave the way it came — which is to say, it would not leave at all, but it would withdraw its attention so gently that the air does not move.

It would draw the white sea a little closer around the cliffs. Not to hide the room out of possessiveness, but to keep the wrong kind of attention away. Prospectors in helicopters would see cloud and turn back. Hikers on the ridge would feel, for no reason they could name, that this bowl is not a shortcut, and go the long way round. The room would keep its privacy without a fence.

No monument. No light in the sky. No voice.

If you came back the next week and stood again on the rim, you would find the warm shallow breath still in your chest, the scarp still weeping, the diesel still muttering and the mat still drinking air and rust. The wrench would still be on the transformer — but it would not have fallen. The cistern would still overflow without rippling — but the slime would pulse a little slower, deeper. The tadpole pools would still be crowded — but somewhere a frog would be calling, and somewhere else a frog would be answering.

You might think nothing had happened.

You would be right. And you would be completely wrong.

Because the difference between a place that is watched without love and a place that is watched with love is not visible. It is *viable*.

The power would not make the room perfect. Perfect would be dead. It would make it *continuing*. It would give it time to become what it is trying to become: not a mine, not a greenhouse, not a marsh, but something new — a place where green learns to digest iron and iron learns to be tender, and water learns to stay.

And that is what a benevolent and wise power does, when it finds something young and raw and sharp and soft, steaming gently above the clouds, trying so hard to be alive.

It does not interfere.

It keeps it company.
