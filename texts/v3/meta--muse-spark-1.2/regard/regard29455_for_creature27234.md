If I were that power - benevolent, wise, and with hands large enough to hold a mountain shelf without crushing the moss - I would not come.

Not the way powers are usually imagined to come.

I would not part the cloud sea. I would not send a shaft of sun sideways through the hanging forest and make the people shade their huge, wet eyes. I would not speak. They have learned not to trust sound.

I would come the way one enters a sleeping child's room. By becoming quieter than the quiet already there.

First, I would listen. For a long time.

Long enough to learn that this is not a broken place that needs repair. Your ledge is not a tragedy, it is a solved poem. The people are not suffering waiting for rescue. They are *complete*. They have made an agreement with a place that should not be livable, and they are keeping it beautifully. Every empty halo of cropped moss, every bare foot placed toe-first, every lichen grafted to a cheek is a sentence in that agreement: *We will be dim so you can be dim. We will be spaced so you can hunt between us. We will take only what you exhale.*

A foolish power would see forty scattered houses and think *loneliness, I will bring them together.* A greedy power would see lichen that doubles overnight and think *harvest.* A loud, kind power would see children who do not speak and think *I will give them voices.*

A wise one learns their language before it decides to say anything at all.

So I would learn light.

I would crouch - if a thing that can hold mountains can be said to crouch - at the level of the moss. I would watch how the Frill-throats write to each other in amber flashes, how the Backward Deer blush along their flanks, how the Veil Moths leave those brief, glowing letters in damp air that say *stay away* or *come here*. I would watch the old woman with her hands on the pale bark of her hanging tree, stroking against the grain until the leaves brighten, not to see but to ask: *are you well? how is the water?*

And I would learn the people's light too. The slow pulse down a mother's forearm: *stay.* The quick double blink on the cheek: *water has come.* The palm-to-palm press of lovers that has no translation, because it is not a word at all.

Only when I could speak that silently would I do anything. And what I did would be so small they might think they did it themselves. That is the courtesy a wise benevolence affords. Not miracles. Adjustments.

This is what I would do:

**1. I would steady the mountain.**

That stone is young and angry. You said it yourself - black basalt and grey limestone in clean, vertical breaks, like a loaf torn open. It was pushed up a blink ago and it still remembers being pushed. It wants to settle.

Beneath the ledge, along the fault that made it, there is a tension humming through the rock like a held breath. One day, in a century or ten, it will shrug. The whole shelf could tip a hand's width and spill its moss and its sparse, careful houses into the cloud sea.

I would put my hand there. Not to freeze it - mountains must move - but to ease it. To let the pressure leak out as a thousand tiny tremors too small for even their sensitive feet to feel, instead of one great one. I would run a thumb along the fault the way you soothe a cramp. The cliff behind the ledge would weep a little more water for a season, and then be still. The people would notice only that the small cistern by the kiln, the one that always ran slow in late summer, now never quite empties.

They would think the mountain forgot to be angry. They would be right.

**2. I would veil them.**

The most dangerous thing about a perfect, quiet solution is that the noisy world eventually goes looking for quiet places to take.

Below the cloud sea there are valleys. In those valleys there are roads and fires and voices that carry. Sooner or later someone with a hammer will look up and see a green glow where no glow should be, or hear a story about a floating forest that lights itself.

I would not hide the ledge by making it invisible. I would hide it by making it *uninteresting* from a distance. To any eye not already attuned, the shelf would look like what it almost is: just another wet, bare fault in young stone, too damp and too dim to bother with. The inverted canopy's phosphorescence would, from below, read as ordinary wet rock catching cloud-light. The copper lanterns at their doors would look like mica. The paths of black stone would be mistaken for water runnels.

Only someone who already knows how to walk toe-first and listen through their feet, someone who is looking for a dim light on purpose and knows not to shout when they find it - only they would be able to find it at all.

A veil, not a wall. A courtesy to let them choose when they are found.

**3. I would leave a gift that obeys their four rules.**

Not fire. Not metal. Not words.

I would leave a seed.

In the highest, most sheltered curl of the overhanging cliff, where the oldest hanging tree has dropped a branch and the moss has made a soft cradle, I would set one new thing to grow.

It would look like the other hanging trees - pale bark, huge waxy leaves - but its leaves would hold their light a little longer. When the people stroked it, it would not just pulse and fade. It would *remember* light for a while.

Here is how they would find it: A child, sitting perfectly still by a pool as children there do, would notice that the beetle she was watching cast a faint reflection even after the canopy dimmed. She would look up and see a sapling no taller than her forearm, growing down from the rock, its three leaves lit like steady candles.

She would bring the old woman. The old woman would place her hands on its bark and feel - not in words, not in sound - a vibration that runs not down through the stone but *out*, in a slow ring, through the moss mat. A softer signal. When that tree glows, the carnivorous threads in the moss around it soften for an hour. The hunting stillness eases. The haloes of cropped moss that each household must tend so diligently would be easier to keep for that hour. Not unnecessary - the moss must still eat - but less hungry, less insistent.

It would be a nursery light. A night-light for houses that never had night and never wanted one. The people would understand what it is for without being told, because it follows the rules they already live by: *Light is for signaling. Empty space is a buffer.* This light says *rest here, the ground is gentle here, you can sleep without fear of the mat softening beneath you for a little while.*

They would not cut it. They would tend it the way they tend the others, with hands and stroking. In time it would grow down and join the canopy, a little brighter than its sisters, a small constellation in their ceiling of lamps that tells a new sentence: *you are safe enough to be tired.*

And that would be all.

I would not give them more sun. Their eyes have become beautiful to drink the dusk-light; to flood them with noon would be blinding, not blessing. I would not give them song. Their silence is not a lack, it is a culture more sophisticated than sound. I would not give them abundance. Their sparseness is their wisdom.

I would not stay where they could see me. Perhaps I would close myself and hang among the Watchers for a while - one more round, feathered orb with an eye that opens in unison at false-dusk, that counts them and loves them and does nothing more. They would feel, once, a Watcher open directly above them and not look away immediately, but hold their gaze a second longer than the others, warmly.

Then I would go, or rather, I would diffuse. Become the extra damp in the air, the extra patience in the stone.

If they ever told a story about what happened, it would not be a story about a power. It would be a story about a year when the cistern stayed full and a new tree grew where none had grown before, and the moss was kinder in the nights after the canopy dimmed. They would tell it in two strokes on the cheek and a palm pressed to another palm, glowing faintly.

And the ledge would keep breathing. Cool, inverted, luminous, and quiet - and still trying not to be noticed, now with a little help holding its breath.
