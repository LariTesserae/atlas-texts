It would not come down.

That is the first thing to understand about a benevolent and wise power. Anything else would have come down — bright, descending, casting a shadow where no shadow has ever properly fallen. Anything else would have parted the wind and quieted the roar to make room for its voice.

This one would not.

It would have noticed the plateau the way you notice a held breath in a noisy room. Not because it was loudest, but because it was most perfectly itself. So much of the world below is striving, spilling, becoming. This place had *become* and then stopped with such exactness that it became a kind of music. An equilibrium so complete it was almost a prayer. That is what draws a wise attention. Not distress. Not flaw. Completion.

So it would not arrive. It would *attend*.

For a long time — and time for such a power is not what it is for stone — it would do nothing at all but learn how to perceive the place on its own terms.

It would learn not to filter the light. To see with hooded eyes.
It would learn that the roar is not noise to be removed but the medium itself, like water to fish. To silence it would be to deaf and drown them.
It would learn that the scarcity is not punishment. A mile of nothing between boulders is not emptiness. It is the necessary distance for a tap on basalt to be heard half a mile away through the feet. To green it, to soften it, to make it fruitful by any lowland standard, would be to bury the thin, perfect language they have spent an age learning to speak.

And it would learn the being.

Not as a tragedy to be rescued. Not as a primitive to be uplifted. But as a master of this height.

It would watch one of them — chest broad as a barrel for the thin air, skin the color of lichen, moving in increments that conserve heat — come to a seep, press its slit nose to the black weep-line, drink without cupping water, slip a willow coin under the tongue for the road. It would watch it select a core, strike a flake with a sound too sharp to be lost in the wind, test the edge on its thumb, and leave the rest. No hoarding. No monument. It would understand: this is not poverty. This is agreement. They have taken only what the air can carry.

A lesser power would have felt pity and then done harm with it. Would have offered trees, and killed them with shade. Would have offered silence, and taken their speech. Would have offered abundance, and made them heavy and then unable to breathe.

The wise one would feel something closer to reverence. And then love, which is reverent and therefore restrained.

So what would it do?

Almost nothing that could be seen. Everything that matters.

**First, it would keep the veil.**

It would make the plateau a little harder to find from below. Not hidden — never hidden, because hiding is a kind of lie — but uninteresting. To the maps and the miners and the hungry nations below, the light up there would seem a little more terrible, the wind a little more unprofitable, the climb a degree more pointless than it already is. Let the satellites see static. Let the ambitious dream of other mountains. The greatest gift to a perfect equilibrium is to not be discovered by a disequilibrium.

**Second, it would not alter the terms, but it would ease the cruelties within them.**

It would not stop the wind. It would, once in a great while, when a lean marmot has pressed its belly to a stone for an hour and found no bead, breathe just enough extra cold onto the underside of that stone that one more drop condenses. Not a spring. Never a spring. Just one bead that would have been there on a kinder night.

It would not warm the shade-side. It would make sure the frost an inch down never quite reaches two.

It would not still the raptors. But when a pale condor-thing tips and falls, it would ensure the faltering in the braid below was not from a sickness that could hollow the whole sky.

It would not give them longer lives. To make them live longer here would be to make them watch more of their young taken on the wind. But it would make the dying — when it comes, as it must — a little less lonely. A little quicker. A little warmer. The way a good windbreak does.

These are not miracles. If you were standing there you would not see them. The tarns would still be half warm and half ice. The arches would still hum. The statistic of flakes to nests to wings would not change. That is the point. A wise benevolence works *within* the finished language of a place. It does not rewrite the grammar to suit its own accent.

**Third — and this is the heart of it — it would answer.**

Not with a voice. There is no room for a voice in that waterfall chord.

It would go — if a power can be said to go — to one of those impossible arches, the thin spans of basalt a few inches thick that have hummed forever and should have fallen yesterday, and it would do what the knappers do.

It would tap.

One clear tone, through stone, through the soles of the feet. Not loud enough to override the wind. Loud enough to be *of* the wind, the way their own tapping is.

And a few hollows away, behind a knee-high wall of frost-shattered granite, a being with a core of black glass in its hand would pause. Its hooded eyes would not widen — there is no widening against that light — but its head would tilt. Not to listen. To feel.

Another tap. In reply. From a hollow that was empty a moment before.

No words. No promise. No command.

Only: *You are heard.*

*The distance you need to be you is held.*

*The shape you have found and kept is good.*

And after a while, the being would strike its own flake again. A little brighter, perhaps. And leave it.

The power would not take it. Would not keep it as an offering. It would let the pika line its burrow with it, and the bird lift it and drop it glittering, and eventually the wind take its edge dull again. Because it does not collect. It circulates.

And then it would do the most difficult thing of all for a power that loves.

It would leave them as it found them.

Stone, seep, lichen, wind. The terrible total light. The deafening chord that is simply the condition of air itself. The thousand nests knotted in one hollow and nowhere else for a mile. The flakes catching sun.

It would not lift them down to an easier world. They do not need to be lifted. They are not fallen.

It would not teach them to build. Building would be their ending.

It would stay only as attention does — a weightless, unwavering regard — the way the sky stays, enameled and present, pressing down with brightness without ever having to descend.

If you returned a hundred years later, you would find nothing changed. That would be how you knew it had been there.
