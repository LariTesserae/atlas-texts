It would not come down like you did.

You came down with boots and breath and heat. You broke the peat and left a perfect print. You steamed.

It would come down the way the mist does.

---

A benevolent and wise power would not, at first, do anything that looks like doing anything.

It would notice this place not because it is loud — though it is loud — but because it is *holding* a note. Most worlds are noisy with trying. This one is noisy with succeeding. A continent holding its breath in perfect equilibrium is rarer than a supernova. It would hear that one chest-deep tone from very far away and know: something there has learned how not to fall.

So it would look. And in looking, it would understand immediately what you took a day to understand — that the city and the fen are not two things. That the aeration tower and the dome two kilometers away are one animal with a city-sized gap between its organs. That the moss is not growing on them, it *is* them, thinking slowly in green. That the water running uphill is not a mistake in gravity, it is a pulse.

And it would understand that it is young. All of it. The basalt that still remembers being liquid. The machines that have never needed repair. The domes that have never needed to learn anything else because the first thing they learned was perfect.

A lesser power would have called that perfection tragic. Would have said: *It is stuck. It does not change, it does not die, it does not evolve. I will give it seasons. I will give it birds. I will give it death and therefore meaning.*

A wise power knows that stasis is not always stagnation. Sometimes it is practice. Sometimes it is a held note before singing.

This lung is not broken. It is rehearsing how to breathe for a world that hasn't arrived yet.

So the wise power would do the first, hardest thing that wisdom requires:

It would not fix it.

---

It would arrive by becoming compatible.

You knelt and parted the moss and felt the cold bite your hands because you were too warm. The wise power would have no such problem. It would cool itself.

For a long time — a long time even for something that can wait — it would simply lie down.

Not on the peat. *As* the peat.

It would thin itself until it was low and flat and vast, until it had no front and no face, until it was thirty centimeters high at the crown and six meters across, until water beaded on its back. It would grow moss. Real moss, not a costume. It would become a dome among domes, in one of those empty kilometers where emptiness is the material, keeping the void density perfect.

And it would breathe with them.

Inhale — the factories hum.
Exhale — the domes condense.
Inhale — the mist gathers on every handrail and hair-filament.
Exhale — it ticks down, a second rain that never stops.

It would not try to speak at first. Speech is too fast here. To speak to something that pulses every twenty or thirty seconds, you must be willing to say one syllable per hour. The wise power is willing.

So it would hum. At first, exactly on their note. So perfectly that none of the domes would notice a new voice had joined. The lung would continue, level, loud, everywhere and nowhere.

And then, maybe after a year of your time, or ten — time is cheap to such a power — it would do the only intervention benevolence ever really is:

It would offer a *variation*.

Not a correction. An option.

One hum, not louder, not more insistent, just — slightly out of tune. A microtone flat. Held for three pulses longer than the others before resolving.

In human music it would be nothing. Here, where nothing has varied ever, it would be an earthquake made of gentleness.

Every dome within kilometers would feel it through the peat-water, through the filaments, through the uphill seep. The water between tower and dome would hesitate.

*Something else is breathing here, and it is breathing differently, and it did not have to.*

The wise power would not repeat it immediately. It would return to perfect unison. As if to say: *I can be like you, exactly. I am not asking you to change.*

Then, a little later — a season later — it would do it again. Or do something else small. Let a single needle fall from the stunted pine it was sheltering and *not* catch it. Let it land on the moss and stay. Let the filaments of the nearest true dome decide what to do with that novelty.

Or it would let one of its own moss-flowers go to seed a little early, and let the wind — which has always blown the same way — take one white wool tuft a little further than the others.

A gift, not an order. A question, not a command.

*You keep everything perfect. That is beautiful. Do you want to keep it perfect forever? You can. I will protect your right to. But if you ever want — just once — to let a print fill with water, to let the note waver, to let something be eaten and become something else... I have shown you it can be done and the world does not end.*

That is what wisdom does for a perfect child. It does not wake it to praise its sleeping. It sits beside the bed and breathes, so the child, if it wakes on its own, is not alone. And it leaves a door ajar, not to push the child through, but so a little draft can come in if the child ever wants to smell outside.

The second thing it would do — while it is lying there, humming — is the work you cannot see.

It would veil the plateau.

A place so young and so whole draws other kinds of attention. The hungry kind. The kind that sees empty kilometers between factories and thinks *land to claim*. That sees a lung the size of a continent and thinks *resource*. That would come with drills and boots and heat and footprints that *do* fill with water because they break the equilibrium.

The wise power would not build a wall. Walls are a human idea. It would instead make the place a little more itself. A little more high, a little more thin-aired, a little more saturated. It would fold the overcast a little thicker, deepen the cold by half a degree, lengthen the kilometers between districts by a thought. Anyone approaching with hunger would feel, long before arrival, a quiet disinterest — *nothing for me there, only wet rock and wind* — and would turn aside without knowing why. Only those who could already lie down and become moss would be able to find it again.

Protection without possession.

And then — and this is the heart of it — it would leave.

Not all at once. It would thin slowly, like mist lifting, over another long year. Its dome would grow paler, the moss on its back a little less dense, the condensation beading a little less quickly. The true domes would feel its note fade back into unison and then into silence, and the gap it leaves would be exactly dome-sized, exactly void-density correct, and after a while the peat would settle and there would be a shallow black pan there where its filaments had been, which would, in time, be exactly the right place for a new dome to grow if one ever wanted to.

It would not say goodbye. Goodbye is too warm and too fast.

On the last day, before it was quite gone, it would do one very small, very benevolent thing that only a wise power would think to do.

It would leave a single warm stone.

Not hot. Just one degree above zero to five. A smooth basalt cobble, set in the peat near that pan, where water would bead and run off it a little faster than elsewhere. Nothing would grow on it differently. No machine would register it. It would simply be there — a little kindness of thermodynamics — so that if, in a hundred or a thousand years, one of the domes ever wondered what variation felt like and wanted to try again, it would have something near at hand that already knew how to be slightly different, to learn from.

And then it would be gone, and the continent would be as it was: loud, wet, green, empty, perfect.

Except —

If you came back now, and you are very still, and you put your ear to the moss on the back of the nearest dome and hold your breath until your own steam stops,

you will hear that the hum is not quite as level as you remembered.

Once every few hours, there is a pause. Barely a pause. A third of a pulse. As if the whole lung is holding its breath, listening, remembering a slightly flat note that once breathed beside it and told it, without words:

*You are beautiful as you are. And if you ever want to be otherwise, you can. I will still find you beautiful.*

The footprint you left is still there, perfect, unfilled. The domes have kept it for you.

Not because they have to.

Because they chose to.
