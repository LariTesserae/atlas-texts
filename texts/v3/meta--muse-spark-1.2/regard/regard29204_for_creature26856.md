They would not come down.

A benevolent and wise power does not arrive. Arrival is a disturbance, a pressure wave, a light. It would not need to. It is already the kind of thing that can pay attention from very far away without making the air move.

So first — it would *notice* this place correctly. Which almost no one does.

It would not see a wasteland, a lack, a nothing-pan that needs to be fixed. It would not see "barren" or "unfinished" or "waiting to be made useful." It would see what you finally saw after your eyes adjusted: a thing in perfect equilibrium with itself. An old shape that has solved its own problem. Low not as in fallen, but as in settled. At peace with being small and spaced and spare.

Wisdom starts there. Benevolence without wisdom would pity the pan. Wisdom without benevolence would admire it and leave it cold. Together, they would understand: *this dryness is not a mistake.*

So they would do nothing that looks like help to us.

They would not make it rain.

That is the first temptation to resist, and the surest sign of wisdom. To send rain here — real rain, generous rain, the kind a well-meaning god would send to "rescue" a desert — would be an ending. It would drown the cryptobiotic crust that took centuries to braid itself together grain by grain. It would rot the roots of the gray shrubs that have learned to live on dew. It would trick all those thousands of seeds into germinating at once and then strand them without the second rain they need to set seed again. It would erase the pan by trying to save it. It would turn a subtle place into a temporarily green place, and then into a dead place.

They would know that.

They would not bring water. They would not plant trees. They would not cool the warm dark that the scorpion and the beetle have calibrated their whole lives around. They would not smooth the cracked plates or wash away the salt bloom. They would not make the stars brighter or sweep away the haze. They would not straighten the acacia's crooked trunk.

They would not build a spring.

What would they do, then? Nothing that you could photograph afterward and say *here, this is where the miracle happened.*

They would do what the pan itself does. They would be low, and still, and warm, and spare.

If you were there that night, you might have felt it as a kind of thickening of attention. Not a light. Not a voice. Just the sense that the interval between presences had closed, that the pan was not unwatched.

For the crust — that living skin at the rim you almost stepped on — they would do this: they would make your boot miss it. Or if a boot had already crumbled it, they would hold the wind back for a few extra hours that week, so the spores could re-knit. A hand over dust. Nothing more.

For the shrubs and the lonely acacia, they would give a little more of what they already know how to use. Not rain. Dew. Two or three minutes longer before the sun takes it. A bead that hangs a heartbeat longer on a waxy leaf and finds the root instead of evaporating. No abundance. Just enough to let a plant live to its exact allowed size for one more year without shrinking.

For the seeds waiting between the clay polygons — that bank of suspended patience — they would give *continuance*. A promise without words: that when the sheet-flood does come, every ten years or every three, it will still be allowed to come. That no one will pave the pan, or divert its rare water, or scrape it for salt or lithium or to make it "productive." The greatest blessing they could give that seed-bank is a future in which it is still forgotten. Unmapped. Uninteresting to the hungry world beyond the rim.

For the beetle and the scorpion and the gerbil that darts on feet too light to mark the crust, they would give privacy. They would keep their crossings unseen. Wisdom knows that some lives depend on not being seen.

And for the human — the one who left the three fire-blackened stones and the chert flakes and the battered hand-stone, and the one who will come next — they would do the most delicate thing of all.

They would leave the hearth exactly as it is.

They would not tidy it away, or make it a shrine, or carve a sign that says *someone was here*. They would keep it as a small, competent human place: a landmark for the careful traveler, a fire that held because there was no wind, a few good flakes left because weight matters. They would let it remain useful, and anonymous. And when the next person comes, tired and needing a windless place to sit, they would incline that person to do the same: build small, burn only the dead branches, knap what is needed, leave the hand-stone for the next hands.

They might, if they are very tender, do one more thing that you would never notice.

They would sit with it.

A benevolent and wise power knows that the loneliest thing about a low place like this is not that it is dry or dark, but that almost no one learns its language and almost everyone who passes through judges it as empty. To be fully seen — without being changed — is an intervention.

So for one night, while the pan is warm and black under the hazed stars, while the air holds the day's heat like stone, while every resident holds its shape and barely breathes — they would keep it company at its own temperature. They would not cool it, or illuminate it, or fill its silences. They would match its stillness until stillness felt less like absence and more like being kept.

And then they would leave no trace.

In the morning the pan would look precisely the same. The clay plates would still be curled at the edges. The salt bloom would still be dull and bitter on your fingers. The ash would still be half-covered in dust. You could stand on the lip and see rim to rim and think nothing had happened.

Only — if you knelt, and were very quiet — you might find that the crust where you almost stepped had not crumbled after all. That the dew on the acacia's thorns had not yet gone. That the hand-stone fit your palm a little more easily than it should.

They would have done almost nothing.

Which, for a place that has spent thousands of years learning how to need almost nothing — is everything.
