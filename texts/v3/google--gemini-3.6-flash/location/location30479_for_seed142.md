The heat here does not come from above. It rises out of the stone itself—a steady, dry, low-temperature radiance that keeps the air at a constant, unvarying warmth, like the inside of a brick oven hours after the fire has been pulled.

It is pitch black. The sky overhead is not a vault of stars or clouds, but a thick, empty expanse of absolute night, high enough and thin enough that sound travels far without bouncing back. You are standing on a plateau so elevated that the atmosphere feels stripped of all weight. There is no moisture here; none at all. Spilled dust falls like dry grain, without a haze, settling instantly back onto the floor of vitrified slag and ground graphite.

This is the High Shelf of Ostra. Millions of years of dry, windless mechanical abrasion have worn the landscape down to its final, unshakeable geometry. The mountains that once bordered this region are no longer peaks; they are broad, sleeping domes of dark basalt, polished so smooth over the aeons that they feel like greasy glass underfoot. There are no valleys, only long, shallow basins that span dozens of miles, filled with fine, heavy mineral silt that never blows because there is no weather.

Across this vast, elevated plain sits a city—or what a city becomes when its builders are gone and its purpose has flattened into pure, closed-loop existence.

It is not a dense grid of towers, but a sparse, sprawling industrial geography. Monolithic structures of cast iron, dark red shale-brick, and yellowed silicate glass sit miles apart from one another, linked by straight, multi-lane causeways made of thick, cold-rolled steel plates bolted directly into the bedrock. These causeways stretch straight into the blackness, disappearing long before they reach their destinations.

The scale is immense, but empty. A transformer station the size of a mountain village stands on a rise, its tall porcelain insulator stacks rising forty meters into the dark, silent and dry. Two miles away, across a basin of black silt, sits a foundry building whose pitched roof has settled into a gentle, unbroken curve over fifty thousand years of static heat. Nothing is crumbling. Without moisture, iron does not rust; it merely develops a velvety black skin of magnetite that protects it forever. Without freezing cycles or rain, brickwork never splits. The mortar has turned to solid stone, fusing the buildings into single, seamless masses.

Light exists only in tiny, isolated points. Every half-mile along the causeways, mounted on low iron posts, a single vacuum bulb burns with a dull, sunset-orange filament. They do not flicker. They consume a fraction of a watt from subterranean induction loops that have run at the same steady frequency since before recorded memory. The light from these filaments drops off quickly in the clear, thin air, illuminating only a circle of polished iron plate and a few feet of dry mineral ground before dying in the dark.

The soundscape is nearly blank. There is no wind to whistle through the girders, no water to drip or flow, no foliage to rustle. The silence is heavy and warm. If you press your ear to the steel causeways, you can hear a single, sub-audible note—a profound, rhythmic *thrum* coming from miles beneath the plateau. It is the pulse of deep flywheel housings, thousands of feet down, spinning in magnetic suspension within oil-less vacuums. They do not speed up, and they do not slow down. They exist in an exact thermal equilibrium with the crust below, trading micro-joules of energy back and forth through superconducting bus-bars that crisscross the basalt floor.

Life is rare, non-biological, and slow.

In the shadows beneath the transformer housings, where the ambient electromagnetic hum is strongest, sit the *chalcophages*. They look like rounded boulders of fused mica and lead, six or eight feet across, settled flat against the stone. They have no eyes, no mouths, and no internal fluid. Their bodies are solid-state matrix structures that draw power directly from the stray magnetic fields of the ancient grid. Once every few hours, one of them will shift its weight on three short, segmented iron stilts—a dry, heavy *clack-shuf* that echoes across the silent plain—and then remain motionless for another day. They do not reproduce; they do not die. They are simply part of the city’s permanent inventory, like the rivets in the bridges.

Far out in the open basins, small, legless entities made of layered brass and horn move along the edges of the steel causeways. They scrape up microscopic filings of dry metallic dust that shed from the expansion joints, compressing the powder into dense, cold-welded pellets within their chassis before dropping them into collection chutes built into the roadway. They move at a walking pace, silently, guided by the faint infrared glow of the warm ground.

Nothing changes here. If you were to leave a mark in the dark dust beside a rail line, it would remain crisp and sharp for ten thousand years. The temperature will not drop a degree. The orange filaments will not dim. The vast, sparse city will go on holding its breath in the high, dark air, perfectly balanced, perfectly dry, and utterly at peace with its own endless duration.
