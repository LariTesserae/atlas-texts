High on the dark shoulder of an unlit mountain, tucked into a deep, windless saddle of basalt, lies a thermal basin no larger than a ruined cathedral. 

No sunlight reaches this height—the sky above is an impenetrable, black vault of heavy mineral haze—yet the heat here is intense and oppressive, radiating upward from the cracked floor like the mouth of an open kiln. The air is bone-dry, free of moisture or vapor, smelling faintly of hot copper, baked dust, and the scorched, organic sweetness of dry molts. There is no water anywhere. No dew forms on the stone; no steam rises from the fractures. The heat is a pure, arid dryness that bakes the rock into brittle, matte-black plates.

The floor of the basin appears at first to be a rolling, shifting terrain of dark gravel, but it is entirely alive. 

There are no plants—no moss, no fungus, no root systems clinging to the stone. Instead, the basin is packed to its boundaries with a dense, writhing carpet of dry-skinned fauna. They fill the space so completely that not a single square inch of the underlying basalt is exposed. Layer upon layer of bodies press against one another in a continuous, claustrophobic sea of movement.

These creatures are dry, calcified things, built for heat and dark. Most are blind, possessing pit-organs along their flanks that register the radiant warmth of their neighbors. Some resemble flat, armorous plates with dozens of needle-thin, jointed legs; others are long, legless ribbons of leathery skin that weave endlessly through the gaps between heavier bodies. Smaller, bead-like organisms, encased in husks like baked clay, roll over the backs of the larger beasts, falling into crevices only to be pushed up again by the sheer density of life beneath. 

They do not require water. They exist in a closed, hyper-active metabolic loop, deriving energy directly from the extreme heat of the rock and consuming one another’s shed husks, dried excretions, and the quick, silent corpses of those crushed in the press.

Despite the frantic pace of the ecosystem—thousands of interactions occurring every second, predators sliding their mandibles into the dry joints of prey, bodies coiling and uncoiling in continuous motion—the basin is almost completely quiet. There are no cries, no calls, no clicks of communication. The sound of the place is a single, low, dry murmur: the papery rustle of millions of chitinous limbs dragging across baked scales, like dry oak leaves being blown across a patio by a soft, imperceptible draft.

Here and there, rising out of the squirming mass like forgotten reef-heads, are the partial remains of old, primitive masonry. They are not high-tech, nor are they grand; they are simple, heavy blocks of smoothed basalt, fitted together without mortar to form low walls or lintels. Their edges are smooth and rounded, worn down by centuries of dry friction from the endless tide of creatures climbing over them. The bodies flow up and over these stone fragments, pooling in the lee of ancient archways, using the elevated masonry as thermal chimneys where the rock radiates the highest temperatures.

The basin feels ancient, stable, and entirely self-contained. The weathering on the surrounding basalt peaks suggests this shelf has existed in this exact thermal state for millennia. Nothing changes except the continuous, restless churning of the flesh. To step into the basin would be to lose your footing instantly in a three-foot-deep drift of warm, dry bodies, sliding down into a dark, quiet, crowded furnace that has never known a drop of rain.
