The basin is no larger than a city block, sunken forty feet below the surrounding grade between high, dripping walls of dark dressed granite. It is a pocket of warm, dense infrastructure where heavy industry and thick plant matter have spent a century growing into a single, saturated fabric.

The heat here is immediate and thick—a heavy, tropical humidity fed by the exhaust of low-pressure steam boilers and the continuous evaporation of water from a hundred weeping surfaces. The air smells of wet leaf mold, hot cylinder oil, crushed ginger-root, and the bitter, metallic tang of oxidising iron.

At the center of the basin sits a pump yard. There is no soil in the traditional sense; the ground underfoot is a three-foot-thick mattress of tightly woven fibrous roots, liverworts, and rotting oak decking, through which cast-iron floor grates periodically emerge like ribs. Underneath this carpet, water moves constantly in shallow, brick-lined sluices, carrying a thin skim of irridescent machine lubricant toward central sumps.

The architecture is layered and vertical, despite the modest footprint. Massive cast-iron stanchions, greened by verdigris and coated in a felt-like fur of creeping moss, rise from the marshy floor to support catwalks made of perforated steel plate. These walkways are heavily overgrown; ferns with fronds the length of a man’s arm burst through the iron mesh, forcing foot traffic to brush past broad, wet leaves that leave streaks of green moisture on canvas sleeves.

The machinery is scaled for heavy, patient work. A pair of horizontal beam engines sits in open-air pits at the western wall. Their flywheels, twelve feet across and cast with thick, rounded spokes, turn at a slow, relentless eight revolutions per minute. As they spin, their lower rims dip into troughs of warm drainage water, throwing a constant fine spray into the surrounding bank of broad-leafed arums and wild monstera. The plants near the engines have grown huge on the heat and moisture; their leaves are glossy, thick as leather, and pitted where hot droplets of boiler condensate have dripped onto them day after day for decades.

Everything here is loud, but the noise is rhythmic and mechanical rather than chaotic. The dominant sound is a deep, chest-thumping *chug-shuck... chug-shuck* from the steam cylinders, punctuated by the sharp hiss of automatic drain cocks venting into beds of wet gravel. Beneath that sits the continuous, heavy roar of water falling over a series of short, stepped brick spillways that cut across the southern edge of the basin. The sound bounces off the damp stone retaining walls, creating an acoustic environment so full that speech requires shouting, though there is rarely anyone here to speak.

The technology is built on heavy metal and thick timber, but the plants have taken over structural duties. In places where steel tie-rods have rusted thin under the constant drizzle, thick, corded vines—some as thick as a thigh—have been trained along the same spans, bound to the iron with heavy brass clamps. The roots of giant ficus trees have wrapped themselves around the foundations of the boiler housings, their bark fused under enormous pressure against the riveted plates. The trees do not tear the machinery apart; instead, they cradle it, holding the heavy pumps true against the shifting, waterlogged earth of the basin floor.

Light comes from above, clear and diffuse, filtered through a high canopy of wild fig and cultivated shade trees that overhang the top of the granite walls. The sunlight strikes the rising plumes of steam, turning them into bright, glowing columns that illuminate the lower catwalks in flashes of warm, pearlescent white. 

Water is everywhere—dripping from the undersides of iron stairs, trickling down the bark of structural roots, weeping from the mortar joints of the retaining walls, and spurting in tiny, persistent jets from the packed glands of brass gate valves. Small, dark snails with thick, smooth shells are the only obvious life, clinging by the dozens to the warm, wet casings of the feed-water pipes where the heat is most consistent.

Nothing here moves quickly except the water and the reciprocating pistons. The trees add a ring of wood each year; the iron sheds a thin layer of scale that settles into the moss; the bronze valves acquire another coat of dark bronze-rot. The yard exists in a steady, heavy equilibrium—a machine that has forgotten where its iron ends and its roots begin, churning through the wet heat of the valley, hour after hour, with no sign of stopping.
