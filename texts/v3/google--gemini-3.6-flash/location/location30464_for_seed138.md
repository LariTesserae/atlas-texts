The basin is forty paces across, dropped like a square, open-topped box into the saturated mud of the lower bottoms. Its walls are tall—three times the height of a man—and made of raw, dark grey concrete so recently cast that the imprint of the rough pine form-boards remains crisp and sliver-sharp in the grain. The corners of the retaining structures have not yet rounded; their edges are hard, razor-like, and cool only on the outside, though the air inside the box is thick and suffocatingly hot.

A heavy, wet heat lingers here without variation. It is generated from below and held by the high, square walls, which block whatever breeze might pass over the marshy flats beyond. Steam creeps constantly off the surface of the central sump—a low, square pool where four thick-walled iron pipes discharge black water at a steady, uninterrupted flow. The atmosphere is nearly opaque with humidity, filtering the sky down to a yellow-grey twilight that never brightens and never goes entirely dark. A few caged incandescent bulbs, mounted low on the concrete pylons, cast sickly halos through the mist, their glass frosted with a perpetual coat of warm oil and condensate.

The place is a valve node, an industrial drain-pocket built to clear water from the sub-grade foundations of the surrounding city district, though the city itself is visible only as the blunt, dark silhouettes of elevated railway gantries passing overhead beyond the walls. 

Inside the basin, the layout is open and spare. There are no piles of rubble, no clutter of tools, no loose debris. The floor is composed of thick, smooth-troweled slab, sloped gently toward the central pit. A pair of massive, green-painted centrifugal pumps sit on raised pedestals near the western wall, their iron casings twice the girth of a horse. They do not cycle on or off; they run at a single, immutable frequency. The drive belts—wide strips of vulcanized rubber—whir through the damp air with a heavy *thwack-thwack-thwack*, while the iron housings vibrate with a low, deep thrum that shakes the warm water pooling around their bases.

Water is everywhere, though it does not fall as rain. It bleeds through the cold joints between the concrete pours; it runs in greasy ribbons down the faces of the vertical iron conduits; it trickles off the undersides of the steel catwalks that cross the pit. The air smells of wet slag, warm cylinder oil, and swamp gas. 

Despite the mechanical noise and the high heat, the basin is dense with small, quick life. The vegetation is nearly zero—only a thin, black-green crust of micro-algae coats the submerged steps of the pit, and a few pale, thread-like weeds struggle in the mortar gaps where the brass grease-lines pierce the wall. But the fauna thrives in the constant, thermal dampness. 

Thousands of small, dark-shelled snails cling to the warm concrete just above the water line, feeding on the greasy scum. In the shadows under the pump pedestals, fat, slick-skinned frogs sit motionless on the wet iron flanges, their throats pulsing in time with the stroke of the hydraulic pistons. Large, brown-winged midges hover in dense, vibrating clouds around the low-hung lamp cages, dropping by the hundreds into the oily water, where small, blind-eyed river fish snap at them from the dark. Rats—heavy-bodied and wet-furred—move along the tops of the hot-water pipes with casual, unhurried balance, ignoring the thunder of the machinery entirely.

The sound is immense and rhythmic, a three-part cadence that fills the small space until it feels solid. There is the wet, gurgling roar of the four discharge pipes filling the sump; the deep, iron-lung thump of the main drive cylinders; and the high, thin hiss of steam leaking from a flange on the high-pressure return line. The sounds do not echo; the air is too wet and thick for echoes, swallowing the harsh edges of the metal-on-metal clatter and leaving only a heavy, crushing resonance that vibrates in the teeth.

Nothing changes here. The water level in the pit does not rise or fall by an inch; the pumps do not overheat, nor do they cool down. The temperature stays locked at a wet, feverish heat that keeps the iron from rusting quickly, coating it instead in a protective skin of warm, oily moisture. The light does not shift; the overhead sky remains a flat, featureless sheet of steam-pushed grey. The crisp, un-eroded corners of the fresh concrete remain sharp, rinsed clean of dust by the endless weeping of the walls. It is a small, heavy machine-pocket, perfectly balanced between the wild water beneath the earth and the rigid city above it, locked in an endless, sweltering present.
