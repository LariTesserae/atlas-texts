The basin sits low, a shallow oval no wider than a small rail yard, bounded by old earthworks that have long since grown a skin of stiff, blue-gray lichen. The air down here is cool—consistently cool, carrying the sharp, clean scent of crushed wet fern, cold iron, and damp soot. 

There is a vastness of empty space on the floor of the basin, despite its modest size. Only four or five large structures stand within it, separated by wide expanses of flat, dark green ground. But the ground is not soil. It is a dense, springy substrate of living root-marrow and compressed bark, so tightly woven that it rings dull under heel, punctuated every few yards by sunken iron plates with square drainage perforations. Where metal meets plant, there is no seam; the roots have swallowed the margins of the iron, growing around rivets and flanged edges until the metal appears to have been cast directly into the wood.

The structures themselves are hybrids of heavy industry and slow, timbering biology. One is a boiler house—or something that functions like one—whose outer walls are formed from the hollow, lignified trunk of a single massive, low-slung plant. Its wood is thick as a ship’s hull, dark and slick with a thin sheen of cold condensation. Out of its upper crown protrude three riveted steel stacks, pitted by decades of weather, leaking thin, pale wisps of steam that smelling faint of resin and ozone.

The tech here operates on a deliberate, mechanical rhythm, but at a pace so slow it almost escapes notice. Inside a open-sided shed—its roof framed by arched, fibrous ribs that drip slow, clear water into a copper-lined trough—a massive flywheel turns. The wheel is iron, eight feet across, but its spokes are wrapped in living, copper-hued tendrils that hum with a faint electric current. Every three or four minutes, a wet *clack-hiss* echoes across the quiet yard as a hydraulic valve shifts, driven by the internal sap pressure of a nearby bulbous root trunk. A iron piston moves two inches, draws a breath of cold air, and settles back into rest.

Water does not flow in open streams here; it collects. It gathers in bead-work along the undersides of the broad, leather-textured leaves that overhang the perimeter. It pools in the hollows of old brass gear housings and drips from the jointwork of overhead pipe conduits. Occasional light mists roll down from the earthwork rim, coating the ironwork in micro-droplets before dissipating, leaving the surfaces permanently damp but never flooded.

Erosion here is a settled matter. Nothing is raw; nothing is newly broken. The corners of the iron machinery have been rounded by years of damp air and plant friction. The wooden walls are polished smooth in places where vine-cables drag across them during their slow, seasonal shifts. The balance between the mechanical and the botanical was struck long ago, arriving at a state of quiet, durable permanence.

Animal life is sparse and secretive. Now and then, a pale, shell-less mollusk, the length of a finger, glides along the cool brass tubing of a pressure gauge, leaving a shimmering trail that dries clear. High up near the open sky, small, dark birds occasionally dart across the width of the basin, not stopping, passing from one outer ridge to the other without descending into the cool, silent works below.

The light is daylight, unmodified by heavy canopy—the plant life is thick underfoot and in the walls, but open to the sky above—yet it feels filtered, tempered by the slate-gray atmosphere of the low ground. Everything can be seen clearly: the fine rust on a dormant crane arm, the steady drip of cold water onto a brass plate, the slow, rhythmic swell of a living storage tank taking on fluid.

It is a place of work, but the workers are absent or transformed into the machinery itself. The soundscape remains muted—the soft *drip-drop* of water on metal, the low hum of electricity through root-conduits, the occasional wet sigh of a release valve, and then long, open intervals of stillness where the cool air just sits in the space, waiting for the next slow gear to turn.
