The water is not sea and not sky, but a warm, heavy medium—a soup of mineral tea and dissolved tallow, warm as a pulse and thick enough to hold a shadow for seconds after whatever cast it has torn away. It fills the low basin completely, extending for miles beneath a invisible ceiling where the fluid simply thickens into grease and then into low, bruised fog.

There are no fish here. There are no crabs, no pale deep-sea worms, no sightless squids darting through the dark. Nothing breathes air or water with lungs or gills. Instead, the void left by animal life is violently occupied by chlorophyll and iron.

Below, the ground is an un-eroded, jagged basin of freshly cracked black basalt. The rock is young—its edges are razor-like shear planes, vitreous and sharp as scalpel blades, jutting out of dark silt like broken glass. It has not had time to be rounded by currents. Every rift in the floor is a fresh wound in the earth’s crust, venting plume after plume of scalding, iron-rich liquid that smells of sulfur, machine oil, and boiled leaves.

Drowned into this jagged floor is a vast, shattered industrial anatomy. It does not lie dead like a shipwreck; it operates with frantic, chaotic terror.

Massive cast-iron flywheels, twelve paces across and utterly uncorroded, spin underwater at impossible speeds. They do not push water so much as tear it apart, creating vast, screaming envelopes of cavitation—hollow, vacuum-bright bubbles that collapse with thunderous implosions, rattling the basalt floor. Submerged iron piston-cylinders as thick as silos pump furiously, driven by some unknown pressure from below. They thrust upward through matted beds of wet, rubbery vegetation, spraying cascades of amber sparks directly into the warm fluid. The sparks do not extinguish; they drift through the liquid like glowing, slow-motion embers before striking the leaves of underwater jungle-ferns and igniting tiny, silent, underwater explosions of green gas.

The plant life is unnervingly fast and impossibly dense. It clings to every gear tooth and high-voltage conduit. Thick, fleshy ribbon-weeds—crimson and translucent yellow, with sharp, serrated margins—sprout from the raw joints of the iron machinery. They mature in minutes. A brass housing, scoured clean by an underwater jet of steam, is covered within three hundred seconds by a matted carpet of spongy, velvet-green moss that pulses in direct, electric synchronicity with the dynamos below it. Long, rope-like vines coil around spinning drive shafts, snapped by the torque, pulverized into green slurry, and instantly regenerated by the warm, nutrient-choked current.

Light is dim, filtered through thirty feet of churning, brownish-cyan water and dense canopy, but it is never still. It comes in jagged, blinding bursts from open transformer banks bolted to the basalt. These sub-aquatic electrical arrays are uninsulated; thick, violet lightning arcs continuously between naked copper bus-bars through the saline water. Where the arcs strike, the water does not boil away—it fractures into strange, crystalline sheets of blue glass that hang suspended in the liquid for a breath before shattering into sinking dust.

The sound is not a rumble; it is an absolute, bone-crushing violence. Because sound travels four times faster in warm liquid than in air, the landscape does not offer acoustic distance. The screech of cavitating impellers, the rhythmic *thrum-THUMP* of heavy hydraulic rams, the tearing of fresh rock as new fissures split the basin floor, and the continuous, dry *crack* of submerged high-voltage discharge all strike at once. The vibration is so dense it feels solid, a thick, physical wall that presses against every surface, vibrating through the iron, through the volcanic rock, through the fibrous stalks of the giant kelp-trees.

And reality here is fluid, continuously unmaking and remaking its own rules under the pressure of the machines and the sap.

In places, the water moves backward. A torrent of heavy, cold fluid falls *upward* out of an open shaft in a iron turbine housing, climbing toward the dim surface like a column of clear salt. Elsewhere, a patch of dense, fern-covered rock will simply invert its density: the basalt floats upward like cork, carrying its load of screaming machinery and entangled vines, while the water around it freezes into a warm, gelatinous jelly that hums at a pitch high enough to shatter glass.

Huge brass heat-exchangers, thirty feet high and covered in razor-sharp barnacle-like growths of pure nickel, vent vast clouds of hot, stinking foam. From within this foam, fully-formed structures seem to coagulate—complex webs of copper tubing and glass-sheathed wiring that grow like coral, sprout leaves, catch fire under water, and are immediately swept away by the turbulent undertow into the dark, open reaches of the basin.

Nothing rests. There is no decay, only violent displacement. A machine is built by self-threading steel cables rising out of a rift, runs at red-hot efficiency for twenty minutes while submerged in forty feet of warm liquid, becomes entirely choked by a hyper-growth of bulbous, bioluminescent spore-sacs, and is then crushed into flat scrap by a fresh upthrust of glass-sharp basalt—only for three new turbines to sprout from its flattened wreckage before the dust settles.

The air above the surface—if there is a surface—is distant, irrelevant. Down here, in the warm, roaring, green-and-iron darkness, the world is an engine that feeds on its own roots, spinning so fast that its edges have begun to fray into pure energy, noise, and leaf.
