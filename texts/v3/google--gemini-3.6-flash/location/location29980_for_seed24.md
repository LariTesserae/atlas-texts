The space is a low, shallow basin, no larger than a small village square or the floor of a roofless cathedral. It sits sunken into a vast, flat expanse of iron-rich shale, bounded on all sides by low, smoothly curved ridges that fold inward like gentle shoulders. The air inside the basin is thick with a steady, dry warmth—a lingering thermal mass held by the stone itself, unchanging day or night.

There is no light here, only a deep, warm-toned gloom. The sky above—if there is a sky—does not shine; the atmosphere is a dense, featureless darkness that swallows sight beyond twenty paces. What can be discerned comes through tactile presence and the faint, red-black silhouettes of the terrain against the dark.

The floor is composed of smooth, buffed siltstone, worn down by ancient dust-winds that have long since ceased. The erosion here is complete; there are no sharp edges, no raw fractures, no fresh scree. Every lip of rock has been planed into a soft, rolling contour. It is a landscape in absolute rest, holding a frozen equilibrium where nothing moves, nothing falls, and nothing decays.

Water does not exist in this basin. The atmosphere is entirely non-aqueous, parched to a bone-dry crispness that preserves everything in its current state forever. Without moisture, there is no mud, no rust, no scent of damp earth—only the clean, faint smell of warm, baked mineral dust.

Vegetation is sparse, rising at long intervals from the fine, dry silt that fills the rock crevices. These are not green or fleshy plants, but woody, cork-like spindles, pale as bleached bone. They stand three or four feet tall, perfectly rigid, shaped like twisted horn or coiled wire. Their outer bark is dry and papery, rattling faintly only if brushed against, though there is no wind to stir them. Between one spindle and the next lie yards of empty, polished floor. Near the center of the basin, a small cluster of low, cup-shaped husks grows directly out of the flat rock, their hollow centers filled with fine, motionless mineral dust.

No animals haunt these hollows. No insects crawl through the silt, no birds pass overhead, and no burrows break the smooth slope of the ridges. The silence is heavy and unbroken, a quiet so dense that it feels like a physical layer resting upon the stone.

Near one edge of the basin, resting in a shallow depression carved by some long-forgotten process, lies a scatter of dark basalt stones. These are not structural—no walls have ever been built here, no hearths cleared, no earth moved. But the stones bear the unmistakable marks of human or pre-human hands: clean, deliberate fractures along their margins, where flakes were struck away to create heavy, crude chopping edges and flat scrapers. They lie where they were laid down, half-buried in dry dust, their sharp knapped edges slowly softened by eons of ambient decay. They are simple, manual tools from a primitive age, sitting in the warm darkness like forgotten weights.

Nothing changes here. The temperature remains constant, radiating from the floor into the stagnant air. The shadows do not lengthen or shift. The sparse, woody stalks do not grow, nor do they drop leaves. The basin exists in a quiet, permanent balance—a small, dark room carved into the crust of a quiet world, held in perpetual, warm stillness.
