The basin is no larger than a cathedral precinct, perhaps four hundred paces from one curved wall of smoothed, worn-down basalt to the other. Its floor lies low—so low that the sea must be near, though there is no salt in the air, only the heavy, sweet-sour vapor of bruised vegetation and warm, recirculated water. The stone beneath the silt is unimaginably old; its edges were rounded off by rivers that dried up before the current flora had a name, leaving a flat, shallow dish where water cannot sink away and so must constantly move, weep, and re-collect.

Here, city and forest are not merely adjacent; they are the same cellular system. The architecture is composed entirely of vascular, high-tensile plant matter—a dense, pale-grained wood that grows with the structural precision of extruded steel. Pylons as thick as ancient oaks rise from the warm, inch-deep water covering the floor, but they do not branch like natural trees. Instead, they curve into flying buttresses, ribbed vaults, and suspended platforms that mimic the layout of a dense civic center, though built at a scale that leaves vast, airy voids between its main structures. There are wide plazas suspended twenty feet above the water, woven from living root-mats so tightly bound they feel like vulcanized rubber underfoot. 

Yet despite the layout of an organized settlement—complete with aqueducts, tiered walkways, and tall, hollow spires that resemble municipal towers—the density is low. The air feels spacious, almost sparse, broken only by the huge, clean spans of the green-gray architecture and the bright, unclouded daylight penetrating every level.

Nothing stays fixed for long. The technology that underpins this glade is invisible as machinery, operating instead at the molecular level within the sap and cellulose. It drives an aggressive, hyperactive metabolism. A living archway spanning a pool may hold its shape for an hour, serving as a conduit for pulsing, nutrient-rich fluid, before its cellular anchors dissolve with a wet, tearing sound, causing the structure to drop into the water like a shed skin. Within minutes, a dozen new shoots—stiff as iron rods and thick as a man’s chest—spear upward from the mud, weaving themselves into a totally different geometry: a spiral stair, a reservoir wall, or a tall, flute-like chimney that begins immediately to vent hot steam into the clear air.

Because of this constant, chaotic turnover, the noise is immense. It is a wet, mechanical carnival of growth and collapse. The landscape pops, groans, and gurgles with hydraulic pressure. High in the open vaults, pressurized cambium valves snap open with the sound of pistol shots, releasing mists that catch the sun in brilliant, fleeting rainbows. Below, in the flooded shallows, the water is in perpetual turmoil—not from wind, but from the thrashing of countless small, armored organisms that feed on the falling detritus of rapid decay. 

These creatures are abundant and restless. Some resemble pale, many-jointed crayfish with shells made of iridescent silica; they crawl over the submerged roots, shaving off dying fibers with scissor-like mandibles. Flying things—fat, four-winged organisms with soft, transparent bodies that hum like high-voltage transformers—dart between the rising spires, carrying glowing clusters of genetic material from one growing node to another. 

The water itself is warm—nearly blood-heat—and crystal clear despite the frenzy. It weeps continuously from the undersides of the living platforms, falling in thick, heavy curtains that ring against the ancient stone floor. Where the water splashes onto the exposed basalt, it reveals the stone's absolute smoothness; eons of flow have polished the rock until it reflects the sky like gray glass, showing the shadows of the shifting plant-towers above.

There are no doors, no glass, no metal rivets. The "city" functions without a population of citizens, or perhaps the plants and their attendant swarms *are* the citizens, constantly rebuilding their habitat according to some complex, non-Euclidean algorithm encoded in their sap. A high, ribbed corridor might end abruptly in mid-air, its tip exploding into a burst of broad, blue-tinted leaves that absorb the midday sun, converting light directly into kinetic movement that sways the entire tower to and fro with a deep, rhythmic creak.

In the center of the basin sits a broad pool where the turbulence subsides for a few yards. Here, the water is held in a shallow ring of calcified moss that shifts its boundaries by a few inches every minute. At the middle of the pool, a thick column of clear sap rises three feet into the air—not as a fountain under mechanical pressure, but as a stable, standing cylinder of fluid held together by surface tension so extreme it resembles carved glass. Inside the fluid, tiny, bright green structures no larger than grains of sand build and unbuild miniature geometric lattices, glowing faintly under the direct sunlight before dissolving back into the stream.

The air remains warm, wet, and bright. The scent of ozone from the molecular rearrangement of the plants mingles with the earthy reek of fast-rotting leaf-slurry. A giant wall of woven reed-wood, fifty feet high and perforated like a lace curtain, suddenly shudders, loses its rigidity, and collapses into the shallow water with a roar like a falling wave, sending warm spray across the basin. Before the ripples reach the outer stone wall, fresh green needles are already breaking the surface, reaching up toward the light, beginning the city again.
