The mountains here have no rounded shoulders. They are raw, geometric extrusions of black slate and virgin granite, so newly birthed from the deep crust that no rain has yet softened their edges, no glaciers have carved their flanks into valleys. Every crest is a knife-edge; every cliff face looks freshly snapped, as though a continent-sized quartz crystal were still mid-shatter. There is no soil, only the sharp, unweathered stone, pristine and razor-bright where it catches the low, twilight gleam.

Yet despite the youth of the rock, the world is draped in a dense, cold moisture. A perpetual dimness hangs over the region—a thick, slate-colored atmosphere where the sun is reduced to a pale, displaced ring hovering somewhere above the fog decks. Moisture does not fall as rain so much as it emerges from the air itself, clinging to every surface in heavy beads that never quite freeze, though the air stays bitingly cool.

And where the moisture gathers, life has seized the stone with startling vigor. Thick cushions of cold-hardy emerald moss, saturated like sponges, pad the sharp edges of the basalt. Long, rubbery fronds of silver-tinted ferns spring directly from hair-thin fissures in the unworked granite. They do not wait for dirt; they feed on the damp mineral dust and the greasy soot drifting from the city.

Because a city lives here, stragations of iron and brass stretched across abysses miles wide.

It is an urban landscape, but one built with vast emptiness between its limbs. Massive, riveted iron towers—each thirty stories tall, black with soot and slick with condensation—are anchored by mountain-sized turnbuckles directly into the raw rock spires. Between these towers, suspension cables thick as ancient trees sag across the gulfs, carrying cold-iron gantries, enclosed streetcar tubes, and high-voltage copper lines wrapped in gutta-percha. A tenement block, six tiers of bay windows and zinc roofing, hangs beneath a natural rock arch like an iron wasp nest, suspended five thousand feet above a sea of grey cloud. Half a mile across the void sits a gasometer, its giant brass crown gleaming faintly through the mist, entirely isolated save for a single, swaying catwalk.

The technology is heavy, mechanical, and loud. Massive iron pistons, three times the height of a man, beat a thunderous, slow cadence along the cliffside foundries. Their rhythm is patient—one deep, bone-rattling *THUMP* every twelve seconds—driving hydraulic pressure through miles of sweating lead pipes to power the cranes perched on the high needles. Steam vents cough thick, white plumes into the chill air, where the vapor instantly condenses, raining lukewarm droplets onto the moss-padded catwalks below.

Despite the industrial noise, the animal kingdom thrives in the vast, open spaces between the iron. Millions of pale, long-winged sky-rays—creatures with the span of an albatross and the oily, leather skin of an eel—roost in the lattice work of the iron bridges. They launch themselves into the cool drafts by the thousands, sweeping through the gaps between the tenement towers in silent, undulating ribbons. Smaller things, like blue-shelled glass-spiders, weave dense, water-heavy webs between the high-tension cables, catching the cloud-midge swarms that rise from the damp moss beds. The air is never quiet: beneath the rhythmic bang of the steam engines is a deafening, continuous chorus of click-calls, wing-beats, and the deep, brassy trumpetings of megafauna nesting in the dark spaces beneath the factory foundations.

And yet, reality is slightly untethered here.

The breakdown is not violent; it operates on the same slow, patient clock as the hydraulic pumps. In certain valleys between the spires, gravity pulls at an angle. Water dripping from the high iron viaducts does not fall straight down; it falls toward the nearest mountain face at a thirty-degree slant, forming long, diagonal ribbons of droplets that splash against the vertical rock. In the shadow of the central power station, spatial distances stretch without warning: a catwalk that appears twenty yards long may require a five-minute walk to cross, the metal plates beneath one's boots ringing out fifty paces for every ten visually traversed.

The stone itself is still coming into being. On the lower terraces, where the factory yards end, one can watch raw basalt extrude from the mist in real-time—not as lava, but as solid, cold crystals growing like salt in a solution, pushing rusted iron girders aside with silent, unstoppable force over the course of months.

Shadows, too, are unreliable. Under the flickering sodium lamps strung along the railway lines, a crane’s shadow might lag behind its mechanical arm, or point toward a source of light that isn't there, leaning toward the empty sky where a peak has yet to finish forming.

People—or whatever tends these colossal, clanging machines—are seldom seen out in the open, but their presence is everywhere. Cold light glows from thousands of rain-streaked windows set into the hanging iron tenements. A trolley car, rusted and spattering blue sparks from its roof-trolley, screeches along an overhead cable, its whistle wailing out across the vast, wet chasm, echoing off peaks that were born only yesterday and will not wear down for a million years.
