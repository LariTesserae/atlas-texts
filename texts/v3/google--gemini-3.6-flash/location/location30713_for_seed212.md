The noise precedes the vision, or perhaps the noise is so immense it warps the eyes before the horizon clears. It is not the sound of wind or water, though both are present in small, furious quantities. It is the deafening, continuous violence of cell division and structural failure happening at once—a thunderous shrieking of dry fibers snapping under hydraulic strain, the gun-shot crack of lignified bark splitting open under sudden expansion, the deep, iron-heavy bass of deep roots grinding against one another like shifting fault lines.

Below a bright, unblinking sun that sits high in a pale sky, a vast lowland basin unfolds for miles. It is a city, unmistakably so in its geometry, yet there is not a single stone, nail, brick, or wire within it. It is built entirely of living plant matter, dense and warm, breathing and boiling in its own metabolic heat.

The scale is regional—a broad, sun-drenched depression flanked by young, razor-sharp ridges of green stalk-rock where the ground itself seems to have been heaved up by subterranean tubers. Across the valley floor, the vegetation does not merely cover the earth; it *is* the earth, the substrate, the walls, the roofs, the roads, and the sky-reaching spires. 

The architecture is biological, organized, and terrifyingly dense. Avenues seventy feet wide run in straight, impossible lines through the valley, paved not with cobbles but with a smooth, calcified bark that secretes a dry, amber-like lacquer under the warm sun. Flanking these avenues are towers—multi-story structures woven from the hollow, braided trunks of giant bamboo-like grasses and interlocking tendril-vines. They feature balconies formed from fused shelf-fungi hard as seasoned oak, spires of coiled rattan that stretch three hundred feet into the air, and vaulted colonnades created by the perfectly symmetrical arches of weeping ironwood. 

Yet there are no tools here. No hands shaped these arcades; no minds drew these grids. The city constructs itself through a furious, hyperactive tropism. Plants here do not grow toward the light in slow, patient seasons; they erupt. A skyscraper of tightly wound reed-bundles can shoot out of an avenue in twenty minutes, its walls thickening and calcifying with a earsplitting *screee-pop* that shakes the warm air, only to be overtaken an hour later by a wave of carnivorous ivy that chokes the spire, starves its crown, and causes the entire structure to collapse into a heap of sharp, fibrous rubble. 

This is not an old forest; it is a landscape of violent youth. The edges of every fractured stalk are crisp, green, and wet with sap. The broken wood does not rot—there are no grubs, no beetles, no fungi of decay, no birds, no animal life whatsoever to gnaw or decompose the mass. The fauna index is zero. The only presences are the plants themselves, acting as architects, demolition crews, and citizens.

Because the weirdness here approaches a absolute break in physical law, the city’s geometry resists stable cartography. 

In the central district, where the botanical density reaches a suffocating peak, space folds under the tension of the hyper-dense canopy. A straight avenue of petrified bamboo appears to run for three miles, yet a traveler walking it would find themselves back at their origin after two hundred paces, having traversed a spatial loop created by the curving magnetic fields of a giant, pulsing bulb buried beneath the central plaza. 

Elsewhere, gravity does not pull straight down; it tilts toward the heaviest concentrations of biomass. Near the base of the Great Banyan Vaults—a cluster of fused trunks as wide as cathedral naves—droplets of heavy condensation do not fall from the leaves to the ground. Instead, they fall sideways, drifting in warm, sweet-smelling horizontal rains that coat the vertical bark-walls in shimmering, glass-like films before evaporating into the heat.

Water is an intermittent, precious currency here. There are no rivers, only the sweat of the city. The climate is warm—a steady, tropical eighty-five degrees—and the air is dry between sudden, localized bursts of moisture. Every few minutes, a massive, pressurized xylem-conduit running beneath an avenue will burst under the sheer force of its internal pumping, sending a sixty-foot geyser of tepid, mineral-rich water into the bright air. For thirty seconds, the surrounding blocks are drenched in fine mist. Leaves expand wildly, stretching toward the spray with a wet, rustling roar; then the sap coagulates, sealing the rupture with a sharp *clack*, and the air dries instantly, leaving white, crystalline salt-crusts sparkling on every surface under the glare of the noon sun.

Look closely at the walls of the structures: there are windows, but they are framed by living succulents that contract and dilate like the irises of non-existent eyes, regulating the light that enters the hollow interiors. Inside these towers, there are no floors in the traditional sense, but complex, three-dimensional webs of tension-wood, springy as trampoline nets, layered one above the other in endless, spiraling staircases that lead to rooms whose walls are soft, velvety mosses that hum with the vibration of high-speed fluid transport.

The dynamic state of this place is near-chaotic. Creation and destruction occur at the speed of a storm. To stand in a plaza is to witness an endless, silent-yet-deafening bazaar of biological warfare. To your left, a massive archway of pale willow-flesh is currently weaving itself across a thoroughfare, its tendrils lacing together with the rhythmic, mechanical precision of an automated loom. To your right, a tower grown yesterday is undergoing a structural crisis: its base has been undercut by a sudden, aggressive surge of subterranean tap-roots, and with a sound like a freight train derailment, the top three hundred feet of woven wood shear off, falling into an adjoining courtyard. 

Before the dust of green pulp and crushed leaves even settles, thousands of tiny, needle-sharp shoots are already pin-pricking their way out of the wreckage, using the fresh timber of the fallen tower as fuel to begin building a new, denser labyrinth of terraces.

There is no memory here, no history written on stone, no rusty remnants of an earlier age. Everything is raw, fresh, sharp, and blindingly bright under the high sun. It is a metropolis without inhabitants, an urban engine running on pure, unhinged chlorophyll and hydraulic pressure, forever building its own streets, forever tearing down its own skyline, roaring under the weight of its own impossible, breathless life.
