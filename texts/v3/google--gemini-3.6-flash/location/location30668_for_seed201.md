The sky at this altitude is not blue so much as a deep, hard indigo, stripped of moisture and dust, so clear that the light doesn't scatter—it simply hits. It strikes the high terrace with the force of a white-hot hammer, though the air itself remains thin, cold, and razor-sharp against the throat. 

This is the Shelf of the White Ribs, a hanging ledge no larger than a village square, suspended five thousand meters above a lowland obscured by a permanent ocean of grey fog. The mountains here are absurdly young. There are no smooth slopes or rounded shoulders; the granite and black slate are raw fractures, snapped off as if by a recent blow, leaving edges so sharp they can sever skin if leaned upon. Quartzite veins run through the dark basalt like frozen lightning, unweathered and blinding under the high sun.

Despite the bitter cold—the kind that turns breath to fine ice needles that drop instantly to the ground—the shelf is lush. It is not a forest's lushness, but a dense, compressed carpet of cold-adapted life that fills every depression and crevice between the jagged stone plates. Thick cushions of velvet-moss, deep crimson and liver-brown, grow three feet deep, acting as insulation over the permafrost. From these carpets sprout tiny, thick-stemmed succulents with leaves like frost-covered beads, and dwarf cold-ferns whose fronds are coated in a dense, silvery down that traps radiant heat.

Water here is an event of sun and shadow. There are no streams. Instead, the dark basalt absorbs the intense sunlight, heating the rock face until the deep-buried ice within the stone begins to bleed. The cliff-faces *sweat*. Drops of water bead on the black rock, slide down into the moss, and gather in tiny, glass-clear pockets no bigger than a palm. The moment a cloud passes—or a shadow shifts across the terrace—the sweat stops instantly, freezing into clear, brittle sheets that crack like thin glass with a dry *ping*.

Because the shelf is small and framed by vertical voids—a sheer drop of three miles on one side, a chimney of bare rock on the other—there is a vast sense of emptiness. The air has very little in it. You can see from one end of the terrace to the other with absolute, cruel clarity. Nothing hides behind haze. 

Yet within this small, sharp-bordered amphitheater, the ground is swarming.

Small, heavy-furred creatures, no larger than mice but built like tiny oxen with splayed, six-toed paws, tunnel through the moss carpets, kicking up tiny puffs of silver spore-dust. They hunt high-altitude beetles whose shells are iridescent like oil on water, designed to reflect the burning ultraviolet rays. Along the cliff edges, flat-bodied flyers with skin like stretched parchment hang on the mountain’s thermal updrafts, dropping down to snatch sun-dazed lizards that creep out from the dark fissures to warm their blood on the hot quartzite veins. The sound of all this life is strangely muffled; the thick moss swallows footsteps and scuffles, leaving only the sound of clicking beetle-carapaces, the sudden *whir* of a flyer’s wing, and the constant, subtle snapping of ice forming and breaking under the sun.

Human presence here is faint, ancient, and sparse. It does not dominate the landscape; it sits within it like a dropped tool.

On the windward lip of the terrace stands a low, semicircular wall built of unmortared slate slabs, leaned against one another in a fish-scale pattern to break the gale. Inside the arc lies a pit filled with charcoal dust cold for years. A few artifacts lie embedded in the moss, preserved by the dry cold: a needle carved from a flyer’s wing-bone, a hand-ax knapped from grey flint with its edge still viciously sharp, and a bundle of dried alpine roots wrapped in cured skin and tied with dried sinew. 

Near the wall, three tall markers are jammed into a crack in the rock. They are not stone, but the petrified rib-bones of some massive sea creature, brought up from the plains below by hands that possessed no metal, no wheel, and no beasts of burden. The bones are stained dark by decades of soot and oil, carved with deep, simple tally marks—groupings of three and five—that have caught the frost, turning the incisions into lines of bright white thread.

The terrace moves on a frantic, micro-dynamic clock. It is never static. A shadow cast by a passing bird drops the temperature of a patch of moss by twenty degrees in three seconds, freezing the succulents into glass; the sun touches it again, and the leaves thaw, uncurl, and puff pollen into the thin air in a synchronized release. Predators strike swiftly because the sun is short; the ice melts fast because the rock is black; the life burns through its daily cycle at a fever pitch while the light lasts, knowing that when the sun dips behind the razor-crested peak above, the shelf will return to a dark, absolute silence, cold enough to turn the very air into frost.
