The basin sits low in the landscape, a broad, shallow depression where three brick-lined drainage canals converge and go nowhere. It is a place the size of a small railway yard or an abandoned municipal waterworks: four massive, red-brick mill halls with arched windows, connected by elevated walkways of riveted iron plate, all anchored into a floor of dark granite flagstones. 

There is no green here. No grass sprouts between the paving stones; no lichen softens the sharp lip of the iron gutters; no moss clings to the shaded mortar. The world is entirely mineral, masonry, and metal, cleaned of plant life as though the very concept of a leaf had been systematically scrubbed from the soil before the foundations were poured. In place of moss, the damp surfaces—and almost every surface is damp—are coated in a thin, cold glaze of mineral sweat and dark, odorless machine oil that never quite dries.

The temperature stays at a persistent, clear chill. It is cool enough that your breath hangs in a brief, pale cloud, but not cold enough to freeze the water that drips perpetually from the undersides of the gantry bridges. The light is that of a bright, shadowless noon under a uniform white sky—flat, steady, and unblinking. It reveals every rivet, every water-pitted edge of pig-iron, and every hairline fracture in the brickwork with absolute, merciless clarity.

Everything has been here a very long time, but nothing is falling down. The erosion is ancient and complete. The steps leading up to the main valve-house are hollowed out like stone troughs from millions of identical impacts; the corners of the cast-iron beams have been smoothed by centuries of wet air until they look like poured tallow. The iron has stopped rusting; it has reached a stable, velvet-black patina that rejects further decay.

Though the yard is spacious—large open plazas of dark stone separate the four main buildings—it is violently, deafeningly full.

The sound hits first: a overwhelming, dual roar that fills the skull. The first half of the noise is mechanical—the relentless, rhythmic *THUMP-HISS-CLANG* of three monumental steam-driven beams operating inside the windowless brick halls. They do not exhaust smoke, only a steady, cool jet of condensation that trickles down the outer walls in white mineral veins. The second half of the noise is biological: an endless, dry, metallic scraping made by millions upon millions of hard-shelled bodies.

The fauna has completely overrun the machinery, not as parasites, but as an integral part of its stillness. They are heavy, jointed creatures the size of iron kettles, shelled in smooth, bone-white enamel that matches the porcelain insulators on the telegraph poles above. They have no eyes, only polished silver studs along their flanks, and six stout, bronze-tipped legs that click and rasp against the flagstones. 

They cover the ground three-deep in places, flowing around the bases of the brick chimneys like a living, pale gravel. They carpet the elevated walkways; they cling to the undersides of the iron girders; they cluster in shivering, dense grape-like bunches around the warm brass housings of the drive-shafts. Where a flywheel emerges from the floor to turn its slow, silent arc, fifty of the white carapaces are clamped to its rim, riding it down into the dark sump below and emerging on the other side, glistening with grease, utterly unbothered.

They do not seem to feed on organic matter. Instead, they press their mandibles against the vibrating iron pipes, drinking the cold condensation that beads on the metal, or scraping the microscopic flakes of brass dust from the bushings. Their numbers are staggering—tens of thousands in this tiny, bounded yard alone—yet they do not crowd each other into panic. They move in smooth, deterministic loops, like ball bearings rolling down pre-cut grooves.

Nothing changes here. The dynamic of the basin is locked at zero. There are no seasons, no nightfall, no sudden surges of water, no births that alter the density of the swarm, and no deaths that leave empty shells behind. If a creature falls from a gantry bridge, it strikes the granite with a loud, porcelain *crack*, rights itself instantly, and climbs back up the nearest cast-iron pillar along the exact same scent-trail it left an hour before. 

The three giant pumps strike their three-second beat. The cool water drips from the riveted culvert into the granite sump. The white-shelled carapaces slide over one another with the sound of a thousand iron shovels scraping gravel. The flat, daylight illumination holds the brickwork in permanent, sharp relief. It is a city-fragment built for an industry that ended, now inhabited by a ecology that never started, perfectly balanced, screamingly loud, and utterly motionless in time.
