The basin sits low, a shallow depression no wider than thirty paces across, tucked into the foot of a curved wall of dark, wet siltstone. The air inside is thick, warm, and stagnant, holding a humidity so dense it feels less like air and more like a lukewarm bath that has settled over the stone. High above, a lip of rock cuts off the sky, admitting only a grey, twilight gloom that never brightens and never quite dims into total black.

There is no green here. Not a blade of grass, no liverwort, no moss creeping along the damp seams. The world is entirely mineral and liquid—a landscape of bare, dark grey and reddish-tan rock, made slick by an endlessly quiet sheen of water.

The floor of the basin is crowded to saturation. Hundreds of small, rounded rimstone pools and smooth travertine bosses press shoulder-to-shoulder with no flat earth between them. They overlap like the scales of a heavy fish, each low wall holding a dish of tepid, perfectly still water before spilling over a rounded lip into the next basin below. Nothing is spaced apart; to step here would mean placing a foot directly into one shallow stone basin or balancing on the narrow, wet ridge of another. The density is absolute—stone touching stone, water kissing water, filled to the brim.

Water does not pour or rush into this place; it weeps. It emerges from millions of microscopic pores in the siltstone wall, gathering into fat, slow drops that slide down the rock face without a sound. It trickles across the crowded terraces in thin, warm sheets, less than a fingernail deep. Because the flow is so utterly constant—neither rising after rains nor drying under sun—the water level in every dish stands at the exact same millimeter day after day, year after year. The balance is absolute and ancient.

Here and there, pressed into the tight web of stone pools, are the marks of simple hands. They are faint, almost swallowed by centuries of quiet mineral crust, but distinct enough to reveal old intent. 

Near the back wall, several small slate wedges have been driven with heavy cobbles into natural fissures, splitting a thin trickle of seep into two equal streams. Below one of these split streams, a basin has been artificially enlarged: its interior bears the faint, parallel gouges of a hard point—a piece of flint or quartz used to scrape away the soft limestone until a rounded bowl the size of two clasped hands was formed. 

Elsewhere, low dams made of fist-sized river stones glued together with stiff, dark clay cut across the natural terraces. These crude walls were built long ago to redirect the warm runoff into tiny, rectangular catch-pits, but the mineral water has since deposited a thin, translucent crust of carbonate over the stones, cementing them forever into the bedrock. They look like fossilized masonry, primitive and motionless, caught halfway between construction and natural stone.

Near one of the larger catch-pits lies a forgotten tool: a thick basalt hammerstone, its nose bruised and flattened from old strikes, its sides smoothed by the water that constantly washes over it. It has sat in the same two inches of tepid water for so long that a delicate lip of calcite has begun to grow around its base, anchoring it to the floor of the pool like an outgrowth of the rock itself.

Life is faint and slow. In the shaded corners of the deeper basins, small, blind crustaceans—no larger than grains of rice—drift through the warm water, moving without urgency. Now and then, a soft, pale creature resembling a legless salamander slides across a wet stone ridge between two pools, its skin shining in the dim light, leaving a fleeting ripple in the water that closes almost immediately.

The sound of the basin is a single, muffled texture: the soft, collective sigh of a million tiny droplets falling from stone to stone. There are no loud splashes, no gurgling currents, no wind to rattle the rock. Just the continuous, low-frequency hum of water creeping over stone, repeating the same quiet pattern in an air that never cools, beneath a light that never shifts.
