The altitude here is not measured from a sea, for there is no sea below, only a endless ocean of amber-grey mist that sits three miles down, dense as curd. Up here, on the Great Spine, the world is a series of razor-keen plateaus and towering basalt pylons that feel extruded rather than carved. The rock is impossibly young; its edges are glass-sharp, obsidian-dark, and unsmoothed by time. There are no scree slopes, no weathered slopes of soft earth, only raw, crystalline fractures where the planet’s crust has punched upward in pristine, uncompromised geometry.

It is warm—a heavy, greenhouse heat that lacks the direct glare of a sun. Light filters through a thick, twilight-colored haze that hangs perpetually at the zenith, casting no hard shadows but turning the atmosphere into a dim, bronze-tinted liquid. The air smells of wet zinc, crushed fern, and warm machine grease.

Although there are no roads, no towers, and no signs of chisel or weld, the land functions with the silent, terrifying logic of a high-power switching yard. 

The vegetation does not merely grow; it manufactures. Thick, rope-like tendons of deep verdigris foliage climb the obsidian faces, coiled tightly like the windings of massive induction coils. Where these vines cross, small arc-lights flicker spontaneously under the skin of the bark, discharging silent blue sparks into the damp air. The leaves are thick, leather-heavy, and coated in a perpetual sweat of aromatic oil that drips onto the rock below with a soft, rhythmically synchronized *tap-tap-tap*. In the deep crevices between basalt pillars, fern-like structures made of ribbed, iridescent chitin unfold toward the dim light, their fronds twitching in complex, algorithmic sequences—a million tiny vanes opening and closing to regulate the heat of the stone beneath them.

Water is scarce in bulk, but omnipresent in detail. There are no rivers, but the landscape is continuously damp. Moisture condenses out of the warm, heavy air onto every surface, running in narrow, grease-slick beads down vertical glass cliffs. In small pockets on the high shelf—deep, perfectly hexagonal holes in the stone—water gathers in black, mirror-still pools no wider than a hand, boiling silently without heat before disappearing back into the porous rock.

There is a profound density to everything here. Nothing exists in isolation. Layers of life, mineral, and process are pressed against one another so tightly that it is difficult to tell where the geology ends and the ecology begins. A single square foot of cliff face might hold a dozen metallic-shelled lichens, a network of iron-rich vascular roots pulsing with warm fluid, and a cluster of creatures that resemble polished bronze crankshafts, their segmented bodies turning slowly against one another in friction-free sockets carved directly into the living rock.

The fauna is everywhere, though it makes almost no sound. Thousands of small, heavy-bodied entities—some resembling multi-jointed calipers wrapped in slick, seal-like hide, others like clusters of silver ball-bearings held together by magnetic tension—flow through the vegetation. They move with an unnatural, hydraulic smoothness. A herd of creature-assemblages, forty feet long and shaped like long, segmented iron rods, glides across the high ridge above, their legs moving in precise, tessellated patterns that leave no mark on the hard glass floor. When two touch, there is no call or sign of recognition; they simply mesh together for a moment, their internal gears and biological linkages sliding into place, before separating again to continue their silent patrol.

The world is constantly re-forging itself in real time, yet without the noise of destruction. Reality here feels somewhat unmoored at the margins. A hundred yards ahead, a massive pillar of basalt—three miles wide and jagged as a broken tooth—slides downward into the plateau with the silent, oiled ease of a piston entering a cylinder. As it sinks, the ground around it does not shake; instead, the surrounding vegetation simply stretches, its metallic vines elongating without breaking, adapting instantaneously to the new spatial arrangement. Where the stone vanished, a shallow crater opens, and within seconds, a new structure—part organic lattice, part glowing copper pylon—extrudes upward from the fresh wound, fully formed, dripping with warm condensate, and already covered in creeping, dark moss.

Distance behaves strangely here. The far-off horizon, where jagged mountain teeth pierce the bronze clouds, sometimes appears to be immediately behind the nearest tree-coil, as though space were folded back on itself like a heavy piece of damp cloth. You can look down a long, narrow canyon and see the back of your own shoulder three hundred yards away, misted over by the warm ambient vapor.

Despite the hyper-kinetic nature of the landscape—the relentless, shifting, manufacturing energy that drives every leaf and stone—the silence is nearly total. The sound of a mountain-sized piston descending into the crust is reduced to a faint, bass hum that vibrates in the teeth rather than the ears. The wet rustle of millions of chitinous fronds shifting together sounds like nothing more than a long breath drawn through wet flannel. The atmosphere swallows sound, muffling it in the dense, warm oil-mist, leaving only the low, ambient throbbing of a planetary engine operating at maximum load, deep beneath the sharp, young stone.
