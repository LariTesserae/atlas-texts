The rift is geologically brand-new, a massive drop-fault in the regional plateau where the crust has sheared clean and dropped forty fathoms into the earth. Because the fracture is so recent, the stone has no soft edges. There are no scree slopes, no weathered talus, no smooth water-carved chutes; there are only sheer, razored cleavage planes of dark basalt and grey slate, glistening where fresh quartz seams were split open to the sky.

Despite being below the level of the surrounding plain, the trench floor receives full, bright daylight. The gap above is wide—nearly a mile across—so at mid-morning the sun drops straight down, illuminating a dense, intricate labyrinth of human habitation packed tightly along the rift’s uneven bottom.

The settlement fills every square yard of the trench floor and climbs the step-faults like a coral reef. It is constructed entirely by hand from the abundant, fresh-fractured stone and heavy timber hauled down from the upper rim. Dry-stack masonry forms the foundations: flat slabs of un-weathered slate fitted together so precisely that no mortar is needed, their sharp, un-eroded corners still showing the shock-lines of their initial breaking. Above the stone bases, houses are framed in rough-hewn pine logs, notched with iron axes and caulked with grey clay scraped from the rift’s dampest floor-fissures.

There are no broad streets. The density is absolute. Roofs overlap; second-story timber overhangs almost meet across alleys barely wide enough for two people to pass shoulder-to-shoulder. Ladders made of pine rungs and lashed hemp rope connect lower terraces to upper ledges. Where the rock steps down abruptly, whole rows of small, flat-roofed dwellings are stacked directly atop one another, the roof of one family’s home serving as the courtyard and workspace for the neighbor above.

The air down here is noticeably cooler than on the plain above, trapped between the high, shadow-casting rock walls. Moisture is persistent but sparse—a thin, steady sweat of condensation that beads on the northern cliff faces and runs down in delicate, mirror-like ribbons. These runnels drip into shallow, hand-chiseled rock cisterns carved directly into the bedrock between house foundations. Small channels cut with chisels direct overflow along the edges of the narrow alleys, keeping the footpaths damp and slick.

Vegetation is minimal, confined to the margins where life can take a precarious hold. Tufts of pale grey lichen cling to the damp slate walls, and tiny, star-shaped mosses green the mortarless joints between the lowest stone courses. Here and there, a hardy mountain sedge has taken root in a sun-warmed crevice high on the cliff, its narrow blades shivering in the occasional downward draft of wind that sweeps off the upper plateau.

The settlement is quiet, its soundscape dampened by the sheer volume of wood, straw thatch, and mud-plaster. The high, vertical stone walls do not echo so much as they absorb, muffled by the tightly packed structures filling the trough. The sounds that do emerge are small and functional: the rhythmic, hollow *thump* of a wooden mallet driving a peg into a green timber; the wet, sliding scrape of a clay trowel against a wattle wall; the quiet trickle of water dropping from a roof overhang into a cedar bucket; the soft, shuffling drag of hide-soled boots over damp stone.

It is an active, industrious landscape, constantly in motion at a slow, physical pace. Scaffolding made of thin larch poles is lashed to fresh rock faces where new cell-like rooms are being carved out with hammers and wedges. On flat roof-decks, wool dyed with walnut hulls is hung on frames to dry in the direct sun-shafts. Women move between the cisterns carrying heavy earthenware jars strapped to their backs, while wood-carvers work on narrow doorframes in the alley thresholds, their cedar shavings pooling in the damp stone gutters.

Far above, against the bright strip of open sky framed by the jagged rift edges, an occasional falcon glides on the upper thermal currents, entirely detached from the dense, cool, hand-built world breathing in the shade below.
