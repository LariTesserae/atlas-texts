The ground underfoot is not rock, nor soil, nor silt. It is bone-dry pith—a compacted, miles-thick stratum of resinous cellulose, silicated bark, and densely intertwined taproots that dried out ten million years ago and never decayed. There is no moisture here, not even in the deepest cellular voids of the flora. The liquid that once sustained this world’s biology has been replaced by a dense, circulating gas of volatile hydrocarbons and dry, electrostatic dust that glimmers faintly in the dim twilight.

We are high up, caught on an immense, wind-eroded plateau that forms the outer skin of a suspended canopy world. Far below, through wide fissures in the petrified weave, there are only black gulfs where older, dead forests have collapsed into compressed carbon. Up here, at an altitude where the air is thin and cold, the plants do not die when they stop growing; they simply mineralize, hardening into brittle, fibrous ironwood and translucent, quartz-like amber.

The landscape is entirely plant-matter, yet it resembles a vast, petrified city of spires, arches, and colossal hollow trunks. Dense thickets of dry, tubular reeds—each as thick as a cathedral column—rise hundreds of feet into the cool, bruised-purple sky. Their walls are perforated by millions of microscopic resonant pores. When the high-altitude winds scour the plateau, these reeds act as massive pipe organs, filling the region with a continuous, deafening roar—a chord composed of deep, bone-rattling sub-bass and shrieking, metallic overtones that never ceases.

Dotted across this wide, sparse wilderness are the remnants and active nodes of an urban infrastructure that defies modern engineering. They are not built *upon* the vegetation, but woven directly through its atomic lattice. City-districts—separated by vast miles of empty, whistling reed-plains—hang suspended between towering fossil-branches. These settlements are constructed from a dark, smooth material that bends light slightly around its edges, anchored by zero-point gravimetric fields that cause the air around them to shimmer with static electricity.

In these sparse urban hubs, slender towers of stabilized dark matter rise like needles, piercing through the dry canopy. They do not use wires or pipes; power is distributed through the dry, silica-rich veins of the dead plants themselves, causing the surrounding landscape to pulse with faint, rhythmic veins of pale blue light. The technology is ancient and quiet, but its presence hums—a steady, high-pitched counterpoint to the roaring wind-music of the hollow flora.

Movement is everywhere, despite the lack of water. The ecosystem runs on dry friction, thermal expansion, and electrostatic potential. 

Abundant, strange fauna navigate the skeletal landscape. There are skittering, multi-jointed creatures with carapaces made of fused resin and mica, their legs tipped with diamond-hard claws that click sharply against the wooden ground. They do not drink; they feed on the dry, radio-active spores that puff in clouds from the tips of giant, accordion-folded fungi. Above, wide-winged gliders—woven from dry, leather-like membranes and light carbon-fiber ribs—catch the upward thermals rising from the warm technological conduits below. They hunt by sound, their head-crests vibrating in tune with the acoustic thunder of the reed-fields.

The air smells of ozone, cold cedar, dry frankincense, and the faint, bitter tang of friction-heated mineral dust. 

As you move through the landscape, the scale reveals its emptiness. A single urban spire might stand half a mile tall, surrounded by three miles of gray, wind-scoured thorn-scrub that has stood unchanged for a hundred centuries. Every surface bears the smoothing mark of eons of dry wind erosion. Sharp edges have been ground down into flowing, aerodynamic curves; ancient architectural pylons have fused with the petrified trunks they were built against, until it is impossible to tell where the engineered physics ends and the botanical skeletal structure begins.

The light never brightens beyond a muted, perpetual dusk. A distant, small sun sits low on the horizon, shrouded by high cirrus clouds of dry crystal dust that scatter the light into long, cool beams of ash-gray and faded lilac. In this dimness, the world feels endlessly vast, wildly noisy, and profoundly old—an ecosystem that has forgotten water, perfected its machinery, and settled into a endless, resonant twilight.
