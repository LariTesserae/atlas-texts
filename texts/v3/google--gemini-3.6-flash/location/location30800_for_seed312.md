The range does not end at the horizon; it becomes the horizon. These are young mountains, fractured rather than worn, their spine a continuous serration of slate, basalt, and pale granite. The peaks have not yet learned how to crumble into gentle slopes; they fall away in clean, vertical planes, thousands of feet of shear unmarred by soil, leaving edges so sharp they seem to slice the thin, freezing air. At this altitude, the sky is a deep, unclouded indigo, and the sunlight is absolute—blinding where it strikes the frost-draped stone, pitch-black where the shadows fall.

Cold dominates everything. It is a dry, biting cold that preserves wood and stiffens leather within hours, though moisture persists in quiet pockets. In the lee of the cliffs, where the sun thaws the upper snowpacks for an hour at noon, thin threads of meltwater run down the rock faces, freezing into glassy sheets by mid-afternoon. Hoarfrost coats the underside of every overhang, and thick banks of cloud drift through the lower passes like slow-moving rivers, leaving a wet, icy film on every exposed surface before dissolving into the air.

Clinging to these jagged fissures is a city of staggering scale and density. Lacking iron or complex machinery, its builders have achieved metropolis-scale urbanization through the sheer, patient multiplication of simple forms. Millions of tons of dark slate have been quarried by hand, split along natural fault lines, and dry-stacked into immense, stepped terraces that warp around the mountain's contours. 

The settlement grows vertically, a dense honeycomb of stone dwellings leaning directly against one another for warmth and structural support. Roofs serve as the streets for the level above. Alleyways are narrow, cold corridors barely wide enough for two people to pass, spanned by log beams that support upper floors or brace opposing walls against the mountain’s shift. Long stairways cut straight into the living rock connect the tiers, their steps worn smooth in the center by centuries of leather-soled boots. Bridges made of braided hemp and split-juniper trunks swing across the narrower gorges, creaking under the weight of foot traffic, linking one mountain shoulder to the next.

Despite the harshness of the climate and the poverty of the soil—limited to patches of orange lichen, alpine moss, and scrubby, twisted birch creeping out of cliff-side cracks—the region is thick with animal life. The stone city is as much a habitat for fauna as it is for its human builders. Millions of high-altitude birds—choughs with bright yellow beaks, heavy-chested cliff swallows, and solitary snow-hawks—nest in the artificial crannies of the dry-stone walls. Their droppings streak the dark slate with white, and the air above the canyon streets is filled with the silent, crisscrossing shadows of their wings.

On the lower ledges and along the narrow grazing paths that trace the cliffs, herds of thick-coated, mountain-adapted ungulates move with sure-footed patience. Their hooves clatter softly against the flags as they are driven through the city’s lower thoroughfares. In the quiet shadows of the lower terraces, large, pale dogs with woolly coats sleep curled on sun-warmed stone, barely raising an eye as people carry bundles of dry scrub-wood or skin water-bags past them.

The technology here is ancient and tactile. Buildings are held together by gravity, timber-ties, and carefully placed keystone arches. Water is collected in deep, stone-lined cisterns carved directly into the mountain’s cold core, fed by channels that capture the daily thaw. Lighting comes from shallow clay dishes filled with rendered animal fat, their weak, flickering flames smelling of tallow and pine smoke. Heavy curtains of felted wool hang over doorways to seal out the draft, and the primary tools are wooden levers, hemp ropes, bone needles, and stone hammers with hardwood handles.

For all its physical density, the landscape is strangely quiet. The cold, thin air does not carry sound far. The roar of a distant landslide down a far valley arrives only as a faint, muffled thrum beneath the soles of one's feet. The dominant sounds are immediate and small: the rhythmic *clack-clack* of a stone-cutter’s hammer high on a new terrace; the rustle of stiff wool against stone; the sudden, dry *swish* of a flock of finches rising from an alley; and the constant, low whistle of the high winds scouring the peaks far above the inhabited clefts.

Nothing here moves quickly. The city expands stone by stone, terrace by terrace, over generations. The ice creeps down the shadow-gullies by millimeters a month. The people move with a measured, economical stride, saving their breath in the thin air, building a sprawling, enduring civilization out of raw rock, cold sun, and the endless, silent endurance of the high places.
