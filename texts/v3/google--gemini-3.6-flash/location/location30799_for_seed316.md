The horizon here does not exist; it was ground down long ago and mixed with the rain. 

What remains is a planetary floor so old that every mountain has been reduced to warm, thumb-smoothed basalt under miles of churning mud. It is a world without air gaps. The space between the sky and the silt is filled with an atmosphere so thick with vapor, suspended clay, and hot, atomized brine that breathing it feels like inhaling warm grease. The light never rises above a dull, bruised ochre—a perpetual twilight filtered through fifty leagues of cloud-soup that stays trapped beneath an invisible ceiling of atmospheric pressure.

Everything is warm. Not the sharp heat of a desert, but a heavy, feverish 95 degrees that rises from the core through fissures in the ocean-flat stone. It is a wet warmth that rots nothing because there is almost nothing left to rot; the few plants that exist are not green, but pale, rubbery tubes—hardly larger than fingers—that sprout from the tops of submerged stone knobs, pumping mineralized liquid directly back into the fog before being torn away by the churning ground.

And the ground is always churning. The land does not sit still; it moves with a violent, thick-bodied turbulence that operates on the scale of entire continents. Great waves of heavy liquid clay, fifty yards high and twenty miles wide, roll over one another in complete silence until they collide with the ancient, salt-drowned roots of dead plateaus. When they hit, the sound is deafening—a continuous, low-frequency roar that vibrates the marrow inside a bone before it reaches the ear. It sounds like the world is chewing its own teeth.

There is no room in this landscape for void. The mud is packed against the stone, the water is packed into the mud, the air is packed with water, and the sound is packed into the air until the ambient pressure makes the ears bleed a slow, thin pink fluid that thickens into jelly before it drops.

In this place, reality has grown tired of holding its rules rigid. Because the landscape has been reduced to its absolute minimum components—water, stone, heat, and time—the physics have begun to fray at the edges, like old wool soaked in lye. In certain wide, flat basins where the mud settles for a few hours, the water begins to flow upward in fat, transparent columns, rising twenty feet into the fog before breaking into warm, horizontal spray. In other areas, the density of the soup becomes so great that light bends into tight circles; a traveler looking straight ahead sees the back of their own mud-caked heels walking twenty paces in front of them, suspended in the amber gloom.

Occasionally, the land tries to remember what a structure was. Out in the vast, roaring middle-flats, where the liquid clay is deepest, the churning will suddenly stop over an area the size of a city. The mud will stiffen, crackle with static discharges that smell like ozone and scorched fat, and rise into thousands of identical, sharp-angled pillars—resembling the pilings of a vast, forgotten pier or the ribs of an unmade temple. They stand for three minutes, dripping yellow water into the mist, while a high, screaming whistle rises from the crevices between them. Then the turbulence returns with a wet, crushing slam, and the pillars are pulled back under, dissolved into the infinite slurry.

There are things built here, but they are not the work of hands. They are the calcified husks of old events. Every ten miles or so, rising out of the foam like broken molars, are blocks of black, vitrified salt—some as large as barns, others small as coffins. They are carved with deep, spiral grooves that were not cut, but grown; they are the petrified vents of ancient gas-geysers that died a million years ago. Now they serve only as anchor points for the mud, creating white-water eddies of orange silt that spin endlessly in the dim warmth.

Fauna is nearly absent, for there is nowhere to stand and nothing to hunt. But sometimes, deep beneath the sloshing surface of the clay, something massive moves with the slow, deliberate displacement of a shifting tectonic plate. You do not see it; you see the surface of the mud rise six feet over a mile-wide swath, holding its smooth, curved shape for a long, breath-holding minute while the deafening roar of the landscape drops an octave into something like a groan. Then the mound sinks. A single, blind eye—the size of a millstone, covered in thick grey cataract-skin—might roll to the surface, stare into the amber gloom for three seconds as the warm rain washes over it, and then vanish back into the endless, heavy meat of the world.

The sound never stops. It is the noise of an engine that has lost its oil but has so much momentum it cannot halt. It is the sound of ten thousand waterfalls crashing into tubs of tallow; the sound of the sky tearing along an old seam; the sound of stone being ground back into the primordial grease from which it was first cooled. 

If you stand on one of the salt-stumps and look out into the brown fog, you do not feel that the world is dying. You feel that it died an eternity ago, but forgot to clear away the floor, and now the floor is endlessly re-arranging its own dust in the dark, warm wetness, waiting for a sky that will never open.
