The floor of the basin has no dirt. It is a world carved from unmoistened, raw-edged basalt and sharp, pale gypsum that has never felt the smoothing stroke of wind or age. We are six hundred feet below the sky, inside a vaulted rift six miles wide, but there is no darkness here; the ceiling is a solid, burning seam of white-hot quartz and phosphor-chert that casts an unblinking, midday glare across the entire floor, turning the wet rock into a field of blinding mirrors.

It is warm—a thick, animal warmth like the interior of a lung. Water does not fall as rain, but wells up through hairline cracks in the unworked floor, bubbling quietly into hot, shallow saucers before evaporating back into the heavy air. Every flat surface sweats. Droplets hang from the underside of razor-sharp mineral shelves, dripping onto the backs of the things below with a steady, rhythmic *tink-tink-tink* that is entirely drowned out by the roar of life.

There are no leaves, no mosses, no ferns, nor anything that feeds on sunlight. The landscape is pure stone and pure flesh. 

The fauna is a storm. It is impossible to walk three paces without stepping on something that squirms, clicks, or splits underfoot. Thousands of pale, hard-shelled organisms—some the size of walnuts, others long as a man’s forearm, with hundreds of tiny, glass-like oars—paddle frantically through the shallow thermal pools, churning the mineral water into a gray foam. Above them, suspended between the jagged floor and the glowing vault, fly clouds of thick-bodied, blind creatures with skin like wet bladder-wrack, beating six leather-thin wings to keep themselves aloft in the muggy updrafts. They collide mid-air by the hundreds, dropping like heavy fruits onto the rock, only to be instantly set upon by writhing carpets of legged scavengers that strip them to pale cartilage in seconds.

Everything moves with an urgent, hyperactive twitching. There is no rest in the basin. The sound is an overwhelming wall of friction: the dry rasp of millions of chitinous bellies dragging over crystalline slag, the wet slap of soft bodies diving into salt-seams, the sudden, popping bursts of pressurized gas escaping through mud-plastered burrows, and the endless, clicking chatter of mandibles scraping the raw rock for bacterial crust.

Here and there, sticking out of the shifting sea of bodies, are the marks of people. 

They are small, crude things—marks of an early, desperate hand. Primitive frames made of lashed jawbones and thick, fibrous intestinal cords are staked into the rock to anchor heavy, flat-bottomed wicker baskets. These baskets collect the soft, pale flyers as they fall from the air. There are no towns, only scattered encampments perched on the highest, sharpest ridges where the swarming floor-beasts cannot easily climb. These shelters are built from piled slabs of unshaped field-gypsum, sealed against the damp heat with thick pastes made of crushed shell and animal grease. 

Near the edges of a steaming salt-run, two figures crouch in the white glare. They wear no woven cloth; their aprons are made of cured, scaled hide, stiffened with salt. One holds a heavy wedge of volcanic glass, bound to a river-smoothed bone handle with dried tendon, and uses it to methodically chip away at the hinge of a giant, shell-less mollusk pinned against the stone. They do not talk—they cannot be heard over the racket of the pit—but communicate through sharp, practiced jerks of the chin and hand. Behind them, leaning against a cairn of un-eroded granite, are long pikes tipped with knapped flint, used to fend off the larger, legless predators that slide out of the deep fissures when the water pockets rise.

The geology itself seems unready, still cooling and splitting under the stress of its own birth. Every few minutes, a sound like a cannon shot echoes from beneath the basin floor as a new crack tears through a gypsum bed, sending a geyser of lukewarm, mineral-rich mud fifty feet into the air. Within seconds, the cloud of leather-winged flyers swarms the new vent, fighting for the rich, sulfurous moisture, their bodies casting a momentary, frantic shadow over the sunlit stone below.

It is a place without history, because nothing lasts long enough to age. The rock is too young to have worn smooth; the artifacts are too frail to survive the damp, salt-laden air; and the life is too dense, too loud, and too fast to leave anything behind except a thick, white carpet of crushed bone that the next rising seam will swallow whole.
