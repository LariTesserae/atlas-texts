Below the grade of the outer world lies the Grand Sump of the Kiln-Vaults, a single enclosed hollow no larger than a parish church, built entirely from the hands of dead bricklayers. 

There is no soil here, no bedded granite or natural cave-wall. Every vertical and horizontal plane is made of fired red clay, yellow terracotta tile, or dark, iron-rich mortar. Centuries of friction have rounded every edge. The steps of the spiral treads that cling to the walls are scooped deep like old wooden bowls, worn down by the passage of thousands of bare, wet soles. The pillars supporting the low, ribbed vaults have lost their sharp corners; they are smooth, fatty, and dark, polished by decades of brushed shoulders, oil spills, and the damp bellies of beasts.

The air inside is thick and warm, hovering at the temperature of a fresh loaf of bread. It smells of wet baked clay, rendered tallow, bitter lichen, and the sharp, vinegar-sweat of warm bodies at work. Light enters from above in long, solid-looking shafts through three narrow, iron-grated lanterns set into the apex of the roof vaults. These shafts cut through a perpetual haze of suspended dust, steam, and wing-lint, illuminating the space in slanting bands of rich, butter-yellow daylight. Where the light strikes the floor, the damp tiles glisten like oilcloth.

The space is packed with extraordinary density. Because the footprint is small—perhaps forty paces across—life has expanded vertically, filling every cubic foot between the floor tiles and the ceiling ribs. A maze of scaffolding made from stout ash poles, lashings of oiled hemp, and notched cypress knees forms three tiers of galleries above the ground. Hanging from these scaffolds are hundreds of wicker cages, clay jars, drying racks, and hand-spun hammocks. Nothing is still. 

Water does not flow in rivers here; it moves in a managed, sputtering cycle. Up in the darkness of the high archways, deep-fired terracotta gutters catch the perpetual condensation that forms on the ceiling bricks. The water drips with a rhythmic, deafening *clack-clack-clack* onto copper-sheathed wooden paddles below, driving tiny, chattering splash-wheels that lift small leather buckets. These buckets spill into shallow ceramic troughs routed along the gallery railings. The runoff feeds narrow, green-filmed basins on the lower floor where long-legged subterranean fowl splash and pick at water-beetles. Moisture is everywhere—in beads on the painted clay faces of wall-gods, in the damp sheen on the wooden walkways, in the dark slickness of the mortar joints—but it never pools deeply. It is a world of damp surfaces and sudden drops, not of standing pools.

The noise is a heavy, reverberating roar, trapped and amplified by the curved brick overhead. The clatter of the splash-wheels joins the endless screech of ungreased wooden winches raising baskets of dried dung and clay pipes. Added to this is the dry, papery rustle of millions of dark-shelled grain-beetles clicking against one another in their hanging clay urns, where they are cultivated for their oil. Flocks of small, pale swallows, blind in one eye from generations in the vault-twilight, nest in the hollow brick niches of the ceiling; they dive through the yellow light-beams with sharp, metallic chirps, hunting the gnats that breed in the drip-troughs.

In the lower tier, short-legged men and women in stiff leather aprons move through the narrow aisles between the brick vats. Their tools are simple, heavy, and silent compared to the space: wooden mallets, scrapers forged from soft hammered bronze, baskets woven from split reeds, and bone needles. They scrape the white saltpeter from the lower walls, clear the drainage spouts with flexible willow rods, and feed handfuls of crushed fern-pulp to the heavy, fat-bodied salamanders that live in the warm, wet crevices beneath the lower vats. These creatures, pale as lard and thick as a man’s thigh, shift lazily in their brick cubbies, their external gills fanning the damp air with a soft *whish-whish*.

Vegetation is sparse, unable to take deep root on the impervious ceramic surfaces, but it clings stubbornly to the wet mortar joints. Strips of dark, emerald-green liverwort outline the base of every pillar, and a pale, velvet-like moss wraps the damp wooden legs of the high gantries. In small, clay-walled boxes hung from the mid-tier railings, dark-leaved garlic and bitter cress grow under the direct path of the sun-shafts, their roots tangled in crushed brick-dust and composted bat manure.

Everything in the vault is in a state of active, settled maintenance. When a brick finally spalls from the moist heat, it is cut out with bronze chisels, ground into powder on the stone floors, mixed with lime, and slapped back into the void before the vault can settle. The environment is completely manufactured, yet it behaves like a jungle—sweating, chattering, recycling its own waste through terracotta pipes and wooden scoops, contained entirely within its ancient, smooth-skinned box of baked earth.
