At this altitude, the sky is not blue but a deep, bruised violet, scrubbed clean by thin air that tastes faintly of iron and frozen grease. The light does not fall; it strikes. It is a blinding, white-hot glare that pours down from an unmoving sun, pinning the mountain pass in a state of permanent, shadowless noon. Yet for all the brightness, the air remains relentlessly cold—a dry, crisp chill that keeps the sweat from ever leaving the skin, freezing it into a thin salt-crust on the back of the neck.

The terrain is an immense, tilted table of pale, wind-scoured granite and grey slate, carved millions of years ago into long, parallel flutes and hollow bowls. The erosion here is old, completed. The edges of the stone are not jagged but rounded like water-worn teeth, polished by centuries of static pressure until the rock looks like fat or pared bone. Miles of open space stretch between the major landforms—great, empty amphitheatres of grey shale where nothing grows except a thin, black lichen that smells of damp wool when trodden on.

There is a strange, persistent dampness that sits over everything despite the lack of clouds. Moisture does not fall as rain; it seeps out of the rock itself. Microscopic beads of condensation continuously form on the underside of every overhang, running down the smooth granite flutes in thin, quivering threads to collect in shallow, crystal-clear basins no larger than a palm. The water is ice-cold, mineral-heavy, and leaves a chalky rim where it slowly evaporates into the thin air.

What dominates this place—what crushes it—is the sound.

It is a deafening, continuous roar that does not waver by a single decibel. It is not the sound of wind, for the air here is strangely still, locked in a rigid, thermodynamic frozenness. The noise comes from the stone itself and the life that clings to it. The massive, wind-carved channels in the higher peaks act as giant, acoustic pipes, catching a high-altitude thermal stream that never shifts its course, humming with a sub-bass thrum that vibrates the marrow in human legs. Overlaid upon this bass is the endless, shrill scream of millions of slate-beetles—hard-shelled, silver-backed creatures the size of a thumb that nest in the damp rock-cracks. They do not sleep, because the sun never sets, and so their stridulation is a unbroken, metallic screech that fills the valley from wall to wall.

Despite the poverty of the soil and the lack of green—there are only scattered, wire-like tussocks of iron-grass rooted in the deepest crevices—fauna thrives here in vast, quiet numbers. Thousands of pale, long-necked grazers with padded, broad feet move across the stone in loose, widely spaced herds. They do not eat plants; they lick the black lichen from the rock face, their broad, rasping tongues making a wet, rhythmic *slap-scrape* that is swallowed instantly by the roaring air. Small, featherless flyers with leathery wings the color of wet slate leap from the high ledges, banking hard through the thin air to snatch beetles from the rock face before dropping back into the shadowless hollows.

Human presence is faint, almost erased by the scale of the stone, but it exists. There are no huts, no stacked-stone walls, no roads. The people who pass through here build nothing that stands higher than a knee, knowing that to interrupt the airflow is to draw down the crushing acoustic resonance of the peaks. Instead, they live in the natural undercut flutes of the granite.

Near a damp basin at the edge of a shale field lies a temporary camp. It consists of nothing more than three large, dried hides of the long-necked grazers, stretched flat over the slick stone and held down by heavy river-pebbles brought up from the lower valleys. On one hide sits a set of tools: a heavy hammerstone of red jasper, three carefully struck flakes of black volcanic glass with edges keen enough to split a hair, and a half-carved needle made from a thick limb-bone. Nearby, a shallow groove in the granite has been used to crush the silver beetle-shells into a thick, shimmering grease used to coat leather against the cold damp.

There are no fire pits. Wood does not grow here, and the air is too thin for a easy flame. The people eat their meat raw, cured in the blinding sun until it turns black and stiff as ash-wood, washed down with the cold condensation scooped from the stone bowls using hollowed horn-tips.

Nothing changes here. The sun remains fixed at its high, hard angle. The roar of the stone pipes and the screaming beetles never dips. The dampness does not dry; the cold does not deepen into frost. It is a world carved down to its bones, bright, loud, and entirely indifferent, operating on a clock that stopped ticking long before the first stone was struck against another.
