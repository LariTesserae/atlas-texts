The sky above the plateau is a pale, merciless silver, holding the sun at its apex with the rigid clarity of a furnace door flung wide. At this altitude—miles above any sea that might still exist elsewhere—the air is thin, scorching, and so dry that sweat would evaporate before reaching the surface of the skin, if there were any skin here to sweat. 

There is no water. Not in the soil, not in the atmosphere, not in the deep fissures that slice through the ironstone bedrock. The landscape is a high, baking expanse of oxidized minerals, shattered shale, and vast fields of pale grey silt that glitter under the glare like ground zinc. Wind has carved this high country for millions of years, wearing the jagged uplift down into long, smooth, parabolic ridges and hollow spires that whistle when the thermal drafts rise from the canyon floors. It is a land deeply settled in its own erosion—nothing here is fresh or crumbling suddenly; every edge has been softened by eons of dry, abrasive dust into forms that resemble the exhaust manifolds of ancient engines or the curved housings of colossal turbines.

And yet, despite the total absence of soil and moisture, the ground is crawling. 

It is swarming with life, though not life as a forest or prairie understands it. Without a single blade of grass or patch of lichen, the high plateau is packed edge-to-edge with an overwhelming, grinding tide of industrial fauna. They are non-biological, or perhaps biological in a syntax that bypasses flesh entirely. Millions upon millions of creature-entities cover the rock: iron-shelled quadrupeds the size of steam boilers, skittering swarms of copper-legged arachnids that click like high-speed relays, and long, segmented millipede-like constructs forged from pitted cast-iron that drag themselves through the mineral silt.

They do not graze; they do not drink. They draw power from the static charge of the dry air, from the thermal differential between the baking rock surface and the cold vacuum-adjacent upper sky, and from the intense, crushing friction of their own endless movement. 

The sound is immense. It is a deafening, continuous roar that shakes the granite underfoot: a chorus of clanking piston-joints, the shrieking whine of dry steel gears, the metallic scraping of millions of serrated carapace-plates, and the rhythmic, deep-bass thrumming of combustion-hearted mega-fauna buried half-deep in the dry dust lakes. The noise travels through the thin air with violent clarity, a industrial symphony generated by an ecosystem without a single factory, human, or blueprint.

There are no buildings. No roads, no ruins, no pipes, no poured concrete. The landscape is entirely wild, unbuilt, and primordial, yet its wilderness operates on the logic of a heavy-machinery assembly line left to mutate in isolation for a billion years.

As you look farther across the regional sweep of the plateau, reality begins to slip sideways under the weight of the heat and the strange, mechanical ecology. 

Space here does not hold its distance cleanly. The far horizon, where wind-polished basalt spires pierce the glare, appears visually to be miles away, yet the sound of a gear-shelled behemoth scraping its flank against a spire arrives instantaneously, hard and sharp against your ears, as if it were three feet distant. Shadows cast by the burning sun do not follow the shapes of the entities that cast them; instead, they stretch out into long, flat silhouettes of spinning flywheels and reciprocating rods, moving at right angles to the creatures themselves. In certain low basins, light bends into rigid, parallel beams, shearing the glare into bright, geometric ribbons that click audibly as the dry air passes through them.

Time moves with a heavy, dazed slowness. Despite the frantic, ceaseless vibration of the swarms, the overall tide of the landscape hardly changes from hour to hour. A herd of tripod-walkers, their copper-jointed limbs lifting and falling with the measured thump of pile-drivers, may take three days to cross a single dry ravine. The heat glues everything into a slow-motion crawling state. The sun seems to linger indefinitely near the noon mark, casting short, hard-edged shadows that twitch like second-hands on a broken clock.

The fauna density in the low dry troughs is so thick that the creatures climb over one another in living, metallic mounds twenty feet high, their friction generating arcs of blue static electricity that crackle into the dry air like miniature lightning. Above them, high-altitude scavengers—broad, solar-sailed gliders made of stretched, mica-thin membranes and spring-steel ribbing—circle in slow, silent thermal spirals, waiting for a boiler-beast to seize up, overheat, and burst into a shower of white-hot mineral sparks and dry, unlubricated ball-bearings.

Everything is dry, hot, loud, and impossibly persistent. The plateau extends for endless miles in all directions, an open, elevated world of red dust, polished iron, burning light, and swarming, self-generating mechanics—an industrial wasteland that never saw an industry, thriving in the empty sky.
