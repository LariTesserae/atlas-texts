The ledge is no wider than the floor of a ruined cathedral, suspended six thousand meters above an endless bed of violet silt. Up here, on the High Horn of Silt-Rim, there is no dampness, no dew, no rain. Liquid is an unknown state of matter. The world breathes in hot, bone-dry thermals that rise from the abyss below, carrying a constant, fine grit that acts like grain-grit sandpaper, polishing every surface to the texture of carved tooth.

Despite the total absence of water, the ledge is choked with growth. It is a lushness born of lipids, silica, and compressed gas. Huge, ribbed accordion-succulents—the *bellow-pith*—stand twelve paces high, their skin like cured pig-iron, expanding and collapsing with deafening *whump-clack* sounds as they gulp the dry, warm updrafts. Stringy, rust-red tendrils, thick as ship’s cables and dry as parchment, loop from rock-face to rock-face, vibrating in the wind like the heavy bass strings of an untuned instrument. Between them grow broad, bowl-shaped carpets of yellow friction-crust, brittle as mica, which spark faintly when stepped on, emitting a smell like scorched hair and dry cinnamon.

The density of objects is surprisingly loose—there is room to walk, wide avenues of wind-swept slate between the mammoth plants—but the vertical space is hyperactive, crowded, and violently alive.

The light never rises above a bruised ochre twilight. A thick overhead ceiling of suspended mineral dust cuts the sun to a dull copper coin, casting long, soft-edged shadows that never quite move, because the sun cannot be seen to travel behind the soup of the upper air.

Yet nothing here is still. The dynamic is terrifyingly kinetic, a perpetual storm without rain. The wind does not blow in a steady stream; it boils up from the chasm in rhythmic, violent pulses. Every few seconds, a gust strikes the ledge from below, snapping the tendrils taut, forcing the bellow-piths to slam shut with thunderous slaps, and blowing clouds of dry, powdery tallow-spores into the air. 

These spores are the currency of the fauna, which swarms through the tumult. Thousands of heavy, triangular beetles with shells like polished jet cling to the lee-side of the basalt pillars, their serrated legs scraping against the rock in a loud, synchronized rasping that sounds like iron file on slate. Ribbon-snakes—flat, five-meter-long strips of dry, iridescent scale with no eyes and dozens of buzzing propulsion-frills—ride the thermal eddies in wild, chaotic loops, snapping at the air-borne tallow-spores and each other. They collide in mid-air with loud, leathery thuds, tangling into writhing knots before falling, uncoiling, and shooting upward again on the next hot surge.

The sound is immense, relentless, and layered:
The low, bass drone of the wind through wind-carved flutes in the granite wall.
The rhythmic, metallic *slap-whump* of the inflating plant sacs.
The high, dry screech of the ribbon-snakes.
The dry, percussive crack of seed-cases bursting open, throwing tiny, glass-hard beads against the rock like handfuls of gravel thrown against a wall.

Human presence—or the presence of something that uses hands—is faint, ancient, and sparse. High on the cliff wall, where the basalt has been carved smooth by wind into deep, hollow niches, small primitive perches hang. They are made from the rib-bones of large chasm-gliders, lashed together with dried gut-thongs and pegged directly into natural fissures with fire-hardened wooden wedges. 

Near the base of a giant bellow-pith lies an abandoned tool kit: three heavy scrapers fashioned from chipped quartz, their edges dulled from peeling tallow-crust from the plants, and a hollow bone tube used to siphon the volatile, dry oils from the plant cores before they burst. There are no metal fittings, no complex joinery, no written marks—only simple, repeated notches carved into the quartz handles, perhaps counting wind-cycles or yields of tallow.

Near the southern lip of the shelf, where the drop is sheer and absolute, a small cairn of wind-smoothed pebbles sits in a shallow depression. It is anchored against the storm-winds by a thick coating of baked plant-grease, rubbed over the stones by hand until it hardened into a dull, amber resin. Embedded in the top of the cairn is a single, massive glider-jaw, its serrated teeth pointing out toward the violet void, whistling a piercing, single-note tone whenever the updraft hits a certain speed.

Everything here has been smoothed by time, yet everything is mid-explosion. The rocks are ancient, established, and worn to soft curves by a million years of dust, but the life upon them is in a state of frenzied, high-temperature iteration—growing, snapping, rubbing, and seeding in the dry, roaring twilight.
