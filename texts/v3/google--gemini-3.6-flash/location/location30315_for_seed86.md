It sits two thousand feet below the upper basalt shelf, a collapsed fault-valley so broad that the opposite wall dissolves into a blue-grey haze of hanging damp. The fracture is young in rock time. Its edges have not been rounded by frost or swept clean by rivers; the cliffs are sheared cleanly, showing black, glassy faces where the basalt cleaved, with talus piles at the base composed of raw, razored slabs that clatter like broken china underfoot. 

No sunlight falls directly into the basin. Light here is a single, immovable state: a dim, cold violet-grey that leaks down through high, choked ceiling rifts miles above. It never shifts. Morning does not come, and evening does not fall; the glow is held forever in the suspension of mist that hangs between the vault and the floor. The temperature remains fixed at forty-four degrees, cold enough to turn a breath to a thin ghost that hangs in the still air before dissolving into the general damp.

Water does not run in beds or torrents. Instead, the basin sweats. Cold condensation gathers on the ceilings and drops continuously in heavy, fat beads that strike the stone below with a dull *plink-plink*. Water weeps from the fresh faults, coating the black rock in a mirror-thin sheath that makes every flat surface gleam under the ambient gloom. Where the floor dips into shallow troughs, cold water collects in clear, gravel-bottomed pools no deeper than a man’s hand, perfectly still until a hoof or a beak breaks the surface.

Plants are rare, timid things here. They do not cover the ground; they only inhabit its wounds. In the narrow, dark fissures where the damp is thickest, a pale, thread-like lichen grows in flat, spreading maps that feel like cold rubber to the touch. A few species of blind, pale-stemmed ferns stick out horizontally from the cliff seams, their fronds translucent and veinless, swaying slightly in the low thermal drafts generated by the breathing of millions of bodies.

For all its barren stone and sparse green, the valley is deafeningly, violently alive. 

The fauna fills the scale of the void completely. High on the sharp spires and along the narrow, un-eroded ledges, countless colonies of pale-feathered cave-fowl nest in terrifying concentration. Their droppings coat the dark basalt in chalky streaks, and their voice is a single, unending roar—a harsh, rhythmic rasping of millions of throats that bounces off the hard granite faces until the rock itself seems to vibrate. Below them, on the jagged floor of the basin, vast herds of thick-coated, slate-grey ungulates move through the boulders. They are broad-shouldered, short-legged creatures with heavy, downward-curving horns used to scrape lichen from the sharp rock. They do not sleep in unison; at any hour, thousands are moving, their sharp cloven hooves ringing on the flinty stone like hammers on anvils, while others lie wedged into rock hollows, their heavy rumination adding a deep, bass hum to the din.

In the shallow condensation pools, thousands of long-legged, blind waders move on slender stalks, spear-checking the clear water for subterranean shrimp. Small, dark-furred quadrupeds run between the legs of the larger beasts, squealing and fighting over dropped eggs or scraps of moss. The air is never clear of wings; thick clouds of heavy, beetle-like insects tumble through the gloom, their hard wing-cases clicking against the stone walls as they are swept along by the rising warmth of the herds.

Humans—or whatever shapes have found their way into this floor—have left almost no scar upon it. There are no walls, no hearths, no dry-laid stones. The valley is too hard, too wet, and too loud for settled life. Yet in the shelter of a massive overhanging slab, where the dripping ceiling is interrupted by a seam of dry chert, there are signs of hands. 

A pile of broken ungulate limb-bones lies stacked against the rock, each cracked neatly lengthwise with a heavy stone to yield the cold marrow within. Beside them sit a dozen hand-axes knapped from the local black basalt—heavy, crude tools with sharp, jagged edges that match the fresh fractures of the valley walls. They are not polished; they are functional, cold, and wet with dew. Nearby, a length of twisted gut, stiffened by the cold, is tied around a heavy granite wedge driven into a crack—a simple tether left behind by hunters who moved through the noise, took what they could carry, and climbed back up toward the surface.

Nothing changes here. The roar of the nesting fowl does not swell at noon or quiet at midnight, because there is no noon and no midnight. The damp does not dry; the cold does not bite harder; the sharp edges of the basalt slabs do not dull. The valley exists in a frozen, roaring equilibrium—an endless, cold room full of stone, sweat, and animal breath, echoing beneath the earth without beginning or end.
