The floor is fired terracotta, pressed by hand while soft and set into narrow, hexagonal tiles that meet at razor-sharp edges. There is no mortar between them, only a fine, dry silt the color of pulverized bone, packed so tightly by thumb-pressure that the seams feel like scabbed cuts.

This is a sunken atrium, thirty paces across, surrounded by six terraced walls of dark, sun-baked clay. Every surface has been shaped by hand-tools—the scrape-marks of flint blades and the shallow, gouged crescents of bone chisels are still raw and unworn. Nothing here has been smoothed by rain or rounded by wind. The edges of the steps are as crisp as folded paper, casting thin, ruled lines of ink-dark shadow across the lower levels.

There is no water in this place. It does not exist as a liquid, a vapor, or a memory. The air carries the sharp, toaster-warm scent of scorched clay and dried pith. Heat lives in the tiles, radiating upward in a steady, motionless bath that keeps the small basin at a constant, fever-like warmth.

Despite the absolute dryness, the atrium is choked with growth.

Rising from the joints between the tiles are dense thickets of papery, jointed reeds—dry as parchment, pale as boiled horn. They do not drink; they feed on the heat of the clay and the low, static pull of the atmosphere. Their leaves are thin, translucent vanes of layered mica and dried fiber that click against one another with the sound of shuffling cards when the air expands. Above them hang heavy, basket-like globes woven from dry tendon and willow-pith, suspended by braided cords from the upper terraces. These globes are bursting with spore-ferns—brittle, fan-shaped growths that shed an odorless, golden flour into the air. The dust collects in the corners of the clay steps in perfect, un-rippled drifts.

The life here is dense, layered, and silent.

In the lower terraces, thousands of small, dry-shelled creatures occupy every niche. There are mites the size of plums, covered in a velvet of stiff, copper-colored bristles, that crawl sluggishly across the vertical clay walls. They do not cling with suction or moisture; their legs end in microscopic, fire-hardened hooks that slot into the tiny chisel-gouges left by the builders. Above them, suspended among the mica-reeds, thin, jointed organisms made of chitin and hollow straw stretch six legs across impossible gaps.

The geography of the basin does not obey a single horizon.

A ladder made of notched ash-wood rests against the northern wall, but as you eye its rungs, the third rung appears to be farther away than the tenth. If you step onto the terrace, your foot settles on a surface that feels flat, yet your shadow falls at a forty-five-degree tilt, trailing across the air itself as if the space behind you were denser than the space ahead. Light—a steady, diffuse amber that seems to come from nowhere and everywhere at once—illuminates a row of carved clay niches along the west wall. Inside one niche sits a small, dried organism with four folded limbs and a skin like tanned pig-leather. It is sleeping, its sides expanding only once every few minutes with a dry, bellows-like hiss. If you look closely at the niche behind it, the interior does not end at the back wall; it opens into a second, identical atrium, perfectly visible, scaled down to the size of a walnut, complete with its own microscopic thickets of mica-reeds and tiny, copper-bristled mites moving across red tiles.

Nothing moves quickly. A large, wingless hopper—its body a lathe-turned cylinder of dried gall-nut and bone-plate—turns its head over the course of an hour, tracking the slow, unnatural rotation of a shadow cast by an unmoving pillar.

The craft of the place is everywhere, total and totalizing. There is no soil, no rock that was not mixed, kneaded, and fired by a human palm. The brackets holding the upper frames are made of horn, boiled soft and shaped with stone pegs. The ropes are hand-twisted from the outer rances of the papery reeds. Yet the hands that made this seem to have worked within a geometry that was already warping as the clay dried. A flight of five steps leads down into a basin that, when reached, is higher than the ledge you departed from. The transition is seamless; your knees do not register an incline, only the simple, persistent warmth of the red floor against the soles of your feet.

Sound is rare and muffled. The dry vegetation absorbs echo, deadening voices into flat, papery thuds. Now and then, a spore-pod on the upper terrace splits with a tiny *snick*, like a fingernail catching on a thread, and a fresh cloud of yellow flour settles over the back of a copper mite. The mite does not shake it off. It continues its slow, patient crawl across the sharp, unworn cut of a chisel-mark, moving toward a corner where the floor folds neatly into the ceiling without ever turning a corner.
