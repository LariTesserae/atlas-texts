The floor is not earth, but wood—a single, continuous expanse of ancient, compressed root-wood, worn so flat and polished by ages of trickling water that it resembles dark, oily slate. 

This is a sunken basin, bounded on all sides by living walls of dense ironwood and silver-fir trunks that press so tightly together they form a seamless, vertical timber face. Above, at the height of a cathedral nave, their branches intertwine into a solid, woven vault that lets through a cool, steady gray daylight. The space inside is immense in its emptiness: a single, vast room three hundred paces wide, devoid of underbrush, rocks, or soil. Aside from the perimeter walls and the ceiling overhead, the interior is nearly hollow, filled only with cool, moist air and the space between living surfaces.

In the center of this wooden basin sits a single piece of ancient machinery: a massive, cast-iron beam engine, broad as a barn and tall as a two-story house. It was not built here recently, nor is there any sign of human habitation surrounding it—no roads, no footpaths, no stone foundations. The engine rests directly on the petrified wood floor, its black iron casing pitted by thousands of years of moisture and wrapped in fine, hair-like tendrils of green moss that have grown into the grain of the metal without choking it.

The machine is running. It has always been running.

Driven by a deep geothermal steam head far below the root-plate, its central walking beam rises and falls with a heavy, deafening rhythm: *thump-clack... hissed-chug... thump-clack*. The sound fills the entire wooden vault, bouncing off the dense bark walls until the air itself vibrates. Iron pipes, thick as oak trunks and green with verdigris, project from the machine's flank, running along the polished floor and disappearing directly into the living timber walls. 

These pipes carry water—cool, clear, and iron-tasting. Every few seconds, tiny copper release valves along the pipe runs vent small puffs of pale steam into the cool air. The steam condenses instantly against the ceiling, gathering on the underside of the broad leaves until it falls back to the floor in a scattered, perpetual drizzle. The moisture pools in tiny, shallow grooves worn into the wood—microscopic rivers that flow across the flat basin toward the walls, where the roots quietly drink it back up.

Despite the roar of the engine and the hiss of the vents, the basin is alive with animals that pay the noise no mind. Thousands of small, grey-winged swifts nest in the furrows of the high timber walls, darting through the wide, empty air space in frantic, sweeping arcs to snap at midges. Heavy, horn-shelled beetles with backs as shiny as wet coal crawl along the iron seams of the engine, scraping at the layer of algae that forms near the warm piston housings. Down in the shallow, water-worn grooves of the floor, small, pale salamanders move slowly through the dampness, completely indifferent to the rhythmic thrum trembling through the wood beneath them.

The air is cool, carrying the sharp, clean smell of bruised pine, cold iron, and wet peat. Nothing shifts or decays here in any noticeable way. The trees do not grow taller; their wood has reached an ancient, hardened equilibrium with the iron engine. The engine does not rust away; its iron is kept oiled by the plant fats secreted by the mosses clinging to its joints. The light remains uniform, a flat, bright daylight filtering down through the green ceiling, casting no harsh shadows. 

It is a closed system, ancient and settled into a final, unyielding state. The great iron beam rises, pauses, and falls. The condensation gathers, drips, and runs. The swifts loop through the tall, empty chamber. The sound is deafening, constant, and entirely unchanged.
