It is an enclosure no larger than a cathedral’s courtyard, buried beneath twenty yards of pale salt-crust, but inside, it feels vast—an illusion carved out by sheer emptiness and blinding light. 

The space is a oval sun-well, a sunken pocket in a dry, low-lying chalk basin. Its roof is not sky, but a translucent lens of amber-colored selenite, naturally fused into a dome. Through this sheet, the daylight is amplified into an incandescent, shadowless glare that fills the hollow with a dry, baking heat—warm as a oven door left ajar, constant and unvarying.

There is no moisture here. Not a drop of dew, no dampness in the soil, no sweat in the air. The chemical ledger of this pocket runs entirely on nitrogen dust, calcified minerals, and solar radiation. Yet the ground is smothered in a thick, choked green.

It is a dry lushness. Covering the floor, climbing the walls, and choking the carved stone terraces is a dense, hair-like forest of glass-cacti and pith-reeds. They do not hold water; their stems are filled with a brittle, starch-dry foam that rattles when brushed. Their leaves are sheets of silvered parchment, crisp as mica, folded into intricate heat-radiating vanes. Above them hang heavy, velvet-textured tapestries of sulfur-moss that draw their sustenance directly from the hot, ionized air. The foliage grows rapidly, creeping across every surface in a frenzy of dry biomass, shedding powdery yellow spores that float like micro-ember storms in the light.

And through this dense, bone-dry jungle runs a city.

The urban layout is unambiguous: a tightly planned, multi-layered grid of streets, plazas, watchtowers, and step-terraces, all meticulously hand-carved from the soft, pale marl of the basin floor. The architecture is ancient and worn—edges smoothed by centuries of blown grit into soft, organic curves—but its organization is absolute. Aqueducts, built not for water but to channel the heavy, sliding drifts of mineral dust, bisect the lower districts. Great public squares are lined with colonnades carved with stone chisels and bone mallets. 

Yet for all its structural density, the city is nearly vacant. The scale is intimate, but the spatial density is ghostly. Towering tenement blocks, chiseled three stories deep into the chalk walls, stand open and hollow, their doorways gaping onto wide, empty avenues where only a few solitary figures move. These structures were built with immense effort using simple lever-and-pulley systems, wooden scaffolds, and hand-forged bronze spades, but they host only a fraction of the population they were built to hold. A walk down a main boulevard takes you past fifty empty thresholds before you spot a single sign of life: a bundle of dry fiber drying on a stoop, or a simple hand-loom staked into the chalk.

The silence you might expect from such an empty place does not exist. The pocket is deafening.

The noise is a roaring, multi-layered acoustical storm trapped within the selenite vault. The primary culprit is the vegetation itself. Millions of dry, brittle leaves and glass-bristled fronds continuously rub together in the thermal updrafts created by the intense light. The sound is not a gentle rustle; it is a thunderous, metallic screech—like a million iron files scraping across dry slate, compounded by the acoustic echo of the carved stone streets. Beneath this rasping scream lies the deep, bass hum of the selenite roof expanding and groaning against the chalk walls as heat pulses through it. To speak here requires shouting directly into an ear; the air itself seems to vibrate hard enough to rattle teeth in their sockets.

Fauna is exceedingly rare, unable to cope with the sheer acoustic violence of the chamber. Occasionally, a chalk-mite—the size of a hand, encased in a thick, porcelain-like carapace—crawls deliberately across a carved lintel, entirely deaf, harvesting the sulfur-moss with scissor-like mandibles. High up near the glowing dome, small, paper-thin gliders with translucent skins drift on the hot air currents, catching airborne spores in fine, hair-like nets before dropping silently into the lower shadows.

The world within this pocket operates under strict, dry rules. Nothing decays; it merely disintegrates into dust. The city's inhabitants—few as they are—do not dig for wells; they harvest the heavy, nutrient-rich dust from the aqueduct basins, kneading it with crushed pith-reed sap into a dry, doughy bread that bakes instantly on the hot stone pavements. They wear garments woven from the tough, dry fibers of the silver-vaned plants, thick enough to muffle the punishing vibration of the ambient sound.

Processes here move in a fast, rhythmic cycle. Every few hours, as the sun above shifts across the selenite dome, the focal point of the light sweeps across the city like a slow, burning searchlight. Where the light strikes, the foliage bursts into immediate, rapid expansion—vanes unfolding with dry, clicking snaps, releasing clouds of golden dust. As the light passes, the plants stiffen again into brittle, motionless statues, leaving the hand-carved streets submerged once more in a roaring, sun-drenched, empty grandeur.
