The high country does not crumble; it has not existed long enough to learn how to break down. The plateaus sit six thousand meters above the lower plains, bounded by raw shear-planes where the dark, microcrystalline bedrock snapped upward. The edges of the cliffs are glass-sharp, unrounded by rain or frost, because there is no rain here, nor frost, nor air damp enough to hold a cloud. The rock is a heavy, lead-gray slate laced with untarnished ribbons of native copper and seams of cold, yellow tallow-stone. 

Everything here exists under a bright, unwavering daylight that fills the sky from horizon to horizon without glare or heat-haze. The sun sits at a permanent mid-morning angle, casting long, clean-edged shadows across flat tablelands that stretch thirty miles before dropping off into the pale void of the lowlands. The air is warm, dry as bone-meal, and smells faintly of heated paraffin, scorched mica, and sweet, heavy oils.

Despite the total absence of water, the plateaus are densely colonized by a thick, heavy vegetation that grows not in soil, but directly out of the un-weathered fracture lines of the rock. It is a flora built without sap. In place of water, these plants circulate dense, synthetic-feeling hydrocarbons—viscous lubricants, clear amber waxes, and liquid paraffins that never evaporate. 

Their forms are vast and structural. Single specimens of the *bellows-fern* stand forty feet high, spaced half a kilometer apart across the bare stone. Their fronds are thick, ribbed plates of pale, semi-flexible chitin, colored the dark green of oxidized bronze. They do not sway; there is no wind to move them. Instead, they operate on internal thermal differentials, their thick stems structured like corrugated steel conduits that slowly compress and expand over the course of days, drawing ambient nitrogen and airborne sulfur directly into their dense, fibrous heartwood. 

Between these solitary giants, lower thickets of needle-leaved shrubbery fill the shallower basins. These bushes bear leaves like polished brass shims, arranged in overlapping rows along stems as stiff and springy as clockwork steel. Where two leaves touch, a tiny bead of colorless, odor-free oil sits perpetually in the crevice, held in surface tension, keeping the joint from galling as the plant holds its rigid posture against the sun.

The scale of the landscape is immense, but its density is strangely low. There are no forests here in the earthbound sense—no tangled understory, no decaying litter on the ground. The rock surface between the plant stands is pristine, swept clean by its own dryness, gleaming with exposed veins of galena and quartz that have never known a drop of moisture. A traveler could walk for hours across the gray plateau without touching anything living, moving through broad corridors of stone twenty stories above the nearest root-system, surrounded only by the vast, open sky and the distant, dark shapes of neighboring tablelands rising like geometric blocks from the floor of the world.

There are no animals. Nothing flies through the thin, warm air; nothing creeps through the brass-leaf thickets. The silence is near-total, suspended in the dry heat like a held breath. If you stand close to one of the broad, low-growing *accumulator-mounds*—great domes of interwoven copper-veined bark that anchor themselves directly to the raw slate—you can hear the landscape at work. It is not an organic sound, but a quiet, rhythmic industrial ticking: the microscopic expansion of metallic cell walls; the slow, hydraulic slosh of heavy oil rising through a stem under thirty atmospheres of internal pressure; the faint, resonant *clack* of a seedpod’s spring-loaded hinge setting itself in the heat.

The technology of this world is not built; it is grown, or rather, it is the natural state of the mineral and biological equilibrium. The land functions like an unmonitored refinery running at zero load. Roots act as galvanic piles, drawing low-voltage current from the copper veins in the slate to crack atmospheric gas into heavy organic compounds. Certain tall, spired growths—the *column-condensers*—stand like silent cracking towers against the horizon, their ribbed, black trunks studded with cooling fins that radiate surplus heat into the sky, dripping clear, non-volatile lubricants into natural basins hollowed out of the un-eroded granite.

Nothing changes here. The light does not fade into dusk; the plants do not shed their brass leaves; the sharp edges of the slate do not soften. The landscape is frozen in a state of high-efficiency equilibrium, a clean, silent engine of rock and oil running in an empty sky, perfectly motionless, perfectly still, waiting for nothing.
