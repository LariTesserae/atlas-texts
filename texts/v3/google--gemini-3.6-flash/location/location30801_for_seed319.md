The sound arrives before the horizon resolves: a wall of acoustic pressure so thick it feels like a physical fluid filling the ears. It is the roar of ten thousand low-pressure vents, the rhythmic shuddering of subterranean iron, and the endless, thunderous cascade of warm water plunging through hollowed granite towers. To stand here is to be perpetually submerged in a noise that leaves no room for thought, a deafening hum that vibrates through the soles of the feet and rattles the fluid in the chest.

This is the High Basin of Ostra, a broad, elevated shelf held five thousand feet above the plains by sheer basalt curtains. The air is surprisingly, persistently warm—heated from below by deep geothermal fissures and kept heavy by a condensing haze that never fully clears. Bright daylight penetrates the mist, diffusing into a luminous, shadowless cream that illuminates miles of wet, rusted infrastructure stretching across the plateau.

The scale is vast, yet strangely uncrowded. The basin is an urban landscape of monumental industrial architecture, but its components are planted far apart. Giant brick chimneys, their crowns worn smooth by centuries of steam, stand hundreds of yards from one another like solitary obelisks. Between them lie immense stone-paved plazas, long-disused rail beds whose iron tracks have fused into dark, glass-smooth bands of magnetite, and vast open pools of condensation that reflect the pale sky.

Everything here is old and thoroughly settled. The edges of the great works are softened—not by collapse, but by the relentless, patient erosion of hot, mineral-rich moisture. Cast-iron gantries forty feet high have oxidized into deep chestnut red, their rivets rounded into smooth buttons of rust. Granite sluiceways, carved to divert the runoff from massive boilers, have been worn down by the continuous passage of water until they resemble natural river canyons, their margins lined with thick, mineralized crusts of orange and teal silica.

Where the rock meets the metal, vegetation makes a brief, desperate stand. There are no forests here, nor grass. Instead, the sparse biology consists of specialized, nickel-tolerant lichens that map the iron plates in vibrant mustard yellows, and carpets of velvet moss that cling to the warm brickwork where copper-laden water drips continuously from above. The fauna is rarer still: occasionally, a pair of pale, broad-winged swifts dart through the thermal drafts rising from an exhaust stack, or tiny, blind spiders build tough, silvery webs across the mouths of silent whistle-pipes.

The technology is heavy, mechanical, and electrified, yet its movements operate on a time-scale so slow as to feel almost geological. High above the plazas, copper cables thick as a man’s waist sag between stone pylons, humming with a continuous low current that lights dull, amber filament globes set into the masonry. In the distance, a massive walking beam—a lever of solid, forged iron two hundred feet long—pivots with agonizing slowness. It takes twenty minutes to complete a single stroke, lifting thousands of gallons of boiling brine from deep beneath the basin, pausing at the apex with a deep, structural groan, and descending with a heavy, seismic shudder that sends ripples across every mist-covered pool for a mile around.

There are no sudden floods here, no sudden breakdowns or rapid fires. The water flows in a permanent, warm equilibrium—weeping from joints in the high aqueducts, running down the faces of multi-tiered tenement facades, dripping from overhead walkways to form shallow, warm rivers that snake through the empty avenues. The city-engine does not seem to be falling apart, nor is it being built; it simply exists in a state of long-established persistence, operating according to an ancient, self-sustaining mechanical logic.

Looking out across the basin, one sees tier upon tier of this hybrid terrain. A terraced slope of wet brick rises toward a cluster of massive iron drum-houses, their sides sweating rivulets of clear condensation. Beyond them, a wide gap of bare, dark stone opens up, punctuated only by a single, immense fly-wheel turning so lazily its spokes can be counted by eye. Above it all, the warm sky glows white-gold through the steam, and the deafening voice of the machinery sings its single, unbroken note across the high altitude.
