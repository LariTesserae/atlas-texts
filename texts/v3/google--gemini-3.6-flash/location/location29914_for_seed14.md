The water here does not yield easily to motion; it is cold, thick with dissolved minerals, and compressed under a sky of ice or solid rock far above. It fills an ancient, flat-bottomed trough hundreds of meters below any surface world, a vast drowned basin where the topography was worn down to smooth, low domes eons ago. There are no sharp crags left, no jagged spires. Everything has been rubbed down by endless fluid friction into a landscape of rounded basalt hummocks, vast fields of egg-smooth cobbles, and endless, deep terraces of dense, grey silt.

It is almost pitch black. What light exists is a faint, bruised violet hue that seems to emanate from nowhere, caught and diffused by the dense cloud of suspended particulate that fills the water column. The fluid is not clear; it is a heavy, churning suspension—a sea crowded with fine glacial clay, ground-down quartz, and microscopic mineral flakes. To move through it is to move through a liquid that is nearly a solid, saturated so completely with matter that there is scarcely a cubic inch of water free from grit or drifting silt.

Despite the profound cold and the crushing darkness, the basin is in constant, violent motion. Immense, invisible sub-surface currents drive through the valley like hydraulic rams. They do not blow like wind; they push with the weight of mountains. Massive, smooth boulders—tons of solid stone rounded like river pebbles—roll across the basin floor in slow, heavy migrations, grinding into one another with dull, deadened thuds. Silt plumes hundreds of feet wide rise and tear through the darkness, forming suspended rivers of mud that scour the bedrock before settling and being torn up again moments later. 

Yet for all this kinetic violence, the world is remarkably quiet. The water is too heavy, the pressure too immense, for high frequencies to survive. The chaotic tumbling of rocks and the tearing of mud banks produce only a deep, felt vibration—an infrasonic hum that vibrates directly through the rock and into any flesh submerged within it. It is a muffled, rhythmic roar, so continuous and all-encompassing that it acts as a strange form of silence.

Life is sparse, tough, and desperate. In the lee of the largest rounded hummocks, where the violent currents eddy and lose a fraction of their force, thin colonies of black, whip-like vegetation anchor themselves to the stone. They are not leafy or soft; they are rubbery, cord-like tendrils, stiff enough to survive the shearing force of the silt-laden water, rooted into narrow fissures with desperate tenacity. 

Fauna is rarer still. Occasionally, a pale, flattened creature—smooth-skinned, blind, and shaped like a wedge—drifts through the turbid gloom, keeping close to the gravel bed. It feeds on the organic detritus trapped in the sediment, sliding between the grinding cobbles with fluid grace before disappearing into the blackness.

Human presence, or the mark of some hand, is ancient and nearly erased, but unmistakable if looked for in the sheltered cracks where the currents cannot reach. Wedged into deep basalt joints beneath layers of compressed mud are simple artifacts of stone and bone. There are heavy, hand-flaked basalt choppers, their edges dulled by thousands of years of cold water; grooved sinker-stones bound with rotted vegetable fiber that has long since calcified; and whale-rib scrapers wedged tightly into rock crevices to hold ancient, lost positions against the flow. There are no buildings, no walls, no ruins of mortar or metal. Whoever worked these stones possessed only the simplest hand tools, carving out a brief existence on the margins of this abyssal basin before the water fully claimed it and ground their traces into sand.

The landscape feels final, a world that has reached its ultimate, eroded state and yet refuses to rest. The rocks will grow no smaller; the valley can be scraped no flatter. There is only the endless, cold thrashing of the heavy water, the dark, saturated slurry pushing through the gloom, turning stone against stone in the quiet deep.
