The air here hits the back of the throat like a wet, heated wool blanket. Buried fifty feet beneath the surface of the plain, the *Sunk-Foundry Precinct* sits in a narrow, sharp-edged trench—a fissure so cleanly cut that the basalt walls still bear the raw, vertical grooves of giant diamond-toothed trenchers. It is a tiny, claustrophobic world, no larger than a cathedral's footprint, but packed to its very brim with heat, iron, and suffocating green.

It is stiflingly hot. The heat does not come from the sun, which never reaches this low, but radiates outward from deep subterranean steam-conduits and massive, bronze-jacketed dynamos embedded directly into the rock face. The warmth has no exit; it pools here, cooking the air until the ambient moisture boils off the boiler casings and collects instantly on every cool surface as a heavy, dripping condensation.

Despite the recentness of the excavation—the rock corners are still razor-sharp, un-weathered by wind or time—the precinct is completely saturated with growth. Plants do not merely grow here; they invade and claim every square inch of available surface. Enormous, waxy-leaved shade-ferns, deep indigo mosses, and thick, rope-like tendrils of heat-loving vines climb the vertical stacks of iron brickwork. They bind together the multi-tiered urban infrastructure: narrow balconies, cantilevered watch-lodges, spiral stairways of cast iron, and stacked, cell-like dwellings designed for workers who have long since migrated or melted into the shadows.

There is almost no empty space. A copper steam pipe six inches thick runs from a wall boiler; within three feet, it is choked by a sleeve of velvet-textured black fungi that feeds on the radiant heat, which is in turn draped in a curtain of dripping silver-moss. Beneath the catwalks, water pockets collect in copper cisterns and iron sumps, warm as bathwater, their surfaces skinned over with iridescent duckweed and micro-algae that hum with faint, slow-pulsing bio-luminescence.

The light is dim, filtered through layers of suspended steam and the thick, ceiling-spanning canopy of broad leaves that have grown across the top of the trench. The only illumination comes from these glowing plants and the occasional dull red spark of a heat-sink valve venting deep within a masonry wall, casting long, bruised-purple shadows across the bronze rivets and wet stone.

Mechanization here has become a passive environment. Heavy flywheels, four times the height of a man, turn so slowly their motion is almost imperceptible—a single revolution taking the better part of an hour. They drive no visible looms or hammers now; instead, their slow, grinding teeth serve as trestles for creeping vines that are gradually wound into tight, woody spools by the machinery's patient, indifferent rotation. Bronze pressure-gauges, their glass fogged permanently with interior sweat, are wrapped in root systems that have cracked their bezels to sip the grease within.

Sound is swallowed whole by the humidity and the sheer volume of plant matter. The atmosphere acts as a acoustic dampener; a shout here would travel ten paces before deadening into a flat whisper. The predominant sound is a low, subterranean murmur—the distant, rhythmic sighing of thermal pressure underground, accompanied by the endless, soft *tink-tink-drip* of warm water falling from iron grates onto heavy leaves.

Animal life is nearly absent. The heat and density leave little room for movement. Very rarely, a blind, porcelain-white snail the size of a fist glides along the hot copper conduits, its shell polished mirror-bright by friction against the metal, scraping away layers of thermal grease and fungus with a silent, rasping mouthpart. Otherwise, the precinct belongs entirely to the slow, heavy interaction between hot metal and wet leaves.

Nothing changes quickly in the Sunk-Foundry. The cycles of the place are measured in the slow accumulation of lime-scale inside the pipes, the gradual warping of iron staircases under the weight of root-systems, and the steady, quiet drip of water that will, in a hundred years, soften the sharp, fresh-cut edges of the basalt trench into something smooth and forgotten.
