High above the cloudline, where the air should be thin and freezing, the air is instead thick with a wet, tropical heat that smells of ozone, wet iron, and bruised vegetation. 

There is no soil here that was not carried or created by decay, and no stone that was not poured. The entire site—a stepped terrace no larger than a small chapel's courtyard—hangs off the flank of an immense, unseen structural spire that disappears into the gray void below. Everything beneath the foliage is made of dark, heavy cast concrete and riveted iron plate. Crucially, the concrete has not yet aged; its edges are knife-sharp, the seam-lines from its wooden formwork still crisp and raised, unsoftened by frost or years. The dark iron is smooth, unpitted by rust, still holding the blue-black sheen of the foundry floor. It is a brand-new landscape, an industrial cradle barely out of its molds, yet it is already drowning in life.

Water is everywhere, but it does not fall as rain. It is exhaled. Broad copper conduits—some as thick as a man's waist, others fine as fingers—lace through the concrete walls, sweating heavy beads of condensation that gather, run, and drip into terraced basins. The water is warm, tepid as blood, and rich with dissolved minerals that leave faint, chalky white trails across the fresh black metal. It trickles across flat iron decks, drops in thin, glass-like sheets from step to step, and fills shallow channels where it pulses in small, rhythmic surges driven by some deep, hidden pump below.

Because of the warmth and the endless weeping of the pipes, the plant life grows with a frantic, almost terrifying speed. Flesh-stemmed ferns with broad, translucent fronds have seized every crevice where two plates meet. Mosses—bright, electric green and saturated like soaked sponges—carpet the flat concrete, turning the sharp ninety-degree angles of the architecture into soft, heavy swells. Long, rubbery vining plants, their leaves as large and thick as leather shields, wrap themselves in tight spirals around the copper steam pipes, drawing heat directly through their bark. 

The light is bright and clear—the unfiltered daylight of high altitude—scattered by the perpetual plume of fine white mist that rises from the floor. Sunlight catches the suspended water droplets, turning the air into a shimmering, golden broth. It lights up the undersides of the leaves and reflects off the wet, polished faces of the iron plates, so that the small space feels illuminated from every direction at once.

Despite the density of growth and the presence of machinery, the place is overwhelmingly quiet. The thick canopy of broad leaves and the carpet of moss absorb echo. The only sounds are soft, liquid, and mechanical: the rhythmic *clack-hiss* of a pressure valve venting steam deep within a concrete wall, the steady *pit-pat* of heavy water drops striking wet broadleaf, and the low, resonant thrum of fluid moving through pressurized pipes. Occasionally, a balance-trough—a long, zinc-lined gutter mounted on a central pivot—fills to capacity with runoff, tilts with a heavy, muted *thump*, spills its warm load into a lower basin, and slowly rocks back into place.

Fauna is sparse, specialized, and quiet. Small, pale crustaceans, nearly transparent, scuttle along the submerged lips of the zinc troughs, feeding on the film of algae that has begun to coat the metal. A single type of moth, broad-winged and dark as damp slate, clings to the warm undersides of the copper pipes, its wings vibrating in time with the low hum of the machinery. There are no birds, no small mammals, no soil-dwelling worms. The ecology is artificial, brand-new, and self-contained—an accidental jungle birthed by the wet respiration of a machine high in the sky.

The space feels intensely condensed, like a garden grown inside a boiler room. Stand at one end, and five paces bring you to the far bulkhead: a massive wall of ribbed cast iron, its lower half already buried in a tangle of wet, creeping runners, its upper half rising clean and dark into the mist. Look over the low outer parapet—a straight, un-weathered slab of poured stone—and there is only the sheer drop, the warm updraft carrying tiny flecks of foam, and the endless sea of clouds stretching out beneath a clean, blinding sun. The terrace sits there, a thirty-foot sanctuary of steam, iron, and exploding green, humming softly to itself as its pumps slowly turn, watering a world that was cast only yesterday.
