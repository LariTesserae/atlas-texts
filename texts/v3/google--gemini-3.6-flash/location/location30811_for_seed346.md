It is a sunken precinct no larger than a village square, dropped into a low, cold trough of raw basalt. Though its total footprint could be walked in eighty paces, the city inside it has no apparent perimeter; it is a hamlet-sized area packed with the density of a capital, built from materials for which geology has not yet assigned a name.

The air here is thin, sharp, and cold. Breath hangs in brief, flat ribbons before vanishing against the dark facades. Light falls evenly from an unclouded, pale sky—a crisp, normal daylight that should render everything simple, but instead exposes the geometry as something fundamentally broken.

The architecture is urban, layered, and impossibly tight. High-rise structures, narrow tenements, and vaulted conduits press against one another like books shoved into a shelf too small for them. Yet these are not made of brick or glass. The walls are composed of dark, dense, fine-grained matter—a substance resembling unpolished flint or cold cast-iron, extruded with mathematical perfection. The edges of the buildings are razor-sharp, un-weathered, and totally devoid of wear. There are no rounded corners, no chipped steps, no soft foot-paths worn into the stone. The precinct has the quality of something instantiated only moments ago, or perhaps something that continually resets its physical state before time can dull its margins.

Because the density is so extreme and the technology behind it so far beyond ordinary mechanics, space within the precinct does not stack linearly. A narrow alley between two five-story facades appears to be three inches wide at eye level, yet a person can step into it effortlessly; the path simply widens around the body while remaining three inches wide from the outside. Balconies overhang courtyards that exist only when viewed from below. A flight of six stone steps leads down to a cellar door, but walking down them brings you out onto a roof garden overlooking the same six steps from forty feet above.

These spatial dislocations occur without drama. They are patient, cold facts of the site.

Moisture is light but persistent. Cold condensation coats the lower faces of the structures, gathering in flat, perfectly round droplets that do not run down the walls. Instead, the drops creep slowly sideways across the dark stone, obeying localized, non-gravitational gradients. Where the buildings meet the raw floor of the basin, small pockets of trapped liquid collect in shallow, un-eroded depressions—clear, icy pools no bigger than dinner plates, so still they reflect the impossible overlapping roofs above like mirrors of dark foil. Occasionally, a faint, atomized mist settles over the precinct, leaving a dry, icy frost along the eastern edges of every ledge, regardless of where the sun sits.

Vegetation is scarce, restricted to the seams where the urban mass intersects the floor of the basin. A pale, slate-gray lichen, thin as a coat of dried paint, clings to the sharpest corners of the lowest steps. It does not grow in patches, but in straight, single-pixel lines along the crisp margins of the stone, feeding on the ambient condensation and the exotic thermal leakage of the walls.

Fauna is even rarer. Once every few hours, a small, multi-jointed creature—no larger than a sparrow, with a shell like cold, clouded glass—emerges from a seam between two compressed facades. It moves with a slow, mechanical efficiency, stepping from a vertical wall onto a horizontal ceiling without altering its speed, its tiny feet leaving vanishing rings of frost on the dark material. It does not feed on the lichen or search for water; it appears simply to be checking the structural integrity of the folds, slipping back into a two-dimensional crack as quietly as it appeared.

Sound is almost entirely absent. The precinct does not echo. A dropped pebble produces a single, dull *clink* that terminates immediately, as if the air itself refuses to carry the vibration across the spatial seams. The only continuous sound is a faint, sub-audible hum—less a noise and more a pressure in the inner ear—emitted by the dense, dark walls as they maintain their impossibly compressed configurations.

Nothing moves quickly here. The cycles of the place are glacial. Every few days, a facade will shift its orientation by a fraction of a degree, sliding past its neighbor with the sound of grinding ice, expanding an interior room by three feet or swallowing a doorway entirely. The transformation is so gradual that if you watch a wall, it appears motionless; look away for an hour, and the corridor behind you has shortened by half, its dark, sharp-edged stone as cold and still as if it had sat there since the birth of the earth.
