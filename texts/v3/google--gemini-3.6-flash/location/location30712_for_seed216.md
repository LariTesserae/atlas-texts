There is no stone here, nor earth, nor any mountain raised by tectonic thrust. What exists is a frame—a continent-spanning scaffold of joinery, tied, spliced, pegged, and grown, held miles up in a hot, misty sky. 

The structure is total. Beneath the thick carpet of creeping ferns and wet liverworts lie not rocks, but gargantuan beams of petrified, hand-shaped heartwood. Every surface is an assembly: trunk spliced to trunk with hand-braided cordage the thickness of ancient oaks; carved wooden mortises three miles wide into which whole forests of pillar-trees have been socketed and pinned with timber dowels. Yet, though everything was fashioned by hand—fashioned with stone wedges, bone mauls, and manual labor so old it has lost its history—the hand of the builder has long been swallowed by the wood itself. The beams have sprouted; the pegs have rooted; the tied joints have fused into swollen, scar-tissued burls that now form the hills, ridges, and valleys of this suspended world.

Because the scale is vast, the frame is mostly emptiness. Between one braided wooden highway and the next lies a mile of warm, hollow air filled with slow-drifting wisps of vapor. Standing on the lip of a carved rain-trough, one looks down into an abyss of hollow architecture—layers of crossed beams, spliced trusses, and hanging root-stairs vanishing into a dim, amber-tinted fog. It is a world of immense, quiet voids bounded by living carpentry.

The elevation is so great that the air ought to be thin and cold, but it is not. A thick, warm greenhouse effect holds the high altitude in a permanent, humid sweat. The temperature never drops; it remains at a feverish, heavy warmth that encourages continuous growth and slow decay. Rain does not fall from distant clouds above; rather, the atmosphere condenses directly onto the architecture. The beams are perpetually wet. Water seeps out of the heartwood, trickling along miles of carved drainage channels—cut by simple stone chiseled grooves that have been smoothed down by millenia of running water—before spilling over the edges into the lower voids in silent, curtain-like falls.

The light is a constant, suffocating dimness. Up here, above the unseen lower world, the sky is covered in a dense, glowing canopy of broad-leafed climbing vines that have stretched between the highest spars. Only a pale, green-brown twilight filters through. It casts no sharp shadows, only soft, heavy gradients across the weathered bark-floors and carved wooden stairs.

Despite the emptiness of the space, life is dense upon the surfaces. Thousands of species of wingless, soft-bodied creatures move across the wet wood. Blind, pale salamander-like gliders creep along the undersides of the great trusses, their webbed feet silent against the damp moss. Flocks of featherless, membranous flyers—no larger than seeds—drift down through the empty spaces between the beams, falling like autumn leaves until they catch a warm updraft rising from the hollow depths. They make no sound. The world is extraordinarily quiet. The only noises are the wet, heavy *plop* of condensation droplets hitting fat leaves, the slow, rhythmic groan of two-mile-long wooden arches flexing under their own weight, and the muted rustle of chitin against wet bark.

Reality behaves softly here, degraded by the age and impossibly massive growth of the structure. In the larger hollows between structural bays, gravity loses its orientation. Water weeping from a spliced joiner does not always fall straight down; sometimes it forms floating, warm spheres the size of houses, suspended in the empty air, coated in a skin of water-moss and inhabited by pale, swimming larvae. Nearby, a staircase carved into an ancient, horizontal limb may slowly twist until it runs along the underside of the beam, yet a traveler stepping upon it feels their heels settle firmly against the step, untouched by the abyss hanging above their head. 

Time moves at the pace of wet rot and cambium growth. Processes are active, but slow and wet. A fallen leaf the size of a boat does not dry out; it is consumed within days by a creeping, gelatinous mold that glows with a faint, cold bioluminescence, leaving behind a lacework of skeletonized fibers that the local fauna use as nest-webbing. Elsewhere, the living wood actively repairs the old architecture: where a knot-wedge has rotted away, the surrounding grain curves inward, weeping amber resin until the gap is sealed with a hard, glassy scar.

Everything is crafted, everything is grown, and everything is old. It is an environment of weathered wood and infinite space, where the handiwork of forgotten stone-tool builders has become the sky, the ground, and the weather itself.
