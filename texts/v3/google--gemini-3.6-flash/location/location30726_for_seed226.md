High on the shelf of an unweathered mountain, the world is sharp-edged and young. The stone here has no roundness; it exists in pristine fractures, clean-sheared granite plates, and basalt columns so straight and dark they look split by an axe only hours ago. No gravel has had time to grind into sand. The scree at the base of the cliffs consists of heavy, razor-cornered blocks that clatter like broken plates when touched. 

This high pocket—no larger than a few city blocks, enclosed on three sides by vertical grey rock—holds a dense, stacked urban grid built entirely with early craft. It is not a ruin, nor is it unfinished; it is an organized, multi-tiered settlement constructed from rough-dressed stone monoliths, heavy green-wood timbering, and hand-twisted cordage of braided root and leather. Streets six feet wide climb in steep stone flights, passing four-story stone facades with deep-set, unglazed window apertures. Log aqueducts, bound with cold-beaten copper hoops, run flush against the cliffside, carrying water from higher ice-seams into a network of stone channels that cut across every terrace.

Yet despite the concentration of buildings—the narrow alleys, the overlapping stone eaves, the systematic terracing—the place feels hollow, almost empty. The interior chambers are vast, high-ceilinged vaults left entirely bare, containing only smooth flagstones and the occasional heavy cedar bench. The scale of the architecture suggests thousands, but the space between presences is wide and silent. An observer could walk three levels of terraces without meeting anything more than a single person hauling a basket of wet lichen, their bare feet slapping quietly against cold, damp slate.

Water is everywhere, though it does not flow in wide rivers. It is a weeping landscape. The un-eroded rock face continuously bleeds cold, crystalline water through tiny structural fissures. It drips from the underside of timber balconies, pools in the corners of stone steps, and sheets smoothly down the vertical rock faces, turning the dark basalt into mirrors. The atmosphere is dense with a fine, suspended spray that never quite settles into fog; it catches the daylight and holds it, filling the entire basin with a crisp, luminous glare.

Because the air is cool—sharp enough to frost the breath at night—the vegetation grows with thick, wet luxury. Massive carpets of black-green liverwort and silver-rimmed mountain moss cushion every stone seam, so lush they obscure the sharp joinery of the lower buildings. Ferns with thick, fleshy fronds spring from the gaps between building blocks, their undersides laden with heavy water droplets. Pale, tendriled vines climb the timber scaffolding, binding the cedar beams to the rock with thick, woody cords. The smell is an intense mix of crushed spruce needles, cold iron, wet rock, and rich, turned soil.

The sound of the place is surprisingly immense. It is not the sound of a crowd, but of physics trapped in a stone funnel. The continuous drop and run of water down hundreds of stone surfaces creates a deep, resonant hum that vibrates in the chest. Wind coming over the high notch above the cliff strikes the hollow window openings of the upper tiers, producing a low, organ-pipe whistle that changes tone as the gusts shift. Beneath it all is the thunder of an unseen torrent—a glacial stream running far below the floor of the basin through an un-eroded, subterranean slot canyon, echoing up through the drainage grates carved into the center of every street.

Animal life is sparse and specialized. A solitary species of heavy, slate-colored hawk nests in the high niches of the stone facades, drifting silently through the wide spaces between the upper balconies. In the stone aqueducts, small, blind glass-crayfish move against the cold current, their shells translucent in the bright alpine sun. 

Nothing happens quickly here. The rock is too hard to wear down, the water too cold to cause rapid decay, and the wood, saturated and chilly, resists rot. The people who tend the terraces work with slow, patient repetition—hammering copper pins into timber, laying another course of raw basalt blocks on the upper ramparts, or scraping moss from the water channels so the flow remains true. The basin sits in its high notch, bright, wet, loud with the voice of water and wind, perfectly assembled yet wide open to the sky, suspended in a state of perpetual, un-eroded beginning.
