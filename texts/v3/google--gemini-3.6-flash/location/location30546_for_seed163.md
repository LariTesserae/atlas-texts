At this altitude, the sky has a pale, brilliant density that forces the eyes downward, where the glare meets a world made entirely of water, iron, and living fiber.

There is no soil here. The ground is a continuous, five-foot-thick mattress of leathery alpine moss, silver-stemmed liverwort, and dense, rubbery turf that yields underweight with a heavy, wet hiss. Beneath this green mantle lies raw black basalt—jagged, unweathered, and sharp. The mountain shelf was pushed up too recently for rain or ice to have carved proper riverbeds; the stone has no gorges, no smoothed gravel beds, no gentle slopes. Instead, water simply sheets across the un-etched rock beneath the root-mats, millions of gallons of icy condensate and melt moving in a shallow, relentless tide toward the plateau’s outer lip.

The light is blinding. At six thousand feet, the thin air carries the sun without filtration, striking the dripping foliage and turning every suspended droplet into a burning point of silver. The landscape stretches out for miles—an open, high-altitude shelf bounded by raw, tooth-like crags in the distance. The scale is vast but uncluttered; giant, hollow-stemmed vascular pillars, thick as ancient oak trunks and smooth as oiled kelp, rise fifty yards apart, shooting straight up into the thin air before fanning out into flat, saucer-like leaves that catch the sky-water and spill it back down in heavy, crystal cords.

Despite the density of the plant mass, the plateau feels open, even sparse. There are huge voids of empty space between the green pillars, filled only with the glare of the sky, the low mist rising from the turf, and the isolated shapes of heavy industry.

Every half-league or so, bolted directly into the black rock through cuts in the moss-crust, stands a piece of massive, riveted ironwork. These are not towns or settlements, but solitary industrial nodes: cast-iron pumping houses three stories high, copper-clad condenser towers, and long, suspended steel flumes that run on high trestles across the wet expanse. They are painted in faded oxide red and dark lead gray, their seams weeping rust that stains the surrounding moss a vivid, metallic orange. 

The sound is deafening. It fills the ears until it ceases to be heard as sound and becomes a physical pressure in the chest. It is a dual roar—the vast, continuous thunder of water sheeting off the plateau’s precipices combined with the slow, deep-cycle thrumming of steam-driven engines. Inside the distant pump-houses, massive iron pistons four feet across rise and fall with a heavy, wet *clack-thrum-hiss*, drawing cold, mineral-saturated sap and water from deep beneath the basalt floor and forcing it through the elevated pipelines. High-pressure steam vents from relief valves along the conduits with a sharp, screaming hiss, condensing instantly into brilliant white plumes that catch the fierce sunlight and drift across the green shelf like shattered clouds.

The air is cold—crisp enough to turn breath into vapor the moment it leaves the lungs—but the intense light gives the skin a phantom warmth. It smells of crushed fern-leaf, wet iron, cold coal-smoke, and ozone.

Life other than the plant mass is exceedingly rare. A surveyor might walk for two days along the line of the iron flumes and see nothing that moves on its own power, save for small, flat-shelled beetles with backs like polished jet, clinging to the bronze grease-cups of the piston housings, feeding on the tallow and the warm, oily runoff. No birds cross the glare of the high sky; the roar of the air and the machinery seems to have driven them down into the lower valleys.

Everything here operates on a patient, industrial timescale. The giant vascular plants grow by millimeters over decades, their fibrous roots slowly winding around the iron footings of the pumping stations, binding the machinery to the mountain. The engines themselves are governed to run at low, rhythmic speeds, built to pump for a century without human intervention. Long steel cables, thick as a man's arm, stretch across the moss between the stations, moving so slowly on their iron pulleys that their motion is invisible until watched against the line of a distant crag—a steady, silent transfer of mechanical power across leagues of wet, glowing green.

Water pools in the hollows of the ironwork, clear and cold as glass, reflecting the brilliant sun and the towering, green-stemmed horizon. The flumes carry their heavy liquid load eastward toward invisible drop-offs, the pumps beat their heavy, deafening heart-stroke, and the cold water sheets through the dark roots, endlessly washing over stone that has not yet learned how to wear away.
