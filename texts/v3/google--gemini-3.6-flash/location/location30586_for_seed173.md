The room is forty yards wide and completely filled with cold, dark water. 

There is no soil here, no sea-floor mud, no rock outcropping. Every inch of the boundary—the six-sided floor, the vertical walls, the ribbed barrel vault above—is fabricated from thick, riveted iron plates. The edges of these plates were once sharp and rectangular, but centuries of water movement have softened them. Rust has not dissolved the metal so much as rounded it, smoothing the heavy hex-bolts into dull knobs, turning the seam-lines into faint, undulating folds. The structure has settled into its final, ancient state: a drowned metal cell that has forgotten whatever surface it once served.

The water is heavy and cool, holding a constant, low temperature that licks against the iron. Visibility extends only twenty feet through the particulate-laden gloom before dissolving into a deep, greenish-black fog. Light comes from a single surviving string of submerged industrial lanterns, encase in thick, glass globes greened by age. They hum faintly, casting thin, flickering beams of sulfurous yellow through the water, illuminating clouds of floating rust-dust and microscopic organisms.

Despite the emptiness of the space, it is not still. In the center of the hall, a vertical shaft six feet thick rises from a circular housing in the floor plates and disappears through a seal in the ceiling vault. Every twelve seconds, with a dull, concussive *THUNG* that vibrates straight through the water into your chest, the shaft drops three feet, holds, and then slowly groans back up to its apex. 

The sound in the room is immense. Water magnifies every mechanical friction: the rhythmic *chock-chock-chock* of a massive copper gear train turning in the dark upper corner; the low, rushing roar of water being drawn through a caged floor grate; the deep, iron groan of structural plates shifting against one another under external hydrostatic weight. The acoustics are tight, reverberating violently within the metallic boundaries, turning the room into a ringing acoustic chamber.

Life has colonized the machinery, though plant life is rare. Only a sparse, velvet-like turf of pale, cold-water algae coats the non-moving iron surfaces, giving the rusted floor a slippery, greenish-grey sheen. But animal life is dense and active. 

Shoals of small, silver-scaled pollack weave in synchronized ribboning patterns through the gaps in the giant gear teeth, unbothered by the slow, grinding metal. They use the suction currents created by the central piston to carry them effortlessly across the chamber. Massive, long-whiskered ling cod lie motionless in the shadow of the intake pipes, their dark bodies blending with the corroded iron until a sudden tail-flick sends them darting into the open water to snatch a glass-shrimp. 

On the lower floor plates, where the current is broken by structural girders, hundreds of heavy-shelled spider crabs scramble over one another, picking at the barnacles and tubeworms that crust every rivet. Where a high-pressure valve vents a jet of slightly warmer water into the room, the water shimmers with thermoclines, and tiny, translucent mysid shrimp swarm in thousands, a dense, fluttering cloud suspended in the yellow glow of a lantern.

The room operates on a mechanical breath. As the main shaft cycles, the water level within the sealed intake grates shifts, creating a predictable current that swings from east to west across the chamber every few minutes. The fish shift with it automatically, turning their heads into the flow to catch suspended organic matter. The crabs anchor themselves to the floor plates with heavy claws until the surge passes. 

Everything here—the metal, the water, the mechanics, the sea life—has found an equilibrium. The machine does not seem to be broken, nor is it quite functioning as its builders intended. It simply runs, a drowned iron pump room at the bottom of the dark, working through its slow, loud, endless cycles while the ocean lives inside it.
