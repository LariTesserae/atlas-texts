At an altitude where the sky ought to be thin and blue-black, the air is instead thick with suspended, blindingly bright water. The sun sits overhead—not as a distinct disk, but as an all-surrounding, dazzling glare that fills the haze until every direction glows like frosted glass. It is cold here, a wet, sharp cold that coats the teeth and freezes in tiny, bead-like lenses on the eyelashes, yet the water never fully turns to ice. It remains liquid, heavy, and constantly in motion, weeping down vertical faces of polished dark mineral.

There are no leaves. No lichen crusts the stone; no thin velvet of algae coats the seep-lines. The world is entirely geological and hydrological, built of dense, blue-grey basalt, fractured slate, and veins of pale, translucent quartz that cut through the mountain like frozen fat. The terrain does not flatten out into plateaus; it is a dense, crowded wilderness of verticality—needle-thin spires, fluted gully systems, and terraced rock terraces packed so tightly against one another that to move through the space would mean climbing endlessly up, across, and down through narrow, chimney-like gaps.

Erosion has worked this high altitude for vast spans of time. The edges of the stone are not sharp or newly broken; they are sculpted, scoop-marked, and smoothed into liquid-looking curves, as though the mountains had been poured out and then endlessly scrubbed by heavy wet wool. Water runs everywhere—not in grand rivers, but in a million high-pressure ribbons that skin the surface of the rock.

Yet the water does not obey a single horizon.

Here, high up in the glare, the geometry of reality is slipped slightly out of joint. A thin sheet of water, pale and clear, crawls *upward* across a eighty-foot slab of wet basalt, fanning out into a smooth, shivering apron before dropping off the top lip to fall toward the sky, vanishing into the luminous fog above. In other places, heavy droplets—each the size of a fist—hang suspended in the gaps between adjacent spires, drifting sideways in slow, rhythmic pulses like beads strung on invisible wire. Where two cliff faces meet at an angle, the shadow cast by the bright light falls not away from the sun, but toward it, casting a cold, indigo darkness across surfaces that should be bathed in glare.

The sound is immense. It is not the soft hiss of a rain-soaked meadow, but a roaring, metallic din. The acoustic properties of the canyon-choked plateau are twisted; every drop that strikes the polished stone produces a sharp, anvil-like chime. Millions of these impacts combine into a continuous, deafening vibration—a loud, ringing chorus of iron and glass that rattles the breastbone. When the wind moves through the crowded spires, it doesn't whistle; it produces a low, rhythmic thrumming, like a heavy copper cable being dragged over rough wood.

Scattered throughout this high, wet maze are the marks of an old, simple hand.

They are not ruins of cities or grand temples, but small, deliberate alterations of the rock. In the sheer face of a blue-grey ridge, five feet above a dripping ledge, a series of shallow, circular sockets have been carved into the stone using harder rock or crude obsidian points. The gouge-marks are coarse, uneven, and unpolished—the work of hand-swung stone hammers. Driven into some of these sockets are cold-pounded plugs of native copper, greened by centuries of damp, holding down thick, hand-carved stone gutters that route the upward-flowing water away from small, dry niches recessed into the rock.

Inside these sheltered niches sit low, dry-stacked cairns of heavy slate. There are no inscriptions, no iron brackets, no mortared joints. The stones are simply notched together with precise, manual care—simple lap-joints and ground-flat edges that fit so closely they keep out the wet glare. These tiny structures—none larger than a chest or a low dog-house—are scattered thinly across the regional expanse: one per spire, perhaps, or tucked into the hollows beneath overhangs where gravity pulls sideways instead of down.

Nothing lives here in any sense that breathes or rots. There are no insects in the mist, no birds riding the strange upwellings, no spores floating in the cold, wet air. Yet the landscape is not static; it is fiercely active. The mineral world itself moves on a fast, violent clock. The water carrying heavy loads of dissolved silica and lime deposits skin-thin layers of white crust over the dark rock in the span of minutes, only for a sudden shift in the glare to dissolve the crust back into liquid with a soft, audible *snap*. 

The light shifts continuously, though there are no clouds to break it. Great beams of scattered brilliance swing across the valley-sized expanse like searchlights, bending around the curves of the basalt needles, refracting through the suspended water-globes into short, thick bars of violet and amber that remain stationary in mid-air long after the light that birthed them has moved on.

A traveler here—if such a thing could endure the noise and the blinding, cold wet—would find no soil to stand upon, only the endless, polished ribs of the world, ringing like struck bells under a rain that falls up, down, and sideways all at once.
