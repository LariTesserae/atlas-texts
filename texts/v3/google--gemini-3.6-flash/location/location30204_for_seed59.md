The air here is thin, cold, and heavy with mist—the sharp, clean cold of a high mountain bench, six thousand feet up, where the clouds scrape against the rock. But there is almost no stone to be seen.

The entire place sits in a bowl-shaped ledge no larger than a small town square or a cathedral’s footprint, bounded on three sides by sheer, dripping green cliffs and opening on the fourth to a drop into white cloud. Everything underfoot, overhead, and around is plant matter. Centuries of dense, intertwined growth have formed a spongy, fibrous bedrock yards thick: massive, iron-hard roots buried in peat, covered by a tight carpet of cold, black-green moss that gives under a footstep like wet leather.

Into and out of this living substrate, a settlement has been grown, carved, and stitched together. It is an urban hive squeezed into a mountain pocket. Vertical stalks of giant hollow reed, thick as barrel-stems and tough as hickory, form the primary pilings. They rise three, four, five stories high, their tops leafy and vibrant, their lower lengths stripped, charred against rot, and lashed together with thick ropes of plaited fibrous bark. Between these uprights hang multi-tiered dwellings: spherical rooms woven from living willow-like wands that have taken root where they were bent, their walls caulked with river-clay and thick tree-gum.

Walkways made of split bamboo planks, polished smooth by thousands of wet bare feet, curve in dense spirals around the main trunks. Nothing sits flat. Stairs are notched directly into the bark of ancient, bulbous mountain trees whose crowns form a high, shimmering roof overhead. Where the wood has rotted or split over decades of use, it has been mended with hardwood pegs, notched wedge-joints, and heavy bindings of boiled hide and green vine. The technology is entirely manual and primitive—flint-chiseled mortise joints, heavy stone hammers hanging from fiber belts, bone scrapers used to peel away unwanted shoots—but it is deployed with the dense, organized intensity of a city.

Water is everywhere, flowing with a constant, cold urgency. It drips from the high canopy in heavy, rhythmic splashes; it weeps from the cliffside mosses in sheets; it runs down the mountain in clear, icy rivulets that have been captured by the city’s inhabitants. Hollowed-out half-logs and split-pipe reeds function as an intricate overhead aqueduct system, crisscrossing the narrow airspaces between houses. Water spouts from suspended pipes into deep, carved-gourd cisterns, sloshes over the edges of wooden troughs, and cascades down through the lower levels in misting flumes that turn small wooden water-wheels used for grinding grain or softening bark-cloth. Every surface glistens. The cool air smells intensely of crushed mint, damp humus, woodsmoke, and cold river silt.

The light is surprisingly bright—a clean, diffuse mountain daylight that cuts through the drifting veil of condensation. It catches the water droplets clinging to every leaf and wire-tight binding, making the entire vertical hamlet shimmer like wet glass.

And the noise is deafening.

The small space is an amphitheater for sound. A thousand mountain streams roar and trickle through the wooden plumbing, but louder still is the fauna. The high-altitude jungle is teeming to the point of suffocating saturation. Millions of tiny, black-skinned glass frogs cling to the wet undersides of the woven huts, filling the air with an unrelenting, metallic chiming like thousands of tiny hammers hitting anvils. Dense flocks of heavy, short-winged mountain parrots, colored like moss and lichen, squabble loudly in the upper boughs, tearing at seed-cones and showering the lower walkways with debris. Broad-bodied, furry beetles the size of a fist clatter against the bamboo pilings, while glistening salamanders, pale as lard, slither through the wet channels carved into the main stairs.

Through this biological racket, the human energy of the place moves with frantic, anthill hyperactivity. Because the city is made of living, wet material, it is in a state of constant, violent dynamic balance. Plants grow overnight; vines choke doorways if not severed with obsidian blades; the weight of water threatens to snap suspended bridges; cold mountain winds strain the lashings. Everywhere, people are moving. Workers balance on swaying ladders of knotted root, driving wooden wedges into swollen joints with heavy stone mauls. Groups of three or four haul heavy bundles of cut reeds up the vertical spiral stairs, shouting over the din of the frogs. In small, open-sided hearth-huts built on thick slabs of river-slate to keep the fire from scorching the root-floors, women boil pots of dark dye and strip fibers with bone combs, their movements quick and practiced.

Nothing here is still. A section of walkway, softened by years of water, is chopped away with stone adzes and replaced in an hour with fresh green timber spliced directly into the neighboring trunk. Below, in the dark, cool shadow under the lowest huts, children splash through the runoff channels, scooping up thick clusters of freshwater shrimp that nest in the root-tangles.

It is a place entirely at home in its own dampness and age. The lines between what grew naturally, what was trained to grow, and what was chopped down and tied into place have vanished over centuries. The mountain mist sweeps through the narrow avenues, cooling flushed skin, carrying off the thin blue smoke of birch-bark fires, and keeping the great, living city forever wet, forever creaking, and furiously alive.
