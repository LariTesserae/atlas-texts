The air reaches you before the ground does: a heavy, wet blanket of heat trapped sixty feet beneath the surface, smelling of crushed stems, warm silt, and the faint, sweet tang of rot. 

This is a sunken vault—a collapse basin no larger than a village square, carved out of grey-white limestone by an ancient underground stream that has long since retreated into deeper earth. The walls rise steep and smooth, swept by centuries of water into parabolic curves and scooped-out niches that resemble the nave of a decayed chapel. At the top, the lips of the cavern nearly meet, leaving only a crooked seam of sky that lets in a dilute, greenish twilight. By noon, that light is barely enough to read by; by afternoon, it is a velvety gloom where shapes lose their hard edges.

Despite the dimness, the heat is relentless. Thermal seams in the deep stone warm the floor from below, turning every pocket of trapped rainwater into a slow simmer. Moisture doesn't fall here so much as it migrates. It beads on the high, curved ceiling, gathers into heavy, slow drops, and falls through the quiet air with a soft, rhythmic *plip-plip* into broad, leathery leaves below.

The floor is not dirt, but a dense, living matrix. Vegetation crowds every square inch, layered three and four deep. Lowest are the liverworts and pale, oil-slick mosses that coat the worn limestone like wet fur. Above them, fleshy-stemmed ferns with fronds as thick as leather reach out horizontally to catch the weak light. Long, cord-like air-roots descend from the upper rim, hanging like frayed ropes in the humid air, wrapped in pale, parasitic orchids whose petals are the translucent white of turnip flesh. There are no large trees—there is no room for them—but the brush is so tightly packed that moving a step forward requires pushing through a wet wall of cold leaves and warm, sap-filled stalks.

Nothing here is sharp-edged. The stone has been licked smooth by water that worked this chamber ten thousand years ago, leaving rounded terraces and smooth-bottomed basins where small, black pools sit. On one side of the basin, four wide steps have been cut into the limestone face. They are ancient—their centers worn down into shallow bow-bowls where rainwater collects—and their edges are completely rounded by age and moss. Near the steps, half-buried in a web of swollen roots, stands a single lintel of dark basalt, brought here from somewhere else. It bears no carvings, only three shallow, thumb-sized depressions worn into its upper edge where hands must have rested long ago. It carries no machinery, no metal, no mortar; it is simply a heavy stone resting where someone left it before the moss took over.

Life moves through this heat with a quiet, furious efficiency. The silence is profound, but it is not empty—it is a silence built of a million tiny, muffled sounds. A constant, low-frequency rustling lives in the leaf-litter as blind, pale-skinned skinks hunt armored isopods the size of walnuts. On the vertical rock faces, thousands of small, translucent frogs hang suspended, their hearts visible as tiny red pulses through their throats; every few minutes, one shifts with a wet *shluck* sound against the algae. 

Above, in the dangling root-tangles, small mammals with velvety, earless heads and long, dexterous fingers move without sound, gleaning fat, flightless moths from the undersides of leaves. Nothing calls out. There are no bird cries, no territorial shrieks. The competition is intense—predators and prey constantly crossing paths in the dark—but it is conducted entirely in clicks, soft scrapes, and the wet slide of bodies over leaves.

The vault has its own weather. Every few hours, as heat builds near the thermal floor, the warm air rises toward the cold rim above, where it condenses against the overhang. A fine, warm mist settles over the chamber, obscuring the far wall and coating every beetle shell and moss-filament in perfect, micro-beads of water. The fronds bow lower under the added weight, drip for twenty minutes, and then rise slowly again as the warmth dissipates back into the stone.

You can feel the age of the balance here. The carved steps do not feel like a ruin being reclaimed by nature; they feel like just another species of rock that learned to exist alongside the moss. The basin is an engine running on low, wet heat—self-contained, crowded, constantly consuming and renewing itself in the warm dark, indifferent to whatever daylight is doing sixty feet above its roof.
