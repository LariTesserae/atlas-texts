The air here is thin enough to turn a deep breath into a sharp, copper-tasting needle in the chest. We are far above the timberline, far above the clouds that hover like a gray floor thousands of feet below, bridging the gaps between a series of long, flat-topped slate spines.

It is perpetually twilight. The light does not fall from a sun; it emanates evenly from the underside of a dense, motionless haze that caps the sky—a cold, lilac-tinted dimness that leaves no room for shadows, only varying depths of slate, charcoal, and ice-blue.

Despite the vast, hollow space stretching across this high basin, the silence one might expect at such an altitude is entirely absent. The landscape is deafening. A continuous, flat, low-frequency roar fills the region, a sound like a freight train passing through a subterranean tunnel, though there is no train and no tunnel. It does not rise or fall with a gust; it is a fundamental property of the air itself, produced where the atmosphere shears against geometry that has forgotten how to be spatial.

The mountain floor is tilted. Not merely steep, but wrong: the regional bedrock—a dark, oil-slick schist—lies angled at roughly thirty-five degrees to what the inner ear insists is down. Yet, if one stands upon it, the lean feels natural, as though the vector of gravity was carved into the mountain eons ago and locked into place while the rest of the world rotated away. 

Moisture is everywhere, but it never falls as rain. It sits suspended as microscopic beads on every surface. The slate underfoot is perpetually slick with a cold, greasy film of water that does not freeze into solid ice, nor does it dry. In the shallow depressions of the schist, tiny pools of black water sit at the same thirty-five-degree angle as the stone, their surfaces impossibly sloped, holding their liquid geometry without spilling a drop down the gradient.

Vegetation is almost nonexistent, reduced to thin, scab-like patches of velvet-black lichen that cling to the lee of rock formations. In places where the water-sheets meet the lip of a cliff, a pale, hair-thin moss grows in strict, parallel ribbons. It does not sway in the wind; it remains rigid, locked in the frozen airflow that sweeps across the plateau.

There is no erosion in progress—the erosion happened long ago and then stopped, settling into a permanent equilibrium. The edges of the slate ridges are neither sharp nor crumbling; they are rounded, smoothed into soft, ancient contours by millions of years of friction that has reached its final state. The stone is done falling. The scree fields at the base of the ridges are cemented together by time and ice, as immovable as bedrock.

Across a two-mile span of the basin, four or five structures stand miles apart from one another. They are crude, dry-stacked shelters built from slate tiles, ceilingless and oblong. They were built by hand, but without tools; there are no chisel marks, only chosen stones fitted together with painstaking patience. They sit directly on the tilted bedrock, their walls aligned to the wrong horizontal plane. Nothing lives inside them. Inside one, a small pile of splinters—bone or fossilized wood—rests in a corner, coated in the same un-evaporating dew.

Fauna is exceedingly sparse, but present. Every few miles, one might spot a *kell*—a heavy, low-slung creature the color of wet iron, its body supported by three pairs of short, thick limbs tipped with wide, soft pads. They move without hurry, crawling along the sheer vertical faces of the ridges with the same casual ease as across the basin floor, because to the *kell*, "down" is simply whichever surface their bellies are closest to. They do not appear to hunt or forage; they remain stationary for hours, their flat, featureless heads turned toward the violet sky, breathing the loud, cold air.

Nothing changes here. The light does not fade into night, nor does it brighten into morning. The temperature remains fixed at a degree that turns breath into a short-lived plume of mist before it dissolves into the damp haze. The loud, iron roar from the sky never misses a beat; it is a sky that has finished moving, a mountain that has finished settling, and a world that has found its strangest, coldest point of rest.
