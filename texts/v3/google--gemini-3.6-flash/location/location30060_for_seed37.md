It sits three yards beneath the dry crust of a salt-plateau, accessible only by a crack no wider than an elbow, yet once inside, the space widens into a bright, cool pocket—a single oval chamber eighteen paces long. 

Despite being underground, the light here is sharp and high-key. It drops through the roof via a thick, natural lens of un-eroded selenite crystal. The glare does not warm the air; it stays flat and cold, around forty-five degrees, illuminating the rock with the crisp clarity of a surgical theater.

The geology is raw, almost embryonic. The walls are not worn smooth by ancient water or cracked by frost; they are freshly formed planes of pale grey marl and green-tinted gypsum, meeting at razor-sharp angles as if the stone were poured yesterday and had not yet learned how to weather. There are no scree slopes, no dust, no soil. The edges of the rock where the floor meets the wall are so sharp they could slice leather. 

Moisture exists only as a subtle, persistent dew. Every surface is beaded with micro-drops of water that do not run down the walls; instead, they remain suspended on the mineral facets, holding the bright overhead glare in thousands of pinpoint reflections. Occasionally, a single drop will detach from a ledge, but rather than falling, it drifts horizontally across the room, nudged by a air current that has no clear source, before snapping against another wall with a tiny, dry *tick*.

The room is sparsely furnished by nature. A few low ridges of un-etched basalt cut across the floor, acting as low benches. In the far corner sits a single built object: a three-legged stool carved from a dense, tarry wood that does not belong to any local flora. One leg has been swallowed up to the knee by the raw rock of the floor, not as if it sank into mud, but as if the stone grew around it while the wood was still fresh. Nearby, a row of eight shallow notches is scored into the gypsum wall, each cut clean and un-weathered, as if made with a copper chisel ten minutes ago.

Vegetation is nearly absent. A pale, hair-like fungus grows in thin, perfectly straight lines along the geometric seams of the rock, following the crystal lattice rather than light or gravity. It does not branch. It simply draws white, taut threads across the grey stone, as thin as spider silk and stiff to the touch.

And yet, this tiny, chilly vault is crawling with life—though the life obeys a geography that has unspooled from standard logic.

Hundreds of small, shell-less creatures, no larger than almonds, move across the pale facets. They resemble translucent land-planarians, but they possess jointed, glass-like needles for feet that make no sound as they walk. They do not turn or curve; they only move in rigid, ninety-degree angles, stepping up vertical walls and across the ceiling without changing speed. As they cross the bright pools of selenite light, their bodies split down the center, becoming two identical creatures that march side by side for a dozen strides before one melts back into the damp stone, leaving no trace behind.

In the center of the pocket, where the air is coldest, a school of tiny, air-dwelling fry—fish no longer than a fingernail, with eyes like jet beads—swim through the open atmosphere. They do not fly; their tails beat against the cold air with the exact mechanics of creatures in deep water. They feed on the floating dew drops, swallowing them whole. When two fry collide, they do not bounce apart; they briefly merge into a single, two-headed organism that swims with double speed for three seconds before snapping back into separate bodies with a soft, wet sound like a opening mouth.

The soundscape is almost entirely silent. There is no wind, no dripping water, no rumble of distant machinery. The only audio is a low, rhythmic *hiss-click*—the sound of the selenite roof expanding by fractions of a millimeter under the glare of the sky above, paired with the faint, needle-point skittering of the planarians on the virgin gypsum.

Processes here move rapidly. Biological cycles that should take seasons occur over minutes. A pale, segmented grub emerges from a pinhole in the un-eroded floor, grows a shell of clear calcium carbonate, mates with its own shadow on the wall, casts off the shell, and dissolves into a smudge of clear grease—all in the time it takes for a single drifting dew drop to cross the room. The grease then hardens into a fresh layer of rock, slightly elevating the floor.

The room is building itself from the inside out, using its own dense, hyperactive fauna as raw construction material. The air tastes faintly of iron and cold salt. It feels like standing inside an egg that is being painted and inhabited while the shell is still wet, completely hidden from the dead, dry world three yards above.
