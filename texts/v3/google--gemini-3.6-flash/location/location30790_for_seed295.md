The ground beneath is not soil, nor mountain rock, but a continuous, ancient expanse of hand-fitted basalt masonry. It stretches out into the gloom across an entire high-altitude valley, rising in endless, sweeping terraces that step upward into the dark. There is no visible natural substrate; every slope, incline, and flat platform has been encased in thick, dry-stacked stone or paved with broad, dark flagstones whose edges have been worn smooth by eons of rain and the passage of countless feet.

The air is warm and heavy with a dampness that doesn't fall as a cloudburst, but clings to everything as a fine, continuous bead of moisture. Cool mountain air sits far above, but down within these vast masonry amphitheaters, the stone retains a deep, low thermal heat, creating a humid, protected microclimate. Water collects in long-established stone gutters, pooling in shallow, circular cisterns carved directly into the terrace floors before spilling over rounded lips into the tiers below. The sound of this trickling is entirely lost, however, beneath the sound of life.

The noise is a deafening, monolithic wall. It fills the dark like a solid physical force, vibrating through the soles of the feet where they touch the paving. Millions of tree frogs, thick-thighed and glistening dark green, pack the lip of every drainage channel and moist stone joint, their rhythmic croaking joining the unrelenting, high-pitched rasp of cricket species that blanket the vertical terrace walls. Above them, in the dense, vaulted canopy of broad-leafed timber that has grown out of deep stone planters built into every wall, unseen roosts of heavy-bodied nocturnal birds call out in booming, flat baritones. The sound does not rise and fall; it remains at a constant, crushing volume, amplified and bounced by the endless hard angles of the ancient architecture.

It is very dark. The sky above is buried under both a thick layer of high mountain fog and a unbroken canopy of massive, broad-crowned trees whose trunks emerge from purpose-built stone wells every few dozen yards. No sun reaches these lower tiers, and at this hour—or perhaps at all hours—the landscape exists in a deep, indigo obscurity. Shadows do not move quickly here; there are no torches, no lanterns, no glowing sigils or mechanical light. The only illumination is a soft, non-directional twilight that filters sideways through the distant gaps between high architraves, barely enough to reveal the texture of wet stone and the glistening backs of millions of crawling things.

The flora is lush, but strictly disciplined by the stone beneath it. Thick blankets of star-moss fill every seam between the flagstones, creating soft, springy borders around every stepped threshold. Heavy, fleshy-leaved vines climb the smooth basalt faces, their tendrils finding purchase not by breaking the stone, but by adhering to the mineral crusts left behind by centuries of slow water runoff. Ferns with fronds the length of a man’s arm burst in dense, symmetrical rosettes from carved niches that line the grand staircases. Everything is green, wet, and heavy, yet nothing is overgrown to the point of ruin; the stone and the plants have reached a long-settled equilibrium, where the masonry holds the earth and the roots anchor the mortarless blocks in place against the weight of the mountain.

Fauna occupies every available inch of volume. Beneath the broad leaves of the terrestrial ferns, armadillo-like isopods the size of river stones crawl in slow, armored lines along the terrace edges, grazing on algae. In the deeper, windowless chambers that tunnel into the mountain beneath the upper terraces, thousands of small, short-winged bats drop from vaulted ceilings, their wings making a dry, papery whisper that is felt as a breeze on the cheek long before it can be distinguished from the deafening roar of the frogs. Long-tailed, blunt-nosed lizards cling vertically to the carved friezes, their throats pulsing as they wait for nocturnal moths that flutter down from the canopy. The air is dense with insect life—heavy, slow-flying beetles that bump softly against the stone cornices, and midges that gather in tight, vibrating clouds above the cisterns.

This is an environment of immense, patient scale. Standing on a mid-level promenade, the terraces drop away into dark, broad steps that suggest a floor miles below, while behind, the stepped architecture climbs until its upper balustrades vanish into the damp, leafy ceiling. The structures are not crude, but their construction belongs to an ancient, pre-tool logic—massive blocks keyed together by precise weight and geometry rather than metal clamps or mortar, built to outlast any civilization that dreamed them up. There are no wheels here, no gears, no pipes of forged bronze, only the eternal, carved chutes that guide the mountain’s condensation down through a thousand levels of dark, living stone.

Change happens here across centuries. A single flagstone may settle a fraction of an inch over a hundred years as the mountain beneath it breathes; a fern colony may slowly migrate three steps down a grand staircase over a generation as its spores find moisture in new joints. The warmth is constant, held by the mass of the basalt; the dampness is continuous, fed by the high cloud that rests perpetually on the upper peaks; and the deafening chorus of the dark valley never stops, an unbroken, roaring pulse of life inhabiting a city made entirely of stone.
