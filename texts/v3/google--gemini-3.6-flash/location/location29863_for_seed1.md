The horizon is long, low, and smudged by a perpetual frigid drizzle that never quite settles into rain or freezes into ice. It coats everything in a greasy, high-tension film of black brine. The land has no high places left; eons of water and friction have planed the basalt and iron-bedrock down to a vast, undulating floor of smooth, dark curves, pitted with shallow basins where heavy, cold water sits and quivers.

There is not a single leaf, stem, or filament of moss in all this open breadth. Vegetation never took hold here, or was worn away so long ago that even its molecular memory is gone. Yet the plain is packed wall-to-wall, impossibly dense, with a writhing, hard-shelled ecology that leaves no bare stone uncovered. 

They are creatures of cold oil, bone, and magnetite—segment upon segment of nickel-grey carapaces, jointed legs like iron rivets, flat-skulled scavengers the size of anvils, and blind, tubular things that slide through the brine in endless, interlocking knots. They exist in such numbers that the ground itself seems to be crawling, a shivering pavement of chitin and wet metal. They press against one another with the dry, terrifying sound of a million iron shovels turning over gravel. They do not eat plants; they feed on the current.

For though there are no buildings, no ruins, no pipes laid down by any hand, the landscape operates with the deafening, brutal efficiency of a heavy-industrial shipyard. 

The technology here is not constructed—it is geological and evolutionary, an ancient, accidental mechanics that grew out of the earth as naturally as basalt cools into pillars. Beneath the smooth, water-eroded stone lie deep, natural armatures of magnetic iron ore, turned by the slow, grinding torque of the planetary crust. The movement generates a massive, low-frequency galvanic field that hums through the wet mud. 

Hydraulic pressure from subterranean aquifers forces icy, mineral-laden water up through ancient boreholes with the rhythmic, mechanical thud of a steam manifold—*THUMP-clack, THUMP-clack*—a beat that echoes across twenty miles of flat plain. Steam does not rise from these vents; instead, they spout plumes of super-cooled fog that smell of ozone, wet tin, and butchered tallow.

Where the electrical currents arc through the surface pools, the water doesn't boil—it thickens into a iridescent sludge, glowing with a faint, violet luminescence that provides the only real light in the crepuscular gloom. 

This is where the reality of the place begins to fray at the seams.

The laws governing matter here have grown worn, like the rocks. In the low troughs of the plain, gravity doesn't always pull straight down; it slants toward the heaviest concentrations of the swarming fauna, so that the black water clings to the sides of low stone hummocks in vertical sheets, never dripping. Shadows do not match the creatures that cast them—a slow, hundred-legged thing crawling across a wet slab casts a shadow shaped like a spinning flywheel, or a shower of sharp, frozen sparks that makes no sound.

Sound itself travels at wrong speeds. The deafening screech of iron-on-iron—a sound produced when subterranean strata slide against one another—reaches the ear long before the tremor is felt underfoot, leaving a strange, sickening temporal lag between cause and effect. Sometimes a sound will lodge in a pool of brine, repeating its harsh, metallic clang every few seconds like a recorded loop, long after the source has gone silent.

The air is cold enough to turn breath into sharp needles of frost, yet the atmosphere refuses to freeze solid. It remains a dense, condensing soup of liquid fog that carries sound with brutal clarity: the endless, rhythmic *THUMP-clack* of the natural pumps, the violent hiss of galvanic arcs snapping across the mud, the wet, crushing sound of tens of thousands of metallic bodies grinding together as they migrate toward the strongest electrical nodes.

Look out across the regional expanse: there are no trees to break the view, no hills to climb, no walls to shelter behind. Just miles of smooth, ancient black rock under a leaden, twilight sky, covered entirely by an engine made of flesh, ore, and cold water—running continuously, producing nothing, belonging to no one, deeply settled into its final, noisy form.
