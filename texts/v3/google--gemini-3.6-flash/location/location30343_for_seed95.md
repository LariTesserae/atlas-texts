The sky is high, pale, and washed clean by altitude, casting an even, unblinking light across a tableland that seems to have outlived its own geology. This is a mountain shoulder worn down over uncounted millennia into a vast, undulating pavement of grey and ochre siltstone. The sharp edges of the world were planed away eons ago; what remains are smooth, humped crests, shallow basins, and fluted rock-tongues that look almost melted, like tallow left too long in the sun. The air is warm—not humid or tropical, but imbued with a dry, radiant heat that pulses upward from the deep, sun-baked stone.

At first glance, the plain appears barren. Plant life is minimal: thin ribbons of black wire-moss tucked into narrow structural cracks, and patches of crustose lichen—bright rust, slate-grey, and pale sulphur—that stain the rock faces like dried paint. There are no trees, no shrubs, no green leaves to catch the wind.

Yet the ground is not empty; it is thick with life of another kind. 

The density of fauna here is staggering, so high that the geology itself is partially obscured beneath a living skin. In every shadow cast by the rock swells, animals are packed shoulder-to-shoulder, layered three or four deep. They are largely small, slow-blooded, and muted in color, perfectly adapted to the dry warmth and the high terrace. 

In the long, shallow furrows of the bedrock lie thousands of pale, soft-bodied glass-lizards, their translucent scales gleaming like polished quartz as they rest stacked upon one another to preserve the moisture caught during the dawn fog. Beside them, filling every pit and depression, are carpet-bugs with wide, flat carapaces the color of dry river mud. They overlap like roof shingles, tens of thousands in a single basin, their legs locked to form a continuous, armor-plated floor. Higher up on the sun-warmed domes, legless gliders—creatures like fat, leather-skinned ribbons—lie motionless in sprawling sun-basking carpets, taking hours to shift a single body length.

Because there are so many presences, the atmosphere feels dense, saturated with breath and skin-heat. And yet, it is extraordinarily quiet. There are no bird cries, no mating calls, no territorial screams. The noise of this massed life is restricted to a dry, ambient murmur: the infinitesimal scraping of chitin against sandstone, the whisper of dry bellies dragging over rock, the faint, rhythmic click of breathing spiracles opening and closing in unison. It sounds like dry leaves being turned over by a gentle wind that isn't there.

Water does not flow here in rivers, but it is constantly present in small, patient quantities. Every night, the air at this altitude cools just enough for humidity to condense against the vast expanses of exposed stone. By mid-morning, dew pools in tiny, cup-like depressions carved into the rock. 

These cups are not entirely natural. Scattered across the landscape are the faint, crumbling marks of an ancient, pre-tool intellect. Shallow basins the size of shallow bowls have been ground directly into the bedrock using rougher hand-stones, connected by faint, eroded gutters that channel the morning drip toward central hollows. Here and there, low windbreaks made of flat, dry-stacked slate slabs curve against the prevailing draft. They are unmortared, half-buried in windblown mineral dust, and so old that their top edges have been rounded by wind into the same soft curves as the hills themselves. No metal touched these stones; they were gathered, laid, and shaped by hands or claws that left no other record.

The creatures use these structures without knowing who built them. The glass-lizards line the carved gutters to drink the thin, clear film of water before the sun burns it off; tiny, wingless velvet-moths cluster in the shade of the dry-stone walls, their soft bodies soaking up the dampness held in the dark joints between the slabs.

Everything here operates on a long, slow clock. A glider might take three days to cross a ten-foot slab of sandstone to follow the creeping edge of a shadow. The carpet-bugs molt once every few years, leaving behind fragile, paper-thin husks that the wind grinds into a fine, pale dust, which settles back into the crevices to form new soil for the lichens. Predation is so slow as to be almost imperceptible: a heavy, blind rock-toad, wedged deep into a fissure, slowly extends a thick, mucous-coated tongue once an hour, drawing in whatever tiny arthropod has drifted against its snout, swallowing with a single, muscular contraction that takes minutes to complete.

It is a landscape settled into its final form—a world at the end of its mountain-building, where the stone has ceased to rise, the water has ceased to cut valleys, and a dense, quiet fauna has blanketed the warm, high ruins of the earth, waiting out the centuries in a state of patient, crowded stasis.
