There is no light here. Not the faint violet of a deep sea trench, nor the pale grey that filters down through long cave-shafts from the surface. The darkness is absolute, a thick and tactile void that presses against the eyes until the mind begins to invent its own fleeting, phantom sparks to fill it.

It is warm. The heat does not rise or fall; it rests in the air like a wet wool blanket, held at a constant, fever-skin temperature by deep geothermal veins running through the high, un-eroded core of the mountain mass. High above the world’s weather, sealed inside a pocket of tectonic stone that has never seen a sunset or a frost, the atmosphere is saturated to its absolute limit. Air here is almost liquid. Every breath fills the lungs with warm, mineral-heavy steam that leaves a faint coating of salt on the lips.

The scale of this place is small—a single, hidden vault no larger than a wide hall, embedded deep within a massive uplift of fresh rock. Yet within this pocket, the density of presence is overwhelming. Nothing has been cleared. Nothing has been smoothed away. The geology here is embryonic, raw, and un-weathered: razor-sharp basalt dikes rise from the floor like closely spaced fins; conchoidal fractures of black obsidian jut from the walls in crowded, glassy fans; long, un-broken needles of cooling-mineral stone hang from the ceiling, their tips descending to within inches of the rising floor-structures. To move through this space is to slide sideways between vertical sheets of sharp, newly minted earth, feeling your way through an overcrowded gallery of un-made stone.

Water is omnipresent, but it does not flow with the violence of a river or the rhythm of rain. It sweats. The rock is so young that its microscopic pore-spaces are still expelling the water trapped during its formation. Thick, warm beads of condensation cover every inch of glass-smooth obsidian and rough basalt. The water gathers on the edges of the stone fins, trembling in the dark until its weight pulls it downward in slow, heavy drops. 

*Plink.*

A single drop falls into a shallow, hand-carved stone depression. Eight seconds later, another. The rhythm does not vary. It has not varied in five hundred years. Because the vault is completely sealed from atmospheric shifts, there are no drafts, no pressure changes, and no temperature gradients. The landscape is locked in a perfect, warm equilibrium. A drop that forms today is identical in volume, speed, and path to the drop that formed a millennium ago.

In this perpetual dark, the human marks are faint, deliberate, and entirely tactile. They are scattered through the dense stone maze, invisible except to the fingertips. 

Here, wedged tightly between two parallel basalt fins, is a ladder made of thick, un-barked mountain wood, lashed together with hand-braided vegetable cordage. The wood has not rotted; in this sterile, lightless warmth, micro-organisms struggle to break down tough lignin, and the ladder remains as firm and resinous as the day it was wedged into place. Nearby, resting on a flat, razor-edged shelf of black stone, lies a small collection of tools: a heavy hammerstone made of rounded river-cobble (brought up from some distant valley that saw the sky), two knapped flint chisels with dulled edges, and a broad, shallow bowl of coarse, unglazed clay.

The clay bowl sits directly beneath a weeping seam in the wall. It is full to the brim. The water inside it does not evaporate, nor does the bowl overflow; the excess creeps over the lip in a thin, continuous surface-tension skin that runs down the side and into a tiny, hand-pitted drain hole in the floor. 

Vegetation is nearly absent, reduced to quiet, specialized presences that do not require the sun. Where the warm water trickles continuously over the rougher basalt faces, a pale, gelatinous microbial skin has established itself. It feels like wet velvet to the touch, barely a millimeter thick, feeding on the dissolved sulfur and iron that weeps from the raw stone. In the deepest crevices, long, ghostly root-threads—belonging to trees that grow hundreds of feet above on the outer mountain slopes—have pushed through tectonic micro-fractures, hanging in the warm, humid air like long, unspooled skeins of silk, drinking the atmosphere directly.

Fauna is rarer still. Once in a long while, the quiet is punctuated by a tiny, dry clicking sound—the movement of a sightless, translucent crustacean, no larger than a thumbnail, moving along the wet face of an obsidian blade. Its shell is soft and pigmentless; its long, thread-like antennae sweep the warm dark, feeling for the microscopic mats of microbial slime. It moves without urgency, pausing for hours at a time, entirely attuned to a world where speed has no purpose and seasons do not exist.

To be in this landscape is to lose the concept of age. The rock feels as though it was cracked open five minutes ago—its edges are too sharp, its faces too clean, untouched by the rounding forces of wind, ice, or plant roots. Yet the primitive clay bowl sitting on its ledge, left by someone who navigated this maze by touch alone, feels equally present, equally un-decayed. 

There is no sound here except the water. Not a trickle, not a rush, but the quiet, spatial geometry of drops: the deep *thump* of a heavy fall striking a hollow clay vessel; the high, glassy *ping* of a drop hitting a sharp obsidian ledge; the soft, continuous *hiss* of warm vapor condensing on cold mineral faces. The sounds do not echo far; the extreme density of the stone fins and hanging needles breaks the sound waves, muffling them into a small, intimate room of liquid ticks.

Everything sits in its place, held by the heat, the moisture, and the dark. The sharp rock wait. The water gathers. The pale roots hang motionless in the warm steam, waiting for nothing at all.
