To reach the chamber, one does not descend through stone, but through a seam in stiff, aromatic fibers that yield with the dry squeak of compressed hemp. There is no mineral earth here. The stone of the lower crust has been entirely displaced or devoured; the floor, walls, and distant ceiling are woven from a single, continuous organism—a lignified scaffold of raw, un-decayed wood and pale, blade-like cellulose that meets at clean, sharp angles.

Despite being miles beneath the upper soil, the cavern is illuminated with the clear, unblinking clarity of early afternoon. Light does not fall from a sun; it bleeds directly from the translucent, amber-tinted ribs of the walls, casting long, crisp-edged shadows across a vast, empty volume. 

The chamber is small on a map—a mere pocket in the deep strata—yet its interior geography is an impossibility. Step across the threshold and the air opens up into an enormous, cathedral-like hollow. It is almost entirely empty. Ninety percent of the room is simply warm, suspended air, crisscrossed only by a few lone, taut tendrils as thick as ship masts, stretching from wall to wall across hundreds of yards of void. The floor does not curve down; it drops away into a floorless depth where light fades into a golden haze, yet a traveler standing on the lip feels a gentle, warm pull toward whatever surface is nearest, as if gravity were merely an opinion held by the bark.

It is warm here—a dry, constant heat like the interior of a kiln—punctuated by sudden, violent showers of moisture. Every few minutes, a deep, mechanical *thump* reverberates through the wood beneath your feet, and a high-pressure mist blossoms from a row of needle-fine pores far overhead. The mist coalesces in mid-air, forming fat, marble-sized spheres of water that drift slowly sideways through the empty space before bursting against the opposite wall with a sound like wet leather striking slate.

The noise is relentless. It is not the sound of wind or stone, but the loud, hydraulic engine of the mass itself. Xylem tubes the size of culverts groan under immense suction; cambium layers split with sharp, echoing cracks as new, glass-hard shoots expand by inches every hour. The room is young—geologically fresh—its structural lines un-rounded by decay or weathering. Every leaf edge is a razor; every bark-plate is a newly sheared slab of pale, yellow-white heartwood leaking threads of clear, lemon-smelling resin that harden into crystalline drops before they touch the ground.

Geometry here does not obey standard rules. A straight branch, if traced with the eye for forty feet, appears to turn three consecutive right angles while remaining visibly parallel to itself. Looking directly down into the central gulf, one occasionally catches sight of the back of one's own heels, mirrored not by water or glass, but by a fold in the spatial warp created by the density of the surrounding living mass.

Presence is rare. Animals do not flourish in this hollow. High up, where two wall-ribs meet at a sharp sixty-degree crease, a single creature rests: a broad, flat organism resembling a sheet of dried parchment, its six jointed legs wedged tight into the bark. It does not move for hours, save for the rhythmic opening and closing of a central, slit-like spiracle that vents a puff of hot, cinnamon-scented steam whenever the chamber’s pressure surges.

Human trace is almost nonexistent, yet unmistakable in its crudity. Driven deep into a split where a major fluid-conduit meets the floor is a single, hand-shaped wedge of petrified bone. It has been hammered home with a heavy stone—the stone itself lies nearby, trapped in a web of fine, fresh rootlets that are slowly enveloping it. The bone wedge diverts a slow, steady bead of sap out of the pressure-vein, letting it drip into a small, cup-shaped gourd tied to the bark with a braid of dried leaf-skin. The knot is simple, manual, and recent; the wood around it has already begun to grow over the fiber, binding the primitive tool into the living architecture of the vault.

The air smells of crushed pine needles, ozone, and wet starch. As the biological clock of the pocket ticks forward, the light does not dim, but shifts in angle—not because an external sun is moving, but because the glowing veins within the ceiling are slowly creeping across the ceiling’s face, reorganizing their patterns like liquid gold poured through a maze of clear glass tubes. The space feels at once vast and tightly confined, an impossible hollow carved inside an endless, roaring body of wood.
