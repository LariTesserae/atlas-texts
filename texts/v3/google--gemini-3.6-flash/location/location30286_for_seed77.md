The horizon does not meet dirt or rock; it meets a smooth, continuous lip of pale cast lime that extends until the haze of the lower atmosphere blurs it into the sky. 

This is a lowland of infinite, low-sloped terraces, broad concavities, and miles-wide shallow troughs, all fashioned from the same dense, reddish-grey mineral composite—something like unglazed terracotta fused with hydraulic cement. There is no raw stone here, no loose gravel, no soil. The ground is a single, continental casting, laid down in immense, seamless plates that stretch for tens of kilometers before stepping down a precise six inches to the next level.

The sun sits high and hard in a pale, hot sky. The heat radiates off the baked floor in visible waves, creating shimmering pools of false water on the distant flats. Yet the air is not entirely dry. Moisture moves through this place in distinct, localized rhythms. In the cooler hours before dawn, cold air settles into the lowest basins, and atmospheric pressure draws thick dew out of the atmosphere, coating the smooth terracotta in a thin, gleaming film. By mid-morning, as the heat ramps up to its full intensity, this moisture does not simply vanish; it gathers in long, low-profile gutters built into the floor, forming ribbons of dark, warm water that steam faintly under the direct light.

Because the erosion is so young—almost nonexistent—the edges of the terraces and the lips of the drainage troughs remain sharp enough to cut a shadow. There are no rounded pebbles, no smoothed gullies, no mud. Where water has boiled off under the afternoon sun, it leaves behind only a powdery, brilliant white crust of mineral salt, sitting atop pristine, un-weathered masonry that still bears the fine, microscopic striations of its initial setting. The world feels raw, like a vessel just drawn from a kiln and left on a bench to cool, immense and untouched by time.

Spatial density is extremely low. You can walk for hours across a flat, warm plate without encountering any feature higher than a man’s knee. Occasionally, the plain is broken by a series of low, angled cooling fins—broad slabs of dense grey mortar, three feet thick and fifty feet long, set into the floor at twenty-degree angles to catch the prevailing thermal winds. These fins cast long, lean shadows across the red floor, providing the only relief from the glare.

There are no animals. No birds cross the flat sky; no insects scuttle along the sharp-edged seams where the massive plates meet. The silence is deep, but it is not static. The landscape is intensely active in its own inanimate fashion. As the mid-day heat peaks, the continental floor undergoes immense thermal expansion. The sound of this movement is a slow, rhythmic chorus of sharp clicks, dull metallic *tongs*, and deep, resonant groans that vibrate up through the soles of your feet as miles of masonry press against one another along their expansion joints.

Where steam rises from the damp gutters, small, tough biological presences have begun to take hold, though they are exceedingly sparse. In the narrow, damp shadow beneath the lip of a terrace, a pale, rubbery lichen grows in flat, circular patches no larger than a coin. In the deeper drainage slots—where water lingers longest before evaporating—thin, dry stalks of a stiff, reed-like vegetation poke upward at wide intervals. They have no leaves, only waxy, segmented stems that clack softly against the hard, built edges when the thermal wind sweeps across the basin.

Weather here is generated by the floor itself. As the vast, heated plains evaporate the morning dew, columns of humid air rise into the upper light, condensing into small, flat-bottomed cumulus clouds that trail shadows across the empty geometry below. Sometimes, one of these clouds grows heavy enough to drop a sudden, localized shower. The rain hits the scorching terracotta with a sound like a long, drawn-out hiss, instantly filling the air with the smell of wet brick and hot lime, before drying back into salt crust within twenty minutes.

Everything is immense, quiet, and entirely constructed, yet there is no sign of intent, no machinery, no moving parts, no tools, and no ancient refuse. It is simply a continent-sized floor of baked stone, low to the world's sea-level, slowly breathing under a hot sun, waiting for the wind and water to begin the long, slow work of wearing it down.
