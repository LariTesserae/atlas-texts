The air in the low basin is hot, wet, and heavy as wet fleece. It does not move. It has never moved. A perpetual dim light, filtered through a permanent ceiling of warm, grey condensation, rests over the terrain like grease on water. 

There is no green here. The word has no referent. The ground is a raw, black floor of fresh basalt and iron-rich mineral crusts—geologically embryonic, jagged, and unsoftened by time. The edges of the rock terraces are knife-sharp, un-eroded by the warm moisture that bleeds ceaselessly from their fissures. Mineralized water, thick with dissolved salts, wells up from the sub-surface, weeping over raw, unweathered stone and pooling in shallow, glass-flat pans across the basin floor. 

Sprawling across this low volcanic shelf is a vast, suffocatingly dense city, constructed entirely without wood, straw, or rope. 

The architecture is an immense, hand-carved sprawl of stacked basalt slabs, packed silt-clay, and heavy, vitrified mud-brick. Buildings are piled one atop another, sharing walls, roofs, and foundations, forming a dense, honeycombed hive that stretches to the misty edges of the valley. Because there are no trees, lintels and roof beams are fashioned from the massive, arching rib-bones of deep-water bay-beasts or thick slabs of split slate. Doorways are covered by heavy, greasy hides sewn together with sinew. Narrow stairways, carved directly into the un-softened rock, squeeze between three-story stone tenements, dripping with warm condensate that trickles down the sheer, dark facades.

Despite the total absence of foliage, the basin crawls with life. The ecology is entirely animalistic and mineral-fed. Thick, rubbly carpets of pale, non-photosynthetic slime-mats line the warm water channels, grazed upon by swarms of fat, armored mollusks the size of shield-bosses. High above the stone roofs, the dim air is dark with broad-winged, leathery gliders—blind creatures that nest by the tens of thousands in the carved eaves of the stone city. They shriek continuously, a sharp, metallic clicking that ricochets off the hard obsidian faces of the dwellings.

In the mud-filled gutters between the stone stacks, blind, pale-skinned amphibians with muscular, paddle-like limbs churn through the warm runoff, fighting over scraps of offal. The city’s inhabitants—densely packed into every courtyard, terrace, and subterranean cellar—live in immediate, tactile contact with this fauna. Hides hang from stone pegs to dry in the damp heat; bone-rot and render-tallow smell thick in the air, mixed with the acrid, metallic tang of raw sulfur and wet iron.

Technology is simple, heavy, and brutal. There is no refined metallurgy here; tools are fashioned from knapped flint, dense river-stone, bone, and horn. Thousands of manual workshops operate simultaneously on open-air stone platforms. 

Because of this, the acoustic landscape is staggering. The city is violently, deafeningly loud.

It is a wall of sound that never ebbs. Hundreds of thousands of stone hammers beat in a frantic, uncoordinated rhythm against block and bone. The crack of split rock, the scraping of heavy slate over slate, and the endless *thump-clack* of bone shuttles weaving hide-thongs echo continuously off the un-eroded, sound-reflective rock walls of the valley. Added to the human clamor is the unending roar of the fauna: the shrieking of the roosting sky-gliders, the bellowing of giant bottom-feeding mud-beasts kept in stone pens along the water pans, and the continuous, hollow hiss of thermal vents venting pressurized steam into the saturated air. 

There are no seasons here, no day or night in any recognizable sense, no dry spells, and no sudden floods. The atmosphere exists in a locked, warm equilibrium. The water wells up at the same rate it condenses from the grey sky; the steam rises at the exact speed it cools; the stone remains sharp, black, and unworn. The city simply exists in this clamorous, steamy dusk—a dense, crowded metropolis of stone, bone, and flesh, carved into a world that has only just finished cooling.
