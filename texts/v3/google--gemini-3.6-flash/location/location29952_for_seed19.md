The air at seven thousand meters is cool, dry, and entirely motionless. It holds no moisture, no scent of damp earth, no vapor trails, and no wind. It is an air that has been scrubbed by eons of high-altitude drought until only a pale, crystalline gas remains, cold enough to crisp the edges of skin but never cold enough to freeze, for there is nothing here to freeze.

This is the Oolitic Spine, a region of ancient, planed-down mountain roots floating at the edge of the upper sky. The peaks were worn flat ten million years ago; what remains is a vast, interconnected landscape of high terraces, sheer slate steps, and needle-like spires made of dense, pale-gray limestone and talc flour. Everything here has settled into its final, immutable form. There are no landslides, no tumbling stones, no shifting scree. The rock has forgotten how to fall.

It is in the geometry where the world has tired of its own rules. Space here does not bend smoothly; it folds and stacks like stacked cards. Standing on the edge of the Great Shelf, one looks down into a dry gorge four miles deep, only to see the opposite cliff face continuing *above* one's head, mirrored and upside down, without any visible arch connecting the two. Shadows do not lengthen or shorten; the sunlight sits at a perpetual, crisp thirty-degree angle, illuminating every fissure with dry, white clarity. A stone lifted from the ground and released does not drop; it remains where it was left, suspended in the silent equilibrium of an altitude that has outlived gravity.

Yet, despite the absolute dry stillness, the landscape is suffocatingly full. 

Every surface—every cliff face, every shale terrace, every underside of the impossible suspended rocks—is coated in life. It is not plant life; vegetation is scarce, appearing only as thin, brittle ribbons of pale lead-colored moss that cling to quartz veins, completely dry to the touch, snapping like resin if pressed. 

The density comes from the fauna. 

They are dry-bodied creatures, millions upon millions of them, packed shoulder-to-carapace across every square inch of stone. There are slate-shelled crawlers the size of a human palm, their backs carved with geometric ridges that match the cleavage of the surrounding shale. There are long, jointed needle-flies with parchment-thin wings that do not fly, but hang suspended by the thousands in the static air, motionless, held in place by the same spatial locking that holds the rocks. On the vertical walls, broad, flat-bodied organisms resembling lacquered tortoises overlap like shingles on a roof, their pale underbellies fused to the rock by dry, mineralized secretions.

They do not fight. They do not hunt. They do not make a sound. 

The biological economy here operates without water, sustained by the constant, low-level absorption of ambient atmospheric static and dry mineral dust. A human ear pressed against the living carpet hears no heartbeat, no breathing, no digestive churn. At most, there is a faint, microscopic rasp—the sound of a billion chitinous legs adjusting by a fraction of a millimeter once every three days, a dry, papery whisper that dies instantly in the thin air.

Interspersed among this vast, silent swarm are the marks of hand-tool users who lived—or sit locked—within the same frozen state.

Every few hundred meters along the terraces, small, primitive stone cairns rise from the rock. They are constructed without mortar, made of flat slate discs stacked with deliberate, hand-hewn precision. Small stone pegs, chipped from chert and flint using basic stone hammers, have been driven into the seams of the spatial folds, anchoring short lengths of braided sinew that hang down into mid-air, perfectly straight, pointing toward invisible gravitational wells.

In the shallow niches under the shelf edges sit small, square shelters built of dry-stacked shale. They are no larger than dog kennels. Inside them lie tools: hand-axes of black obsidian polished by thumb-friction, bone chisels with blunt, hammered heads, and small clay pots containing pulverized red ochre. On the rock walls above these shelters, handprints have been blown in ochre dust—faint, overlapping outlines of six-fingered palms pressing out from the rock face.

There are no skeletons in these shelters. The primitive occupants did not leave their tools behind when they died; rather, the tools and the shelters have simply reached the same thermodynamic pause as the mountains themselves.

To walk across the Spine is to move through an endless, open gallery where scale loses its meaning. You can step across a two-inch crack in the shale and find yourself five miles to the north, standing on a pinnacle that looks down upon the place where your foot was a moment ago, while the same silent, silver-gray beetles crawl over your boots without biting, without fleeing, merely occupying space alongside you.

The sky above is a flat, pale turquoise, uncluttered by clouds. The sunlight remains constant. The air does not move. The insects do not die, nor do they hatch. The mountains will not erode any further; they have reached the bottom of time, and there they stay, packed with silent bodies, wrapped in pale stone, waiting for nothing.
