There is no stone beneath this city. The ground for thirty leagues in every direction is a single, continuous expanse of petrified heartwood—an ancient, undulating plateau raised five thousand feet into the cold, thin air. Ages of wind and icy fog have worn the timber floor down into smooth, rolling swells, exposing growth rings as wide as two-lane roads. Where geological strata would exist elsewhere, here there are only the sweeping curves of grain, polished to a dull, oily sheen, dark as wet walnut and hard enough to turn a chisel.

The city does not sit *upon* the land so much as it was carved from it and wired into its circulatory system. It is a settlement of immense scale, but extraordinarily sparse. The structures—massive, hollowed-out burl-nodules, multi-tiered spires grown from ancient gall-tissue, and black-iron engine houses—are separated by vast, open sweeps of naked wood. Elevated viaducts of riveted steel stretch miles across the undulating grain, carrying empty iron hoppers on thick braided cables from one isolated district to another.

Everything is cool, damp, and bright. The light is the stark, unsoftened sun of high altitudes, casting long, sharp-edged shadows across the dark expanse, yet the air carries a persistent chill. Moisture does not fall as heavy rain; instead, it settles out of the high-altitude cloud-sheets as a fine, cold condensation. It beads along the miles of cold-drawn copper piping that crisscross the terrain, running down in thin, clear threads to collect in the shallow, carved gutters that follow the growth rings. Tiny, velvet-like mats of black moss line these channels, the only low vegetation that dares grow over the petrified wood.

The technology here is heavy, loud, and industrial, but its logic is tangled with the biology of the plateau. Across the open floor of the world, spaced a mile or two apart, stand the Sap-Stamps: multi-story towers of cast iron and corroded bronze. Their great iron pistons drop with a deafening, rhythmic *THUD-CLACK* into the deep heartwood wells, drawing up viscous, icy resin that hums with static electricity. The sound does not merely travel through the air; it travels through the wood substrate itself, so that standing anywhere on the plateau sends a percussive, jarring shudder straight up through the soles of your boots, striking the back of your teeth before the sound ever reaches your ears.

It is in this acoustic and spatial coupling that reality begins to shear. 

Because the landscape is a single, continuous organism-turned-substrate, space does not behave strictly according to distance. Walking *with* the grain of the floor allows a traveler to cover three miles in a matter of hundreds of paces, the horizon bowing inward as though pulled through a narrow lens. Walking *against* the grain stretches the distance impossibly; a brass-and-timber municipal building that appears fifty feet away might take an hour of hard, knee-straining effort to reach, its windows looming huge and detailed while remaining completely out of arm's reach.

The city’s infrastructure leans heavily into these physical distortions. Telegraph wires do not run through the air; copper spikes are hammered directly into specific growth rings, carrying electrical signals across leagues almost instantly through the natural conductive channels of the wood. The buildings themselves exhibit a slow, unsettling habit of structural shift. A warehouse built from grown cedar-galls may, over the course of a week, pinch its doorways shut as if reacting to a cold draft, only to open them again three growth rings to the left.

Fauna is nearly absent. Very little can survive the pounding vibrations of the Sap-Stamps and the cold, mineral-thin air. Occasionally, one might spot a rust-wing—a solitary, crow-sized creature with thin, leaf-like membranes and a heavy beak made of oxidized iron—perched silently on an overhead cableway, watching the machinery turn. They do not sing; they simply tilt their heads in time with the deep, percussive pounding of the pistons.

The atmosphere is dominated by process. Cold steam vents from the tops of the gall-towers in great, white plumes that freeze mid-air into floating crystals before falling to the dark wood. The air smells sharply of ozone, crushed juniper, cold iron, and damp, ancient cellar-wood. It is a world settled into its final, primordial state—a giant, living machine that has replaced the earth beneath it, thrumming endlessly in the cold light at the top of the world.
