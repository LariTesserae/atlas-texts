The drop into the basin is sudden—a sheer, jagged tear in the plateau where the earth seems to have collapsed only recently in geological time. The cliff walls are dark slate and fractured basalt, their edges sharp, angular, and raw, showing no smoothing from centuries of wind. Far below this tectonic trench, miles across and open to a pale, overcast sky, lies a landscape that seems to have filled every available inch of space with wet life and ancient, mortarless stone.

It is cold here. The air carries the damp, heavy chill of an underground spring exposed to daylight, smelling of cold peat, crushed stem, and wet gravel. Your breath blooms into small, slow clouds that linger in the still air before dissolving into the mist.

What looks at first like a natural rockslide on the basin floor resolves into an enormous, dense settlement—an urban sprawl of dry-stacked stone, terraced fields, and roof-meadows, built without a single scrap of metal or timber framing. Thousands of low-ceilinged dwellings are constructed entirely from massive, split schist slabs and river cobbles, huddled so closely together that the walls of one structure form the terrace foundation for the next. Streets are not paved roads, but narrow, carved sluiceways and stone staircases through which cold, clear water continuously flows.

The city is dead-silent in its movement, but it is far from empty. 

Water saturates every layer of this place. It weeps from the sharp seams of the canyon walls in thin, glistening curtains, pooling in carved cisterns at the upper tiers before cascading through stone gutters down into the lower city. The roofs are deep beds of living peat and turf, growing thick with pale mosses, broad-leaved butterbur, and dwarf mountain-ash. From above, the town looks less like masonry and more like a stepped, hummocky wetland that has learned to stack itself upward.

There is no open ground. Where stone walls do not touch, cold-hardy ferns fill the gaps, their fronds wet with beads of fog. Heavy mats of liverwort creep over the lintels of open stone doorways. Small, fat-stemmed shrubs choke the narrow gaps between buildings, their roots binding the loose stone into impossible, vertical gardens. The density is absolute; to move through the basin is to brush against wet foliage, cold wet stone, or the sodden coats of grazing animals.

The fauna has adapted entirely to this stone-and-peat hive. Heavy-bodied, mountain-built goats with thick, oily wool clamber across the sod roofs, cropping the dwarf willow. Flocks of dark-feathered sea-birds nest in the structural hollows left intentionally in the drystone walls, their soft calls swallowed almost instantly by the damp air and mossy surfaces. In the stone channels that divide the alleyways, fat, pale-bellied trout navigate the cold currents, darting under the thresholds of homes where water runs directly through the ground floors to keep them cool.

Nothing here hums or clanks. There are no wheels, no chimneys spouting charcoal smoke, no iron hinges or glass panes. Doors are woven from thick, wet reeds weighted with river stones; tools left on stone benches are chipped flint, smooth river granite, and carved bone. The architecture is pure, hand-stacked geometry, built by a culture that used the endless supply of freshly shattered rock from the canyon walls to carve out an existence entirely attuned to the damp, cold climate.

Light drops down from above in an even, shadowless grey—bright enough to see the pale veins in the cold fern leaves a mile away, but soft enough that the black wet slate never glares.

The environment is in quiet, constant motion. A trickle of stone-grit slides down a young fault-line at the edge of the valley; a runoff channel overflows its lintel, sending a fresh film of water over a flight of three hundred stone steps; a herd of goats shifts from one roof-terrace to the next, their soft, cleft hooves clicking muffled on the wet flags. The air carries only tiny, dampened sounds: the steady, low *drip-drip-drip* of condensation falling from slate eaves into moss, the rustle of heavy leaves under a mountain bird’s foot, and the muffled splash of water shifting through stone. 

It is an ecology that has swallowed a city, or a city that has grown like a lichen over the basin floor—packed tight, freezing, wet, and endlessly self-sustaining in the cold light beneath the rim.
