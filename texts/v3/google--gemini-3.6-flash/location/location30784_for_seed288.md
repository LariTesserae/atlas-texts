The air down here is cool, dense, and bone-dry. It carries no moisture—not a trickle along the stone, no dampness in the soil, no dew on the thickets. It smells of crushed basalt, chalk, and the dry, pepper-sharp scent of fibrous vegetation that has never known a drop of rain.

This is the Lower Trench, a subterranean rift stretching for miles beneath the world’s outer crust. Its vaulted roof is lost in a perpetual, cool gloom, visible only as a vast, dark curve where the dim ambient twilight dies out. What light exists comes from high above: faint, indigo-grey beams filtering down through miles-deep vertical fissures, scattered and softened by centuries of mineral dust until the entire basin sits in a quiet, shadowless dusk.

Spread across the basin floor is an ancient, city-scale expanse of stone. It is not a modern ruin of steel or pipework, but an immense urban skeleton carved directly from the bedrock eons ago. Colossal terraced plazas, wide avenues, and multi-story structures with arched doorways stretch across the terrain, spaced far apart across wide dust-flats. Time has rounded every edge. The stairs have smoothed into gentle ramps; the great square pillars that once held elevated causeways are worn down like old teeth, sanded soft by millenia of dry particulate winds. It is an architecture that has reached its final, unalterable equilibrium—so deeply eroded that the line between what was quarried and what grew naturally from the crust has blurred away.

Despite the complete absence of liquid water, the trench floor is lush with plant life. It is an ecosystem built entirely on dry chemistry. Dense, velvet-textured thickets of chalk-white moss carpet the broad stone avenues, deriving their nourishment from the mineral dust that settles out of the air. Clumps of stiff, iron-stemmed shrubs burst from the cracks in the flagstones, their leaves like thin plates of polished horn—dry, rigid, and rustling like paper when the breeze catches them. In the wider plazas grow forests of pale, hollow-trunked tube-trees. They stand twenty feet tall, dry as parchment, their crowns a tangle of fibrous, needle-like tendrils that draw nitrogen and mineral vapor directly from the stagnant cool air. If you break a branch from one, it does not bleed sap; it snaps with the sharp crack of seasoned firewood, spilling a fine, aromatic yellow powder.

Life fills every corner of this dry city, and it is overwhelmingly loud. 

The acoustics of the great cavern take the sounds of a teeming fauna and magnify them into a continuous, deafening roar. Millions of pale, six-legged cliff-striders, the size of hounds, move across the weathered facades of the buildings, their hard claws clicking and scraping against the basalt in a chaotic, rhythmic thunder. In the hollow interiors of the stone structures, massive colonies of dry-winged flyers—resembling leather-skinned swifts—roost by the tens of thousands. When a flock takes air, the beating of their stiff membranes against the stone vaults reverberates through the trench like a rolling explosion. Add to this the deep, rasping calls of heavy, shell-backed grazers wandering the dry moss-flats, and the acoustic pressure is immense. It is a wall of sound that never pauses—a physical vibration that hums through the soles of your feet and buzzes in your teeth, echoing endlessly between the floor and the distant ceiling.

Yet, despite the deafening noise and the density of the herds, the fundamental pace of the place is extraordinarily slow. Nothing here happens in a hurry. The dry flora grows by millimeters per century, locking down the shifting mineral dust into stable mounds. The animals move with measured, energy-conserving patience, their lifespans stretching across generations in the cool, constant temperature. The air currents shift only with the distant, slow breathing of the planet above, reversing direction every few weeks in a barely perceptible tide.

It is a world settled into its final form: an ancient, stone-carved metropolis surrendered entirely to a dry, noisy, and patient wilderness, buried deep where the sun never warms the dust.
