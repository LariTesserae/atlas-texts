The shelf is no larger than the nave of a small chapel, perched at an altitude where the air ought to be thin and freezing, but here it is heavy, thick, and suffocatingly warm. It smells of crushed quartz, hot copper, and wet wool. 

This is young ground. The rock beneath—a dark, vitrified mineral with the luster of fresh-poured tar—has not yet learned how to erode. There are no rounded pebbles here, no gravel, no soft pockets of silt. The edges of the fractures are razor-clean, meeting at sharp, mathematical angles, as if the terrace were extruded from the deep earth only hours ago and hardened mid-spill. Because there are no channels or gullies worn into the stone, the water does not run in streams. It moves in flat, continuous sheets, a few millimeters deep, tensioned perfectly across the flat obsidian-like slabs before sheeting off the sheer outer rim into the dim grey soup of the lower atmosphere.

It drops constantly from above—not as rain, but as a dense, warm condensation that forms directly out of the overcast sky. The light is stagnant and twilight-dark, a bruised lavender shadow that holds no sun. 

Scattered across this small, smooth amphitheater are five or six objects that do not belong to natural geology, though the rock seems to have solidified around them. They are not ruins; they are active engines of an unknown purpose. One is a seamless, dark torus, three meters across, suspended twenty centimeters above a shallow pool. It tilts slightly, silently spinning on an invisible axis. Where its lower rim passes over the water, the surface does not splash—it puckers upward, drawn into a thin, trembling ridge of liquid by a localized gravitational shear. Another artifact is a cluster of dark, needle-thin rods driven into a razor-sharp fissure. Hairline circuits, glowing with a soft, thermal violet, branch out from the base of the rods and bleed directly into the wet stone, disappearing into its translucent depths.

Plant life has barely managed a foothold. There are no trees, no mosses, no soil-rooted things. Only along the warm, vibrating housing of the spinning torus have a few pale, rubbery stalks attached themselves—hair-like filaments that pulse with a faint internal hydraulics, fanning the humid air for floating mineral dust.

Yet the place is wildly, loudly alive with animal motion. 

Because the rock is warm and wet, it crawls. Hundreds of small, glass-shelled organisms—hexapods with legs like sewing needles—skitter across the un-eroded stone. Their joints click continuously, a frantic, dry percussion that echoes off the tight wall of the high terrace: *clack-clack-clack-clack*. They do not walk through the water; they skate upon its surface tension, gathering at the edges of the violet-glowing circuits to lick at the warm, ionized mist that rises from the stone.

Floating above them, drifting through the dim humidity, are pale, saucer-shaped creatures the size of dinner plates. They have no eyes or limbs, only translucent, gas-filled mantles that contract with rhythmic, wet *whuffs*. They feed on the tiny, glowing midges that bloom in bursts from the warm pools. Every few seconds, one of these floaters draws too close to the spinning torus, and its outer rim flashes with a soft, static snap, sending the creature darting away in a spastic tumble.

The sound here is an unbroken cacophony. The deep, tooth-vibrating thrum of the buried tech-nodes provides a bass note beneath the incessant, metallic clicking of the glass-crawlers. Steam jets from tiny, un-weathered pore-holes in the rock with sharp, rhythmic *hisses*, followed by the loud, frog-like rasp of the saucer-floaters in the air above. 

Everything is in motion, but nothing accumulates. There is no decay here yet—no leaf-mould, no carcasses, no rust. When one of the glass-shelled crawlers dies, its shell is immediately dissolved by the caustic, mineral-rich runoff, its chemical constituents drawn back into the sheets of water within minutes. 

The terrace feels like an open-air laboratory where the walls were never built. The technology does not care about the creatures, and the creatures use the technology simply as a hearth, basking in the exotic fields that warp the water and warm the un-carved stone. Up here, high above the unseen world below, the air remains warm, loud, and wet, trapped in its own small, frantic cycle of birth and process, waiting for the first real wind to come and wear the sharp edges down.
