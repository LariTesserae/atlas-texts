Underfoot, there is no earth. The ground is a continuous, hand-laid pavement of thick, unfired ceramic tiles, set in overlapping fish-scale courses that step down into a immense, shallow basin. The floor flexes slightly under weight, resting on millions of buried, hollow clay urns packed together like honeycomb. Everything here was made by hand, and made recently; the edges of the tiles are crisp and sharp enough to score leather, their surfaces still bearing the faint, parallel whorls of finger-prints pressed into the wet clay before it was left to dry in the dark.

The basin extends for miles into the cold gloom, a regional expanse of dense, recursive masonry. There are no hills, only massive, stepped revetments of coarse, river-silt brick, held together with dark, wet mortar that smells sharply of lime and crushed shell. Between these revetments rise thousands of narrow, interlocking spires—not carved, but constructed from raw slate shingles wedged tightly against one another and lashed with thick strands of bitumen-soaked hemp rope. 

The architecture is overwhelmingly dense. Columns stand less than a forearm’s length apart, clustered in thickets that force movement to be a sideways, shuffling affair. Above, low-slung vaulted ceilings of rough terra-cotta tubes curve down to meet walls made of packed gravel sealed in woven reed baskets. There is no wide open air, yet the scale is vast; looking through the gaps between the brick piers, the maze of stacked ceramic and bound timber continues until it dissolves into absolute, pitch-black distance.

It is cold here, a heavy, clammy chill that sits deep in the lungs. No sun reaches this expanse, and there are no lamps. What light exists is a faint, bruised non-light—perhaps a distant glow filtering through miles of porous, unglazed pottery higher up, or simply the eye trying to invent shapes in the dark. 

Moisture is omnipresent, but it does not fall as rain. Instead, the air condenses continuously against the cold mineral surfaces. Fine beads of water gather on the faces of the bricks, running downward in quiet, shuddering rivulets to pool in small, shallow depression-tiles engineered into the floor. The water collects in tiny, stagnant basins, cold and tasting faintly of iron and calcified dust.

Because the world is constructed largely of hollow shapes—tubes, urns, perforated bricks, and pipe-like vaults—it is extraordinarily loud. The sound of water dripping is amplified into an overwhelming, rhythmic metallic clatter. A single drop falling from a slate overhang into an open clay pipe below creates a booming, bell-like *pock* that travels for hundreds of yards, bouncing off millions of hard, unpolished surfaces. The cold wind, pushed through the basin by some unseen, low-altitude atmospheric gradient, whistles and hums through the millions of weep-holes left in the walls, creating a roaring, multi-tonal pipe organ effect that vibrates in the chest.

Nothing grows here. There are no weeds pushing through the mortar, no moss on the wet slate, no mold on the hemp cordage. The world is purely mineral, wood, and resin. The only living presences are rare: long, low-slung creatures with many jointed, needle-thin legs made of something resembling soft chitin or hardened cartilage. They move without sound across the sharp edges of the tiles, their pale, eyeless heads scraping at the saltpeter and white lime efflorescence that leaches from the wet seams of the brickwork. They pay no attention to the sound or the dark, slipping into the narrow channels between the hollow urns when the wind shifts.

The entire landscape is in a state of continuous, slow, physical tension. Because the tech is primitive—dependent on friction, gravity, hand-knitted cord, and uncured mortar—the structures are constantly settling. Deep within the labyrinth, a slate shingle slips an eighth of an inch, triggering a sharp, cracking sound like a rifle shot that reverberates across the basin. Elsewhere, a wall of clay blocks, soft with moisture, groans as its wooden shear-pins bend under the weight of the terrace above it. 

You walk through a world that feels as though it was built all at once, yesterday, by an unimaginable number of hands using the simplest tools imaginable—trowels, stone hammers, braided fiber, and fire-baked mud—and then left in the dark to sweat, ring, and settle into itself.
