The water here is heavy, dark, and bitterly cold. It does not sit in a quiet basin; it rushes, shears, and violently coils across an environment that is entirely, unbreakably made.

There is no natural seabed here, no stone formed by mantle or fire, no mud or sand. The world as far as the dim, slate-blue twilight allows one to see is an infinite, high-altitude complex of artificial terraces, colossal curved causeways, and sweeping, hollowed plazas. Once, perhaps eons ago, this architecture had hard corners, crisp joinery, and functional intent. Now, after uncounted ages buried beneath a plunging, high-velocity ocean, every edge has been ground down to a liquid softness. The material—a dense, pale-grey vitrified ceramic or cast composite—looks like melted bone or water-worn ivory. Miles of sweeping staircases have eroded into smooth, dipping slides; massive pillars stand like worn sea-stacks, thinned and polished by the relentless friction of the deep currents.

Though elevated high above some unseen, lower abyss—a mountain-top city or a suspended sky-plateau swallowed by the sea—the atmosphere is entirely submerged. Light arrives only as a faint, cold glow from above, filtered through hundreds of feet of churning, icy water, casting long, diffuse shadows across the vast, empty spaces.

Nothing grows here. There are no kelp forests, no carpets of moss, no algae staining the smooth, pale surfaces, nor even the simplest lichen. The composite ground is scoured perpetually clean, sterile and slick, stripped bare by the shear forces of the fluid environment. 

Yet the ocean itself is far from empty. The cold water is dense with animal life that has adapted to the smooth, plantless geometry of the ruins. Great schools of pale, blind ribbon-fish—their bodies long and translucent as iced glass—sweep through the hollow archways in glittering, undulating sheets. Heavy-shelled creatures, shaped like river stones, anchor themselves inside the worn drainage ports and structural niches, extending fine, feather-like tendrils directly into the rushing water to catch drifting organic detritus. Sinuous, muscular predators with thick, rubbery skin cruise the upper reaches of the causeways, banking effortlessly through the chaotic slipstreams, using the submerged architecture to break their line of sight as they stalk smaller current-riders.

And the water never rests. The defining state of this world is a violent, chaotic turbulence. Deep, freezing brine-jet currents tear through the open plazas like underwater gales, colliding with upwelling thermal plumes that surge from the deep interiors of the submerged buildings. Massive eddies spin up spontaneously, sweeping across the pale tiles, tearing fish from their shelter and rattling the heavy-shelled organisms in their niches before dissolving into tumbling clouds of micro-bubbles. Down-wellings fall through the open elevator shafts and sky-wells like subterranean waterfalls, driving cold, heavy fluid deep into the subterranean chambers below.

The sound of this place is a muffled, low-frequency roar. It is quiet to the ear in terms of sharpness—there are no clicks of machinery, no voices, no sharp impacts—but the water transmits a massive, continuous thrum. It is the sound of millions of tons of liquid shifting against millions of tons of ancient, rounded stone: a deep, rhythmic humming that vibrates directly through the vitrified floor, punctuated only by the wet, rushing hiss of a passing current or the muffled, echoing call of a large swimmer echoing through the drowned, endlessly open galleries.
