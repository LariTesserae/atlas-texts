The valley sits like a dropped tooth in the basalt shelf, a contained fissure no more than eight hundred paces across, roofed over by an overhang of dark slag that turns the sky into a thin, violet slit. 

It is stiflingly hot—a dry, baking radiation that wells up directly from the floor. There is no moisture here, nor has there ever been; the air tastes of sulfur, ground talc, and the hot metallic tang of sun-baked hematite. Sweat would not form here; any liquid would vanish before reaching the surface of the skin. The heat does not rise and fall with a sun; it is a permanent, heavy press generated by the deep mineral strata below.

Yet the floor of the rift is dense with growth.

These are not plants of water and sap. They are dry-chemistry flora: thick, jointed cane-structures made of polymerized resin and silicate lace, rooted in dry pumice dust. They grow with a strange, rapid urgency, fed by the hot gas vents that lace the canyon floor. Large, fan-shaped fronds of gray wax overlap in thickets, clattering against one another in the warm drafts like dry slate tiles. Tall, hollow stalks—some as wide as a man's waist—rise twenty feet into the dimness, their sides perforated with geometric breathing-holes through which the rising hot air whistles continuously. When these stalks mature, they split open with loud, violent *cracks* that sound like rifle shots, scattering clouds of fine, yellow sulfur-spores into the dark air.

Woven directly into this rattling forest is an empty city.

The settlement is built of raw, hand-chipped shale and blocks of scorched tufa, laid without mortar. It follows a strict, dense urban logic: stacked terraces cling to the fissure walls, connected by long, steep staircases carved into the live rock. There are clear districts—a cluster of low, domed granaries with narrow, lip-less doorways; wide, circular plazas paved with flat river-stones (though no river ever ran here); and narrow, canyon-like alleys where the eaves of opposing dwellings almost touch. The masonry is primitive—lintels are simple slabs of heavy basalt, shaped by stone hammers whose impact-pocks are still visible under the dust—but the layout is deliberate, organized, and complete.

Despite this urban density of stone, the place feels cavernously empty. The buildings are hushed shells. There are no doors on the jambs, no furniture inside the small, square rooms, no ashes in the low hearths that were built into the corners of the terraces. The gap between the structures is vast and still; the city was built for a density it does not hold, or perhaps it was abandoned before its streets could be filled.

The light is dim, a perpetual reddish dusk filtered through the dust-choked air and the narrow ceiling-slit above. It casts long, soft-edged shadows that barely move. Without a bright light source, the colors of the valley are compressed into a narrow range: dark bronze, ash-gray, brick-red, and the dull mustard of the sulfur-spores coating the stone ledges.

The silence you might expect from such an empty, animal-less place does not exist. The valley is loud with mechanical and mineral friction. 

The heat causes the basalt walls to expand and groan in long, deep-register shudderings that vibrate through the soles of the feet. The dry flora is never still; as the warm gas currents shift through the canyon, millions of wax-leaf fronds click, rattle, and scrape against the stone masonry. High above, where the hot air escapes through the roof-slit, a steady, hollow roaring sounds like the chimney-draft of a blast furnace. Every few minutes, a ripe seed-pod explodes somewhere in the thicket with a sharp, echoing snap, followed by the soft hiss of dry dust settling onto flat stone terraces.

Nothing moves by breath. No insects crawl through the sulfur-mats; no birds cut through the thermal drafts under the slag ceiling. The ecosystem is an engine of stone, heat, and resin, running through cycles of growth, fracture, and decay without a single drop of liquid ever passing through it.

The edges of the buildings are worn. The steps of the long staircases have been rounded by centuries of dry grit blowing down from the plateau, smoothed into gentle, concave bowls. The stone is settled, at peace with its own weight. The city does not look ruined; it looks completed, as if its builders put down their stone chisels, swept the plazas clean of chips, and left the valley to its hot, clattering, dry growth.
