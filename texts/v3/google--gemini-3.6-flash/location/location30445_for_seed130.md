The ground is not stone, though it has forgotten how to be anything else. 

It is an endless, low-lying expanse of scoured plating and cast iron, worn down through millennia of chemical rains until the sharp angles of tread and rivet have smoothed into something resembling river-rounded shale. Where seams in the decking have parted, they do not reveal soil; they open onto lower, darker decks—subterranean galleries flooded with cold, tea-colored condensate that murmurs as it flows toward unseen sumps miles away.

The scale is vast and flat, an open basin extending past the limits of sight under an unyielding darkness. There is no sun here, nor moon, only a thick, low-hanging ceiling of dark structural lattice—so high it generates its own ceiling-drizzle—punctuated at long intervals by the dull, cherry-red embers of dying transformer housings or the cold, sour-green glow of high-voltage insulators arc-ing in the mist. 

It is wet, continuously and deeply. The air is cool—a damp, drafty cold that carries the smell of wet rust, crushed quartz, and old machine oil. Water gathers everywhere: in shallow, wide depressions worn into the deckplates, in the long troughs where heavy cable-trunks once ran, and dripping in steady, rhythmic cadences from the overhead girders. The landscape has settled into its final, primordial state of decay—an artificial marsh where the water moves slowly over iron mud and fine concrete silt.

Yet, despite the stillness of the air, the basin is never quiet. The sound is deafening, an overwhelming acoustic presence that does not travel through the air so much as through the marrow of the bone. It is a compound roar: the ceaseless, iron-on-iron thrum of regional subterranean heat-exchangers, the thunderous *clack-hiss* of heavy pneumatic valves clearing atmospheric moisture every three minutes, and the deep, hollow groaning of structural plates expanding and contracting across miles of low terrain. The noise is so dense it creates visible standing waves in the oily puddles, turning every still surface into a concentric web of shivering rings.

Features are sparse. Miles of flat, pitted deck-marsh stretch between presences. Every hour or two of travel might bring a single, massive landmark into view: a hollow cooling tower, three hundred yards wide, its base drowned in a black, tepid tarn; or a solitary transmission pylon, thick as a cathedral spire, its lower crossbeams draped in hanging curtains of condensation that fall like cold rain.

Nothing breathes here. There are no birds in the dark upper girders, no fish in the flooded conduit channels, no insects skirting the edges of the oily pools. The biological world has been reduced to a quiet, stubborn minimum. In the narrow gaps between floorplates, where damp rust-dust has accumulated into a gritty sludge, sparse growths of pale, wire-like lichen cling to the metal. They are tough, cold to the touch, and metallic-smelling, drawing their sustenance from the trickling currents of mineral-laden water and the faint, ambient heat of the deep machinery. Here and there, dark, sulfurous mosses carpet the lee side of abandoned turbine casings, looking like patches of velvet soot.

The landscape is active, governed by cold, mechanical weather cycles. Every few hours, a shift in regional hydraulic pressure causes the basin floor to tilt by a fraction of a degree—an imperceptible motion that nonetheless sends thousands of gallons of shallow water sheets-gliding across the plates, drowning one area of iron-marsh while exposing another to the dripping air. Steam vents rumble to life without warning, belching tall plumes of white vapor that briefly illuminate the dark girders above before condensing into heavy rain that falls back onto the deck.

It is a world that has forgotten its original purpose but keeps performing its rituals out of pure inertia. The engines run because they are heavy enough to keep turning; the water flows because the low basins remain low; the dark stays because no one is left to turn on the lights. The place exists in an old, stable, engineered finality—a constructed wilderness that will remain exactly as it is, roaring in the cold dark, for a very long time.
