At the very crown of the world, where the air thins into a dry, warm haze, the peak does not end in a jagged point. Millions of years of patient wind have planed and scooped the mountain top into a deep, bowl-shaped amphitheater, as smooth as the inside of a sun-bleached tooth.

Below the lip of this basin, the world drops away into sheer, featureless air. Cloud decks drift far beneath, obscuring whatever lies at the base of the massif. Here, at an elevation so extreme that the sky above is a dark, bruise-colored blue, the air is curiously mild—trapped in the stone pocket and heated by the dark, mineral-rich floor. 

It is entirely dry. No rain reaches this altitude; no streams trickle down the rounded basalt slopes. What moisture exists is locked away inside the fleshy, swollen stems of the vegetation that chokes the basin, or held in the breath of the creatures that live here.

The entire sanctuary is small—scarcely two hundred paces across from lip to lip, no larger than a grand cathedral or a crowded city square. Yet within this tiny, high-altitude cradle, space is used with frantic, layered intensity.

A settlement clings to the curved inner walls of the bowl. It is built from the mountain itself: dry-stacked fieldstones, sun-baked blocks of pale clay, and thick, interwoven hurdles of tough, resinous scrub-wood. The dwellings are small, dome-roofed, and stacked directly atop one another like the cells of a wild wasp nest. Narrow ledges serve as streets; steep ladders carved from single lengths of ironwood connect the upper tiers to the lower floor. The stone steps are dished and polished smooth by the passage of countless bare feet over centuries. There is no metal here—no iron brackets, no nails. Everything is lashed with braided plant fiber, pinned with bone dowels, or held fast by the sheer weight of fitted rock.

Despite the total absence of open water, the basin is intensely green, though it is a green of a peculiar, dry variety. Dense thickets of fat, rubbery succulents line every terrace and grow directly out of the mortared walls of the houses. Hanging curtains of pale, thread-like lichen drop from roof ledges, catching the fine dust that floats on the warm air currents. Broad-leaved shrubs with waxy, silver-tinted fronds form a thick canopy over the central floor, shading the lower alleyways and keeping the ambient warmth suspended near the ground.

The light within the bowl is perpetually dim. A massive, overhanging brow of ancient, wind-smoothed granite arches over the western side of the basin, throwing the settlement into a deep, cool twilight even at mid-day. What light enters is soft and indirect, bouncing off the far rim in warm, copper-colored tones.

Because of this shade and the density of the structures, the basin feels thick with presence. It is never quiet. The acoustic shape of the stone amphitheater amplifies every sound, layering them into a continuous, booming roar. 

Thousands of small, swift-winged birds nest in the porous cliff face above the houses, their sharp, whistling calls bouncing endlessly off the smooth rock. Cliff-lizards, long as a man’s forearm and covered in dusty yellow scales, scuttle across the stone roofs with a loud, rhythmic clack of claws. Inside the crowded habitations, the sound of human labor is constant and rhythmic: the dull *thump-thump* of heavy wooden pestles crushing dry roots in stone mortars; the dry rasp of bone scrapers against stiff hides; the low, resonant hum of songs passed back and forth between the upper and lower terraces. Over it all is the deep, organ-pipe whistle of the wind shearing across the outer lip of the basin, high above the sheltered heads of the inhabitants.

Life here moves with an ancient, unhurried cadence. Nothing changes quickly in the basin. The stone was smoothed before the settlement was built; the houses have stood in their current configurations for generations, repaired with the same mud and stone whenever a wall slumps. Without winter, without rain, without the rush of seasons, time is measured only by the slow fattening of the resin-vines and the gradual wear of handholds into the granite.

At the center of the bowl, beneath the dense shade of the wax-leaf canopy, lies the village hearth-space. Here, the air smells of crushed herbs, dry earth, bird dung, and the sweet, heavy scent of burning wood-gum. Families sit shoulder-to-shoulder on benches carved directly into the bedrock. Children play in the dust with carved knuckle-bones, under the watchful eyes of heavy-feathered mountain fowl that strut freely through the doorways. 

Every inch of the place is occupied, touched, and worn smooth. It is a tiny, loud, warm island of life, suspended in the dim air at the top of the dry sky, completely settled into its final, permanent form.
