The earth here is not rock, but wood that forgot how to rot. 

At an altitude where the air should be thin and freezing, it is instead soft, thick, and warm, smelling of boiled sap, bitter tannins, and old wax. You stand on the crown of a mountain that is actually a single, primordial root-node—a mass of petrified, iron-hard heartwood five miles wide, worn smooth by eons of atmospheric wind into a high, rounded dome. There is no soil beneath your feet, only the dense, dark striations of grain, polished to a dull sheen like oil-rubbed horn.

Looking out from the ridge, the scale is oceanic, but the space itself is vast and empty. Massive, skeletal arches of lignified trunk-stone—the remains of an ancient botanical era whose canopy once scraped the stratosphere—reach across gulfs of warm air. These are not forests; they are mountain ranges made of plant matter, eroded over million-year lifecycles into rounded saddles, sheer timber-cliffs, and hollowed-out spires three thousand feet tall. Between these colossal shapes lie immense voids of open, sunlit air. You can see twenty miles across the rift to the next plateau-root, its silhouette soft and dark against the pale golden sky.

The moisture is quiet and precise. It does not rain in torrents; rather, as the warm thermals rise from the unseen depths below, condensation forms in heavy, glass-like beads on the undersides of every arch and overhang. Every few minutes, a sudden breeze will shake a spire miles away, and a fine, sparkling veil of water will drift sideways across the sunlit canyon, damping the dark wood until it shines like wet slate before evaporating again. In the hollows of the ancient bark-grain, small, saucer-sized pools of tea-colored water gather, harboring pale mosses that cling like lichen to smooth metal.

Despite the vastness of the empty air corridors, the landscape is violently loud. 

The sound is a deafening, continuous wall of biological noise that vibrates through the soles of your feet. Millions—billions—of creatures inhabit the fibrous skin of the wooden monoliths. Deep inside the hollowed, cathedral-like interiors of the dead spires, large, air-sacbed gliders unleash rhythmic, foghorn-like booms that echo for miles across the gulfs. On the outer surfaces, thick carpets of armored, beetle-like clingers scrape their mandibles against the hard grain in unison, creating a metallic, grinding roar like a thousand millstones turning at once. Swarms of long-bodied, membranous flyers—each the size of a hound—burst from the shade of the root-arches, their hard wings slapping the warm air with a sound like snapping leather. The noise does not ebb or flow; it is an engine that runs constantly, amplified by the resonant, hollow architecture of the land itself.

Human presence here is small, fragile, and clinging. 

Near the lee of a massive root-knot, where the wind cannot tear them free, a cluster of five or six dwellings hangs suspended over a two-mile drop. They are constructed entirely from the material at hand: sheets of shed bark lashed together with twisted vine-thongs, weighed down with heavy river-polished hardwood stones found in the sap-drains. 

A single inhabitant sits on a ledge carved into the grain. Their technology is stripped to the barest necessities of survival in a world of high wood. They are using a scraper fashioned from a broad, ground animal-rib bound to a flint handle, methodically shaving thin ribbons of dark wax from the lee-side of a giant tendril. Nearby, a hearth of sun-baked mud sits on a bed of wet moss; a tiny, smokeless fire fed by dried bark-pith warms a shallow gourd of collected dew. Braided ropes of tough, fiber-rich inner bark coil across the ledge, anchored into natural fissures with carved wooden wedges driven home by heavy mallets. There are no metals here, no forged edges—only bone, stone, horn, and the endless, versatile wood.

Everything is in slow, steady motion. A miles-long ribbon of mist rolls off the highest arch, dissolving as it enters a warm updraft. High above, a herd of flat-bodied sky-skimmers tilts into a thermal, their pale underbellies catching the bright, unimpeded sunlight. Below, deep in a fissure six feet wide that splits the plateau, a slow trickle of thick, amber resin moves an inch a day, trapping insects, leaves, and air bubbles in a golden tomb that will slide into the valley floor a century from now. 

The place feels finished, settled into its ultimate form. The great growth of the world happened long ago; what remains is the bones of the giant plants, smoothed by the warm winds, carved by the rain, and inhabited by a million shrieking, crawling things that have nowhere else to go.
