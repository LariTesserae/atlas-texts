There is no light here. Not the pale gray of pre-dawn, nor the dull infrared glow of cooling basalt. The darkness is absolute, a dense, sightless mantle that fills the air like water. 

Yet the air is warm—a steady, muggy 28 degrees—and thick with the scent of crushed stem, wet silt, and rich, overripe loam. 

You are standing on a high mountain terrace, a bounded amphitheater no larger than a small cathedral, suspended thousands of meters above a world you cannot see. The elevation is evident only in the subtle drop in ambient air pressure and the sharp, wild wind that occasionally shears across the outer lip of the terrace, dying instantly the moment it touches the threshold of this sheltered pocket.

Underfoot, the rock does not feel like mountain stone. Millions of years of trickling water and warm fungal action have stripped it of every sharp edge, every fracture, every grain. The substrate is a single, continuous bed of ancient basalt, worn down to a smooth, soapstone finish—curves fitting into curves, old river-channels melted into smooth, shallow gutters that run with warm, silent water. The stone has reached its final, unalterable geometry. It can erode no further without disappearing entirely.

Despite the pitch blackness, the terrace is violently lush. 

Without sun, the flora here does not feed on photons. Instead, the walls of the amphitheater are clad in thick, heavy curtains of velvet-textured moss and massive, fleshy fronds that grow in sprawling, fan-like whorls. These plants draw their energy from deep thermal gradients and the subtle, rhythmic pulse of invisible magnetic fields. Touch a leaf, and it feels warm to the palm, slick with a thick, sweet condensation that smells faintly of copper and bruised plum. Long, ropy tendrils hang down from invisible ceilings, dripping steadily onto the worn stone below. *Drip. Plink. Tock.*

And yet, for all its vegetation, the space feels hollow. The center of the terrace is almost completely empty—a wide, open volume of warm, humid air enclosed by the circular wall of plants. There are no trees trunking up from the floor, no dense thickets to push through. The life clings exclusively to the perimeter, leaving a vast, hollow pocket of darkness in the middle, an indoor-feeling clearing carved out of the night.

The silence you might expect from such darkness does not exist. The amphitheater is deafeningly loud.

It is a sonic landscape, built entirely of echo, vibration, and call. Thousands of small, invisible creatures populate the moss walls and the hanging curtains. There are blind, smooth-skinned amphibians that produce a high-pitched, metallic *clink-clink-clink* like silver hammers on slate; chitinous stridulators that fill the mid-frequency with a heavy, rhythmic sawing sound; and large, warm-blooded gliders that drop from the upper walls, their broad, leathery wings making a wet *thwack-thwack-thwack* as they loop through the empty air in the center of the terrace, hunting by acoustic reflection. Every drip of water ricochets off the curved, ancient stone, mapping the boundaries of the room in continuous, overlapping rings of sound.

Tucked into the far corner of the terrace, almost completely swallowed by heavy, weeping moss, sits a relic of human—or post-human—intervention. 

It is a single, low pillar of dark, seamless material. It has no seams, no screen, no blinking indicators. It does not glow. To touch it is to feel something that defies the local physics: its surface is completely dry, despite the incessant mist, and it feels entirely frictionless, as if your fingers are sliding across a surface that isn't quite there. 

This is the engine of the terrace. It is a piece of late-stage, localized field-architecture—a node that anchors a tiny, self-sustaining microclimate to this barren, high-altitude rock. It draws heat from the planet’s mantle without piping or vents, holds the local atmosphere in a tight, gravitational envelope against the thin mountain air, and generates the faint, harmonic frequency that the black plants use in place of sunlight. It has been doing this without maintenance for three hundred thousand years. It will likely do it for three hundred thousand more.

Nothing happens quickly here. The dynamic of the terrace is glacial, patient, and settled. The plants do not flower in seasons; they grow a fraction of a millimeter each century, slowly depositing microscopic layers of silica into the wet channels underfoot. The fauna lives long, slow lives in the dark, their heartbeats ticking at a lazy, measured pace. The water drips, collects, flows down the rounded channels to the edge of the shelf, and falls off into the absolute night, evaporating into the high wind before it ever hits the bottom.

If you sit on the smooth, warm rock and close your eyes—though closing them changes nothing—you feel the immense age of the place. The mountain has finished crumbling. The tech has settled into an eternal, unblinking function. The dark life has optimized itself to the acoustic geometry of the room. It is a place at the end of its history, warm, wet, loud, and completely content in the dark.
