There is no earth here, no raw soil or unworked rock. The floor of the world is a continuous, hand-laid expanse of heavy granite flagstones, sun-dried terracotta tiles, and massive puddled-clay basins that stretch out across a low, flat valley for miles. 

Every surface bears the distinct, fresh marks of primitive labor: the rhythmic bite of copper chisels, the dimpled press of human palms in soft mortar, the rough scrape of hand-dragged timber. Yet these edges are impossibly sharp. Despite the water that constantly sheets over them, the masonry shows zero erosion. The corners of the ashlar blocks are knife-cut crisp; the mortar seams are pristine, as if the builders laid down their wooden trowels only moments ago.

The heat is immense and unwavering, an unyielding 100 degrees that never dips or rises. Overhead, there is no sun, only an endless, vaulted canopy of fine white lime-plaster suspended high above the valley, radiating an intense, shadowless, blinding light that saturates every corner of the landscape. 

Water is everywhere, though it behaves according to an unsettling, quiet logic. It does not rain. Instead, warm water wells up silently from hair-thin joints between the perfectly fitted floor-slabs. It fills hand-carved channels cut into the stone, moving along them at a lazy, constant pace. In places, this water flows gently *uphill* along flat terracotta gutters, rising three or four feet over a series of low steps without sloshing or speeding up, before pooling in wide, shallow basins constructed of fired brick. The atmosphere is dense with vapor—a heavy, tropical humidity that hangs motionless in the air, gathering on every surface in fat, glittering beads that never drop.

Despite the emptiness of the vast open plazas, vegetation erupts in dense, deliberate clusters. There are no wild roots breaking through the floor; the flora grows exclusively from millions of hand-chiseled planting wells and sunken stone troughs built into the pavement. From these containers spring extravagant, swollen-stemmed ferns, broad-leafed arums the color of bruised plums, and thick, flowering vines that wrap tightly around solitary stone pylons. The plant growth is unbelievably lush, vibrant, and entirely motionless. Leaves do not sway, for there is no wind. A fallen flower, petrified in its prime, sits on a wet tile with its petals bright and untarnished, refusing to rot or dry out.

Spacing across this regional floor is sparse. Between the dense islands of green, there are vast, quiet reaches of wet, polished stone, open to the glaring white sky. A single, fifty-foot obelisk—constructed of rough river-cobbles bound with river-mud and lashed with dry fiber rope—might sit alone in the center of a paved flat the size of a town square. A mile away, a cluster of hand-carved stone cisterns sits half-submerged in shallow water, draped in thick sheets of emerald moss that never yellow.

Life moves through this static, built wilderness in soft abundance. Gliding across the wet granite are long, six-legged salamanders with skin like polished jade. Flat-bodied, glass-clear crabs crawl silently in and out of the water channels, their legs making tiny, muffled clicks against the fired tile. Dense clouds of iridescent, stingless flies hover above the water basins, frozen in glittering swarms that barely move a few inches to the left or right over the course of hours. Pale, crestless birds with wide, leathery wings rest on the high stone lintels, their black eyes open, watching the unmoving landscape without making a sound.

The soundscape is heavy, muffled, and almost entirely suppressed by the wet heat and the vast, flat architecture. There is no thunder, no breeze to rustle the thick leaves, no splash from the upward-flowing streams. The only sound is a low, faint, ubiquitous *hiss*—the sound of moisture continuously condensing against the scorching, hand-carved stone, a soft breath that never exhales, holding the entire valley in a bright, permanent, hand-made equilibrium.
