The light comes first, continuous and severe, striking from an sky the color of cold tin.

At this altitude—where the atmospheric crust thins until the stars are visible even at midday as faint, needle-sharp points—the world is a series of vast, chalk-white plateaus and carved shale ridges that stretch beyond the horizon. There is no moisture here, no clouds born of steam or mist, no damp soil, no rain. The air is entirely dry, thin, and cold, carrying a sharp, clean scent like crushed flint and dried mint.

Yet the high ridges are not bare. They are carpeted in an extraordinary, dense vegetation that owes nothing to water.

This dry flora blankets the tablelands in wide, isolated ribbons, leaving vast expanses of polished white quartz gravel and wind-beveled limestone between them. The plants are fibrous and heavy, rooted deeply into the dry mineral seams of the rock. Instead of soft, fluid-filled leaves, they bear thick fronds of woven silica, pale copper, and slate-blue chitin that click softly together when the dry winds move across the height. Some resemble immense, sprawling ferns made of spun glass; others form broad, cushion-like hummocks of silvery thread that yield underfoot with the dry, crisp crunch of frozen lichen. They do not circulate sap; their internal vessels carry fine, oily resins and dry, micro-crystalline powders that trade charges under the intense sunlight, storing energy directly from the glare and the friction of the high air.

Because the atmosphere is so clear, the glare is absolute. Light reflects twice: once from above, where the pale sun sits like a burning coin, and once from below, bouncing off the polished salt-plains and quartz-beds that lie nestled between the plateaus. Under this double illumination, the dry canopy glows with an inner, metallic sheen—violet, pewter, and pale straw.

The wind here does not blow in gusts; it flows like a heavy, cold river that has carved its channel over millions of years. The landscape is intensely old, settled into a deep, geometric equilibrium. The peaks are not jagged; they have been smoothed into long, sweeping curves, hollowed out by eons of dry sand-scour into perforated arches, fluted spires, and immense, saucer-shaped amphitheaters that catch and amplify the quietest currents of air.

There is a rhythm here, a weather system made entirely of dust, light, and electricity. In the early morning, as the cold sun climbs, the temperature rises just enough to expand the dry air in the deep canyons. Great columns of pale, dry dust rise thousands of meters, carrying clouds of microscopic, glowing spores from the high ridges into the upper atmosphere. These spore-fronts drift across the continental expanse like dry thunderheads, casting long, translucent shadows over the salt-flats before settling slowly back to earth fifty leagues away. When a spore-front passes over a canopy of glass-ferns, static charges crackle faintly through the fronds, setting off a slow wave of bioluminescent ripples—pales greens and yellows—that travel across the plateau in the midday sun.

The sounds are small and vast at once. There is no thunder, no rustle of wet leaves, no drip or rush of streams. Instead, there is the long, low whistle of wind passing through the carved stone arches, a sound like a distant pipe organ held on a single, eternal note. Close to the ground, there is the dry *chitch-chitch* of silica leaves expanding in the heat of the light, and the occasional soft, sliding murmur of fine quartz sand slipping down an ancient talus slope.

Fauna is rare, solitary, and quiet. Long-limbed, paper-thin grazers with shells of layered mica move through the copper-colored thickets on silent, padded joints. They do not drink; they rasp the dry resin crusts from the undersides of the fronds with serrated mandibles, their digestive fires converting the minerals into light, fibrous energy. High above, in the thin blue-black of the upper sky, broad-winged gliders—organisms as thin and wide as sails, made of translucent skin stretched over hollow bone—ride the dry thermals for weeks without landing, their shadows sweeping silently over the white plateaus.

Everything in this place operates on a vast scale and a long clock. The ridges have been where they are since before memory; the dry forests have expanded and contracted across the salt-plains in slow, rhythmic pulses that take centuries to complete. It is a world carved down to its bones, bright, cold, and immensely spacious, holding a lushness that has forgotten the need for water entirely, thriving instead on the pure, unyielding pressure of light and stone.
