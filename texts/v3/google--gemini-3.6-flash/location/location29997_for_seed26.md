The ground beneath your boots is not stone or earth, but interlocking plates of cast iron, three inches thick, stamped with a worn diamond tread. Every seam is fresh: the welds are rough, unground beads of silver-grey alloy, still wearing their purplish heat-stains, their edges sharp enough to snag wool. There is no soil here, no granite, no clay. For miles in every direction, the world is a fully fabricated basin, a sunken valley of heavy industry carved into the deep crust of the earth, vaulted over by a ceiling so distant it generates its own ceiling-weather.

It is bitterly cold. The air holds a damp, freezing weight that smells of wet copper, machine grease, and ozone. 

Above, lost in the dark beyond the reach of the widely scattered light-nodes, cold condensation gathers on massive steel I-beams. It drops continuously—a steady, fine precipitation that smells faintly of mineral spirit. This rain falls onto iron plains, sloped concrete aprons, and vast terraced steps designed to channel runoff into open zinc-clad troughs. Puddles gather in the low spots between structural plates, black as ink under the dim, widely spaced lamps, shuddering whenever the deep machinery cycles.

The light is poor and thin. High-voltage mercury-vapor fixtures, mounted on towering lattice-masts a quarter-mile apart, cast long, bluish-white puddles of illumination across the deck. Between these islands of glare lie vast gulfs of shadow, where the shapes of giant industrial assemblies—silent transformer yards, windowless pump-houses, and three-story hydraulic accumulator cylinders—rise like monolithic ruins, though they are newly minted. There is no crowding here. The scale is regional, yet the density is low; hundreds of yards of empty, wet iron plating lie between one towering structure and the next, giving the basin the feeling of a desolate, flooded shipyard built for giant vessels that have not yet arrived.

Sound dominates the darkness. It is loud—a layered, acoustic tempest that never settles. Nearest is the continuous, roaring splash of cold runoff plunging down vertical iron conduits the size of grain silos, discharging into deep subterranean spillways. Behind that lies the low, bone-rattling 60-hertz hum of massive electrical bus-bars, and every few minutes, a deafening *clack-THUMP* echoes across the valley as an automated high-pressure switch gear trips miles away, sending a shockwave through the iron floor plates that vibrates up through the soles of your feet.

Movement is constant, though mostly inanimate. The basin is an active thermodynamic organism. Cold drafts sweep across the floor, pushing banks of freezing white fog through the legs of the transmission towers. Steam vents belch clouds of thick vapor into the cold air; the vapor hits the chilly atmosphere, condenses almost instantly into ice-crystals, and falls back to the deck as a light, gritty sleet. Huge expansion joints along the floor groan with a sound like tearing sheet metal as the temperature fluctuates.

Life is rare, but present where the machine permits it. In the narrow gaps between the iron plates, where the heat from buried steam-conduits bleeds upward, a pale, rubbery fungus grows in thick, slick ribbons. It feeds on the oil-film floating on the water. On the wet, vertical faces of the concrete retaining walls, sparse mats of a dark, hair-like aquatic moss cling to the rough, un-weathered form-lines left by the wooden casting frames. 

Fauna is even sparser. Once or twice, in the beam of a distant inspection lamp, something moves along the lower lip of a dry cable-trench: a blind, pale crawler, the size of a rat, with slick, translucent skin and long, hair-thin feelers. It moves with a rhythmic, mechanical urgency, scraping grease-molds off the brass rivet-heads before vanishing back into the dark mouth of a drainage pipe.

The weirdness here is not magic, but an excess of alien logic. The landscape feels like a place built by an automated process that forgot to stop, or one designed for an inhabitant with entirely non-human needs. There are stairs with three-foot rises that lead to blank iron walls; massive, beautifully machined brass valves forty feet in diameter, turning so slowly their movement is invisible, humming with internal friction; and wide, polished steel channels that carry perfectly clear, freezing water from one dark expanse of the basin to another, for reasons no living eye is left to interpret.

Standing at the edge of a three-tiered concrete terrace, looking out over the misty, iron-paved expanse, the world seems sharp-edged and newborn. The metal has not had time to rust; the aggregate in the concrete is bright and unweathered. It is a fresh, vast, mechanical underworld, wet with its own newly made clouds, thrumming with power, completely indifferent to the dark that holds it.
