The roof of the world here is not sky, but a ceiling of unweathered slate and fracture-born ironstone that hangs five miles above the basin floor, utterly hidden in the warm, dry dark. There is no air movement to speak of—no thermals rising, no draft pulling toward distant vents. The temperature sits at an unvarying, blood-warm heat, held in perpetuity by the immense mass of the deep crust. It is a still, heavy climate that has neither morning nor evening, nor seasons, nor the passing of weather.

The ground is a vast, continuous plain of young geology. Because water has never touched these strata, there are no smoothed pebbles, no river-cut gullies, no mud, and no soil. The terrain is made entirely of sharp, crystalline planes, raw basalt steps, and fresh fault-scrapes whose edges remain as keen as knapped obsidian. Walking here requires care; every ledge is a blade, every slope a field of untrod glass and jagged feldspar plates. The dust that fills the crevices is not silt, but fine mineral ash and crushed quartz, bone-dry and abrasive.

Though darkness dominates—reduced to a thick, velvety dimness interrupted only by the faintest bioluminescent crusts along certain ore veins—the basin is far from empty. It is densely crowded with vertical and horizontal forms. Massive, needle-thin basalt fingers shoot up from the floor in clustered groves, so close together that a traveler must squeeze sideways between their cold, sharp sides. Above these, hanging ribbons of mineral dripstone—formed not by dripping water, but by ancient, high-pressure magma seeps long since solid—crisscross the dark in tangled, frozen lacework. 

In the narrow alleys between stone columns, life is abundant, strange, and entirely animal. Without sunlight or moisture, there are no plants—no moss, no lichen, no fungal caps. Instead, the ecosystem relies on a dense, non-aqueous web of lithotrophic and detrital fauna. Soft, blind creatures with thick, leather-like hides creep along the sharp rock faces, their undersides coated in a thick, dry grease that protects them from the razor edges. They move without hurry, cropping the fine, chalky efflorescence that blooms directly from the warm mineral seams. 

Higher up, clinging to the underside of suspended stone shelves, are colonies of jointed, many-legged crawlers with pale, translucent shells. They do not make sounds. Their communication, if any exists, is a silent press of antennae against rock. Larger, slow-bodied predators—blind, tube-shaped entities with ringed jaws made of calcified plate—slither through the narrow passages, hunting by heat and the faint vibration of footsteps. Everything here is dry to the touch; no slime or moisture coats these organisms. They exist in a state of waterless metabolism, extracting hydrogen and trace elements directly from the dry rock and the heavy, sulfur-tinged atmosphere.

Scattered throughout this crowded dark are the quiet marks of a primitive sapience. These are not grand cities or complex ruins, but small, manual interventions in the landscape. Every few miles, nestled into the crook of a basalt cluster, sits a low ring of stacked, unmortared stone plates—shelters built by hands that used no metal. Near these rings lie simple hand-tools: scraper blades knapped from volcanic glass, heavy hammerstones bruised at the ends from pounding mineral crusts, and long needles carved from the shed ribs of the larger burrowing fauna, likely used to pull fibrous mineral threads from the rock faces.

These inhabitants, if they are near, move as silently as the fauna. Their structures are built purely for spatial containment—small, dark niches stacked two or three high against the rock pillars, like dry-stone hives. There are no hearths, for there is nothing to burn and no cold to ward off; there are no wells, for there is no liquid to draw. Fabric is unknown here, replaced by supple, dried hides stitched together with mineral sinew to form simple mats and carrying wraps.

The soundscape is almost entirely flat. Without rain, wind, or running stream, the basin holds a deep, muffled quiet. The only breaks in the silence are immediate and close: the sharp *clack* of a crawler’s carapace dropping an inch onto a stone plate, the dry, rasping slide of a leather-hide grazer scaling a feldspar wall, or the brief, brittle crunch of mineral dust under a heavy foot. Beyond these small, near sounds, the distance holds only a low, ambient thrum—not a sound of movement, but the physical weight of millions of tons of warm rock resting in static equilibrium.

Nothing shifts here. A pile of knapped glass flakes left beside a stacked-stone ring ten thousand years ago sits precisely as it was dropped, uncorroded by air, unwashed by rain, untouched even by wind-blown dust. The sharp edges of the fault lines stay sharp; the blind fauna complete their silent, identical cycles generation after generation; the warm air neither rises nor falls. It is a vast, crowded world preserved in perpetual dark, perfectly balanced, forever young, and completely still.
