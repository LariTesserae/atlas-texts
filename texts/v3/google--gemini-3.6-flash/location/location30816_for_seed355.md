The heat here is a physical weight, thick as grease and warm as breath. It hangs in an unbroken, ink-black sky that feels only inches above the highest stone, pressing down into a broad, low-slung depression where an ancient city sleeps in the dark.

It is a city built without iron, glass, or fire. Its scale is regional—stretching across miles of sunken valley floor—but its density is strange and wide. Massive structures stand isolated in the gloom like forgotten monuments, separated by broad, wet plazas paved with single, seamless slabs of dark, porous rock. There are no narrow alleys or crowded tenements. The buildings are monumental, hollowed shapes: immense fluted domes, gently leaning obelisks, and vast colonnades whose pillars are thick as old trees and spaced fifty yards apart. Every edge has been soft-melted by time. The stone looks less constructed and more like it was poured, allowed to set, and then exposed to a million years of warm, dripping moisture.

Water is the primary actor in this landscape, but it does not obey a single master.

The air is saturated at near-boiling humidity. Condensation runs down every surface in thick, continuous sheets, filling the city with the perpetual sound of wetness: the soft, heavy *plink-plink* of drops falling from thirty-foot lintels, the quiet gurgle of shallow channels, and the low, wet hiss of steam escaping from hairline vents in the plaza floors. The water is hot. Puddles that gather in the dished steps of ancient staircases give off faint, twisting wisps of vapor that disappear immediately into the dark.

Yet the water moves strangely, infected by the broken logic of the place. In wide plazas, shallow lakes three inches deep move in slow, deliberate ripples toward the center, climbing up the bases of carved monoliths and flowing *upward* along their eroded fluting, gathering in shimmering, hot masses at the summits before evaporating into the black ceiling. Elsewhere, water hangs suspended in mid-air—spheres the size of human heads, perfectly still, glowing with no light of their own but holding a dim, bruised-purple refraction of something unseen. If you walk through them, they burst softly, washing over skin with the temperature of fever before reassembling behind you.

The darkness is nearly absolute. It is a zero-point brightness, an environment where sight is reduced to feeling out the outlines of things through the wet sheen on their surfaces. There are no stars, no moon, no lanterns. The only illumination comes from rare, anomalous phenomena: moments where the water creeping up a wall strikes a seam in the stone and bleeds a faint, cold, sulfurous glow that dies after a few seconds, or thin trails of liquid light that form on the pavement, track across a plaza like spilling mercury, and sink into the rock without a sound.

Nothing lives here that walks, flies, or crawls. The city is entirely devoid of fauna. There are no calls, no scraping of chitin, no breath other than the thermal sighing of the ground itself. 

Vegetation is equally starved, present only as a functional skin on the architecture. A rubbery, dark blue-green biofilm coats the lower third of the monoliths, smelling faintly of old sea-bed and copper. In the deeper depressions where hot water pools continuously, pale, thread-like filaments grow from the stone, fanning out like delicate underwater roots. They do not need light; they pulse softly in time with the thermal vents beneath them, expanding when a wave of hot steam rolls through, then shriveling into thin, dark veins against the rock when the air cools by a fraction of a degree.

The city is old—so old that its original purpose has dissolved into the geology. The erosion is complete and settled; the landscape has achieved a strange, permanent equilibrium with its own decay. A grand avenue, wide enough for forty men to walk abreast, runs straight through the center of the valley, but after a mile, the stone underfoot begins to pitch upward at a impossible angle. You walk along it without feeling an incline—your feet register flat ground, even as the horizon of the stone street turns vertical and disappears into the pitch above. 

To stand in the open space between two towers is to feel small, damp, and uncalibrated. The silence is profound, built from the quiet, rhythmic sounds of a world working through its own strange, solitary processes. A plume of boiling mist rises from a carved basin to the left; a hundred yards ahead, a massive stone archway, worn smooth as a river pebble, drips hot rain onto a street that has been wet for ten thousand years. The air stays hot, the dark stays thick, and the water continues its slow, impossible migrations through the stone.
