At nine thousand feet, the air should be thin and sharp, but here it is heavy, warm, and thick with the smell of sweet, wet cellulose. 

There is no dirt underfoot, nor stone, nor metal. The plateau—which stretches six miles across between two hidden mountain knees—is a single, contiguous floor of turned heartwood. It has not been carved from existing trees; rather, the floor itself was grown horizontally, laid down in interlocking hexagonal tiles of dense, pale wood that are six feet thick and continuously exude a clear, viscous water from their seams. The tiles are unweathered. Their edges are crisp and soft as fresh rind, showing no sign of grain-rot, foot-wear, or frost-heave. It is a land freshly cast, as if the biological mold was lifted only hours ago, yet it stands motionless, locked in a permanent, warm noon.

Rising from this vast, damp parquet are towering structural forms—pavilions, aqueducts, and wide-flanged buttresses—built entirely from living timber. They are not crude or knotted; they follow the precise, repeating geometries of fine masonry, but with no mortar or toolmarks. Columns four hundred feet high are formed from bundled, spiraled stems that have grown so tightly together they form smooth, fluted cylinders of pale chlorophyll-tinted alabaster. These pillars support broad, vaulted roofs made of overlapping, rubbery leaves the size of sails, turned upward to catch a perpetual, fine mist that hangs in the quiet air.

The water does not fall as rain. It condenses directly out of the sunlit atmosphere onto the high arches, where it gathers into fat, glass-like beads that run down the fluting of the columns in continuous, silent ribbons. At the base of each column, the water pools in broad, shallow basins that have been grown straight up out of the wooden floor like lip-vases. 

The scale is vast and curiously empty. Standing on one of the grand stairways—whose steps are soft, fibrous terraces that yield slightly beneath weight without leaving a mark—you can look out across miles of open space. The structures are spaced far apart. A single, colossal bio-arch might span three thousand yards of open, shimmering wooden plain before anchoring into another cluster of ribbed spires. There are no small details, no debris, no litter of fallen leaves or dead twigs. The vegetation does not shed; it expands only by thickening its own walls from within, keeping its outer surfaces perpetually raw, pale, and gleaming with condensation.

Despite the emptiness, the landscape is profoundly loud.

Without wind or animals to make noise, the sound comes entirely from the internal hydraulics of the architecture. Deep within the floor and the great pillars, water is being drawn upward under immense osmotic pressure. The wood hums with a deep, resonant *thrum* that vibrates through the soles of your feet—a low-frequency bass like a thousand wet organ pipes being fed by a steady current. Where the internal water-channels narrow inside the high arches, the liquid whistles and roars behind thin walls of translucent bark. Every few seconds, a main vascular valve somewhere beneath the plain opens with a sound like a wet cannon shot, followed by the long, ringing acoustic echo off the hollow leaf-vaults above.

Fauna is nearly absent, reduced to rare, specialized forms that seem more like moving parts of the architecture than independent creatures. Occasionally, a pale, blind slug the size of a log crawls with agonizing slowness across a wet join in the wooden floor, its skin the exact color and texture of the damp heartwood, leaving behind a trail of liquid resin that immediately hardens into a seamless, glass-smooth seal over the seam. It does not eat the wood; it merely smooths it, maintaining the absolute, un-eroded integrity of the surface.

There is no twilight here, no breeze, and no decay. The warm light filters through high, pale clouds that never move, illuminating an endless, wet city that was never inhabited, built without hands, and set into an equilibrium so rigid that even the water falling into the basins never splashes higher than the basin’s edge. It is an embryonic world, completely finished, waiting in the loud, wet sunshine for a time that does not pass.
