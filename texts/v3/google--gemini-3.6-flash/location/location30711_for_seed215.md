The geology here is unnervingly young. The mountain shelf looks as if it were cleaved out of the planet’s crust only weeks ago; its edges are sharp enough to cut leather, its fault lines untamed by frost-heave or river-carving. There are no rounded cobbles, no soft scree slopes, no soil to speak of. The rock is a dark, crystalline basalt, fractures showing raw, glossy planes that catch the high-altitude sun like broken glass.

Yet across this raw, un-eroded plateau spreads a settlement of immense scale. 

It is not a dense hive, but a vast, sparse metropolis—an urban geography stretched across hundreds of square kilometers of mountain crest. Monolithic structures rise directly from the sheared bedrock without foundations or excavation marks. They are formed from a matte-grey compound that displays no seams, no fasteners, and no signs of weathering. Towers three kilometers tall taper into needles so fine they vanish into the upper wind, held rigid against mountain-gales by localized inertia-damping fields that emit no sound and cast no light.

The air is cold—sharp enough to burn the nostrils—and thin, but heavily saturated. Water is everywhere, though there are few established rivers; the landscape has not had time to carve riverbeds. Instead, moisture condenses continuously out of the low-passing cloud decks onto the cold, unyielding surfaces. A thin, sheet-like film of water glides over public plazas five miles wide, spilling over razor-sharp basalt terraces in endless, clear curtains. Weeping rock faces frame high-level transit corridors where no vehicles run, but where the ground itself glides softly beneath the feet of whatever passes.

Vegetation has barely laid claim to the place. In the joints where raw basalt meets the seamless grey urban compound, there are black lichens, patches of silver-green velvet moss no thicker than a fingernail, and occasional tufts of a oily, needle-leafed scrub that smells of turpentine when stepped on. There are no trees, no meadows. The color palette belongs entirely to dark stone, wet grey alloy, cold sky, and water.

Despite the sparsity of plants and humans, the shelf is alive with movement. 

Herds of heavy-bodied, six-limbed quadrupeds—covered in dense, oily matted wool the color of wet iron—move across the vast, empty plazas. They treat the urban grid as a natural topography, migrating along forty-lane causeways that bridge ten-thousand-foot canyons. Their hooves make a dry, rhythmic *clack-clack* sound on the flawless pavement. Above them, in the structural alcoves of towers that house no visible inhabitants, thousands of pale, broad-winged pelagic birds nest in squeaking colonies. They dive off the edges of the city into the cloud layers below, hunting in the damp, sunless gorges where geothermal vents push warm mist up the cliff walls.

The tech operating here is so far beyond mechanical rust and wire that it feels like a property of the environment itself. Warmth emanates from the pavement in precisely tuned bands—just enough to keep the surface film of water from freezing into black ice, despite the sub-zero air. Invisible pressure gradients keep snow from accumulating on the upper concourses, pushing the drifts off into the abyss in white, powdery plumes. The architecture doesn't fight the high elevation or the tectonic youth of the range; it pins the mountains in place, lacing through the fresh faults with buried force-lattices that quiet the earthquakes before they can start.

The soundscape is wide and quiet. The wind is a long, low whistle through the gaps between needle-towers. Water drips constantly—a soft, metallic patter onto smooth stone. The birds cry out in thin, whistling keys that echo off the sheer facades, and every few minutes, the low, resonant *thrum* of a thermal column shifting somewhere deep in the city’s heart vibrates upward through the soles of your boots.

It is a place of infinite elbow room. You can walk for an hour across a elevated promenade, past empty amphitheaters sized for tens of thousands, with nothing beside you but a sheet of flowing rainwater, a passing herd of iron-wool beasts, and the cold, bright sun reflecting off a million un-rounded edges of black rock.
