The basin is forty paces across, perfectly octagonal, and sunk deep into the earth, though no earth is visible. Every surface—the floor, the steep sloping walls, the stepped tiers that ring the perimeter—is constructed from heavy, hand-molded terracotta bricks and thick slabs of dark, hand-chiseled basalt. The joints are plugged with tar and crushed lime, ancient and turned black with age. There is no soil here, no exposed bedrock, no patch of gravel that was not carried in and laid down by hand. 

Yet it has stood long enough for the water to make its own choices.

A cold, heavy mist settles into the hollow, trapping a perpetual gloom. Light enters only from above, filtered through a thick, stagnant layer of grey fog that rests like a lid on the upper rim of the walls. It is cool enough that a man’s breath would hang in pale plumes, though no man walks the floor. 

Water is everywhere, moving with a purposeful, noisy intensity. It streams down the stepped basalt walls from broad, hand-carved sluices near the lip—simple, trough-like channels cut with stone adzes into the masonry. The water hits the stone steps in continuous, drumming curtains, cascading down into deep runnels that have been worn into the hard baked-clay floor over hundreds of years. The water has ground its own winding paths straight through the tile glaze, polishing the terracotta down to a smooth, pale orange heart.

Because the space is small and hollow—an open stone chamber without trees, columns, or earth to absorb the sound—it is loudly, overwhelmingly acoustic. The slap of falling streams against basalt echoes off the angled walls, multiplying until the air feels dense with vibration. Beneath the roar of the inflows is a constant, wet sloshing and a sharp, metallic clicking that ricochets from wall to wall.

The clicking belongs to the life that has claimed the stone. 

Despite the total lack of green—there are no trees, no shrubs, only a slick, soot-colored film of micro-algae clinging to the damp mortar joints—the basin is teeming with animals. Thousands of pale, hard-shelled crustaceans, each the size of a fist, crowd the lower steps and the flooded floor channels. They have flat, paddle-like legs and heavy, blunt pincers designed not for crushing wood or plant matter, but for scraping the bacterial slime from the wet brickwork. They move in dense, jostling ribbons along the masonry seams, their legs tapping a frantic *tick-tick-tick-tick* against the glazed clay.

In the deeper drain-wells—simple square holes lined with pegged oak timbers that drop into the darkness below the floor—slick, heavy-bodied creatures nest. They are long and lunged, like thick-bodied salamanders the length of an arm, with pale, rubbery skin that never dries. They wedge themselves into the drainage grates, their blunt heads protruding into the rushing water to catch the crustaceans that get swept away by the current. When they call to one another, they produce a low, booming thump that strikes the hard walls like a wooden mallet, vibrating in the chest of anything standing nearby.

Every few minutes, the dynamic of the floor shifts. A drain clogs with shed carapaces and accumulated silt, causing the cold water to back up rapidly across the central flags. The shallow pool spreads until it reaches a secondary set of relief grooves carved deeper into the floor tiles. The salamander-like beasts writhe in the sudden swell, snapping at the dislodged crustaceans, until the weight of the water clears the obstruction with a loud, sucking *gurgle*, and the floor drains back down to a glinting, wet sheen.

The craftsmanship of the place is raw and brutal. You can see where broad stone chisels bit into the basalt blocks; you can see the thumbprints left in the clay bricks by whatever workers shaped them before they were fired in crude kilns. Bronze pegs, green with heavy verdigris, pin the larger slabs together at the corners, though many have rusted or pulled loose under the relentless pressure of the shifting water. 

There is no sense that this place was abandoned; rather, it feels as though the original builders simply provided the stone carcass, and then stepped back to let the water and the pale creatures finish the work. The ecosystem operates with a harsh, rhythmic efficiency on entirely artificial terrain. The mist thickens, a wall sluice surges with a sudden pulse of ice-cold water, and the chamber rings out with the hollow roar of splash, stone, and scrambling shell.
