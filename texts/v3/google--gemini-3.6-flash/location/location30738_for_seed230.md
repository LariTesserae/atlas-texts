High on a shelf of cold granite, wedged between two blunt peaks where the ridge narrows to a notch, sits a tiny, ancient settlement. It is no larger than a village square, perhaps eighty paces from its eastern retaining wall to the steep drop on the west, yet its layout is undeniably that of a city. 

It was laid out by people who thought in grids and terraces. There are narrow paved lanes no wider than a man’s outstretched arms, raised steps carved directly into the living bedrock, small square precincts that suggest public plazas, and a network of shallow drainage channels running down the center of every corridor. Everything is constructed from the local gray schist and heavy granite, split by hand and stacked without mortar. The masonry is dense and precise; the stones are fitted so tightly that even centuries of frost have failed to heave them apart, though wind and ice have smoothed their once-sharp chiseled edges into soft, bone-like curves.

The air up here is thin, cold, and clean, smelling faintly of wet slate and old dust. The sun hits the terrace directly, casting long, sharp-edged shadows across the flagstones, but the heat never takes hold. The stone remains cool to the touch, chilled from deep within the mountain’s core.

There are no streams here, nor heavy rains, but the altitude brings a quiet, persistent moisture. Cold air pooling in the notch causes water to condense slowly against the shaded northern faces of the stone walls. Dark, glossy streaks of dampness creep down the dry-stacked masonry, feeding thin mats of black moss in the tightest crevices. Where these seeps gather at the base of the walls, the carved stone gutters catch the slow, rhythmic drips and guide them into small, square cisterns sunk into the paving. The sound of this water—a sporadic, metallic *clack-drip* into the stone basins—is often the only sound for hours, save for the low, steady hiss of thin wind passing through the gaps in the stone lintels.

Vegetation is reluctant and minimal. Pale gray and rust-orange lichens map the surfaces of the stones in overlapping rings that must have taken centuries to grow. In the seams between flagstones, where windblown grit has gathered over generations, small tufts of stiff alpine grass grow in isolated clumps, their blades dry and yellowed at the tips. A single stunted willow, no taller than a man's knee, has wedged its gnarled trunk into a high joint between two foundation blocks, its tiny, tough leaves shining with dew that never quite evaporates before the shadows return.

The infrastructure is dense, but the space is empty. The low doorways—framed by massive lintels of dark, hand-dressed stone—lead into small, roofless chambers where the earth floor has settled flat and hard. Simple timber pegs, turned black and petrified by age, still project from some of the interior walls, and occasionally a green streak on the stone reveals where a cold-hammered copper pin was driven to hold some long-vanished shutter or drape. There are no grand towers or vaulted halls; the urbanity of the place lies in its organization, its careful management of space, elevation, and water on a scale so reduced it feels like a model of a capital city carved for a shrine.

Fauna is nearly absent. A solitary lizard, slate-gray with a pale throat, occasionally pauses on the sun-warmed lip of a drainage trough, holding its head aloft before slipping silently into a dry stone joint. High overhead, the pale outline of a mountain swift cuts through the sharp blue sky without making a sound, leaving the terrace to its silence.

Nothing is moving toward decay here, nor is anything growing. The initial settling of the stones ended long ago. The frost has done what work it could; the weak corners have already spalled off; the timber that could rot has already rotted away, leaving only what is impervious to time. The light moves across the flagstones daily, tracing the same sharp geometric shadows across the same damp gutters, holding the mountain shelf in a cold, silent equilibrium that feels as permanent as the granite beneath it.
