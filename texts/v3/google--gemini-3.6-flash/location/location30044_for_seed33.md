It is a land of sheer, impossible altitude, where the world has been pulled thin and stretched upward until only the skeletal framing of reality remains. 

There is no moisture here—not in the soil, not in the air, not in the shadows. The sky is an unblinking, slate-colored twilight, vast and dry as powdered bone. Light does not fall from a sun; it diffuses sideways through the atmosphere, a cool, dim silver glow that casts shadows toward its source rather than away from it.

Across a region that spans hundreds of leagues, the terrain is composed of dark, fine-grained basalt, alabaster, and dry salt-slate. It rises in vertical cliffs five miles high, separating colossal, empty basins from one another. But this is not merely a mountain range. The mountains are a city.

Or perhaps it is more accurate to say that the geology here expresses itself in the vernacular of a metropolis. There are no tool marks on the stone, no iron joints, no glass, no mortar, no evidence of levers or wheels or hands that held a chisel. Yet, the cliff faces are carved into thousands of tiered terraces, vaulted arcades, wide public plazas, and impossibly slender spires that pierce the upper air. Entire mountain masses have grown or settled into the shape of residential districts—districts meant for things that needed neither doors nor windows. High overhead, stone bridges three hundred feet thick span five-mile voids between peaks, bowing gently under their own weight, weathered so smooth by eons of dry wind that their edges are round as sea glass.

Despite the scale of the architecture, the land is overwhelmingly empty. The "buildings" stand miles apart across the grey gulfs, isolated monoliths in a vast, dry sea of air. A plaza capable of holding a million souls sits perched on a ledge overlooking a ten-thousand-foot drop, inhabited by nothing at all. 

The air is cool, carrying a dry, sharp chill that settles on the skin like cold linen. There is no wind in the traditional sense, but the atmosphere moves in thick, quiet currents of micro-fine silicon dust. These dust-rivers flow down the grand staircases of the peaks in silent, liquid-like cascades, filling the lower urban troughs with deep, dry currents before thermal updrafts lift the silt back to the summit-rims. It is a dry hydrological cycle, a weather system made entirely of earth and air, endlessly recycling the dust of ancient, eroded peaks.

Where the architecture meets the sky, reality loses its grip on its own rules. 

In the high districts, geometry becomes unreliable. A boulevard running straight along a ridge will appear to be ten miles long, yet walking it requires only a few dozen paces; conversely, a narrow doorway carved into a basalt wall may lead into a hallway that stretches into an infinite, dim horizon from which no sound returns. Gravity does not pull strictly downward; it leans toward the mass of the nearest smooth wall. If you walk along the side of a thousand-foot arcade, your feet remain firmly planted against the vertical stone, the world tilted ninety degrees around you, the twilight sky now a vertical wall of grey to your left.

The spires themselves flex. In the upper thermal currents, thousand-meter stone towers sway with the slow, supple grace of kelp in a deep ocean, bending decades-long arcs through the thin air without cracking or breaking, their mineral structure soft as tendon before settling back into rigid stillness.

Life is absent of green. Chlorophyll has no place here, nor does water-based tissue. In the dry, shaded corners of the colonnades, brittle shelves of grey selenite and calcified quartz bloom like bracket fungi, growing a fraction of a millimeter every century by drawing electrostatic charges from the passing dust. 

Fauna is exceedingly rare, but it exists in the spaces between the spires. Every few days, a *slate-skimmer* might be seen—a creature six paces long, constructed entirely of hollow mica-plates and dry, horn-like cartilage. It possesses no eyes or lungs. It sails the cold dust-updrafts on wide, rigid wings that make a sound like dry leaves sliding across slate. It feeds on the static electricity generated by the dust-rivers, clinging to the high vertical facades with long, needle-fine claws of raw iron, perfectly motionless for weeks at a time until the atmospheric currents shift.

The soundscape is almost absolute silence. There is no rustle of leaves, no liquid splash, no voice. The only sounds are subtle, low-frequency events: the dry *hiss* of silicon dust pouring down an empty stone elevator-shaft that was never built by a mason; the deep, musical ringing of a five-mile bridge vibrating in the cold air; and the occasional sharp *clack* of a mica-skimmer adjusting its grip on a cliff face ten leagues away, carrying clearly through the dry, dense air.

It is an ancient, settled world that has outlived its own physical laws. The city was not built to be lived in; it is simply what stone does here when left alone long enough in the cold and the dark. It respires, it shifts, it expands its empty avenues, waiting for nothing, changing only by the slow, dry friction of air against an impossible grid.
