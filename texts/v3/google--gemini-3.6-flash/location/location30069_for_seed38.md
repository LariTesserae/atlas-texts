The horizon here does not meet the sky with stone or soil, but with a vast, rolling continent of heartwood. 

This is the High Sieve: an ancient plateau raised thousands of meters into the warm upper air, stretching across a span so wide it generates its own slow-drifting weather systems. There is no mineral earth beneath it. For fifty miles down, the geology is composed entirely of compressed, fossilized, and still-living plant matter—a mountain range grown rather than uplifted, worn smooth by millions of years of gentle, equatorial rainfall.

The air at this altitude is warm and heavy, saturated with a fine, continuous mist that never quite settles into rain and never quite dissipates into cloud. Light enters the landscape indirectly, filtered through miles of overhead canopy and suspended moisture, turning the air a soft, bronze-green gloom. The sun is only ever a paler smudge in the haze above; night is merely a deepening of the bronze into a thick, velvet black.

Because the plateau has existed in this state for uncounted epochs, the erosion here is complete and profound. The sharp geometries of ancient growth have long since rounded off. Topography is defined by huge, swelling hummocks of corky bark, broad valleys of densely packed moss-slurry, and sweeping terraces carved by water through centuries of slow, acidic dripping. Ridges that may once have been the branches of gargantuan trees are now gentle, mile-wide swells, covered in a carpet of pale yellow liverworts that yield like deep carpet under the slightest weight.

Water is everywhere, but it rarely flows with violence. It weeps. It gathers in shallow, saucer-like depressions carved into the crowns of petrified stumps, forming tea-colored, tepid pools that overflow so slowly their movement is invisible. It seeps through the porous, fibrous ground, running through sub-surface conduits of hollow bark, dripping from undersides into lower layers that no light ever reaches. The water is warm, scented with resin, tannin, and the faint, sweet smell of ancient, stable decay.

The density of life is quiet but overwhelming. Every cubic meter of space holds something living. The bark-cliffs are draped in thick, rubbery vines that do not leaves, but translucent, water-filled sacs the size of barrels. Between them, nestled in the crevices of the decaying substrate, grow colonies of bladder-ferns, glass-sponges of terrestrial moss, and broad, shelf-like fungi that have hardened over centuries into shelf-rock strong enough to bear the weight of a wandering beast.

The fauna moves with the same patient slowness as the landscape itself. There are no fast predators here, no sudden flights. Huge, legless grazers—their hides the texture and color of wet cedar—slide through the upper moss-mats, leaving deep, smoothed furrows that instantly fill with water and become new streams. Smaller creatures, soft-bodied and pale, inhabit the gloom between the canopy layers: lung-moths with six-meter wingspans that drift rather than fly, resting for weeks at a time on the trunks; blind, broad-pawed burrowers that chew slow tunnels through the living wood, their metabolisms so quiet they generate almost no heat. 

Scattered across the vast terraces are the marks of something that once built, though whatever built here possessed no metal and used no fire. They are not cities, but faint, organic architectures swallowed by the world: tall, chimney-like spires of calcified reed-work, now hollow and filled with rain; circular platforms woven from the living roots of a vanished tree species, fused into single, petrified discs fifty paces across. In places, the wood-rock has been carved into long, shallow steps that lead down into black, water-filled basin-caves, their edges so worn and rounded by rain that they look less like masonry and more like the smoothed lip of a pitcher plant. No tools remain. If there were ever artifacts, the damp and the rot have long since reabsorbed them into the substrate.

The soundscape is hushed, dampened by the infinite depth of the moss and the soft, porous nature of the ground. The loudest noise is the continuous, low-frequency *thrum* of water shifting through deep vascular channels far below—a sound felt in the soles of the feet rather than heard in the ears. Above that, there is only the rhythmic *tink-plop* of heavy condensation falling from upper fronds into lower pools, the wet rasp of a grazer scraping lichen from a wall of cork, and the long, sighing exhalations of the air-sacs as the canopy swells and contracts with the diurnal temperature drift.

Nothing changes quickly here. A tree-crest three miles wide may take four hundred years to tilt and collapse into the valley beneath it, and when it does, the fall is muffled, swallowed by the deep cushion of peat. A pool may take a millennium to carve a new channel through a barrier of fossilized root. 

It is a world that has reached its final form: vast, high, warm, and wet, suspended between the clouds and an unseen earth below, endlessly digesting and renewing its own ancient body in the quiet bronze light.
