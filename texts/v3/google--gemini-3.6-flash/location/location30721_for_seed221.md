High up in the ribcage of an immense, cloud-swallowed atmospheric pump, there is a recessed gallery no wider than a ship’s cabin and no taller than a man’s reach. It sits six thousand feet above the unseen ground, anchored inside a massive structural knuckle where three iron girders converge. There is no soil here, no rock, no timber. Every surface is cast iron, cold-rolled steel, or unpolished bronze. 

The edges of the metal are raw and un-softened. The toolmarks from the planers and milling heads are fresh, crisp enough to cut an unprotected finger; the flash-lines from the sand-moulds have not been filed down, and the corners of the structural flanges remain as sharp as the day they were poured. Nothing has been smoothed by age or weather. The environment exists in an embryonic, un-eroded state, as though the machinery were completed only hours ago—and yet it has stood in this exact, locked condition forever.

It is bitterly cold. The air has the razor-thin, ache-inducing weight of high altitude, saturated with a perpetual, freezing fog that condenses instantaneously on every cold surface. Water does not merely drip here; it weeps, streams, and throbs. Thick, oily rills of icy condensate run down the vertical faces of the steel plates, pooling in the narrow channels between rivet heads and overflowing in freezing, translucent sheets. A thin, slushy film coats the horizontal crossbeams, kept from solidifying into hard ice only by the continuous, violent vibration of the structure.

There are virtually no plants. In a few stagnant corners where grease and cold runoff collect into a black paste, a slick, ink-colored lichen forms microscopic crusts over the lathe-marks, but it goes no further. Vegetation has no foothold on this wet, cold iron.

Instead, the niche belongs entirely to the fauna. They are everywhere, packed into the pocket-scale space with claustrophobic density.

They are pale, slick-skinned things, the size of a hand or a forearm, with segmented, glass-hard exoskeletons and jointed legs tipped with soft, copper-colored sucker-pads. They do not resemble crabs or frogs or insects so much as some strange, cold-blooded synthesis engineered specifically for the machine. They nest in the crevices between gear-teeth, cling three-deep to the damp undersides of the hydraulic conduits, and pile into squirming, trembling masses within the recesses of the structural gussets. Thousands of them occupy a space no larger than an armchair. Their bellies are translucent, showing dark, rhythmic pulses of icy hemolymph, and their mouthparts scrape continuously at the cold zinc coatings of the bolts and the thin films of machine oil that sweat from the stuffing boxes.

The light is dim, thick, and amber. It comes from a single, heavy glass bulkhead lamp mounted behind a protective cage of brass wire. The bulb within is dim and soot-stained, casting long, shivering shadows through the dense lattice of pipes and moving rods. The fog catches the light, suspending a dull yellow halo in the middle of the recess that barely reaches the back corners.

And the sound is absolute. It is a deafening, crushing wall of noise that leaves no room for thought. 

From deep within the wall behind the gallery comes the low, bone-shattering *thump-hiss* of a thirty-foot double-acting steam piston, a bass note so intense it turns the liquid in the eye to jelly. Above this thrumming foundation sits the high, agonizing shriek of pressurized air escaping through a bank of pin-hole relief valves, accompanied by the wet, rhythmic *clack-clack-clack* of brass check-valves operating at forty cycles a second. To this mechanical din, the creatures add their own voice: a collective, terrifying stridulation. Millions of hard, segmented limbs scrape against raw, wet iron in a shrill, incessant chatter that matches the frequency of the machinery’s hum. The noise fills the small space so completely that it ceases to feel like sound and becomes a physical pressure pushing against the chest and temple.

Nothing moves toward a conclusion here. The environment is frozen in an absolute, invariant equilibrium. The temperature never drops enough to freeze the weeping water solid, nor rises enough to clear the fog. The pistons do not wear down their brass bushings; the sharp iron corners do not rust or dull; the squirming mass of blind, cold creatures neither grows nor starves. They do not lay eggs that hatch, nor do they die and decay; they simply exist in a dense, static, squirming saturation, held fast in the roar and the wet cold of a high-altitude machine that has no beginning and no end.
