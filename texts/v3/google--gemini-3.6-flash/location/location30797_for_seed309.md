The air down here is warm and smells of boiled bark, copper grease, and the sharp, vinegar tang of fermenting sap. 

It is a single underground basin—perhaps three hundred yards across—carved into the deep basalt, but the stone is barely visible. The floor, walls, and load-bearing pillars are made entirely of a dense, self-healing wood that resembles petrified teak, laid down over centuries in thick, interwoven grain. This is not a wild forest that swallowed a settlement; it is a settlement that was grown from seed to hold machinery, then weathered until the line between timber, masonry, and iron ceased to mean anything.

Thirty feet above, the vaulted ceiling curves out of sight into gloom. The light is faint—a low, amber-green dusk that never brightens. It comes in part from the dull red glow of carbon filaments inside thick glass globes, wired through copper conduits that have been grown *into* the bark, and in part from fat, translucent galls that sprout along the main trusses, pulsing with a slow, cold bioluminescence.

Despite the total dominance of plant tissue as the primary building material, the layout is strictly urban. There are clear avenues—broad, shallow channels worn smooth by decades of foot traffic and heavy sleds—running between multi-tiered structures. These buildings are hollow, living monoliths, their windows framed by swollen lignite lintels, their doorways linteled with cast-iron plates that have been swallowed up to their rivet-heads by expanding cambium. Overhead, narrow gantry bridges made of forged iron grating span the gaps between tiers, supported by thick, fibrous cables that drip with amber resin.

Water is not a flood here, but a constant, sweating presence. The atmosphere is heavy with condensation. Every few minutes, a fat droplet releases from a high wooden vault, falling through the dim air to strike an iron boiler housing with a loud *ping*. Small, trickle-fed basins carved into the root-pavement collect these falls; the water is tepid, rich in tannins, and leaves a dark red stain wherever it overflows.

The mechanical heart of the place is far from quiet. Distributed through the space, spaced widely apart across the open basin floor, are six massive, slow-stroking pumping engines. Their flywheels—twelve feet across, made of heavy pig iron—are mounted directly into the crotches of massive, load-bearing trunks. The trees do not fight the iron; they clamp it, grow around its bearing boxes, and feed off the thermal warmth of the friction. As the iron arms rise and fall with a heavy, wet *chug-clack*, they flex the surrounding wood, which in turn compresses vascular chambers within the trunks, driving sap through overhead pipe-networks with a rhythmic, wet hiss. 

Because the structures are spaced sparsely across the floor, the cavernous air between them feels vast and empty of static clutter, but it is utterly saturated with movement. 

The fauna is terrifyingly dense. The space is a roaring engine of insect and amphibian life. 

Millions of heavy, bronze-shelled beetles, each the size of a fist, move in continuous, disciplined processions along the copper conduits, lured by the faint hum of the electrical current. Their chitinous legs clicking against the metal create a sound like rain on a tin roof, overlaid across the deep, iron thrum of the pumps. High in the dark vault, clouds of pale, blind midges form swaying columns thirty feet tall, their collective wing-beat producing a vibrating drone that rattles the teeth. 

On the lower levels, long-bodied, skinless salamanders—eight or nine feet from snout to tail—slither through the wet gutters between the plant-tenements. They are slow, blind, and indifferent to the ironwork, their pale hides glistening in the amber light as they snap mindlessly at the beetle queues. Nothing here is still. If a brass valve vents a spurt of hot steam, a hundred small, furred things leap from the surrounding bark-crevices to drink the damp heat before scattering back into the dark.

The erosion here is long-settled. There are no sharp edges left. The iron flanges of the old boilers have been licked smooth by damp air and the soft bellies of climbing lizards; the wooden staircases rising to the upper tenements are dished deep in the center from generations of tread, the grain polished like dark glass. The biome and the machine have reached a noisy, humid equilibrium. The engines keep the vault warm and drive the sap; the sap seals the pipes and lubricates the wooden slides; the fauna cleans the surfaces, eats the rot, and fills every cubic inch of the dim, cathedral-like air with a deafening, vibrating roar of life.
