The light arrives at this altitude without softening. It strikes the broad, high shelf of the mountain plain and splits against millions of razor-thin plates of grey slate and milky quartz. These rock sheets do not lay flat; they stand on end, driven upward by ancient tectonic compression into a dense, vertical forest of mineral blades. They are packed together with barely a finger’s breath between them, stretching for miles across the high basin, so tightly crowded that from a distance the terrain looks like the dark, bristling hide of a buried beast.

There is no soil. There is no moss, no lichen, no green stain of mold or algal haze. The world here is entirely inorganic, composed of stone, water, dissolved salts, and the hard, cold glare of the sky.

Despite the altitude, the place is perpetually wet. A thick, cool film of water rises continuously through capillaries in the subterranean rock, pushed up by immense subterranean pressure from deep glacial reservoirs beneath the plateau. The water does not bubble or fountain; it weeping-bleeds smoothly from the grain of the stone itself, oozing upward along every vertical face to coat the plates in a glassy, sliding skin. The surface never dries. The high sun, brilliant and unclouded, turns the entire basin into a blinding optical engine: every wet blade of rock acts as a mirror, bouncing harsh, white rays back and forth through the narrow gaps until the air within the stone-thicket seems to glow from the inside out.

The cold is constant, hanging just above the freezing point, keeping the water thin and fluid. It does not freeze into ice, nor does it evaporate into fog; the thermal balance of the plateau is absolute, locked in an unchanging equilibrium where the heat of the sun and the cold of the bedrock cancel each other out precisely, hour after hour, century after century.

Because the rock blades are packed so closely, the wind cannot sweep through the plateau cleanly. Instead, the high-altitude air is forced through millions of sub-millimetre fissures between the stone sheets. This creates a sound that is total and annihilating. It is not the whistling of a gale or the roaring of a storm, but a solid, unbroken wall of acoustic force—a high-frequency, metallic scream layered over a deep, vibrating sub-bass hum produced by the resonance of the quartz plates. The noise does not rise or fall. It does not gust. It is a single, permanent sound, so loud that it vibrates the water skin on the rocks into fine, stationary grid patterns and deadens any sound made within it. To stand at the edge of the plateau is to be swallowed by a roar that feels heavy, almost solid, pressing against the lungs and skull like deep ocean water.

In the dark, narrow slits between the wet plates, where the blinding light filters down into a cold, pale silver, life exists in minute, strange isolation. There are no plants to feed on, but the water carries a rich suspension of iron and sulfur compounds scraped from the deep crust. Tucked deep within the vertical channels are rare, flattened creatures—pale, chitinous organisms no thicker than a coin, with long, glassy cilia that anchor them against the continuous upward creep of the water. They do not move quickly; they remain motionless for years, their translucent bodies pressed flat against the quartz, absorbing the mineral wash through their undersides. Occasionally, a hard-shelled mite, blind and colorless, crawls along the submerged edge of a stone blade, its needle-like legs leaving faint, temporary scratches in the slick mineral film before the water smooths them over again.

Nothing changes here. The water does not erode the plates any further; the stone has reached an equilibrium with the liquid’s chemical signature, and the fluid simply slides over surfaces it sculpted eons ago. No rock falls, for there are no free boulders to tilt; the blades support one another in a vast, locked matrix that spans the entire regional shelf. There are no seasons on the high plain, no twilight that brings frost, no morning that brings heat. The light stays hard, the rock stays wet, the sound stays deafening, and the vast, packed forest of stone continues its silent, screaming existence under the open eye of the sky.
