The light comes first, hard and white, stripping shadows from the rock until the basin offers nowhere to hide the eye. It is the high-altitude glare of a noon sun trapped between cloud-silt and ice, reflected off millions of wet, black surfaces until the air itself seems to shimmer with liquid brilliance. 

We are in a shelf-valley, no wider than three hundred paces across, set high on the shoulder of a graying basalt massif. The mountain around it has been worn smooth over unimaginable epochs; its ridges are rounded like old knuckles, its gullies softened into broad, dripping gutters. It is very cold. Breath does not merely plume here—it drops as fine, sparkling crystals that rustle against the wool of a sleeve before vanishing into the mud.

Despite the altitude and the frost, the basin is sodden. Water is everywhere, not as snow or glacier, but as a restless, high-pressure sweat emerging from the pores of the rock itself. It seeps from horizontal seams in the cliff-face, trickles down carved slate troughs, and pools in hundreds of shallow, stone-rimmed basins that spill perpetually into one another. The ground is a saturated mattress of black gravel, wet silt, and thin, rubbery skins of dark liverwort that cling to the lee side of every boulder. There are no trees, no shrubs, no tall grasses—only this slick, ink-colored carpet, barely a millimeter thick, holding fast against the cold.

Within this small, blinding, dripping amphitheater sits an urban density made entirely by hand from cold stone and animal grease. 

It is a town, or perhaps a single, colossal dwelling that has forgotten its borders. Hundreds of low, windowless cells are stacked eight or ten deep against the curved rear wall of the basin. They are constructed without mortar, built from thin slabs of slate and basalt, carefully dry-stacked and calked with grease-soaked lichen and beaten mud. The roofs are flat, shingled with broad, flat river stones, and connected by a maze of ladders made from lashed thighbones and notched birch poles. 

There are no broad streets, only vertical chimneys of air between houses, narrow enough that two people must turn sideways to pass, and winding stairwells carved directly into the wet bedrock. Water runs down the middle of every walkway, channeled by simple, hand-chopped wooden flumes that spout from terrace to terrace, driving small, clacking wooden pestles that pound dried fish-meat in stone basins.

The sheer volume of life within this tiny footprint is dizzying, yet it moves in near-total silence. 

The air is packed with presences. Thousands of human inhabitants—wrapped in heavy, oiled skins sewn with fine bone needles—move through the vertical alleyways alongside an incredible density of small, amphibious fauna. Slick, six-legged creatures the size of hounds, their hides the color of wet iron, scramble up the slate roofs using webbed claws, hauling nets of pale, wriggling larvae up from the lowest seep-pools. Flocks of fat, featherless birds with leathery, tallow-covered skin huddle in every cranny, their round black eyes blinking against the blinding light as they endlessly preen one another’s greasy skin. 

Everything is in constant, hyperactive motion, yet the soundscape is almost entirely muted. The cold air is too thin and wet to carry noise far, and the everywhere-presence of running water absorbs the clatter of stone on stone. There is only a low, wet *shuck-shuck-shuck* of feet sliding through silt, the dull *thwap* of wet hides being stretched over wooden frames, the rhythmic click of bone needles, and the soft, continuous hush of a thousand tiny waterfalls. 

The town is never still because the basin itself is unstable in its wetness. The water does not flow in steady streams; it surges and recedes in strange, irregular pulses, driven by some subterranean rhythm deep within the mountain. Every few minutes, a seam in the upper rock will open with a wet *gargle*, spilling a warm, steaming wave of mineral-heavy water over a upper terrace. Immediately, dozens of workers scramble up the bone ladders in a silent, ant-like frenzy, redirecting the flow with handheld slate baffles, carving new channels into the mud with wooden spades, and shifting stone slabs to shield their storehouses before the water freezes into a dangerous glaze. 

Down below, another wall is being dismantled even as it is being occupied. Because the slate is laid without lime or cement, houses are temporary arrangements. If a spring dies, the three houses built around its run-off are torn down within an hour, their stones passed hand-to-hand up the terraces to be reassembled where a new trickle has just appeared. The city crawls across the basin floor like a living coral, dying on one edge and sprouting on the other, its geometry constantly reconfiguring under the fierce, shadowless light.

Inside the lower cells, out of the blinding glare, it is dark and smells of wet tallow, cold basalt, and the sweet, earth-like breath of the six-legged hounds sleeping three-deep under the benches. Fire is rare—there is little to burn except dried moss and rendered fat—so warmth comes from spatial density. Five or six people squat in a cell no larger than a merchant’s cart, their hands busy in the dimness: scraping fat from hides with ground-flint blades, twisting wet plant-fibers into cordage, or carving intricate, interlocking teeth into bone pegs that will hold the next roof against the wind.

When you look up from the bottom of the basin, through the crisscrossing network of bone ladders, dripping wooden spouts, and the frantic, silent traffic of men and beasts, the sky above is an unbearable, polished mirror. A red-bearded man, balanced on a narrow ledge three levels up, drops a heavy slab of slate into place with a dull *whump*. A surge of icy spray hits his face; he doesn't blink, merely wiped his mouth with an oiled cuff and reaches for another stone, his silhouette crisp and black against the infinite white glare of the high sky.
