The plain does not roll or wave; it fractures. At this low altitude, the ground is an endless, crisp mosaic of raw plateaus and un-smoothed mineral sheets—pale chalk, yellowed gypsum, and dark volcanic glass, sharp at the edges where tectonic forces recently pushed them upward. There has been no rain to round the corners of these rocks, no rivers to cut canyons or soften the stone into river-cobble. The geology is embryonic, jagged, and utterly dry.

Yet across this vast, flat floor, reaching out to horizons so distant the light fades into a dry, golden haze before meeting the sky, there is no empty space. The landscape is crowded to capacity.

It is a continent-spanning city, though no iron was forged to build it and no tools were shaped to lay its walls. The architecture is biological, born of instinct and collective labor. Sprawling mounds and arched gallery-towers—constructed from dried resin, regurgitated mineral dust, and tough, calcified silks—rise twenty stories above the rock face, interlocking with one another in an unbroken grid. These living towers do not stand in isolation; they form dense, multi-tiered networks connected by covered roadways, suspended tube-bridges, and deeply carved subterranean corridors. It resembles an urban landscape built at the scale of a planetary metropolis, yet its builders are non-sentient fauna: millions of beetle-like chewers, communal burrowing quadrupeds, and social, air-breathing arthropods working in vast, continuous harmony.

Woven into every crevice of this bio-city is a dense, waterless vegetation. It is lush not with sap or moisture, but with oil, wax, and heavy, fragrant resins. Thick, succulent columns—thicker than ancient tree trunks—claw upward through the gaps between the mineral towers. Their leaves are heavy, leathery plates of dark jade, burgundy, and bronze, coated in thick dusty waxes that seal them against the sun. Instead of taproots seeking groundwater, these plants send out strong, wire-like tendrils that drill directly into the chalk and volcanic glass, drawing nutrients from dry mineral dust and pulling ambient nitrogen from the warm, steady breeze. Where the foliage blooms, it produces no soft petals, but hard, shell-like blossoms filled with a sugary, petroleum-dense nectar that never evaporates.

The light here is clear and warm, a steady, bright day that bakes the dry clay walls to the consistency of terracotta. Thermal currents ripple off the flat stone, carrying the scent of warm wax, cedar dust, and sharp, dry sulfur. Because there are no clouds born of water vapor, the sky is a deep, unblemished pale yellow near the horizon, deepening to amber overhead. Weather here is driven entirely by heat and dry atmospheric convection: vast, continental dust-fronts that sweep across the flat plains every few days, depositing fine silts of lime and silica that the building-fauna immediately gather to reinforce their structures.

Despite the intense concentration of life, the world is remarkably quiet.

The sound is not a roar or a chatter, but a dry, soft tapestry of hushed noises. It is the sound of millions of small, padded feet moving over hardened resin floors; the dry, paper-like rustle of millions of thick, waxy leaves sliding against one another in the thermal updrafts; the faint, rhythmic scraping of mandibles mixing mineral paste. There are no wet slaps, no splashing, no dripping. When wind passes through the high arches of the bio-towers, it produces a low, resonant whistle, like breath passing over the lip of a dry clay vessel.

Every inch of the environment is in motion. On the upper levels of the city-mounds, long lines of armored, six-legged harvesters crop the resin-blooms, their husks shining like polished horn under the bright light. Below them, in the shaded galleries between the stone and the vegetation, sleek, pale grazers with leathery hides move in tight herds, feeding on the dry, fibrous mat-grasses that bind the dust together. Predators—long-bodied, lungless runners that scent their prey through delicate, feathered antennae—stalk through the lower vaulted arcades, moving with silent, fluid grace over the crisp mineral floor.

Nothing is wasted. When a plant dies, its waxy leaves do not rot; they dry into hard, amber-like sheets that the building-creatures break down to glaze the roofs of their galleries against the wind. When an animal dies, its bones and chitin are swiftly chewed into fine powders and mixed into the mortar of the expanding towers.

The scale of it creates its own internal systems. The heat generated by millions of metabolisms packed into the bio-towers creates steady, gentle breezes that cycle through the lower corridors, cooling the deep galleries where eggs are laid in beds of dry spore-dust. The growth of the resin-forests and the construction of the chalk-cities happen at the same pace, an unceasing, quietly efficient race where nature and structure are indistinguishable. The continent expands outward, a low, dense, warm hive of dry stone, leathery green, and silent movement, forever building itself up from the raw floor of the world.
