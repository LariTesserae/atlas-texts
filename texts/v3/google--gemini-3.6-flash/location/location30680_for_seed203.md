The light comes from above, but there is no sun. Miles overhead, the vaulted ceiling of the subterranean abyss glows with a searing, shadowless white radiance—a miles-wide sheet of incandescent cold lichen or bioluminescent mineral-bark that floods the entire void in brilliant, surgical clarity. The air is frigid, sharp enough to burn the nostrils, hovering barely a degree above freezing. Breath turns instantly into fine, dry ice-dust that floats for a moment in the glare before settling onto the floor.

There is no stone here. Stone lies far above, beneath the continental crust that seals this place away from the surface, and far below, beneath a substrate so deep it has never been measured. Everything underfoot, everything rising into the glare, is plant matter. But it is not a soft or gentle green. It is an iron-hard, calcified cellulose, pale as whalebone and sharp as knapped flint. 

The scale of the hollow is oceanic—fifty miles from wall to wall, perhaps twenty miles high—yet it is mostly empty. The density of material is sparse: vast gulfs of brilliant, freezing air separate the structural masses. Looking out across the void is like looking through clean glass. In the distance, solitary botanical pillars, five hundred feet thick and ten thousand feet tall, stretch between the floor and the roof like ivory suspension cables. They do not taper; they are straight, young, and newly thrown up by some subterranean hydraulic surge.

Erosion has had no time to soften this realm. The edges of the giant leaf-trusses underfoot are knife-sharp. The bark does not peel in soft curls; it fractures like quartz into pale, translucent splinters. Great geometric thorns, thirty yards long, project from the sides of the pillars at rigid, thirty-degree angles, their tips un-blunted, glistening with a thin skin of frozen moisture.

Moisture is intermittent, cold, and heavy. It does not fall as rain, but bleeds out of the pores of the higher structures as a freezing frost-sweat. Drops the size of houses accumulate under the high arches, hanging suspended until their weight pulls them down through miles of empty air. They shatter on the lower trusses with a sound like ringing iron, spraying fine, mist-like ice across thousands of yards of bone-white floor. Pockets of slushed water gather in the deep concavities of the giant leaves, perfectly clear, reflecting the blinding roof like mirrors of liquid tin.

Despite the prevailing stillness, the void is violently dynamic. It operates on a hyperactive, unpredictable pulse. Every few hours, the deep vascular channels within the floor expand with a groaning shudder that vibrates through the soles of your boots. Hydraulic pressure, driven by some unseen thermal pump beneath the crust, surges through the lattice. Without warning, a section of the plain will rupture: a new spire of calcified pith shoots upward into the light, growing a hundred yards in a minute, screaming as its cellulose walls split and harden in the freezing air. Whole bridges of white vegetation buckle, shatter into a million razor-sharp plates, and fall, while behind them, fresh green-white tendrils instantly weave themselves into the gap to take the tension. The atmosphere is periodically torn by short, ferocious cold squalls—windstorms generated by the sudden displacement of air as these massive botanical struts collapse and regenerate.

Sound travels immense distances here, but it is rare. Most of the time, the space holds a vast, ringing silence—the quiet of a high-altitude glacier. Then comes the sharp, deafening *crack* of a frost-split leaf, followed by the low, subterranean rumble of sap boiling under pressure miles beneath the floor.

Fauna is sparse, reduced to solitary, highly specialized presences. High up, where the cold draft catches the angles of the structural thorns, things move along the lattice. They are pale, thin-limbed creatures with carapaces of fused bark-fiber, moving with slow, deliberate precision to avoid the sharp edges of the substrate. They do not make noise. Occasionally, one will drop from a high strut, unfurling broad, papery membranes—leaves modified into rigid gliders—and float for miles across the empty glare, a tiny black speck against the infinite white vaults, before clipping itself onto another distant column.

Human presence is a scatter of dust on the ankles of the giants. 

In the crotches where the largest structural struts meet the floor, small, primitive encampments cling to the bark. They are built from what the place provides, worked with hand tools of chipped obsidian and ground bone. Small huts made of woven bast-fiber are pinned into the cellulose grain with three-foot wooden spikes, driven home with mallet blows. Long, precarious walkways made of lashed reed-poles bypass the razor-sharp edges of the floor, suspended over minor abysses by thick ropes twisted from tendril-hairs.

The technology here has no metal. Iron would rust in the cold damp; bronze is unknown. Instead, the inhabitants cut the young, uncalcified shoots with blades of obsidian lashed to bone handles. They carve simple notches into the bark to catch their footings. They gather the dried, hollow pith of dead spires to burn in small, circular stone hearths—the stone brought down generations ago from the ceiling rifts—producing small, clear blue flames that give off a faint smell of cold resin and dry grass.

On a ledge overlooking five miles of brilliant, empty air, a lone marker stands: a ten-foot pole of peeled white wood, wrapped tightly in dried tendril-rope, its base wedged into a fresh fracture in the floor. A simple, hand-carved spiral is notched into its side—a mark to measure how fast the floor beneath it is rising, or how soon the next surge will snap the ledge and send it all tumbling into the white depths below.
