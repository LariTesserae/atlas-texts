High in a mountain cirque where the air is thin and cold enough to turn every breath into a lingering phantom, there is an enclosed amphitheater of dark, unweathered basalt. The rock here is young—sheared so recently by subterranean forces that its edges remain razor-sharp, glassy, and black, untouched by the smoothing scrape of ice or centuries of gravel-slide.

Yet the basin is impossibly wet and thick with life. 

From the sheer, fractured cliffs, water does not merely trickle; it weeps in a continuous, heavy sweating. Thick, oily mosses—emerald and bruised purple—gulf the stone, dripping cold rivulets into deep pools. Ferns with fronds as long as arms spring from every crevice, their undersides silvered with perpetual condensation. The light never rises past a gloomy, slate-grey twilight, held in check by a low cloud-ceiling that rests directly upon the rim of the cirque like a lid.

There are no buildings here. No brick, no mortar, no rusted iron beams, no trace of human hands or tools. 

And yet, the hollow is an engine.

Out of the living bedrock grow pristine, uncarved mechanical forms made entirely of native quartz, magnetite, and dense obsidian. A row of towering rock sleeves—bored with microscopic precision through the cliff face—houses gigantic, self-lubricating pistons of heavy iron-veined basalt. They rise and fall in a deafening, rhythmic cadence: *thrum-clack... HISS... thrum-clack*. 

These are not artifacts left behind; they are geological structures that function as industrial machinery. Massive rotary cams formed of solid, translucent amethyst turn smoothly against plates of native copper, driving pneumatic pumps that force cold, mineral-laden water upward through hollow rock conduits. Thick black grease—a hydrocarbon sweat produced by specialized, rubbery fungi—seeps from the joints, smelling sharply of wet earth, cold ozone, and coal tar. 

The space is wide and sparse despite its lushness. The engine-components stand forty paces apart, separated by stretches of dark, waterlogged gravel and solitary, fern-wrapped basalt pillars. There is room to walk, room to stand beneath the massive, rising rock-cranks without touching them.

Reality here operates under a distorted set of physical laws. The machine parts move with terrifying velocity and immense mass, yet they produce no heat and cause no friction-wear on the pristine stone. Where a rock-piston strikes its anvil with a force that vibrates in the marrow of your teeth, the water pooling at the point of impact does not splash; it shatters into fine, floating droplets that freeze mid-air, drift upward, and melt again back into the cloud-lid. The loud, metallic *clang* of the quartz valves echoes slightly *before* the valves actually close, as if time within the cirque stutters by a fraction of a second.

Fauna fills every gap in the machinery. Thousands of pale, amphibious creatures—resembling thick-bodied salamanders with carapaces of iridescent copper-plate—crawl over the cycling pistons, harvesting the oily fungal grease with scraping mandibles. Above them, in the dim, damp canopy of fronds and steam-vents, flocking organisms resembling blind, heavy-feathered kingfishers dart between the moving parts. They roost directly upon the hot-cold pneumatic rock valves, unbothered by the thunderous concussions, their high, rhythmic whistles weaving into the roar of the mechanical pulse. Long, pale worms thread through the clear quartz conduits, churning through the high-pressure water streams like micro-turbines.

Nothing changes here. The cirque exists in a state of absolute, frozen equilibrium. The pistons have been striking the same obsidian anvils at the exact same interval for an eternity that has no past and no future. The ferns do not drop leaves; they exist at the absolute peak of their unfurling. The rain never grows heavier, the mist never clears, the dim twilight never shifts toward dawn or pitch darkness. 

It is a wilderness that is simultaneously an industrial factory floor, locked in a perpetual, loud, perfect loop, high above a world that operates by entirely different rules.
