The altitude is high—so high the air should be thin and freezing—but there is no air here. There is only the Silt-Sea, an ocean suspended against the ribs of an unfinished peak, hanging like a swollen, tea-colored blister above an unseen abyss. 

It is warm. Not the warmth of sun-baked shallows, but the thick, oppressive heat of an animal’s throat. The liquid is heavy, greasy with suspended minerals, holding a temperature that never varies. It fills a horseshoe-shaped cirque no larger than a village square, bounded on three sides by sheer, black volcanic glass that has not yet had time to dull. The rock here is young, razor-edged, and fractured; its edges are fresh enough to shave leather, untouched by smooth erosion because the fluid does not wear the stone down—it tears at it, snaps off fingers of basalt, and then glues them back together in wrong configurations.

To be inside this basin is to be crushed by sound. 

The roar is absolute and continuous, a deafening, low-register cavitation that vibrates through the teeth and the marrow of the spine. It is the sound of a mountain trying to boil without a flame. The fluid does not flow; it churns in a violent, hyperactive state of perpetual creation and collapse. Giant, tear-shaped voids—caverns of absolute vacuum—rip open in the middle of the water, hold their shape for three heartbeats, and then implode with shockwaves that crack the surrounding obsidian. Where the voids collapse, the water flashes into brief, glowing sheets of violet steam before condensing instantly back into heavy, warm liquid.

Light barely survives here. It arrives from somewhere above—a dim, bruised twilight that filters down through forty fathoms of murky, sediment-choked water. It reflects off the flat faces of the sharp rock in dull, leaden glints. There are no seasons, no day or night, only the thick, purplish gloom and the occasional spark of friction-light when two stones grind together in the current.

The rules of space are torn at the edges. In the center of the cirque, a pillar of liquid thirty feet wide streams steadily *upward*, moving faster than a falling stone, breaking the surface of the submerged basin to punch toward a ceiling that isn't there, before folding back on itself like a liquid flower and plunging down again. Nearby, a pocket of water five yards across remains entirely motionless—a perfectly rigid cube of suspended brown fluid, clear as resin, while a roaring torrent of gravel and warm water shears past its six faces without disturbing a single particle inside it.

Sparse marks of intentionality cling to the jagged walls. They are not buildings in any human sense, but small, scattered structures made of stacked, unmortared slate and fossilized bone, wedged into the crevices of the young rock. They bear no tool marks; the stones appear to have been pressed together while still soft, like kneaded clay, and left to harden in the dark. They resemble drowned shrines or the anchor-points for nets long since rotted away. They are empty. Whatever built them left no iron, no bronze, no glass—only these low, squat cairns that somehow resist the deafening pressure of the turbulence.

Life is almost absent, unable to hold a footing in the tearing, unstable medium. But occasionally, tethered to the highest, sharpest needle of basalt, a single kelp-like ribbon—pale as lard and thick as a man’s torso—beats violently against the current. It has no leaves, no roots, only a massive, muscular sucker that holds fast to the glass. It feeds on the pressure itself, pulsing in rhythm with the imploding vacuum-pockets, expanding and contracting like an exposed heart. 

Farther down, in the shadow of a half-collapsed stone cairn, tiny, translucent specks drift through the gloom. They are not fish; they are fingernail-sized discs of silica that open and shut like eyelids, flashing faint, yellow warnings whenever the water’s density shifts. They vanish within seconds, swept into one of the sudden, roaring down-drafts that scours the basin floor.

The floor itself is a moving mosaic. It is covered in a thin, scattered layer of black gravel that never settles. The gravel moves in geometric patterns—shifting from concentric circles to sharp-angled grids—driven by the acoustic vibrations that shake the basin. Every few minutes, the floor gives a violent jerk; a new fissure opens in the young basalt, spitting out a plume of boiling, sulfur-heavy water that smells of old copper, before the stone snaps shut again, pinning the liquid in place.

Nothing here rests. The basin is a small, warm, deafening engine set on a mountain-crest, continually destroying its own geometry and rebuilding it out of mud, sound, and heavy water. It feels temporary, yet it possesses the brutal, terrifying energy of something that has been tearing itself apart for five minutes or five thousand years, entirely indifferent to whether the sky above it ever clears.
