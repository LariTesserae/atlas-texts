The hollow is no larger than a village square, framed by the smooth, melt-worn ridges of a ridge-line that gave up its peaks to water eons ago. Here, the rock has no corners. Millions of years of warm rain and mineral seepages have rounded every ledge into sweeping, bone-soft terraces, so completely degraded that the geology feels less like stone and more like polished wax, dark gray and slick with continuous moisture.

The air is thick, warm as breath, and soaked in light. A high, unblinking sun saturates a layer of suspended mist, turning the space into a luminous bowl. Sunlight does not fall in beams here; it fills the valley like fluid, bouncing off wet rock, reflecting off shallow pools, and glittering in the film of condensation that covers every leaf and shell. 

Despite the brilliant glare and the humid warmth, the place is overwhelmingly silent.

It is a crowded silence, dense with life that makes no noise.

Across the broad, shallow basins of water—some no deeper than a finger’s depth, resting in the smooth depressions of the bedrock—life swarms in endless, dizzying variations. Thousands of small, translucent creatures with segmented, glass-like plates glide across the wet stone on cushions of tension. Schools of blind, ribbon-thin organisms weave through the submerged mosses in synchronized, fluttering spirals. In the damp air, clouds of midges with iridescent, triangular wings hover in tight geometric formations, shifting and breaking apart without a whisper, their flight completely silent, absorbed by the heavy, moist atmosphere.

On the higher, drier stone steps, fat, multi-limbed amphibians lie basking, their skin continuously undulating with patterns of turquoise and ochre to mimic the sun-dappled water. They do not croak or call; they communicate in rapid, thermal pulses that warm the air around them by fractions of a degree. Larger things—pale, six-legged grazers with bodies like soft, water-filled bladders—move between the scattered flora, delicately plucking fleshy spires with rubbery beaks. Their heavy feet settle onto the wet moss with only the faintest wet *slap*, instantly swallowed by the damp air.

The vegetation is lush, yet it does not crowd the space. The plants stand like isolated sculptures in a gallery, widely spaced across the wet stone terraces. There are tall, segmented reeds that resemble blown glass, their hollow stalks filling with condensed water until they tip, spill silently, and spring back up. Farther along the rim, broad-leaved canopy plants with thick, pale trunks spread wide umbrellas of translucent green, casting soft, emerald shadows over the glittering rock below. Everything is perpetually dripping, perpetually growing, fueled by the relentless light and the mineral-rich water that bleeds from hidden fissures in the ancient rock.

Woven into this ancient, living landscape are the light-touch remnants of an unthinkable technology.

They do not look like machines. Spaced far apart along the terraces stand thin, needle-like spires made of a material that is neither metal nor glass, but something that gently bends the air around it. One spire, no thicker than a sapling, rises six feet from a shallow pool; where it touches the water, the liquid does not freeze, but boils without heat, sending up thin, cool plumes of mist that feed the surrounding vegetation. Another artifact, a flat, ring-shaped device floating motionless three inches above a mossy ledge, slowly pulls ambient light into its center, casting a cool, blue-shifted glow over a small cluster of feeding creatures. 

These structures have no seams, no power sources, no rust. They have sat here so long that the local ecology has grown around and through them; small, shell-backed organisms use the field emissions of the floating ring to warm their eggs, while a species of creeping lichen draws electrical current directly from the spires' smooth surfaces. The technology does not dominate the landscape; it has been eroded into it, settled into the ecosystem like a permanent, physical law.

Dynamic processes are everywhere, happening in a quiet, rapid pulse. Water trickles, pools, evaporates into mist, condenses on leaves, and falls back to the stone in a closed, frantic cycle. A predator—a flat, leaf-shaped creature that glides over the wet rock like a shadow—engulfs a cluster of glass-shelled grazers; the strike is instantaneous, water splashing silently, leaving the wet stone clean again in seconds. Sunlight shifts through the vapor, changing the color of the rock from graphite to pearlescent silver as the mist thickens and thins.

It is a world settled into its final, most stable iteration. The mountains have already been reduced to dust; the technology has long forgotten its creators; the animals have traded sound for efficiency. Everything here moves, eats, breeds, and dissolves in a dazzling, sunlit hush, framed by rounded stone that has forgotten how to be sharp.
