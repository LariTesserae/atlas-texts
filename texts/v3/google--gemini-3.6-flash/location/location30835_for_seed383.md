The ceiling of the basin is lost in a glare of cold, white luminescence—not sunlight, but the steady glow of thick, crystalline veins running through miles of overhead basalt, casting an unblinking, shadowless brightness down upon the floor of the shelf. It is a world sunk deep below the outer skin of the earth, a wide subterranean plain five miles across, surrounded by sheer, ancient palisades of banded shale that have long since stopped crumbling. Everything here has settled into its final, exhausted geometry. The edges of the cliffs are soft as worn soap; the boulder-fields at their bases have been smoothed by eons of mineral seepage into low, rolling mounds of pale slate.

The air is perpetually cool, holding the clean, metallic chill of a deep cellar. Moisture does not fall as rain; it sweats directly from the overhead stone, collecting in shimmering beads along every lintel and arch before falling in tiny, soundless droplets onto the flagstones below. There are no rivers, only shallow, glass-clear puddles that sit in the hollows of the streets, reflecting the blinding vaults above.

Across this vast, sunken expanse spreads a city, though it resembles a city only in its geometry. It is built entirely of hand-stacked, unmortared chalk-blocks and dark river-cobble, worked with simple stone mallets and copper wedges. The walls are thick, low, and rounded, rising in terraces that step down toward the center of the plain. There are thousands of structures—beehive huts, long open-air colonnades, narrow aqueduct troughs, and vast circular plazas—yet they are distributed with a strange, wide emptiness. An avenue fifty paces broad might contain only a single, low-slung dwelling before opening into a barren square of polished limestone. The density is that of an abandoned metropolis, yet it is not abandoned at all.

Nothing grows here. There is no moss on the lintels, no lichen on the wet slate, no green anywhere in the spectrum. The world is entirely mineral: alabaster, chalk, wet soot, basalt, and the cold silver of salt-crusts forming in the dry corners of the courtyards. 

Yet the plain is teeming with life.

In the absence of plants, an ecosystem of pale, dry-shelled fauna occupies every surface. Long-bodied, segmented creatures the length of a man’s forearm scuttle along the carved channels of the streets, their soft, cartilage-tipped legs making almost no sound as they scrape the salt-sweat from the stone with chitinous mandibles. Higher up, on the flat roofs of the chalk-huts, clusters of translucent, wingless gliders huddle together, their bodies throbbing with faint, pale-cyan light as they filter the damp air for airborne spores and mineral dust. Smaller things—pale, blind mites and smooth, eel-like burrowers—ribbon through the wet silt in the gutters.

The most striking feature of the basin is its silence, coupled with its frantic, continuous motion. 

The place is intensely turbulent, yet muted to a whisper. Hundreds of short, pale-skinned figures—the inhabitants of the hive-city—move through the avenues in a state of ceaseless, chaotic reorganization. Working without draught animals or complex machinery, using only sledges of dense bone, hemp ropes, and hand-cut wooden levers, they are constantly tearing down the structures and rebuilding them in new configurations. A long hall built in the morning is dismantled by midday; its chalk blocks are hauled three hundred yards across a wet plaza and stacked into a cluster of narrow spires before the cold light above shifts its spectrum.

They move with anthill energy. Thousands of paths cross and recross in the bright glare. Sledges loaded with smooth river-stone slide across wet flagstones with only a low, liquid hiss. Stone hammers strike chalk with muffled, soft thuds that die almost instantly in the heavy, damp air. There are no shouts, no commands, no songs; the inhabitants communicate through rapid, tactile gestures of their four-fingered hands and the gentle tapping of knuckles against stone.

Because of this constant construction, the layout of the valley is never stable for more than a few hours. A broad thoroughfare may exist at dusk, only to be choked with a maze of low, stepped shrines by dawn. Aqueduct channels are diverted, extended, severed, and re-joined in a frantic, unceasing choreography. The ancient stone underfoot bears the scars of this activity—deep, polished grooves worn into the bedrock where millions of heavy blocks have been dragged over thousands of years, smoothed down until the floor of the valley looks like carved butter.

The dampness acts as a lubricant for this restless world. The thin film of cold water covering every block allows heavy stones to be moved with minimal force, keeping the noise of the endless restructuring to a faint, ambient rustling—like the sound of dry leaves being dragged across wet glass. 

In the center of the basin sits a broad, shallow basin within a basin, where the condensation gathers into a sheet of water no deeper than an ankle. Here, dozens of the pale builders stand waist-deep in the cold, bright pool, using primitive copper rasps to shape massive, egg-like boulders of white quartz. They do not talk. They do not rest. Around them, the gliders drift down from the high terraces, landing silently on the wet shoulders of the workers to lick the salt from their skin before launching back into the bright, cool air. 

Everything here is ancient, hand-worked, and in a state of perpetual, noiseless upheaval, existing under an unextinguishable sky of glowing rock that never warms the air, nor ever lets the shadows fall.
