The floor of the basin is not stone, nor earth, but a compacted, freezing lattice of living wood. Miles beneath the upper crust, where the warmth of the mantle has been blocked by dense basalt shelfing, a vast sub-surface rift spreads out across a regional scale. Its terrain is composed entirely of pale, cold-adapted timber—an interlocking weave of subterranean gymnosperms whose trunks are as thick as grain silos and whose roots form the cliffs, terraces, and floor of the valley itself.

The air is bitterly cold. Breath turns instantly to a thick fog that hangs low to the wood-grain deck before settling as a fine layer of rime frost. Moisture is subtle but persistent; icy condensation gathers on the undersides of high, shadowy boughs and drips hundreds of feet down into the dark, striking wood and iron with sharp, irregular sounds. Pockets of black, slushy water sit trapped in the natural bowls formed by coiled root-systems, reflecting nothing.

Above all else, the valley is deafening. 

The sound does not come from biological life, but from a scattered, lingering industrial effort buried within the plant mass. Miles away across the vault, massive riveted iron engines—steam-driven pumps and low-frequency geothermal vents installed decades ago—continue to stroke on automated, long-duration cycles. The acoustics of the wood-walled cavern gather these distant mechanical thmms and magnify them into a relentless, bone-shivering roar. The air vibrates continuously. It is a noise so heavy it presses against the chest, a steady, low-end acoustic violence that drowns out speech and fills every hollow in the wood.

Despite the volume, physical motion here is slow. The engines stroke at long intervals—a single piston the size of a row house descends over the course of three minutes, shrieking through rusted guides, before holding for twenty. The plants themselves grow at a glacial pace, their pale, cork-like bark hardening under the strain of frozen dampness and immense structural weight. Ice forms in thin, deliberate sheets over iron rungs and needle-mats over months, untouched by wind.

The architecture of the space is young and sharp. The rift is a recent geological rupture, and the plant-substrate has snapped rather than rotted. Great shear cliffs of splintered white wood rise three hundred feet from the basin floor, their faces raw and fibrous, with jagged fractures that have not yet been softened by decay or water-flow. Huge wood-scraps, larger than ships, lie where they cracked off from the upper canopy, wedged tightly between lower root-arches.

Human presence is sparse, industrial, and largely abandoned. Every few miles, a solitary structure sits half-swallowed by the timber: a high-roofed iron boiler house with its tin cladding buckling under frost; a single lattice-steel crane bolted directly into the heartwood of a vertical trunk; or a line of heavy electrical conduit, as thick as a wrist, pinned along a root-ridge and disappearing into the dark. These artifacts do not form a town or a continuous facility, but rather a thin scatter of specialized outposts built to service the deep pumps.

Light is scarce and monochromatic. There is no sunlight. The only illumination comes from sparse, long-life sodium lamps mounted to rusting gantries, casting a dull, amber glare across the frozen wood. In their weak halos, the waxy, blue-grey needles of the lower scrub glisten with cold wetness. Beyond the reach of these lamps, the vault falls away into a flat, dim dusk that stretches for miles into the far reaches of the shelf.

Fauna is extremely rare. Occasionally, in the quiet troughs between the engine strokes, a heavy, pale-furred animal—resembling a thick-bodied badger or cold-adapted marmot—moves deliberately across a frosted root-bridge, its claws clicking on the hard timber. They pick through the cold detritus for fungal nodules that grow in the stress-cracks of the timber, entirely indifferent to the mechanical thunder vibrating through their paws. They are few and far between; one can travel a mile across the creaking, wooden floor without seeing a single moving shape.

You stand on an elevated ridge formed by three intertwining trunks, looking down into a broad, shallow trough. The ground beneath your boots is hard as granite, patterned with the tight, concentric rings of cold-stunted growth. Below, half-submerged in a mat of frozen, pale moss, a heavy cast-iron flywheel turns once every quarter-hour with a terrible, shrieking groan of gears that sends a shower of ice chips flying into the amber dusk. The air smells of pine resin, cold iron, and damp coal-ash. The rift continues outward, vast and shadowy, a deep world of pale timber and heavy iron, operating silently in every way that matters, beneath an absolute wall of sound.
