The water here does not chill. It sits at a heavy, blood-warm equilibrium, stagnant in the deep pockets and moving with a slow, muscular pulse through the wider channels. There is no light—none from above, where the surface lies under hundreds of fathoms of silt-laden water, and almost none generated from within. It is a world carved from pitch, understood entirely through pressure, temperature, and the constant displacement of fluid against skin.

In this darkness lies an urban landscape, vast and dense, but built without a single tool, forge, or line of code.

It spreads across a sunken, low-lying basin—a regional shelf miles wide that has settled into its final, eroded shape over eons. The architecture is immense and organized, possessing the unmistakable geometry of high-density settlement: long, straight thoroughfares flanked by multi-tiered facades; sprawling, vaulted plazas; and slender, tapered spires that reach toward the invisible ceiling of the sea. Yet every surface is curved and softened, rounded off by millions of years of warm, mineral-rich currents. Stone and calcified bone have merged into a single continuous medium. The edges of doorways are melted like old wax; windows are smooth, elliptical apertures; steps have dissolved into gentle, sloping ramps covered in a fine layer of dark, velvet-like sediment.

This is a biological metropolis. It was secreted rather than constructed—excreted over thousands of generations by massive, sessile colonial organisms that grew in coordinated networks, laying down dense lime and silica according to the hydraulic demands of the water. Though the original builders are long dead, their mineral skeletons remain as a permanent urban framework, endlessly inhabited, modified, and burrowed into by the creatures that followed.

Plants are almost entirely absent. Without sunlight, there are no weeds, no kelp forests, no swaying fronds. Only a thin, sticky sludge of chemosynthetic bacteria coats the interiors of the lower vaults, feeding on sulfur-rich thermal trickles that seep up through cracks in the seabed. This sludge is the base of everything, a dark, nutrient-dense soup that smells faintly of iron and warm copper.

Where vegetation fails, fauna fills every available inch of volume. The density of life here is suffocating. 

The city’s streets and chambers are crowded with bodies. Millions of blind, pale creatures navigate the architecture in a perpetual, silent scramble. In the narrow alleys between calcified apartment towers, thick ropes of soft-bodied sea-worms twist around one another, anchored to the smooth stone by suction, their feathered gills pulsing in unison to draw in the warm water. Crawling over them are smooth-shelled, crab-like scavengers the size of shield-bosses, their long, jointed legs clicking faintly as they scrape the bacterial sludge from the walls. 

Higher up, in the open airspace—or rather, the open water—between spires, school after school of translucent, eel-like predators move with fluid precision. They have no eyes; instead, their flanks are pitted with delicate, electrosensory pores that read the subtle muscular twitches of the swarms below. When they strike, there is no sound, only a sudden, violent swirl of displacement that sends ripples washing against the surrounding stone.

The dynamic pace of the place is constant. It is an ecosystem in perpetual motion, driven by the rhythmic belching of warm subterranean water from the deep faults at the basin's floor. When the thermal currents surge, the water temperature rises a degree or two, and the entire city responds. Thousands of filter-feeding fans bloom simultaneously from the elliptical windows of the towers, turning the facades into feathered, swaying walls. When the surge ebbs, the fans retract with a soft, collective *shuck*, leaving the stone bare and bone-pale once more.

Sound travels far in this dense, warm fluid, but it is muffled, stripped of high frequencies. The soundscape is mostly quiet, a low-frequency ambient hum made up of countless tiny frictions: the rasp of millions of radulae scraping stone, the low, organ-pipe resonance of currents pushing through hollow spires, and the distant, heavy thud of large, deep-sea megafauna shifting their weight in the mud of the outer basin. 

Nothing here is new, and nothing is decaying quickly. The erosion is in balance with the accretion; as the water slowly dissolves the base of ancient arches, the encrusting colonies of tube-worms and lime-secreting mollusks reinforce the upper spans. It is a city that grows, erodes, breathes, and feeds as a single, multi-species organism, lost in the absolute dark, endlessly repeating its warm, quiet routines beneath the weight of an ocean.
