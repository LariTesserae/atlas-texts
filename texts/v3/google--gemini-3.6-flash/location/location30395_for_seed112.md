Up here, at the highest altitude of the world, the air is cold, paper-thin, and intensely bright. The sun does not warm this elevation so much as it bleaches it, pouring down in a hard, blinding glare that turns the horizon into a seam of white fire. There is no moisture—no mist, no rain, no dew, no rivers. The sky is an empty, frozen violet, scrubbed clean of clouds.

Yet the world below the sky is entirely made of wood.

It is not a forest in any recognizable sense, but a continental scaffolding of dry, iron-hard vegetation that forms both the ground and the ceiling of the region. Miles-thick trunks of petrified, silica-laced grain rise from unseen abysses below, arching into colossal vaults and interlocking girders. The mass is absolute—plant life is the sole substrate, the rock, the soil, and the atmosphere’s bones—yet the landscape feels cavernous and empty, defined by immense spaces between its structural spires. A valley here is not a depression in dirt, but a three-mile gap between two fluted, bone-white trunks of giant ironwood that stretch from horizon to horizon like suspended bridges.

Because there is no liquid water, this plant-system operates on a chemistry of dry minerals, volatile resins, and trapped atmospheric gasses. The wood does not rot; it weathers. Centuries of gale-force winds have polished the outer surfaces to the smoothness of carved ivory, fluting the bark into deep, aerodynamic grooves that scream continuously in the gale. Where the wind catches the edges of the high vaults, it has ground the timber into smooth, concave hollows, exposing the dense, layered rings beneath—rings tinged rust-red, sulfur-yellow, and metallic copper from the mineral salts the roots pull from the deep mantle.

Despite the complete absence of human craft or artificial structures, the environment functions with the terrifying, rhythmic violence of an unshielded industrial forge.

The plants themselves are mechanical. Lacking water to pump through vascular tissues, the larger spires rely on internal gas-combustion and thermal expansion. Deep within the core of the mile-wide trunks, dry volatile compounds build pressure under the fierce sunlight until massive, valved nodes at the joints snap open. The sound is a deafening, continuous thunder—a heavy, mechanical *THUMP-CLACK* that shakes the air every few seconds, followed by the shrill, roaring hiss of pressurized gas venting into the cold atmosphere. 

Across the wide, sparse expanses, giant piston-trees rise and fall in slow, staggering cycles. As the sunlight heats their south-facing bark, internal chambers expand, driving massive, calcified rams upward into the canopy with the grinding screech of wood under millions of tons of pressure. When the shade falls, they contract, dropping back with a shuddering BOOM that rattles the dry timber for twenty leagues. 

Static electricity blankets everything. The constant friction of wind scouring dry wood, combined with the movement of these organic engines, generates enormous electrical potential. Copper-threaded vines, thick as anchor cables, hang between the high arches, swinging wildly in the turbulence. Where they brush against one another, fat, blinding arcs of blue lightning snap across the gap, illuminating the pale timber lattice in strobe-like flashes.

The noise is vast and unceasing. It is a deafening orchestra of a world operating at maximum mechanical stress: the base-drum thud of gas-valves, the iron screech of expanding trunks, the crackle of arc-lightning, and the endless, multi-toned howl of the wind blowing through millions of wind-carved timber pipes.

In the vast, open channels between the spires, dynamic storms of dry dust rage. These are not sandstorms, but tornadic swirls of golden mineral pollen, glass-sharp wooden fibers, and pulverized bark, lifted by fierce thermal updrafts. They sweep through the high corridors at terrifying speeds, shifting the landscape’s temporary features in hours—filling a timber trough with fifty feet of sparkling yellow dust, then scouring it bare again before the sun reaches its peak.

Yet fauna thrives in the turbulence. Nothing soft or wet lives here; the animals are built of chitin, horn, and spring-steel tendon. Armored, beetle-like organisms the size of locomotives cling to the piston-trunks, using heavy, zinc-tipped mandibles to harvest the hot, sulfurous crust that forms around the gas vents. They move with jerky, clockwork efficiency, indifferent to the thunderous vibration of the bark beneath them. In the wide, empty air-gaps between the arches, vast flocks of paper-winged gliders drop from the high vaults. They ride the screaming exhaust-plumes of the tree-valves, their dry, translucent membranes humming like buzz-saws as they catch the updrafts, diving through the static-arcs to feed on airborne pollen-dust before rising back into the blinding glare of the upper sky.

It is a place ancient and settled in its physics, long-accustomed to its own violence. The erosion is complete—every edge has been tested by wind and friction, every movement locked into the grand, deafening rhythm of the living machinery. The air smells sharp, crisp, and metallic: a mixture of cold ozone, crushed pine-resin, dry sulfur, and the toasted, smoky scent of hot ironwood grinding against itself forever under an unblinking sun.
