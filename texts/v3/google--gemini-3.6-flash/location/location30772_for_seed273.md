To look down is to look into a mile-deep gulf of riveted iron, where the shadow of one colossal flange falls across the sunlit face of another. There is no stone here, no native granite or sedimentary clay. The mountain range is an assembly of stepped hull-plates, stacked cooling towers, and gargantuan steel trusses that stretch across the horizon in long, jagged ridges. Where a cliff would fall away in natural granite, here a wall of corrugated bronze drops sheer three thousand feet, its seams weeping lines of bright copper-green verdigris into the abyss.

The light is piercingly bright. At this elevation, above the lowland haze, the sun hits the wet, metallic surfaces with an almost violent brilliance. Glare reflects off sheet steel, off the backs of glossy, broad-leafed epiphytes, and off the standing pools of water that gather in the hollows of massive structural I-beams. The air is warm and dense with humidity, carrying a heavy, fertile smell—equal parts crushed foliage, ozone, and wet iron dust.

Nothing is bare. The built substrate, though total, has reached a ancient equilibrium with the forest that coats it. Over centuries, a thick layer of damp rust-humus has collected in every horizontal channel, every gear-valley, and along the terraced walkways of the lower decks. Out of this dark, mineral-rich soil rises a dense, multi-tiered canopy. Tree-ferns with trunks as thick as boiler pipes lock their roots into old bolt-holes, prying at the heads of ancient rivets with a slow, relentless force that takes decades to yield a millimeter. Trailing vines weave through iron latticework, binding adjacent towers together in thick, green hammocks that sway slightly in the mountain winds.

Moisture is everywhere, though it rarely falls as open rain. Instead, the warm air rises from the invisible depths below, condensing continuously against the cooler undersides of the giant metal overhangs. Water drips in a endless, scattered pulse: *pat-pat-pat* on big palm leaves, *clack-clack* on exposed copper flashing, filling narrow, rust-stained gutters that run along the edges of every terrace. In places, broken water-mains—pipes six feet in diameter, long separated from whatever pump once fed them—trickle clear, mineral-laden streams that cascade down twenty decks before breaking into fine, rainbow-lit spray.

The sound of the place is loud, a complex tapestry of the biological and the mechanical. Thousands of thick-billed, oil-black birds nest in the hollow interiors of the structural girders; their dry, clicking calls echo endlessly off the sounding-board of the steel cliffs. Swarms of heavy, iridescent beetles clamber over the mossy brass plates, their hard wing-covers rattling like handfuls of pebbles against the metal. Beneath all the animal noise lies the slow, patient pulse of the world’s hidden interior. Every seven or eight minutes, deep within the framework of a neighboring spire, a counterweight shifts. The sound begins as a low, structural groan that vibrates through the soles of your feet, builds to a wet, pneumatic sigh as a three-story piston settles half an inch deeper into its sleeve, and dies away into the chatter of the forest. 

The scale is regional, an endless geography of industrial plateaus and iron chasms. Looking out across the valley, one sees another "mountain"—a sprawling, multi-tiered refinery deck five miles long, its upper gantries swathed in brilliant green creepers, its exhaust stacks long transformed into vertical gardens of red bromeliads. Between the peaks run suspension cables as thick as ancient trees, slung low across the gulfs; birds roost along them in long, evenly spaced rows, silhouettes against the glare of the sky.

Time moves sluggishly here. The erosion is not a process of violent collapse, but of long-settled integration. The metal does not break; it softens under the wet weight of moss, rounding its sharp edges, its iron oxides blending into the rich brown silt that feeds the lower thickets. A steel staircase, its risers completely filled with fern roots and damp soil, forms a natural, terraced cascade for a condensation stream. The handrails are buried under three inches of living velvet-moss, warm to the touch from the solar heat stored in the heavy iron core beneath.

High up, on a broad platform that might once have been a crane-staging area, the density of life reaches a saturation point. Broad-leafed palms, wild orchids with thick, rubbery petals, and creeping figs choke the control cabins. A species of pale, short-tailed monkey moves through the framework, leaping from one rusted winch-housing to another, their calls sharp and ringing against the bronze plating. Below them, tucked into the dark crevice where two structural beams meet at a forty-five-degree angle, a pool of trapped rainwater reflects the blinding blue of the upper sky, framed entirely by iron nuts the size of shield-bosses and the delicate, unfurling fronds of fresh green ferns.
