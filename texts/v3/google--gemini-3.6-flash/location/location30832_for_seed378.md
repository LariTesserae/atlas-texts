At this altitude, the sun is a blinding, untamed furnace, yet the plateau does not bake dry; it sweats. 

The landscape is a high mountain shelf—a miles-wide tableland of stepped travertine, black volcanic glass, and pale limestone terraces that stretch toward a sky bleached almost white by light and water vapor. There is no soil here, no moss, no scrub, no lichen. Not a single blade of green breaks the stone. Instead, the ground is an uninterrupted expanse of wet mineral: smooth, rounded basins carved by centuries of hot, mineral-saturated upwelling; sheets of dark obsidian running with sheets of warm water; and white sulfur-crusts that glare like salt under the midday rays.

Across this vast, shimmering terrace lies a sparse, sprawling industrial city. It is not packed together, but stretched out over leagues, its elements separated by wide plains of steaming rock. Heavy cast-iron viaducts step across the terraces on thick, rust-darkened pylons. Copper-sheathed condensation towers rise thirty stories into the glaring heat, their lattice frames dripping with hot, clear droplets that shimmer as they fall. Miles of riveted steel pipeline snake across the stone, thick as old trees, pulsing with the slow, internal surge of pressurized brine. 

There are no streets in the traditional sense, but elevated iron gantries and wet stone causeways that connect cluster-nodes: open-air processing plants with massive flywheel housing, silent battery banks set in stone vaults, and vast, circular settling tanks where white mineral precipitates out of boiling water. Everything is constructed on an urban, infrastructural scale, yet the structures stand far apart, leaving vast corridors of raw, weeping stone between them.

The heat is immense—a thick, suffocating warmth generated equally by the overhead glare and the thermal water boiling out of deep fissures. Steam rises everywhere, not in violent geysers, but in slow, heavy plumes that drift across the iron gantries, softening the sharp edges of the machinery and catching the light like beaten silver.

And yet, for all its scale and industrial density, the world is eerie in its silence. 

The steam swallows sound. The heavy, moisture-laden air dampens vibrations. The massive iron flywheels turn at a pace so deliberate their hum is felt through the stone rather than heard. The only sounds are soft, liquid, and small: the rhythmic *tink-tink-tink* of hot water dripping from a copper valve onto a steel beam; the low, wet sigh of a vent releasing pressure miles away; the smooth trickle of scalding streams sliding over polished basalt.

In the complete absence of plant life, an extraordinary fauna has claimed the city and the stone.

Because there is no flora, energy does not come from photosynthesis of leaves, but from heat, minerals, and the chemical soups of the terraced pools. The fauna is abundant, creeping, soaring, and scuttling across every surface.

Along the iron pipelines, millions of hard-shelled, tile-backed crawlers the size of hand-shields move in slow, organized ribbons. Their shells are formed of deposited iron oxide and calcite, matching the reddish-brown of the rusted steel. They scrape sulfur and mineral sludge from the metal joints, their tiny rasping jaws making a sound like dry sand falling on glass.

In the deep travertine pools, translucent, leggy creatures—resembling long-limbed mantises cast in clear glass—stand waist-deep in near-boiling water. They wait with motionless patience for the tiny, shimmering brine-skimmers that hatch from the thermal sediments. Above them, riding the hot updrafts between the condensation towers, are wide-winged gliders with leathery, sun-bleached membranes. They do not flap; they drift on the thermal columns, their pale forms silhouetted against the blinding sky, diving occasionally to snatch crawlers from the upper ironwork.

The ecosystem is entirely dynamic, caught in a continuous, active loop. Water wells up from the subterranean furnace, flows through the open channels and human-scale sluices of the city, deposits its minerals onto the stone and the ironwork, and evaporates into the blinding sky. The fauna eats the minerals, preys upon itself, and dies, leaving its calcium and silica exoskeletons to be cemented into the floor by the mineral waters.

Over centuries, the built world and the geological world have fused into a single, functional organism. Iron pillars have become encased in thick jackets of white travertine, turning them into pale, ribbed columns that look organic. Rusted steel overflow pipes have been swept over by mineral flow, transforming them into artificial waterfalls that spill hot, crystal-clear water into deep basins below.

The air smells of wet iron, hot zinc, sulfur, and the sharp, clean ozone left by heavy condensation under an unshielded sun. It is a world that feels old, fully settled into its own strange logic—a high-altitude, sun-drenched forge where metal, water, and stone have built a city, and where life thrives without ever having known a tree.
