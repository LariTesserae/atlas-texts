High on a mountain shoulder where the outer air usually runs thin and cold, a strange pocket of heat lingers. It is a shallow, natural amphitheater—no more than eighty paces from the rear cliff to the lip where the mountain drops away into blue space. The air here is as warm as a green-house, heavy with the smell of wet mineral and bruised fern, held in place by the dark, heat-retaining face of the basalt curve behind it.

The stone underfoot has lost all its sharp edges. Eons of water, seeping from a horizontal seam high on the wall, have worn the basalt into swooping, liquid shapes. There are no scree piles, no jagged fractures; the rock looks like poured wax or petrified mud, scooped into shallow basins, rounded benches, and smooth, gutter-like channels that guide slow rivulets across the terrace. Everything is coated in a micro-thin layer of silica glaze deposited by the warm water, giving the grey-red stone the soft sheen of wet pottery.

Water is everywhere, yet it does not rush. It weeps. It gathers in glassy, lip-full pools that spill over without a sound, tracing predictable paths down the polished curves into the next bowl. The atmosphere continuously condenses on the overhanging cliff, falling in single, heavy drops that strike the quiet pools with a clear, resonant sound: *clink... clink.* 

There are no birds. There are no insects hovering over the surface of the water, no water-striders breaking the tension of the quiet basins, no worms in the dark silt beneath the roots. The silence is absolute, save for the dripping water and the faint, rhythmic sighing of warm vapor rising off the dark stone into the bright, high-altitude sunlight. The light is direct and golden, unsoftened by lower-atmosphere haze, making every drop of water on the foliage glisten like a tiny bulb.

Because there are no animals to chew, trample, or pollinate, the plants here have evolved into strange, self-contained solitary forms. They do not form thickets or tangled undergrowth. They are spaced widely across the wet stone, each plant occupying its own wide territory of polished rock like a sculpture on a pedestal. 

Near the center of the terrace grows a specimen of heavy, jade-colored succulent whose leaves are thick as arm-braces and tipped with translucent, water-filled bladders. Further back, where the cliff casts a long shadow, a cluster of giant, velvet-ribbed fronds emerges directly from a natural basin in the rock. The fronds do not sway; the warm air is still, and without animal life to stir them, they remain motionless for hours, slowly absorbing the mineral-rich moisture that trickles past their roots. Reproduction here is a matter of decades: heavy, spherical seed-pods, smooth as glass, grow on long stalks until their weight drops them into the water channels, where they roll slowly downhill, guided by the erosion grooves to the next empty rock-pocket.

Though the terrace is devoid of structures—there are no walls, no hearths, no shelters carved into the cliff—there are signs of a distant, patient human presence. 

On a flat shelf of rock beside one of the upper pools rests a collection of simple tools. They are not steel or iron, but hand-flaked obsidian blades and heavy, rounded river-cobbles with shallow grooves pecked into their centers to fit a palm. Beside them lies a basket woven from tough, fibrous root-strips, completely calcified by the weeping water until it resembles a fragile porcelain sieve. Near the edge of the basin, someone long ago used a hard stone to laboriously notch the rock lip, creating a small, precise V-shaped spillway to redirect a stream of warm water into a secondary soaking bowl. The notches are smoothed now, their sharp corners softened by centuries of mineral buildup, but the intent remains clear: a simple, manual intervention to shape the flow of the shelf.

The air does not cool when the sun reaches its zenith. The dark basalt wall absorbs the sunlight and radiates it back out in steady, amber waves, keeping the terrace suspended in its warm, humid twilight even as the winds howl thousands of feet below.

Nothing moves quickly here. A seed-pod takes three seasons to swell and drop. A blade of mineral-glazed fern expands by a finger's breadth over a year. The obsidian scrapers sit on their stone bench, untouched, while a layer of clear lime slowly forms over their sharp edges, binding them millimeter by millimeter to the mountain itself. The terrace sits in its high, bright isolation, wet and warm, entirely content to drip away its ages in silence.
