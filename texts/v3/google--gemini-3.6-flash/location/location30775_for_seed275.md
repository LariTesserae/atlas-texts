Deep beneath the planetary crust, where no rain has ever fallen and no subterranean rivers carve their channels, lies an expanse of cold, light-drenched timber and iron. 

The air is crisp, sitting at a steady, biting cool that smells of freshly planed cedar, dry quartz dust, and hot machine oil. There is no moisture here—not a drop of sweat on the skin, no fog in the air, no dampness in the soil. The soil itself is not earth, but a dense, compressed substrate of bone-dry cellulose, pulverized resin-granules, and un-eroded, razor-faceted basalt. 

In this subterranean vault, the vegetation does not depend on water. Life here operates on a non-aqueous chemistry of lipid-powders, dry volatile ethers, and static electrical charge absorbed from the surrounding rock. The flora is total; it forms the floor, the walls, the sky-ceiling, and the structural skeleton of the region. Massive, square-trunked iron-woods—trees with bark like corrugated slate and interiors of dry, horn-hard timber—rise hundreds of meters from the cavern floor to anchor into the hanging root-buttresses of the ceiling. Their fronds are not soft or leafy, but sheets of flexible, mica-like silicate that crackle faintly as they flex in the mechanical ventilation currents. 

Woven directly into this endless, dry forest is an industrial city of vast proportions and surprising emptiness. 

The architecture is layered and organized, built from dark cast iron, riveted bronze plate, and the very timber upon which it rests. Multi-tiered factory complexes cling to the flanks of five-hundred-foot-wide trunks like giant iron barnacles. Suspended cat-walks, heavy cable-car tracks, and copper-clad power lines span miles of open air, reaching across the cavernous gulfs from one forest-spire to the next. Yet despite the scale of the construction, the city feels open, almost sparse. Wide plazas made of polished horn-wood sit empty between the refinery towers; massive warehouse structures of perforated sheet-metal stand isolated in vast glades of dry, crystalline ferns. There is room to breathe. The cityscape does not crowd; it stretches out across a regional expanse, broken by grand voids of light and timber.

The illumination is bright and even—a cool, daylight-equivalent intensity provided by miles of high-mounted magnesium filaments and copper arc-conduits running along the ceiling vaults. There are no shadows of night here, only the steady, brilliant white light that gleams off the un-weathered, sharp-edged rock ledges and the brass housings of heavy machinery. 

Because there is no water, there is no true erosion. The rock faces and the hard timber formations do not feature the smooth, rounded curves carved by rain or rivers. Everything is embryonic, sharp, and pre-formed: the basalt looks as though it was cracked open yesterday; the silicated plant structures emerge from the ground in pristine, geometric prisms with razor-thin margins. The world feels raw, caught in a permanent state of just-coming-into-being, preserved perfectly by the absolute lack of moisture.

Despite the heavy industrial presence—the silent conveyor belts transporting crushed resin-bark, the great gear-driven saws mounted on tree-flanks, the electrical distribution stations hum-locked into the living wood—the landscape is remarkably quiet. The dense, dry plant matter acts as an acoustic sponge. The roar of a piston-driven compressor two miles away reaches the ear only as a soft, rhythmic thud, like a distant heartbeat pulsing through the floor. Footsteps on the cellulose substrate are completely muffled.

Fauna moves through this dry, illuminated underworld in surprising numbers, adapted entirely to its synthetic-organic rhythms. Large, six-legged arboreal grazers with carapaces of matte chitin move along the iron scaffolding, scraping dry paraffin-wax blooms from the steel beams with scissor-like mandibles. Swarms of small, furred gliders—bone-dry and soft as ash—launch themselves from high industrial platforms, catching the warm, dry updrafts rising from the electric generator banks. They make no sound as they drop through the white light, landing softly on the dusty, mica-fronds below.

Everything here is in motion, though the motion is deliberate and measured. Vines of dry, wire-like tendrils creep along high-tension cables at a visible crawl, harvesting the static charge to grow new, sharp-edged thorns. Piston-driven harvesters crawl along elevated rails, selectively trimming the structural slate-bark to feed the distillation plants below, where volatile dry-oils are extracted to fuel the regional grid. 

It is a world built from the collision of total forest and heavy industry, suspended in a dry, underground sunlight that never fades, where the stone never wears down and the machines run in quiet, perpetual harmony with a forest that needs no rain.
