The plateau sits miles above the tree line, a continental shoulder of dark slate and gray granite holding a sky of immense, unclouded brightness. The sun here is sharp and thin, casting hard-edged shadows across a high-altitude landscape that glitters with continuous moisture. Air moves cold and clean over the stone, smelling faintly of mineral silt and wet ash.

For scores of miles in every direction, the tableland is not merely rock, but an immense, dense city of stone, built by hand so long ago that its edges have softened into the natural geology. There are no iron beams, no glass, no wheels, and no mortar. The architecture is a marvel of dry-stacked masonry: multi-story blocks, narrow cell-like chambers, terraced plazas, and miles of honeycombed arcades, all carved or built from the same dark slate beneath them. The density is startling. Buildings press against buildings with only arm-width fissures for passages; multi-tiered streets step down stone terraces in long, formal flights, layered five or six levels deep into the mountain’s mantle.

Yet no trees grow here, and the vegetation is sparse—restricted to crusts of black and copper lichens that paint the north-facing walls, and thin ribbons of pale grey moss creeping along the joints of the pavement. Without timber, every roof is an arch of corbeled stone, every lintel a single heavy slab of granite.

Water is the master of this place. Though the sky above is bright, the high altitude draws up moisture from the warmer valleys below, generating a perpetual cycle of condensation. The upper walls of the city act as massive cold traps. Moisture gathers on the stone in thick, trembling sheets, weeping down every carved facade, pooling on thresholds, and spilling into a network of hand-cut conduits that runs along every street.

Because the city follows the slope of the plateau, the water is always moving. It flows in clear, silent sheets over smooth slate stairs, collects in deep square cisterns cut into the centers of forgotten plazas, and cascades from upper terraces to lower ones in thousands of small, steady falls. The surfaces never dry; the dark rock shines under the brilliant light like polished hematite.

Despite the vastness of the ruins and the constant motion of the water, the landscape is profoundly quiet. The sound of liquid is everywhere, but it is muffled—a soft, low murmuring of trickles, the rhythmic *plink* of droplets falling into deep wells, and the faint sigh of cold wind passing through thousands of empty, doorless entrances. Sound does not echo well here; the wet stone and the thin air seem to swallow it quickly.

The dynamics of the environment are ceaseless and physical. As the sun moves, water warms in the shallow basins, releasing wisps of pale vapor that rise a few feet into the air before the mountain wind shears them away. Higher up the slopes, where the shadows linger cold, thin rime-frost forms along the stone troughs in the morning, melting by midday into cold, clear currents that swell the lower channels. Small particles of silt are shifted stone by stone, washing down into vast, flat catchment basins that now resemble wet, dark mirrors reflecting the sky.

Fauna is rare and secretive. Occasionally, a solitary mountain bird with broad, ash-colored wings will drift high overhead, casting a fast shadow over the endless roofs before dipping down toward the valley edge. In the deeper cisterns, where the water stays cold and still, tiny, blind amphipods cling to the masonry, feeding on the microscopic detritus washed down from the upper terraces. Otherwise, the city belongs entirely to stone, light, and water.

Walking through the higher terraces, the scale reveals itself as almost endless. From a high stone rampart, one can look out over leagues of stepped roofs, square chimneys, open court-wells, and long aqueduct-spanning ridges, all glowing with wet daylight until they fade into the blue haze of the far horizon. The city has become its own watershed, a built mountain that collects the sky's dampness and passes it down, decade after decade, through millions of carved channels back to the earth below.
