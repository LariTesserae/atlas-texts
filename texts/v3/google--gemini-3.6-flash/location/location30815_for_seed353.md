It is a world baked dry in absolute pitch, resting high above any forgotten floor on an endless, suspended shelf of iron and petrified timber. 

The heat is not humid; it is the dry, oppressive radiation of a kiln that has been sealed for centuries. The air feels heavy with mineral dust, scorched cellulose, and the sharp, hot scent of oxidized grease. There is no water here—not a drop of dew, no mist, no dampness in the deep crevices. Life, if it can be called that, operates on a fierce, non-aqueous chemistry driven by hot hydrocarbon resins, mineral silicates, and the raw thermal energy pulsing through the floorplates.

Nothing here is unmade. The floor beneath your feet is a tightly woven lattice of heavy structural girders, copper-braided cables, and thick, iron-hard roots that have grown *into* the metal rather than over it. The vegetation is total, but it bears no green. The plants are black, dull bronze, and charcoal-gray—xeric titans with skins like scorched leather and hulls of rigid silica. They do not rely on water pressure to stand; their trunks are dense, tubular scaffolds of ironwood and calcified fiber, hollowed out to serve as chimneys for rising heat.

Because there is almost no light, the landscape reveals itself only in degrees—mostly as silhouettes against dull, red-glowing thermal conduits or the faint, bruised-violet luminescence of sap-veins burning with static charge. Looking outward, the scale is regional, extending in all directions across a vast, elevated valley of machinery and biomass. But there is no open sky and no clear horizon. The density is stifling. Above, below, and around, every cubic inch is occupied: iron-oak trunks fused with steam-chest housings, thickets of barbed copper-vines clogging dark catwalks, and massive, bulbous galls of dry amber bulging through rusted floor-grids.

It is ancient beyond measure. The exposed metal has not rusted from moisture, but has been worn smooth and dark by eons of dry abrasive winds and the continuous, grinding expansion of the wood. Millennia-old gearwork remains trapped inside the heartwood of giant resin-conifers, their teeth still occasionally catching and turning with a slow, agonizing groan as the trees shift under the heat.

Despite the near-total silence—punctuated only by the deep, muffled hum of subterranean boilers and the distant, dry *crack* of expanding timber—the world is violently dynamic. It is a slow-motion storm of construction and decay.

In the dark, a sector of the canopy undergoes a thermal spasm. A massive burl of petrified fiber, baked to a critical temperature by a buried heat-pipe, splits open with a sudden, dull detonation. It does not spill seeds, but releases a furious, fast-growing web of dry, black tendrils. These tendrils lash across an empty gallery, searching for tension. Within minutes, they latch onto an ancient flywheel housing, cool slightly, and contract with immense force, pulling the rusted assembly three feet to the left and locking it into a new, permanent posture. Elsewhere, an overgrown catwalk succumbs to the sheer weight of a century’s worth of dry, iron-bark buildup; it shears off and collapses downward into the dark, smashing through three lower strata of fibrous conduits before wedging tight in a dense, dark crotch of brass-wound trunks.

There are no animals. No insects crawl through the fiber-mats; no birds nest in the copper canopy. The only agency belongs to the synthesis itself—the endless, blind iteration of machines that feed heat to the plants, and plants that grow into structural beams to hold the machines in place.

If you stand still in the smothering heat, your boots resting on a warm, vibrating seam of iron and bark, you can feel the landscape breathing. It is a dry, creaking respiration of metal cooling, wood expanding, and hot resin dripping onto scorching steel deep below in the dark—a world settled into its final, ancient form, yet tearing itself apart and rebuilding every hour without a single eye to see it.
