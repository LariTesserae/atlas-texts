The basin is small—scarcely fifty yards across from wall to wall—sunken between curved, water-worn cliffs of dark slate that arch slightly inward at the top, trapping a perpetual hood of cool, grey condensation. The floor sits low, a natural sump where three shallow channels converge, though there is no sky above, only the heavy, dripping belly of the ceiling and the tangled underside of iron framing long ago wedged into the rock.

The air is cold and clammy, smelling of wet coal-dust, copper, and tallow. Water is everywhere, but it rarely falls as rain; it weeps from the slate in fat, steady sheets, beads upon every exposed curve of metal, and pools in a network of shallow, interlinked basins paved with smooth river-shingle and crushed slag.

No green things grow here. There are no ferns in the crevices, no moss on the wet iron. Instead, the non-mineral textures belong entirely to dark, rubbery biofilms and pale sulfur-crusts that coat the underside of every ledge. Where a plant might take root, there is only a thick, felted skin of iron-oxidizing bacteria—a deep, velvety orange where the water is fresh, fading to a liver-colored grease where the flow stagnates.

In this narrow space, matter is packed astonishingly tight. Nothing stands alone. A massive, cast-iron engine casing—its cylinders long since filled with calcified gravel—serves as the anchor for a dense lattice of copper conduits, lead pipes, and hand-riveted flues. These structural remains are not scattered like ruins on an empty plain; they are stacked and wedged together like driftwood in an eddy, smoothed by centuries of cold runoff until the line between worked metal and bedrock has blurred. A heavy steel crank-arm protrudes from a bench of shale, its eyelet worn oval by the passage of grit-laden water; from the crank-arm hangs a cluster of translucent, leather-walled sacks that pulse with a slow, mechanical rhythm of their own.

These are the basin’s primary inhabitants: blind, soft-bodied creatures the size of lantern-globes, anchored by tough cartilaginous stalks to the ironwork. They have no eyes or limbs, only pleated, siphon-like mouths that cup the lips of dripping pipes. As cold water fills their inner chambers, they swell, translucent and veined with grey humors, until their weight trips a tiny cartilaginous valve, expelling the liquid in a sharp, wet spurting sound and drawing in another gulp. Hundreds of them cling to the pipe-tangles, breathing the water that flows through human-built channels long after the builders vanished.

Below them, in the shallow pools, small glass-shelled isopods move in dense, clinking herds. Their carapaces are clear as quartz, revealing the dark orange mineral paste churning in their guts. They graze upon the iron-velvet, their tiny brass-colored mandibles making a sound like dry sand falling on paper. Larger things—pale, legless lung-slugs four feet long—slither through the drowned gear-trains at the basin's center, their rubbery flanks scraping against rusted teeth as they search for the gelatinous egg-masses anchored inside old valve-housings.

The light is low, fixed in a twilight that never shifts. It comes from three sources: a cold, sour phosphorescence that blooms in the scum on the static pools; tiny, dull-amber sparks that occasionally strike within the damp junction-boxes of lingering galvanic circuits; and the dull sheen of the moisture itself, which catches whatever faint luminance floats in the mist and spreads it across every wet surface.

Despite the quiet, the place is rarely still. It operates on a slow, hydraulic clockwork. Water collects in an upper iron cistern—a remnant of some forgotten filtration scheme—until the weight overcomes a rusty counterweight. With a heavy, metallic *thnk-clack* that echoes softly off the slate walls, the cistern tips, dumping a barrel’s worth of cold water into a sloped trough. The surge turns a half-submerged wooden paddlewheel three heavy revolutions.

As the wheel turns, it drives a train of wooden pegs that bump against a line of hanging brass bells, producing a muted, chorded chiming—*clink, tonk, chime*—that vibrates through the water and the stone. The lung-slugs pause, their siphon-tips lifting into the damp air; the glass-shelled isopods freeze, their carapaces gleaming in the dim phosphorescence. Then the water settles back into its channels, the wheel comes to rest with a soft squelch against the mud, and the steady, dripping silence re-establishes itself.

The rules here are simple and old. Metal provides the bone; water brings the salt and the cold; the soft, pale flesh fills the gaps between rivets. Nothing rots, because nothing is warm enough to decay quickly; instead, dead things are slowly coated in iron-rust and lime, turned to heavy, dark monuments before they can fall apart. A dead slug lying in a pipe-trough does not soften; it turns to orange stone, its form preserved down to the wrinkles on its breathing orifice, becoming another ridge over which the cold water must climb on its way to the sump.
