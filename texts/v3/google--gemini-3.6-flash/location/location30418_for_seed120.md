It is no larger than the inside of a wide, hollowed bell, hanging at an altitude where the air should be ice, yet it boils.

Here, miles above any visible plain, tucked into a notch of a needle-thin spire that has no bottom, exists a single, saturated cell. There is no stone here—not a pebble, not a fracture of natural basalt. Every square inch of the enclosure has been covered by hand. The walls are thick, wet terracotta, smeared on in overlapping palm-strokes that are still damp, still cooling from whatever low-fired furnace produced them. Bone pegs the size of a forearm are driven through the seams at three-inch intervals, binding the earthenware tiles to a hidden skeleton of boiled ash-wood and twisted gut. Nothing here has been eroded by wind or time; the fingerprints left in the orange mud are sharp enough to cast shadows, and the edges of the carved bone pegs are still hairy with shaved marrow. It is a landscape finished moments ago, yet already overflowing.

The heat inside the pocket is thick and sour, like the breath of a large herbivore. Steam rises not from a single vent, but from the floor itself—a floor made of hand-packed clay, grooved with thousands of tiny, parallel drainage channels that lead nowhere. Because the spatial logic of the cell is folded, water does not settle. It runs along the sloping mud floor, reaches the curved wall, and continues up the curve without breaking contact, weeping continuously toward the highest point of the ceiling before dripping back down through the heavy air as a hot, greasy drizzle.

Light is a low, rust-colored twilight. It enters through small, round apertures set into the thick walls—not windows, but plugs of clear fat and tallow stretched thin over hoops of dried rattan. The outside world is invisible through them, reduced to a blinding, gray-white glare that indicates the dizzying height, but inside, the light turns the color of a wet copper kettle.

In this dim, boiling cradle, plant life grows with terrifying speed. Thick, fleshy vines with the pale, translucent green of boiled celery do not grow out of soil; they root directly into the damp thumbprints of the wall plaster. Their tendrils are knotting themselves around the bone pegs, thickening as you watch, their leaves uncurling with tiny, wet popping sounds that contribute to the constant noise of the place.

And the noise is immense. It is a wet, enclosed roar—the sound of condensation rushing through tiny clay flues, the rhythmic *thump-thump* of expanding ash-wood beams adjusting to the moisture, and a deep, harmonic whistling caused by the wind outside shearing past the fat-plugged apertures. The air hums so hard that the droplets hanging from the tallow panes vibrate into tiny, star-shaped geometric patterns before they fall.

Space inside the pocket is unreliable. Though the outer walls suggest a chamber no larger than a small hut, walking along the edge takes far too long. A person stepping around the central pillar—a thick, spiraling column of hand-molded cob, wrapped in wet hemp cord—finds themselves descending a long, shallow ramp that shouldn't fit within the spire’s profile. The ramp is lined with hundreds of small, unbaked clay pots, each packed with glowing, blue-stemmed liverwort that thrives in the dark, heavy moisture. The temperature rises with every step downward, though "downward" remains a relative term; looking over the edge of the ramp reveals the very ceiling you just left, dripping its hot rain onto your own hair from five feet above.

Animal life is nearly absent, save for small, blind, white-shelled snails that move over the fresh terracotta with surprising speed. They do not eat the vines; instead, they scrape the soft, wet surface of the unbaked clay with their radulae, leaving long, bright ribbons of smoothed earthenware behind them as they graze on the mineral heat.

Everything is under construction, everything is enclosed, and everything is moving. The wood swells; the clay sets but never dries; the vines bind the walls tightly enough to keep the pressure from blowing the pocket apart into the empty sky. It is a self-contained, boiling seed of human-made earth, perched on the tip of nothingness, humming like a struck anvil.
