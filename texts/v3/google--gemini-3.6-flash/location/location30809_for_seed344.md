The space is small—a narrow, sunlit trench no wider than a courtyard, pinched between two walls of raw, jet-black stone. The stone is young, extruded so recently in earth-time that its fractured faces are still razor-sharp, like shattered bottle glass. There are no smoothed corners here, no gravel beds or soil drifts. The ledges look newly snapped, their edges capable of tearing skin at a touch.

It is suffocatingly hot. The heat does not come only from the bright daylight cutting straight down into the cleft, but from the floor itself. Thermal water, clear and heavy with mineral salts, bleeds from microscopic fissures in the basalt. It doesn't bubble violently; instead, it shimmers in shallow, mirror-flat pools that sit at the threshold of boiling. The air is so thick with moisture that it feels like breathing wet wool. Condensation gathers on every overhead lip of stone and falls in fat, scalding drops that strike the rocks with hard, metallic ticks.

Dominating this pocket of stone is a single piece of heavy machinery, wedged into the floor as if the rock cooled around its iron boots. 

It is a low-frequency displacement engine, cast from thick, dark iron that has oxidized into a crust of wet, orange-brown rust. A central piston, eight feet across, rises and falls with a cadence so slow it demands patience to track: a long, agonizingly slow inhalation that takes nearly a minute, followed by a heavy, shuddering drop. 

When the piston drops, the sound is staggering. 

It is a deafening, chest-cavitating thrum—a combination of iron striking brass and thousands of gallons of superheated water being forcibly shoved through subterranean channels. The noise fills the narrow trench completely, reverberating off the glass-hard walls so intensely that the air itself seems to thicken and shake. Between these monumental thuds, the silence is broken only by the continuous, high-pitched roar of steam escaping from a rusted safety relief valve near the engine’s crown. The whistle is loud enough to drown out speech, a sharp, white-noise hiss that tears through the heavy humidity.

Despite the intensity of the noise and the heat, the place feels remarkably empty.

There are no animals. No flies circle the stagnant pools; no lichens have had time to establish themselves on the razor-edged stone. The water is too hot and too alkaline for anything to swim in it. Along the wet base of the iron engine, where the steam vent washes over a rusted gantry, a few isolated clumps of rubbery, stringy pale vegetation cling to the metal—tough, fiber-rich growths that look more like scalded kelp than land plants. They grow in thin, solitary threads, separated by yards of bare, gleaming wet iron.

A line of riveted iron pipe, four feet in diameter, emerges from the western stone face, spans the central pool, and disappears straight into the floor. It doesn't look maintained; it looks abandoned to its own automatic routine. Where the iron joints have failed, thin, needle-like jets of boiling water spray across the open space, carving white, mineral-encrusted arcs through the air before splashing into the dark basins below.

The light is plain, even daylight—uncolored by canopy or shadow. It hits the steam plumes as they rise out of the trench, turning them into brilliant, white columns before they dissipate into the dry air fifty feet above. 

Everything here exists in a state of violent, slow-motion balance. The rock does not erode; the mineral water is too young, the stone too hard, and the process too brief for smooth channels to have formed. The machine does not race; it plods through its massive, thermal cycle, displacement by displacement, pounding its rhythm into the earth. The air remains trapped, hot, loud, and wet, a hidden engine room buried in a crack of raw stone, operating for no one.
