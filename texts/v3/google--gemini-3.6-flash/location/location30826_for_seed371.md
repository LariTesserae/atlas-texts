It is a vertical notch carved into a young, shattered mountain face, five thousand meters above any valley floor. The stone here has no rounded corners; it was broken off yesterday in geological time, leaving razor-sharp arrêtes of black basalt and slick, dark shale that cut through cold mist. The sky above is pinched into a thread of navy black, swallowed almost entirely by the converging walls of the fissure, leaving the notch in an oppressive, thick gloom.

In this narrow gap—no wider than a chapel, bounded on three sides by sheer, dripping rock—there are no paths, no stairs, and no structural enclosures. Yet it is packed to capacity with massive, unhoused industrial machinery.

Huge cast-iron flywheels, twelve feet across and coated in a sheen of cold moisture, spin within inches of the unweathered stone. Reciprocating steel shafts as thick as tree trunks punch horizontally through the darkness, their strokes hammering against the cliffside with a rhythmic, bone-rattling thud that vibrates through the bedrock. Massive drive chains, heavy as anchor cables, snap and whip through the narrow clearances, throwing off fine sprays of black grease and freezing water. There are no walls, no roofs, no catwalks; the machinery is bolted directly into the live basalt with jagged steel pins, left entirely exposed to the icy air.

Despite the brutal chill and the dark, the notch is violently overgrown. 

Where the heavy iron plates meet the stone, dark, fleshy mosses have taken hold, forming carpets three inches thick that shiver with the vibration of the engines. Cold-hardy ferns with long, serrated fronds crowd every crevice, jamming themselves between static machine housings and the sharp rock ledges. They grow in astonishing, suffocating density—layered frond over frond, glistening with grease-filmed condensation. Thick, ropy roots bind the crushed gravel and iron filings into a dark, rich mud that smells of ozone, cold wet earth, and heavy mineral oil. 

The air is cold enough to turn every breath into a thick cloud, but the moisture is constant. It does not rain in torrents; instead, a persistent, cold sweat coats everything. Condensation gathers on the undersides of the iron beams, dropping in fat, heavy beads onto the lush canopy of ferns below. Steam vents from relief valves in short, screaming bursts, instantly condensing into fog that swirls wildly in the draft created by the plunging pistons.

There are no animals here. No birds roost in the iron lattice; no insects crawl through the sodden moss. The atmosphere is too violent, the air too thick with cold oil mist, the pounding of the machinery too deafening. The only life is the frantic, silent vegetation, anchored stubbornly in the dark, feeding on the damp mist and the trace heat radiating from the overworked bearings.

The noise is a continuous, churning storm. The low, deep groan of strain in the metal mixes with the high-pitched hiss of pressurized condensation and the wet slap of heavy leaves whipped by the air currents of passing counterweights. Every few seconds, a massive linkage shifts with an iron screech that echoes up the vertical shaft, followed immediately by the dull, explosive thud of a hydraulic ram striking its stop.

Everything in this small, enclosed shelf is in violent, chaotic motion. The plants are perpetually lashed by wind and spray; the iron shakes so hard that droplets of grease fly off in glowing arcs through the dark; the sharp rock face itself seems to tremble under the strain of the machinery buried into its heart. It is a place of raw iron, fresh stone, and cold, wet green—unpeopled, unbuilt, and roaring endlessly in the dark high above the world.
