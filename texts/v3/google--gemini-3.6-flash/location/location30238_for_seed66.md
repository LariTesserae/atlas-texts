The shelf sits miles above the lowland plains, a vast plateau of worn basalt and heavy masonry that catches the upper winds and traps the warm, wet air rising from below. Bright daylight hits the plateau squarely, unfiltered except by the thin ribbons of warm condensation that drift off the ironwork. It is a place without a single leaf, stem, or blade of grass—a world built entirely of stone, metal, brick, and shell.

From a distance, the shelf looks like an ancient, half-melted machine city, continental in breadth, rising in stepped tiers toward the interior peaks. The scale is large enough that localized rain-fronts form over its central industrial basins, dropping brief, warm showers that roll off million-roofed slopes and collect in thousands of miles of interconnecting aqueducts. Everything is wet. The air smells of wet slate, hot grease, iron oxide, and the dense, sweet musk of millions of living bodies.

The built environment is dense and deeply aged. Brick tenements, boilerhouses, coal-chutes, and elevated rail-girders are stacked four and five layers deep, but there are no sharp edges left. Centuries of warm rain and mineral runoff have rounded the granite cornices until they resemble river boulders. Massive cast-iron girders, thicker than tree trunks, are pitted and dished; where steam vents have exhaled against stone walls for generations, the brickwork is hollowed into smooth, glossy scoops. The architecture is distinctly industrial—heavy rivets, counterweighted crane-arms, brass valves the size of carriage wheels, and towering brick chimneys—but it has settled into its landscape as if it were a natural geological formation.

Because there are no plants, the color palette is strictly mineral and animal: dark slate-grey, rust-red, tarnished verdigris, and the dull ivory of millions of tiny shells. Every surface not continually washed by hot industrial discharge is coated in animal life. 

The density of fauna is suffocating. Heavy-shelled, multi-legged crawlers—some as small as fingernails, others as long as timber beams—encrust the wet vertical walls like barnacles on a wharf pier. They scrape at the iron oxide and mineral deposits, their chitinous legs creating a continuous, dry *clack-clack-clack* that echoes down the narrow brick alleys. In the warm runoff channels that cut through the paved streets, pale, lungless creatures with sleek, oil-slick skin wallow in dense masses, twisting around one another like thick ropes as they filter micro-fauna from the current. Airborne life is equally dense: vast, shimmering clouds of membranous, hard-bodied fliers drift through the bright air between the boiler-towers, moving in slow, rolling murmurs that cast flickering shadows across the wet roofs.

The technology here does not belong to a frantic, bustling era; it belongs to a long-settled, patient mechanics. Massive walking-beam engines, six stories tall and anchored into the solid rock of the plateau, cycle with agonizing slowness. A single stroke of a piston might take twenty minutes, accompanied by a deep, resonant groan of iron that shakes the water in the street-gutters. Giant brass valves turn once every few hours under the weight of suspended stone counterweights, releasing steady, hissing plumes of warm vapor that coat the nearby masonry in fresh wetness.

Water moves constantly, but without violence. It weeps from the joints between brick layers, cascades in smooth sheets over carved stone spillways, and trickles down the iron ladders that hang between city tiers. Because the temperature stays comfortably warm, the moisture does not freeze or shatter the stone; it simply dissolves it, millimeter by millimeter, over hundreds of years, smoothing every staircase into a sequence of shallow, water-filled bowls.

Sound fills the landscape completely. The sheer volume of fauna provides a high, vibrating treble—the rasping of mandibles, the wet slapping of amphibious bodies in the cisterns, the drone of countless wings. Beneath this lies the heavy bass of the environment: the endless roar of falling water, the rhythmic *thump-hiss* of the slow-cycling engines, and the deep, hollow acoustics of a vast, elevated city built entirely of hollow iron and stone.

Movement across the landscape happens at a gradual, patient pace. The animal clouds drift on the warm updrafts, taking hours to cross a single tier. The runoff trickles down through the five-layered streets to the lower cliffs, where it spills back into the haze of the lowlands. Nothing is hurried. The city does not seem to be expanding or decaying; it exists in a long, wet equilibrium, deeply worn, densely inhabited, and humming with heavy, warm life.
