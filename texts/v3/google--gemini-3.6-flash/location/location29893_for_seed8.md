The land has nowhere left to fall. 

A billion years of weather have planed the stone down to its cellar, leaving a vast, gently warped pavement of dark red ironstone and pale gray granite that stretches out toward a flat, pale horizon. There are no hills here, only wide, low swells in the rock—curves so gradual that a human eye reads them as absolute flat until the water reveals their shape. 

And water is everywhere, though it rarely flows. It sits in broad, skin-deep sheets across the stone, warming under a heavy, bright sun. It fills shallow basins the size of courtyards, trickles through hair-thin fissures, and drips continuously from the lips of low, rounded ledges that are all that remain of ancient cliff faces. The air is warm and dense, holding a permanent, shimmering haze just above the stone, smelling intensely of wet iron, warm algae, and the faint, sweet decay of damp silt.

Despite the open horizon, the landscape feels full, not of structures or tall growths, but of light, sound, and water. The sun sits high in a pale sky, reflecting off thousands of interconnected pools until the ground shines brighter than the air above it. 

Nothing grows high. Soil has no purchase on this polished basement rock; any fine dust that settles is carried away by the gentle, lukewarm sheets of rain that sweep the expanse every few afternoons, leaving only the bare stone and the pockets of grit wedged into its joints. Vegetation is sparse, appearing in widely separated patches—stiff, jointed rushes the color of tarnished copper rising six inches out of the shallows; thick, rubbery mats of black lichen that grip the dry crests of the rock swells; and occasional clumps of a low, wire-stemmed shrub with tiny, oily leaves that release a sharp, turpentine aroma when heated by the midday glare. Between these scattered outposts of green and black lie tens of yards of open, flooded stone, glistening like wet slate.

Yet the emptiness is noisy. Without trees or high soil to absorb sound, the flat pane of the regional shield acts as a soundboard. The air vibrates with a continuous, ringing chorus of small life. Millions of fat-bodied, dark-green frogs sit at the edges of the warm sheets, their trills and clacks meshing into a steady, deafening roar that rises and falls with the passage of thin clouds across the sun. Glass-winged dragonflies, four inches across, hover in thousands over the warm shallows, their dry wings clicking like rattling paper as they defend territory over the submerged algae mats. Small, pale wading birds with legs like reeds step deliberately through the tea-colored water, their harsh, whistling cries carrying for miles across the flat expanse.

Below the surface of the water, where the sun penetrates directly to the reddish silt, life is dense and indifferent to the sky. Tiny, shrimp-like crustaceans kick through the warm brine, leaving fine wakes in the fine sediment. Snails with thick, chalky shells graze the slimy under-edges of the stone lips, their rasping mouthparts making a dry, clicking rustle beneath the surface that blends with the dripping of the ledges.

There is no sense of urgency here, nor any mark of a tool or a footstep. The landscape exists in a state of late-stage equilibrium, having shed its peaks, its canyons, and its deep soils eons ago. It is a surface that has reached its final form: too low to erode further, too hard to easily break, kept perpetually wet by the warm, thick atmosphere that hangs over the low latitudes. 

As the afternoon wears on, the light shifts from a stark, yellow glare to a rich, bronze sheen. The temperature hardly drops; the massive slab of underground stone has absorbed the heat all day and now radiates it back into the damp air, keeping the pools as warm as bathwater. The sound does not diminish either; as the light softens, the insects grow louder, their buzz joining the relentless *tink-tink-plip* of water falling from shelf to basin across miles of open, glistening iron. 

Change here is measured not in seasons or storms, but in the imperceptible rounding of a granite rim by a trillion wet drop-impacts, or the slow, centuries-long creep of a lichen colony across an inch of damp rock. The world is wide, warm, flat, and entirely self-contained, humming loudly in the sun.
