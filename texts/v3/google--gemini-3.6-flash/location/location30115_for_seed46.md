High above any horizon that holds clouds, tucked inside a fracture along the upper flank of a solitary, razor-ribbed peak, sits a chamber no larger than a courtyards-worth of floor. It is open to the sky only through a narrow vertical vent, through which a harsh, unsoftened light falls straight down, casting hard-edged shadows that do not bleed or soften.

There is no soil here, and no dust. The rock underfoot is fresh basalt and virgin obsidian, so newly born from heat that its edges remain knife-blade sharp. Nothing has ever eroded it. No raindrop has ever pitted these vitreous planes; no wind has ever carried silt across them to polish their corners. The stone looks wet where light hits it, but it is bone-dry—a mirror-black surface scored with cooling cracks so precise they look like etched circuits.

The heat is immense and still. It bakes out of the rock itself, a radiant press that hovers near the boiling point of lead, yet the air is completely devoid of moisture. There is no water in this place—not in liquid form, not as vapor, not bound into clay. 

In place of water, a different chemistry feeds the life that fills the fractures.

And it is lush. Though sparse in its spatial footprint—long expanses of vacant, mirror-black stone separate each cluster—the growth here is thick, complex, and heavy. It does not look like green vegetation, nor does it wilt in the furnace heat. It is a dry, vitreous flora of carbon-nanotube tendrils, mica-leaved fronds, and heavy, bulbous succulents made of solid resin and sulfur-glass. 

From the deep fissures in the un-weathered obsidian sprout thick, jointed stalks that resemble articulated brass conduits. They branch into broad, fan-shaped leaves made of ultra-thin flexible quartz that chime softly when the temperature shifts. Below them, matting the shade of the rock faces, are dense cushions of metallic moss—fibrous networks of copper-hued wire that draw electrical potentials directly from the thermal gradients in the mountain's core. Where a leaf breaks, no moisture leaks out; instead, a slow, honey-thick resin of liquid polymers and hot bitumen beads at the fracture, sealing it almost instantly in a glossy scab.

There are no buildings here. No cut stones, no iron beams, no footprints or forgotten tools. Yet the landscape operates under an unmistakably industrial logic. 

The physics of this pocket are mechanized without a machine in sight. The rock itself grows along precision-machined axes. In the empty spaces between the quartz-frond thickets, solid basalt pillars are seated within naturally occurring socket-joints. Every few hours, driven by the slow, subterranean expansion of deep mineral plates, a pillar will rise three inches with the smooth, hydraulic hiss of a piston, lock into a brassy seam of native pyrite, and hold there for days before settling back down. 

Thick, cable-like root structures—some as wide as a man’s torso and wrapped in braided silicates that look identically like armored electrical conduit—run across the bare floor from one plant cluster to another. They do not seek moisture; they carry current. A faint, low-voltage arc occasionally leaps between two adjacent quartz fronds with a dry *snap*, leaving behind the sharp, sweet smell of ozone and hot tin.

Reality breaks down gently around the edges of this shelf. 

Because the space is pocket-scaled and isolated at high altitude, light does not behave entirely linearly. Looking across thirty feet of flat obsidian, the perspective buckles slightly, as if viewed through the heavy glass of an industrial pressure portal. A cluster of sulfur-succulents on the far side appears both ten yards away and immediately against the iris. Shadow casts itself at angles that do not match the sun’s height through the sky-slit; instead, shadows align themselves to the invisible magnetic field running between the mineral seams, pointing steadfastly north-northeast regardless of the hour.

There are no animals. Nothing crawls across the razor-edges of the glass stone; no insects buzz among the quartz blossoms, which do not open or close for pollinators but pulse with a steady, lukewarm phosphorescence when the thermal gradient peaks.

The soundscape is almost entirely silent, saved for the slow, heavy breath of the mountain’s engine. A deep, sub-audible 50-hertz hum resonates up through the soles of feet from deep below the obsidian floor. At long intervals, a quartz leaf snaps as its internal stress changes, sounding like a wine glass breaking on marble. Then the quiet returns—hot, dry, brilliant, and absolute.

Everything here happens on a patient, geological schedule. The quartz fronds grow by vapor deposition, taking decades to extend a single joint. The resinous succulents swell over centuries, slowly pulling trace metals from the un-eroded rock and refining them into pure, glittering seams within their translucent skins. It is a world under construction, raw and un-softened by age, functioning with the silent, perfect precision of an unpeopled factory high above the clouds.
