The ground is not rock, though it rings like basalt beneath a heel. It is iron-bark—cold, fibrous, and freshly split. 

Here, at an altitude where the atmosphere thins to a sharp, metallic bite, the world is constructed entirely of plant matter that has forgotten how to be soft. Great structural ribs of dark, resinous vegetation—some as wide as mountain ridges, others tapering into jagged, needlescale spires—thrust upward out of the lower gloom. They do not form a dense forest carpet; instead, they span the sky in a vast, hollow lattice. 

The emptiness is the first thing that strikes you. Between these titanic, arching boughs lie miles of pure, freezing void. You can stand on the knife-edge crest of a horizontal trunk, look down through two thousand meters of black air, and see only another distant, glistening arch spanning the dark, far below. The world is almost entirely volume, almost entirely empty space, held together by a scaffold of living, freezing timber.

It is bitterly cold. The air holds an intermittent, stinging wetness—not rain, but heavy clouds of sub-zero mist that sweep through the chasms, coating the windward side of every trunk in rime and clear, spiky sheets of glaze-ice. Where the sap bleeds from fresh structural fractures, it freezes instantly into dark, amber-colored glass, sharp enough to cut through leather boots.

The features here are young and unsoftened. The great wooden ridges have not had time to weather; their edges are raw, crystalline, and splintered. Massive tendrils, thick as towers, hang snapped and dangling in the abyss, their sheared ends exposing tight, wet annular rings the color of bruised copper. The land is still growing, or still fracturing under its own immense, high-altitude weight.

There is almost no light. The sky above is a void of bruised indigo, occasionally punctured by the cold, unblinking prick of a distant star. The landscape generates no warmth, but deep within the furrows of the thickest trunks, a dull, bruised-purple bioluminescence oozes from cold, rubbery lichens—just enough to outline the terrifying drops and the massive, curving silhouette of the archways.

And yet, it is extraordinarily loud.

The sound never ceases. The vast, empty space acts as an acoustic chamber for the movements of the titan-plants. As the temperature drops, the wood contracts with deafening, cannon-shot cracks that reverberate for miles through the void. The high-altitude gales blow through the hollow, frost-cracked hearts of dead limbs, creating a low, thrumming roar like a thousand organ pipes playing a single, dissonant chord. 

Through this dark, roaring emptiness, life moves in abundance.

The fauna here has adapted to the vertical, iron-hard terrain. Thick-bodied, six-legged crawlers with claws tipped in mineralized bone move along the undersides of the arches, picking at the frozen sap. Packs of long-bodied, white-furred gliders leap from limb to limb across the multi-mile gulfs, their leathery membranes whistling through the black air before they strike the distant bark with a heavy, wet thud. Down in the deep furrows, blind, armored burrowers scrape at the fibers, their chittering calls echoing across the gulfs.

Human presence is tiny, clinging to the edges of this vegetable leviathan. 

High up on the lee side of a great, curving limb—sheltered from the worst of the rime-bearing mists—are the scattered marks of habitation. These are not stone houses or metal towers. They are crude alcoves chiselled directly into the dead outer bark using flint wedges and heavy bone mallets. 

Small shelters are lashed together with braided sinew and tough, fibrous bark-strips. Fires are rare and precious, fed on dry resin-chips and kept hidden in deep, hand-carved niches so the wind does not snatch them away. To cross from one ridge-trunk to another, the inhabitants deploy long, swaying bridges woven from tough, root-vines, pegged into the ironwood with fire-hardened wooden spikes. There is no metallurgy here; tools are made from the dense, mineralized bones of the giant gliders or the glass-sharp flakes of frozen sap.

The world is constantly in motion. It is an ecosystem driven by frost-heave and structural stress. Every few hours, a moisture front rolls through, burying a sector of the lattice in heavy frost. The added weight causes thousands of tons of timber to shift, bending with low, terrifying groans before settling into new configurations. Seeds the size of boulders swell in their frozen pods, bursting open with explosive cracks to cast long, trailing roots into the abyss, searching for another limb to grip.

You stand on a ledge of dark, frozen fiber, clutching a hand-carved rail of bound vine. Beneath your feet, the tree-mountain shudders—a long, deep vibration that runs up your spine as, somewhere ten miles out in the black, a new arch settles into place against the cold stars. The wind screams through a hollow branch above, spray-dropping ice across your face, and in the deep shadow below, something vast and furred launches itself into the dark, silent air.
