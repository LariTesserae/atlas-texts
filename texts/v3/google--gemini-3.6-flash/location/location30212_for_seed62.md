At nine thousand feet, the air is thin, chilled, and violently clear. The sky above the sharp-edged terraces of the Oakhaven Rift is a hard, unclouded azure that pours down an almost painful volume of light. Everything glares: the wet, quartz-veined slate; the oily chrome of dormant turbines; the million tiny lenses of cold condensation beaded along the underside of fleshy, dark-green leaves.

This is a young landscape, uplifted so recently in geological terms that the frost and wind have not yet rounded a single corner. The rock underfoot is split into clean, razor-sharp prisms—slates, dark basalts, and pale greenschists—cracked open like fresh obsidian. Yet despite the razor edges of the geology and the bite of the mountain air, every fissure, ledge, and iron plate is choked with an astonishing, silent density of life.

It is a jungle of cold altitude. Thick, waxy fronds as wide as skiffs crowd against one another, their edges notched like sawblades. Below them, mosses grow in dense, rubbery cushions three feet deep, swallowing the jagged stone underneath. Liverworts and pale, plate-like lichens seal the rock faces in overlapping scales of jade, pewter, and verdigris. Moisture does not fall here as heavy rain, but arrives in the early morning as a dense, icy mist that clings to every surface, leaving the plants dripping continuously with clear, cold beads that never seem to evaporate in the bright sun.

Embedded throughout this choked green basin are the relics of a massive, scattered industrial grid. They are not ancient ruins—the iron is not rotted through, and the paint on the steel bulkheads is still thick and leaden—but they are deeply wrapped in the flora. Half-buried in a cleft of raw basalt sits a three-story brass condenser house, its cooling fins choked with climbing ferns whose veins shine with a faint, metallic sheen. Massive, braided copper cables, as thick as a man’s thigh, stretch across the basin from spire to sharp spire, sagging under the weight of hanging orchids and heavy mats of wet moss.

In places, heavy iron walking-beams and drive-pistons emerge from the rock, motionless or moving at a rate so agonizingly slow it escapes the eye. A flywheel forty feet across, cast from dark grey iron, stands half-submerged in a bed of giant horse-tails; if you press your palm against its cool rim, you can feel a vibration once every few minutes—a deep, subterranean heave as some unseen pneumatic system miles beneath the mountain mountain shifts its weight. 

The silence here is total and heavy. The air is too thin and the foliage too dense for sound to carry. The four-foot-deep carpets of damp moss absorb every footstep; even the drip of water from the iron manifolds into the rock-pools makes no audible splash, silenced by the thick scum of duckweed and oil-films that coat the water’s surface. There are no birds in the upper canopy. Occasionally, a pale, blind beetle with legs like spun glass will crawl from a grease-nipple on a dormant engine, take thirty minutes to navigate a single copper rivet, and vanish into the wet shade of a fern root.

The weirdness of the place lies in its quiet metabolic fusion. The machinery does not seem to have invaded the forest, nor has the forest simply overgrown an abandoned works. They exist in a shared, slow economy. The leaves of the broad-ferns leak a thick, amber-colored resin that smells faintly of kerosene and pine grease, running down the rock faces in clear channels to lubricate the sliding joints of buried steel rods. In return, the cold copper pipes that thread through the soil sweat a constant trickle of mineral-rich condensation, feeding the root-systems of plants that could not otherwise survive on the barren, unweathered slate.

Looking out across the basin, the landscape steps down in jagged, pristine shelves toward a distant, cloud-filled gap in the peaks. Each shelf is a repeating pattern of brilliant light, sharp black shadows, emerald density, and dull iron grey. Nothing here is in a hurry. The rust spreads at the rate of a glacier; the ferns expand by an inch a decade; the sky remains a fixed, blinding dome. It is a world of immense, quiet pressure, held in perfect, frozen equilibrium on a high shelf of the earth.
