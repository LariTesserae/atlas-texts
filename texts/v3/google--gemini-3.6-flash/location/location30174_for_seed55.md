The water here does not fall or flow; it simply *is*, an endless, freezing abyss of absolute immersion. It holds a cold so deep that it feels heavy, pressing against every surface with the weight of miles of liquid stacked above. There is no surface sky, no horizon of air, only a profound, midnight indigo that shifts into pure black within fifty yards in any direction.

There is no natural earth here. No mud, no silt beds, no volcanic basalt or oceanic sediment. The floor of this world is entirely made.

For hundreds of leagues, the substrate is a single, continuous expanse of cyclopean masonry—terraced plazas, low-sloped stairways forty strides wide, broad avenues paved with fitted slate, and endless tiers of sunken foundations. Every square foot of this drowned continent was shaped by hands. Yet it is extraordinarily old. The water, patient and vast, has been grinding at these structures for thousands of years. The sharp corners of the ashlar blocks have been smoothed into soft, soap-like curves. Ramps have lost their grain; step treads have been worn down until they resemble gentle waves of stone; massive stone pilasters stand rounded like ancient, blunted teeth.

Despite the near-total darkness, vegetation thrives in dense, suffocating abundance, adapted to the glacial chill and the heavy pressure. Thick, rubbery forests of cold-water kelp, black and deep olive-green, root themselves in the eroded joints between the paving stones. Their stalks are as thick as timber beams, rising straight up into the blackness for hundreds of feet, swaying with the microscopic drift of the deep abyssal currents.

Below the canopy of the kelp, the masonry is coated in a velvet carpet of pale, fibrous aquatic mosses and spreading tendrils of aquatic ivy. It fills every crack, creeping over carved stone lintels and wrapping around the bases of massive, hand-cut obelisks. Where the stone has cracked under the slow, epochal shifting of the seabed, dense clusters of broad, translucent underwater ferns erupt from the gaps, their fronds fanning out in static, fan-like arrays that never see the sun.

The level of technology preserved in these sunken ruins is starkly primitive. There are no engines, no glass, no complex metals rusting in the salt. Instead, the signs of manufacture are raw and manual. Heavy copper rings, beaten into shape with hand-tools, remain driven into the stone piers, secured by wedges of petrified hardwood. In small, sheltered alcoves along the drowned avenues, one finds stone hammers bound to bone handles with twined kelp-cordage, left sitting on slate workbenches as if set down yesterday. Simple slate sluice-gates, designed to be lifted by hand-turned wooden levers, sit jammed open with river-pebbles. Huge anchors carved from single blocks of granodiorite lie resting in the plazas, their hempen ropes long turned to mineralized sludge.

It is a world of immense, quiet mass. The scale is staggering—one can swim or drift for hours over a single terraced amphitheater, its outer tiers dissolving into the black water ahead while its base disappears into the dark below. The architecture stretches endlessly in all directions, a continent-sized lattice of simple stone construction now completely submerged and reclaimed by the cold flora.

There are no animals here. No fish flash through the dark foliage; no crabs crawl across the softened stone steps; no worms burrow into the mortarless joints. The silence is nearly absolute. Water is a fast conductor of sound, yet there is almost nothing to hear—only the faint, microscopic *creak* of a kelp stalk flexing under a slow-moving current, or the low, sub-audible thrum of the deep water itself, moving as a single, continental mass across the stone plain.

Nothing changes quickly here. The temperature is locked near freezing, varying by fractions of a degree over centuries. The growth of the black ivy is so slow that a single leaf takes decades to uncurl against the damp slate. The erosion continues, atom by atom, rounding the ancient buildings, smoothing the hand-carved stone tools, settling everything deeper into its final, drowned, and everlasting form.
