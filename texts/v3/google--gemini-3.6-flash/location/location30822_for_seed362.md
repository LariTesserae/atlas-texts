The light here does not warm the air; it simply floods it. It is a cold, brilliant radiance, sourceless and hard, reflecting off miles of pale mica-textured leaves and polished steel struts with a glare that makes the horizon vanish into silver fog. 

There is no water. Not in the soil, not in the air, not in the organisms that cover every square foot of the lowland plain. If you cut into a stem—thick as a ship’s mast and ribbed like a cooling radiator—it does not bleed sap or moisture. It yields a fine, bone-dry graphite powder, or a thick, amber-colored grease that smells faintly of ozone and crushed zinc. The atmosphere is crisp and thin, staying at a perpetual, freezing chill despite the blinding light above.

The ground is entirely manufactured. Step down through the dense scrub and your foot will not find earth; it meets a floor of riveted steel plate, bronze mesh, and vitrified ceramic insulators, all heavily worn, pitted, and rounded by centuries of dry friction. This is an industrial plain the size of a continent, laid out in endless, flat terraces that step down toward a distant, nameless basin. But the machines do not stand bare. They have been consumed, overtaken, and duplicated by a flora that uses iron and voltage the way terrestrial plants use mud and rain.

Vegetation fills every volume. It is a high-density thicket of silicon-cellulose briers, copper-veined succulents, and immense, tubular mosses that grow directly out of the exhaust vents of cold, buried dynamos. The plants do not compete with the built environment; they are continuous with it. A line of cast-iron pillars, thirty feet tall and set twenty paces apart, does not merely support a tangle of vines—the vines themselves, over generations, have grown hollow, deposited iron into their own bark, and taken on the load-bearing duty of the decaying pillars. Where an engine block sits rusted in a basin, a cluster of pale, blade-like fronds sprouts from its cylinder heads, their roots replacing the pistons, sliding silently up and down in dead lubrication as the fronds expand and contract with the passage of electrical tides.

Sound is almost entirely absorbed here. The density of the dry bio-mass—billions of papery, lacquered leaves overlapping like shingles—muffles everything into a deadened hush. When a breeze passes through, it does not rustle; it sounds like dry sand sliding down a glass chute, a soft *shhh-shhh* that dies almost immediately. Deep beneath the roots, miles down in the sub-structural layers, there is a low, subterranean thrum: the heavy, slow stroke of ancient pumps pushing dry mineral dusts through buried conduits.

The rules of the landscape are subtly broken. Spatial geometry here has been softened by long exposure to something unnatural. In certain long corridors between the iron hedges, perspective does not narrow to a point; it expands, so that walking forward for a hundred paces makes the distant structures appear larger, not smaller. Shadows do not always fall away from the light; sometimes they pool around the bases of the mechanical trees like dark, viscous grease, remaining stationary even when the light shifts its intensity. A vine will occasionally loop through a solid bronze girder without displacing the metal, the plant tissue and the forged alloy occupying the exact same space in a seamless, crystalline weave.

Weather exists here, though it carries no moisture. It arrives as cold, sweeping fronts of static electricity and fine quartz dust. When a storm rolls across the lowland, the sky turns the color of brushed nickel. Millions of metallic leaves tilt in unison, clicking dryly against one another as they align themselves with the passing magnetic field. Sparks the size of needles leap from leaf-tip to leaf-tip across miles of forest canopy, a silent, blinding wave of blue light that travels from one horizon to the other without a single sound of thunder.

Fauna is nearly non-existent, but not entirely absent. Very rarely, between the thick coils of fiber-optic roots, something moves. They are not animals in any traditional sense—perhaps small, clockwork-bodied scavengers with carapaces of vulcanized resin, moving with a stiff, twitching economy of motion. They do not breathe or drink. They pick through the dry debris, clearing away fallen mica-leaves and petrified pollen-tubes to keep the ceramic insulators free of buildup, then vanish back into the hollows of the iron trunks.

Everything feels settled, ancient, and immovably in place. The initial friction between the industrial substrate and the biological takeover ended long ago; they have worn each other down to a state of quiet equilibrium. The metal has been softened by dry rot and root-acid; the plants have grown heavy, rigid, and permanent, anchored by lead and copper. It is a vast, silent, brilliantly lit factory that produces only itself, endlessly reweaving its dry, cold life across a floor of iron.
