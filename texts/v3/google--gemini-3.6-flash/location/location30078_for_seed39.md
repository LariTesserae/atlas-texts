At two thousand meters above the tree line, the world has not yet learned how to wear down its edges. 

The mountains here are not old, rounded ridges or weathered domes; they are vast, newly uplifted blocks of black shale, raw basalt, and iron-heavy schist. Their fracture planes are sharp as broken flint, untamed by ice-sheets or centuries of rain. Huge, razor-cornered slabs jut into the sky at absurd angles, creating a landscape of sudden abysses, narrow shelves, and broad, tilted plateaus that stretch for miles beneath a flat, slate-gray sky.

It is cold—a wet, biting cold that sits in the marrow. The air is thin and heavy with mist that hangs permanently at this altitude, condensing on every vertical surface. The rock faces do not merely wet; they weep. Countless cold threads of mineral-laden water trickle down the dark stone, collecting in shallow, mirror-bright pools on flat ledges before spilling over the next drop. Where the water moves over high-iron seams, it leaves thick streaks of bright rust against the dark obsidian-like basalt, staining the stone like dried blood.

Despite the emptiness of the terrain, there is a heavy, almost mechanical energy driving the place. It holds the scale and pressure of an industrial floor, though not a single stone has been worked by a tool. Deep within the mountain’s un-eroded roots, natural hydraulic forces operate with terrifying precision. Water enters deep, vertical frost-cracks, freezes, expands, and shears thousand-ton blocks of stone with a sudden, echoing *thump* that vibrates through the rock like the blow of a steam-hammer. Elsewhere, subterranean pockets of cold gas and pressurized thermal brine hiss softly from narrow vents, sending thin, pale jets of vapor straight up into the damp air, maintaining a steady, rhythmic pulsing against the silence. 

The rock itself carries a low, natural galvanism; high concentrations of magnetite and un-refined copper veins run through the freshly exposed strata, creating subtle electromagnetic charges. In the damp mist, this manifests as a faint, static hum that makes fine hair stand on end near the rock faces and causes thin, blue sparks to jump between wet mineral crystals when the wind blows hard across the crests.

Plants have barely secured a footing on this raw geology. Vegetation is sparse and low to the ground—dark, leathery lichens that stain the rock black and ochre, creeping liverworts clinging to the wet edges of the steam vents, and small, tough cushions of alpine sedge tucked into narrow seams where a few handfuls of pulverized stone have gathered. There are no bushes, no trees, no soil as it is commonly understood—only the mineral bone of the planet and the cold water washing over it.

Yet life fills every available pocket of this cold, open space.

Because the mountains are fresh and un-softened, they offer thousands of unreachable, sheer-walled crevices, and these are densely inhabited. Clinging to the dark cliffs are massive colonies of high-altitude seabirds—heavy-bodied, oily-feathered divers that utilize the tremendous updrafts rising from the abysses. Their low, guttural calls echo faintly through the mist, muted by the vastness of the empty air. 

On the broad, wet plateaus below, herds of squat, thick-coated ungulates with broad, splayed hooves move across the wet scree. They feed on the salt-rich mineral crusts and the dense patches of moss that line the warm edges of the gas vents. Their coats are pale, almost silver, making them drift like ghosts through the dim, grey light. In the cold, quiet pools that step down the valleys, thousands of dark, slow-moving amphibians thrive in the mineral-dense water, feeding on clouds of tiny, mid-altitude insects that hatch continuously from the wet margins.

The soundscape is quiet, but never dead. The acoustic environment is swallowed by the vast, open space, so noises do not echo so much as they drop away into the mist. You hear the endless, soft hiss of condensing fog; the heavy, rhythmic *drip-drip-drip* from overhangs; the distant, sudden clatter of a fresh shale-slide loosened by frost; and the low hum of the wind shearing against the sharp, un-eroded rock needles above.

Everything here is in motion, but it moves at a primal, unhurried pace. Clouds drag across the sharp plateaus, dropping freezing rain that instantly turns to ice on the stone, only to be thawed an hour later by a shift in the volcanic steam currents below. Scavengers follow the herds across the jagged terraces; raptors lean on the cold gale, watching the mist-choked gaps for movement. 

It is an raw, unfinished room of the world—a place where the Earth is still assembling its skeleton, cold and wet and loud with life, completely unconcerned with anything beyond the weight of its own stone and the endless cycle of its water.
