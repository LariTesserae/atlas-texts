The water here does not fall, nor does it surge. It simply exists as a continuous, warm mass, holding a high-altitude basin that was fashioned long ago by human or near-human hands.

This is a sunken sanctuary or a cistern built atop a forgotten peak, submerged entirely beneath a deep, quiet body of thermal fluid. There is no silt floor, no bedrock, no natural stone left exposed. Every surface within this bounded space—a room roughly the size of a parish church, vaulted above and tight at the sides—is made. The floor is composed of thick, interlocking tiles of coarse red terra-cotta; the walls are stacked blocks of dark, dense river basalt; the overhead arches are held together by keystones held fast with thick, hand-pounded copper pins. 

Yet, there are no sharp edges left. The tech that built this place was crude—simple wedges, heavy mallets, manual drag-lines, and iron-tipped chisels—but time has performed a second, far longer labor. Aeons of warm, mineral-thick water circulating through the chamber have softened every masonry seam. The square corners of the lintels are now as smooth as river-pebbles. The chisel marks that once striped the basalt have been polished away into subtle, wave-like ripples that can only be felt with a bare palm. The mortar between the tiles vanished centuries ago, but the blocks have settled so deeply into one another under their own massive weight that not a single seam gapes.

It is dark—not an absolute, terrifying void, but a heavy, velvet obscurity. Light rarely reaches down through the depth of the water above. What little visibility exists comes from the faintest bioluminescent secretions of the life that crowds the stone, a dull, bruised-purple sheen that reveals no distant horizons, only the immediate texture of the wall a arm’s-length away.

There is no green here. No kelp sways from the ceiling; no soft carpet of algae cushions the steps; no microscopic plankton blooms to cloud the liquid. The world is entirely mineral and animal.

Despite the absence of plant life, the chamber is densely inhabited. Every carved niche—originally intended, perhaps, for burial urns or simple tallow lamps—is packed with pale, hard-shelled organisms resembling flat, broad-backed isopods. They do not swim; they move with infinite slowness across the polished terra-cotta, scraping the bare, warm tile with horn-like mouthparts, feeding on the microscopic animal mucus that coats the masonry. In the joins where the copper pins have oxidized into dark turquoise crusts, clusters of translucent, blind worms anchor themselves, their thread-like feathery gills pulsing once every few seconds in the warm current. 

Thick, muscle-bound swimmers with smooth, scaleless skin glide between the stone pillars. They have no eyes—only long, sensitive barbels that brush against the basalt walls as they navigate the tight, vertical geometry of the space. They move without tail-flaps, displacing the water with a slow, undulating heave of their ribbon-like bodies, slipping into narrow archways carved into the stone floor and vanishing into the lower drainage channels.

The sound here is almost nonexistent. Water dampens all high frequencies, leaving only the low, acoustic weight of the room itself. If one presses an ear to the rounded edge of a basalt pillar, there is a faint, rhythmic *clack-clack-clack*—the collective, untiring scraping of millions of tiny chitinous legs against hand-carved clay. Occasionally, a single bubble of thermal gas, released from some deep pipe beneath the terra-cotta floor, rises through the gloom with a soft, heavy *thrum*, brushing past a cluster of worm-gills before dissolving into the dark water overhead.

Nothing changes quickly here. The temperature remains fixed at the warmth of a sleeping body. The current is a barely perceptible upward drift, driven by the heat beneath the floor tiles, lifting tiny shed bits of shell and skin toward the dark ceiling, where they slowly settle back down onto the shoulders of the carved stone faces that decorate the wall-brackets. These faces, smoothed by thousands of years of fluid friction, have lost their noses, their lips, and their eyes, returning to simple, egg-like ovals of polished black rock that watch nothing in the dark.

The boundary of this world is simple: a ring of massive, rounded pillars supporting a ceiling of flat slate slabs. Beyond the pillars, the built structure drops off into sheer, submerged drop-offs that lead into deeper, colder water, but within the chamber, the density of life and stone creates a closed, self-contained pocket. It is a place that was labored over with primitive, muscle-driven intensity, abandoned to the water, and then slowly claimed by a blind, plantless fauna that asks nothing of the world except the warm, enduring surface of hand-cut rock.
