The floor of the depression is not soil, but a fractured pavement of black, vitreous shale, cracked into vertical plates that stand like the leaves of a tightly packed book. They do not bend or crumble underfoot; their edges are fresh, glass-sharp, and cold. Across the entire basin—a low, wide trough stretching three or four miles between low-sloped basalt ridges—these stone leaves grow so close together that a hand can barely be slipped between them. They crowd the space completely, a dense, dark lattice rising waist-high in some places, shoulder-high in others, creating a maze of microscopic, razor-edged alleys.

There is no green here. No moss cushions the joints between the stones, no lichen tarnishes the glossy dark faces, and no roots bind the silt below. The world is entirely mineral, chemical, and wet. 

The wetness is absolute, but it does not fall as rain. It sits in the air as a heavy, cold aerosol—a dark fog that never quite resolves into droplets, hanging motionless under a ceiling of low, bruised-gray cloud that smothers the light to a perpetual dusk. Everything drips, but nothing washes clean. The water is thick with iron and dissolved lime, coating the slate leaves in a greasy, translucent film that smells of wet copper and cold cellar dust. Where the water pools in the narrow gaps between the stone plates, it forms dark, stagnant ribbons that reflect nothing of the overcast sky above.

Despite the windlessness of the fog, the valley is deafeningly loud. 

The sound does not come from animals or storm, but from the earth itself breathing through its fresh fractures. Deep beneath the black shale, immense reservoirs of cold water and compressed air are shifting through narrow, acoustic channels. The sound forces its way upward through the millions of slate leaves, turning the dense field of stone into a continuous, physical roar. It is a compound noise: a low, bone-shaking hum like a furnace draft, layered over with the sharp, metallic *tink-tink-tink* of cold water snapping off millions of sharp stone points at once. It fills the ears until thought becomes heavy and slow, vibrating in the chest and the marrow of the limbs.

Here and there, breaking the endless, crowded grain of the slate, sit the remains of deliberate labor. They are crude, massive, and exceedingly rare. Long walls of uncemented, hand-broken basalt boulders stretch across the basin, half-drowned in the slate needles. They do not enclose fields or shelter interiors; they are simply thick, low dikes, six feet wide and three feet high, running in straight lines for a hundred yards before terminating abruptly in the black mud. The stones are scored with faint, rhythmic gouges—not tool-marks from steel or bronze, but the smooth, wide depressions left by soft, heavy scraping, as if shaped by immense patience and hard bone. No wood, metal, or cloth remains. Whatever constructed these dikes left nothing behind but the stacked weight of the earth itself, slowly being devoured as the young shale fractures up around it.

Movement in the landscape is almost imperceptible. The valley changes on a scale of decades, not hours. The slate plates are expanding, pushed slowly upward by the hydration of deep anhydrite beds far below. An inch of movement takes a century, but the tension is visible in the razor-straight alignments and the fresh, un-weathered white seams of quartz that snap under the stress, adding a rare, dry *crack* to the humid roar of the air vents.

Fauna is nearly absent, existing only at the margins of life. In the deepest, blackest crevices between the slate leaves, where the greasy water accumulates into thick, jelly-like crusts, small creatures abide. They are blind, pale, and flattened like coins to slide between the stone edges. They have no limbs, moving instead by the slow, rhythmic peristalsis of their thick, rubbery undersides, feeding on the autotrophic bacteria that crust the iron-rich stone. They do not react to sound or light; they exist only as slow, muscular pulses in the cold dark, moving an inch a day along the wet glass of the shale.

Above them, the air remains dim, heavy, and loud. The light never brightens past the shade of slate exposed under water. A traveller standing in the basin sees only a short distance before the density of the vertical rock and the thickness of the cold, suspended moisture merge into a single, dark-gray wall. The horizon does not exist; there is only the immediate, sharp proximity of the stone, the icy grease on the hands, and the overwhelming, endless vibration of the ground singing through its cracks.
