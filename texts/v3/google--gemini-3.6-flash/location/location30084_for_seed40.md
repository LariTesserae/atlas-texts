There is no dirt here, no bedrock, no unworked stone. The ground is a floor of cold-rolled, zinc-coated steel plate, five inches thick, stamped with raised diamond-tread patterns that have not yet been worn smooth. Every edge in this place is dangerously new. The milled slots in the iron uprights still carry the tiny, parallel striations of the cutting head; the heavy hexagonal nuts holding the gantry trusses have clean, knife-sharp corners that would shave the skin from an uncareful palm.

We are high up—so high that the atmosphere is thin, cold, and blindingly clear. The light does not come from a distinct disc in the sky, but from everywhere at once: a flat, shadowless radiance that strikes the high-altitude haze and bounces back off millions of wet, galvanized surfaces. It is a surgical, unforgiving glare. It reveals every rivet line, every braided cable, every junction where six-inch steel beams meet at clean, unyielding right angles.

Despite the intensity of the light, the air is chilled—just above freezing—and dripping with a quiet, persistent damp. It does not rain in the traditional sense; rather, the cold metal sweats. Moisture condenses continuously out of the thin mountain-level air, running down vertical I-beams in thin, clear sheets and collecting in tiny, perfectly circular beads on the undersides of horizontal trusses. Where the water pools in the depressions of the steel floor, it forms shallow, mirror-like lenses an eighth of an inch deep, reflecting the crowded geometry above.

And the geometry *is* crowded. The scale is vast—stretching out for miles in every direction, an industrial plateau the size of a mountain valley—but the volume within that expanse is saturated to the point of suffocation. Pipelines three feet in diameter run parallel to one another in banks of twenty, suspended by heavy iron stirrups. Catwalks cross catwalks; dense thickets of copper-wound electrical conduits climb vertical shafts like the trunks of iron trees; massive, cold-pressed steel housings, high as three-story buildings, stand so close together that a person could barely walk between them. Everything is stacked, layered, interlocked.

Yet for all this massive industrial presence, the place is almost soundless.

The noise of a world made entirely of engines has been swallowed by the air or dampened by the sheer mass of cold metal and wet film. A piston five yards across, housed in a polished steel cylinder, glides downward with only a soft, pneumatic *hiss-shuck* that dies three steps away. The high-voltage conduits do not buzz; they emit a faint, lukewarm vibration that can be felt through the soles of one's boots rather than heard. When a drop of condensation finally detaches from a beam twenty feet up and hits a steel deck, the *ping* is sharp, tiny, and immediately gone, absorbed into the wide, cold silence.

Life has found its way into this manufactured high-tier, though it is thin and specialized. Vegetation is rare and pale. In the tight, dark seams where two plates are bolted together, away from the direct glare, a translucent, glass-like mold forms delicate, fan-shaped webs. Here and there, a crust of zinc-gray lichen clings to the unpainted heads of heavy bolts, deriving whatever nourishment it needs from the wet zinc coating and the stray trace-minerals in the air. There are no leaves, no soil-rooted stems, no green.

Fauna, however, is strangely abundant, having adapted to the endless lattice of structural iron. Thousands of small, jointed creatures—the size of field mice, but with hard, mica-sheathed carapaces and flexible copper-wire legs—scuttle along the undersides of the gantries. They move with clicking efficiency, harvesting the condensation droplets with tiny, sponge-like mouthparts or scraping the pale mold from the seams. High above, in the dense webwork of the upper girders, flocks of heavy, flat-bodied organisms with wings like sheets of thin, oiled tin roost in long, silent rows. They do not sing or cry out; they simply adjust their grip on the wet steel with soft, leaden taps of their claws, watching the vast plateaus of machinery below with pale, compound eyes that reflect the blinding overhead light.

It is in the spatial logic of the place that reality begins to fray.

Because everything is so freshly made, so precisely right-angled and densely packed, the eye expects a predictable, mechanical order. But the order is broken at its foundations. In certain wide bays between the piston housing banks, perspective does not close properly toward the distance. A long catwalk that runs straight for five hundred yards will seem to widen rather than narrow, so that walking along it feels like approaching an immense, gaping threshold that never grows any closer.

Elsewhere, the condensation behaves unnaturally. On certain vertical steel pillars, the wet film does not run down toward the floor; it creeps slowly *sideways*, drawn along the steel by a local, horizontal gradient of gravity that does not affect the air or the visitor's balance. In the shadow of the large transformer housings, one can stand and look at their own shadow cast upon the deck, only to watch it detach slightly from the heel—lagging half a second behind every movement, like a dark cloth pulled across wet iron.

These breakdowns are not violent. They do not tear the steel apart or shatter the silence. They are patient, structural errors—as if the world were designed by a brilliant engineer who understood metal, weight, and electricity perfectly, but had only a vague, secondhand memory of how space and time were supposed to hold together.

Movement here is glacial. The entire region is an active mechanism, but its cycles operate on long, quiet durations. A massive arm of solid cast iron, thirty feet thick, extends across a half-mile gap between two machinery platforms; it moves so slowly that its motion is invisible to the eye. Only by sitting on the cold deck for two hours can you notice that the shadow of the arm has shifted three inches across the diamond-plate floor, or that a new set of alignment pins has silently slipped into place with a wet, greased *click*.

You can walk for hours along the high catwalks, your breath misting softly in the cool air, without ever touching anything that was not forged, turned, or welded. Beneath your feet, through the open steel grating, there are only more layers: gantries below gantries, pipes below pipes, stretching down into a bright, wet, dense haze where more steel beams cross in silent, impossible geometries. Above, the light remains flat and brilliant, bathing the sharp edges of the new world in an endless, damp, silver glow.
