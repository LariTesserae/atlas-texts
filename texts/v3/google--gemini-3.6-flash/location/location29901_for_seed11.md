The country is a hollowed sky-gulf, a vast regional basin where an ancient continent has been worn down to its final, impossible skeleton. What remains are not jagged, young peaks, but towering, smooth-sided monoliths and perforated spires—some miles high, yet barely fifty paces wide at their bases. Eons of violent air currents have planed the granite down to silky, curved contours, carving deep flutes and thousands of cavernous, pass-through windows through the bodies of the stone giants. The landscape is mostly air; the density of earth here is almost nothing, a wide room filled with dizzying empty space between vertical stone needles.

The atmosphere is a roaring, warm furnace. Powerful thermal updrafts rise continually from unseen, sun-baked lowlands far below the cloud floor, rushing upward through the stone needles with violent velocity. This constant motion creates a deafening, continuous roar—a subterranean bass boom mixed with the high, screaming whistle of compressed air driving through the hollowed-out windows of the monoliths. The air vibrates in the chest; speech is impossible. To communicate here is to gesture or press one’s forehead against the wet slate to feel rhythmic vibrations.

Despite the dry openness of the upper air, the realm is perpetually damp. The thermal updrafts carry humid, moisture-heavy air from below, which cools rapidly as it hits the upper heights. Water does not fall as gentle rain; it condenses instantaneously on the lee side of the rock faces, sheeting down the smooth stone in shimmering, violent films, or atomizing into dense, warm spray mist that churns sideways across the voids. 

Where this moisture clings, life smothers the stone. The vertical world is astonishingly lush. Hanging gardens of thick, fibrous creeping vines, broad-leaved succulents, and deep, spongy carpets of emerald moss drape the rock walls. These plants are built for the acoustic and kinetic violence of the place: their leaves are long and ribbon-like, slicing through the air without tearing, and their roots produce a viscous, tar-like sap that glues them to the wind-buffeted granite. In sheltered pockets—cavities worn into the stone by ancient riverbeds long since lifted and erased—clumps of gigantic, pale ferns unfurl, their fronds glistening with thick, warm condensation.

The dynamic energy of the place is hyperactive. Clouds do not drift; they are seized by thermal eddies, torn into shimmering rags, spun into sudden short-lived spouts, and slammed against the spires before evaporating into the sunny sky within minutes. The light is clear, bright daytime, but it is constantly broken into frantic, moving lattices of sun and shade as mist-vortices whip across the light shafts. In certain fluted rock tunnels, the sheer speed of the wet wind creates weird localized phenomena: water drops fly horizontally between spires, or stream *upward* along the sheer cliff faces against gravity, pulled by intense pressure differentials.

Human presence is sparse, fragile, and almost swallowed by the roaring scale. Scattered along the mid-sections of the monoliths, nestled into the leeward hollows where the wind is muffled to a dull thrum, are tiny, hand-carved niches and primitive bone-anchored shelters. There are no iron beams or complex machinery here. The structures are born of hand-chipped flint, tough slate wedges driven into rock fissures, and thick cables woven from the tough core fibers of the hanging vines. A lone suspension bridge, little more than three braided ropes and slate foot-planks, sways wildly across a three-hundred-yard gulf between two spires, lashed with rawhide to ancient petrified tree stumps. 

In some places, primitive hands have altered the stone: small, smooth-chiseled holes cut into the lips of the wind-windows act as sonic baffles, turning a deafening, stone-shattering whistle into a deep, tolerable hum around a dwelling pocket. Piles of smooth river stones, brought up from some long-forgotten lower level, sit in dry crevices, alongside simple hand-pounded copper scrapers used to peel dense moss from the rock to harvest the dripping water beneath.

Fauna is exceedingly rare, highly specialized to the atmospheric tempest. Now and then, a pale, leathery creature—neither bird nor reptile, but something with broad, sail-like skin membranes—will shoot out from a high cave, lock its rigid limbs, and ride a thermal updraft thousands of feet into the upper air in a single, silent second, disappearing into the glare of the sky. Lower down, clinging to the underside of wet moss-mats, lie heavy, slow-moving shell-fauna that feed on the algae deposited by the airborne spray, oblivious to the thunderous gale raging inches from their shells.

It is a world settled into its ultimate, end-of-life geography—smoothed, emptied, and reduced to bone—yet animated by an endlessly violent, warm, and verdant pulse. The stone has surrendered almost all its mass to the air, leaving only a vast, roaring theater where wet greenery clings to ancient, whistling towers.
