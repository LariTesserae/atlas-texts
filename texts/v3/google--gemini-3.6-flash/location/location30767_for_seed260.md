The first thing that arrives is not sight, but the weight of the sound. 

It is a deafening, monolithic roar—a continuous, sky-filling vibration like the exhaust of a buried star or the throat of a continent grinding its teeth. It does not echo. Echoes require air with moisture to carry soft rebounds; here, the atmosphere is thin, cold, and entirely, impeccably dry. The sound simply occupies every square inch of space, saturating the bone, vibrating the teeth in their sockets, and pressing against the sternum until breathing feels like swallowing dry sand.

Yet nothing moves quickly to match this violence. The landscape is frozen in a patient, glacial birth.

We are standing on a high massif—an extreme, impossible altitude where the sky is a flat, dim violet-gray, devoid of clouds, sun, or stars. The light does not fall from above; it seems to emanate sideways from the horizon, casting long, razor-sharp shadows that do not taper or fade. Because there is no water vapor in the air—not a droplet, not a frost-crystal, not a trace of humidity—there is no atmospheric perspective. A spire twenty miles away appears as crisp, hard-edged, and dark as a stone held three inches from the eye. 

The geology here is embryonic. This is raw earth, newly birthed from some subterranean forge and entirely un-eroded. There are no rounded pebbles, no river-cut saddles, no smooth talus slopes. Every cliff face is a pristine fracture plane of dark feldspar and volcanic glass, as sharp as a fresh scalpels edge. Pyramidal peaks rise like needles into the dim gloom, their faces geometrically perfect, meeting at corners that could cut skin. 

And yet, despite the absolute dryness and the cold, the sheer vertical faces are lush.

It is a vegetation that has never known water. In place of moss and leaf, the black obsidian ledges are smothered in vast, thick carpets of dry, fibrous velvet—tufts of dusty ochre, pale verdigris, and tarnished silver that feel underfoot like densely woven horsehair. Giant fronds, wide as shields but thin as parchment, erupt from dry fissures in the crystalline rock. They do not droop; they are rigid, held erect by internal skeletons of hollow silica. They do not photosynthesize sunlight through sap; instead, they drink the ambient static electricity that crackles through the dry atmosphere, their edges glowing with faint, brush-like discharges of violet light. 

Further down the gorge, the growth becomes dense, almost jungle-like in its layering, though it makes a strange, dry, clicking noise whenever the immense sound-waves shake its stiff, chitinous leaves. Great braided cables of sulfur-yellow vine writhe across the cliff faces, binding the sharp stone edges in dry, fibrous knots. Where they gather in the hollows, they form deep, springy beds of dry gray fluff that smell faintly of cold iron and crushed stone.

There are no animals here. No insects crawl through the velvet lichen; no birds ride the frigid, bone-dry drafts between the spires. The emptiness of life is total, making the deafening roar feel strangely vacant—a soundtrack for an empty theater.

Yet, there are marks of hands.

Scattered across the regional expanse, perched on impossibly narrow ledges or wedged into the acute angles where two raw slate faces meet, are small, primitive structures. They are built of the local stone—unworked, sharp-edged slabs stacked with crude, careful geometry. They resemble low wind-cheats or solitary cells, barely tall enough for a person to sit upright. 

Whoever built them used no metal, no mortar, no lime. The stones are held together purely by gravity and by thick, tight lashings of the dry sulfur-vine, knotted with simple, repetitive hitches. Outside one structure sits a tool: a wedge of dense basalt, its head bruised from percussion, its edge chipped into a crude hand-axe. Beside it lies a pile of shaved silica-stalks, bundled together like tinder, though there is no sign of fire ever having burned here. 

The structures are empty. They feel neither ancient nor new; in a world without water, wind-abrasion, or rot, a stone shelter built an hour ago looks identical to one built ten thousand years ago. Nothing decays. A flake of flint dropped on a bed of dry velvet stays precisely where it fell, unburied by soil, unwashed by rain.

The weirdness of the place deepens the longer one stands beneath the roar. Gravity here is not entirely honest. In the middle distance, a cluster of thin needle-spires bends at an impossible thirty-degree angle without snapping, their growth patterns adjusting to a downward pull that seems to tilt toward an invisible mass off to the west. A handful of dust, picked up from a dry ledge and tossed into the air, does not fall straight down; it drifts sideways in a slow, unnatural spiral, suspended by the sheer kinetic push of the deafening sound waves, before settling like fine pollen on the velvet growth below.

Nothing changes quickly. The sky remains its flat, dim shade. The cold stays steady—crisp, biting, but unmoving. The lush, dry foliage hums with static, slowly expanding its crystalline fronds by a millimeter over the course of years. The immense, ear-splitting roar continues without cadence or pause, an eternal, heavy pressure holding the raw, sharp mountains in place. 

It is a landscape waiting for its second day of creation, locked in a cold, loud, beautiful paralysis at the very top of the world.
