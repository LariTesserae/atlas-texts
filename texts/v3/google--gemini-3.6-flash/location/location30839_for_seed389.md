The needles rise into the upper air with the sharpness of freshly broken flint. They are young mountains—scarcely mountains at all, but violent, unweathered fractures of black basalt and iron-rich amphibolite that have punched through the crust and kept their razor edges. Nothing has rounded them. There are no scree slopes, no gentle shoulders of soil, no soft detritus. The rock faces drop six thousand feet in single, flat-cleaved planes, shimmering in the intense, high-altitude heat that radiates outward from the mountain’s still-cooking core.

Bolted directly to these sheer, un-eroded faces is an ocean of black iron and riveted bronze: the city-foundry of Oakhaven-on-the-Spire.

It is a settlement of immense, crammed density, yet it sits suspended over a regional void. The air at this elevation is thin and dry, but the rock itself sweats. Deep within the fractures, thermal reservoirs boil, forcing plumes of superheated steam up through artificial shafts and natural fissures. The steam meets the cold high-wind pockets, collapses into heavy droplets, and coats every exposed surface in a persistent, lukewarm sheen. Copper gutters, three feet wide and green-crusted at the seams, run along the underbellies of hanging tenements, catching the runoff and directing it into massive, spherical boilers suspended between the cliffs by iron chains as thick as old trees.

There is no green anywhere. Not a blade of grass, no moss clinging to the wet iron, no lichen on the basalt. The world is entirely mineral, metallic, and chemical. Where the water drips onto hot, bare stone, it leaves behind crusts of bright yellow sulfur, white saltpeter, and dark, iridescent bands of iron oxide. 

The heat is pervasive and heavy, baking from the rock and hummed out by the city’s vast, slow-breathing engines. Yet despite the industrial saturation—the towering chimney stacks that rise like secondary spires, the cable-car trestles spanning three-mile gulfs, the multi-tiered foundries clinging to the cliffside like iron barnacles—the realm is remarkably quiet. The thin air swallows sound. The great piston houses, driven by thermal expansion and steam pressure, do not hammer with frantic violence; they move with an immense, glacial patience. A single counterweight, cast from fifty tons of crude pig-iron, descends past a residential lattice over the course of three hours, its movement marked only by the low, wet groan of oiled cable sliding over a brass sheaf.

When a sound does travel, it is isolated and crystalline: the *clack-clack* of a solitary copper-wheeled car descending a 70-degree rack-rail; the long, sighing vent of a moisture condenser releasing three thousand gallons of distilled water into an elevated cistern; the distant, hollow drop of a dropped rivet falling into the blue-grey haze of the abyss below, never striking bottom within earshot.

Looking out from the lower walkways—narrow iron grates through which the empty miles of atmosphere are continuously visible—the scale of the region reveals itself. The landscape is not a valley, but a cluster of seven distinct, needle-sharp spires, each shrouded in its own web of grey pipes, walkways, and suspended furnace-housing. Between them, miles above any ground level, stretch the long, sway-backed bridges of lattice steel, carrying high-voltage direct current cables and pressurized water lines. Sunlight hits these structures cleanly, unblocked by fog or cloud, casting sharp, un-diffused shadows across the dark metal facades. The light is bright, white, and unforgiving, highlighting the wet glare of condensation on every bolt-head.

Life, where it exists, is sparse and strange. It has nothing to do with the biology of plains or forests. Small, flat-bodied creatures with chitin the color of tarnished brass cling to the undersides of steam pipes, motionless for days. They have no eyes; they feed by absorbing the dissolved heavy minerals from the hot condensation that trickles over the iron joints. Occasionally, a high-altitude glider—a creature with pale, leathery, triangular membranes stretched over slender bone spars—will drop silently from the upper spires, stall in the rising thermal draft of a furnace exhaust, and drift slowly across the gulf toward another spire, its shadow sweeping over thousands of dark, wet windows.

Nothing here changes quickly. The rock is too young and hard to erode; the rain comes only as these internal steam-sweats; the city does not tear itself down or build new suburbs, but slowly thickens its skin. Year by year, another layer of iron plates is riveted over the old ones to keep the internal heat from cooking the living quarters. The pipes grow thicker with scale. The brass sheaves wear down by fractions of a millimeter per decade.

It is a world built at the limit of vertical space, where the earth has offered up hot, sharp iron and left no room for anything that needs soil, soft rain, or haste to survive.
