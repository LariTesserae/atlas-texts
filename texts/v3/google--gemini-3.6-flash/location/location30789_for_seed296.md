Up here, the world has forgotten how to be sharp. The mountains are so old that their peaks no longer pierce the sky; they have been worn down into colossal, rounded spines—the smoothed molars of an ancient continent, lifted five miles into the thin, cold air and left to dissolve over millions of years.

The town, if it can still be called a town, does not sit upon these ridges so much as it infests them. It clings to the vertical flanks and hollowed-out amphitheatres of the worn basalt, a dense, vertical lattice of dark brick, riveted iron, and green-black stone. Buildings are stacked six, ten, twenty tiers deep, their rear walls carved directly into the ancient, saturated cliff faces. Where the masonry ends, iron catwalks extend into the gloom, supported by massive, rust-caked trusses that vanish downward into the endless, shifting sea of low cloud.

The air is cool—a sharp, cold breath that tastes of wet slate, copper, and crushed fern. Moisture here is not a matter of rainstorms, though heavy, brief downpours do sweep through the passes; it is a permanent condition. A thick, icy fog rolls steadily off the upper peaks, condensing on every cold surface. The iron railings are continuously beaded with water; the brickwork bleeds tiny, steady rivulets that gather in zinc gutters and trickle down through a labyrinth of overhead pipes. Everything glistens with a dark, oily sheen.

Despite the extreme altitude and the chill, the place is suffocatingly green. Vegetation does not merely grow here; it claims every square inch of untouched or neglected surface. A carpet of emerald-green liverwort and thick, spongy moss covers the roofs, softening the hard angles of the industrial architecture. Long, pale tendrils of hanging fronds trail down from iron girders, dipping into suspended wooden flumes that carry condensed mountain water from tier to tier. In the crevices where the stone has crumbled into dark, rich silt, broad-leafed ferns push through, their undersides dripping onto the flagstones below.

The light never quite arrives. It is a world of permanent grey twilight, filtered through miles of dense overcast and steam. The sky above is an unmoving blanket of pearl and graphite, casting no sharp shadows. Down in the narrow alleyways between the stacked tenements, the gloom is broken only by the dim, warm amber of carbon-filament bulbs and gas lamps encased in heavy brass cages. Their glow bleeds into the fog, creating soft, circular halos that hover over the wet cobbles.

The city operates with a slow, heavy rhythm, its mechanical pulse muffled by the mist and the sheer mass of vegetation. There is a deep, low thrum that reverberates through the rock itself—the steady, cyclical stroke of massive steam-driven pumps set deep within the mountain's core. Every few minutes, a hidden relief valve opens somewhere in the lower tiers, releasing a long, sighing plume of white vapor that rises through the iron grates of the walkways, smelling faintly of coal oil and wet cedar. Overhead, heavy iron cableways groan softly as ore baskets and water buckets glide between the isolated spires, disappearing into the grey haze only to re-emerge hundreds of feet away.

Yet for all this machinery, the landscape is strangely quiet. The dampness swallows sound. The clatter of iron boots on a gantry travels only a few yards before dying out. The most persistent noise is the liquid music of the place: the wet *drip-clack* of water falling from an iron girder onto a broad fern leaf, the steady rush of condensate slipping through copper tubes, the distant, hollow roar of an invisible waterfall plunging through a fissure in the cliff face deep below.

Life here is specialized and sparse. Human presence feels small, woven quietly into the immense scale of the rock and the steam. People move along the tiered arcades wrapped in heavy, oilcloth coats, their heads turned down against the wet draft, carrying brass lanterns or bundles of harvested damp-crop from the hanging gardens. Of wild animals, there are few: small, pale amphibians with translucent skin that cling to the dripping undersides of the iron bridges, and heavy, slow-moving moths with dark, velvety wings that congregate around the dim yellow lamps, their flights soundless in the heavy air.

It is a world settled into a strange, stable middle age—a place where primeval nature and heavy, industrial human effort have ground against one another for so long that they have reached a weird equilibrium. The iron is turning back into rock; the rock is being channeled through iron; and above it all, the cold fog continues its slow, infinite passage through the ancient passes, leaving everything drenched, dim, and quietly alive.
