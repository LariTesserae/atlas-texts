The air is dry to the point of ring, baked by a radiant, unseen hearth below. It carries no moisture—not a mist, not a droplet, not even the faint chemical softness of humidity. It is a atmosphere of thin, warm gas, heavy with suspended mineral flour and sulfurous dust, kept in constant, shuddering motion by thermal updrafts that roar softly as they rise out of the dark.

Here, the world does not slope; it plunges and impales. The elevation is absurd, a vertical labyrinth of basalt needles, obsidian fins, and shattered calcified plates rising miles above a floor that remains entirely hidden in shadow. Because water has never touched these stones, there are no canyon cuts, no rounded river-pebbles, no soft fans of scree. The erosion is non-existent. The edges are razor-sharp, freshly fractured, glittering like broken dark glass where the rock has split open only hours or decades ago. It is geology in its embryonic storm: mountains are not being worn away; they are being driven upward, buckling, cracking, and expanding under immense internal heat.

The light is dim—a heavy, suffocating dusk. The sky above is a ceiling of dark indigo and bruised slate, through which no sun clearly cuts. What illumination exists bleeds up from the depths in faint, amber-red pulses, catching the undersides of high, floating stone arches and casting long, vertical shadows that stretch upward into infinity. 

Everything is warm. The rock faces radiate heat like cooling kiln bricks. To touch a wall is to feel the faint, deep-seated vibration of the earth below—a low-frequency shudder as new magma chambers press against the crust, popping seams in the mountain roots with dry, deafening *clacks* that echo up through the chasms.

In this environment of jagged pitch and hot drafts, life is an anomaly of the barest kind. There are no leaves, no roots, no stem-structures. Attached to the windward edges of the high spires are flat, copper-colored crusts—non-biological or pre-biological mats of mineral-consuming lichenoids. They do not grow so much as crystallize, drawing trace elements from the hot dry gases that rush past them. They are tough as horn, completely dry to the touch, and leave a faint, metallic smear if scraped from the glass-smooth stone. 

The dynamism of the landscape is frantic, yet oddly silent. The landscape changes not over millennia, but over days. A needle spire, three thousand meters tall, splits down its spine with a sound like a snapping whip, its half-falling outward into the gloom, only to jam against an opposing wall and form a high, precariously wedged bridge. Hot thermal plumes burst from fresh cracks, howling softly like furnace doors left ajar, carrying flakes of grey mica into the sky where they float like embers.

Yet, amid this violent, raw formation, there are marks of intention.

They are sparse, scattered across the highest, most precarious ridges where the air is thinnest and the heat most intense. There are no cities, no machinery, no wires or glowing lights. Instead, there are dry-stacked megaliths—monolithic slabs of untrimmed, dark stone wedged into the fractures with uncanny precision. On certain high peaks, cut straight out of the unweathered rock face, are deep, rectangular niches, broad enough to hold a house, but entirely empty. 

Near the edges of these cutouts stand tall, thin cairns of obsidian, built without mortar, balanced so delicately that the hot updrafts make them sing with a dry, metallic rattle, yet they do not fall. Some of these structures have been sheared in half by recent tectonic shifts, their upper portions carried hundreds of meters upward on new rock fins, leaving the lower courses hanging over deep, lightless gulfs. Who—or what—stacked these stones did so without iron tools, leaving no marks of chiseled labor, only smooth, melted-looking planes where the rock was pushed aside like warm wax.

Sound travels strangely here. The dry air carries high pitches poorly, deadening the sound of crumbling stone into a dull, thudding click. The primary sound is the breath of the heat: a deep, continuous, multi-toned whistle as warm gas forces its way through thousands of vertical vents and hollow spires. It sounds like an endless organ note, shifting pitch whenever a new crack opens in the floor below.

Standing on one of the narrow, unweathered terraces, looking out across the expanse, the view is a chaotic forest of dark spires fading into amber-tinted gloom. There is no horizon—only higher peaks piercing the dark clouds above, and deeper abysses sinking into the glow below. The rock beneath your feet is hot, sharp, and rising. The world is incomplete, unfinished, and fiercely dry, building itself endlessly in the dark.
