The basin is no larger than a parish churchyard, tucked into a damp, low-lying fold of the valley where cold air cannot settle and warm vapor never fully leaves. It was once a power-house or a pump-station—something built with heavy riveted plates, cast-iron flywheels, and thick copper bus-bars—but the distinction between the structure and what grows within it vanished long ago.

There is no soil here that is not root-matter, and no rock that is not iron. The floor is a dense, springy mattress of compressed peat, liverwort, and dead wood, five or six feet deep, soaked through like a sponge. Beneath it, buried deep in the green weave, you can feel the flat tops of iron floor-plates, long since rusted into soft orange scales and bound tight by the fibrous cable-roots of the giant reed-ferns. 

Every surface is occupied. Where a vertical bronze conduit rises from the moss, it is wrapped in three distinct layers of climbing fig, whose leaves overlap like scales on a pangolin, glossy and dark as oil. The air is warm—a steady, conservatory heat—and smells of crushed mint, wet iron, transformer fluid, and the sharp, clean tang of bruised starch.

Light comes from above, bright and unscattered, falling through the open skeleton of a vaulted iron roof whose glass panes dropped out decades ago. The trusses remain, thick as oak limbs, completely encased in mosses so saturated with water that they drip perpetually. The rain here is not a weather event; it is an internal system. Moisture drawn up through the deep root-mats by the sun’s warmth condenses on the cold iron overhead and falls back down in a steady, heavy patter, clicking against the broad, rubbery leaves of the lower canopy.

In the center of the space sits a boiler housing, sixteen feet tall, constructed of overlapping steel plates pinned with thumb-sized rivets. It is no longer hollow. A single, colossal succulent—something like an agave, but with fleshy leaves the thickness of cedar planks—has taken root in the firebox. Its growth has expanded with such relentless, hydraulic force that it has split the boiler along its seams. Iron plates four inches thick have been bent outward like peeled orange rind, their edges raw and torn, held in place by the very leaves that ruptured them. 

The technology here is not dead; it has been integrated into a single, slow-motion circuit. Copper cables, thick as a man’s wrist and green with verdigris, trail from ruined junction boxes into the heart of the reed-beds. Sap runs through these beds under immense pressure. In places, where a main root has squeezed through a brass pipe-fitting, the plant has conformed to the threading, screwing itself into the metal until the seal is watertight. 

The sound is constant and unexpectedly loud. It is not the sound of wind or wildlife—there are no birds here, no frogs, almost no insects beyond a tiny, blind species of white gnat that hovers over the warmer pools. The noise comes from the place itself. It is a wet, mechanical thrum: the low, resonant groan of iron beams being flexed by expanding wood; the wet, rhythmic *clack-hiss* of a spring-loaded pressure valve that still operates, triggered every few minutes by the buildup of sap and steam within a buried pipe; and the unending, hollow roar of water rushing through underground conduits that have been turned into root-lined chimneys.

Movement is almost imperceptible, yet the forces involved are vast. A cast-iron drive-pulley, eight feet in diameter, hangs from a central wall. Wrapped around its groove is not a hemp rope, but the main stem of a woody liana. Over the course of a day, as the plant expands in the afternoon heat and contracts in the cooler night, the wheel turns maybe half an inch. It grinds against its dry bronze bushing with a sound like a distant foghorn—a single, long-drawn note that echoes off the wet brick perimeter walls and dies away in the steam.

Everything is stable. The rust has reached a depth where it no longer scales; it has formed a dark, purple-black patina under the thick coats of algae. The plants do not rot where they touch the iron; they feed on the mineral salts leaking from the joints. The water is clear, tinted amber by tannins, pooling in iron troughs and brass drain-basins where tiny, thread-like roots form thick, floating carpets that can support the weight of a stone.

It is a small, completely filled world—bounded by four moss-dripping brick walls, open to the sky, humming with a slow, monstrous energy that belongs neither entirely to the engine nor to the leaf.
