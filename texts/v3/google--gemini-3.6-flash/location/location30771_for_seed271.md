The basin sits roughly sixty paces across, tucked into a gentle fold of old, low country where the rock long ago gave up its sharp edges. It is a shallow hollow, walled on three sides by soft, water-worn sandstone that curves inward like the ruins of an unroofed hall. The air inside stays warm and remarkably still, trapped by the lip of the rise above, holding a thick, humid weight that never quite turns to rain.

The ground here is a mix of fine, dark silt and smooth, oval pebbles. Nothing moves quickly. There are no breezes to stir the air, no leaves to rustle, and no animals to cross the mud. 

Water does not flow in a stream, but it is everywhere in small, patient quantities. A faint, slick sheen coats the base of the northern rock face where moisture slowly seeps through invisible pores in the stone. It gathers in tiny, saucer-sized depressions along the floor—shallow pockets of clear water that sit so still they look like dark glass set into the earth. Occasionally, a single heavy drop falls from a hanging lip of sandstone, breaking the surface of a pool with a flat, quiet *plink*, then the water resets its mirror immediately.

Vegetation is sparse and kept to the margins. Patches of dark, velvety moss cling to the seams where the rock meets the silt, so deep green they are almost black in the dim light. In the drier crevices higher up the wall, a few tough, pale liverworts and stunted, leathery fronds sprout in isolated clusters, separated by wide stretches of bare, damp stone. They do not seem to grow or wither; they simply occupy the damp crevices, held in a permanent, suspended maturity.

Near the center of the hollow, half-buried in the silt, sit four or five large, square-cut stones. Their corners have been rounded by centuries of seepage and condensation, blurring the line between what was quarried and what was eroded by nature. They are arranged in a loose, broken right angle—perhaps the corner of an ancient, unmortared retaining wall or a simple hearth long abandoned before tools of iron were forged. No other trace of build remains; no charcoal, no timber, no scrap of metal. Just the heavy, square stones settling deeper into the mud year after year.

The light inside the basin is uniformly dim, filtered down from an overcast sky that never seems to clear or darken further. It casts no sharp shadows, casting instead a soft, amber-grey glow across the wet rock faces. There are no sudden shifts in temperature or light; morning and afternoon feel identical here, held in a warm, closed equilibrium. 

The silence is dense. Without insects, birds, or the sound of wind in trees, the acoustics of the small hollow make even the faint condensation drips sound distinct and isolated. Between those infrequent drops, the quiet returns instantly, complete and unbroken, as if the hollow were a room sealed off from the rest of the land.
