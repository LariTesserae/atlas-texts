The world is a high shelf of black, knife-edged basalt suspended above an unseen floor, vast enough that its cold, damp air mass generates its own slow, motionless climate. 

Here, at an altitude where the sky is hard and pale blue, the mountains have no soft contours. They are young—geologically abrupt, extruded only yesterday in the memory of stone. Their ridges are razor-thin, their faces sheer, unmarred by wind-chiseled caves or scree slopes. There are no loose pebbles. Every block of rock is a monolithic slab with edges sharp enough to cut, standing miles apart from the next. Between these giant teeth lie vast, empty gulfs of air, miles across, where nothing floats and no birds cross. The scale is oceanic, but the space is mostly clear, cold vacuum and dripping iron.

Despite the altitude and the bitter cold—a frost that bites at the skin without ever quite freezing the moisture—the landscape is saturated. Water does not fall as soft rain; it bleeds directly out of the atmosphere and the pores of the young rock. Every vertical wall of basalt is a weeping face. Thick sheets of liquid cling to the stone, moving downward in heavy, glassy ribbons that do not ripple. Where two slabs meet, water pools in shallow, mirror-like troughs that spill over edges in endless, silent curtains, dropping thousands of feet into the lower void.

Clinging to this wet, razor-sharp geology is an immense, heavy flora. It is lush, but completely devoid of grace or movement. Dark, rubbery ferns with leaves the size of skiffs grow directly out of the sheer basalt, their roots secreting a pale slime that anchors them against gravity. Mats of thick, indigo-colored moss, three feet deep and dense as wet wool, wrap the lower skirts of the rock towers. There are thickets of jointed, pipe-like reeds that rise sixty feet high, filled with cold fluid that throbs at a slow, mechanical rhythm, though no wind ever moves them. The vegetation is motionless. It does not sway, drop leaves, or bloom. It exists in a permanent, swollen greenness, fed by the constant condensation and the minerals leaching from the fresh rock.

Across these empty miles, straddling the chasms between the basalt spires, sits an industrial urban sprawl that seems both ancient and freshly riveted. It is not a city for crowds; it is an architecture of pure function expanded to a planetary scale, yet populated by no one. 

Bridge-works of blackened cast iron, five hundred feet tall and wide enough for trainyards, span the gulfs between the peaks. Massive, cylindrical factory complexes are bolted directly to the sheer cliffsides, their zinc-plated roofs weeping cold condensation into the moss below. Pipes the diameter of houses crawl up the mountainsides like iron snakes, wrapped in sweating canvas and copper bands, pumping cold brine and oily sludge between distant, isolated hubs. Heavy electrical substations, suspended from steel cables thick as tree trunks, hang in the open air between spires, surrounded by a faint, violet halo of corona discharge that turns the wet air sour with the smell of ozone.

The technology is massive, loud, and mechanical. Piston houses as large as cathedrals sit on stone shelves, their exposed flywheels—two hundred feet across—turning with a sluggish, terrifying momentum. Steam—or something like it, cold and dense—vents from brass nozzles directly into the ferns, coating their broad leaves in gray grease.

And the sound is relentless. 

It is not a sound that rises and falls; it is a solid wall of acoustic pressure that has remained at precisely the same volume for centuries. The churning of the massive flywheels, the deep, gut-shaking groan of structural iron expanding against cold stone, the high-voltage hiss of the suspended cables, and the thunder of millions of tons of water falling off the basalt shelves merge into a single, deafening roar. It is a drone so vast and continuous that it ceases to feel like noise and becomes a physical property of the air, like the pressure or the temperature.

Nothing moves that isn't made of iron or stone, and yet nothing rots. The air holds no spores of decay, no insects to burrow through the rubbery leaves, no worms in the black silt beneath the moss. If a steel beam drops from a gantry, it lands on a bed of wet indigo moss and remains there, unrusting, bathed in dripping condensate, forever.

The light is flat and uncompromisingly clear. There are no clouds—only the perpetual mist that clings to the iron girders and the wet rock face, illuminated by the bright, high-altitude sun. Shadows are hard, blue-black shapes cast across miles of empty air onto the faces of neighboring spires. 

In this world, time has no traction. The young rock will never wear smooth, because the liquid that coats it does not erode; it preserves. The heavy ferns will not wither. The giant brass pistons will continue their slow, deafening stroke, driving cold fluid through the iron veins of the bridges, bridging an empty, wet abyss that never changes, never warms, and never falls quiet.
