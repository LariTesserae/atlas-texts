The floor is a continental basin of cold-rolled steel and glazed white ceramic tile, stretching out beneath an artificial sky of luminous, soft-gold ceiling grids that vanish into warm haze thousands of meters above. There is no dirt here. There is no stone, no clay, no sand. Every surface—from the low, sharp-edged drainage troughs to the massive octagonal pillars supporting the overhead machinery—has been stamped, cast, or extruded. The geometry is fresh and precise; the seams between the giant iron floor plates are knife-perfect, free from corrosion or pitting, despite the unending wetness.

It is hot, thick with the heavy humidity of a laundry house or a boiler room. The air holds a constant, warm condensation that coats every hard corner in a shimmering film of pure water. Droplets form continuously on the undersides of high structural I-beams, gathering until they fall in heavy, quiet splashes into the shallow, slow-moving gutters that divide the floor into a grid of kilometer-wide squares. The water smells faint and clean—distilled, with a light trace of hot copper and mineral oil.

No leaf grows in this place. There is no moss along the wet tile joints, no lichen on the iron, no mold in the dampest recesses. Green is entirely absent from the spectrum. 

In place of plants, the fauna has overwhelmed the structure.

They cover every square meter of horizontal and vertical real estate in a soft, squirming density that blurs the sharp industrial lines of the architecture. On the wet floor plates, millions of flat, pale creatures with slick, rubbery hides lie packed together like living cobblestones. They do not fight or disperse; they shift in place with a gentle, rhythmic undulating motion, sipping the film of warm water through breath-holes along their flanks. Above them, clinging in thick, glistening clusters to the vertical faces of the porcelain pillars, are thousands of segmented, legless entities the color of wet liver, their translucent bodies revealing internal organs that glow with faint, ambient phosphorescence.

Higher up, where the copper conduit trunks run in miles-long bundles, winged things hang suspended by their tails. They are soft-bodied, hairless, and rounded like plump fruit, their leathery wings folded tightly around themselves. They do not fly so much as drop from beam to beam in slow, calculated falls, landing with soft, wet thuds among the carpet of ground-dwellers before crawling back up the iron struts. In the shallow water channels, dense knots of long, pale eel-like organisms weave endlessly through one another, creating a thick, writhing rope of muscle that fills the gutters from bank to bank, filtering the runoff through thousands of delicate, feathery gills.

Despite the sheer volume of life, the world is hushed.

The noise of the ecosystem is muffled by its own density. Millions of soft bodies absorb sound. The air carries only a low, rhythmic *squelch*—the collective shift of soft hide against wet tile—punctuated by the steady *plip-plip-plip* of condensation falling from the high rafters. The machinery itself does not clatter or grind; it operates in a state of frictionless, hydraulic perfection. Deep within the floor plates, huge pistons cycle with a sound like a drawn-out sigh, moving fluid through buried conduits with a subsonic thrum that can be felt in the soles of the feet rather than heard.

Nothing changes here. The light from the high, gold-glowing ceiling does not dim, pulse, or cycle. There are no dawns, no twilights, no shadows that lengthen or retreat. The temperature rests perpetually at a heavy, tropical thirty degrees Celsius. The water does not evaporate into clouds, nor does it dry out; it cycles through the floor drains, rises as warm vapor through the hollow interiors of the structural columns, condenses on the high roof, and drips back down in an eternal, closed loop.

The creatures do not seem to age or decay. When one of the soft ground-dwellers dies, its body is quietly dissolved by the warm, chemically tuned water within hours, its biomass absorbed by its neighbors through their skin, leaving behind only a brief, clear smear on the ceramic tile before the spot is covered once more by the slow, crowding movement of the swarm.

To walk across this landscape is to wade ankle-deep through a living, breathing mat of warm flesh, resting upon an infinitely precise foundation of wet iron and polished tile. The horizon is always the same: endless rows of pristine, cream-white pillars rising out of a writhing carpet of pale life, disappearing into the warm, bright, golden fog overhead. There is nowhere to climb above the wetness, nowhere to step that is not made, and nowhere to look that is not crowded with silent, gentle motion.
