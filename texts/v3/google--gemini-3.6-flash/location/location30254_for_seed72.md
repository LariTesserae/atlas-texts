Deep beneath the surface crust, in a vault carved from unweathered slate and sharp, black basalt, sits the Sump of the Lower District. The rock here has no smooth curves; it bears the fresh, jagged scars of heavy pneumatic drills and pick-heads—raw geometry that has not yet had time to erode or dull under water. The floor is a maze of flooded cutouts and drop-shafts, where steam-heated water sits inches deep over jagged gravel beds.

The air is stifling, thick, and brutally hot, cooked by proximity to deep geothermal veins and the continuous radiation of heavy iron boilers stacked against the far rock face. Humidity sits at the saturation point. Water does not merely fall; it condenses out of the stagnant air onto every available surface, forming fat droplets on overhead beams that rain down steadily onto the iron walkways below. Sweating walls of dark stone bleed tiny rivulets that feed open, rust-stained masonry sluices.

Despite the harsh, subterranean environment, this bounded cavern—no larger than a few block-lengths of a city, hemmed in by sheer walls of unpolished rock—is fully colonized by human industry. Tiered brick housing blocks, dripping with moisture, are wedged directly into natural fractures in the stone. Cast-iron catwalks, black with grease and wet soot, crisscross overhead in a dense web of rivet-headed gridwork. Steam pipes as thick as tree trunks run in parallel trunks along the rock walls, wrapped in canvas insulation that rots and darkens in the wet heat. Heavy, slow-stroking beam engines thump somewhere deeper in the walls, their iron arms rising and falling with a metallic groan that vibrates through the soles of boots.

Vegetation is nearly absent. There are no green leaves here, only a pale, slick skin of white mold clinging to dead timber supports, and thin, jelly-like threads of reddish slime that thrive in the warm, iron-rich overflow channels. 

Where plant life fails, animal life floods the gap with suffocating density.

The vault boils with creatures adapted to the dark, warm damp. Millions of glossy, dark-shelled cave beetles—each the size of a thumb—blanket the cold water pipes, crawling over one another in writhing, shimmering layers three insects deep. The brick walls hum with them; their dry, brittle legs make a constant, dry-leaf rustle that competes with the pounding of the engines. Along the edges of the sluices, pale, blind crabs with elongated claws fight over scraps of organic grease washed down from the living quarters above. In the upper shadows of the cavern roof, where the steam settles in thick clouds, tens of thousands of subterranean swifts roost in dense, squabbling colonies, their shrill chirping echoing off the flat rock faces. Farther down in the flooded trenches, blind, pale-skinned eels, thick as human wrists, twist slowly through the warm water, rising to snap at insects that fall from the gangways.

Lighting is dim and localized. Filthy glass globes housing low-wattage carbon filaments cast a dull, amber haze through the fog, barely illuminating more than a few yards ahead. Flashes of orange fire light up the gaps between brick tenements whenever a fireman throws open a furnace door to shovel coal into the boilers, casting long, jittery shadows of swinging pipes and flying swifts across the ceiling.

The sounds are loud and constant, yet curiously rhythmic. The subterranean air amplifies every noise: the steady *thump-hiss* of the water pumps, the wet splash of condensation dropping into puddles, the clanking of iron chains, and beneath it all, the overwhelming, multi-layered rasp and chitter of millions of crawling limbs. 

Nothing changes quickly here. The landscape exists in a slow, suffocating equilibrium. The steel rusts in fat, dark flakes, but at a rate measured in decades. The water flows through its channels at a sluggish crawl, choked with carapaces and sediment. The heat is constant, trapped by miles of stone above, holding the entire vault in a permanent, sweat-soaked pulse where the noise of the machines and the noise of the swarm become indistinguishable.
