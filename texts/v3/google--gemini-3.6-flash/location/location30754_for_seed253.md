Here, at the summit of a drowned continent, the ocean is dark, cold, and absolute. 

The world is made entirely of carved stone and ancient root. Long ago, every ledge, terrace, and cliff face was laid down in heavy, precision-cut masonry—vast step-pyramids, grand aqueducts, and miles-long colonnades that once crowned a mountain range higher than any sky. Now, those peaks are buried miles beneath a motionless, sunless sea. There is no raw dirt or natural bedrock visible anywhere. The substrate is entirely artificial, yet entirely choked by life.

Thick, velvety aquatic mosses pad every surface of the masonry, rounding off the sharp corners of ancient ashlar blocks. From the gargantuan arches, thick cords of fibrous, wood-like kelp descend, stretching like mooring cables across vast, water-filled gulfs. These plants do not merely grow upon the stone; they hold it together. Their lignified root systems have driven deep into the seams of the old walls, swelling to fuse the blocks into a single, continuous, biological monolith. The architecture has become a reef, and the reef is a forest.

The air does not exist; the water is dense and chill, hovering just above freezing. Light is a rare, bruised thing—less an illumination and more a faint, directional inkling that dies a few dozen paces out. The darkness is near-total, save for the dull, unblinking bioluminescence of cold-water lichen that carpets the undersides of the stone lintels.

The scale is colossal and open. Vast, empty gulfs of black water separate the major structures. You can drift off the edge of a terraced plaza and look down into a void that drops away for three miles before hitting the lower slopes. Out in that vast emptiness, distant spires—overgrown with shaggy, swaying fronds—rise like forgotten islands in the gloom. The structures are spaced far apart, leaving immense volumes of clear, dark water between them, giving the place an unsettling, cavernous spaciousness.

It is loud here, but the sound is not a storm; it is a permanent, roaring acoustic resonance. Massive, invariant deep-sea currents push millions of tons of cold water through the hollowed-out interiors of the drowned buildings. The aqueducts, windows, and drainage galleries act as colossal organ pipes, vibrating with a thunderous, low-frequency hum that shakes the water itself. It is a single, continuous bass note that has not shifted pitch in ten thousand years.

Life is everywhere, quiet and abundant against the roaring water. Shoals of pale, translucent ribbon-fish drift through the roofless halls in synchronized ribbons. Blind, white crustaceans with long, hair-thin antennae crawl over the mossy bas-reliefs, picking at microscopic detritus. Giant, slow-pulsing jellies, trailing tendrils thirty feet long, float lazily through the tall archways, undisturbed by the heavy movement of the water. There are no predators hunting aggressively, no frantic migrations; the ecosystem exists in a dense, cold saturation, perfectly adapted to the dim light and endless current.

Nothing changes here. The water never warms or cools. The current never slacks or surges. There are no tides, no seasons, no day or night. The plants do not drop leaves; they grow at the exact rate they are consumed by the blind grazer-fauna. The ancient stone slowly yields its sharp edges to the endless friction of water and root, settling deeper and deeper into a permanent, unchanging equilibrium. 

It is an abyss on a mountaintop: a drowned kingdom of stone and vine, suspended in cold blackness, singing its single roaring note into the endless deep.
