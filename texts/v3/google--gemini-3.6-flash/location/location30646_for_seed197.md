The space is the size of a town square or a cathedral’s central nave, but it has no stone, no iron, and no clear ceiling. Instead, it is bounded on all sides by an old, overgrown urbanism made entirely of wood, fiber, and living cell wall. 

It sits in a low, sun-warmed depression. The air smells of wet cellulose, bruised rind, and rich, turned soil. It is damp in pockets—warm condensation collects in the grooves of giant fibrous pillars and runs down in thin, shivering ribbons, pooling in shallow bowls carved out of ancient, petrified root systems. The water does not stay long; it is sucked back into the substrate through microscopic pores, only to be sweated out again fifty feet higher up as a fine, warm mist.

What looks at first like a city is actually a single, monumental plant that has long since settled into the shape of a settlement. There are multi-story avenues carved by decades of directed growth: hollow, towering trunks that function as tenements or grain silos; high-wire sky-bridges of tightly braided tendrils that span the empty air; wide, flat terraces of hardened leaf-pulp that jut out like balcony floors. There are no tools here, no metal, no joints held by nails or mortar. Every staircase is a helical spur of hardened cambium; every doorway is a sphincter of bark that expanded long ago and stayed open. The erosion is advanced and peaceful—sharp edges of old leaf-structures have rotted back into smooth, greyed-out curves, weathered by years of warm rain and biological wear until the wood looks like polished river silt.

Yet inside this city-shaped organism, the space is strangely hollow. The interior volume of the plaza is vast and empty. You can look across a hundred yards of open, warm daylight before hitting the opposite wall of living towers. The air within this central void is clear, save for the occasional falling seed or floating filament of spores, giving the place the quiet, vaulted spaciousness of a cleared ruin—if not for the noise.

The sound here is catastrophic.

It is a deafening, continuous wall of acoustic force that vibrates the marrow of the jaw. The hollow trunks act as giant organ pipes, catching the warm, rising updrafts and converting them into a deep, sub-bass thrum that rattles the leaves. Layered over this bass is the ceaseless, high-pitched frenzy of millions of tiny inhabitants: chitinous beetles the size of knuckles scraping their wings against the dry, ribbed underside of bark awnings; thick-bodied amphibians wedged into root-pipes, inflating throat-sacs to blast out rhythmic, air-horn whistles; and the wet, booming *crack* of pressurized seed-pods exploding overhead every few seconds, launching heavy nut-kernels across the open atrium like mortar shells.

And beneath the animal racket is the sound of the vegetation itself—a wet, straining roar of hydraulic pressure. Millions of gallons of sap are being pumped through vascular conduits as thick as tree trunks, whistling through internal valves, surging against bark walls, making the very ground beneath your feet tremble like the deck of a wooden ship in a gale.

Here, the rules of physical space have begun to unravel under the weight of so much living mass. 

Light falls from a clear, high sun, but it does not travel in straight lines through the plaza. In the center of the open air, the sunlight bends slightly around dense pockets of humidity, fracturing into bright, geometric bands that cast shadows at impossible angles—a towering root-wall might throw a shadow that bends upward against a floating vine, or duplicate itself three times across a flat terrace. 

Distances expand and contract depending on where you look. A hanging bridge made of interwoven willow-thin shoots appears to be fifty paces away, but a bird flying toward it seems to take minutes to cross the gap, its wings beating in a slow-motion blur before it suddenly snaps into scale against the bark. Gravity, too, seems like a suggestion the fauna has forgotten to follow: heavy, six-legged tortoises walk unhurriedly up vertical bark cliffs, their splay-toed feet bonding to the wood with a wet, suction-pop, while small, featherless rodents leap into the open air and drift upward for several feet on local warm-air eddies before catching a passing branch.

Everything is in motion. The landscape is not a backdrop; it is a rapid, churning engine. Vines grow visibly, their tendrils sweeping through the air in wide, blind circles like slow clock hands, seizing onto anything that holds still for more than an hour. A terrace on the east side of the square slowly unfurls a fresh layer of pale green felt to catch the midday sun, while on the west side, an older section of bark sloughs off with a thunderous *crash*, raining soft, rotten loam onto the lower roots where hundreds of red-shelled scavengers immediately swarm to consume it.

It is warm, bright, hollow, and loud beyond endurance. It feels ancient, as if this plant-built city has stood in this low basin for ten thousand years, slowly building its own sky, its own physics, and its own deafening, endless conversation.
