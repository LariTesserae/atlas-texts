The basin sits in a perpetual, sweltering damp. It is a broad, low-lying sink where the air feels as heavy as wet wool, trapping the heat close to the ground. Across miles of waterlogged mudflats and shallow, stagnant lagoons, the remains of a vast industrial district stretch toward a hazy, iron-tinted horizon.

There is no green here. Not a blade of grass grows in the cracks of the cobblestones; no ivy clings to the towering brick stacks, and no algae greens the edges of the sluggish canals. The world is entirely mineral, metallic, and animal. 

The architecture is immense in footprint but sparse in distribution. Sprawling foundry complexes, cathedral-sized boiler halls, and elevated iron viaducts stand like isolated monoliths across the flat landscape. Between these cavernous structures are vast, empty spaces—flooded yards paved in dark granite, broad canal junctions where the water has risen over the banks, and miles of sunken rail line where cast-iron ties lie buried under an inch of lukewarm, mineral-rich silt. The masonry is old and rounded, its sharp corners long softened by centuries of heat, steam, and sulfurous rain. Red brick has turned the color of wet liver; iron girders are thick with velvety, saturated rust that never quite dries, continually sloughing off in wet flakes into the mud below.

Water is everywhere, though it rarely falls as clean rain. Instead, it drips, seeps, and condenses. The high temperature keeps the air at the point of saturation, so that cold iron beams inside the shadow of the boiler houses weep continuous streams of warm water. The ground is a sponge of wet slag, wet ash, and water-logged clay. Steaming rivulets trick systemically along the gutters of long-abandoned haul-roads, carrying fine particles of iron oxide and crushed lime toward the central lagoons.

Despite the dim, overcast sky—a flat, bronze-grey sheet of cloud that diffuses the sun into an omnidirectional, glareless heat—the basin is teeming with life. It is not an alien life, but a familiar one stripped of its flora and multiplied to a suffocating density.

Without plants to form the base of the food web, the ecosystem feeds upon itself, the rich mineral runoff, and the endless accumulation of organic silt. Millions of dark, shiny mud-crabs carpet the exposed brick ramps of the shipping basins, their shells clicking softly as they sift through the iron-rich mud for microbes and detritus. The damp, shadowy interiors of the brick warehouses are packed solid with roosting swallows and heavy, grease-feathered gulls; their droppings coat the dormant machinery in a thick, white glaze that smells sharply of salt and ammonia. 

In the warmer pools, where runoff from deep underground pipes still bubbles up at near-boiling temperatures, long, pale eels and thick-bodied carp navigate the sunken engine beds, feeding on swarms of blind, aquatic larvae. Where the iron structures meet the water, clusters of heavy, black-shelled mussels bind the rusted pilings together, three feet deep, their shells constantly filtering the grey mist that settles on the water's surface. 

At nightfall—or during the deeper lulls in the twilight—the air above the open flats becomes thick with insects. Heavy, slow-flying beetles, their carapaces stained the same dull orange as the rusted girders, bump blindly against the brick facades. Dragonflies with six-inch wingspans hover over the flooded rail yards, hunting midges that rise in dense, vibrating columns from the warm mud.

Yet for all this biological density, the basin is remarkably quiet. The animal life moves with a muted, humid efficiency. There is no wind to rustle leaves, because there are no leaves to rustle. The sound of the insects is not a roar, but a low, subterranean hum that blends with the constant *drip-drip-drip* of condensation falling from the upper ironwork into the black water below. Occasionally, a section of water-soaked masonry settles into the mud with a dull, wet thud, or a flock of gulls erupts from a cooling tower with a sudden, muffled clatter of wings, but the sound is quickly swallowed by the thick, warm air.

Processes continue slowly, automatically. In some of the deeper, partially submerged sub-basements, automated sump pumps—driven by the natural pressure of geothermal steam rising through deep boreholes—continue to stroke with a slow, mechanical pulse: *clack-hiss, clack-hiss*. They lift hot, iron-stained water out of the lower galleries and discharge it onto the surface, keeping the grand, empty avenues at a constant depth of two inches. 

The heat does not rise and fall with the day; it remains locked in the heavy stone and the wet earth. As the light dims further, turning the foggy air from bronze to a deep, bruised charcoal, the silhouettes of the grand chimneys and the long, low factory roofs blur into the gloom. The eyes of millions of small creatures glint in the low light along the water’s edge—a shimmering, restless margin where the constructed world is being slowly, quietly digested by the life that has outlived its makers.
