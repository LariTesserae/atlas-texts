The water here does not fall; it rests. High among the jagged, black-crested shoulders of a mountain range that should by rights be scoured by cold winds, there is instead a deep, suspended sea—a vast, lukewarm basin held aloft on a table of young rock. To stand—or drift—within it is to exist in an elevated bath, thousands of feet above the lowlands, encased in an olive-bronze twilight that rarely changes from morning to night.

The geology is raw, almost embryonic. The basalt beneath the liquid has no soft edges, no soil, no sand. It erupted recently in geological time, cooling into smooth, bulbous pillow-lavas and towering, octagonal basalt columns whose faces are as crisp and razor-sharp as freshly chipped obsidian. No frost has ever cracked these surfaces; no rain has worn their arrises into gentle slopes. The rock is naked, dark, and pristine, emerging directly from the planet’s warm interior into the holding tank of the sky-sea.

Yet despite the hardness of the stone, the landscape is thick with life. Lush, ribbon-like macro-algae and bronze-leafed sea-vines do not wait for soil; they anchor their fibrous holdfasts directly into the micro-fissures of the unweathered glass. They climb the vertical cliffs in dense, undulating curtains, forming floating canopies twenty yards thick that catch the dim light filtered through the upper water column. The plants are heavy, rich with oils, and warm to the touch, warmed from below by tiny, sulfur-sweet thermal vents that bubble up through the basalt floor in lazy, wavering plumes of refraction.

Within this drowned, high valley sits a city.

It is a settlement of stone and rope, constructed with painstaking, primitive labor. Thousands of hand-hewn obsidian and granite blocks—cut with horn wedges and smooth river-cobbles—are stacked without mortar into terraced, square-built towers and wide, cellular complexes. Because the water supports much of the masonry's weight, the buildings reach improbable heights for unmortared stone: six-story structures lean toward one another across narrow, submerged lanes, their tops tied together with fat, braided cables of cured kelp-skin to prevent them from swaying in the gentle internal tides.

The streets are paved with flat, unworked basalt flags, swept clean of silt by the slow drift of the thermal currents. Square doorways and unglazed window-apertures stack atop one another like the cells of a mud-wasp nest. Roofs are made of heavy slate slabs covered in thick layers of living moss-sponges, which serve as insulation and bedding. Between the towers, bridges made of hollow, sun-dried reed-bundles—weighted with lead stones so they hover in mid-water rather than float to the surface—span the gaps, allowing passage from one district to another without descending to the street floor.

Technology here is simple, quiet, and tactile. There are no chimneys, no fires, no forge-sparks. Metal is absent or rare, replaced by ground-shell blades, knapped flint chisels, and bone needles. Tools are tethered to wrists with fine sinew so they do not drift into the dark crevices between the pillow-lavas if dropped. Machinery consists of wooden-pulleys and counterweighted stone elevators used to haul heavy baskets of harvested tubers up from the lower terraced gardens.

The air—or rather, the water—is dim. The sun above is only a pale, brassy disk suspended in a ceiling of greenish mist far overhead, its rays broken into long, motionless shafts that fade into indigo before they reach the deepest alleys of the lower city. 

Fauna is sparse, making the city feel curiously uninhabited even where the architecture is dense. Occasionally, a single, pale, ribbon-bodied glass-eel, four paces long, will weave its way through the upper canopy of kelp, its translucent ribs catching the light like a ladder of silver needles. Small, sessile barnacle-mollusks cling to the corners of the stone houses, their tiny feathery fans sweeping the water in rhythm with the heat-plumes, but there are no shoals of fish, no screaming birds, no sudden darting movements. 

The sound of the place is a heavy, muffled silence. Water dampens everything to a dull, rhythmic hum. The loudest noise is the occasional *thud-clack* of a stone mallet working somewhere three terraces down, or the low, vibrating groan of a kelp-cable taking the strain of a shifting timber bridge. The thermal vents produce a perpetual, low-frequency murmur—a sound felt in the chest more than heard in the ear—like a distant engine idling at the bottom of a well.

Change happens across years, not hours. The basalt columns grow thin coatings of white carbonate crust over generations; the kelp cables are replaced once every decade as they soften and rot; the buildings do not fall, because there are no storms here, no waves, only the slow, languid churn of warm water rising from the volcanic earth, holding the high, dark city in a state of quiet, suspended youth.
