The air is cold and thick with a steady, atomized drizzle that never quite resolves into rain, yet coats every surface in a silver sheen. The world here is low and vast—a wide, dish-shaped basin stretching for miles toward unseen, fog-wrapped rims. There are no sharp edges anywhere. Time and relentless water have smoothed the stone into something resembling folded skin or cooled wax. The bedrock is a dark, grease-slicked slate, worn so flat that water does not flow in rivers, but sheets across thousands of acres at a depth no greater than an ankle, pooling in shallow, saucer-like depressions before spilling quietly into the next.

Nothing green grows here. There is no grass, no scrub, no moss, nor any recognizable foliage. The mineral substrate is bare except for a thin, rubbery biofilm—a pale, tallow-colored scum that thrives in the cold damp, drawing its energy from the sulfurous mineral wash sweating up through microscopic pores in the rock. 

Despite the lack of plants, the basin is intensely, chaoticly alive. 

The sound hits first, long before the details of the landscape clarify in the dim, shadowless light. It is deafening—a roaring, rhythmic metallic thunder that vibrates in the chest and teeth. It is the sound of millions. Across the endless gray flats, vast herds of armored, low-slung creatures—the size of oxen, but shaped like broad, flat beetles—are moving. Their underbellies are equipped with heavy, chitinous radulae, chitin-toothed scraping plates that scrub the tallow-scum from the wet stone. The friction of millions of hard plates grinding against ancient slate creates a dry, scree-like roar that echoes across the open plain, overlaid with the liquid sloshing of their heavy bodies through the surface water.

Smaller things dart between them: pale, long-legged scavengers with oil-slick carapaces, scooping up the churned mineral pulp left in the herds' wake; blind, eel-like predators that weave through the ankle-deep sheets of water, striking at the joints of the scrapers. Above, where the gray sky hangs low and bruised, flocks of membranous, rib-winged gliders drop like thrown knives to snatch creatures from the shallow pools, their shrieking lost entirely beneath the ceaseless roar of the feeding herds.

Here and there, rising above the sheets of sliding water, are small hummocks of stone—slightly higher swells in the bedrock that remain dry. On these isolated mounds sit the faint, scattered marks of habitability. 

They are crude, low shelters, constructed without metal or wood. Instead, they are built from the pale, calcified rib-arches of dead scrapers, laced together with stripped intestine and weighed down at the base by heavy, water-worn river stones. They look like the carapaces of giant, dormant beasts, half-merged with the gray terrain. Outside one shelter, a low hearth pit glows—not with wood-fire, but with the thick, yellow flare of animal grease rendering over a pile of porous, oil-soaked chalk.

Near the hearths, simple tools lie on the wet stone: hand-axes knapped from dark, glassy basalt, heavy bone needles, and scrapers made from the jawbones of predatory eels, their serrated edges used to strip fat from thick, rubbery hides. A line of woven gut-string stretches between two stone pegs, hung with strips of black, fatty meat drying in the damp wind, preserved by the salt mist that hangs perpetually over the basin.

The inhabitants of these shelters are few and widely spaced. A single family might occupy a hummock, their presence almost swallowed by the sheer expanse of the gray plain. They move with a slow, heavy economy of posture, wearing long coats stitched from overlapping plates of waterproof carapace and lined with greasy down. They do not speak; the thunder of the basin makes voice useless. They communicate through sharp, emphatic hand-gestures and the rhythmic drumming of bone staves against the hollow rib-posts of their huts.

The world operates under a strict, ancient logic. The water moves in slow, hydrostatic tides, driven not by a moon, but by the periodic swelling of underground reservoirs that force fresh, cold water up through the center of the basin, sending a ripple of cold brine across the flats before it retreats back into subterranean cracks. When the tide surges, the herds turn together, their deafening rasping shifting pitch as they face into the current, scraping faster as the fresh mineral wash hits the rock.

The light never brightens beyond a heavy twilight. The sun is a pale, smudged coin behind miles of dense, weeping cloud, casting no crisp shadows. Everything—the slick stone, the wet bone frame of the shelters, the chitinous backs of the beasts, the faces of the people—glistens with the same dull, metallic rain. 

As the eye travels outward toward the horizon, the scattered shelters vanish into the gray haze, the flat stone runs on without interruption, and the deafening, grinding roar of the land feeding on itself fills every corner of the sky.
