The mountains here were rounded off ten million years ago. What remains of the range is not a set of jagged peaks, but a vast, drowned staircase of smooth green basalt and water-sculpted granite, where whole tablelands sit three miles above sea level yet lie entirely underwater. 

It is a high-altitude ocean, or a drowned continent perpetually sliding over itself. The water is cold, clear, and emerald-hued, fed by atmospheric rain-belts that never break. In some valleys, the current moves with the steady, crushing weight of a glacier made of liquid; in others, the ocean simply spills over the lip of a thousand-foot shelf, dropping into the lower fog in a curtain of foam so dense it supports its own floating gardens of liverwort and cold-water kelp.

Everything is in motion. The dynamic here is hydraulic and relentless. Swings in the upper surges shift millions of tons of water from basin to basin every few hours, carving deep, musical whirlpools into the ancient limestone terraces. The stone itself bears the signature of this perpetual movement: every edge is bowl-smooth, scalloped like the inside of a sea-shell, pitted with ancient pot-holes where granite boulders the size of houses have been spinning in place since before the first iron was forged.

Despite the cold—the air rarely rises above forty degrees and the spray carries a bone-deep chill—the biological density is extreme. Vegetation coats every surface that isn't scrubbed bare by the main flumes. Massive, rope-like bladders of dark amber weed root themselves in the basalt grooves, trailing hundreds of yards down the waterfalls, their air-sacs tugging against the torrent. Above the waterline, where the peaks break into the grey light as slick, whale-backed islands, the rock is buried under three feet of sodden, spongy peat, dwarf willow, and giant cold-ferns that drip continuously into the froth.

Below the surface, the water is crowded with silver. Heavy-bodied cold-water fish—armored sturgeon with pale underbellies, deep-chested salmon, and schools of translucent ice-char—fight their way up the flumes, jumping from basin to basin against currents that would tear a man’s clothes off. Web-footed otters the size of black bears hunt through the drowned fern-forests, their dark fur slicked flat, emerging onto rusted iron platforms to crack open heavy, thick-shelled mussels.

Human presence is sparse, heavy, and purely functional. It has been bolted directly into the bedrock to keep from being washed away.

Every few miles, perched on the narrow lip of a terraced abyss or wedged into a basalt notch, sits an industrial relic. These are not delicate ruins; they are heavy, cast-iron pump houses, copper-sheathed sluice-towers, and massive grease-blackened winches mounted on granite piers. Long, heavy cables of twisted iron wire—some as thick as a tree trunk—span the gaps between drowned peaks, dripping with weed and vibration. They disappear into the foam, holding submerged dredging buckets or tethering timber-pontoons against the racing tide.

Here and there, a coal-fired boiler still chugs in a dry recess beneath an overhanging ledge. Its short stack vents black smoke into the perpetual mist, and the rhythmic, metallic *thud-clank* of a double-acting steam pump joins the deep bass roar of the falls. These machines were built to regulate something—perhaps to drain a valley that flooded anyway, or to clear silt from submerged granite quarries whose stone blocks, carved with ancient lifted marks, still lie stacked in neat, drowned rows fifty feet below the surface.

The sound is immense and layered. It is not a single roar, but a choir of water working against iron and stone. There is the high, tearing whistle of spray pushed through narrow rock slits; the heavy, gut-shaking thud of a million gallons striking a submerged shelf; the clatter of loose cobbles rolling in a chute; and the deep, groan of iron chains strained to their limit as the tide shifts the floating timber booms.

The light is steady and grey, filtered through miles of hanging mist and low, fast-moving rain-stratus. It catches the emerald translucency of the water as it crests the drop-offs, making the top six inches of every surge glow like liquid glass before it shatters into white lace against the rocks. Down in the drowned valleys, the light fades into a clear, moss-green dusk where the rusted girders of flooded bridges look like the ribs of stranded whales, lost among the swaying stalks of the deep weed.

Nothing stays dry. The air is more than half water, a dense aerosol of cold freshwater spray that tastes of iron, wet granite, and crushed pine needle. To move here is to wade, swim, or climb up wet iron ladders while a river runs down your sleeves. The world has settled into this state; the mountains will not erode any further, the iron will rust no faster than the water can coat it in silt, and the cold current will go on turning its stone wheels until the boiler fires finally go out.
