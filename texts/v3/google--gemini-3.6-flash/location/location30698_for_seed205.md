There is no stone here, nor soil in any sense that a spade would recognize. The continent underfoot is a single, ancient shelf of lignified tissue—a dead-wood plain five thousand meters above the lower haze, hard as slate, cured by centuries of high-altitude sun. Beneath the bleached cork surface lie kilometers of petrified vascular bundles, compressed peat, and dry, tubular conduits through which the sap of an earlier epoch once ran.

It is hot—a dry, baking heat that radiates upward from the dark grain of the floor. The sun sits heavy in a sky pale with fine, floating wood-dust.

Because the land is entirely made of plant matter, it has grown its own geography. But it is not a thicket; it is immensely vast and surprisingly empty. Miles of flat, polished timber-plateau stretch out between single, cathedral-sized features. A visitor could walk for half a day across a smooth expanse of sun-bleached bark-crust—marked only by the giant rings of ancient growth-spurt nodes—before reaching the base of the next vertical column.

These columns are the *Choked Flues*. They rise hundreds of meters straight up out of the wood-plain: hollow, chimney-like trunks of dead ironwood, each as wide as a fortress, their outer hulls fluted and scarred by wind. They stand far apart from one another, casting long, purple shadows across the dry grain of the world.

Water does not gather in rivers; there is no loose silt to carve. Instead, the climate is driven by transpiration and condensation trapped within the plant architecture itself. Deep inside the hollow flues, where the sun cannot penetrate, the air remains damp. Nightly temperature drops force moisture out of the hot air, coating the interior walls with thick, cold sweat. This water drips into deep, resin-lined bowls—galls formed by long-extinct parasites—creating clear, stagnant wells fifty feet above the plain. Outside these wells, the landscape is dry, save for the sudden, brief rain-shafts that break off the high-altitude cloud caps. These micro-storms are narrow—sometimes only a few hundred paces wide—and sweep across the timber floor like gray pillars, dampening the cork for twenty minutes before the heat drinks it back up.

The sound is relentless. It is not the sound of wind over rock, but the acoustics of an endless wood instrument played by high-altitude convection. The wind drives through the open flues, producing a low, sub-audible vibration that shakes the marrow of the legs. Layered over this drone is the deafening chorus of the fauna.

The animal life does not merely inhabit the wood; it works it. Millions of hard-shelled, armored arthropods—some as long as a man’s forearm, with bodies of iridescent bronze chitin—cling to the vertical ridges of the flues. They do not sing with lungs; they strike their ribbed bellies against the dried timber, using the hollow columns as sounding boards. The result is a rattling, rhythmic roar that drowns out speech within three miles of any spire. Swarms of high-flying, ribbon-tailed gliders dart between the distant towers, their leathery wings making a sharp *whir-snap* as they catch the thermal updrafts rising from the hot floor. The air is thick with them—a shimmering, chaotic ecology of drill-mouthed borers, sap-skimmers, and heavy, slow-moving herbivores that scrape the dry lichen from the wood-plains with bone-like palps.

Erosion here is a process of polishing and flaking rather than washing away. The wind has scoured the flat lands until the hard, dark late-wood stands up in sharp, parallel ridges, while the softer spring-wood between has been hollowed into shallow, smooth troughs. Huge natural arches—the petrified aerial roots of long-collapsed canopy trees—span the empty air between low swells in the plateau, cured by the heat until they ring like iron when struck.

Traces of intelligent life are sparse, small, and fragile against this scale.

Where a flue has split open from ancient heat-stress, one might find a handful of shelters tucked into the deep fissure. They are built entirely of peeled bark-shingles, bound with dried vine-gut and sealed with boiled dammar resin. The people who live here—if they are still people—move with silent, sure-footed lightness across the polished wood-grain. They carry no metal; there are no ores to dig from the heart of a plant. Their tools are knapped from fire-hardened wooden galls—dense, glass-like growths that form when certain vines attack the ironwood—and carved from the heavy mandibles of the larger borer-beetles.

Near these scattered shelters, small signs of manual craft appear:
Deep, hand-carved channels cut into the bark to catch the condensate as it seeps from a flue’s base.
Rope bridges, suspended eighty feet in the air between two bark-ridges, woven from the tough, silky inner fibers of dead climber-vines.
Faint, dark glyphs burned into the sun-bleached timber using focused sunlight and polished resin lenses—markers indicating which wells are sweet and which have turned bitter with tannin.

The world moves at the slow pace of seasoned wood expanding and contracting under the sun. At midday, as the heat reaches its peak, the ground itself begins to speak: a loud, echoing *crack-snap* travels through the continent as three-mile-long plates of dry bark shift by a fraction of an inch, settling under their own massive weight. Then the sound fades back into the deafening rattle of the bronze beetles, the whistle of the high winds through the empty hollows, and the shimmering glare of the sun on an endless sea of dry, carved grain.
