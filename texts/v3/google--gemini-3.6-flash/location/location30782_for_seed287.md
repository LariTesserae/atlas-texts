The vault is eighty feet below the terrace of sandstone that caps the basin, squeezed into a seam of black shale that was cleared out with black powder a century ago and then forgotten.

It is hot inside—not the dry heat of the upper sun, but a heavy, stagnant fever that smells of boiled river silt, wet iron, and the sharp, vegetable tang of bruised stalks. The air does not move in breezes; it shifts in slow, thick boluses, pushed around by the rising heat of the low machinery buried beneath the floorboards. Everything drips, though it never rains. Condensation forms on the undersides of the cast-iron roof girders, gathers in dark, fat drops that hang until their weight snaps them off, sending them down through three tiers of rusted grating to strike the copper casings below with a sharp *clink*.

The space is no larger than a small chapel—perhaps sixty paces from the curved brick end-wall to the blocked intake archway—but it feels crowded to the point of suffocation. The density is not rock, but a tight, layered weave of forgotten industry and fleshy, shade-tolerant greenery. 

There are no animals here. No earthworms stir the black sludge in the drainage channels; no spiders spin between the cold pressure gauges; no flies hover above the pools of warm condensate. The vegetation has evolved to do without them. Plants propagate by long, jointed runners that feel their way along the seams of rivets, or by thick, oily spores that drop straight down into the crevices of the floor. Without insects to trim or pollinate, the flora grows in solid, heavy sheets: waxy-leaved climbers with stems as thick as a man’s wrist that have strangled the ladder rungs; low, liver-colored mosses that form coats three inches deep over the housings of the electric dynamos; and broad, heart-shaped fronds that spread flat against the hot copper boilers, sucking the radiant heat straight out of the metal.

The light is a murky, tea-colored dimness. It filters down through three narrow glass-and-iron lanterns set into the vaulted ceiling, but the glass has long since turned green with algae and gray with soot. What reaches the floor is not beam or shaft, but a soft, directional gloom that turns the wet leaf-tops silver and leaves the hollows under the turbine casings in pitch blackness.

A continuous, thunderous noise fills the hall, so loud that a shout would be swallowed two feet from the mouth. It is not the sharp clatter of modern machinery, but a low, subterranean pounding—the iron heart of a slow-stroke hydraulic engine buried somewhere in the wet shale beneath the foundations. Every eleven seconds, the entire room shudders. The water standing in the iron drip-pans forms precise concentric rings; the heavy ferns hanging from the catwalks bounce in unison; and a column of pale, wet steam vents from a cracked flange near the ceiling with a dry, screaming whistle that lasts four seconds before dying back into the thump of the stroke.

The erosion here is long past the stage of jagged collapse. The stone walls have been licked smooth by a hundred years of warm mist. The sharp corners of the brickwork are rounded like river cobbles, coated in a dark, vitreous glaze formed by the union of lime-wash and machine oil. Where iron plates meet, the rust has not flaked away into orange dust; instead, under the constant heat and moisture, it has cured into a hard, glossy black crust, smooth as magnetite, upon which small, white rootlets have etched intricate, branching maps.

Structures are everywhere, but they no longer serve human feet. A iron catwalk hangs at an angle, its central span rusted through and replaced by a braided suspension bridge of living vine-stems. A control desk—its brass levers bent sideways like grass pushed by a footstep—is completely buried beneath a dome of thick, gelatinous fungus that glistens like wet gut in the low light.

Change happens, but at an agonizing pace. A single turn of a brass valve, seized by mineral deposits, might take fifty years to crust over entirely. A fern frond, growing against the hot face of a steam pipe, scorches into black carbon at the tip while its base pushes out three new shoots in the humid air. The machinery does not seem to be dying so much as it has settled into an endless, self-sustaining loop: the hot pipes warm the roots; the transpiration from the dense leaves fills the air with moisture; the moisture settles back into the sump wells to feed the low, invisible boilers that keep the iron heart beating every eleven seconds, forever, in the warm dark.
