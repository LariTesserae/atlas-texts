High on the shoulder of a basalt ridge, sheltered from the mountain winds by a semi-circular wall of dark, wet stone, lies an open terrace no larger than a village square. At this altitude, the air should be thin and cold, but here the rock holds a steady, radiant warmth—fed by deep-seated geothermal veins that heat the water seeping through every seam and crack in the cliff face.

The space is structured like a roofless vault. The cliff behind it rises three hundred feet, curved like the apse of a cathedral, while the outer edge drops away into a void of sunlit mountain haze. Everything within this pocket is drenched in bright, unfiltered light, saturated with moisture that hangs in glowing, golden sheets where sunbeams strike the fine spray. 

It is wet with an ancient, settled patience. Water does not rush here in a torrent; rather, it *weeps*. Millions of tiny droplets emerge from the black basalt, coalescing into thin, glistening ribbons that slide over moss-covered terraces into shallow, stone basins. These basins are smoothly carved, rounded by centuries of steady dripping until their lips are as seamless as lip-balm on skin. The water is mineral-heavy and warm to the touch, depositing a translucent crust of pale travertine over everything it touches, petrifying fallen leaves into delicate, stone-sheathed fossils before they can rot.

Despite the lushness of the plant life, the space feels uncluttered, almost spare. The vegetation does not form an impenetrable jungle; instead, it consists of a few massive, perfectly spaced individuals. A pair of giant fern-trees, their trunks as thick as ancient columns and covered in fibrous dark fur, arch thirty feet overhead, their fronds spreading broad emerald ceilings that leave wide gaps of open sky between them. On the damp stone floor, fleshy-leaved succulent mosses grow in thick, raised cushions the color of jade, separated by broad ribbons of dark, polished slate that are kept clean by the constant, gentle flow of water. 

There is a strangeness to the flora born of the mineral warmth and high sun. A species of low, sprawling reed grows with stems of brilliant, metallic yellow, hollowed like pipes; when the warm air currents rise from the wet rock, these reeds vibrate with a faint, breathy pitch. Broad, heart-shaped leaves the size of shields hang from the cliff face on thick vines, their undersides coated in a silvery fur that gathers condensation until it forms heavy, fat drops that fall with rhythmic precision into the pools below.

The acoustics of the terrace are startlingly loud. The hollow curve of the basalt amphitheater catches the sound of a single, slender waterfall—falling from a notch high on the cliff—and amplifies it into a deep, booming roar that fills the chest. The sound does not feel violent; it is a steady, physical reverberation, a continuous low drumroll that vibrates through the soles of your feet. The dripping of the high walls, echoed and multiplied by the stone, creates a dense, ringing metallic chime underneath the thunder of the fall, like a hundred tiny hammers striking brass anvils.

Of animals, there is almost no trace. Now and then, a solitary creature—something like a heavy, broad-shelled lizard with pale, translucent skin—crawls with extreme slowness across a sunlit patch of slate, its claws making a faint clicking sound against the stone before it disappears into a deep fissure. High above, small black birds occasionally cut across the opening between the cliff rim and the sky, but they never land here; the sonic pressure of the amphitheater seems to keep them at bay.

Human presence is absent, yet the mark of simple, ancient intent lingers at the margins. Embedded in the crotch of a fern-tree, eighteen feet off the ground, is a heavy chunk of black obsidian, its edge expertly knapped into a crescent blade, now half-enveloped by the tree’s slow-growing bark. Near the base of the waterfall, three large river stones—foreign to this basalt ridge—sit arranged in an offset line, their upper surfaces worn completely flat by the repeated scraping of some hand-held tool long since washed away. A row of shallow notches has been chipped into the sheer basalt near a vertical chimney in the rock, providing handholds just deep enough for three fingers; the edges of these notches are rounded and smooth, slicked with a fine layer of mineral lime. 

Nothing changes quickly in this place. The dynamic is one of immense, patient accretion. The travertine grows by the thickness of a hair each season. The fern-trees add a single frond a year. The water continues its loud, booming song, pouring through the same stone channels it cut ten thousand years ago, holding the warm, bright air in a state of perpetual, dripping equilibrium.
