I am three inches above the flagstone, seven inches behind the face of the wall, wedged in the dark seam between two foundation blocks of gray schist. 

I do not stand upon the stone; I am held by it. 

The gap is no wider than the thickness of a silver coin, but my ribs were made for this. They are soft, long, and hinged like thin willow twigs. When I creep into the joint, I exhale until my lungs are flat ribbons, press my belly against the coarse lower block, and let the crushing weight of forty tons of dry-stacked granite settle against my back. It does not hurt. It is a cold, immovable hug. It is the only place in the mountain where the thin, high wind cannot touch my skin.

My skin is the city’s skin. It is rough, fine-grained, and slate-gray, broken by tiny, raised scales the color of rust-orange lichen. If you were to pull away the stone above me—which no frost has managed to do in three thousand years—you would not see me until I moved. 

And I almost never move.

***

Time here does not pass in hours; it passes in pulses and temperatures. 

My heart beat once when the sharp shadow of the eastern peak crossed the center drainage gutter. It will beat again when the shadow touches the lip of the north cistern. Between those two beats, a hundred drops of water will condense on the lintel above, run down the dark, polished streak of stone, and drop into the basin.

*Clack-drip.*

The sound travels through the granite far better than it travels through the thin air. It reaches the pads of my unblinking feet as a microscopic ring—a sharp, cold vibration that tells me the water is still moving, the notch is still breathing, and the clear, tiny things in the dark basin below are shifting their antennae to feed.

I know those tiny ones, though I have never seen them. I taste them in the moisture that seeps into my crevice. The water carries the cold grit of the upper crags, the bitter tang of the black moss spores, and, high up near the lintel, a faint, metallic sweetness—the green rust of a cold-hammered copper pin that turned to powder long before my great-grandmother pressed her first sticky egg into this same deep joint.

The copper is in the water; the water is on the stone; the stone is against my skin. We are all made of the same cold chemistry.

***

Above me, two inches higher in the same vertical seam, a thread of soot-black silk passes through the dark. It is anchor-line for the weaver in the lintel hollow. 

I can feel her through my claw-tips. She is suspended in the draft, her long, jointed legs resting against a petrified wood peg that hasn’t held a curtain in a thousand years. She reads the wind; I read the stone. When a gale comes blowing hard from the west, dry and whistling through the notch, her silk tightens and twangs against the schist. When the wet fog rolls up from the lower valleys, heavy with the smell of wet pine and deep earth, the silk slacks, weighed down by microscopic beads of frost.

We do not fight for space. She owns the air in the doorway; I own the dark between the blocks. 

Occasionally, the wind brings us a gift. A high-altitude moth, its pale wings battered by the upper air, blown five thousand feet above the trees where it belongs. It tumbles across the cold flagstones, seeking shelter from the gale, and creeps into the first dark gap it finds.

It enters my seam.

I feel its arrival long before I hear it. Its tiny, dry feet make a sound against the schist like sand falling on paper. *Scrape-shuff. Scrape-shuff.* 

My heart beats twice in rapid succession—a sudden, violent heat in my chest. My ribs flatten further. My slate-colored claws, tipped with tiny, adhesive pads that can grip a single grain of quartz, find their traction. 

I do not pounce. Pouncing is for creatures of the sun, creatures with warm blood to burn. I slide. I become a moving shadow within a still shadow. My skin makes a faint, dry *tink-tink-tink* against the granite—a sound so small, so perfectly matched to the mountain, that if a man were standing in the empty plaza outside, he would only think a flake of ice had sloughed off the lintel.

The moth does not know I am here until my jaws close over its dusty, frozen thorax. The dust of its wings tastes of low places, of sap and rotting leaves—things that have no meaning up here on the granite shelf. I swallow it slowly, absorbing its moisture, its tiny spark of valley heat, and then I return to my torpor.

The meal will last me three weeks. My heart slows again. One beat. Two beats. The shadow moves another inch across the plaza.

***

People lived here once. I know this not because I remember them, but because the stone remembers them. 

Natural rock does not fracture at ninety-degree angles. Natural rock does not leave channels three inches wide running down the precise center of a slope to catch the seep. The people who built this place understood the weight of granite and the cruelty of ice, but most of all, they understood *lines*. They built this notch so that nothing is wasted, nothing is loose, and nothing falls.

They made these joints for themselves, or perhaps for the frost, but in doing so, they carved a world that fits my body with absolute, mathematical perfection. I am the exact width of their patience.

Sometimes, on the clearest afternoons, when the sun is low and the shadow of the stunted willow stretches long and thin across the paving, the terrace warms by half a degree. The stone gives off a faint, dry smell—the smell of old dust and baked quartz. 

In those moments, I creep an inch closer to the mouth of the joint. I do not come out into the light—the swift overhead would see my shadow, and the sun would dry my skin—but I put my snout at the very edge of the gap, where the cold air meets the bright, sharp silence.

I watch the light move across the flagstones. I watch the slow, single drip fall from the lintel into the square basin. I feel the mountain beneath me, cold and impossibly deep, anchored to the center of the earth.

Nothing is changing. Nothing needs to change. The city is waiting, the water is dripping, and I am holding the stone together from the inside.
