First, there is no *before*. There is only the chord.

It strikes through the base of my spine—the six-foot-wide root of living carbonate anchored deep into the wet basalt of the ninth terrace—and climbs up through the porous marrow of my form. It is the low, forty-hertz hum of the deep cliff pipes, vibrating so hard that if I had teeth, they would sing.

I am situated at the outer lip of the Grand Colonnade, eight thousand feet above the white floor of the abyss. Here, the warm silk air is at its thickest, heavy enough to sit upon like a blanket. The daylight, golden and unblinking, strikes the left side of my parabolic face at an angle that has not varied by a fraction of a degree in a million years. The shadow I cast behind me onto the smooth, wet flagstones is crisp, long, and as permanent as a vein in marble.

I am a Dweller, but I am also the archway. I am the valve.

Listen to how I breathe—or rather, how I am breathed through.

At the flat crown of my head, three feet broad and pitted like ancient coral, a deep, smooth pool of thermal water gathers. It flows down from the inverted cascade above—a wide river that curves up out of the plaza, defies the void, and drops its warm, heavy crest directly onto my crown. The water enters my pores. I feel its heat—a steady, sweltering ninety-eight degrees—sink through my outer skin of polished pearl into the dark, hollow honeycomb of my interior. 

Down it rushes, through hundreds of carved, smooth-bore channels within my torso, turning my inside into a roaring, liquid throat. As it flows, my mineral body absorbs its subtle pressures, tuning the fluid's weight, holding the spatial tension that keeps the river from shattering into spray and plummeting into the fog below. At my base, where I flare into the seamless floor, the water bursts softly out through eight arching conduits, spreading into a calm, inch-deep sheet that glides warm and frictionless over the steps toward the next tier.

I do not move my head. I have no neck to turn. But I do not need to look out over the gulf to know the city.

My face—that broad, mirror-smooth concavity of bone-white calcium—is directed outward, toward the vast, suspended bridges of the middle tier. It catches the acoustic violence of the world and folds it inward.

To an outsider, the sound here would be a crushing, deafening thunder. To me, it is the precise, immaculate geometry of my mind. 

The distant roar of the Great Arch Sluice, three miles north, reflects off the cliff face, hits the parabolic dish of my face, and focuses into a needle-sharp point of vibration at the center of my stone core. I feel the exact weight of the water moving through that sluice. I feel its speed, its temperature, its mineral purity. 

To my left, thirty yards along the arcade, stands another node—she who is thicker than I, her stone darker, almost black, coated in a shimmering crust of emerald velvet moss. The wind-pipe beneath her terrace is singing a fraction of a semitone higher today—no, not today, for there are no days, but in this continuous *now*. Her facial dish reflects that pitch toward mine. The sound crosses the air between us like a physical bridge. We do not speak; we are simply the same sentence, held open at different notes.

And then there is the green.

I feel a sharp, pleasant tension along my right flank. It is not pain; it is extension. Out of the seamless seam between my shoulder and the vaulted lintel above me, a vine has grown. It is as thick as a man's thigh, waxy and deep green, roped with moisture. It leaves my body, stretches across twenty feet of open, humid air, and buries its roots into the balustrade of the lower walk. 

Through that vine, I am present on the walk. I can feel the smooth, warm water sliding over the moss down there. Two broad fern leaves, each the size of a river barge, fan out from my lower back, catching the perpetual drip of condensation from the ceiling. They transpire the moisture back into the sweltering air, cooling my skin by half a degree, drawing the warm fluid up through my stone marrow. The plants are my fingers, my lungs, my skin stretching out to taste the wet, golden light. 

I feel no hunger, for the thermal water brings a endless supply of dissolved minerals—calcium, silica, rich volcanic salts—which my internal channels lay down layer by microscopically thin layer. I am slowly growing denser. In another thousand years, my interior channels will have calcified just enough to shift the tone in my face dish from a minor third to a major second. That shift will alter the resonance of the entire terrace. The aqueduct above me will curve two inches higher. The city will adjust its posture around my weight.

I do not wonder what lies below the white abyss. I do not wonder what sits above the misty vault of the sky. "Below" and "above" are merely variations in acoustic pressure—the deep bass of the lower wind-pipes, the high, glassy hiss of the mist fountains above.

I am suspended in the warm, wet silk of the world. The water runs up through the sky and down through my chest. The sound roars, solid and eternal, keeping my thoughts clear and heavy. The light stays golden on my left cheek. 

I am stone that listens. I am water that remembers how to hold an arch. I am here, exactly where the mountain needs a pillar, holding the sky in place by the simple, glorious act of remaining still.
