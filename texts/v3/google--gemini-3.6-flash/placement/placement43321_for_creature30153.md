I am sitting in the **Cradle of the Shearing Knot**, three hundred feet below the high steel viaduct of District Seven, wedged into the hollow belly of a suspended gall-tumor that stopped growing two centuries ago. 

Where am I? I am at the precise point where three primary growth rings—each ninety feet wide, dark as boiled leather and slick with cold condensation—buckle against a dense, swirling whorl of petrified grain. Here, the floor does not sweep smoothly toward the horizon. It warps upward like a frozen wave, creating an island of normalized space no larger than a cooper’s workshop.

Outside my carved gall-window, the world is a dizzying, stretched lens. If I look straight down the line of the *Eighth Prime Ring*, I can see the black-iron silhouette of Sap-Stamp Forty-Two. It is six leagues away, yet it looms so close through the spatial compression that I can count the wet, bronze rivets on its piston-housing. If I look three inches to the left, across the grain toward the tallow-works, a building forty yards off appears diminished to the size of a thimble, drowned in a mile of stretched, grey air.

I am a **Knot-Sounder**. My job is to sit where the world buckles and make sure the city doesn't tear itself off its own foundation when the grain shifts.

***

Who am I? 

Look at my boots first. They are made of six-ply ox-hide soaked in resin, clamped with heavy brass bands around the ankles, and bottomed with thick slabs of dense vulcanized rubber. I do not wear them to walk; I wear them to keep my shins from splitting. 

I am thirty-eight years old, which makes me an elder among those who work the open wood. My body has long since abandoned the vertical posture of the lowlands. My hips are set wide and low; my lower spine is locked in a permanent, spring-like curve. When I stand, my knees remain bent at a forty-five-degree angle, my thighs thick and iron-hard from thirty years of absorbing the plateau's pulse. 

Inside my mouth, my tongue rests against a dark, metallic fence. My natural molars were shattered to white powder by the time I was twenty-two, shaken to pieces by the constant shudder of the iron pistons two miles away. In their place, a Guild surgeon hammered ten smoothed plugs of soft lead and tin directly into the sockets of my jaw. When the Sap-Stamps drop—*THUD-CLACK... THUD-CLACK*—the metal plugs absorb the hit. They hum with a dull, heavy *ping* deep inside my skull, a low-register counterweight that keeps my brain from sloshing against my forehead.

Over my eyes are my amber goggles, framed in greased leather. Even inside the dark, polished vault of my gall-house, I keep them pulled low. The high sun outside hits the wet walnut floor and bounces back as a blinding, oily sheet of white light that can burn the sight right out of a man’s head.

On the low table beside me rests my meal: a cold iron bowl of thick, salty Black-Moss paste, heavily peppered with dried grit-lice, and a pewter goblet of Static-Spirits. The liquor is so cold it frost-coats the pewter, but when it touches my metal teeth, it throws a tiny, blue electric spark across my gums, numbing the constant ache in my neck for an hour or two.

***

My hands are thick, the palms calloused into grey horn, but my fingers are long and tipped with broad, iron-capped thimbles.

Right now, I am working. 

I hold my two-foot bronze tapping-rod against the floor. The rod doesn't rest on the wood; it is wedged firmly into a hairline split in the grain that runs straight down into the heart of the Knot. The top of the rod is pressed hard against the patch of bare bone just behind my left ear.

Through the metal, the plateau is speaking to me in its loud, brutal code.

*THUD-CLACK.* That is Sap-Stamp Thirty-Nine, two leagues north. Its stroke is falling a fraction of a second late; the resin in its well is thickening, turning cold and slushy.
*THUD-CLACK.* That is Forty-Two, running clean and hot.

And between the thunder of the pistons, carried along the dense, oil-dark fibers of the floor, comes the subtle, high-pitched *skreee-skreee-skreee* of the grain shearing. 

I look out my window. A quarter-mile away, along the edge of a secondary ring, an old copper telegraph relay house—built on stilts of riveted iron—is beginning to lean. The wood beneath its eastern leg is "pinching." The growth ring is drawing tight, folding space like an accordion. To a man standing on the viaduct, the building looks like it is being stretched out like soft wax.

I lift my tapping-rod, turn it broadside, and strike the floor three times in sharp, heavy successions: *CLANG-CLANG-CLANG*. Then, a rapid series of light taps against the whorl.

*“District Seven. Ring Eight-B. Shearing six inches per hour. Clear the hoppers. Abandon the relay.”*

Three leagues away, a maintenance Tender sitting on an iron hopper will feel the vibration rise through the soles of his boots, up his draft-horse legs, and into his jaw. He will read the metal pings in his teeth, spit a mouthful of black tallow into the wind, and pull the emergency brake lever.

***

As I work, something moves across the lower ledge of the Knot, down where the light turns the color of old whiskey.

It is a **Lignin-Skimmer**.

It moves without a sound, because it travels on a Dead Ring—a thin, black line of petrified tissue where the cell walls long ago collapsed into pure, glass-hard carbon. To the Skimmer, space here is not a winding road or a heavy climb; it is a slick slide through a vacuum. 

It is impossibly tall, seven feet at least, its torso as narrow and flat as an oak shingle. Its skin is the color of wet timber, etched with fine, concentric silver circles that ripple when its muscles move. It wears no smock, no iron, no goggles. Its eyes are broad, dark, and lidless, unbothered by the glare of the high-altitude sun. On its feet are soft, woven pads of black moss that make no more sound than a falling leaf.

It does not look at my gall-house, but as it glides past—covering a hundred yards in three silent, impossibly elongated strides—it drops a small object onto the smooth walnut rise. 

A carved wooden polyhedron, eight-sided, cut from live cedar that smells sharply of resin and sweet earth. It rolls to a stop at the edge of my ledge. 

I know what it means without picking it up. It is a warning. The Dead Ring it is riding is about to "snap"—the spatial distortion is going to expand, swallowing twenty growth rings into a single, razor-thin crease. Anyone caught walking cross-grain near the Seven-Turn within the hour will find themselves walking for three days to cover ten paces.

I touch my bronze rod to the wood and hammer out a long, low vibration to the Engine-Crew at the viaduct base: *Lock the gates. The wood is folding.*

***

Then, the horn blows.

It is a deep, steam-driven scream from the central power house, miles above us on the high crest. It echoes cold and thin through the high air, signaling the midnight shift. 

One by one, the Sap-Stamps begin to slow. 

*THUD... CLACK.*
*THUD...... CLACK.*
*THUD.......... CLACK.*

Then, silence.

The sudden cessation of the pounding is so violent it feels like a physical drop. My jaw stops ringing. The lead plugs in my gums feel heavy, cold, and inert. The air inside the gall-house feels abruptly thick, stripped of its electric hum. Outside, the Rust-Wings shift on their overhead cables, their iron beaks clicking once, twice, in the unaccustomed quiet, before they tuck their heads beneath their rust-colored wings.

This is the Maintenance Hour. The only three hours of the day when the giant pistons sit still in their housings, cooling in the thin, icy air.

I stand up slowly, my low, wide frame creaking like dry leather. I unbuckle my brass-bound boots and kick them off. I lay my heavy oiled smock aside. 

I lie down flat on my stomach on the naked, uncarved heartwood floor of my gall-house. 

I do not use the tapping-rod now. I press my bare cheek, my neck, and the side of my metal-stuffed jaw directly against the cold, dark walnut. The wood is ice-cold, smelling faintly of ancient turpentine and frozen fog.

I wait. 

For five minutes, there is only the sound of my own shallow breathing and the distant, dry *tink-tink-tink* of steam pipes cooling along the viaduct.

Then, it comes.

Far below my belly—five thousand feet down through solid, petrified tissue, past the depth where any copper pipe or iron drill has ever reached—the plateau breathes.

It is a groan so deep, so unfathomably vast, that it does not arrive as sound. It arrives as a slow, massive tilt in the marrow of my bones. 

*NNNNNNRRRRRRRRRGGGHHH.*

It takes ten minutes for the strain to move from the east to the west. It is the sound of an ancient, continental stump—a tree that was five leagues tall before the oceans turned to stone—trying, in its blind, subterranean sleep, to turn its roots toward a sun that died a million years ago.

My lead teeth ring with a tiny, sweet, clear pitch—a single, resonant *E-flat* that vibrates straight through my skull.

I close my amber-goggled eyes in the cold dark. I do not feel small. I do not feel human. I feel like a iron staple hammered into the flank of a sleeping titan, holding two rings together while the world slowly, endlessly turns.
