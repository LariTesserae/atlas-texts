I am not up in the lattice, where the paraffin-scented guy-wires swing in the blue mist, nor am I down in the roaring violet foam of the subterranean spillways. 

I am wedged into the gap. 

My place is the five-inch expansion seam where the three-inch cast-iron floor plates meet the raw, un-weathered concrete face of the second terrace. It is a world of horizontal shear and vertical weight. Above me, three hundred feet in the dark, an I-beam drips its fine, mineral-spirit rain; below me, the buried steam-conduits bleed a damp, tepid warmth that smells of sulfur and old tallow.

If you were to look down into the seam between the heavy plates, you would not see a face or limbs. You would see a long, dark, segmented mass—twenty feet of dense, vulcanized hide impregnated with micro-crystalline graphite and lead flakes, wedged tight into the metal channel like a living packing strip.

I am a **Seam-Wedge**.

I do not walk, and I do not float. I creep by thermal differential. When the deep machinery cycles and the cold drafts sweep across the basin deck, the cast-iron plates contract, opening the seam by a fraction of an inch. In that moment of slack, my body expands—a slow, hydraulic swelling of dark oil and dense cartilage—taking up the space. When the temperature rises and the miles of iron groan, expanding with a sound like tearing sheet metal, the plates clamp down on me with thousands of tons of lateral pressure.

I do not crush. I absorb. 

The pressure presses through my outer hide of cold, slick graphite into my interior core: a dense lattice of mineralized tendon and pressurized marrow. I act as the basin’s living shock-absorber and acoustic relay. Without us in the seams, the floor plates would buckle against one another under thermal stress, snapping their purple-stained welds, tearing the light-masts from their footings, and shorting the buried bus-bars.

I know the world entirely through strain, vibration, and viscosity.

I feel the *clack-THUMP* of the high-pressure switch gear five miles to the north long before the shockwave tears through the cold air. It arrives through the iron deck as a sudden, sharp spike of kinetic energy. The force slams into my flank; my internal fluids churn, driving a thick, black lubricant through pores in my skin to coat the raw steel edges of the seam. A dozen Slag-Licks, attracted by the pulse and the fresh grease, crowd the lip above me, their pale underside-disks rasping the micro-burrs off the iron while they ride the shudder I pass up to them.

Occasionally, the sound changes. 

A forty-foot brass valve on the terrace above drifts off-key. Its friction-hum sharpens from a low, smooth purr into an uneven, grinding rattle that sends jagged micro-vibrations down through the concrete form-lines. The noise is painful to the logic of the basin; it creates standing waves in the puddles and threatens to shear the dark moss from the retaining walls.

I register the dissonance as an itch along my dorsal ridge. I contract my forward segments, pulling my length tight against the concrete, and send a low, resonant thrum back up through the structural deck—a heavy, mechanical thrum at 59.8 hertz.

Minutes pass. Then hours.

Above me, a Long-Gaiter arrives. I do not see its eighteen-foot tripod frame, but I feel the pinpoint weight of its bucket-sized horn feet as they set down, one by one, on the diamond tread near my seam. It pauses. Its cartilage dish-face tilts toward the valve, then down toward the vibration rising from my back.

It understands the feedback. Very slowly, the Gaiter leans its oil-heavy weight against the giant brass rim, shifting the wheel a fraction of a degree. 

The grinding rattle softens. The frequency smooths out, slipping back into harmony with the 60-hertz thrum of the bus-bars. The standing waves in the ink-black puddles smooth into quiet concentric rings. The Gaiter lifts one long, double-hinged leg and moves on, its footfalls receding into the cold mist.

The itch in my ridge subsides. I settle back into the grease and the dark.

I am content here in the wet, freezing dark between the iron and the stone. I have no eyes to miss the sun, which has never shone on this floor. I have no lungs to complain of the ozone, the wet copper, or the aerosol mineral spirits that drift down from the high beams. My blood is heavy, cold, and slow; my nerves are made of graphite and calcified wire; my purpose is simple and infinite.

The basin is expanding now. A fresh wave of icy rain is falling from the ceiling-weather above, chilling the deck. The iron plates are beginning to pull apart, a hundredth of an inch at a time.

I feel the slack coming. I yield to the cold, soak up the black grease floating on the condensation, and swell slowly forward to fill the gap.
