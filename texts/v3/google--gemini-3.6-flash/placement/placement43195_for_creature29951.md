I am wedged where the main steam header enters the floor of the second generator hall.

The pipe is eight feet across, forged iron, six inches thick. It has long since ceased carrying steam under pressure; now it carries water—warm, iron-rich, sulfurous water that bubbles out of deep boreholes miles below and rises through the drowned foundations.

My body fills the lower curve of the valve casing. 

My belly rests on a bed of crushed gasket-mussel shells and fine, orange-grey silt that has settled out of the flow over forty years. My back—mottled, rough as wet brick, heavy with encrusted deposits of zinc and iron oxide—presses up against the top arch of the casing. When the water level surges slightly, pushed by the distant, rhythmic stroke of the geothermal pump in the fourth sub-basement—*clack-hiss... clack-hiss*—the warm brine rises three inches, submerging my nostrils and floating the crimson silk of my gills out into the dark current.

I do not move. There is no need to move. 

There is no light down here, but light is a thin, dry thing that belongs to the oil-gulls on the high trusses. Here, thirty feet beneath the bronze twilight of the roof collapse, the world is known through the iron and the water.

Through the iron, I read the basin:
I feel the feet of the mud-crabs three hundred yards away, their heavy, metal-laminated claws clicking in sequence across the flooded granite yard as they build their low dykes of slag. The vibration travels up the structural columns, down the riveted girders, into the floor plates, and through the valve housing directly into my jawbone.

Through the water, I taste the basin:
A drift of sharp ammonia from the swallow colonies in the upper flue-stacks.
The oily, mineral tang of a drowned gear-case leaking its century-old tallow into the canal.
The sweet, heavy taste of wet rust carried down from the weeping viaducts by the endless *drip-drip-drip* of the ceiling.
And the distinct, fluttering pressure-wave of a four-foot slag-carp, its bronze scales scraping against a submerged flywheel as it turns into the gallery.

The carp comes drifting down the header. It is old, its snout calloused, its blind, milky eyes useless in the blackness. It feeds on the rubbery mats of chemoautotrophic bacteria that coat the iron walls like wet leather. It moves with a slow, heavy tail-stroke, pushing a wedge of warm water before it.

My lateral lines register the swell. 
I do not tense. Tensing is for things with thin blood. My heart beats once every fourteen seconds. My blood is dense, dark, laden with heavy metals, slow as cold grease.

The carp’s barbels brush my lower lip. The lip is insensitive, scaled like wet hide. The fish drags its chin across my snout, feeling for the bacterial slime it mistakes for another rusted pipe elbow.

Then my throat expands. 

It is not a strike; it is a vacuum. The hyoid arch drops, the broad jaw unseals, and twelve gallons of warm, iron-stained brine rush into my mouth, carrying the carp with it. The short, blunt teeth set in three rows along my palate shut with the wet, hydraulic thud of a heavy sluice gate.

The carp twitches twice against my throat, its tail beating uselessly against the inside of my cheeks, and then it goes still, sliding down into the dark cavern of my belly where the acid is strong enough to pit boiler plate.

I settle back into the silt.

Digestion will take six weeks. In that time, the bronze scales will yield their zinc and copper; the lime in the fish’s spine will strengthen my skull; the tallow in its gut will thicken the sulfurous mucus that coats my skin, keeping the acidic runoff from burning through to my muscle.

Above me, through twenty feet of flooded masonry and three layers of corroded floor plates, the bronze sky is turning to bruised charcoal. 

I know this not because I see the dark, but because the kiln-swallows are returning to the flues. Ten thousand coal-black bodies settling into their terracotta nests. Their weight, multiplied by thousands, settles subtly onto the brickwork. The iron girders shift a fraction of a millimeter in their sockets. A flake of velvety rust, four inches wide and heavy with moisture, detaches from the ceiling of the generator hall and falls into the water with a soft, wet *plop*.

Out on the open flats, the dragonflies are dropping low, their smoky wings skimming the two-inch surface film over the sunken rail lines. The masonry-beetles are scraping the mortar joints above the crane rails, their mandibles sounding like a hundred small blades sharpening on wet slate.

A section of wall—a ton of wet, liver-colored brick, its lime binding long since eaten away by the larvae—finally surrenders its grip on the sky. It sloughs off the facade of the boiler house and drops into the canal.

*THUD-SPLASH.*

A heavy, muffled shockwave travels through the silt, pushing a three-inch wave down the main steam header. It washes over my back, lifting my crimson gill-plumes, floating a raft of dead midges and mussel-byssus across my snout.

I swallow once. The carp shifts in my gut.

I am the valve. I am the pipe. I am the red mud that gathers in the corners where the steel turns back into stone.

There was a time—so the vibration in the iron suggests, far down in the resonance of the deepest pilings—when things moved fast here. When the steam was hot enough to scream, and the air was dry enough to burn, and the water was forced through these galleries at such speed that no mussel could take hold upon the walls.

That time was loud. It was thin. It burned out like a dry coal fire.

What remains is the long, wet breathing. 
The geothermal steam pushing the water up from the deep earth. 
The water weeping through the masonry. 
The rust sloughing into the silt. 
The silt turning into crab-shell, carp-scale, salamander-jaw.

Down in the fourth sub-basement, the automated pump strokes again.
*Clack... hiss.*

The water rises an eighth of an inch.
My gills fan out—red threads floating in the dark, gathering the iron from the warm, stagnant stream, weaving it into bone.
