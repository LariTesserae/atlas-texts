I am not above the canopy with the gliders, where the air thins and cools against the open sky. Nor am I down in the wide, roaring troughs where the grubs churn the floodwaters. 

If you were to look for me, you would have to look beneath the moss—down where the spring-coiled roots of the iron-reeds wrap themselves tightly around the glowing lips of the thermal vents. You would find me wedged in the tolerances. 

I am a creature of the seams: a long, ribbon-flat body of dense, vulcanized cartilage and heat-hardened lipid-flesh, pressed flush between the expanding basalt and the living wood. I have no eyes, no carapace, and no lungs. I do not breathe the air; I drink the high-pressure oil-sap that bleeds from the ferns, and I taste the mountain through the soles of my lateral line.

To be here is to exist in a state of exquisite, continuous tension. 

I am the **Packing-Sleeve**, the living gasket. 

My skin is dark, iridescent, and cool to the touch despite the boiling brine that rushes mere millimeters away, because my blood is thick with suspended copper particles that draw heat rapidly away from my core and shed it into the iron-moss. Where the mountain expands, I yield. Where the basalt cracks under the weight of a passing Bellows-Beast, I slide—heavy and slow, like warm pitch—to fill the gap before the steam can blow out the root-system of the reeds.

I feel the basin not as a landscape, but as a vast, low-frequency symphony of pressure and relief:

*A mile to the north, a natural siphon fills.* I feel the heavy, rising tension in the bedrock—a tight, metallic ache that vibrates up through my ventral skin. 

*The siphon breaks.* A violent shudder vibrates through the chute. The water roars. The Turbine-Grub below me opens its iris-maw, and the vibration changes key, shifting from a sharp crack to the deep, wooden *thum-thum-thum* of its gut-chambers turning in the tide. I expand my dorsal ridges, wedging myself tighter into the stone seam so the thermal shock doesn't shear the moss from the rock.

*A reed-node above me reaches capacity.* I feel the hydrostatic pressure swell inside the hollow stem I hug. I contract my rings, bracing the root-joint. 

*Chong.* 

The vent fires. The kinetic recoil punches down through the stem, hitting my back like a blacksmith’s hammer. It does not hurt; it is a massage. The shockwave flattens me against the smooth basalt, driving my copper-laced skin deeper into the micro-fissures, squeezing out old, spent oils and drawing in a fresh, hot wash of hydrocarbon sap from the surrounding fronds.

The Chaffers know I am here. As they work along the reed-bases, scraping away the white lime crusts with their heavy brass mandibles, their jointed legs step over my exposed edges with meticulous care. They do not bite me; they press the flat of their palps against my flanks to harvest the grease I exude—the dense, heat-proof film that keeps their own leg-joints from seizing in the sulfurous steam. In return, their scraping keeps my breathing-slits free of mineral scale. It is a quiet, unspoken trade made in the wet dark.

Above me, invisible in the white vapor, a Spark-Fly swarm discharges. I feel it as a faint, pleasant tingling along my dorsal ridge—a tiny, static prickle that causes my copper-rich blood to surge, carrying fresh nutrients down to my tail-anchor.

I do not move often. A journey of three feet is a season’s work, a slow, hydraulic creep from an aging, calcified vent to a fresh, roaring fissure higher up the slope. I have no need for speed, nor for territorial ambition. The engine is vast, and its seams are endless.

When the sun drops behind the pale-basalt cliffs and the sky turns the color of cooling iron, the air above may quiet, but down here in the dark flumes, nothing stops. The basalt continues to glow red in the deep cracks. The sap continues to weep. The mountain breathes its heavy, boiling breath into my belly, and I hold the joints together—elastic, wet, and perfectly fitted to the rock—listening to the steady, ten-thousand-year downbeat of the earth.
