First, there is no vision. Vision is a thin, dry sense, meant for light that travels far through empty space; here, space is filled to capacity with wet moss, dark oil, cold iron, and rising steam. 

Instead, there is only the gradient.

Above me: six thousand meters of cold mountain air, descending as a silver, greasy mist that filters through three feet of crimson sphagnum. It arrives at my upper rim as a slow, freezing trickle, laden with paraffin, crushed leaves, and dissolved iron rust.

Below me: the deep basalt root, fractured and heavy, where cold water hits white-hot ironstone miles down and turns to pressurized, sulfurous heat. It comes surging up through a tube of sintered limonite—a natural pipe three feet wide, lined with micro-crystalline quartz and ancient biological slag.

I am where these two infinities meet. 

I find myself locked to the lip of a geothermal chimney, midway down a long, dark-green terrace that slants toward the western fog. I am not a wanderer; I have no feet for the floating peat, no wings for the high mist. I am a **Vent-Anchor**, one of eight in a calcified ring, fused side-by-side with my kin until our individual shells form a single, bronze-and-black collar around the throat of the vent.

My outer casing is pure mineral geology—layers of iron oxide, calcium carbonate, and heavy tallow secreted over four hundred years of steady growth. My outer skin is cold, rough as iron slag, and coated in a slick sheen of sulfur-loving biofilm. But inside this thick armor, my body is dense, dark, and fibrous—a ring of heavy, petroleum-slick muscle that expands and contracts with the patience of an engine running in a flooded cellar.

My world is measured in three-second cycles.

*One.*
The pressure builds in the limonite pipe beneath my mantle. The thermal water boils against my closed lower valve. I feel the steam expanding—a hot, white thrust that rattles the iron shaft and vibrates through the deep basalt. The heat floods through my core, warming the cold rain that has collected in my upper lip.

*Two.*
I hold. The muscle inside my bronze shell swells, locking the passage shut. Around my base, in the warm, quiet runoff pool that spills over my outer flange, a hundred Ball-Bearings—tiny, heavy spheres of dark limonite—grind softly against my calcified skirts, scraping the sulfur-algae from my hide. Through the bedrock beneath my anchors, I feel the low, seismic *thud* of a Low-Treader herd stepping through the fern-mats three miles north. The vibration hits my iron casing like a faint chime.

*Three.*
My mantle relaxes. The valve snaps open with a wet, hydraulic give.

*Thrum-pah.*

The burst of warm, sulfur-rich steam shoots upward through my center, clearing my throat, tearing through the overhang of crimson moss, and blooming into the cold mountain air above. A shower of warm, oily condensation falls back over my rim, feeding the dark liverworts that seal my margins against the peat.

Then the valve settles shut again. The cold rain from the sky washes over my upper lip, cooling the scalded skin. *One.*

To be here is to know complete, heavy permanence. I do not experience hunger as a sharp, frantic thing; I draw iron flakes and sulfur directly from the steam as it passes through my gullet, stripping the minerals to build my shell thicker, heavier, more tightly wedged against the stone. 

Occasionally, a Conduit-Creeper glides past my subterranean roots, its slick, ribbon-like body brushing against my lower calcified flanges as it hunts through the dark water pipe. I feel its subtle electric pulse—a faint, tickling charge against my iron armor—before it vanishes down the slick raceway toward the deeper pools.

Above my head, out in the broad, soft light, the skimmers float on the warm column of my breath, their wide leather wings catching the steam I release every three seconds. They do not know my name, nor I theirs; but my breath keeps them aloft, and their falling dross feeds the black liverworts that keep the peat from crumbling into my shaft.

I am not an inhabitant on the Fen-Shelf; I am a rivet holding the floor together. I am the bronze lip where the mountain breathes.

The rain continues its silver, sweating fall. The peat presses cold against my flanks. The deep rock burns below. And inside my iron shell, the heavy muscle flexes, waits, and lets go.

*Thrum-pah.*
