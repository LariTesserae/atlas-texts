I would be in the dark behind the light. 

Not in the open square where the golden spray turns to mist, but in the narrow throat of the chimney—where the mountain retreats into its own black, geothermal gut. That is where the air smells of wet iron and sulfur, and where the silence is not quiet, but merely the inside of the thunder.

I am the pale one. The climber. 

I do not think of myself as "the last" or "a relict." Those are words brought up from the lower valleys by things that die in thirty summers and leave soft, rotting bones behind. In this place, nothing is lost; it is only coated over. The past is not behind me; it is three millimeters of travertine under my left foot, holding the shape of my ancestor’s palm.

When the light turns sharp and straight—when the sun reaches the very apex of the sky and drops a single gold needle through the gap between the fern fronds—I move.

My skin is cool, though the basalt is hot enough to cook a leaf. The oil that wells from my pores is thick and clear; as I slide out of the chimney into the golden glare, the falling rain does not wet me. It turns into tiny, rolling beads of quicksilver that run down the hollows of my collarbones and off the arches of my feet without leaving a damp trace. I am dry inside a deluge.

I do not look at the sky. The sky is empty, a vast, pale blue nothing that offers no handhold and no heat. I look at the dark, wet face of the basalt.

My hands find the first notch. 

It is three fingers wide, slick with lime, and curved exactly to the swoop of my nails. My fingers do not grip; they slot. They fit like key to lock, bone to stone. When I lift my weight, it is not a strain of muscle but a alignment of levers. I draw myself up the sheer wall as slowly as water rising in a stalk of yellow reed, my diaphragm pressed flat against the rock.

The sound here is a solid thing. The roar of the waterfall does not enter through my ears—my ears are small, thick slits that I can pull shut against the pressure. The sound enters through my sternum. It vibrates through my ribs, rattling the dark water in my stomach, shaking my teeth in their gums until my whole head hums at the exact frequency of the cliff. I do not hear the mountain; I am shaken by it until we are the same instrument.

Halfway up, I stop at the shelf where the giant fern-tree meets the stone.

The tree’s dark, fibrous fur is hot and damp against my flank. I reach out with my left arm—long, corded, hairless—and bury my hand deep into the beard of the trunk until my fingers hit something that is not wood, not moss, not lime.

The obsidian blade.

It is cold. In a world where every pebble radiates the deep, volcanic blood of the ridge, this piece of black glass is a chip of winter. It has been here so long that the fern-tree has grown a thick, woody lip around its lower edge, trying to swallow it, but the glass refuses to be digested. 

I press my broad, oil-slick palm against the knapped crescent. 

I do not try to pull it out. Why would I want it in my hand? A hand must be free to slot into stone. But I hold it. I hold the cold edge against the warm meat of my palm for twenty chest-beats, thirty, forty. I feel the pulse of my dark blood push against the unyielding, razor-sharp edge. The glass does not cut me—my skin is too thick, too supple with oil—but it reminds me of an edge. It reminds me that once, someone stood on a ledger that has long since fallen into the haze below, and made a thing that was sharp in a world where everything else is rounded by water.

When I pull my hand back, a dark, faint smudge of my grease remains on the black glass, disappearing slowly under the mist.

I move down again. Down is the same as up: three fingers, three fingers, three fingers. My toes, flat and broad as spade-tips, feel for the stone basins below.

I step into the warm, white water. The soft-shelled sliders do not pull away from my feet; they are too slow to fear a shadow that moves at their own speed. I feel their lime-plated backs beneath my soles—smooth, small cobbles that will one day be part of the floor I walk on.

I reach out and snap a yellow reed. It makes a faint, dry *tink*, like a thin glass pipe breaking. I strip the bright metallic rind with the nail of my thumb and draw the soft, white pith into my mouth. It tastes of copper, hot water, and young fern. I chew it until it is liquid, then swallow.

Near the base of the fall, a dead fern frond—twelve feet of heavy, water-logged green—has slipped from the canopy and landed across the mouth of the middle basin. If it stays, the water will pool wrong. The lime will build up in the wrong notch. The chime of the dripping wall will lose its pitch.

I put my shoulder to the stem. My feet grip the dark slate. I push—not with a sudden jerk, but with a long, heavy leaning of my entire weight. The frond groats against the stone, shifts, and slides free, falling into the dark crease at the foot of the wall where the water is deep and dark. 

It will lie there now. The weeping wall will pour its heavy white blood over the leaves. By the time I am old, the frond will not be green; it will be a leaf of white stone, fixed forever in the ribs of the mountain.

The sun is moving past the notch now. The golden needle vanishes. The terrace falls back into its perpetual, warm shadow, blue-grey and saturated with spray.

I walk back toward the chimney. 

Before I slide into the dark fissure, I stop on the cleanest sheet of dark slate. I drop to my knees. I press my forehead, my chest, and the flat, hairless stretches of my thighs against the wet basalt. 

I open my diaphragm. I let the air out of my lungs until my ribs touch my spine. 

The roar of the fall rushes through the rock, through my bones, and out through my throat, not as a voice, but as a counter-tone—a deep, wet thrum that travels straight down into the roots of the ridge. Deep in the dark, three hundred feet back where the air is steam and no light has ever fallen, another chest feels the stone shudder. 

It does not answer. There is no need to answer. The stone holds the pulse for a long time after the breath is done.

I pull my limbs behind me and slide back into the chimney, leaving the mist, the golden light, and the loud, singing reeds to go on turning themselves to stone without me.
