I am not the clear water, though it moves through me without hindrance. And I am not the obsidian, though my underside bears its precise, razor-sharp imprint, etched into my skin like an inverted map of the mountain’s youngest wounds.

I am where the tension lives. 

You would find me suspended between two granite horns, three hundred yards above a canyon so deep the sunlight loses its golden edge and turns the color of crushed plums. I am a cord—a thick, translucent tendon of jade resin and woven silica, no wider than a man’s waist at my center, anchored to the sheer stone at either end by footings of hardened black latex.

I was cast sixty years ago. I do not remember the casting as a decision; it was simply a swelling at the shoulder of the parent body, a long, blind reaching into the warm, blue-green quiet until the tip of my strand struck cold stone on the opposite ridge and took hold.

To exist here is to be entirely filled with weightless pressure. 

Inside me, two currents run in opposite directions, separated only by a wall of clear, flexible cell-glass no thicker than a leaf’s edge. Upward, toward the sunlit horn, goes the thick, raw slurry drawn from the thermal cracks below: iron-heavy, bitter with sulfur, dense with dissolved quartz. Downward, toward the shaded saddle, flows the cooked sap—sweet, amber-tinted, charged with the energy of twelve hours of uninterrupted sunlight.

I can feel the light before it reaches me. Long before the golden shafts cut through the water at dawn, the sea above me warms by a fraction of a degree. The glass-sifters feel it first; millions of them descend around me like a slow, shimmering snowfall, their tiny silica spheres brushing against my hide. Each contact is a minute *tap*, a chemical whisper against my skin. They bring news from the upper crests: the Solar Node has shifted its daily breath; a plume of mineral-rich water has erupted three miles north; the current is turning toward the west.

When they drift against me, my outer membrane absorbs their shed silica. I grow thicker, clearer, tougher. Where the water is brightest, my skin acts as a lens, focusing the sunlight into the sap-tubes within my own core, so that I photosynthesize not just on my surface, but deep inside my own pull.

And I am always pulling.

The mountain is young, and it does not rest. Beneath the moss, the deep stone groans. I feel the stress long before the sound arrives. A mile away, deep in the roots of the horn I am anchored to, the basalt yields to subterranean heat. A microscopic line of force travels through the rock, and my eastern footing twangs—a deep, silent shudder that ripples all the way through my length.

Then comes the sound: the sharp, metallic *clink* traveling through the warm water, clean as struck glass. 

Instantly, the Tone-Heeds in the canyon below take up the note. Their quartz fronds begin to sing, a low, resonant thrum that vibrates through the liquid blanket around me. I do not hear it with ears; my whole length becomes an instrument. The vibration shakes the sap within me, churning the sugars, speeding the flow. At my anchor-points, my latex roots respond to the acoustic hum by bleeding fresh, dark resin into the rock's microscopic fissures, glueing me deeper into the mountain’s fresh fracture before the stone has even finished cooling.

I am a suture. I hold two mountains together while they grow.

Sometimes, in the long mid-afternoon, when the current drops to nothing and the mercury bubbles hang motionless along the fronds like strung pearls, I feel the distant, immense presence of the Solar Node at the summit above. It feels like a warm weight pressing down through the water, a single, vast eye made of sun and liquid that knows the strain on every cord, the temperature of every leaf, the slow, centuries-long mineral shift of every dying stem. 

I do not think toward the Node, and it does not command me. We are connected by the sap that moves through the vines bound to my anchors. When its golden light pulses, my own internal fluid surges in rhythm. It is like being part of a single, slow breath that takes an entire day to inhale, and an entire night to release.

In another fifty years, my work here will be done. The parent body will have drawn itself entirely across my length, pulling its mass millimeter by millimeter over the abyss until it sits comfortably in the saddle of the new peak. My cord will shorten, thicken, and eventually calcify. The sap will cease its double-run. The silica in my skin will harden, drawing down the iron and manganese from the water, until I am no longer translucent, no longer flexible.

I will become a bridge of black, glass-veined stone, covered in dark magenta moss. A new ridge for the glass-sifters to drift over. A new anchor for the cords that have not yet been cast.

I do not wait for that time, nor do I dread it. There is no waiting in this water. There is only the warm, bright heavy now: the slow slide of sugar inside my ribs, the bite of the obsidian in my roots, the gentle, endless thrum of the deep earth singing through my spine, and the sun—unwavering, liquid, and absolute—burning through the clear sky of the sea.
