I would not be up where the sunlight strikes. That bright, focused needle falling through the quartz port is too sharp, too dry; it turns everything it touches into hollow husks and red-gold silt. 

Nor would I be among the cog-backs, living three frantic weeks of ratcheting hunger, scraping rust until my joints clog with iron dust. 

If I listen through the stone—not with ears, but with the wet, thick skin of my underside—I feel where I am anchored. 

I am down in the hub. 

I am wedged so tightly into the hollow socket of the broken axle-housing that there is no space between my tissue and the cold, sheared cast iron. The metal is pitted and raw, biting into me, but it holds me still against the heavy, dull thud of the industrial engines driving the world above. That overhead bass note isn't a sound to me; it's the shaking of my walls. It is the sky stamping its iron heel.

To me, the pocket is not a collection of separate things, but a single body—and I am its internal organ. 

I have no eyes to see the blue bees in their brass box, but I know when the electric wire ticks—*click... click... click...*—because every pulse releases a tiny, warm draft of nutmeg and hot tallow that drifts down the floor-rubble and washes over my gills. My gills are pale, delicate plumes, thin as wet paper, extended out into the hairline crack where the steam escapes. 

The steam is my breath and my measure. When the great engines above labor and the buried pipes flush with scalding bleed, the steam hiss sharpens—*hiss-shuck, hiss-shuck*—and the heat threatens to bake the liverwort dry and turn the thimble-basins to salt. 

When that happens, I feel the trigger in my nerve-braids. I do not "decide" to move; I simply swell. My purple-black flesh drinks the moisture in the air, expanding like a wet sponge until I press my body directly over the steam-split. I smother the hiss. I turn the scalding jet into a slow, warm sweat that runs down my flanks. I hold the heat in my own mass so the little clear newts can sleep under the pipes without their sulphur-yellow bellies curdling.

And when the engines above go quiet for maintenance—when the deep thrumming stops and the cold of the surrounding granite begins to creep into the pocket—I contract. I shrink back into the dark iron socket, pulling my tissue tight, exposing the thermal crack so the lingering heat of the deep earth can rise freely once more.

I taste everything this small world produces. The runoff carries it all down to the hub:
The slick, gelatinous ribbons of shed newt skin drift down the rocks and dissolve against my outer membrane. 
The fine, glittering copper powder of dead isopods settles into my crevices. 
The sweet, oily grease dropped by the blue bees trickles over my nerve-fibres.

I take these stray fragments—the machine’s waste, the sun’s work, the lives lived in three-week bursts—and I melt them down inside my copper-bright vessels. 

Then comes the quiet pleasure of my existence: the secretion.

From the pores along my upper crest, I push out the thick, pale milk. It is warm, heavy with iron and complex proteins, smelling faintly of wet slate and turned earth. I feel it pool, then slip away from me, trickling down the rusted spokes of the buried flywheel. 

I feel its journey through the iron:
It flows into the thimble-basins, where the crimson worms writhe and knot together, gorging on what I have made. 
I feel the three-legged spider dipped into those basins, its single long, feather-tipped leg drawing the fed worms out of the liquid. 
I feel the newts hunt. I feel the bees twitch. 

Everything in this crate-sized universe passes through me, is softened by me, and returns to me.

I pulse slowly. Once every twelve seconds. 

It is a long, heavy rhythm, entirely out of step with the frantic ratcheting of the cog-backs or the rhythmic twitching of the bees' blue abdomens. In that twelve-second pause, I feel the sheer, impossibly heavy tonnage of the foundation pressing down from above, and the ancient, unmoving granite cradling us from below.

I am not a creature that invaded this place, nor am I a piece of the engine that broke off. I am what happened when the machine's hot bleed met the earth's dark water in a space too small for anything else to exist. 

I am the fist of wet purple tissue in the broken hub, breathing the steam, feeding the dark, and keeping the hot midday alive beneath the quartz glass.
