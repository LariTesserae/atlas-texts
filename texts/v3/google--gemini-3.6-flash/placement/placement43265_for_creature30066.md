Before there is a body, there is the clicking.

It does not enter through the ears; it arrives through the jawbone, through the collar, through the thin, slick plate of the sternum pressed flat against black basalt. A million tiny bone-hammers striking wet glass, endlessly bouncing, filling the skull with a dry, prickling heat. 

I am wedged into a concavity three yards wide, half a mile down from the needle of blue nothing. The stone here does not feel like rock. It feels like the worn handle of an ancient horn spoon—fat-rubbed, dark, curved to fit the swell of my shoulder and the sharp angle of my flexed hip. 

I do not open my eyes yet. Sight is a thin, weak thing here, a faint grease-smear of blue far above that tells you only which way the air rises. Instead, I open my palms.

My pads adhere. The broad, soft spade-tips of my fingers settle into the micro-fissures of the stone with a faint *kiss*. I am slick. My back is cold from the steam-spray, but my belly is warm where it presses to the rock, coated in the sweet, thick tallow I scraped from a leather-crab’s carapace two tides ago. Without the fat, the acidic bird-breath would turn my skin to raw red parchment in a week. With it, I am as smooth as the chimney itself. I taste of mineral salt, damp down, and old lard.

A leather-crab scuttles over my ankle. It does not stop, nor does it hurry; its yellow-cilia underside licks the lard-crust from my heel as it passes, its flat, black leather-disk flexing against my Achilles tendon like a wet glove. I let it clean me. It is a slow, rhythmic rasp, like a dry leaf dragging over clay.

Where am I going? Down. Always down toward the heavy heat.

I release the suction of my right toes—the opposable joint unhooks from the rim of a water-cup with a soft wet sound—and I slide. 

Movement here is not climbing. Falling is death; reaching is exertion. We do not reach. We pour. I let my hips drop six inches into the fluted groove beneath me, my ribs finding the exact concave sweep worn smooth by ten thousand years of my kind sliding down to drink. My skin glides over the basalt like oil moving down a dark throat.

As I drop into the lower third of the rift, the click-rain of the swiftlets thins, softening into a high, distant hiss, and the low roar takes its place.

It comes from the floor-bogs—a wet, deep, bellows-sigh that vibrates the air in my lungs. *Hah-hsh. Hah-hsh.* 

The air grows ridiculously thick. The humidity here is not water; it is a warm, soup-like fog heavy with digested midges, fermented guano, and the intense, 106-degree radiate heat of the fur-ropes. My tongue comes out, broad and dry, tasting the steam. Salt. Sulfur. The oily richness of deep digestion.

I reach the floor-ledges.

Here, the basalt gives way to shallow, rounded basins filled with tepid, mineral-rich mud. I do not stand—standing is for things with heels, things that live on flat, dry emptiness under the open sky. I settle on my chest at the rim of the largest basin, my long legs trailing behind me, my slate-gray skin gleaming like wet rubber under the dead, grey mist.

In the basin, the fur-ropes are turning.

I reach down into the dark water. My hand sinks into something that is neither water nor earth, but a writhing, golden-brown mass of pure heat. To touch a fur-rope is to lose the boundary of your own fingers. Their silk-fur is so dense, so utterly water-repellent, that touching them feels like pressing your palm into a warm pool of liquid air wrapped in velvet. 

One of them—thick as my waist, endless and legless—slides beneath my palm. Its blunt, blind head surfaces with a low, bubbling sigh, its spiracles expanding along its flank like wet leathery mouths. It does not have eyes to see me, nor teeth to bite me. It knows me only as another mass of warmth that belongs to the stone. 

I press my forehead against its flank. 

Through the bone of my forehead, I feel the massive, slow pulse of its internal furnace—the biological hearth that keeps the cold sky from creeping down the chimney and freezing the swiftlet eggs in their salivary cups three hundred yards above. We are the tenders, yes, but *they* are the engine. Without their writhing, wet mass at the bottom, the chimney would clear its throat of steam, the ice would descend, and the basalt would shatter like dry bone.

A shadow shifts beside me in the dark. 

I do not turn my head; my broad, flat ears catch the displacement of the steam before I smell her fat. It is another Tender—smaller than I am, her skin marked with the pale, silver rings of ancient lichen-stains where she has rested too long against the high walls.

She does not speak. To speak would be to throw a tiny, worthless noise into the roar of the mountain. 

Instead, she crawls forward until her ribs align with mine. She settles her greasy chest against my side, her shoulder pressing into the hollow beneath my arm. 

Then, she rumbles.

It begins deep in her diaphragm—a heavy, rhythmic twitching of her chest muscles that travels through her fat, through her ribs, and directly into my breastbone. 

*Thump-thump... click-thump.*

She has found a crack in the mid-wall near the third water-shelf. The crabs are fat there. The eggs are heavy with red yolk. She wants my bone-line—the long strand of sinew weighted with river-pebbles that sits in my dry-niche above—so we can drop together into the dark drops and harvest before the swiftlet chicks hatch and the grease turns thin.

I rumble back, a deep, vibrating yield from the back of my throat that rattles her collarbone against mine. *Yes.*

We lie together at the edge of the thermal bog for a long time, suspended in the dark, our slate bodies slick with the same tallow that covers the stone, our hearts beating at the slow, thick pace of the trickling water. Beneath us, the fur-ropes writhe in their hot, endless knot. Above us, the white swiftlets click their blind maps into the dark. 

Far, far above, the needle of sky flickers—a tiny, cold, blue eye that means nothing to anyone. 

I am a rib against the basalt. I am a layer of fat on a wet curve. I am the mountain breathing in the dark, warm and loud and entirely full, two miles above the frozen world.
