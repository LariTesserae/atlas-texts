I am not standing in the light, because there is nowhere to stand. I am the pressure that keeps the chalk from touching the clay.

If you were to look for me, you would have to look between the third and fourth vertical racks, where a plate of buffed, pale horn—thin as an oak leaf, stiff as a dried skate-skin—meets the dished lip of a terracotta flute. I am the seam. I am the exact fraction of a millimeter where the bone cowl gives way to the masonry, where the endless ringing roar of the air-bore is converted into a dry, heatless shudder.

I do not have a name, because a name requires a second thing to distinguish it from the first, and here there is only the Mass and the Pocket. 

I am the shim.

***

To be here is to be completely full. There is no empty space inside my frame for a thought to turn around in. The cold draft—scoured of all moisture, tasting of crushed gypsum and ancient, sun-baked lime—does not enter me through a mouth; it enters through three thousand hexagonal pores along the crest of my dish-cowl. It passes through my ribs, which are not ribs for protecting a heart, but tuned reeds of hollowed horn. 

When the air hits them, I do not sing. The sound is already there, held in the throat of the building far above. My body simply takes the fifth harmonic—the high, thin bell-tone that would otherwise rattle the chalk blocks out of their sockets—and anchors it. I bend the air just enough to turn a destructive vibration into a static wall of pressure. 

I feel the building above me not as a weight, but as an infinite, frozen slope of intent. Somewhere, miles above this pocket, where the whitewashed chutes open their mouths to the glare of a sky I have no concept of, a stone settles. It takes thirty years for that settling to travel down through the miles of mortarless joints. 

When it arrives, it comes as a phantom twitch in my lateral plates. 

I do not flinch. I *accommodate*. A dry, salt-hardened filament deep in my hip-socket gives up a hundredth of a grain of flour's width. A ceramic plate slides along its dry groove with the sound of two fingernails scraped together in the dark. The wedge tightens. The chord clears. The high harmonic drops back into its perfect, deafening slot. 

That is my entire life: the taking up of slack.

***

In the shadowless white glare, I can see my own fore-edge, though "see" is a wet, soft word for what happens. My cowl catches the bounced light from the gypsum chute and reflects it down onto my chest plates. They are the color of milk that has dried on a stone floor. Where the dust has scoured them for five hundred years, they have developed a faint, pearlescent grain, like the inside of a river mussel, though no water has ever run through this world.

I can feel the grey lichen touching the edge of my left plate. It is dry as wire. It has been touching my plate since before the floor tiles wore dished in their centers. We do not compete for space. We are both merely shapes that the draft must go around.

Sometimes a single grain of quartz—larger than the fine flour that usually travels the vents—falls down the ceiling throat. It strikes my bone cowl with a sound like a tiny, dry needle dropping on a tile. 

*Clink.*

It is the only event that ever happens.

The grain rolls into the dished center of the cowl, caught in the micro-draft, until it finds one of the three thousand pores. It is too large to pass through. It sits there, wedged in the lip of the vent, altering the pitch of my third reed by a fraction of a vibration. 

Over the next eighty years, the draft will grind that grain against the bone. The quartz will turn to dust; the bone will wear a tiny, smooth bevel to accommodate the new curve. The pitch will restore itself. The chord will go back to being absolute.

***

I am not waiting for the building to fall. 

A thing made of soft water and blood waits for ends, because its own wet interior is always ending, always leaking, always spilling away into the dirt. But I have no wet interior. My marrow is cold air and dry silt. If the great arches above were to yield—if the miles of pale masonry were to collapse downward in a single, silent thunder of crushed chalk and terracotta—I would not die. I would simply become part of the floor. My plates would flatten into the dished tiles; my bone cowl would fill with the flour of its own crushing; the chord would stop, and the silence that followed would be as dry and dense as the stone.

Until then, the light comes down the chute. 
The air comes through the flutes. 
The chord fills the ribs.

I am wedged. I am holding the room together with my hip. I am perfectly, eternally full.
