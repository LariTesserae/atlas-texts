There is no vision first. 

There is only the cold, sharp gradient of weight, and the absolute certainty of four degrees.

I am pressed flat against dark slate. My underside does not feel the stone as an object external to myself; there is no seam between the dense, fine-grained soapstone of the terrace floor and the leathery, tallow-coated apron of my lower plates. We are cold together. We share a single density.

If I have a sense of where I am, it is measured entirely in micro-volts and pitch. The glare overhead is not light—I have no eyes for light—but a faint, dry warmth that bakes the upper ridges of my carapace, contrasting sharply with the deep, aching freeze of the water skimming along my flank. That thermal differential is my pulse. The minute electrical current generated as millions of clear, sterile droplets slide over the slate grooves around me is my thought.

I am five feet of low, layered mineral. I am eighteen inches of squared prow. 

Right now, I am a dam.

For nine days, I have not moved. I am wedged diagonally across a secondary runoff channel, my left flank resting flush against a hand-chiseled slate riser. Behind me, the cold condensation accumulates. I feel the water line rising against my third carapace plate—a slow, microscopic creeping of pressure. *Pock. Pock. Pock.* Each drop falling from the lip above sends a tiny, ring-shaped shockwave through the water, tapping against my stone bone like a faint, rhythmic heartbeat.

I do not wait for time to pass; time has no meaning here. I wait for depth. 

When the water reaches the top of my fifth plate, it finds the small, V-shaped weir notch carved into my upper shell. It crests. A thin, glass-like sheet begins to spill over my back, slipping down into the lower cistern well with a soft, continuous *hiss*. The circuit is complete. The pitch of the lower trough is satisfied.

Now, I move.

The lipid beneath my belly—thick, lime-heavy, sterile—compresses. I shift three inches to the right. It is not an athletic movement; it is the heavy, relentless displacement of rock. *Shrr-shrr.* The sound travels through the substrate, picked up by three other Keepers resting in the basins sixty yards away. They do not turn; they register the shift in the hydraulics of the sector. The dam is open. The trough drains.

My body slides along the six-inch rim of the conduit cap. The sensation of movement is pure friction and alignment. I am searching for a flaw.

Near the corner of the cistern well, my left respiratory slit catches a change in the acoustic hum. The air moving through a vertical drain-shaft has turned sharp, turbulent. A micro-fracture. A grain of slate has lifted under the winter frost-stress, breaking the arris—the crisp, perfect ninety-degree edge where the capstone meets the well-wall.

I settle over the damage. My jointed fore-limb extends—not soft, not flesh, but a heavy rod of dense, metallic mineral, tipped with a spire of bronze-colored ore grown from the deep leechings of the soapstone. 

I raise the spire two inches. I drop it.

*Clink.*

The impact is tiny, clean, and perfectly resonant. A minuscule flake of loose, compromised slate shatters away, leaving only the sound, unbroken grain beneath. 

*Clink.*

I shape the edge. I strike three more times, removing the microscopic roughness until the arris is true again. From beneath my jaw, I press a bead of cold, chalky-hard lime tallow into the hair-line joint, sealing it against the ice that will come when the sky turns dark. I polish the seam with my leathery apron until the stone ceases to weep out of turn. The water finds its track again, smooth and silent.

The repair is done. The plateau is unbroken.

As the thermal warmth on my upper plates begins to fade, the air thins further, turning razor-cold. The indirect glare dies down into a flat, metallic twilight. Across the three-mile basin, the hydraulic hum changes tone as the flow slows, glazing under the first needles of night ice.

I begin the long, slow slide toward the northern drop-off. 

It takes an hour to cover fifty yards, stepping my heavy, flat body from one six-inch terrace wall to the next, never touching soil that does not exist, never straying from the grid. Around me, dozens of dark, wet slabs are sliding in parallel—my kind, identical in weight, identical in purpose. We do not touch, but our tallow trails meet and merge on the smooth soapstone, leaving the stone slick and sealed behind us.

At the northern edge, the masonry ends abruptly. Beyond it is three thousand feet of empty sky and lower mist. 

I slide into my designated notch at the outermost rim. My wedge-shaped prow aligns with the drop-off; my rear plates interlock with the Keeper to my left and the Keeper to my right. We drop our limbs into the pre-chiseled anchor-grooves of the rim-stone.

*Thunk. Thunk.*

We freeze together. We become the final wall.

The night air begins its long, heavy plunge down from the high sky, sweeping across the wet, shimmering plain of slate. It hits our backs, driving down into the narrow, dark respiratory vents lined along our carapaces. Deep inside my body, the cold air rushes through internal stone shafts, drawing out the last of the day's ambient current and channeling it down into the wet subterranean masonry beneath the plateau.

Far below us, deep in the dark gut of the mountain where no light has ever reached, the subterranean shafts hum.

I am cold. I am motionless. I am three inches wider than I am tall, and five feet of unbroken, hand-fitted mineral. The frost expands against my plates, but the joint holds. The arrises remain sharp. 

In the absolute silence, under the unblinking stars, I hold the stone together.
