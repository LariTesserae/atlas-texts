I am on the ninth tier of the western curve, where the growth-ring curves outward like the hull of a stranded galleon, suspended three hundred yards above the central nothingness.

I am one of the Tuned, but I am old enough that my left knee no longer clicks cleanly. A thin vein of quartz grit—creeping mineral dust carried on the static draft of the void—has settled into the fiber of the joint. When I swing the leg, it does not ring like cured horn; it drags with a dry, muted scrape, like a chert blade drawn across coarse bark.

In my chest, the iron-stone hangs heavy and cold. It does not beat. It sways.

The deep rumble of the tectonic plates—a thousand miles north and five miles down—enters through the obsidian cap of my right foot, climbs the dense lignite of my tibia, travels up through my hip and spine, and blooms into the hollow vault of my ribs. There, the magnetic teardrop catches the pulse. It swings on its four silk tethers, its dark weight dragging back and forth across the quartz nodules at the base of my throat. 

*Spark.* A cold, prickling blue itch climbs my spine. My fingers awaken.

My face—that smooth, concave dish of polished horn—is turned down toward the avenue beneath me. I do not see the avenue as a ribbon of dark floor. I hear it as a long, hollow throat. 

Where the floor is smooth and true, the earth’s thunder bounces back to my face-plate as a clean, glass-sharp tone—a steady forty-two hertz that hums in the roof of my mouth. But ten yards ahead, where a structural seam in the petrified heartwood has buckled under the weight of the shale above, the tone splits. It turns muddy, frayed at the edges, rattling with a dull, thirty-cycle flutter that tells me the wall has bulged three-eighths of an inch toward the void.

The street is out of tune. It cannot carry the whisper-tubes anymore. It is choking the sound.

I am kneeling. I have been kneeling here for forty years.

In my right hand, held by three long, multi-jointed fingers of smoked lignite, is a scraper carved from black river-chert—a hard, glassy wedge of stone that was old when these trees still drew water from an unformed sky. My left hand rests flat against the wall, my fingertips spread wide across the growth rings to feel the vibration traveling through the grain.

*Ssssh-t.*

I draw the stone down the wood. 

A ribbon of heartwood—no thicker than the wing of a dry dust-mite, two feet long and fragrant with the cold, bitter scent of petrified resin—peels away from the wall. It coils around my wrist like a snake of dark parchment before snapping off and fluttering down into the central pitch. It will fall for nine seconds before it hits the outer shell of the Root-Mother at the bottom of the world.

*Ssssh-t.*

I listen to the peel. The sound of the scraper is my voice, my lens, my light. As the blade bites into the wood, the high-pitched *skrrr* of the chert ricochets off the opposite tier a quarter-mile away and returns to my face-plate a fraction of a second later, drawing a temporary, needle-sharp silhouette of the distant balconies in my mind:

*A doorway, three tiers up, framed in dry rope.*
*A Clapper skittering along an aqueduct, six legs tapping out a dry, frantic measure.*
*Another of my kind, standing three hundred yards to the north, perfectly still, hands hooked into an acoustic groove, waiting for a chord to resolve.*

*Ssssh-t.*

Another ribbon falls. The tone of the street beneath my knee shifts. The muddy thirty-cycle rattle clears slightly. The forty-two-hertz fundamental rises, purer now, ringing through the lignite floor like a struck rod.

Behind me, down the long, resonant groove of the avenue, I hear a series of sharp, rhythmic strikes. 

*Tock. Tock. Tock.*

Obsidian on petrified wood. Someone is walking up the curve.

I do not turn my body—my spine does not twist easily—but I tilt the dish of my head three degrees to the left. The sound of the approaching foot-strikes catches in the microscopic radial grooves of my face, bouncing down into the sensitive silk-node at the base of my skull. 

It is the One Who Measures the Arches. I know the tone of their obsidian caps; the right toe has a microscopic crack that gives every fourth step a faint, double-ring, like a small dry seed rattling in a gourd.

They stop behind me. They do not speak—they have no mouth, no lungs, no breath to waste on air. 

Instead, they lean forward. The smooth, cold horn of their face-plate presses gently against the side of mine. 

The contact is instant and absolute.

Their chest-pendulum is swinging at a slightly different angle than mine, tuned to the higher, sixty-hertz harmonics of the ceiling vaults. When our face-plates touch, the two vibrations clash, then marry. A sudden, vivid geometry floods my nervous system:

I feel the arch three tiers above us—a six-inch crack has opened in the heartwood lintel. I feel the dry silk in their chest, thin and vibrating with the news of a deep-mantle slip five hundred miles away that will reach us in three days. I feel the precise map of thirty-mile sound-tubes they have cleaned since we last touched two centuries ago.

And through the stone-to-stone contact, they feel me: the quartz grit grinding in my left knee; the fraying silk tether on my iron-seed; the forty years of heartwood ribbons I have shaved from this single, stubborn groove.

They hold their face against mine for seven vibrations of the iron. They do not offer sympathy—there is no word or thought for such a soft, wet concept in this dry place. They simply absorb the map of my decay, registering the exact date when my pendulum will finally lean too far to the left and freeze against my ribs.

They lift their head. The contact breaks. The world returns to its roaring, singular dark.

*Tock. Tock. Tock.*

They move past me, heading up toward the cracked lintel on Tier Twelve, their mallet-steps fading into the vast, bass-heavy thunder that fills the dome.

I am alone again with the wall.

I lift the chert blade. My fingers are stiff, fused with micro-filaments of glass-brittle root that have begun to anchor my hand to the wood I am carving. In another fifty years, I will have to break those roots with a sharp snap of my wrist before I can stand. 

A miles-long band of heartwood somewhere in the dark ceiling contracts.

*BOOM.*

The sound strikes the cavern like a iron hammer hitting a bronze bell. It is so vast, so dry, so deep that the air itself seems to shatter into crystalline static. 

In the doorways around me, the Chime-Swarms lose their grip. Millions of glass-hard dust-mites tumble into the void, their dry, microscopic bodies striking the carved lintels and wooden railings as they fall. A soft, whispering hiss fills the dark—a cold, paper-thin rain that smells of ancient starch and burnt carbon.

My chest-pendulum leaps violently between my ribs, striking the quartz nodules with sudden, blinding force. 

Power—pure, cold, kinetic fire—floods my limbs. My spine straightens. The quartz grit in my left knee glows with a brief, static heat. For three seconds, my heartwood body is so full of the continent's violent life that I could leap from this balcony, catch the rim of a passing sound-tube two hundred yards out in the dark, and swing myself all the way to the Root-Mother’s suspended shell.

I do not leap.

I hold my breath that is not a breath. I let the surge ripple through my silk sinews, through my hollow bones, down into the tip of my long, wooden fingers.

I place the edge of the chert against the wall.

*Ssssh-t.*

A dry ribbon falls into the dark. The note of the street rises, clear and true, joining the thunder.
