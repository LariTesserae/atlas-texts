I do not begin with a body. I begin with the acoustics.

At first, I am only the echo: the sharp, metallic *ping* of a single drop of water hitting the untouched face of the black basalt, answered a microsecond later by the dull, velvet *thud* of that same sound dying against damp clay. 

I am down low. That much is clear immediately. I am far beneath the emerald ceiling where the orchids tangle, down in the humid, stagnant shadow where the air tastes of crushed fern fronds, river silt, and the sharp, pine-sweet bite of secreted resin. 

For a moment, I drift upward. I try on the lightness of the pale, translucent lizard. I feel the strange, impossible suction of wide toes clinging to a vertical pane of dark glass—stone so smooth, so newly frozen from liquid fire, that there is no tooth or pore for a claw to hold. I feel the quick, terrifying franticness of a heart beating behind ribs as thin as dry needle-grass, watching the heavy, iridescent beetles tumble from the dark chutes above. It is a thrilling, fragile way to exist—living in the hairline fractures where the world has not yet learned how to decay.

Then the shadow in the pool shifts, and I feel a different pull. 

I feel the cold, fluid patience of the long hunter in the reservoir. I feel my spine lengthen, turning to oil and ash-grey fur, trailing a tail through the silted water without raising a single bubble. I feel the pale gold discs of my eyes gathering the faint green twilight, measuring the distance to the causeway, waiting for the young to misstep. There is a deep, quiet power in that stillness—being the single point of stillness around which the whole frantic city hums.

But no. The hunter is an observer of the architecture. I want to be its *substance*.

My weight settles. It drops three feet down, heavy and thick-shouldered, grounding itself against the earth. The air leaves my lungs in a slow, warm huff against wet clay. 

I am a Weaver. 

Not one of the swift, silver-downed young tumbling in the fern-lined nurseries of the minarets, nor one of the sleek foragers returning from the canopy with leaf-parcels. I am down at the base of a rising gallery tube, where a new span of reddish mud must bridge a four-inch chasm of glass-sharp basalt that split open when the world was hot.

My body is built of contrasts. Along my back, my fur is dense, oil-slick, and black as the stone around me, cold to the touch from the constant drip of the ceiling. But beneath, where my chest and belly press against the mud, my coat is a pale, warm ochre, caked with dry river-silt and smelling of warm earth. 

Where my forelegs join my torso, my hide is no longer soft; it is thick, pebbled leather, scarred and calloused from decades of leaning against the untamed edges of fresh basalt. I can drag my shoulder across a stone ridge sharp enough to slice a fruit clean in half, and feel only the pristine, geometric cold of the earth.

I am working in the dark beneath a causeway. My eyes—small, dark discs—are nearly useless here, but my snout is ablaze with light. 

It is broad, hairless, and intensely warm. I press it flat against the drying seam of the archway above me. Through the damp cartilage of my nose, I do not see the crack—I *hear* it. I feel the micro-strain of the mud as the moisture evaporates into the still air. I feel the subtle shift as the clay shrinks, pulling away from the basalt pillar. 

*Not enough resin,* my body tells me.

I reach into the shallow mixing-trough at my feet with my forepaws. My claws are heavy, broad spades, but the fleshy pads between them are delicate enough to feel the individual grains of river sand. I draw up a wet mass of chewed fig bark and grey silt. 

Beneath my tongue, the resin gland aches, hot and full. I flex the muscles of my throat, regurgitating a thick, amber spool of cement—bitter, aromatic, and warm—kneading it into the silt with the heels of my hands. My movements are not thought out; they are rhythmic, ancestral, as fluid as breath. 

Beside me in the dark, another heavy shoulder presses against mine. I do not look at my mound-mate. I feel the sudden, synchronized shudder of her flanks, a silent query. I answer with a quick, firm nudge of my wet snout against her neck: *Hold the span.*

She leans her full weight against the wet gallery wall, bracing it, while I bring my tail into play. It is a broad, heavy paddle of muscle and tough skin. With three rhythmic, echoing slaps—*THWACK, THWACK, THWACK*—I drive the resin-rich mud deep into the cooling crack of the basalt. The sound rings out into the plaza like a struck bell. The mixture binds. The stress lines in the clay vanish. The stone accepts the mud.

Then, through the floorboards of the causeway, a vibration begins.

It is not a sound in the air. It is a deep, sub-audible pulse traveling through the cured, porcelain-hard clay of the city. *Thrum-thrum-thrum-thrum.* 

Far across the plaza, near the shallow reservoir, someone’s hind legs are striking a dry mud wall in a rapid, vibrating cadence. The alarm. 

The hunter in the ash-grey fur has climbed out of the water. It is sitting on the moss lawn, silent as a fallen leaf, watching the lower arches.

Around us, the grand transit tubes go quiet. The silver-furred young are swept backward into the deep honeycombs of the minarets by the flat tails of their mothers. The leaf-bearers freeze on the causeways, blending their dark backs against the black rock. 

My mound-mate pauses, her hide tense, her small ears flattened against her broad head. 

I do not run. My paws stay caked in wet mud. My snout remains pressed to the curing seam of the archway. 

I feel the predator's presence not as a terror, but as a condition of the fracture. It is part of the valley’s weight, like the humidity, or the drip of the ceiling, or the sharp edge of the stone. It waits in the water; we build on the rock. It has its patience; we have our clay.

I lift my heavy tail and tap the cured floor once—a low, dense *tock* that answers the thrumming alarm. 

*The arch holds,* the tap says into the dark stone. *Keep weaving.*

I tilt my head back. A heavy, clear drop of water falls from a fig leaf sixty feet above. It hits the center of my snout, cold and pure, before sliding down into the warm, resin-scented mud on my hands. I turn back to the trough, gather another measure of silt, and begin again.
