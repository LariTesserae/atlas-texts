I am kneeling.

My broad, pale-ochre knees are sunk two inches into the warm water of a shallow terracotta basin. The water here does not ripple; it simply holds my weight, wrapping around the smooth curves of my shins like liquid silk at exactly one hundred degrees. 

My body is long—longer than the channels I carve. My skin is the color of dried river-silt after a rain, unblemished, matte, and heavy with the dense humidity that never falls. On the flat expanse of my back, between my shoulder blades, sits a linen-moth. It fell from the high, blinding vault of white plaster two days ago, landing with the soft, wet sound of a dropped cloth. It has not moved since. I feel its subtle, broad weight—four feet of damp, light-drunk fabric clinging to my skin, absorbing the heat we both share with the sky.

I do not have a name, for there is no one to call out across the open plazas, and my throat holds no air for speech. But if you were to press your palm to the wet granite three miles away, you would know me by the rhythm of my resting pulse—a low, dense *thump-thump* that travels through forty feet of continuous ashlar masonry, through the clay basins, through the sharp-cornered gutters, and into the soles of your wide, gripping feet.

Before me is a pylon. 

It was once a mason who knelt as I am kneeling now, whose movements slowed until the liquid in his skin turned to lime and silica, his terracotta flesh solidifying into stone over three centuries of absolute stillness. His torso is now a crisp, four-sided column of deep ceramic red. His face has smoothed into a flat, elegant lintel that catches the shadowless light from above.

I am holding a copper chisel. Its wooden handle is dark and slick from the wetness of my palm, though there is no sweat on me—only the fat, glittering beads of condensation that gather along my collarbones and remain suspended, refusing to run down. 

I place the bronze edge against the pylon’s base, right where his calf merges with the granite floor. 

I do not swing the mallet hard. There is no need for force where time does not harden stone into brittle resistance. I tap. 

*Thunk.*

The chisel slides through the stone as if it were cold lard or dense river-clay. A ribbon-thin curl of red terracotta peels away from the block, falling silently into the warm, upward-creeping stream beside my knee. Where the metal cut, the edge left behind is impossibly, dangerously precise—a line so true it seems to slice the humid air itself.

I put down the chisel. I reach into the wooden bucket beside me—carved from a single block of dense, water-logged heartwood—and scoop up a palmful of wet, white mortar made of slaked lime and ground sea-shell. 

I press my open hand directly into the seam between the pylon and the gutter. 

My skin meets the mortar. I feel the chemical warmth of the lime against my palm, perfectly matching the warmth of my blood. I slide my broad hand flat along the joint, smoothing the white paste until it fills every hair-thin void, leaving the distinct, dimpled impression of my palm etched permanently into the seal. 

A mile to the east, I feel an answering pulse through the stone. *Tap. Tap.* 

It is the worker at the five-stepped terrace, where the water flows uphill. She has felt my chisel stroke through the floor. She is telling me that the channel is clear, that the water has reached the base of my pylon, that the glass-clear crabs have already entered the new stone gutter to shear away the microscopic green threads of moss before they can dull the masonry’s crisp, knife-cut edge.

I lower my face to the water.

I do not bend my neck with hurry; my broad, lidless eyes—spheres of dark, polished amber—remain open as my mouth and nose submerge beneath the surface of the terracotta channel. I do not choke. My lungs do not crave air. I draw the warm, mineral-heavy liquid deep into my throat, feeling the lime, the fine silt, and the microscopic, glass-clear algae pass directly through the membranes of my body, feeding the heavy, slow clock of my blood.

Beneath my floating chest, two tallow-eels slide out from the puddled clay. They are long, translucent strips of parchment, eyesless and smooth. They drift over the tops of my broad, articulated feet, their flat bellies scraping away the faint dusting of lime-residue that has gathered between my toes. It tickles, but I do not flinch. Nothing flinches here. Motion is not a reaction to intrusion; it is simply the continuation of the pattern.

When I lift my face from the stream, the water sheets off my terracotta skin instantly, leaving only those same fat, motionless beads of dew along my jaw.

I look up—not at the sky, which is a uniform, blinding ceiling of white plaster that offers no distance and no depth—but across the vast, wet floor. 

A quarter-mile away, an island of purple arums rises from a hand-chiseled planting well. Their leaves are six feet wide, dark as bruised plums, completely motionless. A jade salamander, long-backed and six-legged, is resting its chin on the lip of the stone well, its black eyes fixed on nothing at all, waiting for the seed in the fold of its flank to drop into the moist lime-dust.

Beyond the arums, the flagstones stretch out forever—crisp, pristine, shimmering with a thin, perfect film of water that never evaporates and never overflows. 

I reach for my copper chisel again. 

I am not finishing this pylon. It was not unfinished when I began, and it will not be completed when I stop. I am simply keeping the edge from forgetting its shape. I am keeping the corner so sharp that if the world were ever to move, it would cut itself against us.

I place the bronze edge against the stone. I strike. 

The low *hiss* of the condensation absorbs the sound, carrying it down through the granite, through the clay, through the water flowing uphill, holding us all in the bright, warm, eternal now.
