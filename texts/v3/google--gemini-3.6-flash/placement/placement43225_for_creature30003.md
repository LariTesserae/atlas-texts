First, there is only the heat. 

It does not come from a sun; it comes from behind my back, where my thick, hairless length is wrapped twice around a trunk of soft-annealed copper. The copper pulses with a hot, liquid heartbeat that is not mine, but which my skin knows as intimately as its own fluid chambers. 

I am suspended upside down in the gold mist. My eyes—if I ever had them—are long gone, smoothed over by generations of wet air into pale, translucent skin that senses light only as a deep, Amber pressure against my forehead. Above me, the ceiling grid hums with its endless, dry radiation. Below me, thousands of meters down through the warm fog, the world sighs. *Thrum-ah. Thrum-ah.* A piston stroke, miles away, felt as a gentle vibration in the tip of my tail.

I am heavy. My body is a plump, oblong vessel filled with clear, dense fluids—a hydraulic bag shaped like a pear, encased in a skin that feels like waterlogged suede. I have no ribs to hold me shape-rigid; I am kept firm by the pressure of my own internal pumps, which draw the heavy, humid air into my lateral vents and condense it into cool water within my core.

For days—or what would be days if there were nights—I hang here, absorbing the overflow of the high copper lines. My skin darkens slightly in the light, turning from milk-white to the pale pink of a boiled root. I grow warmer and warmer, baking in the trapped heat of the upper basin, until my fluid chambers reach their limit. I am full. I am hot. I am ready to drop.

The release is not a decision. It is a hydraulic shift.

A valve in my tail relaxes. The grip on the copper conduit loosens with a wet, suction-release *kiss*. 

Then comes the long, glorious silence of the descent.

I do not fly. Flying requires effort, sharp angles, feathers, purpose. I simply yield to the density of the air. I unfurl my broad, skin-membrane flaps—soft, heavy sails of skin that catch the rising convection currents. The gold light recedes above me, fading into a luminous, cream-white fog. The air grows cooler, thicker with the scent of hot mineral oil and pristine, distilled water. 

I float down like a dropped fruit through a sea of warm steam. 

Below me, the endless grid of the floor comes into soft focus: the knife-clean seams of iron, the shallow gutters lined with crimson gills, and the vast, pale carpet of the Sole-Mats—millions of soft, oval bellies shifting in unison. 

The impact is painless. It is a slow-motion, heavy *whump*—a soft, wet thud that ripples out through the living floor for twenty meters in every direction. The ground-dwellers beneath me do not bruise or tear; they yield like thick, liquid foam, swallowing my weight, cushioning my wet belly against the iron plates.

I lie there, stunned and wide, spread across a dozen pale discs of living blubber. 

This is the cooling. 

The floor is wet with the cold runoff from the ceramic pillars. Through my porous underside, I drink. My overheated core dumps its thermal load directly into the Sole-Mats, who take the heat eagerly, passing it down through their cilia into the cold-rolled steel plates beneath them. In return, their cool, distilled film seeps up into my skin, refilling my internal reservoirs, flushing my vascular channels with fresh, clean water laced with microscopic traces of copper and oil.

Around me, the Channel-Weavers thrash gently in their shallow tile gutters, their red gill-bunches fanning the water clean. A Great Shepherd’s spoon-shaped hand passes overhead in a slow, six-meter arc, clearing a stray smear of moisture from a drain grate. It senses my presence—a temporary mound of heat on the flat grid—and its thermal pits glow with a faint, welcoming teal as it sways past.

I stay down here for hours, or perhaps a year. Time has no edge when nothing rots. 

Gradually, my core turns cool and heavy once more. My fluid chambers are topped off; my skin is slick and firm. The urge to rise returns—not as an ambition, but as a swell of buoyancy in my upper chambers.

I pull my leathery flaps tight against my ribs. My short, blunt fore-limbs—ending in wide, soft suction-pads of thick cartilage—find the base of an octagonal ceramic column. 

The climb begins.

It is a slow, rhythmic march upward against the glazed white tile. *Tack. Tack. Tack.* My suction pads grip the smooth porcelain, leaving faint, temporary circles of clear mucus that are instantly washed clean by the continuous downward sheet of condensation. I pass the Liver-Segmented ones, clinging in their deep-maroon clusters near the internal heat pipes, their translucent bladders pulsing with soft teal light as I brush past their wet flanks. They do not shrink away; we are the same blood, divided only by height.

Up and up, out of the cool, wet air of the gutters, back into the warm, gold haze of the high rafters. 

The copper conduit trunk looms out of the mist—warm, solid, and humming with its subsonic song. I reach out with my long, muscular tail, feel the curved metal, and wrap myself tightly around it once more.

I pull myself up. I tuck my head against my chest. My skin meets the copper. The heat begins to build again inside my core.

I am a drop of water in an engine that never stops. I am a breath drawn by a room that never closes its mouth. I am home, and I am full, and the ceiling is glowing softly overhead, forever.
