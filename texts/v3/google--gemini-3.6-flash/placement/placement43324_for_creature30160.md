I begin not in a name, but in the roof of my mouth. 

There is a cold, bitter disc of *wax-flesh* tucked beneath my tongue. I do not bite down. To bite is to flood the jaw and lose half the liquid to the dry gullet all at once. Instead, I press it gently upward against the ridge of my palate with a slow, unconscious rhythm born of forty years on the stone tiers. The pale jelly bleeds its cool sap drop by drop, thin as morning spit, running down my throat so slowly that my body forgets it is drinking at all.

Where am I? 

I am halfway up the western shadow, on the fifth tier, sitting on a bench carved so deep into the basalt that the stone curves smoothly around my kidneys like a warm hand. My toes—wide, square, and capped with soles as thick as dried hide—are hooked over the lip of the ledge. Below me, three tiers down, two women are pounding dry bitter-roots in a mortar carved from a single chunk of grey granite. *Thump. Thump.* The sound travels up the stone wall straight into my spine before it ever reaches my ears. 

I am a scraper of the high thread-lichen, a binder of scrub-hurdles, a voice pitched to the third resonance of the granite overhang. My chest is a dark bellows, barrel-wide; when I take in the warm, thin air of the basin, my ribs spread like the stays of a woven basket, holding the heat inside where it belongs. I do not sweat. My skin—dark as a drowned stone, thick with the heavy fat of the copper-down fowl—shines softly in the bounced light. 

Around my knees, a young clacker rests its dusty yellow chin on my ankle. Its belly is hot from its morning vigil on the high eastern rim, and its pleated side rises and falls against my shin, dry as parchment. I do not move my leg. To move unnecessarily is to waste heat, to waste breath, to wear down the ironwood ladders a fraction of a second faster than the sun requires.

Above us, the sky is not blue like a lowlander’s sky—though I have no real word for *lowlander*, only *the ones beneath the wool-deck*, the ones who live where the birds vanish every dawn. Up here, the sky is the color of an old bruise, dark and thin and pressing down on the lip of the bowl like a heavy lid. But inside the bowl, under the great curving brow of granite, everything is bathed in a twilight the color of an old copper bowl that has been rubbed with oil.

Listen. 

If I tilt my head back against the rock, I can hear my daughter speaking from the eastern ledges, two hundred paces across the open air. She is not shouting. Her voice is a low, rolling chant, pitched into a shallow dish in the cliff face behind her door frame. The stone takes her words, rounds off their corners, and slides them along the curve of the bowl directly into the alcove above my head:

*“…the grey sludge is thick on the seventh ladder… bring the wide gourd when the swifts return…”*

I do not answer with a call. I raise my right hand, flat-palmed, toward the copper-tinted air, knowing the shadow of my arm will catch the bounced light off the far rim and flash dark against the pale clay wall of Tier Three. She will see it. Everything is seen; everything is heard. There are no corners in the tooth-bowl that the sound does not lick clean.

At mid-day, when the swifts are long gone into the white ocean beneath the cliffs, the basin turns inward on itself. I reach into the fiber-sack at my hip and pull out a bone needle, long and smooth, polished by the hands of my uncle before he was carried up to the windy niches. I am mending a breath-mat. The scrub-wood fibers are tough, smelling of old resin and dry earth, and as I pull the braid tight, I breathe out across the weave. The fine moss lining catches the moisture of my lungs—a faint, damp shadow on the grey fibers—and freezes it there, holding it until the morning squeeze. Nothing escapes the bowl. Even the air we give away is gathered up again.

I look up, past the wasp-nest domes of clay and fieldstone, past the dense, waxy canopy of the floor-shrubs where the heavy mountain fowl are clacking their soft, wet *crrr-crrr* in the shade. 

High up, where the smooth basalt gives way to the porous, honeycombed granite of the outer lip, the niches sit in the dry wind. I can see the feet of my grandfather protruding slightly from his shelf. They are the color of smoked meat, bound tight in braided lichen-cloth, motionless against the dark bruise of the sky. He has been sitting there since the thresholds of Tier Five were three finger-widths higher than they are today. He is dry. He is light. He listens to the booming roar of the mortars and the whistling of the swift-wings, holding the wind off our heads.

Someday, when my teeth are ground down to smooth pink pads from the resin-wood and the succulent-skins, they will carry me up the single-wood ladders. They will oil my skin one last time with the orange fowl-fat, wrap me in the moss-mats I mended with my own fingers, and set me in the stone window beside him. 

I will sit there, wide-chested and light as an empty gourd, facing inward toward the copper light. I will watch the women scrape the morning lichen with spatulas of swift-bone; I will watch the yellow lizards clack across the clay roofs; I will hear the low, smooth chanting of my children's children bouncing from ledge to ledge across the warm, loud cradle of the rock. 

Nothing will change. The wind will shear across the lip above us, dry and wild, but down inside the tooth, the warm breath will stay trapped in the stone, passing from mouth to mat, from leaf to throat, forever.
