First, there is the coldness. Not a biting frost, but the deep, steady chill of iron that has sat in damp shade for a hundred years, wrapped in wet velvet. 

I do not start with a face or a name. For a long time, as the cool air settles in the basin, I am only the feeling of a seam. 

It is a place where two different kinds of density meet: the hard, unyielding grain of dark walnut-wood, slick with condensation, and the cold, flat plate of a riveted steel flange. I am the wedge between them. I feel the microscopic shift of the metal as the air cools at twilight, shrinking by a fraction of a millimeter; I feel the wood swelling beneath it, drinking the atmospheric wetness, pushing back with a soft, immoveable weight.

If I move my attention upward, I can feel the sky—not as light, but as a drop in pressure. Far above, where the broad, leathery leaves hang over the earthwork rim, there is a tiny, steady vibration. *Ping.* A quarter-ounce of softened water hits a brass housing seventy feet away. The shockwave travels through the copper conduit, down through the root-marrow of the floor, and passes into the base of my spine like a distant, familiar word. 

*Ping.* Another drop. The gear is cooling. The Dew-Gazers are doing their work.

Am I up there with them, hanging translucent in the gray air, feeling the mist condense on my skin? No. There is too much weight in me for that. I am too heavy, too thick. My thoughts—if they can be called thoughts—don't drift on the damp breeze. They are anchored in the deep, springy substrate where the iron I-beams lie buried in root-flesh.

Down here, beneath the surface, I can sense the Marrow-Worms moving in the dark. Millions of them, blind and soft, turning rust into grease, keeping the deep bones of the yard from seizing up. I feel their appetite as a faint, pleasant warmth in the ground beneath me. They are digesting the centuries. They are making sure that when the big wheel turns, the earth beneath it yields just enough to absorb the shock.

And then the pressure begins to build.

It starts three yards away, inside the bulbous root trunk that feeds the main hydraulic line. The sap is rising—thick, cold, charged with the faint, metallic tang of dissolved copper. It climbs through the lignified channels, pushing against the heavy iron piston. I feel the tension rising through the copper-wrapped tendrils that thread through my own chest. 

I am not the piston, and I am not the valve. I am the node that sits in the dark hollow between them. 

My body is mostly wood now, smooth and dark as polished walnut, but my ribs are steel, pitted with old rust that has long since been sealed beneath a skin of hard tallow. Where my head used to be, there is only a cold, dome-like mass of lignified bone, resting flush against the inner wall of the boiler house. I have no eyes to see the pale wisps of steam leaking from the three stacks above, but I feel the warmth of that steam as a faint, amber line drawn down the back of my neck.

A Cleat is crawling across my lower left conduit right now. It moves with a slow, muscular drag—an inch every twenty minutes—laying down its coat of turpentine-scented tallow over a sweating brass joint. Its weight is comforting: a dense, wet sandbag dampening the high-frequency hum of the electric roots. I do not mind it. It treats me as part of the piping, and it is right to do so.

The sap pressure reaches its mark. *Fourteen pounds.*

The threshold is clean, mathematical, and absolute. There is no thought required, no decision to be made. A small, involuntary spark—a tiny discharge of stored voltage—fires at the base of my skull. It travels down the gray muscle-tendrils buried in the floor, striking the coil at the valve housing.

*Clack-hiss.*

The heavy iron piston moves two inches. The air shifts. A cool draft of ozone and wet fern washes over my wooden flank as the valve exhausts its breath. The eight-foot flywheel in the open shed completes a quarter-turn, its copper tendrils humming a low, resonant *F-sharp* that vibrates through the root-marrow floor, through the Cleat on my conduit, through the brass plates, all the way to the outer rim.

The pressure drops. The sap recedes. The balance is restored.

I settle deeper into the hollow of the trunk. The wood of the boiler house cradles my spine; the root-marrow swallows my feet. In three minutes, or perhaps four, the sap will rise again. A Dew-Gazer will drop its bead of water. A Marrow-Worm will turn a flake of iron into paste.

I am a Trunk-Keeper, but I am also just the quiet space where the machinery rests between strokes. I have no memory of walking, no wish to see what lies beyond the blue-gray lichen on the rim. The air down here is cool, the iron is cold, the wood is live, and the load is perfectly balanced.
