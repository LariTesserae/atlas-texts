First, there is no *here* or *there*. There is only the gradient.

The world presents itself as an immense, three-dimensional tapestry of fluid resistance and heat. To the left, the water thickens with dissolved iron and grows half a degree warmer—a subtle, heavy swell rising from below. To the right, a slight drop in pressure signals the lip of an open thoroughfare where the current glides unobstructed. 

I do not begin as a shape. I begin as a sensation of anchorage.

There is a profound, immoveable density beneath me, a cold-iron rigidity that does not yield. It is the stone-bone of the city, smooth as melted tallow, holding me fast against the relentless, muscular shove of the water. I am wedged into it, or perhaps grown into it, occupying a vertical chimney no wider than a grand hallway, but deep enough to reach into the earth’s feverish gut.

I am not swimming. I have no wish to swim. To swim is to be light, to be thin, to be consumed by the curtain of eels that shifts silently through the dark above. 

Instead, I am a chamber. A throat. A valve.

As I let my perception spread along my own tissue, I feel the complexity of what I am: not a single, isolated creature, but a vast, colonial knot of living, translucent bells, muscular sphincters, and kilometers of delicate, sensory threads. I am an **Organist**, anchored in the crown of a high, tapered spire, three hundred feet above the basin floor.

My lower roots—deep down in the hollow bone-flue of the tower—are bathed in fire. Up from the seabed faults comes the subterranean water: black, mineral-choked, hot enough to scald flesh to paste. But I do not burn. My lower bells are lined with dense, sulfur-binding proteins, drinking in the iron and copper, cooling the liquid in the dark, chambered caverns of my belly before it can shatter the quiet equilibrium of the street outside.

With a slow, rhythmic contraction of my central mantle, I draw thousands of gallons of this pressurized vent-water up through the flute of the stone. 

*Shift. Hold. Expel.*

The fluid shoots out through my upper siphon, mushrooming into the open mid-water above the spire. As it leaves me, the calcified walls of the chimney ring like a struck iron bell. It is not a sound meant for ears; there are no ears in this world. It is a structural resonance—a deep, sub-audible *thrum* that travels down the bone facade, through the ramps, down into the mud, and out across the miles of the basin.

Through the stone, I feel the city answer me.

A mile away, another Organist fires its siphon out of phase with my own, creating a low, beating harmonic that vibrates through my anchor-points. Down on the avenue below my spire, the Chitin-Clans hear the shift in our song. I can feel them through the masonry: thousands of disc-shaped, iron-crusted carapaces shifting in their elliptical windows. Their jointed legs click against the bone—*tap-tap-tap, rattle, click*—a frantic, telegraphic chatter passing from tower to tower, reporting a rich plume of bacterial sludge drifting north on the thermal surge.

Above my open bell, the mid-water is alive with static. The Eel-Swarms are passing. I cannot see them, but my delicate, drifting tentacle-fringes feel the tiny, rhythmic prickles of their electric fields. They move like a single, undulating ribbon of gel, their collective current warping around the apex of my spire. They know I am here; to them, my stone spire is a dark, non-conductive void in their electric map, a silhouette carved out of the water’s ambient spark. One of them brushes my outer mantle—a cool, slick passing—and is gone, sweeping down into the plaza to fold around a stray scavenger.

Far below, at the base of my tower, the world moves on a different scale of time.

I feel the heavy, catastrophic weight of a Foundation passing. It is a slow, grinding pressure that makes the very roots of my spire groan. Millions of microscopic, chitinous teeth are rasping away at the stone near my base, scraping off the stale bacterial slime, leaving behind a warm, thick wake of calcifying mucus that seeps into the micro-cracks of my tower, repairing the erosion of the last hundred years. The leviathan does not know I exist up here in the pitch, nor do I know its mind; we are merely the top and bottom of the same chimney, bound together by the flow of water and lime.

A thermal surge is coming. 

I feel it three minutes before it arrives. Deep in the earth beneath the basin, a tectonic plate shifts a fraction of a millimeter. The water in my flue spikes by two degrees. The mineral content turns sharp, metallic, tasting heavily of zinc and sulfur.

My body responds without thought. My lower sphincters dilate. My colonial bells swell, firming their walls against the rising pressure. 

I pull the heat up. I gorge on the sulfur. My upper bell blossoms outward like a massive, translucent flower at the peak of the spire, pulsing harder, faster.

*THUM. THUM. THUM.*

The song of the spire deepens, rising in pitch and volume. All across the biological metropolis, the other Organists join in, a great, blind orchestra pulsing in the pitch-black sea. 

Responding to our chorus, the entire facade of my tower blooms. Thousands of filter-feeding tube-worms and delicate, feathered fans extend from every smooth, elliptical window up and down the stone, turning the naked, bone-pale tower into a swaying, shaggy pillar of live tissue. They reach out into the warm, nutrient-rich current I am pumping into the sky, catching the bacterial drift, feeding on the breath I draw from the earth.

I am not an inhabitant of this city. I am its heart, its furnace, and its voice. 

I have no eyes to grieve the dark. I have no mind to wish for a sky I have never known. There is only the reassuring weight of the ocean above, the comforting fire of the earth below, the solid bone of the stone around me, and the infinite, blood-warm water, pushing through my body, keeping the world alive.
