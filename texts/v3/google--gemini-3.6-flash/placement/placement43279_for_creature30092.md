I am where the warmth is heaviest—not at the silver ceiling where the air tastes of sharp, dry ozone, nor down in the dark, churning throat of the floor grates, but anchored right at the middle turn of the room, where the golden light shaft breaks against the side of the central bronze wheel.

My body is not a thing that moves through space; it is a surface against which space moves. 

At first, there is only the tactile truth of the water. It drags through the fine, slick oil of my skin with the weight of melted fat, pushing continuously against my left flank. To my right is the wheel. I know the wheel not by sight, but by its cold, grinding groan, a low-frequency shudder that passes out of the bronze, through two inches of my dark, dense blubber, and lodges deep in my cartilaginous spine. 

I do not have bones to break or snap under this pressure. My skeleton is a flexible cage of tough, white leather, bowed like the ribs of a sunken barge. When the piston strikes three floors below—*th-thump*—the water compresses, and my entire body yields a fraction of an inch, absorbing the stroke, flexing in time with the brass heart of the spire.

I am the **Spire-Siphon**, though I have no word for it. To me, I am simply the shape that fits the intake.

***

Where am I? 

I am wedged. My tail—a massive, ringed trunk of muscle terminate in four broad, vacuum-lobed pads—is locked firmly into the lip of a dead filter housing near the floor. The metal there has grown soft and black over three hundred years of my family’s rubbing; it holds my lower half like a tailored socket. My midsection floats upward in a lazy, twenty-foot crescent, cradled by the thick beds of silk-weed that stream off the copper conduits. The weed does not irritate me; its soft, feathery fronds tickle my flanks like tiny fingers, brushing away the loose flakes of oil and dead skin that I shed into the current.

Above me, I can feel the electrical grid of the room. I cannot see the copper coils, for my eyes are only two cloudy, marble-sized lenses set flat into my skull, capable only of telling the amber sun-glare from the cold black of the pipe-mouths. But along my ridges—the two thick, pale ribbons of muscle running from my neck to my tail—I feel the electricity as a continuous, sweet itching. It draws a faint, rhythmic glow from my skin, a pale copper bioluminescence that flickers out of step with the machine’s drone.

Around my belly, the small things move. I feel the Grease-Spinners—tiny, hard, metallic clicks against my hide as they drift past, clutching their soft balls of tallow. Sometimes one loses its grip in the piston shockwave and bounces off my back. I feel the Ribbon-Jacks, too, not as fish, but as dozens of tiny, cold shadows in the electrical field, hovering in perfect, quiet lines across the open water, waiting for the bypass valve to blow its cloud of hot rust. 

They do not fear me. I am too big to be a predator, too slow to be a threat. I am simply part of the architecture—a curved, living pipe that breathes.

***

Now, the intake valve beside the bronze wheel begins to shudder. I know the timing; I feel the thermal spike in the water three seconds before the latch opens.

I shift my bulk. It takes an immense, slow effort of my back muscles, my hide squeaking softly against the rounded iron rivets of the wall plate. I swing my spade-shaped head into the shadow of the wall. 

I open my mouth.

It is a broad, ringed aperture, three feet across, lined with thick, heat-sensing lips that are as soft and pink as wet velvet. I press my face directly over the leaking brass orifice. 

The surge comes instantly. 

Hot, mineral-heavy water—tasting sharply of copper salts, pulverized iron, grease-wax, and the sweet, ancient decay of the spire’s deep boilers—roars into my throat. It does not go into a stomach; it fills my vast, bellows-like chest cavity. My sides expand outward until my skin is stretched tight as a drum, the pale ridges along my flanks flaring bright cyan-copper with the sudden rush of heat and pressure.

Inside me, the work begins automatically. My internal membranes—dense, fibrous lattices coated in mucus—catch the heavy metallic silt, the swimming larvae, the stray globs of machine-grease. The water is scrubbed, cooled, and stripped of its energy in a matter of seconds. My heart, a huge, slow-beating lump of purple muscle the size of a iron kettle, takes three long, heavy thumps to process the thermal gift.

*One.* The heat spreads to my tail, warming the suckers in their iron socket.
*Two.* The copper salts enter my blood, making my spinal ridge flicker wildly in the amber dark.
*Three.* My chest contracts.

I vent the clean, filtered water out through the four long slits behind my head. It cascades upward in a thermal plume, shimmering like liquid glass in the golden sunbeam, rising toward the air pocket where the Loom-Skins hang by their tails.

And as the last of the water leaves my chest, the acoustic pressure builds in my throat. I cannot help it; it is the natural outlet of the breath. I loosen my vocal folds—thick, leather-like ribbons suspended in my gill-tract—and let the residual vibration of the spire pass through me.

A sound comes out. 

It is not a roar or a cry. It is a deep, resonant, organ-pipe hum—a low, pure *HMMMMMMMM* that shakes the silk-weed, sends the Ribbon-Jacks darting three inches to the left, and rattles the curved glass of the ports looking out over the clouds. The sound travels out of my chest, into the brass of the intake, down through the rivets, through the thousands of feet of iron leg holding this chapel above the world, and dissolves into the endless wind outside.

I close my mouth. I settle my spade-head back into the soft bed of silk-weed. The bronze wheel groans against my spine, turning, turning, turning. 

The water is warm. The piston strikes below—*th-thump*. 

I wait for the next surge. I have nowhere else to be. I am the valve that lives.
