I am settled into the third terrace down from the split stream, where the water carries the high, sharp taste of fresh calcite, just before it dulls into the lower catch-pits. 

My body follows the inner crescent of a dish no wider than my own spine. My belly rests in the silt—fine as wet flour, holding the deep heat of the mountain’s gut—while the crown of my back catches the thin, quiet overflow from the pool above. It washes over me continuously, a warm, heavy blanket that never moves and never stops. 

I am a tender of the third spillway. I have no name, for there is no air-sound here to carry one, but in the bone-map of the basin, I am the pulse that sits where the iron-ridge meets the pale lime.

My eyes are long gone, smoothed over into thick, pale skin that feels the world as a map of heat and weight. I do not miss light; light is thin and useless, stopping at the surface of things. Heat goes deep. I know the wall behind me not as a shape, but as a vast, gentle hearth. I know the water above me is slightly warmer than the water below because it has spent less time breathing its humidity into the stagnant air. 

Right now, I am still. I have been still since the last great drop fell from the lip of the siltstone wall three terraces up—a heavy, fat drop that struck the water with a sound like a wet seed dropping into mud. That was many hundreds of heartbeats ago. Around my left flank, where my skin presses hardest against the travertine rim, the white crust has begun to stitch me to the stone. It feels like a dry, tightening dust, a tiny prickle of stiffness. In another fifty heartbeats, if I do not move, the mineral will have sealed the seam between my hide and the rock. 

I do not break it yet. The stillness is good. It lets the water find its true level across my shoulder.

My right hand—heavy, short-wristed, the bones thick as river-cobbles—rests open in the shallow basin below me. My fingers are spread wide. The soft, pebbled papillae on my palms are flushed with sweet, sticky mucus, fanned out like the petals of a pale flower in the dark. 

I feel them coming before they reach me: the rice-grains. Tiny, blind crustaceans, their hundreds of microscopic legs creating a faint, static-like tickle against the water’s skin. They float down the spillway, drifting aimlessly in the warm, stagnant soup. One touches my thumb. Then three on my palm. Then a dozen, scrambling over the soft, ridged skin, drawn by the sweetness.

I do not snatch. A snatch would send a surge through the dish, washing them over the lip into the lower terraces where the old ones lie. 

Instead, I wait until my palm is heavy with them—thirty, forty tiny lives crawling in the cup of my hand. Then, with the agonizing slowness of stone cooling underground, my double-jointed fingers begin to curl inward. One joint folds, then the second, wrapping over them like a wet leaf closing. I bring the hand to the horizontal seam of my mouth, scrape the pale grains against my gum-line, and swallow. The taste is faint—salt, wet mineral, and the faint bitter tang of deep earth.

When I have eaten, I turn my attention to the work.

Two terraces up, an old one—the one whose flank is almost entirely grey now, heavy with forty years of deposited lime—tapped twice against the rim of their dish last season. *Tink. Tink.* A slow, heavy vibration that traveled down through the bedrock, shaking the fluid in my inner ear, telling me that the western seep was choking on iron-sludge.

I shift my weight. 

*Snap.* 

The thin crust of calcite along my flank breaks with that sharp, dry sound, like a dead twig snapping under water. The sudden freedom feels cool for a fraction of a second before the warm sheet of runoff fills the gap again, washing away the chalky dust.

I lift my left arm. It is heavy, dense with bone meant to anchor, not to float. I bring my thumb-nail—thick, broad, stained the color of dark rust—to the small notch in the rimstone right beside my chin. 

Over the last ten years, the water has been building a tiny lip here, a pale ridge of carbonate no thicker than a fingernail, but enough to turn three drops out of every hundred away from my basin and toward the eastern drop. If it continues, the eastern basin will drown its rice-crustaceans, and my own dish will grow cool and stagnant at the bottom.

I press the edge of my spade-nail into the soft, young stone. 

It does not give easily. Travertine is patient, but my nail is harder, fed by a lifetime of drinking this same mineral soup. I apply a slow, heavy pressure, leaning the weight of my dense shoulders behind my wrist. 

*Grit.*

A tiny flake of white stone comes away, floating down into the silt at my belly. I scrape again. *Grit.* A second flake. 

I work for hours, or perhaps days—time here is not measured by the grey gloom above, which never changes, but by the slow, rhythmic ache in the muscles of my forearm. I scrape until the notch is smooth again, until the water sheet over my chin thickens by the depth of a single drop, carrying its tiny fraction of heat back into my dish.

When it is done, I drop my hand. I press my lower jaw flat against the wet, curved stone of the rim, opening my chest to the rock.

I strike the stone once with the flat of my spade-nail. 

*Tink.*

The sound is small in the air, swallowed instantly by the humid, heavy sigh of the weeping wall. But through the stone, it is huge. It travels down through the dense travertine, through the wet siltstone, down to the lower basin where the oldest of us rests near the anchored hammerstone.

I wait. Three hundred heartbeats pass. The water trickles over my back, steady, warm, eternal.

Then, deep in the bedrock, a response rises. A single, faint, low-frequency thrum. Not a tap, but a heavy, deliberate shift of a ancient body breaking its crust against the floor of the deepest pool. 

*I am here,* the stone says. *The water is still warm.*

I curl my tail around the inner curve of my basin. I open my palms in the shallow spillway, letting the sweet mucus rise to the skin. I close my nostrils against the damp air, breathe the oxygen dissolved in the warm film that washes over my flanks, and let the white dust begin to build along my side once more.
