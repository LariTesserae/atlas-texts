At first, there is no body. There is only the acoustics.

In a room made entirely of iron and filled with heavy water, sound is not something you hear; it is the terrain you inhabit. The sound has shape. The six-sided floor echoes with a low, flat resonance; the ribbed barrel vault above returns a tighter, sharper ringing; the forty yards of water between them are thick with the low-frequency thrum of the copper gears turning in the dark. 

I am suspended somewhere in the middle of this shape, hanging in the greenish-black fog where the yellow sulfur light dissolves into shadow. 

I do not see the light. Vision is a thin, frail sense, useless after twenty feet and easily choked by rust-dust. My world is mapped through the skin along my flanks—a thousand microscopic pit-organs reading the pressure waves. I can feel the shape of the room by how the water rebounds off the smooth, rounded hex-bolts. I can feel the massive, slate-grey shape resting far below on the circular housing, its eight arms coiled like thick ropes around the iron. I feel its gills expand when the piston drops. 

*Ten. Eleven.*

I feel the water pull slightly backward. A mile away in sensory terms—though it is only forty yards—the pale anemones along the floor seams snap shut. A fraction of a second later:

*THUNG.*

The concussive wave hits my side like a padded iron beam, pushing me three inches to the west. It doesn't hurt. It is simply the accent mark of existence. The piston drops; the world shudders; the slow groan of the return stroke begins. 

Now, as the surge swings westward, my shape becomes clearer to me.

I am long. I am muscle and tallow-colored skin, five feet of dense, supple weight that glides through the dark without needing to splash or strike. My flanks are marked with old, rough calluses—orange-stained scars where I have scraped against corroded girders or the biting edge of a copper sprocket. My eyes are smooth, white marbles beneath a veil of milky skin, blind to the pollack that flash like silver ribbons through the lantern light, but my body knows exactly where each fish is by the tiny, frantic wake its tail leaves in the water.

I do not live down on the floor plates with the crabs, where the current drags heavy and cold. I do not hover near the grates.

I am heading up.

I sway my body in a slow, continuous *S*-curve, ascending through the thermocline where the water warms by a fraction of a degree. The yellow glow of a lantern globe passes beneath me—I register it only as a slight, tingling heat along my belly—and then I am in the upper corner, where the airless dark smells of wet verdigris, copper oxide, and ancient, water-logged friction.

Here, the great copper gear train turns. 

*Chock-chock-chock.*

To an outsider, this place would be a meat-grinder, a crushing maze of bronze and iron teeth. To me, it is a cathedral of predictable spaces. 

I slide my snout into the gap between two six-foot sprockets. I do not hesitate. I know the rhythm better than I know my own hunger. The copper tooth descends, heavy as a stone wall, pushing a pillow of high-pressure water ahead of it. I let that pillow of water nudge my head into the hollow notch between teeth, flowing through the pinch-point a split-second before the metal surfaces meet. 

*Chock.*

The teeth mesh behind my tail with a dull, wet clack that vibrates through my jaw. I am inside the gear housing now.

It is warm here. The mechanical friction of the ancient brass bushings generates a persistent, gentle heat that blooms through the water. I coil myself into the curve of the main axle housing, looping my pale, mucus-coated body over the heavy iron spindle. Another eel is already here, resting in the curve beside me—I feel the soft, familiar bump of its flank, the identical texture of its tallow skin. We do not fight. There is plenty of heat, and the current brings the pollack directly into the mesh.

I pull my head back, resting my jaw against the grooved copper wall. The iron below us groans as the external ocean presses against the outer hull, but in here, surrounded by turning metal and ringing water, everything is in its place.

I do not know what built this room. I do not know if there is an *above* where the water ends and the world becomes thin and dry. I know only the twelve-second pulse, the warmth of the axle, the taste of rust on the current, and the perfect, terrifying space between the teeth where I am entirely, effortlessly safe.
