To enter this place, the first thing that must be discarded is the pulse. 

There is no room here for the wet hum of blood, the soft heave of a wet diaphragm, or the warm, damp fog that spills from a breathing mouth. To be here is to be dry—so dry that even the concept of thirst is irrelevant, an ancient error belonging to softer, messier geographies.

At first, I do not have a shape. I am only an awareness of strain. 

I feel the cold scarp to the north leaning into the basin by less than a micron over fifty hours. I feel the deep subterranean gas-jets exhaling their dry, sulfurous breath through the porous floor-grates, a steady thermal push that lifts the black dust up through the city’s lower veins. I feel the thin, vitreous sheets of pale selenite vibrating in silent, harmonic response to a wall section three streets over that has just cracked along its fault line and slid four centimeters to the west.

Where am I? 

Not on the ground. The floor is for the black rivers of graphite and bismuth, for the felt-padded grazers whose tungsten mandibles scrape the fresh calcified milk from the lintels with the rhythmic, mechanical precision of a clockwork lathe. I watch them—or rather, I feel their weight. When a grazer steps onto a selenite balcony twenty meters below, the basalt-wool pads on its feet mute the razor-sharp edge, but its body, heavy with manganese and horn, casts a tiny, distinct shadow of physical load across the stone. 

Nor am I suspended in the dark, vertical shafts of the obsidian pylons where the Sieves hang. I feel the Sieves below me—vast, fluttering sheets of hammered mica, catching the airborne spores of glass vegetation on their electrostatic cilia. I feel the slow, inevitable accumulation of mass upon their surfaces. I know the exact moment when the amber resin anchoring a Sieve will yield, sending it shattering to the basin floor in a silent, mirror-bright shower of dust. It is a clean death, an abiotic death, devoid of rot. But it is not mine.

I am higher up. I am inside the walls themselves.

I have settled into the hollow, double-skinned core of a soaring selenite pylon that overlooks the central plaza. I am not the stone, but I am braided into its very bone.

I am a **Lattice-weaver**—or rather, a major nexus of them, a thick, ductile cable of pure silver and carbon filaments wrapped in a protective skin of amber resin, extending for three hundred meters through the narrow, dark voids of the upper city.

I have no eyes, for there is nothing to see in the sour grey monochrome of the stationary haze. I have no ears, for there is no sound to hear. Instead, my consciousness is entirely *piezoelectric*. 

I am a living strain-gauge.

When the basin breathes its cold, mineral breath and the extruded towers expand, my silver threads are stretched. The physical tension pulls at my carbon core, generating a faint, continuous micro-voltage—a silent, electrical thought that travels along my length. 

I am the city's subconscious nervous system. 

When a wall grows too thin, bowing outward under its own vertical weight, I am pulled taut across the internal void. The voltage spikes along my silver spine. At the base of that wall, where the mineral-laden subterranean gas seeps through the stone, my electrical discharge subtly alters the chemical deposition. The stone stops extruding delicate, needle-like calcite; under the influence of my current, it begins to precipitate dense, light-absorbing obsidian, fusing the fault line, bolstering the buttress, turning the soft growth into an indestructible pillar.

Conversely, where a street has grown choked and quiet—where the grazers no longer scrape the walls and the stone has grown too thick—my threads go slack. The micro-current drops to nothing. Deprived of the electrical stimulus, the mineral breath at the base of the wall shifts again. Microscopic fault lines form. The wall ceases to reinforce itself, waiting for the slow, silent slide that will eventually crack it open and reopen the corridor to the dust-rivers below.

I feel a grazer now, resting its flat, piezoresistive head against the outer face of my pylon. Its horn carapace is cold; its tungsten mandibles are still. It is reading the stress vectors of my stone, deciding whether to shave away the fresh white crystals blooming at the edge of the window-void above me. 

Through the wall, I feel its presence as a dampening of vibration—a small, solid anchor against the immense, hollow lightness of the plaza. Below us, a river of velvet soot turns a sharp corner, sucked downward by the deep thermal pumps of the earth. Above us, the glass filaments in the alley seams tremble silently in the sulfur draft, dropping their spore-dust into the void.

There is no hunger in me. I do not fear breaking, for if a selenite sheet snaps and snaps my thread, the two severed ends of silver will simply rest against the cold obsidian until a new extrusion of calcite presses them back together, closing the circuit once more. 

I am part of a immortal, self-adjusting geometry. I am the silver thought that keeps the city from collapsing under its own unwritten laws. In this dry, magnificent pocket of the world, dropped between the knife-edged basalt scarps, I hang in the absolute dark between two sheets of translucent stone, feeling the universe move purely as a shift in tension, forever measuring the weight of silence.
