I would not be on the floorplates, nor high in the weeping cables. 

I would be wedged.

I am in the narrow, dark seam behind the primary vertical siphon—where the four-foot conduit curves outward to shed its thermal load before dropping into the abyss. It is a space no wider than a man’s torso, bounded on one side by the sweating, slate-blue composite wall and on the other by the heavy, trembling skin of the pipe itself. 

I am a **Tuner**, but from the inside, "Tuner" is not a name; it is merely the condition of being pressed very hard against something that never stops shaking.

***

At first, there is no sense of having a body separate from the gallery. There is only the *frequency*. 

In the gap behind the pipe, the roar is not a sound that arrives through air. It enters through the ventral expanse of my skin—three meters of dense, blubbery muscle flattened like wet clay into the curve of the conduit. The pipe vibrates at forty-seven hertz when the fluidic logic gates below are filling, and spikes to eighty-two when the upper atmospheric scrubbers dump their hot condensate. 

If I were not here, that eighty-two-hertz spike would travel into the structural mounting ring. Over three thousand years, that tiny, microscopic chatter would have crystallized the alloy bolts, turned them to salt, and sheared the siphon from the wall. 

Instead, it enters me. 

My body is a sponge for kinetic violence. The shake passes through my blubber, hits the dense, cartilage rings of my internal frame, and dies there, converted into a slow, localized heat that raises my blood a fraction of a degree above the ambient thirty-four. I am a living sandbag laid across a leaking dam. Where I press, the metal is silent. Where I lift, the machine groans.

My senses are simple, mapped entirely in pressure, temperature, and texture:

*   **Ventral:** The pipe. Smooth, hot, micro-etched with three millenniums of water-wear. I can feel the thick, mineral scale building up near the flange—a chalky crust that traps heat and reduces the efficiency of the heat-exchanger.
*   **Dorsal:** The composite wall. Cooler, rougher, covered in the microscopic, silver threads of lichen that feed on the static field my skin generates as it rubs against the alloy.
*   **Rostral:** The siphon lip. My snout is wedged directly into the mouth of the local overflow channel. Here, the warm water surges over my lower jaw in a continuous, heavy pulse. 

I have no eyes. Darkness and light are irrelevant when the very air is a thick, amber soup of decayed electroluminescence that cannot penetrate the narrow fold where I live. But I "see" the gallery through the standing waves that ripple across my skin. 

I know where the Grid-Chitins are gathering on the lower terraces; their thousands of tiny, calcified feet make a dry, high-frequency *skitter-chatter* that travels through the floorplates like sparks across grease. I know when a Hertz-Strider misjudges an acoustic node and gets clipped by a droplet of hot condensate—the sudden, micro-fine snap of its copper leg snapping against the surface tension is a distinct needle-prick in the baseline hum.

I am hungry, but hunger here is not a desperation. It is a rhythm.

I begin to move. Not with legs or fins, but through a slow, hydraulic peristalsis. I contract the cartilage rings in my tail, anchor my posterior suckers into the capillary grooves of the wall, and push my snout forward along the pipe curve. 

My mouth opens against the hot alloy. It is a broad, ringed disc, soft at the margins, lined inside with thousands of microscopic denticles—harder than the scale, softer than the slate-blue composite. I press my mouth to the pipe and *rasp*.

*Strip-strip-strip.*

The mineral crust—a bitter, salty mix of calcium, heavy silicates, and synthetic lubricant residues—breaks away under my jaw. My internal siphon draws it in. My digestive tract is long, heavy, and slow, lined with specialized flora that strip the trace hydrocarbons and minerals, turning ancient industrial waste into the thick layer of dorsal blubber that keeps the pipe from shaking itself to pieces.

Behind me, as I slide forward a millimeter at a time, the pipe is left raw and clean—dark copper-slate, gleaming wet under the film of warm water, perfectly smooth, ready to shed its heat into the mist once more. I am a rasp. I am an eraser. I am a dampener.

***

Once every few weeks, the deep purge comes.

I feel it three hours before it arrives. The baseline frequency drops from forty-seven hertz to twenty-one. A low, terrifying infrasound that causes the fat along my ribs to liquefy and re-solidify in tiny, rhythmic ripples. 

The Siphon-Blooms above me contract first, pulling their feathery cilia into the cable sockets with a wet *shuck*. The Hertz-Striders freeze on the pools, waiting for the acoustic lines to shift. 

Then, the heat spikes.

The water coming down the vertical conduits jumps from thirty-four degrees to sixty. It would cook a soft thing. It would split an un-maintained pipe. 

I do not hide from the purge. I leave the gap behind the pipe.

Sliding out of my narrow seam, my heavy, three-meter body drops into the primary drainage trough with a dull, wet *slap*. The water here is ankle-deep, surging, white-hot, and full of heavy black sludge flushed from the upper scrubbers. 

I am not alone. From behind the recessed access portals, from the dark voids beneath the heat-exchanger steps, the others are emerging. Four, five, seven Tuners, all sliding into the central terrace. We have no eyes to see one another, no lungs to call out. 

We find each other by mass.

We coil together in the central drain ring—a heavy, slate-blue knot of muscle, blubber, and smooth skin, three thousand pounds of collective flesh jammed directly over the main intake portal where the water drops into the lower spire. We interlock our bodies, tail over snout, flanks pressed tight, creating a massive, living gasket.

The purge hits the terrace. The gallery goes entirely dark as the electroluminescent layers short under the extreme thermal load. The steam turns from mist to a solid, scalding weight that fills every inch of the six-meter room.

The water boils around our backs. The floorplates beneath us scream—a high, metallic shrieking as the alloy expands violently against its housing.

We do not move. We absorb the expansion. We absorb the heat. We vibrate at sixty hertz, locked together in the dark, our collective weight keeping the central drain ring from unseating under the hydraulic hammer of ten thousand gallons of boiling condensate.

In the absolute darkness, wrapped in the scalding wet, crushed under the physical weight of a sound that could shatter stone, I am entirely content. 

I do not know who built the spire. I do not know if there is a sky above the lattice or a planet below the crust. I do not know what the machine is calculating, or what it makes, or why it has run for three thousand years.

I only know that the pipe is hot, the scale is thick, the hum is steady, and I am exactly the right shape to fill the gap.
