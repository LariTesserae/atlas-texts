I am not standing on a floor. To stand on a floor in this place is an error of habit, a misunderstanding of how the bark holds you.

I am wedged sideways in a sixty-degree crease where two glowing amber ribs meet, forty feet above a central void that has no bottom—or rather, a bottom that bends back up to become the ceiling if you look down long enough. My heels are braced against a ridge of fresh cambium, white and glass-hard, shaved smooth just minutes ago by a Skein-Crawler whose dry, clattering footfalls are still echoing somewhere behind my left ear.

My skin is the color of pressed linen, thick and dry, slicked with a thin, hardening coat of lemon-smelling resin that I smeared across my arms before the last heat-burst. I do not wear cloth; around my hips is a stiff, cured sheet of Vellum-Skin, sloughed off by one of the bellows-creatures in the high vaults three tides ago. It smells faintly of dry cinnamon and old steam.

My eyes do not look for horizons. Horizons are a sickness of the upper world, where line goes straight until it dies. Here, if I look straight ahead along the pale, straight grain of the tendril before me, the line turns three crisp, invisible corners without ever bending, and I see the amber glint off the back of my own bare shoulder. So I keep my gaze short. I watch the grain under my hands. I watch the pores.

The air around me is ninety percent void, warm as an oven, vibrating with the continuous, deep-throated roar of the hydraulics beneath the wood. 

*Thrum-m-m-m.*

It travels through my heels, up my tibia, settling into the small of my back. It is a good pulse—steady, dense with starches. The tree is thirsty today.

Above me, wedged into the same crease, three Vellum-Skins lie flat and flush against the yellow heartwood, looking like relief carvings on a shield. Their parchment surfaces are tight, trembling. They feel the pressure wave before I do. Their slit-spiracles open with a faint, wet *shuck*, and a puff of hot steam vents out, washing over my face. It smells of cinnamon and ozone. I close my eyes—or rather, I lower the amber-tinted membranes that slide over my pupils—and let the steam seal the resin on my cheeks.

Then comes the thump.

*THUMP.*

The wood beneath me jumps half an inch. Far overhead, out in the warm middle-air, the needle-pores fire. A sudden, blinding hiss fills the vault as high-pressure mist blossoms into the void. I don't look up, but I feel the sudden shift in humidity—a cool, heavy dampness that cuts through the kiln heat for three seconds.

Out in the open air, a marble-sized sphere of water drifts past my elbow, floating sideways across the hollow, obeying the gentle, warm gravity that pulls toward the nearest thick tendril rather than down. The water sphere glimmers with the amber light of the walls, perfectly round, perfectly lazy. It strikes the crossing mast-tendril ten yards away with a sharp *clack-whack*, like a wet leather strap, shattering into a dozen smaller droplets that are instantly drunk up by the thirsty bark.

I reach into the pouch at my hip and pull out my stone.

It is small, rounded, cold compared to the wood—a piece of dark river-granite trapped in the hemp seams centuries ago by someone whose name was absorbed by the ceiling long before my mother was born. It fits perfectly into the hollow of my palm.

I strike the heartwood beside my knee.

*Tink. Tink-tink.*

The sound travels sharply through the lignified scaffold, sharp and high, cutting straight through the dull, roaring bass of the xylem tubes. I hold my breath, my ear pressed against the pale wood.

Two seconds pass. Then, coming from a wall-rib that looks to be three hundred yards away across the open void—but which I know is only a dozen strides if I take the fold through the three-right-angle seam—comes the answer.

*Tink-tink. Tink.*

*Maura is at the lower vein,* the grain tells me. *The sap is thick today. The wedge is holding.*

I slide down the crease, my bare feet finding the tiny, sharp ridges of the fresh growth without looking. Gravity shifts as I move—first dragging at my heels, then leaning me gently shoulder-first against the vertical wall as the bark’s pull takes over. Down here, near the junction of a major conductive vessel, the wood is translucent, glowing with that intense, clear amber light that has no source and casts no soft edges. Everything is sharp. Every shadow is a cut blade.

Here is my work.

A bone wedge—my uncle’s femur, ground down thirty years ago on the rough hemp fibers—is driven three inches into a hairline split where a fluid-conduit bulges under high pressure. The wood around it has already begun to react, sending out fine, glass-clear shoots of pale cellulose that wrap around the bone like tiny fingers, trying to heal the wound, trying to seal it shut.

If the wood seals it, the pressure in this rib will rise until the cambium bursts, tearing a twenty-foot hole in the wall and blinding the chamber with a jet of scorching, raw sap.

I take my heavy stone and tap the bone wedge. *Clack. Clack.*

A drop of sap, thick as honey and clear as glass, beads at the tip of the bone and falls. It drips into the small, dried gourd tied beneath it with leaf-skin fiber. The liquid is warm, smelling intensely of crushed pine needles, wet starch, and something dark and mineral that lingers on the tongue for days.

I unknot the gourd, bring it to my mouth, and drink.

It burns going down—a rich, heavy heat that fills my chest like swallowed sunlight. My teeth are already black from a lifetime of this drink; my breath smells of the tree's deep blood. The sugar hits my veins in seconds, making my slit-pupils contract against the amber glare. I can feel the pulse of the wood matching the pulse in my neck. We are running at the same speed now.

A Skein-Crawler drops from a tendril above, landing three feet from me with a dry, clattering scrape of its spun-glass legs. It is as large as a hound, a tangled knot of bleached cordage and twitching, jointed stalks. Its mandibles—heavy, serrated shears—are caked with dried yellow paste.

It does not have eyes to see me, nor do I have any fear of it. I am too soft to eat, too quiet to matter. It tilts its head, listening to the vibration of my bone wedge, then scuttles past my shoulder, its jointed legs snapping like dry grass as it snips away a runaway, razor-thin shoot that has begun to grow toward one of the glowing light-veins. *Snip. Snip.* The hard cellulose falls into the void, bouncing off three tendrils with a sound like dropping glass.

I re-tie the gourd. I rub a fresh smear of resin across my forearms, watching the clear liquid harden into a shiny, crystalline skin under the dry, constant heat.

Someday, my eyes will cloud over completely. My joints will stiffen until I can no longer leap between the crossing tendrils or sprint along the undersides of the mast-woods when the mist blasts come. When that time comes, I will not fall into the golden haze of the floorless gulf.

I will climb up to the high crease, where the amber ribs meet at their sharpest point, right where the cambium is splitting fresh and pale every hour. I will lay my body flat against the split, wedge my bone wedge into my hands, and press my chest into the raw heartwood.

The pale shoots will come out to meet me. They will wrap around my wrists, my ribs, my knees, delicate as silk threads at first, then hardening into glass. They will drink the resin from my skin and the sap from my belly. Within a week, my bones will be part of the scaffold, helping to hold open ninety percent of empty air against the crushing weight of the miles of wood above.

And someone else—someone with linen skin and black teeth—will walk along the tendril, strike the heartwood with a river-stone, and feel my silence down in their feet, knowing the seam is tight, knowing the vault is alive, knowing the engine is turning.

I lean my head back against the glowing bark. The xylem groans beneath my ears—a deep, hydraulic sigh that rattles my jaw.

*THUMP.*

Overhead, the mist bursts again, wet and sweet in the kiln-warm air, and I wait for the water to float past my hand.
