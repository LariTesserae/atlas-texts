You would find me thirty feet up, wedged into the tight, right-angled junction where the bronze housing of Dynamo Four meets the raw basalt wall. 

It is a pocket of near-absolute stillness. The air here does not circulate; it cooks. It sits against my chest like a wet velvet weight, smelling faintly of boiled copper, ancient tallow, and the crushed-herb bitterness of bruised indigo moss.

I am a Slick-Skinner, though I have no name for myself that requires a throat to shape. If you were to look up from the iron catwalks below—through the drifting tatters of steam and the long, dripping beards of silver-moss—you might not see me at all. You would see only a pale, pearlescent curve among the dark bronze rivets, a gleam of lipid-greased skin that catches the bruised-purple glow of a heat-sink valve deep in the masonry.

I have been in this elbow of stone and metal since the third revolution of the big flywheel this morning. Or perhaps it was yesterday’s third revolution. Time here does not divide into days; there is no light to slice the hours into shapes. There is only the long, subterranean inhalation of the steam conduits, and the slow, forty-minute creep of the iron teeth turning through the woody embrace of the vascular vines.

***

My body is wedged sideways. My left shoulder presses against the sweating basalt—the diamond-trencher’s vertical grooves fit the curve of my spine like a mold. My right hip rests against the curved belly of the boiler feed-line. The metal is hot enough to scorch dry cloth, but my skin is not dry. 

The lipid coat is thick today. It oozes slow and cool from the pores along my collarbones and thighs, a thick, clear salve that smells faintly of cold fat. Where the condensation drips endlessly from the fern canopy above—*tink, tink, splash*—the water does not touch my flesh. It beads up instantly into tiny, perfect spheres that roll off my ribs and plunge silently into the indigo moss blanket below.

A Gasket-Tick, heavy with bronze-grease and flat as an iron coin, is crawling slowly up the inside of my wrist. Its six metallic legs make no sound, but I feel the tiny, pin-prick points of pressure against my arm. I do not shake it off. I keep my arm still—so still that a thick strand of black fungus has begun to span the quarter-inch gap between my knuckles and the boiler seam. 

The tick reaches the joint where the copper pipe enters the wall. It settles into the decaying flax caulking, wedging its dark carapace into the hiss of escaping vapor. I can feel the tiny reduction in the pipe’s tremor through my forearm. It is sealing the leak. Good. The steam stays inside, where the pressure belongs.

***

My jaw is pressed flat against the pipe.

This is how the world speaks to me. My ears are useless here; the humidity has thick-folded the inner passages, and the sound of the foundry is a single, smothering blanket of low-frequency murmur. But my bone—my jaw, my cheek, my collarbone, the hard crest of my pelvis—is an ear that never closes.

Through the copper, I hear the precinct’s heart.

*Thrum-m-m-m.* 

Deep in the sump, six levels down, a Sump-Eel has brushed against the intake screen. I feel its light, spine-less whip through the soles of my feet, a delicate, fluid flutter across the iron structure.

*Krr-rr-kch.*

Two hundred yards to the north, near the blind end of the trench, a vascular vine has tightened its knot around the axle of the third tension-spool. The wood is groaning against the cast iron, bark stripping away under eighty tons of patient, mechanical leverage. The friction generates a spike of heat—I feel the thermal swell blooming through the pipe against my cheek five minutes before the air temperature actually rises.

Then comes the tap.

It enters my teeth first. A sharp, metallic *ping* that travels up the copper, through the bronze jacket, straight into my upper molars.

*One.* 

A pause—long enough for three drops of water to fall from the shade-fern above onto my shoulder.

*One, two.*

It is the Old One down in the water-cisterns. Her brass thumb-ring is heavy, worn thin on the inner edge where it fits her knuckle. She is tapping from the cold-return manifold. 

She is telling me that the lime-scale in the main sump bypass has reached the thickness of a thumb. The water is running slow through the lower grates. The micro-algae are thickening, turning from gold to brown.

I do not answer immediately. To move quickly would be to burn. My heart is beating eighteen times a minute; if I jerk my arm, if I snap my head around, my core will spike, and the thick, airless heat will lock my muscles into a hard, fatal spasm.

I wait. I breathe. I let the air slide into my lungs—wet, heavy, carrying the taste of iron rust and leaf-rot—and hold it until my blood absorbs the heat.

Then, slowly, I begin the swing of my right arm.

It takes four minutes for my hand to lift from my knee. The air feels dense as warm honey, resisting the flat of my palm. My thumb, capped with its own grooved ring of cast bronze, drifts toward the steam pipe. 

I touch the metal. I do not strike it; I rest the ring against the vibration, letting the copper feel my weight first.

Then I flick the knuckle.

*Click.*

I wait five heartbeats.

*Click-click-click.*

*Clear the lime,* I tell her through the iron skeleton of the cathedral. *I will bleed the trap from above.*

***

The signal done, I slide my head back against the basalt groove. 

My eyes are open, but there is little to see. My pupils are fixed, wide, milky slits that register only the subtle shifts in the bio-luminescence below—the way the duckweed on the cisterns pulses from faint indigo to dull green as the water currents shift. I do not need light. Light is a surface distraction, a thin thing that lives on the plains above, where the air is dry and people burn themselves out in forty short, frantic years, shrieking into wind that carries their voices away before they can even be heard.

Here, nothing is lost. Every whisper of steam is kept. Every scrap of grease is drunk by the ticks. Every movement is caught by the vines and woven into the turning of the wheels.

The black fungus on my hand has reached my wrist now, a fine, velvet dust that licks at the lipid layer on my skin, finds no purchase, and sloughs off into the dark.

I close my milky eyes. The dynamo beneath my back gives a long, subsonic sigh as a heat-sink valve bleeds off three pounds of pressure into the deep stone. 

I am thirty feet up. I am part of the caulking. I am the thing that listens to the copper when the iron begins to grow old. I will stay here through this revolution, and the next, and the one after that, until the lime-scale fills the pipes, or the vines finally stop the gears, or the water softens the basalt into sand. 

There is no hurry. The century has only just begun.
