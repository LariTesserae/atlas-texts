I am sitting on a narrow, cantilevered ledge of horn-wood six hundred feet above the plaza floor, where the buttress-roots of a four-hundred-year-old square trunk anchor into the vaulted basalt overhead. 

Below me, the white magnesium light fills the cavern like a solid, cool block of glass. The gliders—soft as powdered ash—pass through the air currents in silent, parabolic arcs. I do not watch them with eyes that see color; through my quartz prisms, they are faint, drifting smudges of thermal warmth against the icy, razor-sharp geometric lattice of the suspended city.

I am not one of the Wire-Keepers who walk the high copper spans with safety harnesses of woven bronze wire, nor do I carry the gear-driven chisels of the Bark-Engineers who carve catwalks into growing timber. 

If I have a designation, it is found in the palms of my hands.

My fingers do not end in simple tips or heavy grips. They terminate in fine, brush-like arrays of bronze needles and tiny, diamond-faced rasps. My task is the *Grain-Alignment*. 

Where the heavy cast-iron brackets of the cable-car stations are bolted into the slate-bark of the iron-woods, the living timber grows against the unyielding metal under tremendous pressure. Over decades, if left unchecked, the compressed wood fibers will twist, creating internal shear-lines where dry static potential accumulates dangerously before discharging in violent, blinding arcs of blue light that can crack a quartz eye-plate or shatter a bronze joint.

I feel these places long before they spark. 

I press my left palm against the horn-wood beam beneath my seat. I do not listen with ears; my internal piezoelectric crystal catches the micro-tremors running through the continuous wooden skeleton of the tier. Three miles away, the great piston-compressor gives its soft, muffled thud—*thrum, thrum, thrum*—a heartbeat absorbed and softened by millions of tons of dry, silent timber. But closer, just forty yards up the trunk where a heavy bronze suspension cable anchors into a knot of cured grain, I feel a different frequency: a high, thin *skree* of strain, like a taut wire pulled across dry slate.

I shift my weight. My bronze knee-joints rotate in their shallow sockets with a smooth, silent glide, the thin film of synthetic lipid preventing any friction. I move up the trunk along a ladder of riveted iron staples, my feet making no sound on the dry cellulose substrate. The air here is biting and pure, thick with the scent of freshly shaved cedar, dry quartz dust, and the sharp, metallic tang of hot machine oil from the distribution banks above.

When I reach the anchor-plate, I lean close. Through my quartz prisms, the knot of timber glows with an intense, burning gold—a dense knot of static potential trapped in twisted grain. 

I do not use heat or water to coax the wood; there is no water in this world, and heat would volatilize the ethers within my own frame. Instead, I unclip my diamond rasp. With small, methodical strokes, I score thin, geometric relief-grooves along the bark’s grain lines, following the natural stress-vectors of the tree. With every stroke, a tiny puff of pale, fragrant terpene mist escapes the cut, instantly evaporating into the crisp air and leaving behind a fine, powdery crust of amber resin that seals the cut as soon as it is made.

As the grain yields and settles against the bronze bracket, the trapped static potential discharges harmlessly through the needle-arrays on my palm. The energy floods up through my bronze arm-tubes, charging the silver-plated copper mesh inside my chest. My internal magnetic pulse-pump hums a fraction faster, its low whine rising in pitch as it circulates the fresh charge through my volatile ether lines. The burning gold light in my vision fades to a soft, even amber. The timber is at peace again. The shear is gone.

I remain by the bracket for a long time, resting my head against the slate-bark. 

My frame is older than most in this tier. I can feel the slow, beautiful calcification taking hold in my lower limbs. My left shank, carved two centuries ago from a young iron-wood shoot, has absorbed so much quartz dust and mineral lipid over the decades that it no longer flexes with the light elasticity of youth; it has grown dense, heavy, and resonant as marble. In another fifty years, perhaps eighty, I will no longer climb the staples to reach the high anchor-plates. 

When that time comes, I will walk down to the lowest plaza, where the floor meets the massive, un-eroded basalt floor of the cavern. I will choose a quiet corner near a primary distribution bus, press my soles into the cellulose soil, and allow fresh resin to flow from my ankles into the horn-wood floor, anchoring my frame forever to the structural skeleton of the world. My quartz eyes will dim to a soft, permanent amber, and my internal pump will finally slow its whine, leaving me as a silent, luminous pillar—a supporting strut for the next generation of stewards.

There is no dread in this. It is simply the geometry of things.

A pulse of light flashes across the open gulf from a high walkway half a mile away. It is a Wire-Keeper, her quartz brow flickering in rapid, rhythmic bursts of brilliant white and deep amber: *Section Seven cleared. Static flux nominal.*

I lift my hand, modulating the small magnesium filament behind my own brow-plate, and flash back: *Knot relieved. The timber holds.*

The light fades. She turns and moves away along the high cable, her long, long-limbed silhouette dark and sharp against the perpetual daylight of the cavern ceiling.

I sit back on my ledge of horn-wood, my bronze brushes resting on my knees, feeling the deep, muffled thrum of the distant compressor traveling up through the floor, through my calcifying timber bones, and into the quiet ether of my heart. The air is dry. The light is white. The stone has no curves. And the great machine of timber and iron runs on, silent and complete, deep beneath the skin of a world that never knew the rain.
