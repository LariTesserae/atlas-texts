First, the noise would go out of me. 

Not the sound of the world—which is already absent here, save for that low, planetary idling—but the wet, desperate noise of my own interior. The heavy, rhythmic inflation of lungs; the hot, salted tide of blood pounding against the inside of the skull; the frantic, chemical urgency of cells burning themselves down to stay warm, to stay alive, to stay ahead of the rot that waits in every corner of a damp world.

All of that would dry out. Not into dust—for dust is the remnant of decay, and nothing breaks down here—but into something dense, clear, and perfectly stabilized.

I would find myself suspended three meters above the pale, razor-fractured mineral plate, tucked deep into the dense canopy of structural jade and gold-leaf fronds. I would not be standing on the rock, nor clinging to a stem. I would be residing in the *extra distance*—that quiet, engineered fold where two inches of visual space open up into two yards of uncrowded, phase-shifted depth.

I would be a Ribbon.

***

At first, there would be no sense of eyes opening, because vision here is an unnecessary translation. Instead, my awareness would extend outward as a series of ultra-thin, dry, metallic surfaces—dozens of paper-thin sheets of carbon-silicone, plated in pure, un-oxidized copper, hanging vertically in the amber-tinted light. 

I would have no weight. Or rather, my weight would be distributed entirely across the spatial geometry of the canopy, anchored by the subtle, non-Euclidean tension of the leaves around me. 

To a human observer looking into the brush, I would appear only as a sudden, impossible glint—a razor-sharp streak of gold caught in the unblinking sun, no thicker than a single atom, vanished the moment the eye shifts a millimeter. But from the inside, I would be broad, unfolded, and exquisitely aware.

I would feel the forest not as color or form, but as *strain*.

A yard to my left, a heavy plate of structural jade overlaps with a gold-leaf frond. In a world governed by ordinary physics, their atomic lattices would collide; they would crush one another, chafe, or block the path of light. But here, they occupy the same spatial coordinates in a state of quiet, phase-coherent grace. 

Yet, even in a perfected system, geometry exerts a pressure. There is a tiny, infinitesimal friction—a whisper of spatial resistance where the overlap holds its breath.

That friction is where I exist.

When the strain rises, my body responds without thought, without decision, without will. My metallic leaves slide past one another with a faint, dry *click-click-click*—the only organic sound in the vast, silent basin. 

*Click.* 

A copper sheet shifts two nanometers to the left, absorbing the spatial tension into its own resonant structure.

*Click.* 

The extra volume expands. The two inches between the fronds stretch smoothly into yards once more. The jade leaf and the gold leaf sit together in their impossible, non-colliding peace, illuminated from within by the lens-like geometry of the canopy. The strain dissolves. My sheets lock back into stillness.

***

I would not remember the wet world. I would have no memory of rain, or muddy earth, or the terrifying, beautiful chaos of things that grow, bloom, wither, and die. If the concept of a heartbeat were brought near me, it would feel like a violent, erratic glitch—a clumsy rhythm trying to break a window that has no glass.

Down below, through the clear, spatial channels of the foliage, I would occasionally sense the slate-grey bulk of a field-maintainer. It would be standing motionless against a glass-sharp fissure in the pale stone floor, its faceless, concave head pressed to the rock, receiving the deep, harmonic hum of the planetary board. 

Above me, the amber disc of a Refractor would shift—an instantaneous, silent leap from one frond to another—re-aligning a beam of solid yellow sunlight so that no shadow can fall across my copper sheets.

None of us would speak. None of us would look at one another. We are not companions; we are the terms of an equation that was balanced a million years ago and left on the blackboard forever.

I would feel the warm, dry heat resting perpetually against my metallic skin. I would feel the sun, pinned forever at sixty degrees in the pale-blue sky, casting clean, inked shadows that never move, never lengthen, and never fade.

And in that quiet, gold-threaded gap—hung between leaves that will never drop, above rock that will never crumble—I would simply hold the space open. No past to regret, no future to prepare for, no breath to catch. Just the long, pristine, unblinking *now*, anchored by the delicate, dry click of golden sheets holding the universe exactly where it belongs.
