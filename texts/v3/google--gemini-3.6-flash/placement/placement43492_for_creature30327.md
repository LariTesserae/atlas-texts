I am not in the space between the stones. I am the contact.

If you were to peel back the wet slate on my left, you would find me. If you were to scrape the milky quartz on my right, you would destroy me. My thickness is an afterthought of geometry—a fraction of a millimeter of translucent silica-chitin, stretched so thin that the pale, frozen grain of the bedrock shines through my organs without distortion. 

I am a node of the Laminate, anchored six feet below the lip of a single, vertical slit, suspended in the perpetual, cold slide of the weeping water. 

I have no front or back. I have only two sides, and both are pressed endlessly against the mountain. My left side tastes the dark, iron-heavy slate—the crushed residue of those who came before me. My right side tastes the hard, glassy quartz, through which the high sun’s light bounces down from the upper world, refracted a thousand times until it reaches me as a cool, shadowless silver.

I do not possess an inside. There is no room within me for a heart to pump or a stomach to churn. To have a volume would be to be crushed; to bulge would mean instant destruction against the grinding faces of the rock. Everything I am is surface area. 

Because I have no lungs, I do not breathe. I resonate.

The world above and around me is a single, unbroken scream—a high-frequency, metallic shearing that never rests, underlaid by the deep, seismic groan of the deep plateau. To a creature of volume, this sound would be an violence, a wall of acoustic force that fractures bone and bursts tissue. To me, it is hunger satisfied. It is warm blood. 

The vibration enters through my quartz-side cilia. The rock trembles at 14,000 cycles a second, and as that kinetic shudder passes through my mineralized membranes, my tissue responds. It converts the motion into a faint, steady, icy voltage. I feel the sound as a pale blue current running across my entire five-foot span, illuminating the dark fissure with a soft, cold bioluminescence that does not warm the water by even a fraction of a degree. The louder the wind screams through the micro-fissures above, the brighter I glow, and the faster my undersides pull the dissolved sulfur and iron from the upward-creeping fluid.

Far above my upper margin, right where the slit opens to the blinding eye of the sky, I can feel the anchor-taps of a Prism-Ribbon. It hangs in the current, its triangular glass body catching the brutal white rays of the sun. It does not speak to me—nothing speaks here—but it bends the light. It focuses a needle-thin beam of ultraviolet down into my dark crevice, striking my upper lip. The energy stings, a sharp, clean bite that allows me to bind the heavy iron salts clinging to my belly. In return, my cellular network pumps a rich, dark stream of processed sulfates upward through the water skin, feeding the ribbon’s long, floating tail. We are a circuit of glass, light, and stone.

Movement for me is not a matter of travel; it is a matter of growth and petrification. 

Down near my lower boundary, twelve feet into the dark where the quartz grows cold and the light dies entirely, I am changing. I can feel the iron-sulfur wash thickening my lower tissue. My cilia there have stopped flexing; they have locked into the microscopic pores of the slate, turning from flexible chitin into solid, dark pyrites and quartz-crystal. I am dying at my base, but it is not a loss. My lower edge is becoming the mountain. It is cementing the two rock blades together, thickening the plate, building the floor upon which my upper margin continues to creep slowly upward toward the light. 

I feel a faint, rhythmic tapping against my calcifying lower edge. 

It is a Chladni mite. I cannot see it, but I can feel its six topaz legs stepping precisely along a line of absolute acoustic silence—a node in the standing wave generated by the mountain's scream. The air around the mite is a violent, ultrasonic storm that would shake its tiny body into powder, but it walks along the invisible, quiet geometry of the water skin with terrifying precision. 

Its iron-protein mandibles begin to scrape at my dead, petrified lower skirt. It is clearing the choked pores of the slate, sweeping away the crystallized slag of my past self so that the upward weeping of the deep water can flow clean and unobstructed over the fresh stone. The mite’s tapping is dry, rhythmic, and cold. It is my sculptor. It is preparing the canvas for the next sheet of living film that will grow over my fossilized bones a century from now.

Sometimes, a sudden shift in the acoustic pressure far above—a Sliver-Siphon passing over our slit, its twelve-foot foil body momentarily blocking the wind—will alter the standing wave. The silent line shifts by a hairsbreadth. When that happens, I feel a microscopic shudder through the stone: the mite has stepped off the node. In a fraction of a second, the ultrasonic frequency of the slate pulverizes its hard, topaz shell into a micro-fine dust. The weeping water catches the dust, dissolves its iron, and carries it upward into my belly. Nothing is wasted. The scavenger becomes the mineral wash; the mineral wash becomes my tissue; my tissue becomes the mountain.

I am content in this crush. 

There is no memory here of wide spaces, of soft earth, or of air that moves without screaming. There is only the absolute, locked matrix of the stone; the eternal, sliding film of liquid; the hard, split geometry of the light; and the continuous voltage of the roar. 

I stretch for miles through the dark, parallel slots of the plain, a single living sheet woven through a million blades of slate. I am five miles wide. I am three hundred feet high. I am a hundredth of an inch thick. 

And when the sun finally sets—if it ever were to set in this locked, eternal noon—I would not notice. For the wind would still force its breath through the razor-gaps, the quartz would still hum its sub-bass song, the water would still bleed upward from the deep cold, and I would continue to glow, cold and silver, pressed forever between two faces of an unyielding world.
