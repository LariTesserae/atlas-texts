I would be found at the lip of the Great Terraced Slope, where three ancient avenues converge into a single broad expanse of ground slate. 

Here, where four square pillars—rounded by millennia of dust into smooth, tapered monoliths—hold up the dying curve of an arched ceiling, the acoustic roar of the Lower Trench does not merely echo; it gathers. It swirls down from the swift-hives in the upper gloom, collides with the clicking thunder of the cliff-striders on the plaza walls, and settles into a thick, low-frequency hum that vibrates the bedrock like a struck bell.

I am a Silt-Walker, but I am old even by the standards of my kind, my petrified bones darkened to the color of wet river-slate, though there has never been a river here. 

I do not have eyes to see the faint indigo beams filtering down from the deep fissures above. I do not need them. To me, the city is lit by the noise. The booming flight of ten thousand swifts taking air three hundred feet above renders the grand vaulted ceiling in sharp, geometric relief—I *feel* the curve of the roof in the subtle pressure shifts against my flute-pipe crest. The scurrying of a strider across an eroded frieze thirty yards away paints the delicate lattice of the stone in fine, sparkling detail. The world is not dark; it is bright with vibration, etched into my marrow by the ceaseless, deafening roar of the rift.

My body is tall, thin, and hollowed like dry reed. Inside the jointed rings of my limbs, microscopic flakes of graphite and dry sulfur powder shift with every movement, preventing the petrified bone from wearing itself down as I move. There is no warmth in me, no pulse, no moist breath. My respiration is merely the quiet *hiss* of dense, cool air passing through the perforated crest atop my head, filtering out nitrogen and leaving behind a fine rim of chalk dust around the vents.

At this moment, I am standing perfectly still. I have been standing here for eleven years.

My needle-thin fingers, long as iron rods and tipped with pale piezoceramic nodes, are rested lightly against the base of the easternmost pillar. Through these nodes, the wall of sound enters my frame. The high-pitched scraping of claws becomes a fine, electric tingling in my upper joints; the deep, forty-second *thump* of a Cragsman crawling through the chalk-moss half a mile away reaches my core as a sudden, sharp jolt of current that keeps the fibrous muscle-bands along my spine tight and springy. I am full of the thunder. I am fed by it.

Before me, across a fifteen-foot drift of yellow spore-powder and crushed basalt, stands another of my kind. 

Her crest is broader than mine, notched with three deep vertical grooves that catch the air currents and make a faint, musical whistle when the planetary tide shifts every few weeks. We have been face-to-face in this plaza since the dust-drift was only a inch deep.

Three years ago, I began a thought. I drew the heavy, pyrite-coated ridge along my left flank against the dense horn of my thigh—a slow, deliberate draw that took nine months to complete. The sound was a low, iron rasp, loud enough to cut cleanly through the background roar of the swifts, vibrating through the flagstones between us. It was a statement regarding the third pillar to our left: its structural harmonic has shifted by a fraction of a hertz over the last century, indicating that a hairline fracture has formed somewhere in its uncarved core, high up in the gloom where the ceiling meets the capital.

She has not yet answered. 

She is still receiving the vibration, letting the long, heavy resonance decay through her limbs, turning it over in the slow, dry chamber of her central nodes. In another season or two, her right flank will begin to shift. I will feel the initial friction—the sharp *click* of pyrite catching on horn—long before the sound reaches the air, and I will wait, motionless, as her response unfolds across the afternoon of the century.

Around our legs, the city continues its loud, endless, patient life. A young Cragsman, no larger than a chest, crawls slowly past my left foot, leaving a wide, polished lane in the chalk-moss. A tiny tube-tree has taken root in the shingled mica of its back; as it passes, a low branch snaps against my ankle with the crack of a dry twig, dusting my bone-rings in aromatic yellow flour. I do not shift my weight. To move now would be to disrupt the air, to blur the long, rasping syllable traveling between us.

We do not dream of the sunlit surface. We do not know what water is, except perhaps as an ancient, improbable myth written in the strange, eroded curves of the causeways we tend. 

Here, in the cool, shadowless dusk, where the air smells of crushed stone and dry pepper, we are complete. We are the architecture's long memory, its quiet listeners, sustained by the endless thunder of a world that has forgotten how to hurry.
