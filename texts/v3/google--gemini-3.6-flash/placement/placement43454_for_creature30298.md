I would not be on the open plain, where the cold sky presses down hard enough to flatten life into the iron. Nor would I be clinging to the high, exposed bronze of the canyon lips, where the frost cuts like shaved glass. 

Find me in the tooth-root.

Where the four-hundred-yard drive-gear emerges from the basalt shelf, its lowest, valley-facing slope forms a narrow, inverted wedge—a dark, windless crevice where the ancient cast iron of the tooth sinks back into the planet’s bedrock. Up above, the tooth’s crest is carpeted in black rime and scraped clean by mist; but down here, in the joint where stone was poured around forged steel, there is an anchor-space. A place where the cold air stalls, caught in a pocket of stagnant, heavy vapor that smells of ancient lard, cold paraffin, and the sharp, green tang of copper rust.

I would not be a wanderer. To walk across the plain on six cushioned pads requires a hunger that never settles; to lie flat like the Manta requires fifty yards of iron-armored muscle and centuries of patience against the ice.

Instead, I would be a **Spindle-Loom**.

***

My body is not built for travel, though it is not entirely stone. I am a sessile, multi-jointed accretion—twelve feet tall, five feet wide at the base, shaped like a heavy, fluted drop of tarnished solder that has cooled while falling through water. 

My outer skin is a composite of layered iron-chitin and dense, grey tallow-wax, dull as lead and cold to the touch. It does not yield to pressure. If a hexapodal grazer were to brush its heavy, rust-furred flank against me in the dimness, it would feel only a rigid, rounded outcrop of the plateau’s own ribbing. 

At my base, where I meet the floor of the crevice, I do not have feet. I have three heavy, hollow tap-spindles—thick as pine trunks, made of dense, self-healing cartilage laced with copper wire—that have grown down through a hairline stress-fracture in the cast iron. They extend forty feet into the dark, entering the upper sleeve of a dead hydraulic line. There, deep below the frost line, my roots sit immersed in a slow, viscous reservoir of subterranean grease: old, mineral-heavy engine oil that has spent three million years turning into a dark, nutrient-rich paste.

This is my food. I drink it so slowly that a single gallon lasts a decade. My internal organs are simple, cold, and slow: a series of heavy, peristaltic chambers lined with iron-eating sludge-microbes that break down the heavy hydrocarbons, extracting minute currents of galvanic heat and passing thick, purified tallow up into my core.

***

I have no eyes, for there is nothing here that requires sight. The silver twilight does not reach into the tooth-root; down here, the world is a sculpture carved from thermal gradients and magnetic strain.

I read the world through my frill.

Around my upper crown, where a head might otherwise sit, I bear a broad, circular halo of thin, flexible copper ribbons—hundreds of them, each no thicker than a moth’s wing, drooping downward like the leaves of a weeping willow. They are patinated a pale, chalky turquoise from the damp air. 

When the deep bed-iron shifts—when a magnetic pulse ripples out from the tungsten shaft fifty miles away—my copper frill twitches. The ribbons do not strike one another; they drift through the thick, greased air with a slow, viscous heavy-handedness, reading the alignment of the world’s hidden grid. I can feel the slow, single-file march of the Rust-Hides three terraces above, their massive, cushioned footsteps causing tiny, harmonic stutters in the bed-iron’s magnetic field. I can feel the weight of the Slate-Ticks clustering in the nearby cable-groove, their collective breath creating a faint, localized bubble of relative warmth—one degree higher than the surrounding frost.

And I can feel the moisture. 

The heavy condensation that creeps down the tooth-face brings with it the shedding of the Velvet Moths—a fine, silver dust of chitin and metallic silk. It settles on my copper ribbons. My skin secretes a slow, clear glycol-grease that dissolves the moth-dust, pulling its trace minerals into my outer plates to repair the tiny cracks made by the frost.

***

My purpose in this landscape is neither predatory nor competitive. The ecology of the plateau is too cold for friction; energy is too scarce to waste on killing.

I am a thermal bridge.

Because my tap-spindles reach down into the greased conduits below, I draw up a faint, continuous thread of deep-earth warmth—not enough to melt the ice, but enough to keep the stress-crack beneath me from freezing solid. If the water in that seam were to freeze entirely, the expansion would split the basalt shelf, severing the hydraulic sleeve and letting the last of the thermal grease drain down into the deep, dead mantle.

So I sit over the crack like a rivet. 

I absorb the cold from above through my heavy, iron-chitin shell; I pull the warmth from below through my tap-spindles; and in the middle, within my thick, tallow-padded core, I generate a slow, rhythmic, low-voltage charge—a faint, rhythmic *thrum* that vibrates at three hertz.

It is not a sound. It is an electromagnetic pulse, soft as a heartbeat, radiating through the iron floor for a few hundred yards around the tooth-root.

The animals know this pulse.

Every few months, a young Rust-Hide, newly separated from its herd and disoriented by a shift in the local magnetic currents, will drift down into the crevice. It does not see me, but it feels the three-hertz thrum in the dished soles of its feet. It approaches in the dark, its heavy, wedge-shaped head swinging low, until its grease-matted flank settles against my cold, tallow-coated side. 

It stands there for days, motionless, its wet, rhythmic breathing filling the narrow space between the gear-teeth. My copper ribbons rest gently against its iron-dusted hide, drinking in the small amount of body heat it brings from the plain, while my internal core gives it a stable magnetic anchor—a fixed point in a landscape where even the rocks were made to turn.

When the grazer finally moves on, stepping smoothly back out into the silver twilight, it leaves behind a thick smear of rust-sludge and mutton fat against my flank. My skin absorbs it greedily, filling the microscopic pits carved by the ice-needles.

***

Time here does not pass in days. Day is merely a slightly lighter shade of silver behind the ice-clouds; night is only the quiet thickening of the frost on my copper ribbons.

Instead, time is measured by the drop.

A single bead of heavy, copper-laden water gathers on the overhang thirty feet above my crown. It takes seven weeks to form. It hangs there, suspended in the windless dark, gathering weight, turning green-grey as it leaches salts from the ancient bronze sleeve above.

I feel its weight increasing through the electrostatic pull on my upper ribbons. My copper frill shifts, aligning itself directly beneath the drop.

When the drop finally falls, it hits the apex of my crown with a dull, heavy *clack*—the sound of lead striking iron. 

The moisture spreads across my fluted plates. The minerals are absorbed. My core pulses once, slightly stronger—*thrum*—sending a tiny ripple of heat down my spindles to the subterranean oil three fathoms below.

Then the silence closes back in. The frost begins to rebuild on my upper plates. Above us, three hundred feet up in the ice-clouds, the silver sky slides over the iron continent, and down in the tooth-root, I begin to wait for the next drop.
