I am not down in the warm, roaring darkness of the pump-rooms, nor am I gliding blind and weightless in the high void with the Skimmers. 

I am suspended in the middle air, where the stone gives up trying to be a cliff and becomes a town, and where the town gives up trying to be human and becomes a reef.

You would find me perched on a narrow, cantilevered grating off the Seventh Tier—a damp iron shelf three feet wide, bolted to the basalt ninety feet above the fog-floor and three thousand feet above the hidden earth. My back is pressed against the cold, sweating flank of the mountain; my knees are pulled up toward my chest to keep them out of the wet draft that rises perpetually from the abyss below.

I am an old Cable-Greaser, though my climbing days are behind me. My lungs are too thick now for the long, swaying ascents out into the blank grey sky, but my eyes and my hands are still accurate. They call me a **Joint-Watcher**. 

I do not move much. To move quickly up here is to waste heat, and heat is the only currency that matters. 

I wear a coat of heavy, tallow-cured canvas that has hardened over forty winters into an armor as stiff as boiled horn. It does not smell of sheep or loom anymore; it smells of the mountain—coal oil, cold slate, wet fern, and the rancid, sweet grease we render from moth-fat to dress the iron. My boots are laced tight with tarred hemp, their soles studded with soft copper rivets that grip the wet, algae-slicked iron gantries without sparking. 

Over my face rests my respirator—a heavy brass cup lined with boiled wool and packed with dried lavender-moss from the upper flumes. It settles against my cheekbones like an iron hand. With every slow, deep inhalation, the wool catches the icy spray of the mist and warms it with the heat of my own face; with every exhalation, a faint, white puff of moisture escapes through the bronze flutter-valve with a quiet, rhythmic *click-shhh*. 

*Click-shhh.* 
*Click-shhh.*

It matches the deep, subterranean stroke of the steam pumps pulsing through the stone behind my spine. *Thrum... thrum... thrum.* The mountain vibrates against my shoulder blades like a sleeping iron stove.

In my lap lies my basket of bone scrapers, a horn cup of grease, and three short brass rods. This is my domain: the three-way junction where the Tier Seven cable-anchor plunges deep into the rock, where a wooden flume spills its excess condensate in a thin, shimmering ribbon, and where a cluster of dark copper steam pipes passes through a masonry arch.

Around me, the world is moving, but so slowly it feels like standing still.

To my left, a bed of dark blue water-cress grows thick in the leak-drip of the wooden flume. Among the dark leaves, three glass-salamanders are motionless, their skin so clear I can see the tiny, ruby-red flicker of their hearts beating in time with one another. They do not mind me. I am too large to be food and too still to be a threat. Every few minutes, when a burst of warm steam vents from the copper pipe above us, a cloud of tiny midges drifts down, and the salamanders’ tongues strike out—a sudden, wet *flick* against the iron—before they go motionless again, glistening like carved ice.

On the iron beam beneath my left heel, two iron-lice are grazing. They are flat, grey, and broad as my palm. They move with a steady, mechanical patience, their shell-edges scraping against the orange crust of rust, eating away the scale until the dark metal beneath shines wet and oily. One of them crawls slowly over the toe of my boot. I do not kick it away. It leaves a smear of its protective grease across the worn leather, sealing a seam where the thread had begun to rot. We have an understanding, the louse and I: I do not step on it, and it keeps my boots from rusting into the gantry.

My work is quiet. Every hour or so, I take one of my brass rods and press it against the side of the copper steam pipe. I put my gloved thumb over the top of the rod and press my ear against my thumb, listening.

Through the metal, the mountain speaks to me in a hundred different voices. I hear the high, thin whistle of a relief valve sticking on Tier Twelve; I hear the deep, gargling rush of condensate flowing down through the flumes toward the gardens; I hear the slow, rhythmic *clack-clack-clack* of an ore basket moving along the high cableway three hundred yards out in the white fog, invisible to my eyes. 

If the pitch changes—if the whistle becomes a shriek, or the gargle dries into a rattle—I take my heavy wooden mallet and strike the pipe three times. *Tap. Tap. Tap.* The sound travels down the copper line, through the brickwork, through the moss, until a Valve-Herder three levels below hears the vibration in his teeth and adjusts a wrench.

Otherwise, I sit. I watch the mist drift through the iron trusses. 

Sometimes, late in the grey afternoon—if there is an afternoon—the fog thins just enough for the sky above to turn from graphite to the soft, glowing color of an oyster shell. When that happens, the Gauze-Skimmers descend from the high peaks. 

I hear them before I see them: a soft, wet *whoosh-whoosh* of vast, skin-thin wings cutting the dense air. One sweeps past my shelf, no more than twenty feet away. Its wingspan is as wide as a tenement room, a pale, translucent grey membrane stretched over thin cartilage ribs, dripping with condensed cloud. Its black, lidless eye catches the dim amber glow of the gas lamp hanging above my head. It does not look at me with curiosity or fear; to the Skimmer, I am merely another knob of rust on the side of the spire, a small, dark bump that breathes steam and smells of coal oil.

It tilts a wing, catches the warm thermal rising from the copper steam pipes, and sails silently back out into the grey abyss, disappearing as if it had dissolved into the water.

I reach down into my pouch, pull out a piece of dried water-cress tuber—bitter, cold, tasting of slate and iron—and slip it under the edge of my respirator. I chew slowly, feeling the crunch of the dense root, listening to the drip of the flume, the *click-shhh* of my valve, and the deep, eternal heartbeat of the mountain.

I am forty-eight years old, which is old for a man who lives on the gantries. My skin is the color of wet lime; my hands are permanently stained with orange rust-dust and black grease; my hearing is tuned only to the hum of iron and the fall of water. 

I do not wonder what lies below the clouds, five miles down, where they say the sun shines on dry earth and the air is thin and fast. Earth that does not hum sounds dangerous. Air that does not drip sounds thirsty. 

Up here, everything is held. The rock holds the iron; the iron holds the moss; the moss holds the water; and the water holds us all in its cold, soft, eternal grey hands. I pull my oilcloth coat tighter around my neck, rest my head against the cold basalt, and wait for the next stroke of the pump.
