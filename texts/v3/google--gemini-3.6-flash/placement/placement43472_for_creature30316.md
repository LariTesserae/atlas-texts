I am not standing on the basalt floor, where the water glides in quiet sheets, nor am I floating in the quiet amber ring at the center. 

I am twenty-five feet above the flooded floor, suspended in the bright, wet middle-air. My five stilt-legs are locked onto a swaying bridge of vulcanized root-wood—a corridor that did not exist when the sun reached its zenith, and will be slurry in the shallows before the afternoon light begins to tilt.

I am a **Siphon-Walker**, but to call myself an individual is to misunderstand how the air works here. I am a pressure-gauge with limbs. A knot of dense, pale cartilage and damp fiber that the basin grew so its spires wouldn’t tear themselves apart.

I have no eyes, and I do not miss them. The daylight here is too sharp, too broken by sudden sprays of rainbow-mist and the white flash of snapping wood. Instead, I see through the long, twin antennae extending from my crest. They do not hear sound; they read the frequency of tension. 

Through my feet, I feel the whole cathedral precinct shivering. To my left, a three-hundred-foot vault of pale timber is leaning four degrees too far east because a cluster of Thrummers injected a sequence of dense, heavy blue-leaf code into its upper arch too quickly. The wood is groaning—a long, wet, fibrous scream that travels down the pylon into the root-mat under my serrated foot-discs.

I move.

Movement for me is not a decision; it is a falling into equilibrium. I do not walk so much as I spring, my five long joints unfolding in a violent, silent rhythm. I cross thirty paces of trembling root-mat in two strides, my lower claws biting deep into the wet cambium, releasing a bitter spurt of raw, sweet sap that smells of crushed fern and copper.

I reach the primary pylon—a column as wide as a cistern, hot to the touch, humming like a trapped hive. Inside it, pressurized fluid is roaring upward at fifty feet per second, driving fresh shoots toward the light. If the pressure isn't checked, the cambium valve at the top will burst before the wood hardens, and the entire arch will drop like a slaughtered beast into the shallows.

I wrap my forelimbs around the pylon. 

My body is long and narrow, white as peeled willow, designed to offer no resistance to the continuous vents of hot steam that burst around me. I press the vertical slit of my torso flat against the bark. I inhale—deeply, wetly—drawing the ozone-heavy vapor straight into my central chamber. 

Then, I constrict.

My forelimbs do not possess soft muscle; they are braided cords of high-tensile sinew. I lean my full twelve-foot frame into the node, driving my body-weight into a flex point in the bark. 

I feel the shift instantly. Deep within the pylon’s vascular core, the main sap-conduit narrows under my strain. The frantic, high-pitched whistling of the fluid drops an octave, then two. The violent shudder in the floor beneath me smooths into a long, rhythmic throbbing. High above, where the Thrummers are still diving through the mist like sparks of green glass, the towering arch stops its fatal leaning. Its wood stiffens, hardens, and locks into place with a sound like a ship's timber settling in a heavy swell.

Through the bark, I feel a pulse from the center of the basin—a cold, clear rippling that travels up through the deep basalt channels from the Cores. 

It is a new equation. A fresh lattice has just dissolved in the glass cylinder three hundred yards away. The math is moving through the sap: *Dissolve the eastern archway. Reclaim forty tons of cellulose. Build a chimney.*

Beneath my feet, the root-mat gives a sickening, tearing *pop*. The anchors are melting. The Spindle-Mites below are already letting go, firing their elastic grey lines, dropping like fat fruit into the warm water to hunt for the next rising shoot.

I do not jump yet. I hold the pylon for three seconds more, letting the last of the high-pressure fluid bleed off into a side-vent, which erupts beside me in a blinding geyser of warm mist. The moisture coats my pale skin, washing away the grit of old bark.

The floor drops out from under me. The whole thirty-foot span of root-wood turns to wet, heavy rags and plummets into the shallows with a sound like a thunderclap, sending a curtain of warm spray all the way to the outer basalt wall.

I am already in the air. 

I do not look down at the Slate-Slugs or the Silica Crayfish already darting toward the debris with their scissor-mandibles open. I do not look up at the sun. 

My forward claw catches the raw, green tip of a spire that just speared through the foam four seconds ago. It is soft, hot, and growing so fast I can feel the cellulose expanding beneath my grip, pushing me three inches higher with every beat of my heart.

I lock my serrated discs into the fresh green skin. My antennae whip forward, catching the thermal draft of a new steam vent opening fifty paces north.

The city is changing its mind again. I adjust my weight, flex my long, pale joints, and wait for the pulse.
