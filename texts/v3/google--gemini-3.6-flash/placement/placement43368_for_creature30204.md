I am hanging upside down beneath the belly of an ancient, twisted sky-bridge, three hundred feet above the floor of the central plaza. 

To look at me from across the expanse, if you could see through the shivering haze of warm mist, I would appear like nothing more than a gnarled knot on the underside of the vine—a long, grey, jointed shadow folded back against the river-silt wood. But I am not still. My long, five-jointed toes are working, thousands of microscopic cilia wet-snapping against the cambium with a rhythmic *shuck-shuck-shuck* as I stride smoothly backward over the void.

I am a Dweller in the heavy middle of my life. My skin has lost the soft, pithy pale of the Pod-Born, but it has not yet hardened into the chalky, immovable cork of the Elders. I am flexible, dense, and resonant. 

Where my face would be, my amber dome catches the refracted sunlight. I do not see the sky as a blue dome; I see it through my heat-sensing pores as a shimmering, geometric matrix of infrared currents. Where the humidity pools in mid-air, the sun bends into thick, blinding ribbons of white and gold. I see the invisible updrafts—great, boiling plumes of warm methane rising from the fermenting root-bowls below, carrying the translucent bladders of the Air-Marrow upward in lazy, drifting spirals.

The air does not enter me through a mouth; I do not breathe to speak. My chest is broad, flat, and dense, pressed tightly against the damp bark of the bridge as I move. 

*THUMP-THUMP. CRACK-THUMP.*

Deep inside my ribcage, my calcified heart-organ—heavy and smooth as a polished river stone—strikes against my sternum. The vibration shoots down through my arms and legs, pouring into the vascular system of the sky-bridge. 

*I am here,* my ribs say into the wood. *The Western Conduit is swelling. The sap is thick with spring-sugar.*

A fraction of a second later, a answer hums back up through my feet from a mile away, deep in the root-pillars on the dark side of the basin. The wood carries it at a terrifying speed, a sharp, buzzing pulse that rattles my knee-joints: 

*Trim the leak. The Valve-Borer in Chamber Eight has calcified. The horn is silent.*

I turn. My body dangles over the hundred-yard drop, completely unconcerned with the void. Down below, the floor of the plaza is a violent, churning soup of sloughed bark, red-shelled scavengers, and deep, muddy pools where the new galls are currently bouncing and dissolving. The sound from down there is a physical weight—a crushing wall of sub-bass from the organ-trunk pipes that pushes against my amber head like a heavy hand. It would turn a soft-eared creature’s skull to liquid, but to me, it is simply the weather. It is the steady, reassuring pulse of the house I live in.

Wrapped around my left forearm, my parasite-slug is warm and heavy. It senses my change in direction and twitches, its pale, mouthless head secreting a single, smoking drop of clear acid that drips into the air, falling upward for a few feet toward a bent band of light before dissolving in the mist.

I reach the junction where the sky-bridge meets the wall of Tenement Nine. Here, the local physics have begun to buckle. The trunk of Tenement Nine looks straight, but as I step onto its vertical bark, my perspective shifts; the wall becomes a flat, endless plain, and the sky-bridge I just walked across appears to curve away into a tiny, pinched spiral no wider than my thumb, though I know I could turn back and cross it in fifty strides.

A wet *CRACK* explodes fifty yards above me—a seed-pod bursting. A heavy, rubbery gall shoots past me, tearing through a web of high-wire tendrils, plummeting toward the condensation pools below. I watch it through my amber pores; it is hot, glowing bright orange with the heat of its own pressurized birth. Inside it, I can feel—even through the air, through the subtle acoustic displacement—the soft, terrified, unformed heartbeat of a newborn. 

It will hit the water. The pool will eat the rind. An Elder will reach down and pull it out by its wet, five-jointed wrist.

I do not feel pity or joy for it; I feel the structural necessity of it. Soon, my own skin will begin to dull. My elbows will lose their fifth joint, stiffening into four, then three. My cilia will secrete a permanent, calcified cement instead of a wet vacuum. I will find a gap in the sky-bridge where the wind threatens to tear the tendrils apart, I will wrap my arms around the main trunk, press my chest flat against the grain, and I will stop moving.

The bark will grow over my ankles. My amber dome will turn to dry, grey wood. My calcified heart-organ will stop striking my ribs and become a permanent, dense knot in the wood—a natural amplifier that will take the song of the Valve-Borers and carry it across the plaza for the next ten thousand years.

But today, I am soft enough to move. 

I reach the choked chamber. The Valve-Borer is there, a massive, stone-hard beetle wedged tight into the phloem pipe, its shell now completely fused with the surrounding cambium. It has stopped flexing its abdomen; it has become wood before its time, choked on its own sugar. The organ-pipe above us is dead and silent, letting the hydraulic pressure build to a dangerous, violent shudder within the trunk. The whole wall is shaking—a sickening, arhythmic spasm that threatens to burst the bark.

I uncoil the parasite-slug from my arm. I press its acid-dripping mouth against the seam where the dead beetle meets the sap-conduit. 

*Shhh-hhhh.*

The wood smokes. The acid bites deep. The smell of scorched cellulose and hot resin fills the air, thick and sweet as syrup. 

I brace my broad feet against the bark, dig my five-jointed fingers into the wood's grain, and wait for the precise moment when the pressure reaches its peak—when the ancient, deafening, beautiful city calls for a breath—and I pull.
