The glare hits the eye not as light, but as a cold, flat weight. 

My inner eyelid—the grey, translucent sheet of smoked quartz—slides sideways with a faint, wet click, dimming the blinding azure above the terrace into a manageable, pewter-colored dusk. The sun does not warm. It merely turns the wet slate beneath my heels into a field of mirrors and illuminates the microscopic beads of condensation suspended on the dark waxy leaves of the skiff-fronds.

I am sitting on the knee of an iron rocker-arm where it emerges from a bed of four-foot-deep liverwort. 

My spine is leaned back against the brass bulkhead of the upper condenser house. The metal is cold, but not dead cold; deep within its three-inch plate, there is a faint, rhythmic pulse—a thermal breath that cycles every seven hundred seconds as the deep brine is drawn up from the lower fault and forced through the cooling channels. 

I do not have a name that can be spoken, because my mouth has not opened for seven years, and the air here is too thin to carry a voice even if I possessed the impulse to shape one. If another of my kind were to come up from the lower shelf, he would know me by the pattern of four slow taps I strike with my horn rod against the copper feeder pipe at my left hip: *clack... clack-clack... clack.* The vibration would travel down through the copper, into the wet basalt of the terrace, through the horn-shod soles of his boots, and up into his knees. That is enough. That is my place in the ledger.

My chest is wide—so wide my ribs feel like the iron hoops of an old tallow tub. I take a breath now. It takes eighty seconds for my chest to expand, drawing the thin, icy air deep into the grey sponge of my lungs, holding it under the pressure of my thick breastbone until the blood catches the scant oxygen, and then letting it slide out through my nose in a thin, pale plume that smells faintly of graphite and pine-grease.

In my left hand, I hold the horn pot. It is heavy, turned from the jaw-plate of an ancient tallow-back that died before my grandfather was laid in Piston Chamber Two. Inside it is a thick, golden paste: three parts amber resin scraped from the notches of the sawblade ferns, two parts cold tallow gathered from the rubbing-posts where the beasts lean against the drive-shafts, and a pinch of ground iron-pyrite to give it body.

In my right hand is the brush. Its handle is a piece of straightened copper wire; its bristles are stiff, dark fern-fibers that do not rot in the dampness.

Before me, six inches from my knees, is the guide-way for the vertical valve-stem. 

It is a block of grey gunmetal, eight inches across, set into a bed of razor-sharp greenschist. The valve-stem itself is a rod of polished iron, three inches thick, that disappears straight down into the mountain. Once every twenty minutes, it moves. It does not spring or slide; it creeps downward by three-quarters of an inch over the course of five minutes, then stops, holds, and creeps back up. 

A rust-wicked snail, its shell a heavy, dull-red pyramid of pyrite, has crawled onto the shoulder of the guide-block. It is grazing on a smudge of pewter lichen no larger than a fingernail. I do not disturb it. Its grey-white foot leaks a trail of clear, hydrophobic slime that seals the micro-cracks in the gunmetal better than any grease I could mix. I wait. I have been waiting here since the shadow of the western spire touched the edge of the moss bed four hours ago.

Time does not pass here like a river; it settles like silt in a cold pool.

Farther down the shelf, thirty yards below my perch, a tallow-back has stopped to rest against the main copper hawser anchor. Its broad, flat back is a low island of emerald liverwort, dark against the pale slate. Its ribcage rises—once—its broad flanks expanding with a low, wet stretching sound as the lardaceous fat beneath its hide yields to the movement. Then it holds the air. A trickle of sweat, cold and mineral-heavy, runs off the braided copper cable above it and drips onto its head. The beast does not blink. A cable-walker, long and slate-grey, its clamp-like toes locked onto the copper strands above, hangs upside down six feet higher, its green-blooded belly pressed flat against the wire, waiting for the evening moths to rise from the fern roots.

We are all bound to the same slow stroke.

The valve-stem before me gives its warning. It is not a sound—sound is a weak, fleeting thing that dies in the moss—but a subtle shift in the tension of the iron rod I rest my palm against. The steel grows fractionally cooler.

I dip the fern-bristle brush into the horn pot. The resin paste is stiff, smelling of cold kerosene and damp earth. 

As the iron rod begins its agonizingly slow descent into the guide-block, I bring the brush to the exposed metal. I do not hurry. The stroke of my hand takes two minutes, laying down a thin, amber-colored sheen along the leading edge of the stroke. The metal swallows the grease with a faint, wet sigh. As the rod slides through the block, the friction generates a tiny, fleeting bloom of warmth—just enough to melt the tallow and send the amber resin creeping into the microscopic pores of the iron, driving out the icy moisture before it can settle and pit the surface.

When the stroke reaches its floor, I rest my hand. 

I look out across the Rift. The light is changing, though the sky remains that same brutal, unclouded azure. The black shadows of the spires have stretched fifty yards across the lower terraces, swallowing the three-story brass condenser houses one by one, turning their bright yellow metal to the dull, heavy brown of cold tea.

In thirty years, the iron rod I am watching will have moved down and up roughly seven hundred thousand times. By then, the bristles on my brush will have worn down to the copper wire. I will walk down the slate steps to the fern-clearing, harvest three fronds from the oldest plant near the water-catch, dry them in the dry-air outlet of the secondary turbine, and bind a new brush with fresh wire. 

And when my lungs finally grow too rigid to flare my ribs, when the grey sponge inside me refuses the thin air of the nine-thousand-foot shelf, they will carry me down into the cold vault of Piston House Four. They will lay me on the bed of dry engine grease beside the old ones, wrap my knees in rubber gasket-sheeting, and slide the cast-iron plate shut over my face.

My tallow will seep into the rock. My bones, rich with the iron and copper of eighty years of water, will feed the roots of the sawblade ferns that grow through the seams in the wall. A century later, another Keeper will sit on this same rocker-arm, dip a brush into a horn pot filled with the resin of those same ferns, and lay a thin, amber coat of grease along the stroke of this same rod.

The valve-stem reaches its upper limit. It stops. The iron settles into its quiet, cold rest for another fifteen minutes.

I slide my smoked-quartz eyelid back into its socket. The blinding glare of the quartz veins returns, hard and violent, cutting through the thin air. I lift the brush, dip it once more into the golden resin, and wait for the mountain to take its next breath.
