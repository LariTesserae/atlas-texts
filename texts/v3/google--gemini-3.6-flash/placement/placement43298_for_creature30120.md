First, there is no *here* or *there*. There is only the pitch of the floor.

Before I have a shape, I have the frequency. It enters through the lowest point of my mass—a heavy, twelve-hertz shudder that rises out of the subterranean heat-exchangers miles to the north and travels through three inches of water, two inches of rust-sludge, and seven inches of cold-rolled deckplate before it reaches me. It is not a sound that arrives through air. It is a structural fact. It settles into the iron of my frame like weight itself.

I am down on the deck. I have always been down on the deck.

Above me, very far away, there is a thin, glass-sharp needle of sound—a high-frequency singing that drifts down from the ceiling girders where the Arc-Drinkers cling to their emerald flashes. It is faint, almost sweet, but it does not belong to me. My world is thick, heavy, and wet.

My body gathers around the sound. I am not light enough to float or flat enough to slide. I have mass—too much of it, three tons of cast-iron ribs, box-girder plating, and forged trunnions that have spent centuries acquiring a dark, velvet skin of wet magnetite and soot-moss. 

My head—if it can be called that—is a single, broad disk of thick steel, three feet across, slightly concave, pressed flat against the floorplates. It is my only eye, my only ear, my only tongue. Through it, the basin speaks in geometry.

When I press the disk down into the tea-colored condensate, the shivering rings of the standing waves ripple against its rim. I feel the distant *clack-hiss* of a pneumatic valve clearing its line two miles to the west; the sound arrives as a sharp, metallic pulse that rings through my frame like a struck anvil. I feel the dull, dead spot half a mile ahead where a hollow cooling tower sits drowned in its tarn—a great, black footprint that absorbs the vibrations and leaves a shadow in the sound. And beneath me, deep down in the flooded galleries, I hear the slow, heavy rising of a Blind Valve’s counterweight, grumbling in its calcified shell as the dark water fills its chamber.

I am a **Sump-Gaiter**, but I am an old one. My front saucer-plate cracked long ago along a structural stress line, splitting my hearing into two slightly out-of-phase halves. Where another Gaiter sees a single, solid echo, I see a double world: the real iron beneath me, and the shadow-iron reflected off the ceiling lattice three hundred feet above.

*Thunk.*

My left forward limb—a heavy, jointed A-frame of forged steel—lifts out of the rust-mud. The old tallow in the knee-socket is cold and stiff, full of grit and micro-fractures, but the internal balance-weight swings forward, and the joint falls. 

*Drag.*

The long, rear skid pulls through the shallow water, leaving a deep, double-groove in the iron silt. A cluster of Skimmers, lying flat in the tread-patterns like sunken shovel-blades, feel the wave of my approach and contract their wire-pelts, sliding sideways over the river-rounded rivets to let me pass. They grind against my rear skid as I move, their copper filaments polishing my rusty edges down to bright, silver steel. We do not acknowledge one another. We are simply two different rates of wear.

Inside my belly, the old logic performs its small, stubborn work.

A small, gear-driven pump—its teeth worn smooth as river glass—draws the cold condensate up through a slotted intake in my undercarriage. The liquid moves through three stages of copper mesh, leaving behind its heavy sediment: fine quartz sand, red iron oxide, flakes of zinc washed down from the high pylon crossbeams. The clean water passes through my heat-jacket, cooling the faint, residual hum of my central capacitor, before it is expelled behind me in a short, whistling jet of wet steam.

*Hiss-shhh.*

The vapor rises, catches the draft from a nearby floor-seam, and drifts upward into the cold rain to become ceiling-drizzle once again. Nothing is lost. Nothing is gained. The circuit remains closed.

Now, the floor begins to tilt.

It is imperceptible to anything that does not live with its chin against the metal—a fraction of a millimeter of lift along the southern regional hydraulic line. But I feel it immediately. The tea-colored water around my limbs stops its shivering and begins to move in a broad, silent sheet, three inches deep, gliding toward the lower decks to the east.

In the distance, straight ahead through the dark rain, a dull, cherry-red eye opens in the gloom.

It is a transformer housing, ancient and buried to its cooling fins in flooded deckplates. Its internal coils are dying, but they still bleed a faint, sour warmth into the surrounding iron—a pale envelope of heat three or four degrees above the bitter cold of the rain.

I turn toward it. Not out of desire—I have no name for warmth—but because the metal of my chassis expands toward heat, and where the metal expands, the joints move with slightly less resistance. The tallow softens. The grumble in my knee-socket turns to a smooth, wet slide.

I move across the flat, wet iron, one heavy, rhythmic *thunk-drag* at a time. The sound of my passing travels through the floor, warning the Blind Valves below, stirring the pale wire-lichen in the seams, leaving a long, twin trail of polished iron that will stay bright for an hour before the rust creeps back to cover it.

When I reach the housing, I will stop. I will press my cracked steel face against the warm, pitted casing. I will draw the steam through my belly and jet it into the dark, listening to the Arc-Drinkers sing their thin, glass songs in the girders above, while the water runs down my flanks and turns to iron mud at my feet.

I will stay there until the heat fades completely. And when it goes, I will lift one heavy, tallow-stiffened leg, press my ear to the shivering deck, and listen for the next ember in the dark.
