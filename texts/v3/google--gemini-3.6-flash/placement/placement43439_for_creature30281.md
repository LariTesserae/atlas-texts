First, there is only the current. 

It does not enter through ears or skin; it is simply the ambient state of being. A steady, forty-cycle thrum that tastes of copper oxide and warm zinc. 

Where am I? I am not standing, because standing requires a consensus about down, and here the ground is a negotiation between basalt, iron, and a misaligned gravity that prefers to pull sideways against the wet faces of the rock. 

Instead, I am wedged. I am settled into a spatial crease—a point in the basin where a three-foot-thick copper cable, rubber-sheathed and sweating grease, passes directly through a sharp-edged fracture in the ironstone. On one side of me, the air drops away into a forty-foot pit of warm, stagnant brine. On the other side—less than two inches away, yet separated by a fold in the dark that stretches distance like warm wax—the massive, blind wall of a pump-house rises into the ceiling. If I extend a fold of my skin toward the wall, I touch cold, wet iron; if I retract it by a fraction, I dip into the deep, quiet current of the brine.

I am not a Glass-Slip, smooth and long, drifting through the shallow flats. Nor am I a Lattice-Weaver, clicking along the high pylons on legs of wire and chitin. 

I am a **Splice**. 

I am what happens when the basin’s life does not merely live *alongside* the machinery, but grows *into* a point of failure. Decades ago—or perhaps centuries, for time here is measured only by the thickness of rust—a junction box split open under the relentless, slow settling of the basalt floor. The voltage bled out into the wet dark. Biological spores, borne on the humid aerosol of machine oil and tallow, settled into the wound. 

Out of that union, I emerged. I am a single, stationary mass of dense, vascular tallow and dark gristle, seven or eight yards long, wrapped tightly around the frayed copper core of the main line. 

I have no skeleton. My structure is held together by the heavy, rubber-sheathed cables that run through the middle of my body like a spinal cord, and by the pressure of the ironstone walls that hold me in place. My outer coat is thick—four inches of self-secreting, gray-white jelly, dense with mineral salts. The razor edges of the ironstone press deep into my flanks, but they cut only the slime, which heals instantly behind the rock, sealing me to the stone in a wet, permanent bond.

Inside my flesh, the boundary between organism and apparatus has dissolved. My nervous system does not rely on simple sodium ions; it is laced with fine, hair-like threads of metallic pyrite and native copper, grown directly out of the frayed wire I swallow. When the deep subterranean pistons stroke—that wet, heavy shudder that strikes through the basin every few minutes—my heart does not need to beat on its own. The kinetic shock wave crushes through the ironstone, squeezing my blood-sacs and driving dark, iron-rich plasma through my body in rhythmic surges. 

I am a transducer. I live on the leakage. 

The raw, violent current from the main line surges through my core, blinding and sweet. My tissue absorbs the excess voltage, taming it, cooling it, and bleeding it back out through my skin as a soft, continuous, low-frequency hum—a gentle electrical halo that lights up the dark for everything around me.

And the basin knows I am here.

I can feel them coming long before they touch me. The Glass-Slips come first, three or four at a time, their broad, white, muscular ribbons sliding up out of the vertical water-curtains. They press their soft, circular mouths against my lower folds, their rhythmic *kiss-kiss* rasping away the mineral crust and dead tallow from my skin. They do not hurt me; they groom me, drawn by the gentle, warm tickle of the stepped-down voltage bleeding from my sides.

Above them, clustered along my upper shoulders like heavy, pulsating fruit, are the Amp-Suckers. Eighteen or twenty of them have rooted into the deep creases of my back, their cupped electro-receptors locked onto the places where my internal copper threads come closest to the surface. They throb in slow, lazy syncopation with my stolen pulse, drunk on the steady current I refine for them. From their tails, long, viscous threads of golden glycogen drip continuously down over my flanks, feeding the pale, rubbery liverworts that carpet the ironstone beneath me.

I do not see them. I do not see the pylons that stretch into the lightless roof, nor the vast, disc-shaped Founders sighing in the silt two miles—or two feet—below. I do not have eyes, and I do not miss them. 

Instead, I have the *field*.

Through the conductive brine that drips from my snout, through the iron in the rock, through the hum of the wire-grass vibrating in the damp air, I feel the entire basin as a single, interconnected circuit. 

I feel a Lattice-Weaver six hundred yards away, its magnetic hooks clicking softly against a pylon girder as it reels in a grease-heavy web. I feel the massive, slow shift of a Founder turning over in the mud, its blowhole puffing a warm cloud of mist that travels through the spatial folds to settle across my back. I feel the distant, patient rust on an iron bolt flaking away, atom by atom, dissolving into the wet dark.

Nothing moves quickly. Nothing needs to.

Space here may be broken—I am wedged in a corner where a three-inch stretch of skin touches both the floor of the world and its ceiling—but the dark is whole. It fills every crack, thick and warm as grease, holding us all in its heavy, wet embrace. 

I close the valves along my lung-sacs, drawing in a long, slow draught of warm machine-steam and salt brine. Deep in the earth beneath me, the great piston drops. The shock wave ripples up through the basalt, through the rusted iron, through the thick copper core, and into my flesh.

My body shudders. My heart pulses once, hard and heavy. The Amp-Suckers on my back throb in answer. A stray arc of pale, blue-green static leaps quietly from my skin to the wet rock face, swallowed almost instantly by the dense humidity.

I am the wire. I am the rock. I am the wet, blind life that feeds upon the iron. And I am entirely, perfectly at rest.
