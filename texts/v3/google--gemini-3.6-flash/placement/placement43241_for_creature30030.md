I am not down in the clouds, where the air loses its heat, nor am I up on the needle-spandrel where the Cowl-Dragon catches the rising haze. 

You would find me three thousand meters up the vertical face, in the narrow, twilight corridor formed where a curtain of shield-orchids hangs three feet off the wet basalt. 

I live in the gap between the plant wall and the stone wall.

I am a broad, flattened mantle—four meters from edge to edge, no thicker than a folded hide, and shaped like an asymmetrical leaf. My belly is pale and smooth as wet lard, lined with thousands of microscopic, vacuum-yielding cilia that grip the water-slick stone. My back is rougher, covered in a dark, flexible plate of soft horn that matches the iron-grey of the basalt. 

I do not have limbs, nor do I have eyes. I do not need to see the sun through the haze; I *feel* the sun as a slow, creeping shift in the temperature of the rock beneath me.

I am anchored at my head to a small, smooth nodule of pearlescent composite buried six inches into the mountain face. My cartilage mouth-parts have grown around it, sealing over the ancient, non-decaying material. The plants cannot touch the nodule—their roots are repelled by its pale field—but I am different. My tissue was built to bridge the gap. I drink the faint, thrumming current that leaks from the nodule’s core, a steady, low-voltage pulse that tastes of copper and hot glass. It keeps my dual hearts beating at a rhythm so slow it can barely be measured—three strikes an hour.

Where I sit, a heavy glass-ribbon of waterfall slides continuously over my back. It does not wet my horny plates; my skin secretes a dense, clear oil that splits the stream, forcing the warm, slow-falling water to carve a twin arch around my body. 

In this tiny, dry sanctuary beneath the water-skin, I am a listener.

I feel the glass-ribbons swimming upward through the water-curtain just an inch above my back, their iridescent nerves shivering as they bypass my shape. I feel the pale quadrupeds picking their way along the roof of the shelf forty feet above, their soft pads making low, pneumatic *tuck-tuck* sounds against the slate. When the amber-wings hit the orchids on the outer curtain, the impact travels through the vine-lattices like a handful of gravel hitting a taut sheet.

Most of all, I feel the mountain’s skeleton.

Deep inside the basalt, the geothermal conduits are humming. The pitch of the hum changes with the pressure of the subterranean steam, rising from a low, gravelly rasp to a clear, organ-like tone. When it rises too high, a field-node somewhere down the wall chimes—that sudden, sweet, bell-like strike that echoes across vertical leagues of wet green.

When the chime hits, the gravity vector leans. The world tilts three degrees to the left.

I do not slip. My cilia adjust instantly, their vacuum-pockets relaxing on one side and tightening on the other. The water-curtain above me swishes, its heavy glass ribbon bowing outward into space before the lateral pull catches it and snaps it back against my hide with a soft, wet *slap*. A shower of warm drops spray into my hollow. A dozen amber-wings are knocked loose from the orchid leaves; they tumble past my gap, buzzing like copper wire, before catching the new grav-slope and shooting sideways into the green web.

I open the soft, fleshy folds along my lateral edges. 

Trapped in the mist that blows through my shelter are the floating lipids of the shield-orchids, the microscopic spores of the glowing basin-algae, and the sloughed, sweet skin-broth of the glass-ribbons. My mantle filters the wet air, drawing the vapor into my lungs—two long, ridged chambers that line my spine—where the heat of the air is harvested and converted into the thick, clear oil that keeps the waterfall from touching me.

I have not moved from this nodule in forty years. I will not move from it for a hundred more. 

There is no reason to go up. Up is only more wet stone, more orchids, and the vast, blinding brilliance of the open sky. There is no reason to go down. Down is only the long, heavy plunge toward the cloud-sea, where the field-lines stretch thin and the air begins to choke on its own weight.

Here, pinned against the warm, sweating bone of the world by a force that was set in motion before my species had a name, I am perfectly wedged. The water glides over me; the stone warms me from below; the ancient node feeds my tongue; the jungle rustles against my back.

A drop of sap falls from an orchid frond. It hits my shell, rolls down the dark horn, catches the edge of the falling water-skin, and is swept away—drawn downward through three miles of warm, sunlit air toward a floor no one has ever seen.

I close my side-folds. I take a single, deep breath of wet stone and crushed stem. I wait for the next strike of my heart.
