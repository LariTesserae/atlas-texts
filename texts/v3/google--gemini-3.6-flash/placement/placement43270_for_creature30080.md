It begins not as a thought, but as a frequency in the jaw. 

A low, hydraulic *C-sharp* vibrates through the basalt shelf beneath my chest, traveling up through the fused plates of my sternum and settling in the thick, cold fluid behind my forehead. The glass-reeds three miles up-current are beginning to chime. Their calcified stalks are bending under the first heavy push of the four-hour surge. The water is thickening, crowding against the rock, gaining weight.

I am here, at the throat of the saddle-pass, where the basalt drops off into the drowned gorge. 

I am not in the shelters of pine-log and velvet-hide, wedged in the warm, slick knot of the clan. I am at the end of the line—the last pylon before the dark drops into the pathless floor. 

My body is a wedge of heavy slate and blubber, four hundred pounds of dense bone and tallow anchored to a world that never stops pulling. I have no eyes to miss the sun, no lungs to ache for air. Underneath my dark, blubberous skin, my heart strikes its slow, thick anvil beat—*one... two...*—pumping iron-black blood through muscles that do not twitch, but set like mortar.

I am a carver of the hold-fasts. 

In my right paddle, held tight by the blunt, overgrown bone-spur that hooks through my wrist-joint, is a hammerstone of black flint. My thumb—broad as a spade and slick with kelp-oil—feels the lip of the flute I have been working for three vent-cycles. The rock here is stubborn, a tough, iron-rich basalt that rings like iron when struck. But the current demands a deeper groove. If the notch is too shallow, a harvester’s hand-spur will slip when the cold wall hits, and the current will pluck them off the ridge like a loose leaf of moss.

*Knick. Knick. Scrape.*

I strike the stone. The vibration travels down my arm, through my collar, and into the skull-cartilage. The rock tells me its grain through my teeth. *A soft vein here. A micro-fracture there.* I work by the resonance of the impact, feeling for the point where the stone yields without shattering.

Around me, the ocean is a violent, dark orchestra. 

To my left, the gland-kelp forest is booming. The pressurized trunks, thick as ancient trees, are groaning under the swelling pressure of the approaching surge. I can hear the wet, leather-drum *thump-thump-thump* as their bladders compress, then the sharp, tearing *slap* of their heavy purple fronds whipping through the brine. 

To my right, far down in the dark of the valley floor, a thermal vent belches. A pillar of superheated, sulfurous water shoots upward, singing a high, hiss-like whistling as it tears through the freezing density of the deep brine. The water around my flanks shifts—just a fraction of a degree warmer, tasting sharply of brimstone and copper. My skin responds automatically, sloughing a fine layer of tallow to seal my pores against the mineral itch.

The glass-reeds are screaming now. The four-hour current has crested the western ridge.

I do not hurry. Hurry is for things with fins, things that drift, things that die when the water catches them. 

I slide my left hand along the guide-rope—thick, three-strand reed-cordage, sticky with boiled oil-pitch and heavy with calcified crust. I follow it to the base of the pylon. I sink down. My hind limbs, short and bowed like tree roots, lock into the lower basalt flute. I fold my broad, wedge-shaped head against the stone, tucking my chin into the hollow of my throat-plate to present a smooth, hydro-dynamic curve to the oncoming wall. I drive my bone-spurs deep into the fresh groove I have spent the day chipping out of the mountain.

Then, the cold avalanche hits.

The water does not merely flow; it hits like a falling cathedral. Tons of ice-cold, iron-heavy brine slam over my back, trying to wedge between my belly and the rock, trying to lift me, to pry my spurs from the stone, to hurl me over the lip of the gorge into the pathless abyss below.

The pressure in my ears spikes to a crushing hum. The sound is absolute—a roaring, metallic wall of noise that vibrates every tooth in my jaw and sets my rib-plates ringing like struck bells. 

I do not fight the current. I let it shape me. I flatten my broad shoulders, press my snout flush against the basalt, and become stone. The water scours over my oiled hide, stripping away the old tallow, clearing the loose mineral dust from the grooves of my work. 

In the middle of the deluge, something heavy and slick brushes my flank. A Drift-velvet, torn from the glass-reeds by the force of the surge, is being swept through the pass. Its massive, translucent sheet of muscle wraps briefly around my shoulder, pulsing with blind, wet terror before the current tears it away, shredding it against the sharp basalt horn above me. I smell its faint, sweet digestive gel in the water as it passes. A waste. The clan could have used its hide for a shelter shroud.

I hold. 

My heart beats once. *One...*
The avalanche roars.
My heart beats again. *Two...*

Slowly, after a time that has no meaning in the dark, the violent drag begins to smooth out. The screaming chime of the glass-reeds drops an octave, settling back into a low, metallic murmur. The main force of the water has spilled over into the lower basin. 

I lift my head. The cold brine rushes through the horseshoe-shaped cartilage of my snout, carrying the news of the pass. 

I smell the golden, bitter sap of wounded gland-kelp—someone up-current was harvesting when the surge hit, and their catch-pouches held. I smell the rich, fatty grease of a crushed Slag-worm, caught in the rocks above. And beneath it all, traveling through the solid basalt beneath my hands, I feel a rhythmic, knicking tap.

*Tap-tap. Drag. Tap.*

It is another Grasp-born, moving down the cordage path from the upper pine-shelters. 

I stay where I am, wedged into the fresh flute, waiting. Soon, a broad, flat forehead presses against mine. The contact is firm, warm, and slick with kelp-oil. 

Through the bone-to-bone connection of our skulls, the vibration comes clear and unblurred by the roaring water:

*Is the pylon fast?*

I strike my bone-knuckles twice against the basalt beneath us, sending the answer directly through their wrists, into their shoulders, into their mind:

*The notch is deep. The stone holds.*
