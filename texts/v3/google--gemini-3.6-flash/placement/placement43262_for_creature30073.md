I would be down where the heat is thickest, but not at the bottom of the fissures. Not the rock-toad, whose dark is too absolute, too far from the high, pale sky. 

I would be on the slope where a tallow-smooth dome of ochre siltstone curves down into the lip of a carved gutter—at the precise seam where the leather meets the glass.

At first, I am only a weight. 

A heavy, dark press against the stone, warmed from above by the unblinking sun and warmed from below by the deep heat held in the siltstone’s belly. There is no distinction, initially, between my own skin and the rock. Both are dry, both are warm, both have been here long enough that the sharp edges have dissolved into long, soft contours. 

Then comes the breath. Not mine alone, but the ambient, dry contraction of the plain: the faint, unison click of spiracles three inches to my left, where the carpet-bugs lock their ochre carapaces like roof slate; the minute, dry drag of a glass-lizard’s belly shifting in the shadow beneath my flank.

I am a glider, but I am not alone in being one. I am three feet of bruised-indigo hide, wide as a heavy belting-strap, lying across the curve of the dome. 

My eyes are milky and quiet beneath their horn-caps. I do not need to see the tableland; I feel its geometry in the distribution of heat across my underside. The crest of the dome is hot—a dry, radiant yellow heat that sinks into the dark ridges of my back and turns the dense, oily fat near my spine into a slow, humming liquid. Further down my flank, where my parchment-like vestigial membranes spill into the shallow depression of the gutter, the heat turns cool and grey.

That is where the others are.

Against my left flank, where my dark skin creates a narrow ribbon of twilight against the ochre stone, four wingless velvet-moths are wedged tightly together. I can feel their tiny, furred bodies drinking the faint mist of salt that transpires through my pores. They do not move. They are waiting for the sun to sink two finger-widths lower across the sky, when the shade will widen by half an inch. 

Beneath the curve of my neck, in the hollow where my chin rests against the lip of the stone gutter, lies the tail of a glass-lizard. It is cold and clear as quartz. Through my own belly-hide, I can feel the faint, double-thrum of its hearts—a rapid, fragile ticking compared to my own deep, five-minute pulse.

We do not touch out of affection or intent. We overlap because the plain has no empty space that is not either too hot or too dry. We are a single, continuous fabric woven from leather, chitin, translucent scale, and soft fur, pinned to the siltstone by the weight of the light.

I do not remember the sky when it had wind in it. I have no memory of the ancestral membranes on my sides ever holding air. To me, the membranes are simply blankets—flaps of dry, pale skin that I spread over the rock-carvings to keep the morning dew from evaporating before the glass-lizards can reach the lip.

And the carvings... I know them better than whatever creature made them fifty thousand years ago. 

My underside is not flat; it has been molded by decades of lying in this exact spot. The soft leather of my belly has taken the impression of a shallow, hand-ground gutter. I know the exact place where an ancient claw chipped a flake of slate away to deepen the channel; the scar in the rock fits into a corresponding ridge beneath my third rib. When the dawn fog condenses on my back, the water runs down the indigo furrows of my hide, gathers at the parchment fold of my flank, and drops—*drip, drip, drip*—directly into the tiny cup ground into the stone below.

I am a water-pipe. I am a sun-collector. I am a roof for the moths and a windbreak for the geckos.

A wave of peristalsis begins somewhere near my tail. It is not a decision I make; it is simply the heat moving through my muscles like warm oil through a pipe. The ripple travels slowly forward, taking two full minutes to reach my jaw. My body shifts forward three-quarters of an inch along the gutter. 

Beneath me, the fine dust of ground carapace and powdered siltstone shifts with a soft, dry *hiss*. The velvet-moths adjust their position without unsticking their feet from my hide. The glass-lizard does not wake, only draws its tail tighter into the shadow I cast.

We are settled here. The mountain has stopped growing. The rivers have all gone into the sky or into the stone. There is nothing to chase, nothing to run from, nothing to build. There is only the long, warm afternoon of the world, the slow pulse of fat turning into heat, and the quiet, crowded comfort of being another layer of skin upon an ancient, resting earth.
