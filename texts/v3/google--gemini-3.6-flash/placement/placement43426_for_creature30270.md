I am not one of those who slide. Not anymore. 

My sliding days ended six or seven seasons ago—if seasons had any meaning in the damp dark, which they do not. Time here is measured only by the depth of the white efflorescence blooming along the mortar seams, and by how far down the bitumen has soaked into the hemp.

I find myself wedged in a junction where three revetments of river-silt brick meet a forest of slate spires. It is a narrow, vertical fissure, no wider than the palm of my hand is long. My back is pressed against the uncured clay of the northern wall; my long, flat chest is pinned by the sharp edge of a slate shingle that was wedged into place while my skin was still soft enough to yield.

I am a Builder, but I am in the middle of becoming something else. 

From the hips down, I no longer belong to the air. The dripping lime-water has done its slow, quiet work. My feet—once long and flexible enough to grip the sharp lips of the fish-scale tiles—have fused entirely with the floor. The calcification has climbed my shins like a white crust, soaking into my skin, entering the bone, locking my knees into thick, knotty joints of calcified grit. If you were to strike my left ankle with a stone hammer, it would not bleed; it would ring like a cracked terra-cotta pipe.

Because I cannot move, I have become a node. A keystone that breathes.

My long, ribless torso is still flesh, slick with the cold sweat of condensation and smelling faintly of copper and old fat. My neck, long and sinewy, can still turn three inches to the left, three to the right. My eyes are gone, smoothed over long ago by the pale, scar-like skin that covers my forehead, but my ears—large, thin, and rimless—are pinned flat against the slate behind me, turning my entire skull into a sounding board.

And my hands are still free.

My right hand, with its four long, three-jointed fingers and its square nail of yellow keratin, is hooked into a gap between two slate shingles directly above my head. I hold a wooden shear-pin in place with my middle finger. It is a pin of dense, ancient resin-wood, groaning under a four-ton overhang of gravel-packed reed baskets. I can feel the grain of the wood crying out through my nail—a high, dry, microscopic shudder that travels straight down the marrow of my arm. The pin is shearing. It has been shearing for three years. It will shear for another three before it snaps. Until then, my finger remains.

My left hand hangs lower, resting against a tensioned length of bitumen-soaked hemp that runs horizontally through a weep-hole in the brickwork, stretching out into the pitch-black basin for nearly a mile.

This is my place. I am the junction where the structural strain of the upper revetments is translated into sound.

When the atmospheric wind pushes through the maze, it hits the slate spires around me first. The narrow vertical gaps act as reeds. They whistle in a low, hollow B-flat that vibrates in the soft cartilaginous arches of my long throat. I don't breathe the wind; I let it play me. My lungs, rising up into my neck like thin leather bags, expand and contract in time with the low-altitude pressure shifts.

Below me, under the floor tiles that have swallowed my feet, I feel the stilt-walkers. 

They do not know I was ever a creature that slid. To them, my calcified shins are merely two more narrow pillars of river-silt masonry that need cleaning. I can feel the faint, rhythmic *tack-tack-tack* of their jointed, needle-thin legs as they swarm out of the triangular weep-holes in the tiles. They climb my frozen knees. Their flat, acidic under-snouts scrape at the saltpeter that crusts around my thigh-bones. It is a strange, cool sensation—a thousand tiny, licking mouths dissolving the hard white crust before it can pry my flesh apart from the stone.

They like the sweat that drips from my ribs. It is rich with lime and iron. When they gather in thick, writhing clusters around my hips, their collective weight vibrates at a frequency so high it makes the teeth in my jaw ache with a sweet, metallic resonance.

Above me, a drop of fat forms. 

It has taken three days to gather on the lip of a terra-cotta vault four inches above my nose. I wait for it. My mouth is open, my thin, long tongue rested against my upper palate. 

*Pat.*

It falls. It hits my tongue—cold, thick, tasting of iron, old shale, and ancient compressed marrow. I swallow slowly, feeling the rubbery grease slide down my narrow throat, coating my stomach, keeping the cold from freezing the tiny bit of red liquid that still moves through my upper heart. It is all the fuel I need. One drop every few days. The rest of my body is sustained by the mineral water running down the slate, which my skin drinks up like dry clay.

Then—it comes.

A mile to the east, deep in the gloom where no one has slid for a hundred years, a bed of hollow urns collapses under the weight of a settling terrace. 

The sound does not travel through the air first; it travels through the ground. A sudden, violent *thump-shudder* hits the soles of my calcified feet, shooting up my frozen shins, rattling my pelvis, and bursting into my spine. A micro-second later, the air catches up—a booming, bell-like *POCK* that shatters the low whistle of the wind, bouncing off a million hard, wet tiles until the dark is full of ringing brass.

I feel the shear-pin above my head shift. A thousandth of an inch. The wood shrieks against my fingernail.

I do not panic. Panic is for wide, open places where creatures have legs to run with. Here, there is only tension and response.

My left hand moves. With the flat, thick pad of my spade-shaped thumb, I pluck the bitumen rope.

*THRUNN.*

The sound is deep, oily, and heavy. It travels down the rope, through the weep-hole, into the dark. Three miles away, another long, flat chest pinned between two columns will feel the rope snap against its skin. Another pair of shell-like ears will catch the resonance. A hand will reach out in the dark, grab a stone hammer, and drive a slate shingle an eighth of an inch deeper into a wet seam.

Below me, the stilt-walkers react to my pluck. They pour out of the floor-urns by the thousands, a pale, silent tide streaming past my calcified thighs, migrating toward the eastern revetment where the urns crushed, eager to lick the fresh, wet lime sweating from the broken seams before the wall can tip.

I pull my hand back. I rest my thumb against the cool, wet edge of a floor tile. 

The wind returns to its low B-flat. The water continues to drip—*pock, pock, pock*—into the engineered channels. The lime-water runs down my spine, leaving behind its microscopic deposit of white dust.

By next season, my waist will be stone. By the season after that, my chest will no longer heave, and the slate shingle pinned against my ribs will be locked in place forever, held by a pillar that used to have a name, but now has only a pitch, a frequency, and a weight.

I am fine with this. The basin is vast, the dark is cold, and the wet mud never truly finishes drying. Someone must hold the spires while the mortar sets.
