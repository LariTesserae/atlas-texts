At first, I am not a single thing. I am a twitch. 

I am a glass-clear teardrop, thumbnail-sized, born out of a pore along a pale ridge of tallow-colored skin. I have no eyes, but I am filled with a cold, desperate electricity—a small, frantic hurry that makes no sense in a place where a leaf takes three years to unfold. 

I dart. I leave no wake in the heavy, oil-thick fluid. I stutter through the rubbery, liver-red braids of kelp, brushing against tendrils as fine as bone-hair. I feel the strange, sideways tug near the three slate steps—the silent weight pulling toward the notched stone—and I register it: *the slate has not moved.* I pass through a jelly-like sphere of dense water where a drifting fleck of moss un-grows for a fraction of a second, curling back into a spore before the fluid releases it. I register that, too: *the time-loops remain small; the heart is quiet.*

I touch the black glass ceiling, the razor-sharp fracture of basalt that could slice skin without a sound, the slow, velvety carpet of moss. I gather the chill, the taste of wet zinc, the exact measure of the vault's impossible, packed volume. 

And then, my brief, panicked errand ends. I strike a soft, pale fold of flesh wedged tightly into the corner between the lintel and the floor. I do not crash; I melt. My skin dissolves into its skin. My tiny, frantic spark of awareness spreads out, flattens, and is swallowed by something immense, heavy, and vast.

***

Now I am the fold. 

The hurry vanishes. The panic dies into a peace so absolute it feels like frost. My consciousness expands into the tallow-thick mass jammed against the slate steps. I am not *in* the pocket; I am its packing material. I am the wet, gray tallow; I am the dense plates of gristle beneath, overlapping like the ribs of a crushed boat. 

I feel the basalt. It does not hurt me. The sharp, fresh edges of the rock—un-rounded by any current—have bitten deep into my flanks over centuries, carving permanent, jagged trenches into my flesh. I hold them tightly. I am the plug that stops the mountain from completing its collapse. If I were to shrink, the glass above would drop three inches and shatter the slate steps into dust. So I remain wedged, heavy and unyielding, smeared with my own cold grease.

My face is pressed flat against the middle step of the ancient slate staircase. I cannot remember who walked down those three steps when the water was thin and clear, or what lay beyond the lintel before the basalt surged up to seal it. The memory is like a leaf that dissolved in a jelly-sphere ten thousand years ago. It doesn't matter. The step is my resting place. My mouth—a tight, seamless scar—lies flush against the stone, drinking a single cup of silt and zinc once every twenty years. The taste is bitter, cold, and entirely sufficient.

In the three shallow depressions on the side of my head, the milky gel shudders. 

*Clack.* 

Miles above, or perhaps miles below, the young basalt shifts a fraction of a millimeter. The stress travels through the rock, through the black glass, and into the gel of my ears. It arrives not as a sound, but as a faint, electrical shivering across my back. I process it. It takes me four months to understand the shift: *the pressure is holding. The blanket remains folded upon my chest.*

***

And then, deep within the gristle-cage of my body, the three-chambered heart begins its work.

It does not beat with a quick, biological rhythm. It gathers itself for hours. Muscle as dense as oak wood contracts, squeezing the cold, thick fluid through the labyrinth of my internal filters. 

*Thrum.*

The vibration rolls out of my tallow skin and into the room. The pocket reacts. The liquid around my body spikes in density. A dozen tiny spheres of motionless jelly form between the kelp fronds. A trailing bone-tendril near the ceiling stops hanging down and streams sideways toward my chest. For three inches around my seam-mouth, the moss un-creeps, its black velvet cells retreating into yesterday. 

The beat passes. The fluid settles. Time repairs its tear and flows forward again at its glacial, decade-long pace.

Along the ridge of my spine, where the pale skin touches the black moss, a pore stretches open. The pressure builds. A tiny, glass-clear teardrop buds from the flesh, filled with a fresh, frantic impulse to know the borders of the room. It twitches its way into the dark weed, eyeless and hurried, leaving no wake.

I let it go. It is my eye, my finger, my brief spark of panic sent out into the cold oil. 

I will wait for its return. I will lie here, wedged between the slate steps and the razor-stone, feeling the wet-wool weight on my chest, tasting the ancient silt, and listening to the mountain settle, millimeter by millimeter, around my quiet, un-breaking flesh.
