The ash-wood rod is three feet long, capped at the far end with a flattened button of cold lead. 

You do not hold it with your hands alone. You grip the middle with two calloused fingers to steady the weight, but the blunt, rounded end is wedged firm against the right side of your lower jaw, right where the bone turns up toward the ear. 

At first, there is only the general roar—the thick, wet thunder of the Sump that everyone carries in their skull whether they are awake or sleeping. But as you press the lead tip harder against the canvas wrapping of the six-inch feeder line, the ambient noise falls away. The air-borne clatter of the swifts and the dry, paper-like scrapings of a million beetle legs vanish, filtered out by the bone. 

Through your jaw, straight into the inner ear, comes the real voice of the vault.

It is a high, thin whistling—not a whistle of air, but of water stretched so tight by heat and three hundred pounds of pressure that it sounds like a violin string being pulled through gravel. *Shhh-t-t-t-t.* The canvas under the lead cap is damp, dark, and soft with rot, but three inches deeper, behind the wet hemp twine and the red-lead paste, the cast iron is thinning. A pit the size of a thumb-nail is eating outward from the interior curve where the pipe turns six inches above the flooded trench. 

It has another two weeks. Maybe three, if the firemen don't over-feed the hearths under Boiler Four during the shift-change.

***

You step back, pulling the rod from your jaw. The world floods back in all at once: the suffocating, tallow-smelling wet, the orange glare of a furnace door slinging long, sharp shadows across the wet basalt, the wet *plop* of a fat condensation drop hitting the crown of your shaved, grease-slicked head. 

You are sitting in the belly of an eight-foot iron punt, wedged into the crawl-space beneath Catwalk Seven. 

Above your head, eighteen inches up, the cast-iron grates groan and rattle. Every few seconds comes the heavy, iron-shod *clack-clack* of a shift-worker’s sabots passing overhead, dropping tiny bits of mud and crushed beetle shell down through the grid into the dark water around your boat. 

Here, down in the cuts, the water is six inches deep, running over a bed of raw, unweathered basalt chips that were left behind when the drills broke the floor thirty years ago. The water is ninety degrees, milky with limestone dust and streaked with yellow ribbons of boiler-grease. 

You do not wear sabots while you are in the punt. Your feet are bare, the grey-pink skin of your soles long since bleached and softened into a thick, rubbery hide that doesn't feel the sharp edges of the stone anymore. Your trousers are rolled to the knee, held with tarred string. Your chest is bare, crossed by the faint, shimmering white maps of old steam-scalds—keloids across the left ribs where a relief valve blew its packing when you were twelve.

Beside your knee in the boat lies your gear: 
*   Two spare ash rods of different lengths.
*   A wooden mallet and three iron wedges wrapped in oily rags.
*   A copper-mesh scoop net, green with verdigris at the rim.
*   A wicker basket half-filled with dark, squirming weight—thumb-sized beetles scooped from the main drain pipes while you waited for the steam pressure to drop.

You are a Caulker, but down here in the dark under the catwalks, the titles don't mean much. You are simply the thing that lives in the gaps where the iron meets the stone.

***

You don't move quickly. To move quickly in the Sump is to waste water from a body that cannot afford to lose it. Your breathing is shallow, drawn through the nose, taking in the hot fog in short, measured sips. 

You reach out with the copper net, dipping it under the edge of the pipe where the warm overflow spills out. You pull it back with three blind crabs thrashing in the wire mesh. They are small ones, pale as boiled pork, their long legs tangling in the wire. You knock them against the iron gunwale of the punt—*tap, tap, tap*—a quick, practiced flick of the wrist that cracks the carapace behind the eyes and stops their twitching. You toss them into the corner of the punt behind your boots, where they sit among the wet iron chips.

A shift is ending somewhere above. You know this not by a clock—there are no clocks in the Sump, only the stroke-count of the pumps—but because the main exhaust pipe along the rear wall gives three long, shuddering coughs. *Huuuuh-boff. Huuuuh-boff. Huuuuh-boff.*

The air grows slightly thicker, if such a thing is possible. The amber filaments in the filthy glass globes fifty feet away dim to a dull, cherry-red thread as the dynamos slow down, then brighten again as the secondary engines take the load.

In the dark beneath the catwalk, a pale eel, thick as your forearm, glides out from under a submerged basalt ledge. It smells the crushed crabs at your feet. It moves without ripple, its milky, sightless eye-spots passing within an inch of your heel. You don't strike at it. The eels are the Sump's cleaners; they keep the grease-clots from backing up in the narrow drain-joints. As long as the eel moves, the channel is clear.

***

You lift the ash rod again. Not to work, this time, but to rest.

You press the lead cap not against a pipe, but against the sheer face of the basalt wall where the punt is moored. You put your jaw to the wood.

The rock does not whistle like the pipes. The rock speaks in a lower register. Through three miles of solid slate above and the deep geothermal roots below, the stone carries the pulse of the whole city. You can feel the heavy, five-second stroke of the Great Cornish Pump down in the Seventh Deep; you can feel the tiny, shivering hum of the water-wheel three vaults over; you can even feel the distant, faint vibration of the iron coal-tubs being dragged along the high rails toward the upper galleries where the sun-dwellers live.

It is a good sound. It is cold stone holding hot iron, and hot iron holding warm life.

You reach into your pocket and pull out a piece of chitin-bread, hard and cold, wrapped in a scrap of canvas. You break off a corner with your back teeth—it tastes of bitter copper, salt, and old smoke—and chew it slowly, letting the moisture of the air soften it on your tongue.

Above you, the *clack-clack-clack* of the sabots grows dense, then thins out. A child’s voice—shrill and short—calls out a four-note signal from the high girders, answered by the flat, metallic slap of an iron door closing on Tenement Three.

You sit in the dark, warm water, your skin wet with the vault’s breath, your jaw resting on the wood, listening to the rock grind itself slowly against the machinery. You do not need to be anywhere else. The Sump is full, the pipes are holding, and the water is warm.
