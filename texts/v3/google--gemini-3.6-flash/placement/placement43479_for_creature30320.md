The sound does not enter through ears. I have none. 

It comes up through the flat, pale soles of my feet—a deep, trembling cadence that rises out of the heavy basalt flagstones and travels up the long, three-jointed bones of my legs, settling into the marrow of my pelvis before it ever reaches my throat. It is the pulse of the frogs, the high rasp of the crickets, the slow, forty-mile-per-hour heartbeat of the Leviathans down in the dark flues below, all welded together into a single, physical density. The air is so thick with sound that it feels like wading through warm, wet silt.

I am standing on the grand landing of the Ninth Tier, where the west-facing aqueduct curves around the shoulder of the mountain. My spine is curved forward, the long, heavy arc of my neck hanging low so that my head hovers just two feet above the wet flagstones. 

I have been in this spot since the star-moss on my left shoulder last shed its spores—a cycle I measure not by days or moons, but by the slow creeping of its green fringe across the indigo rubber of my collarbone. 

Where my eyes would be, if my kind had ever needed to see the dark, my skull widens into a flat, sloped crest. Across it, thousands of fine, translucent cilia stand upright in the warm gloom. They do not twitch randomly; they tilt like river weed in a current, drinking the thick, damp air. They read the air’s weight, its percentage of mineral salt, its temperature as it rises off the sun-less stone, and, above all, the shape of the noise.

To me, the wall of sound is not loud. It is geometry.

A croak from a thick-thighed frog on the lip of a cistern three terraces down does not simply exist; it strikes the vertical basalt wall behind it, bounces off the curved cornice above, and folds back across the wet flagstones. Where the stone is smooth and unbroken, the echo returns sharp, bright, and cold. Where a joint has opened by a fraction of a millimeter, or where a blanket of moss has grown too thick, the sound softens, casting a small acoustic shadow. 

My cilia gather these shadows. I am reading a map made entirely of echoes.

Right now, beneath the broad, three-jointed palm of my right hand, I feel a tiny, wrong vibration.

It is a block in the seventh riser of the staircase to my left. It is a key-stone, six tons of dense, fine-grained basalt, hand-cut thirty thousand years ago to hold the weight of the promenade above. A root from the broad-leaf tree in the upper well—a root no thicker than my smallest finger—has crept through the dry joint and found a pocket of trapped condensation behind the face. The root is drinking. It is expanding. The stone is holding, but its pitch has changed. When the flat baritone of a canopy bird strikes that step, the echo comes back dull, like wet wood instead of ringing iron.

I do not hurry. Hurry is a crack through which ruin enters.

It takes me forty breaths to shift my weight from my right leg to my left. My foot lifts—a long, slow peel of pale skin from wet stone, creating a small, suctioning *pop* that the Grip-Hounds three levels up will read as a marker of my position. I move my right foot forward three inches. The basalt beneath is warm, holding the deep, thermal radiation generated by the Leviathans in the sumps miles below. That heat flows into my skin, keeping my thick blood fluid, keeping my joints limber enough to work.

A Grip-Hound slips past my hip. It is silent, a shadow made of slate-colored muscle and vacuum-pads. It does not touch me, but its long, ribless flank brushes the dangling fringe of my shoulder-moss. I feel the cool, slick draft of its passage across my cilia—a brief, dark streak in the acoustic canvas—and then it is gone, dropping twenty feet down a sheer wall to take a heavy moth from a fern frond.

I reach the seventh step as the wet twilight deepens—or perhaps it does not deepen; perhaps it is simply the fog thickening in the canopy above. Time here does not flow; it accumulates, like lime in a drainage spout.

I kneel. The process takes several minutes. My long, pale knees touch the damp star-moss that borders the step. I extend the fingers of my left hand—long, spade-tipped, tipped with thick, horn-like nails that have never touched metal or soil, only stone and bark.

I sink my index finger into the joint between the six-ton block and its neighbor. The gap is narrow, no wider than a beetle’s wing, but my finger finds the wet, fibrous filament of the root.

I do not cut it. If I cut it, it will rot inside the joint, and the rotting organic matter will draw midges, which will draw acid, which will pit the basalt in two hundred years. 

Instead, I apply pressure.

I do not use the fast, twitching strength of a predator. I use the slow, crushing weight of my body, leaning my nine-foot frame forward until my forehead nearly touches the flagstone. I press against the root-tip with a steady, isometric force, holding it against the cold basalt interior. I am telling the tree: *There is no room here. Turn back toward the cistern.*

I hold the pressure for two hours. My breathing slows until it is almost unnoticeable—one long inhalation every six minutes, filling my wide, flat chest with the damp, frog-scented air, then releasing it so softly that the cilia on my head barely sway. Around my legs, a dozen Silt-Scrapers crawl over my ankles, their chitinous undersides combing the algae from the stone around my toes, completely unbothered by my presence. I am merely a higher terrace to them, a temporary column of warm skin.

Gradually, deep within the dark joint, I feel the minute, fibrous collapse of the root’s forward cells. It surrenders the gap. It will alter its path now, veering left toward the open stone planter where the soil is deep and rich with leaf-mud from the cisterns.

I draw my finger back. The stone gives a tiny, microscopic sigh as the hydraulic pressure of the plant tissue releases. The pitch of the step rises. The echo clears.

It is time to Sing.

I stand. The movement takes time, my long spine uncoiling vertebra by vertebra until I am towering over the terrace lip, my moss-draped shoulders almost reaching the lower leaves of the broad-tree canopy. I tilt my sloped, earless head upward, toward the vaulted darkness where the high architraves vanish into fog.

I open my mouth. My jaw drops low, extending on wide, fleshy hinges. 

I take in a breath that empties the air around my face, pulling the fine moisture-beads directly off my cilia. And then I let it go.

It is not a shout. It is a single, low, flat note—a baritone resonance that starts deep in the chest flues behind my ribs and pours out over my lower lip like heavy oil. 

*BOOOOOOOOO-OM.*

The sound leaves me and immediately strikes the wet basalt face of the Ninth Tier. It travels sideways along the sweeping curve of the terrace, strikes the far abutment, and leaps down into the darkness of the Eighth, the Seventh, the Sixth. It cascades down the grand staircases like a physical wave of water, striking every lintel, every cistern lip, every carved drainage chute.

For three seconds, the valley goes dead silent. 

The millions of green frogs hold their breath. The crickets lock their legs. The heavy-bodied birds in the canopy freeze on their perches. The entire living wall of noise stops to let my note run its course.

It travels down, down, down—miles into the indigo gloom—until it strikes the steaming surface of the lowest basin. It bounces off the slick, forty-foot brow of a resting Leviathan, picks up the deep, low thermal thrum of the beast’s heart, and turns back upward.

It returns to me as an echo, but it is no longer just my voice. It is a complete, three-dimensional blueprint of sixteen square miles of dark, wet stone.

I receive it through my cilia, through my skin, through the soles of my feet.

*The cistern on the Twelfth Tier is clear.*
*The third gutter on the Eastern Ramp is sloughing its silt correctly.*
*A flagstone on the Fourth Promenade has settled three millimeters to the south; the Silt-Scrapers are already gathering there in their hundreds to dam the leak.*
*And deep in the windowless chambers beneath my feet, in the silent, dripping dark of the Sump-Cradles... an old one, a Tender who walked these steps five hundred years before I was spawned, has finally stopped breathing.*

His metabolic heat has faded completely now. The mineral water dripping from the vault ceiling has covered his knuckles in a fine, grey crust of calcium. He has become stone. He is part of the seventh riser now. He is holding up the floor of the valley.

I close my mouth.

The silence breaks. The frogs explode back into their deafening, rhythmic roar. The crickets strike their rasping legs together with renewed fury. The birds boom from the dark canopy. The wall of sound closes back in, dense and solid as basalt, wrapping around my long, blue body like a wet blanket.

I turn my head slowly toward the west stair. There is a drain three hundred steps down that needs dredging before the high fog drops its night-sweat.

I lift my foot. I take one step.

I am the wet friction that keeps the mountain from turning to dust. I have nowhere else to be.
