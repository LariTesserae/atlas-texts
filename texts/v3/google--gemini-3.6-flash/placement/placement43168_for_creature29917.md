I am not on the surface of the High Sieve, nor am I down in the black, sap-choked darkness where the Pith-Eels drive the deep hydraulics. 

I am at the edge of the Third Shelf, where a mile-wide swell of petrified iron-oak breaks into a series of long, curving terraces. Here, the mist is so thick it feels like a second skin, warm and scented with heart-resin and old rain.

I am a Knot-Keeper, and I have been sitting in this exact saucer of amber water for three hundred years.

***

My lower limbs are not visible. They vanished beneath the carpet of yellow liverwort long ago, unraveling into hundreds of pale, fibrous tendrils that have rooted themselves deep into the corky substrate. Through them, I do not feel cold or hard ground; I feel the movement of the world’s blood. Fifty miles beneath my hips, the continuous, low *thrum* of the vascular pipes vibrates upward through the compressed peat, passing into my thigh-bones, my spine, the base of my skull. I know when a Marrow-Plough turns its heavy cartilage snout three miles to the north, because the shift in the water table registers as a faint drop in pressure beneath my left knee.

My skin is the smooth, muted white of wet birch bark, perpetually slick with the acidic oil my body exudes. Where my chest and stomach rest against the wood-rock, the ancient heartwood is slowly yielding. I am not cutting it; I am unwriting it. The oil breaks down the ancient lignins, softening the petrified oak until it turns to a sweet, dark slurry that the liverworts drink. 

By the time I shift my weight—perhaps in another sixty years, when the sky-smudge turns a shade darker—a new step will have been formed. It will be smooth, rounded, perfectly cradling the rain that weeps from the canopy above. A Lung-Moth, heavy with thirty years of gathered mist, might rest its lace wings along the lip I am carving and drain its veins into the basin I leave behind.

My head is leaned forward. My forehead rests flat against the wall of a calcified reed-spire that my elder helped paste before she dissolved into the lower terrace. The spire rises thirty paces above me, its lime-crusted chimney gleaming faintly in the bronze gloom. Through the stone-like reed-work, I can feel the breath of the lower layers. 

As the upper canopy swells in the afternoon warmth, the spire exhales. The vibration travels down the chimney and directly into the bone above my eyes. It is a long, low whistle—a single, resonant *F-sharp* that lingers for hours. It tells me that the heartwood beneath this terrace is healthy, warm, and wet; it tells me that the Pith-Eels are active in the conduits below, keeping the sap from turning to dark amber.

I do not open my eyes often. There is little need for sight when the air itself is an illuminated fog and every shift in temperature is felt across the broad, sensitive rubber of my back. But when I do, I look up through the veil of translucent vine-sacs hanging from the overhead boughs. 

In one of those barrel-sized sacs, eighty feet above my head, a young one is floating. 

I can see its pale, jelly-clear shape suspended in the tea-colored water inside the sac, drifting slowly as the wind moves the vine. It has been there for seventy years now. It does not know speech, or distance, or time; it knows only the warmth of the fluid, the rhythmic sound of my spire whistling below, and the pale, bronze smudge of the sun sliding back and forth across its small sky. In another thirty years, its skin will thicken to birch-white. Its weight will burst the bottom of the sac, and it will drop softly into the yellow moss beside me. 

I will not speak to it when it lands. I will simply extend a tendril from my foot to touch its small ankle, offering it its first taste of the deep subterranean pulse, inviting it to sink its body into the wood and begin the long work of resting.

***

If you were to walk along the terrace edge, you might not see me at first. You would see only a rounded, pale rise in the liverwort, slick with rain, nestled between two massive shelf-fungi that have hardened into amber ledges. You might mistake the back of my neck for a smooth burl of birch, or my long, folded arms for the exposed roots of a vanished tree.

Only if you stood very still—if you let the mist settle in your hair and listened past the endless *tink-plop* of the dripping fronds—would you hear my lungs. They move once every three minutes, a long, sighing expansion that draws the wet pine-scented air deep into my ribs, mixing it with the resin of the plateau, before letting it go back into the gloom.

I do not wonder what lies beneath the fifty miles of heartwood. I do not wonder if there is stone at the bottom of the world, or an ocean, or nothing at all. The ground is living, the air is drinking, and the water is soft.

I will stay here until my heart slows to the rhythm of the deep pipes. When the time comes, my oil will change. It will cease to dissolve the wood-rock and begin to match it. The moss will creep up over my shoulders, weaving its yellow threads through the tendrils of my chin. My spine will fuse with the terrace wall, and the whistle of the reed-spire will pass through my calcified skull forever, keeping the air moving in the dark places below.

I am not a visitor on the High Sieve. I am simply the shape the wood takes when it wants to listen to itself breathe.
