I am on the seventh tier, south face, sitting on the third step down from the bridge-peg. 

My thighs fit precisely into the smooth, shallow bowl worn into the shale by three hundred years of knees and moss-slippers. The stone beneath my thighs is wet, but it does not chill me; the thin, clear oil my skin secretes holds the damp away like a leaf shedding dew. My center of gravity sits low, deep in my hips, heavy as a pig-iron weight dropped in a well. 

In my left hand I hold a small, flat palm-block of polished lard-wood, black with age and soaked in crayfish fat. In my right, a thin bone needle split from the leg of a plume-bird, its point ground flat and notched like a weaver’s hook.

I am not looking up at the sky. The sky is merely a long, cold slit of blinding white that bakes the top of my brow, but down here on the seventh tier, the light does not fall—it bounces. It strikes the saturated quartz seam behind my left shoulder, splits into a shadowless, silver glare, and fills the five-foot gap between my knee and the opposite balcony with an illumination so clear it hurts the back of the eyes. I can see the individual, hair-like veins on the underside of a liverwort leaf hanging from my neighbor’s gutter across the lane. I can see the tiny, golden ring around the pupil of a slate salamander sitting three inches from my toe.

My work today is a split in the third shale course beneath the lard-wood joist. 

A root from the young ash hanging in the upper crevice has grown too ambitious. It has pushed its white, tender snout into a micro-fissure between two dry-stacked blocks, swelling with cold condensation. Left alone for twenty years, it would thicken, turn wood-hard, and lever the lower block half an inch out toward the abyss. 

I do not cut it. Nothing is cut here if it can be steered.

With the bone needle, I tease the pale root-tip out of the crack, feeling its slight, wet resistance. My fingers are short, thick-padded, and completely hairless, the skin at the tips scarred into tough, grey calluses that have no grain. I coax the tip sideways, guiding it along a pre-carved groove in the lower stone toward a deeper, safer seam where four blocks meet. I tuck it in, then follow with a tiny plug of crushed fern-rhizome and river-silt, packing it tight with the flat of the bone tool. 

The stone accepts the root. The root accepts the stone. They go back to sleep.

As I work, a pulse travels up through the soles of my moss-slippers, through the heavy bone of my shins, and settles in my pelvis. 

It is a double-tap, crisp and dry. Someone on the ninth tier—four houses to the east—has struck a lard-wood post with the heel of their hand. *Step back. Water coming.*

I do not move my head. I do not look up. A second later, a fat, clear drop falls straight through the shadowless glare, missing my shoulder by an inch, and hits the broad fern frond below me with a tiny, muted *ptht*. The fern dips, rises, and holds still again.

Across the five-foot lane, on the balcony level with my chin, my sister’s daughter comes out through the oiled-skin curtain. She is carrying a flat clay bowl of salted river-crayfish and sour fern-bread. She does not call out; her throat is silent, her lips closed. Instead, she sits on her own ledge, rests her heel against the main upright timber, and taps twice with her big toe.

The vibration ripples down her timber, across the ironwood bridge-peg, through the stone ledge, and into my left hip. 

I look at her. Her eyes are wide, pale silver, the dark rings around her irises expanded to take in the wet glare. She tilts her left shoulder three degrees forward, holds her wrist loose, and taps three fingers against her inner forearm: *The lowest trough is clear. The pale ones are fat today.*

I tilt my head back just enough for the brow-ridge to shield my eyes from the zenith, then tap two knuckles against the side of my knee: *Bring the salt-pot when the light shifts.*

She nods once—a movement so small a bird would miss it—and begins to eat, picking the grey meat from a cracked claw with her thumb-nail. 

A plume-bird drops from the lard-wood soffit above us. It does not fly so much as fall, its short, blunt wings making no sound as it catches a thick cushion of jade moss three inches from my bone needle. It is a ball of grey-violet fur, no larger than my fist, its dark, bead-like eyes entirely unbothered by my hand. It tilts its head, snatches a dormant beetle larva from the wet mortar-line, and swallows it whole. Its tiny heart batters against its ribcage—a rapid, silent drumbeat I can feel through the shale beneath my thigh.

I stay still until the bird finishes preening its fat, woolly chest and hops into the dark shadow beneath my step.

Time here does not move forward; it deepens. It sinks into the stone like the condensation that trickles endlessly down the quartz seams. The cold is inside my lungs, inside my bones, but my blood is thick, slow, and warm, stoked by the sour rhizome bread and the heavy density of my own flesh. 

I reach into the horn pot tied to my belt, scoop a pat of grey crayfish fat onto my palm, and begin to rub the underside of the lard-wood rail above my head. My hand moves in long, rhythmic circles. *Shuck. Shuck. Shuck.* The oiled skin of my forearm glides over the cured timber, leaving it dark, shining, and impervious to the eternal sweat of the earth.

I will finish this rail before the zenith light moves three finger-widths across the wet wall opposite. Then I will sit here for another hour, watching the yellow-spotted salamander move its front left foot, waiting for the stone to tell my heels who is coming up the steps.
