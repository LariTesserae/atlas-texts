It begins not with a shape, but with a weight in the ribs. 

The sound does not enter through the ears; it settles directly into the sternum—a violent, continuous hum that makes the teeth ache in their sockets. It is the voice of the ceiling, five hundred feet above, bouncing off the raw glass of the basalt and driving downward into the gravel troughs. To exist here is to be hummed into solidity by millions of throat-rasps that never stop to catch a breath.

I am down low, where the cold gathers its grease. 

I am wedged between two slabs of black, razored talus, where the air is four degrees warmer than the vault because three hundred flanks are pressed together in the dark. The damp does not soak me. It cannot reach the skin through an inch of grey tallow that smells of iron and cold, turned clay; the heavy drops strike the coarse guard-hairs along my spine, gather into fat, clear beads, and roll down my ribs to drop with a wet *shuck* into the puddle beneath my belly.

My eyes are open, but they tell me very little. They are wide, pale horizontal slits that do not flicker when a cloud of hard-shelled beetles tumbles past. They register only the stillness of the violet gloom—a single, unchanging shade of bruised twilight that clings to the sweating rock. I do not look for shapes. I feel the valley in my hoof-bases: the sharp, metallic ping of a slipping slab three miles to the east; the light, wet skitter of the earless black quadrupeds sliding beneath my thighs to lick the grease from my hocks; the heavy, synchronized sloshing of three stomachs turning over cold moss within the flank pressed against mine.

My head is heavy, held close to the flinty floor by the mass of my neck. 

I reach forward with the broad, spade-like curve of my left horn. It is flattened at the tip, worn smooth and dark from forty years of grinding against glassy rock. I drive it into a hairline fracture in the basalt where the pale, rubber-like lichen grows in thin sheets. 

*Scritch. Scritch.*

The horn bites. The rubbery map yields with a wet, tearing sound. I sweep the spade forward, gathering a curl of cold, veinless green against the wet stone, and draw it back into my mouth with a broad, grey tongue. It tastes of river-silt, wet iron, and ancient, trapped rainwater that has never seen a tree. I chew slowly, mechanically, my broad molars grinding the lichen into paste while the internal heat of my throat turns the forty-four-degree damp into a thin plume of steam that rises from my nostrils and dissolves into the general mist.

Sometimes, the air changes. 

It does not grow lighter or warmer, but a thin thread of *dryness* leaks down from the high rifts—a draft that smells of nothing, of empty air and dead grass from a place where the stone ends. 

With that dryness come the tall, soft things. 

I do not hear them walk; their feet make no iron-clink upon the talus. But I smell the absence of grease on them. They smell of cold skin and dry chert, and their ears are stuffed with pale down that stinks of bird-fat. They move along the edges of the herd like shadows made of cold air. 

When they strike, it is not with a roar. It is a sudden, sharp displacement of weight—a heavy wedge driven into a spine, a short, wet sigh from an old bull two lengths away, and then the smell of warm, copper-heavy blood spilling onto the black gravel. 

We do not stampede for the dry-smelling things. There is nowhere to run in the dark, and the valley has no edges we can reach. The herd simply shifts. We roll our broad, slate-grey shoulders against one another, closing the gap where the bull fell, absorbing his warm space into our own. The tall ones work quickly in the violet gloom; I hear the heavy *crunch-clack* of their basalt hand-axes splitting his longbones for the cold tallow within, the wet slap of his hide being peeled back from the rib-cage. 

Then they are gone, climbing back up into the dry seams with their packs slung heavy, leaving behind only the jagged chert flakes and the cracked, empty marrow-bones. 

Before the dry smell has even faded from the air, the black, sleek quadrupeds are already wriggling through the gaps in our legs, their wet snouts squeaking against the stone as they lick the dark grease from the basalt. High above, the bird-roar swells slightly as a hundred waxy-feathered bodies drop from the ledges to catch the rising thermal of the blood, then settles back into its flat, endless rasp.

I do not turn my head. I do not look for the bull who was beside me. 

I press my belly deeper into the wet gravel trough, feeling the heat of my own mass gather beneath me, trapped between my thick hide and the cold basalt floor. I stretch my neck forward until the cold iron tip of my spade-horn finds the next seam in the glass. 

The water drops from the high vault. *Plink. Plink.*
The roosting fowl cry out into the dark, measuring the distance to my back.
I strike the stone, and eat.
