There is no sensation of warmth, because warmth implies a contrast with cold, and cold does not exist in the rift. There is only *load*.

Heat is not a ambient condition here; it is a physical lever. It enters through the four hematite pads wedged into the concave socket of the fifth terrace step. It travels up through the lower segments—compressed iron-dust held in dark, glassy matrices of fossilized resin—and reaches the bimetallic spine. 

On the inner curve of each joint, the iron-rich layer expands rapidly under the radiant pulse of the floor. On the outer curve, the laminated slate resists. The result is not a muscle flex, but a slow, irresistible arching. The spine bows forward. A joint creaks—a dry, high-pitched *skrrr-tick*—and my upper torso, a heavy, triangular lattice of dark slag and iron, drives itself three inches deeper into the recess beneath the granary lintel.

I am wedged here. I have been wedged here through forty thousand explosions of the sulfur-canes. 

I do not inhabit this terrace; for this hour of the heat-cycle, I am its flying buttress. The shale wall behind me is creeping outward, pushed by the endless, microscopic expansion of the basalt cliff face. My upper limbs—broad, paddle-shaped slabs of dense hematite, worn flat by centuries of grinding against tufa—are locked against the lintel stone. My lower joints are locked into the staircase. When the canyon groans and shifts its weight, the force does not break the wall; it passes through my iron spine, down into the stairs, and dissipates into the dry pumice below.

I do not see the valley. I have no lenses, no eyes, no interior mirror to catch the thin violet slit of non-heat high above. I map the rift through friction and frequency.

From above, carried down through the sheer rock walls, comes the glass-note. It is a long, hollow chord, produced where the rising column of superheated gas cuts through the vitreous lace of a Chime-Weaver stretched across the canyon neck. The note is continuous, but its pitch modulates as the draft swells. 

When the chord rises to a sharp, thin whistling, the heat below is at its peak. My joints bind tighter; the slate plates groan against the amber resin. 

When the chord drops into a low, chest-rattling hum, the draft has slowed. The bimetallic layers begin their tiny, microscopic contraction. The pressure on my spine eases. The hematite paddles slip an eighth of an inch down the face of the lintel with a sound like a heavy chisel dragging across dry slate.

*Crack.*

A quarter-mile down the rift, a mature stalk splits. The shockwave travels through the air as a brief spike in pressure, but through the basalt floor, it arrives as a sharp, clean acoustic ring. 

Seconds later, the Clickers arrive. 

They do not care that I am acting as masonry. To them, I am simply higher ground, a ridge of cool iron-slag rising out of the pumice dust. They come in a frantic, cascading hail—dozens of them, tiny burs of quartz and slag, snapping their curved resin legs against the stone steps. *Tack-tack-tack-tack.* They scramble over my lower limbs, their sticky palps loaded with fresh, mustard-yellow sulfur powder. 

I feel their static charges prickling across my slate plates. As they climb over my main joint, one of them slips, its quartz shell knocking sharply against the hairline fracture in my fourth segment. 

The fracture does not hurt; it *sings*. It vibrates at a higher harmonic than the rest of my frame, a tiny, buzzing ring that responds to every click of the gatherers' legs.

The Clickers disappear through the lipless doorway behind me, carrying their cargo into the windowless hearth-room. Within minutes, the chemical reaction begins inside the chamber. I feel it through the masonry at my back: a low, creeping rise in localized pressure as the sulfur meets the hematite dust on the hot floor, releasing the dense, invisible gas that vents out over my left shoulder. The warm draft ripples past my lattice, vibrating the thin resin flanges along my sides.

This is the breath of the engine. I brace the stone so the room does not buckle; the room bakes the sulfur so the gas will rise; the gas plays the Weavers above; the Weavers' chord tells my bimetallic spine when to hold and when to yield.

Eventually—not now, but in some future cycle when the sulfur-canes have split ten thousand more times—the hairline fracture in my fourth segment will meet a peak thermal surge. The amber resin inside the joint has grown pale and friable from centuries of baking. It will not bend; it will shear. 

The joint will lock permanently at a thirty-degree angle. I will no longer be able to flex when the basalt contracts. My lower limbs will slip from the socket in the step, and I will tilt forward, coming to rest against the wall of the terrace.

I will stay there as the dust settles into my lattice work. 

Later, another Maintainer will climb the rounded steps, its heavy hematite scoops clicking against the stone. It will not lift me, nor mourn me; it will assess my density against the acoustic feedback of the wall. Finding me solid, it will drive its chisels into my brittle resin seams, shearing away my seized legs and laying my heavy, slate-armored torso flat across the threshold of the hearth-room to serve as a new, unyielding lintel.

And I will go on holding up the stone, completely filled by the deep, dry vibration of the rift, while the gas whistles through the door below me and the glass lace sings in the purple dusk above.
