You don’t start with a body. In this place, a body is just a temporarily organized heap of the same wet silt and mineral paste that coats the floor, warmed to eighty-four degrees and given thirty minutes to breathe before the mist comes back down.

So you start with the temperature.

There is a line—invisible, perfectly horizontal—about four feet off the limestone floor. Below it, the air is thick and stagnant, heated from beneath by the earth’s deep, iron-warm ribs. Above it, the air is slightly cooler, carrying the faint, green-bruised chill of the sky seam sixty feet up.

You exist precisely where those two air masses press against one another.

At first, you are only the skin on the back of something. It is a wide, wet expanse, folded like heavy oilcloth, and it is cold on top where the ceiling-drops hit, but burning underneath where the belly touches the stone. Things are growing in the folds. You can feel their tiny, thread-like rhizoids working into your pores—not painful, just a faint, persistent itching that tastes like copper and green sap. The liverworts are thirsty today. They drink the sweat that pooling in the hollow behind your neck.

Then the vibration comes through. Not through ears—there are no ears to speak of, only the broad, flat bone of the lower jaw pressed flat against the terrace.

*Thump.*

A quarter-inch shift in the basalt three feet away. A velvet-head has landed on a fern frond; its weight bent the stalk; the stalk brushed an air-root; the air-root transmitted the tremor down into the dark lintel.

*Thump-thump.*

The message is simple: *The moths are low.*

You open your mouth. It isn't a gesture of hunting or anger; it is simply an unfolding, a slow, wet parting of pale, lip-less tissue. The air inside your throat is hotter than the vault—ninety degrees, thick with the fermented tang of digested moss and the sweet, heavy alcohol of bruised orchids. You exhale.

The cloud goes out from you in a soft, grey puff that visible only as a blurring of the twilight. It hangs in the humid air between the four carved steps.

Above, the root-tangles shiver. You feel the microscopic displacement of air as hundreds of wingless marrow-moths drop from the undersides of the leather-ferns, drawn down by the sap-gas. They fall like fat, heavy seeds through the dark. Behind them come the soft, sticky *slap-slap-slap* of velvet-head palms jumping from vine to vine, trailing their musky heat-signatures through the mist.

Down in the silt by your left hind paddle, the calcifiers hear the moths landing on your back. Their heavy, mineral-crusted backs scrape against the limestone as they turn—*crunch, crunch, sand under a trowel*—coming to harvest the wing-dust and droppings that spill into your skin-folds. Behind them, thin as a pencil and cool as rain, a milk-skink slides across your broad, violet flank, its belly-scales tickling the liverworts as it tracks an isopod into the shade of your ribs.

You are not watching any of this. You have no eyes to see the pale, clear frogs whose red hearts are beating against the basalt beside your left hand.

You feel the frogs only as tiny, rhythmic heat-dots—cooling down as they climb out of the water, warming up as they absorb the heat radiating from your hide. One of them crawls onto your third finger. Its pads are cold and wet, like little circles of ice. It leaves a thin, gelatinous trail across your skin before it hops toward the thumb-sized depressions in the dark lintel to lay its eggs.

Your fingers are already there.

They have been there since the light was a different color, three mist-cycles ago, or perhaps three centuries. The five broad, fleshy lobes of your right paw fit so perfectly into the three depressions in the ancient basalt that the stone and the flesh have reached a thermal truce: the basalt gives up its mineral density; your skin gives up its warm moisture. Where they meet, a thin layer of white calcium paste has formed, sealing you to the lintel like a lichen on an altar.

When the deep earth shudders—a shift so slight that even the air-roots hanging from the rim don't sway—it hits the basalt first.

The dark stone rings silently, a vibration too low for sound, traveling straight up through the bones of your wrists, into your shoulder girdle, and down the long, water-logged curve of your spine.

You feel the fault line three miles down. You feel the underground stream, long dried up, turning over in its sleep deeper in the limestone.

You breathe in. The air tastes of rot, crushed fronds, and the cold, wet lime of the ceiling.

You hold the breath for thirty seconds.

In the dark around you, the skinks pause. The velvet-heads freeze on their vines, their heat-pits glowing red in the gloom. The frogs hold their miniature, scarlet hearts still against the rock. The whole small, wet, crowded world waits for the chest beneath the liverworts to drop.

You exhale.

The mist begins to form at the high rim. The pendulum swings. You sink an eighth of an inch deeper into the ancient steps, close your throat, and let the rain fall on your back.
