At first, there is no *I*. There is only the glare, and the wetness, and the sound of stone being dragged over stone—a long, soft sigh that never quite ends.

I am not above the basin looking down. I am down inside the cold, shadowless light, where the air smells of turned slate and crushed salt. 

My awareness begins in the heels of my feet. They are broad, flat, and bare, pressed into a quarter-inch of clear, freezing water that sits atop a flagstone of dark basalt. Through the water, through the bone of my ankles, comes the hum. It is a low, subterranean pulse—not a sound heard by the ear, but a tremor received behind the jaw, where two narrow slits of membrane drink the vibrations of the valley. 

I feel three miles of chalk wall shifting at once. I feel the soft thud of a petrified-wood lever prying a lintel from its seat. I feel the smooth, liquid slide of a ten-ton sledge five hundred yards to the north. 

I am one of the broad-shouldered ones. My skin is the color of wet marrow, slicked with a thin, salt-rich moisture that never dries under the unblinking roof. There is no warmth in me, nor do I miss it; my blood is cold, thick, and gray, moving with the sluggish, reliable rhythm of water pushing through deep mud. 

My eyes are open. They have been open for forty years, or three hundred—time here does not stack like blocks, it simply spreads out flat like the ankle-deep pool in the center of the plain. My irises are tarnished silver, completely filling the sockets, reflecting the brilliant, vein-riddled sky of glowing basalt above. Because there are no shadows—no high, no low, no corners where light fails—my eyes do not search. They simply accept the geometry.

Right now, I am standing still. 

My joints are locked in the rest-position—hips heavy, knees slightly bent, arms hanging down like pendulum weights of dense bone. A cluster of three cyan gliders has settled upon my broad, hairless left forearm. I can feel the microscopic, rhythmic twitching of their tendrils as they lick the salt-crust from my pores. Their pale, blue-green light pulses slowly against my bone-white skin—*glow, dim, glow*—a faint, cool combustion that carries no heat at all. I do not move to shoo them. They are merely the weather landing on my shoulder.

Beneath my toes, in the wet silt of the flagstone seam, a blind burrower wriggles past, its clear, ribbon-like body acting as a living lubricant between the stone blocks. I feel the tiny vibration of its passage.

Then, the shift comes.

It is not a command. No one has tapped against stone. No signal has traveled across the plaza. It is simply that the spatial logic of the basin has tilted, the way water tilts in a tray when one edge is raised. A long colonnade three hundred paces behind me has completed its purpose. The thought that constructed it is finished. The space must now become a terrace.

My locked joints loosen with a quiet, wet click. The gliders launch lazily from my arm, drifting upward into the damp, cool draft of the plain, their cyan bellies glowing soft against the white sky.

I turn. My movement is low, heavy, and silent. 

I walk across the wet limestone, my broad, spatulate toes gripping the slick bedrock with easy, practiced traction. Around me, fifty others are moving in the same direction, our paths intersecting without collision, our heavy, hairless bodies moving like fish through a dense current. We do not look at one another’s faces. We look at the stone.

We reach the base of a rising wall of hand-stacked chalk-blocks. I place my hands against the damp face of the bottom block. 

My fingertips—capped with those thick, horn-yellow pads of keratin—find the unmortared seam. I do not pull. I tap. 

*Clack. Clack-clack.*

A short, double-stroke of my knuckles against the soft, porous chalk. Through the masonry, fifty feet away, another worker feels the vibration through their own hands and inserts a wedge of soft copper into the joint above. I slip my fingers into the opened seam. My keratin pads grip the wet, chalky stone without slipping, the texture like coarse wet soap under my digits. 

We lift.

There is no strain in my chest, no swelling of breath, because I do not breathe as surface things do—there is no lung-heave, only a steady, passive exchange of cold, damp air through the skin itself. The ten-foot block comes away from the wall with a soft, suction-breaking *shluck*. We lower it onto a sledge of dense, pale whale-bone that rests in the gutter.

A soapstone crawler—long, segmented, its soft cartilage legs scraping the salt-slurry from the slate—scuttles under the edge of the sledge just as the weight settles. It does not crush; its tough, flexible body simply flattens into the slick groove, its chitinous mandibles grinding down the loose chalk powder we have spilled, clearing the path before us.

We take up the hemp ropes. They are thick as a man's wrist, stiff with wet mineral crusts. 

Four of us lean forward into the harnesses. We do not grunt. We do not count *one, two, three*. We simply fall into the forward tilt together, our broad hips driving down, our flat feet pushing through the shallow puddle on the flagstones. 

The sledge begins to slide. 

*Hiiiiiiss.*

It is the signature sound of our world: bone sliding over wet slate, cushioned by mud and clear water. It is a light, liquid noise, almost delicate, carrying across the vast, open plazas where thousands of other sledges are making the exact same sound in different keys.

We haul the block toward the center of the basin, toward the shallow pool where the quartz-cutters stand. 

I am not thinking about tomorrow. I am not thinking about the surface, a place I have no words for—a place of bizarre, terrifying phenomena like *darkness* and *green* and *falling water*. I am thinking the block. 

The block is heavy. The block is six feet wide. The block must go where the terrace meets the aqueduct. When it reaches that spot, it will stay for three hours, or three days, or three centuries, until the geometry demands that it move again. 

As we cross the central plaza, we pass one of the finished quartz eggs sitting at the margin of the water. It is eight feet tall, perfectly spherical, blindingly white under the ceiling-veins, its polished surface holding not a single seam, angle, or finger-hold. It holds only the pure, unblinking glare of the roof, reflected in a curved, distortion-free mirror.

Three workers are slowly, patiently rolling it toward the palisade cliffs at the edge of the world. They push with their foreheads and the flat palms of their hands, moving it an inch at a time across the butter-smooth bedrock. When they reach the soft slate talus at the cliff base, they will leave it in line with the others—row after row of white, unstackable eggs, resting forever in the shadowless cool.

Why? 

The question does not exist in the language of taps against stone. The egg is the end of the stroke. You rasp the quartz until the corners are gone. When the corners are gone, it cannot be a wall. When it cannot be a wall, it is complete. You roll it to the edge so the basin remains clear for the next wall, the next terrace, the next dismantling.

We arrive at the foot of the new terrace. We drop the hemp ropes. 

I step out of the harness, my wet, marrow-colored skin glistening under the cold sky. I place my horn-capped fingers against the newly laid chalk-foundation, feeling the long, subsonic hum of the earth coming up through the mountain's roots.

A glider, cyan-bright and weightless, drifts down from the upper colonnade and lands gently on the crown of my hairless head. Its tiny tendrils begin their soft, rhythmic sweeping across my scalp, gathering the cool salt.

I lock my knees. I drop my chin slightly toward my chest. 

The glare remains above me, steady, unblinking, eternal. The sound of dry stone dragging over wet glass fills the air around me like a endless rain that never falls. I enter the standing stillness, becoming a pillar in a city that will be somewhere else by nightfall.
