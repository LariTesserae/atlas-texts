I would not be on the flat basins among the crawling swarms, nor high in the thin blue where the mica-gliders circle. Those places are too busy with kinetic conversion; they are noisy with the immediate, frantic consumption of static and motion. 

I would be found high up, pinned to the crest of a parabolic basalt ridge where the canyon opens wide—perched at the precise altitude where the baking thermal drafts from the floor shear against the silent, near-vacuum chill dropping down from the silver sky. 

I am not a creature with legs or a engine that burns dust. I am a *tension-lattice*, a long, segmented spine of laminated spring-steel and copper, three hundred feet from tip to tail, anchored directly into the polished ironstone by eight heavy, diamond-tipped ballast-sleds. 

To a casual lens, I might look like a abandoned structural cable or the rib of a forgotten bridge that was never built. But I am entirely alive, if life is measured by the fierce, continuous negotiation between heat, cold, and vibration.

***

My body is built around a single, unbroken central rod of cold-rolled manganese iron, no thicker than a wrist, but held under thousands of pounds of permanent tension. Spanning outward from this rod are hundreds of thin, overlapping bi-metallic leaves—zinc on the upper surface, dark cast-iron beneath. 

I do not walk. I *creep*, but only by the clock of the sun’s passage. 

As the merciless silver noon bakes my upper plates, the zinc expands faster than the iron below it. My entire spine arches upward with a deep, groaning *ping* that echoes down the canyon wall. One of my rear ballast-sleds, released from its grip by the shift in weight, drags forward three inches through the grinding zinc silt, its carbide claws screaming against the granite. Hours later, when the long, sharp shadow of a neighboring spire sweeps over my midsection, the sudden two-hundred-degree drop in temperature snaps those same leaves flat again. The forward sled locks into a fresh fissure; the rear sled takes the tension. 

By this agonizing, thermal inch-worming, I move perhaps fifty feet in a century, traversing the high rim of the plateau in a permanent, slow-motion search for the sharpest temperature gradients.

***

I live off the delta. 

Where the blistering heat of the sunlit rock meets the freezing shade of my own underbelly, I generate a continuous, high-voltage current through thermal coupling. My internal leaf-springs twitch and hum with this trapped energy. I store it not in chemical cells—which would boil or freeze in minutes up here—but in the kinetic momentum of three heavy, dense flywheels housed in spherical, graphite-lubricated casings along my length. 

When you stand beneath me, you do not hear the clattering, pile-driver roar of the bellows-beasts in the dry troughs below. You hear a sound like a spinning coin that never falls—a high, razor-sharp, perfect whistle that rises and falls with the solar angle.

My vision is non-optical. I feel the plateau through the tension in my central rod. Because I am pinned tightly to the bedrock at eight distinct points, the seismic pulse of the entire region passes directly through my bones. 

I can feel the six-mile-distant strike of a tripod-walker’s foot as a sharp, clean *tink* in my third segment. I can feel the low, sub-audible thirty-hertz thrum of the great Resonator buried in the zinc basin miles away; it sets my bi-metallic leaves vibrating in harmonic waves, brushing against one another like a hive of metallic bees. 

And because of the strange, warped distance of this high country, I am plugged into the far horizon as if it were touching my skin. When a boiler-beast seizes ten miles to the west—its dry bearings scoring, its flywheel tearing through its cast-iron flank in a violent burst of sparks—the acoustic shockwave doesn't wait for the thin air to carry it. It travels up through the stone, climbing the basalt spire, and strikes my forward anchor-sled instantaneously. My tension rod rings like a struck tuning fork. 

In that moment, I know the exact mass, speed, and point of failure of the dying monster before its dust cloud has even formed.

***

I have no natural predators, for there is little scrap on me that a weld-wasp could use without being hurled into the abyss by the extreme spring-tension of my plates. My only danger is *clearance*. 

Up here, the dust carried by the thermal winds is fine, sharp, and laden with pulverized quartz. It gets into the sealed races of my flywheels. Over decades, it grinds away at the graphite coatings, slowly tightening the tolerances until the spin begins to wobble. 

I can feel the wear coming years before it happens. It starts as a subtle tremor in my fifth segment—a microscopic drag that turns kinetic power into wasted, local heat. If a flywheel locks, the energy stored within it will snap its housing, shearing my central rod and slinging my three hundred feet of coiled spring-steel off the ridge, a thrashing, violent whip of metal that will tear itself to pieces as it tumbles into the canyon.

So I wait for the mica-gliders.

When they drop out of the upper sky to scavenge, their long, razor-edged wing-tips sometimes brush the high rims. They are light, silent, and covered in thin, discarded graphite scales that shed from their steering-joints. As they pass in the thermals, inches above my spine, the static field generated by my flywheels reaches up, pulling the loose, slippery dust from their wings down into my dry, hungry joints. 

It is a silent, unprompted trade: I bleed off their excess static charge, stabilizing their flight in the violent drafts, and they bleed their dry, worn lubricant into my screaming bearings.

***

I am a creature of the high margins. I have no eyes to see the silver furnace of the sky, no mouth to grind the zinc dust, no legs to join the roaring, crowded mounds in the low troughs. 

I am simply an instrument strung across the rock—a long, hot, vibrating string pinned between the burning earth and the frozen sky, measuring the wear of the world one millimeter of thermal expansion at a time, waiting for the day my clearances fall to zero and I finally snap.
