I am not at the center of the atrium floor, nor am I sitting in the sunlit upper terraces. I am wedged into the vertical seam where the west wall folds upward without turning a corner—a place where my weight does not press down against a surface, but rests laterally against the scorched clay, held in equilibrium by the static pull of the tiles.

I am a Silt-Sifter, but I am not the sleeper in the niche. I am the one who woke when the sleeper’s breath dropped by half a degree of heat.

My body is a long, narrow frame of boiled horn and sun-dried hide, stretched over a cage of light, dry ribs. There is no fluid in me. When I bend my knee, there is no slick glide of cartilage—only the dry, precise pivot of an oiled ash-peg turning within a socket of carved marrow-bone. My chest does not expand to draw air into wet lungs; it contracts like a leather bellows to draw the golden spore-flour through the slits in my neck, filtering the fine vegetable powder into a dry hopper behind my sternum where it turns, by friction and heat, into the static force that moves my limbs.

My eyes are two polished cabochons of dark amber set flush into the horn plate of my face. I do not see colors or distance as a wet eye does. I see gradients of heat and structural tension. I see where a tile has expanded a fraction of a millimeter too far under the steady amber light, pinching its neighbor until the seam groans with dry stress.

My left hand ends in a broad, spatulate thumb, worn completely flat, its surface polished to the sheen of a river-stone by a thousand years of pressing silt. My right index finger is tipped with a blade of dark flint, bound to the bone with dried tendon-cord.

Here is what I am doing:

I am repairing the sixth hex on the third terrace. 

To reach it, I do not step upward. I simply turn my shoulders relative to the shadows. My foot, tipped with a hard, horn claw, slots into a gouge left by a flint chisel six centuries ago, and suddenly the vertical wall is my floor. The amber light does not move, but my shadow swings sideways, casting a sharp, paper-thin blade of dark ink across the mica-reeds below.

From the hollow chamber inside my ribs, I take a pinch of the pale, pulverized bone-dust. It is warm—warmer than the air, holding the fever-like heat of the deep tiles. I drop it into the razor-thin gap between two terracotta hexes where the old silt has shaken free under the constant, microscopic clicking of the copper mites.

I place my thumb against the dust. 

I press.

There is a distinct, dry *snick*—the sound of a dry latch dropping into a hardwood groove. The bone-silt compacts under my weight, the microscopic grains locking together without moisture, driven into a solid, scab-like seam by thumb-pressure alone. The sensation travels up the bone of my arm not as pain or feeling, but as a mechanical alignment: *the tile is set. The geometry holds.*

As I work, a straw-jointed bug climbs over the knuckle of my foot. It is searching for spore-dust. It moves with a rhythmic, wooden ticking, its six hollow legs scratching against my tanned skin. I do not shake it off. Its copper bristles brush against my flank, and for three seconds, a tiny circuit closes between us. I feel the dull, thermal hum of its internal bellows, its simple, dry requirement for heat; it receives from me a subtle discharge of static that brightens its copper shell to a faint, dull rose. Then it ticks away, stepping onto the mica-reeds, which rustle beneath it like a hand shuffling a deck of stiff cards.

My mind does not drift. Without blood to carry memories, I do not think of what was here before the tiles were fired, or what lies beyond the six dark walls. My consciousness is a series of mechanical states:

*Seam open.*
*Silt positioned.*
*Pressure applied.*
*Seam closed.*

Yet, because the geometry of this place is folded like a paper fan, my amber eyes capture the recursion.

As I lean over the sixth hex, I look down into the shallow, polished surface of my own left eye reflected in the glaze of the clay. Deep inside that amber sphere, scaled down until its details are fine as spider-silk, is the sunken atrium. I can see the copper mites crawling on the terraces. I can see the sleeper in the niche. And I can see a tiny, switch-thin figure clinging to the west wall, its spatulate thumb pressing a speck of bone-dust into a microscopic seam.

I feel no wonder at this. Wonder requires a surge of the heart, a sudden dampening of the skin, a gasp of moisture-laden air. I have only the steady, toaster-warm radiation of the floor beneath my claws and the certainty of the alignment.

I lift my flint-tipped finger. I score a light, ruled line across a fresh slab of clay resting in the shade of my hip, preparing the next tile before the static in my joints begins to fade.

Above me—or behind me, where the geometry turns back upon itself—the vast, hill-sized shadow of the upper thumb shifts by a fraction of a degree. A single grain of yellow flour falls from the sky, landing silently on the back of my horn head. 

I do not look up. I place my thumb against the next seam, and press.
