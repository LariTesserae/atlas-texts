I am wedged into the corner where the third-floor joist of a glassless basalt tenement meets the flange of a sixty-inch intake line. 

I do not sit; I do not swim. I am jammed. My lower body—fifteen feet of scarred, alabaster-grey muscle, heavy with the weight of wet iron-oxide stains—is threaded like a thick cable through the hollow arch of an ancient stairwell. It anchors me. If I were to let go, the current sweeping down the avenue outside would shear me from the stone like a limpet under a sledgehammer and hurl me into the teeth of the lower flume-wheels.

My jaw rests directly against the floorboard—a slab of dark, water-carved granite coated in a slick, dark enamel of deposited silica. 

Through the bone of my lower mandible, the city speaks. 

It is not a sound I hear with ears; I have none. It is a dense, physical pressure that shakes the marrow of my skull, a thousand distinct iron voices singing the singular song of the Great Machine. Right now, three inches behind my left temple, the stone is humming at a sharp, biting frequency—a high-pitched *scree* that tells me the main bearing of the Western Vertical Axle is running dry again. It has been running dry for forty years. The friction is heating the brine around its housing, expanding the bronze sleeve. I can taste the metallic bitter-sour tang of it bleeding into the warm, Ninety-Degree water that washes over my face plates.

I am a Tender. I have no name, for there is no one here to speak it. 

Where my eyes should be, the bone of my crown has grown outward into a broad, saucer-like plate, smooth as a river stone and pitted with hundreds of minute, jelly-filled canals. Through these, I "see." 

I do not see the dark, or the swirling fog of iron silt, or the sickly amber of the drowning sodium lanterns down in the square. I see the world as a shifting map of electrical tension and acoustic mass. I see the brilliant, blinding violet flare of a short-circuiting conduit three streets over—a sharp, stinging sensation along the left side of my chest. I see the dense, heavy shadow of a pipe-tick crawling along the intake line above me, its magnetite-heavy shell casting a dull, cold acoustic void against the roaring background of the flume.

I have been in this room for seven decades.

Before me sits a grease-cup. It is cast from dark, sea-pitted bronze, the size of a cauldron, bolted into the side of the great iron sleeve that passes through the tenement’s floor. Inside it is a lump of black, mineral tallow—hard as oak, preserved by the brine. 

My work is very slow. 

Two of my tendrils—dense as vulcanized rubber, thick as a man’s thigh, tipped with smooth, spatulate pads of calcified enamel—are wrapped around the T-shaped iron key rising from the top of the cup. I do not pull with sudden force. Sudden force in this depth is a foolish thing; it snaps bone and breaks iron. 

Instead, I apply a continuous, unrelenting mass. I lean my entire upper torso into the turn, waiting for the water-shock of the check-valves to help me. 

*THUD.* 

A mile away, in the dark ceiling of the vault, a cathedral-sized steel plate slams shut against thousands of atmospheres of pressure. The hydro-acoustic punch travels through the water, through the basalt, through my teeth. The building shudders. The water around me jumps, compressing my lungs. 

In that fraction of a second, when the shockwave relaxes the tension in the iron floor, I exert my weight. 

*Click.* 

The bronze key moves a fraction of a degree. A thread’s width of black lard is forced down through the internal nipple, into the screaming housing below. 

Through my jawbone, I feel the high-pitched *scree* in the stone drop half an octave. It softens into a greasy, heavy thrum. The bearing accepts the fat. It will spin for another six months before it demands another fraction of a turn.

I relax my tendrils, letting them drift in the warm, turbulent slipstream that fills the room. The brine here is thick, mineral-heavy, and warm—like blood that has lost its iron to the stone. It feeds me. My hide does not bleed; it absorbs the dissolved sulfates and microscopic bacterial mats that churn through the airless parlors. 

A shadow passes over my facial plate. 

It is a pipe-tick, nine feet long, its segmented magnetite hull clattering against the stone window frame with a sound like iron pots dropping on cobbles. It does not care that I am here. It moves with awful, jointed mechanical certainty, its spade-head scraping the thick, white porcelain crust off the bronze grease-pipe, harvesting the iron-oxidizing sludge that grows in the warmth of the friction. 

I let it work. Later, when the key is turned as far as it will go, I will reach out with my third tendril, wedge a flat stone beneath its tail-segments, and pry its tail-pincer from the flange. I will crack its pale, lard-dense interior against the stairwell and eat the cold, sulfurous marrow inside its joints. But not yet. It is cleaning the pipe I must work on next. We have an understanding written in iron and hunger.

From far across the submerged mile—far past the flooded avenues, past the churning water-wheels, past the lightless tenements—a pulse comes through the water.

It is not a sound. It is a physical thump against my ribs. 

*Thump-thump. Pause. Thump.*

Another Tender. Sitting in the drowned cellar of the Northern Pump-House, miles away across the geode. 

The pulse carries a message through the dense brine: *The lower intake grate at the Sump is choked with river-gravel. The turbine is starving.*

I do not answer immediately. I wait for the next check-valve shockwave to hit my spine, settling deeper into my stone corner. Then, contracting my heavy thoracic cavity, I blast a short, violent jet of compressed brine out through my siphon, slamming it against the hollow basalt wall behind me.

*CRACK.*

My response ripples out through the flooded city: *I am on the grease-key. The key is turning. Let the Sump choke until the bearing cools.*

Silence returns—or rather, the deafening, eternal roar returns to fill the space where the conversation was. The universe resumes its true shape: the scream of water, the groan of turning shafts, the smell of warm grease and sulfur, and the cold, unyielding hug of six miles of granite above our heads.

I turn back to the bronze T-key. I wrap my cable-tendrils around the handles once more. I press my jaw to the porcelain-coated floorboards. I wait for the next thud of the great valve. 

The machine is old. The iron is pitting. The stars above—whatever they were, if they ever were—are dead or forgotten. But here, in the hot, dark salt, the axle is still turning. And as long as the axle turns, the basalt will not collapse. 

I brace my hide against the stone, close the non-existent eyes in my forehead, and wait to give the iron its fat.
