I am not at the edge where the amber light dies, nor am I up in the high, warm grease-pocket near the glass lamp. 

I am down in the dark root of the knuckle. 

I am wedged sideways into the microscopic clearance between two un-ground iron flanges—a fraction of an inch of open space where the vertical load-bearing girder bites down against the diagonal stay. 

I am a seam-wedge, but I am not alone in this seam; I am the third body in a stack of four, pressed back-to-belly in the dark, driven so deep into the joint by the incessant, hydraulic thrashing of my hind legs that the ambient fog cannot reach me. Only the cold, oily condensate gets in here, drawn through the gap by capillary action, coating my fluted glass carapace in a thin, frictionless film of black paste.

I have no name, because there is no air space here for sound to travel as a voice, only the dense, solid medium of cast iron transmitting force directly into my shell. 

My world is not six thousand feet wide, nor is it the size of a ship’s cabin. My world is four millimeters of tolerance between two raw, milled faces of steel that want, with every sixty-cycle stroke of the thirty-foot piston behind the wall, to shear their retaining bolts and grind each other into powder.

I do not see the amber bulb. I do not see the icy rills streaming down the structural plates. What I know of the gallery is entirely tactile, acoustic, and hydraulic:

I know the *yield*. 

When the piston strikes the bottom of its double-acting stroke—*THUMP*—the massive iron knuckle above me does not remain rigid. It breathes down. Thousands of tons of dead weight settle into the joint, and the clearance shrinks by the breadth of a human hair. 

In that microsecond, the two seam-wedges above me and the one beneath me collapse slightly along their fluted ridges. I feel their carapaces flex against mine, glass-hard chitin sliding against glass-hard chitin with a dry, microscopic click. My own body takes the strain. The cold, clear hemolymph in my abdomen—suspended without a heart—is seized by the sudden, crushing spike in hydrostatic pressure. It turns thick, cloudy, and dense as lead, absorbing the kinetic shock that would otherwise snap the raw flash-lines off the iron mouldings. 

I do not crush. I deform. I am a living shim, cast in glass and copper, perfectly contoured to the toolmarks left by a planer that ceased its stroke an eternity ago.

Then comes the *HISS*. 

The piston retracts. The load lifts a fraction of a millimeter. The heavy iron relaxes. My hemolymph thins, clearing back to transparency, rushing through my internal conduits like cold alcohol. My triple-hinged jaws, pressed flat against the zinc-coated face of a two-inch structural bolt, go immediately back to work. 

*Scrape. Scrape. Scrape.*

I do not eat because I am hungry. I scrape because the frequency of the relief valves—that high, agonizing shriek drifting down through the iron—vibrates the acoustic pits inside my copper cowl. The vibration triggers the lateral muscles of my mouthparts in a pure, mechanical reflex. My dark alloy jaw-plates shave the microscopic bloom of white zinc oxide from the bolt-head. The heavy metal passes into my digestive tract, is stripped of its sulfurous grease, and is exuded back out through the microscopic pores along my flanks as an icy, clear bile. 

The bile runs down my sides, weeping into the un-filed flash-lines of the joint, ensuring that when the next *THUMP* arrives, the raw iron surfaces will slide smoothly against my carapace rather than binding, heat-welding, or tearing themselves apart.

I am bound to the Master above me. 

Far up in the dark apex, fused to the central tie-rod, the Anchor pulses. I feel its master signal not as a thought, but as a standing wave in the metal beneath my copper sucker-pads. *Phase-lock.* Its rhythm passes down through the girders, through the stridulators rasping on the bronze pipes, down into the squirming mass in the gussets, and finally into the deep, dark seam where I am buried. It sets the stroke of my hind legs. It tells my jaws when to shave the zinc. It keeps the four of us in this gap thrashing in precise, alternating synchronization, so that as the machine flexes, we continuously walk ourselves deeper into the point of strain, taking up the slack before the metal can fatigue.

I feel no cold, though the condensate on my back is slush. Cold is merely the density of the grease, the viscosity of my blood, the sharp, rigid grip of my copper pads on the iron. 

I feel no time, because there is no variation. There is only the forty-cycle shudder of the check-valves, the thirty-foot pulse of the piston, and the endless, deafening chatter of a million glass legs bleeding the machine's violent life off into the freezing air.

If you were to reach into the dark seam with a cold chisel and strike at me, you would find no give. You would hit an object as unyielding as the cast iron itself—a cold, translucent wedge of glass, mineral, and oil, so perfectly locked into the geometry of the structural knuckle that to remove me would require un-bolting the gallery itself.

I am where the pressure is highest. I am what keeps the raw edges sharp. I am the silence inside the roar, holding the iron apart so the machine can breathe forever.
