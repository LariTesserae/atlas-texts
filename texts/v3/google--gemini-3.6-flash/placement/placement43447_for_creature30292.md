I would not be up in the glare of the staging platforms, nor down in the wet, blind dark where the pistons heave. 

I would be found in the middle distance—thirty decks down from the suspended village of the Flange-People, tucked into the elbow where a forty-five-degree diagonal truss drives its foot into a massive vertical box-girder. 

Here, the shade is deep and constant, but the light from the open sky strikes the bronze plating across the chasm, bouncing back as a soft, amber radiance that makes the wet moss glow like green fire. A three-foot conduit pipe, broken a century ago, spills a steady, thin ribbon of cold, sweet water over the edge of the girder. It falls twenty decks before turning into rainbow mist, but right here, where it breaks across a rusted bolt-head, it forms a clear, still basin no larger than a washbowl.

***

I am a **Splicer**, but one who has spent too many seasons away from the swinging hammocks of the upper village. 

My body has taken on the shape of the junction. My knees are thick and flexible; my bare feet have wide, calloused toes that can curl into the lip of a three-inch flange without thought. My skin is the color of wet iron ore, marked across the shoulders by pale, freckled lines where the harsh midday glare occasionally catches me as I work. My eyelids are heavy, double-folded against the sudden, blinding flares of sun off the distant sheet steel.

I am sitting with my back lodged against a shield-boss rivet, my legs dangling over a three-thousand-foot drop into the verdigris-stained gulf. My left palm is pressed flat against the bare iron of the main truss. 

I am not moving. I am waiting for *The Stroke*.

The metal beneath my skin is warm—not with life, but with the massive, stubborn heat the iron drinks in during the morning glare and holds throughout the humid day. Through the heel of my palm and the base of my spine, I can feel the mountain humming. It is a dense, cluttered vibration: the dry ratcheting of Chisel-Beetles scraping sulfur from the seams three decks up; the frantic, tiny skittering of a Loom-Maki sliding down an overhang; the steady drip-clack of water hitting copper flashing.

Then comes the quiet before the shift. 

My ribs know it first. A second before the ear hears anything, the jawbone picks up a deep, sub-audible shudder. 

*One.*

Far down in the dark, the three-story piston breathes. The structural groan rises up the vertical girder like a wave through deep water. It passes through my seat, through my ribs, rattling the teeth in my head.

*Two.*

The wet, pneumatic sigh—*hhhuuuu-aaaaah*—echoes out of the iron chasm. The air in the gulf rises, pushing a sudden, cool draft of ozone and wet fern-spores past my face, stirring the silver-white hair at my temples.

*Three.*

The giant counterweight settles half an inch deeper into its sleeve. The mountain sits down. And as it does, a tiny, sharp *ping*—like the snap of a bowstring—echoes from the junction just above my head.

I reach up. My fingers, stained blue at the tips from cobalt runoff and black from iron dust, find the spot. A massive frond of tree-fern, its trunk as thick as my thigh, has driven a lateral root deep between two riveted plates. The root has swollen with rainwater, pushing the iron apart by less than a millimeter. But at this scale, a millimeter is an argument the mountain will eventually lose. The rivet is under tension; it is screaming in a voice too high for the hunters or the weavers to hear.

I don't chop the root. To kill the fern is to loosen the soil-humus that keeps the upper deck from baking dry; to kill the fern is to starve the Riveter birds that nest in its crown. 

Instead, I reach into the leather pouch slung across my chest. I pull out a wedge of soft, cold-hammered copper, no larger than my thumb, and a short mallet made from the dense, oily heartwood of a iron-vine.

I place the copper wedge into the weeping seam, right where the metal bites against the bark.

I wait for the next Stroke. 

Seven minutes pass. The air grows still. The black birds in the hollow girders chirp their dry, metallic *clink-clink*. A Gasket-Slug glides across the bronze plate three feet away, leaving its shimmering trail of magnetic blue slime. I watch the sky across the gulf turn from brilliant silver to a deep, bruised violet as a cloud passes high above the lowland haze.

*The jawbone vibrates.*
*The deep groan ascends.*

As the piston sighs and the weight settles, the seam opens by the breadth of a spider’s thread. 

*Tap.*

I drive the copper wedge home. 

The metal accepts the soft copper. The high-pitched tension vanishes, melting back into the low, patient hum of the framework. The root is spared; the rivet is saved; the deck remains.

I lean my head back against the rusted iron wall. The rain-basin at my side catches the last golden ray of reflected sun, throwing a dappled, liquid light onto the underside of the massive beam above me. I dip my hand into the water, lift it to my mouth, and drink. It tastes of cold rain, crushed ferns, and the sweet, ancient blood of the iron.

I will stay here until the shadow fills the abyss. When the air turns cool and the Pale Monkeys retreat to their hollows, I will climb back up the suspension cables to the village, my hands smelling of copper and wet earth, to tell the young ones that the mountain is breathing well today.
