Inside my head, it is almost quiet. 

The beeswax in my ears is mixed with sheep tallow and pulverized peat, pressed in warm with a thumb until it seals against the cartilage. It cuts the world’s Great Roar down to a dark, thick pulse—the heavy, subsonic thrum of forty thousand tons of emerald water coming over the basalt lip every minute, felt in the marrow of my shins and the roots of my back teeth rather than heard.

I am wedged into the crook of a grease-blackened iron gantry, thirty feet above the lip where the Upper Flume drops off into the grey void. My knees are tucked to my chest inside oilskin breeches that have been rubbed with fish-grease until they feel like wet horn. Beneath the oilskin, three layers of unwashed wool hold my own body heat against me, stale and damp and smelling of mutton tallow and cold soot.

I am a Tender, but I am not on the islands. I am out on the Long Span.

My right hand is gloved in thick cowhide with a half-pipe of cold yellow brass riveted into the palm. The brass rests directly on the Main Haul—a three-inch cable of twisted charcoal-iron wire that spans the two-mile gulf between the North Pier and the Drowned Church. The wire is wet. It is always wet, coated in a fine, clear sheen of cold river-grease that the current splashes up from the eddy-wheels below. 

The cable doesn't just hold weight; it sings. Through the brass in my palm, through the bones of my wrist, up the radius and ulna into my shoulder-joint, the wire speaks its entire path.

I can feel the engine two miles back: *thud... clank... thud... clank.* That is Old Seven, the double-acting beam-pump sitting in its dry-cave behind the veil of the Second Shelf. Her valve-rod is sticking slightly—a quick, dry shudder at the top of her stroke, like a twig catching in a hinge. Someone needs to run a cup of lard down her guide-rail before noon, or the packing will burn out.

Below me, sixty feet down in the green dusk where the water glass-clears before it breaks, I feel a slow, heavy tug that isn't mechanical. It’s too soft, too rhythmic. A Whistle-Hound has hitched its hindquarters into the submerged cradle of Cable Four. I can feel the claw-scrape through the iron—three short, sharp strikes as it secures its tail-wrap, then the rhythmic *tock-tock-tock* of a black mussel shell being cracked against the turnbuckle. The vibration is bright and high, like a small hammer on an anvil. I let him be. The Hounds clear the kelp-tangles off the lower shackles anyway; a little shell-clatter is fair rent.

To exist here is to lose the illusion that the earth is solid. Everything gives. The stone terraces bend under the weight of the basins; the iron cables stretch three feet long in the heavy autumn rain-belts and snap back tight when the cold night-mist settles. Even my own ribs feel expanded, permanently bowed out from fifty years of drawing air that is half mist and three miles high. 

I don't look down often. There is nothing down there but the white lace where the shelf ends, and below that, the endless, churning fog where the lower gardens hang. If a man slips off the gantry, he doesn't hit the stone; the air-surge at the lip catches him first, rolls him like a cobble in a flume, and carries him out into the spray-curtain. They say you don't drown on the way down; the air is so thick with spray you just keep breathing the mist until the bottom finds you.

Instead, I look at the wire. 

In my left pouch, I have six iron splinters and a brass-headed mallet. When a strand of the Main Haul gives out—fraying under the endless sawing of the current—it twangs through the brass in my palm like a snapped lute string. I don't need eyes to find it. I slide down the wet cable on my brass harness, locate the fuzzy patch of iron hair with my bare fingertips, drive a splint into the twist, and bind it tight with grease-soaked marline before the strand can unravel toward the winch.

Sometimes, when the upper surges shift at mid-day and the basins spill sideways, the water drops three feet in a minute, exposing the ancient stone tops of the Drowned Church. I can see the marks carved into the basalt down there—huge, square-cut runes three feet deep, worn so smooth by ten million years of current that they look like soap. Nobody knows what they said. Nobody knows who cut them into the rock before the water came over the top. The Siphon-Ghouls rest their chin-barbels in those carved letters now, their long, milky bodies swaying in the eddy like strips of boiled hide.

They don't care about the iron. The Whistle-Hounds don't care about the pumps. Only we care, and even we have forgotten why the boilers were lit in the first place. Was there a city down in the fog? Was there dry land once at the bottom of the stairs? It doesn't matter. If the fires go out, the submerged winches freeze; if the winches freeze, the silt fills the channels; if the channels choke, the water rises six inches and sweeps the dry-caves clean of wool, larch-bunks, and men.

So we keep the wire tight.

The wire shudders again. A long, smooth draw—the heavy dredging bucket coming up from the Fifty-Foot Terrace, laden with gray river-gravel and ancient, smooth-rolled iron bolts. 

I lean my forehead against the cold, vibrating gantry-iron. The metal is cold enough to ache against my skin, but the rhythm is steady. *Thud-clank. Thud-clank.* 

I tap my brass glove twice against the cable—*drop-hold, drop-hold*—a signal to the boy sitting on the pier-head two thousand yards away through the grey drizzle. 

*All clear on the Upper Flume. Keep the coal coming.* 

Then I pull my hood down low over my tallow-caked ears, wrap my legs around the wet girder, and listen to the world run through my hands.
