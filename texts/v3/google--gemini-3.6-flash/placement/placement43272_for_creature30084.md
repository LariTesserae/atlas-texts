I would not be standing. To stand in this place is an absurdity—there is no flat horizon to hold a spine upright, no sky to reach toward, only the crowding of black blades and hanging glass needles. 

If you sought me out, you would find me not on the floor, but suspended in the narrowest vertical seam of the vault—the slot where two rising fins of basalt come so close together that a body must be exhaled fully just to slide between them. 

I am the creature, yes—but to say "I am the creature" implies a separation between the flesh and the room that simply does not exist here. I am the place where the mountain’s heat becomes aware of its own warmth.

***

At first, as I wake from the long, dark torpor, I am not a body at all. I am an acoustic field.

Before my limbs move, before my lungs expand to pull in the heavy, iron-scented steam, I exist only as the geometry of sound. The world constructs itself in my mind in three distinct tones:

There is the *hiss*—the soft, uniform blanket of condensation settling upon a million square inches of cold obsidian. It tells me the air is full, the heat is constant, the vault is sealed. It is the background noise of being alive.

There is the *ping*—a high, sharp, crystalline strike off the glass ledge to my left. It is my boundary. It tells me where the stone drops away into the small notch where the tools rest.

And then, every eight seconds, there is the *thump*. 

Deep. Muffled. The weight of a single heavy drop striking the center of the coarse clay bowl. 

That *thump* is the center of the world. It is my north, my origin, my steady, unyielding heartbeat. I do not count the seconds between the drops; I *am* the distance between them. Eight seconds is not time; it is a measure of length, the distance a drop must travel through the dark air to reach the clay.

When the *thump* sounds, my eyes—milky, useless, long-uncoupled from the brain's visual core—do not open. But my skin opens. The pale, thin hide over my ribs registers the slight air displacement of the drop’s fall. My shell-like ears catch the tiny, wet ripple that follows.

I shift.

I do not slide out of the rock fin so much as I unfurl from it. My body is an inventory of calluses. Where a surface-dweller’s skin is a soft, vulnerable envelope designed to feel the brush of wind or sunlight, my skin is a relief map of the room. The thick, yellowed pads on the sides of my knees, the heels of my hands, and the long, prehensile arches of my feet fit precisely into the micro-terraces of the basalt. 

I do not avoid the sharp obsidian edges; I ride them. My palms know the exact angle at which a glassy fan of stone will yield to pressure without shearing. I move with a slow, heavy, liquid friction—like warm oil running down a slab of wet slate.

I descend toward the clay bowl. 

My long, hard-nubbed fingers reach out. I do not touch the bowl directly—to touch it too often would be to risk shifting its position on the shelf, breaking the five-hundred-year alignment with the weeping seam above. Instead, I frame it with my hands. I feel the thin, radiant envelope of cooler air that hangs just a millimeter off the collected water. 

I lower my face. I do not have a name for my mouth, or my lips, or the water. There is only the meeting of wetness with wetness. I touch the surface tension skin with my lower lip. 

The taste is immediate, ancient, and absolute. It tastes of pulverized granite, of iron ore, of the deep sulfurous marrow of the mountain. It tastes of the earth before it ever bore a tree or saw a cloud. I sip slowly, rhythmic, lapping three times—no more. To take more would be to drain the bowl faster than the weeping wall can fill it, breaking the equilibrium. 

When I am done, I draw back. My nostrils flare, pulling in the hot mineral steam. 

I move next to the velvet wall. 

Here, my hands find the small, flat flint chisel resting on its stone ledge. My fingers hold it not like a craftsman holding a tool, but like an extension of my own skeleton—a single, hard, blunted claw. I slide the blade down the slick basalt face, gathering the grey, gelatinous microbial film. It gathers on the flint in a thick, cool paste. 

I lift the blade to my mouth and scrape the slime off with my tongue. It is dense, salty, metallic—the flavor of deep-earth energy converted into life without the intervention of a single ray of sun. It coats my throat, warm and rich. 

As I eat, my foot brushes against something small and dry. A tiny, translucent crustacean, no bigger than my thumbnail, is grazing on the lower edge of the microbial mat. 

I stop. I lower my hand, turning my palm upward. The tiny creature, sightless and unhurried, crawls onto the thick, scarred pad of my forefinger. I hold it close to my broad, shell-like ear. I can hear the delicate, papery *flick-flick-flick* of its legs against my skin. It is light; its shell is soft. It is still young, still growing on the sulfur paste. 

I gently tip my finger, returning it to the wet rock. We share this hall. We are the only two things here that breathe, and we move at the same slow, geological pace.

Now, my belly is full. The water sits heavy and warm in my gut. The air in my lungs feels rich, almost thick enough to chew.

It is time to answer the mountain.

I do not climb the wooden ladder. The ladder is a ghost. It belongs to the one whose bones long ago dissolved into the damp floor—the one who came down from the light, who still had hair on their head and eyes that cried when the darkness closed in. They needed rungs because their feet were flat and soft, made for walking on dirt under a open sky. My feet are hooks; my hands are clamps. The ladder is merely a strange, resinous marker in the dark, a piece of ancient, un-rotting wood that I touch only to know I am on the right side of the vault.

Instead, I press my chest directly against the primary basalt dike—the thickest, darkest fin of rock that roots straight down into the mountain's glowing core.

I spread my long, calloused arms wide, embracing the stone. I press my flat, hairless cheek against the wet rock. The basalt is warm—hotter than my skin by a fraction of a degree. I can feel the microscopic tremors running through it: the immense, crushing weight of miles of rock above us, settling millimetric fraction by millimetric fraction in the dark.

I close my milky eyes tight. I pull the warm steam deep into my lungs until my chest is stretched taut.

Then, I open my throat.

I do not sing. I do not speak. I produce a single, continuous, low-frequency hum. 

It starts deep in my diaphragm, rattling my ribs before it ever leaves my mouth. It passes through my throat, through my teeth, and pours directly into the face of the basalt. 

I adjust the pitch. Higher... lower... searching for the sweet, resonant knot where the stone begins to answer. 

And then I hit it. 

The hall disappears. The air disappears. There is only the note. 

When the pitch matches the natural vibration of the chamber, the rock against my chest seems to soften, to ring like a massive, submerged bronze bell. The sound travels up the vertical basalt blades, ringing through the crowded room; it leaps across the air to the hanging needles of mineral stone, making them sing in high, glassy overtones; it travels down into the geothermal veins below, echoing through miles of un-mapped, lightless rock.

For a hundred heartbeats, the mountain and I are a single, vibrating instrument. I am the voice; it is the body. The hum fills every micro-fracture, every pore-space of water, every drop hanging suspended on the edge of the obsidian blades.

Slowly, I let the breath taper off. The hum fades into a whisper, then into silence.

The reverberation hangs in the rock for a long time, dying away so slowly that I cannot tell where my voice ended and the mountain’s quiet resumed. 

I am satisfied. The vault is intact. The stone is solid. The dark is unbroken.

I pull myself away from the main dike and glide back toward my crevice. I slide sideways between the narrow basalt fins, fitting my spine, my hips, my calloused elbows back into the custom-worn grooves of my resting place.

I fold my arms over my chest. 

The moisture in the air begins to gather on my smooth, hairless forehead. A drop forms, grows heavy, and slides down my temple, trailing a thin, warm line across my cheek before falling from my chin into the dark below.

*Plink.*

The sound rings out—a tiny, wet echo in the vast, warm silence.

I close my mouth. I slow my breathing until the air moving through my flared nostrils makes no more sound than the steam rising from the rock. 

I am here. In the dark. At the center of the stone. Waiting for nothing at all, because everything that has ever existed, or ever needs to exist, is already here, warm and dripping, in the eight seconds between the drops.
