First, there is only the hum. 

It is not a sound that enters through ears; it is a structural state. It lives in the thick cartilage behind my eyes, in the rubbery density of my mantle, in the calcified tips of my lower arms where they press against the chamfered lip of a sixty-foot cross-girder. Sixty hertz. A low, rhythmic tremor that tells me the heavy copper core inside Pylon 88-North is alive, pulling current down from the unseen ceiling to the primary intake banks ten miles below.

I do not know what a sun is. I do not know what dirt feels like, or the give of soft sand beneath a belly. To me, the universe is made of four things: the cold, crisp water; the stark, daylight-white beams that slice it into geometric corridors; the roaring currents that keep the body light; and the hard, unyielding alloy that must be kept clean.

I am holding myself against the underside of a major girder-joint, eighty yards above the alloy floor. 

My body is broad, dense, and pale—a marbled, rubbery gray that catches the edge of a 5500-Kelvin floodlight array. I have no need for fins; fins are for the open water, for the delicate Conduit-Darts that sweep through the void in liquid circuits. My world is the steel frame. My eight long, heavy arms do not terminate in soft, wet suction cups, but in hardened, tool-like pads. Where my skin meets the deck-plate, I can feel every micro-fracture, every loose rivet, every vibration of an approaching impeller blade.

With my upper left limb—the tip calcified into a broad, chisel-shaped scraper—I work at a stubborn patch of dead galvanic lichen that has crusted over a luminaire’s quartz lens housing. *Scrape. Scrape. Thunk.* The dead pale-green matter flakes away, caught instantly by the three-knot current rolling past the pylon, drifting off into the pale turquoise void like a handful of silver dust. 

Below me, the light flares bright and clean again. 

Down in the illuminated corridor between this pylon and the next—a ten-mile expanse of crystal water—a school of Conduit-Darts catches the restored beam. Half a million translucent bodies turn in a single, blinding millisecond, a sheet of living foil reflecting the industrial white. They don't look at me. I don't look at them. They are part of the drift, like the mineral salts and the plankton caught in the intake pull. 

*BOOM.*

Ten miles to the west, a hydraulic gate drops. 

The acoustic shockwave hits my mantle a second later—a massive, chest-shivering *CLANG* that rattles the rivets in their sockets and turns the water into a single, ringing bell. 

I do not flinch. The shockwave is a clock. It marks the shift of the western pressure chambers. In the split second while the acoustic roar echoes off the flat alloy floor, I see a long, pale shadow flash from a circular maintenance hatch three girders down. A Vault-Eel—thirty feet of blind, white muscle—strikes out into the open beam, snaps its double-jointed jaw over an unwary Rivet-Mite, and vanishes back into the hollow interior of the girder before the echo has even begun to fade. 

The light returns to stillness. The water clears. I return to the quartz lens.

I am a Strainer, but I am old. My amber eyes, broad and flat on the sides of my head, have watched three generations of Gantry Rays ride the thermal updrafts from the transformer housings below up to the hazy, high-pressure tiers where the structural girders vanish into indigo. I have learned the acoustic map of this sector so intimately that I can tell which turbine in Intake Bay Four has a pitted blade simply by the slight, irregular warble in the floor-plate vibration.

I do not cross the open water. The open water is an empty void, a place without handholds, where the currents can sweep a body into the vortex of a cold-water injection nozzle. Safety is the frame. Safety is the chamfered edge, the yellow-striped cross-beam, the thick rubber insulation of the power conduits.

And above all, safety is the light.

Further down the girder, where the floodlight beam dies out and the water settles into a deep, transparent indigo, the steel becomes dark and unknown. I do not go there. None of my kind go there. We gather at the edge of the glare, our long arms anchored to the rivet-heads, our scrapers working the seams, keeping the quartz lenses clear, keeping the intake grates free of shed skins and drifting algae fronds.

We do not know who drove the rivets the size of oak trees. We do not know what lies beyond the vast bulkheads that frame our horizon. We know only the rhythm of the engine: the pumps must draw, the lights must burn, the seams must be cleared.

A warm updraft catches my lower tentacles—a gentle, four-degree thermal plume rising from the transformer housing at the pylon's base. It smells faintly of warm insulation and ozone. I loosen my anchor-claws, shift my weight, and let the current slide me three yards down the vertical face to the next joint, my hardened pads making a soft, metallic *click-clack* against the zinc-coated steel.

I settle over a fresh casing latch, tap my claw against the alloy to test its tension, and begin to work. Around me, the Basin hums its cold, eternal, daylight song.
