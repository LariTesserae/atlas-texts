My palm is the first thing that knows where we are.

It is pressed flat against the seam where a freshly shattered slab of dark schist meets the ancient, water-smoothed river cobbles of the lower terrace wall. The fat-pad beneath my thumb is broad and calloused, thick enough that the jagged edge of the new slate doesn't cut, only bites with a clean, cold pressure. Deep inside the rock, three inches beyond the wet skin of the stone, a microscopic tremor is traveling down from the plateau rim. It arrives in my wrist as a faint, rhythmic tick: *click... click... click.*

The mountain is shedding. Up on the high rim, where the grey air meets the pale sky, another bed of slate has split along an iron-vein. It will slide before the mist turns to evening drip.

I do not look up. Looking up lets the drizzle settle directly into the wide, flat orbits of my eyes, and though the clear membrane sweeps across them smoothly to brush away the moisture, it is better to keep my chin down, tucked into the thick, greasy fold of my felted goat-wool mantle. The wool smells of peat-ash, cold animal oil, and the sharp, sour tang of the butterbur paste we rubbed into the weave three winters ago. Beneath the mantle, against my ribs, the tunic of sea-bird down is dry and warm, held close by my heavy, low-slung frame.

I am sitting on the third step of the great western sluiceway. Cold spring water, running four inches deep and clear as melt-ice, flows around my bare ankles. My toes are spread wide, gripping the slick, algal velvet of the stone channel floor. I can feel the trout darting past my calves—quick, muscular twitches in the current, seeking the dark overhang beneath the lintel of the communal milk-cellar three tiers down.

To my left, half-embedded in the wet fern-growth that choked the gap between two cellar walls, my daughter is working. She is smaller than I am, her skin still dark and smooth like rain-slicked river granite, her shoulder-folds only lightly dappled with the grey-green dust of young lichen. She has both hands buried deep in a pocket of cold, black peat-mud, packing it between two structural slabs to seal a seepage that threatens to flood a sleeping-niche below.

She doesn't speak. Air-speech is a waste of warmth, an offense to the dark-feathered nesters whose soft roosting-hollows line the wall above our heads. Instead, she leans forward until her broad, flat forehead presses against the lintel stone I am touching with my knee.

She sends her hum down through the schist.

It starts deep in her chest—a low, resonant vibration that bypasses the cold air entirely, passing from her bone through the rock and into my patella. 

*Water coming from behind the third layer,* the hum says, felt rather than heard, a thick, heavy pulse in my joint. *The wall is wet-heavy. It wants to lean.*

I shift my weight. My heavy, dense bones—thick as fence-posts, holding the mass of five decades of stacked slate—anchor me to the flagstone. I place my second palm against the face of the leaning slab. I close my eyes.

Through the fat-pads of my hands, the world reveals its true geometry. I do not see the city as buildings; I feel it as a single, continuous, stepped weight pressing down into the tectonic tear of the basin. I feel the weight of the turf roofs above us, heavy with sodden moss and the sharp, small hooves of the roof-goats cropping dwarf willow overhead. I feel the vaulting of the sluiceway, the deep cellar arches, and below those, six tiers down into the cold dark where the water pools before vanishing into the subterranean deeps, I feel the old ones.

My grandmother is in the foundation wall beneath the central well-house. Her ribs were sealed under ten tons of river cobble when I was still small enough to ride in the back of my father’s wool mantle. Her bone-density has long since matched the schist around her; the wall where she rests has not shifted a finger’s breath in forty seasons. It is a good wall. Dense. Silent.

I draw a long, slow breath through my wide, complex nasal passages. The air enters freezing and wet, smelling of crushed stem and cold mineral grit, but by the time it reaches my lungs, my inner throat has warmed it, stripped the fog away, and rendered it dry. 

I hum back through the stone, a short, dense vibration pressed from my sternum into the lintel.

*Unstack the upper three. Give the spring its gutter.*

She pulls her forehead back from the stone. Her silver irises catch the flat, shadowless grey light dropping from the sky above the canyon walls. She nods once, her lateral eyelids flicking sideways—a soft, wet blink—and reaches for her bone wedge and granite mallet.

We do not hurry. Hurry belongs to things that burn, things that flare and turn to ash. Here, where there is no fire, where the only warmth is the quiet, slow engine of our own flesh pressed together in the dark sleep-niches, everything moves at the pace of water finding a seam.

I lean back against the terrace wall. My mantle absorbs the dampness of the slate behind me, the oily wool repelling the slow, steady drip from the eaves above. A young mountain-ash, no taller than my waist but with a trunk as thick and gnarled as my wrist, has rooted in the mortarless seam right beside my ear. Its leaves are slick with condensation. A single, heavy drop collects on the tip of a serrated frond, sags under its own weight, and falls.

It lands on my shoulder, beads instantly on the hydrophobic sheen of my skin, and rolls down into the grey lichen patch at my collarbone. The tiny, pale micro-flora drinks it up, turning a shade darker, a vibrant, cold green.

Above us, high on the slate rim, the tectonic tick grows louder. *Click. Click. Slide.*

A handful of gravel scatters down the cliff face half a mile away, clattering softly like dry teeth as it hits the upper terraces. A flock of dark sea-birds erupts from a wall-hollow with a hushed *thrum-thrum-thrum* of wet feathers, circling once in the grey mist before settling onto the peat roof of the weavers' tier.

My daughter wedges her bone tool into the seam. The slate gives a sharp, clean *clack*, splitting precisely along its natural grain.

I reach out my spade-nailed hand to catch the slab as it breaks free. It is heavy, cold, and dripping with ancient, underground water. It feels completely right. I take its weight into my arms, hold it against my chest, and together, we begin to build the channel.
