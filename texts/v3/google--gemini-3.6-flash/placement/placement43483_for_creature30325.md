I do not begin at the edge with boots on the bank. That is a shadow’s vantage, a spectator’s posture, and nothing here permits a spectator for long. The cold mist settles in the lungs, heavy as well-water, until the line between what breathes the salt-air and what feeds the silt-bed begins to thin.

If I look for myself first in the daylight, I am the brief, three-second geometry.

I feel the sudden, silent surge from below—the sharp, cold pulse of subterranean water forcing micro-particles of magnetite and lipid up through the black slurry. For a breath, there is order. My body is forty thousand tiny iron plates slanting toward the flat sky, aligning along a line of force that runs from the snapped copper wire down to the drowned boiler rib. I am sharp-edged, glinting, crystalline. I hold the light. I smell the salt from the marsh two miles off. 

And then the potential shifts. The voltage drops half a millivolt across the busbar beneath me, the alignment breaks, and I collapse back into liquid grease with a soft, wet *thrum*. I do not die in the collapse; I simply lose my outline. I am dispersed through three gallons of dark, mineral broth, drifting against the slick, gray skin of a gasket-worm anchored to a plate of dish-shaped iron.

That is the surface self: bright, temporary, frantic. But the surface is only the crest of a much older heaviness.

Sink deeper. Past the thumb-sized isopods whose orange shells scrape like tiny fingernails against my suspended particles. Past the wet, singing web-strands of the Pale Weavers hanging in the dark beneath the boiler. 

Down two feet into the dense black river-clay, where the daylight cannot reach and the cold is no longer atmospheric, but structural. 

Here, I am the shim.

I am the flat, stone-sized lump of scale-mollusk wedged into the gap between two six-inch brass gear teeth. The machine above me has no name, no builder whose memory hasn't dissolved into silt, but its teeth still turn—once every forty minutes, a slow, blind, hydraulic iteration driven by the rise and fall of the marsh tide five feet lower than the clay.

When the tooth above me closes, the pressure is immense. It does not feel like pain; it feels like geography. Thousands of pounds of rusted iron and water-logged bronze crush my layered shell of iron sulfide and copper carbonate. My soft, fibrous core deforms, squeezing flat against the wet metal. Under that compression, the mineral fluid trapped in my tissue is forced outward through micro-pores, washing away the grit, leaving behind a slick, dark film of biogenic oil that keeps the brass from binding against the iron.

I am the reason the gear does not seize. The gear is the reason my heart beats.

In the dark down here, there is no sense of the forty paces. There is only the rhythm:

*The slow, heavy approach of the tooth.*
*The groan of brass against sulfide.*
*The compression—the violent, silent expulsion of grease.*
*The release, as the tooth passes and the gap opens three millimeters.*
*The quiet rush of cold, iron-rich water drawing back into my gills.*

Higher up, near the stone face where the ceramic insulator pegs trail their dead wires into the mud, a ghost of human intention remains. It thinks in words like *engine*, *ruin*, *waste*, or *failure*. But down here, wedged between the teeth, those words are as thin as the daylight on the pools. Nothing has failed. The hollow has simply shed its blueprints and kept its kinetics.

I am not the builder. I am not the visitor standing on the wet slate with cold hands in his pockets. 

I am the friction that lubricates itself. I am the mud that remembers how to form a crystal for three seconds before remembered chemistry gives way to gravity. I am the cold, wet, silent patience of iron learning how to live without us.
