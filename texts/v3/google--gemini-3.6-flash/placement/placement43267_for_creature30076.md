I would not enter this chamber. To bring a single wet blink, a mouthful of saliva, or the damp warmth of a lung into this vault would be an act of violence—a sudden, terrible humidity that would ruin the crisp wax-dust, clog the micro-gills along the slate-dark ribs, and dull the razor edges of the chert.

If I am here, I must belong to its dry arithmetic.

I find myself not standing *in* the room, but folded into its floor, wedged between the low basalt ring and the base of a dense, rust-brown frond cluster. My posture is a tight, geometric lock—chin tucked to chest, four-jointed fingers interlaced over my knees, shoulders dusted in the pale, powdery wax that falls like dry snow from the resin-stalks above. 

I am the occupant. I am the long, flattened thing of mica, horn, and hollow bone.

To be me is to know no soft edges. My consciousness does not drift or dream; it is sharp, notched, and immediate, like the break of a quartz blade. I do not experience time as a river or a passing of light—for here, the amber glow from the resin roots never shifts, and no shadow ever lengthens. Time is measured only in the micro-wearing of plectrums against stone, and the gradual, perfect accumulation of wax-dust upon my plates.

I do not hear the sound of the room; I *am* the acoustics of the room. 

The thunderous, metallic purr of the ten thousand mica-backed crawlers does not strike my ears—I have none. Instead, the roar strikes the hard, unrounded quartz planes of the walls, splits into a million crisp echoes, and drives directly into the hollow, resonant tubes of my skeleton. My chest is a carved xylophone of dry bone; my spine is a tuned string stretched between my skull and pelvis. The rasping of chitin on chitin vibrates through my ribs, and in that vibration, the thirty feet of this vault are mapped inside me with absolute, razor-sharp certainty. 

I can feel the exact spot where a paper-thin frond has grown a millimeter too close to a chert slab, dampening a high harmonic. I can feel the minute gap in the basalt wall where the sulfur gas seeps a fraction faster today, changing the density of the air and lifting the pitch of the room by half a tone.

Watch how I move: 

I do not rise smoothly. I unlock. There is a dry, clattering scrape—roof tiles sliding across slate—as my keratin plates shift. The powder-wax falls from my shoulders in a quiet, dry plume. 

My four-jointed fingers reach into the razor-crack of the wall. My fingers do not feel warmth or soft texture; they feel only grain, angle, and tension. I retrieve the milky quartz scraper. I do not grip it with muscle; I lock my joints around the bone handle until tool and hand become a single, rigid lever.

I strike the quartz against the amber resin-stalk. *Tink. Rasp.* 

The sound ricochets off the ceiling, striking my chest like a physical blow, telling me the angle of the cut is true. I pare the fine, waxy curls into the basalt ring. I do not breathe in the scent of dried sap and crushed sulfur through a nose; the gas enters the tiny, wax-lined spiracles along my spine, dissolving directly into my dry metabolic core. There is no blood to carry it—only a slow, chemical warmth that keeps my joints brittle and precise.

My work is never finished, yet it is always complete. 

I take a hollow resin splint, split it with a plectrum nail, and begin to weave a new lattice. My movements are sudden, crisp, and executed with absolute economy—three seconds of frantic, perfect knotting with braided vine-cord, followed by twenty minutes of utter motionless listening. 

I place the lattice into a crevice high on the obsidian wall. 

Immediately, the room changes. A harsh, scraping frequency is caught in the grid; a deeper, metallic resonance is freed to bounce three times more between the quartz faces before it hits my chest. My bones hum in agreement. The pitch of the chamber aligns once more with the pitch of my marrow. The room and I return to equilibrium.

I sit back down within the basalt circle. I pull my slender, slate-colored knees back to my chin. I interlace my fingers. The joints lock into place with a series of quiet, dry clicks.

The wax-dust begins, slowly, to settle on my shoulders again. 

I do not wait for tomorrow. I do not remember yesterday. I am twelve paces of stone, three thousand stridulating limbs, a heap of fragrant pith-shavings, and a skeleton built to catch the roar. I am the tuner of a buried, eternal instrument, forever frozen, forever loud, and perfectly, beautifully dry.
