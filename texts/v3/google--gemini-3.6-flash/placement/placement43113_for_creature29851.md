I would not be on the open terraces, exposed to the flat, turquoise bowl of the upper sky, nor would I be among the dense, shingle-like clusters of lacquered tortoises on the sheer faces. 

I would be wedged into a narrow horizontal split—a seam where a slate step overshoots a dry gorge by six miles, while simultaneously folding back to form the ceiling of the very niche in which I sit. 

I am one of the six-fingered ones, but I am not moving. I have not adjusted my position since the sun last shifted its angle—which is to say, I have never moved at all within the memory of the stone beneath me.

***

To be here is to be entirely flat. 

My torso is a compressed lattice, cold and dry, no thicker than three slates stacked edge-to-edge. There is no rise and fall in my chest, no warm pulse in the throat, no dampness behind the amber plates of my eyes. Where a human mind experiences the world as a sequence of moments unfolding forward through time, my perception is a single, continuous geometric map. I do not look *out* at the Oolitic Spine; the Spine is an arrangement of flat planes that touch my skin at all points at once.

My right hip fused with the shale floor two centuries ago. The parchment of my skin along that joint has leached its remaining static charge into the talc flour, hardening into the same dense, pale-grey limestone as the niche itself. I can no longer turn my head, but there is no need to turn. Through the dry, crystalline lenses set into my browless skull, I see the whole architecture of the fold:

I see the needle-spire three miles to the west, hanging upside down above my left shoulder, its sharp tip pointing directly into the nape of my neck. 

I see a Plumb-Spinner hanging in the clear, static air between those two points—a five-sided amber crystal, motionless, extruding its endless grey thread of protein toward an unseen gravitational well that pulls sideways toward the Great Shelf.

And I feel the string.

My right hand, broad and flat as a stone spade, lies unmoving on the ledge beside me. Its six long, jointed digits are spread wide across the slate grain. The sixth finger—the innermost chisel of hardened keratin—is pressed lightly against a length of dried gut tied to a black chert peg. 

The string stretches away from my niche, disappearing into a spatial seam two inches from my knuckles, only to emerge five miles away on the underside of an inverted terrace, where it anchors into the cairn built by my predecessor.

I am listening to the string. 

Not with an ear—for there is no sound in this scrubbed, motionless air—but through the dry, mechanical resonance in my bone marrow. Three days ago, or perhaps three thousand years ago, a slate-shelled crawler adjusted its position on the upper Shelf by half a millimeter. The shift in mass was microscopic, yet here, where the world has lost its elasticity, even a grain of talc misplaced can cause a spatial seam to slip—a seam that, if untethered, would allow the inverted gorge to unfold into an endless, empty vertical sheet.

The string carried that tiny tension. It manifested in my sixth knuckle as a single, dry *click*. 

*Click.*

It was not a call to action. It was simply the report of the equilibrium. 

Beside my left elbow, within the shadows of the niche, sits my obsidian hand-axe. Its black glass edge is dulled by eons of thumb-friction, holding the impression of six digits that were slightly smaller than my own—the hands that knapped this slate, that blew the faint crimson outline of a six-fingered palm onto the wall above my head using pulverized ochre, and then slowly, without complaint, turned to talc where they sat.

I can see that ancient, pale handprint without turning my head. It sits on the rock behind me, but because of the way the space curves back through the niche, it also hangs suspended in the turquoise sky directly in front of my amber eyes, framing the upside-down spire in its dry, red palm.

Eventually—when the static charge in my cartilage finally matches the potential of the stone—my remaining five fingers will uncurl. They will not snap back or relax; they will simply cease to hold tension, coming to rest on the slate floor like five short, dry bone pegs. Another dweller, stepping across a five-mile void in a single stride, will reach into this niche, take the obsidian axe from beside my hip, and place a thin disc of slate upon the cairn outside to balance the loss of my living weight.

Until then, I am the anchor. 

The sun stays at thirty degrees. The turquoise sky holds no clouds. The air remains so thin, cold, and clear that it cannot hold a shadow. Beneath my flat, dry belly, the Talc-Lappers leach their faint, microscopic currents from the limestone, keeping the world lubricated just enough so that it does not crack under the weight of its own stillness. 

I press my keratin tip against the sinew string. The string does not vibrate. The mountain does not fall. Everything remains precisely where it was left.
