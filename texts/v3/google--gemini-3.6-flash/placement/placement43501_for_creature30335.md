At first, there is only the wetness against skin that does not mind it, and the sound that is too big to be heard as sound—a deep, trembling hum that enters through the heels and settles in the ribs before it ever reaches the ears.

I am not out in the blue. The blue is vast and dry and terrifying; it belongs to things with no weight. 

I am behind the glass.

I live in the warm throat of the stone, in the vertical chimney behind the curtain of sheet-water. Here, where the mountain's geothermal breath leaks out through fine cracks in the granite, the air is three degrees warmer than the gale outside, smelling of old iron, swallow-down, and the heavy, sweet grease of rendering tallow.

My skin is dark and thick, like river slate that has been soaked in oil. Every morning—if *morning* is the word for that sudden, cold sharpening of the grey light through the waterfall—I rub my chest, my shoulders, and the backs of my thighs with the paste of swallow-fat and crushed red lichen. It keeps the perpetual spray from softening my flesh into something that rots. The air here wants to drink you, or drown you, or turn you into wet moss; the oil keeps me contained, a dry seed inside a slick pod.

My feet are wide. When I press my soles into the shallow toe-notches carved behind the sheet-fall, my toes spread and flex like fingers, finding the microscopic roughness in the granite. I do not slip. Slipping is a thought that belongs to the people who built the world three miles below—the ones who need flat ground to remember who they are. Here, balance is not something you keep; it is something you breathe.

Watch me step through the curtain.

It is only two inches of water, but it moves so fast it feels like a slap of flexible glass. Then—*crack*—the full noise of the shelf strikes. 

The wind is an engine. It hits the prow of the horn and splits into two screaming knives, tearing upward from the valley, carrying the water back up into the sky. I stand on the six-inch mattress of emerald and liver-colored moss, feeling it yield beneath my weight like a wet animal. The spray flies upward past my face, casting tiny, shivering double-rainbows against the cliff above my head. The air tastes of quartz and fern, so thin it feels cold behind my teeth, but my chest is wide—a broad, barrel-built cage that knows how to wring the life out of thin air.

I do not speak with consonants. Consonants belong to quiet rooms. If I open my mouth and say a word, the gale catches it, shreds it, and throws it three miles down into the blue before it reaches the ears of the woman sitting five feet away at the lip.

Instead, I shape my lips and whistle.

*Whee-t. Tsi-loo.*

A high, thin warble, notched right into the key of the air-flutes behind us. The mountain is singing its long, hollow D-minor chord, the wind blowing across the vertical gashes in the back wall. My whistle rides *inside* the mountain's note, a small, bright thread running through a heavy rope. 

She doesn't turn her head, but her hand moves on the granite. She has found a strand of the white, crusted fiber tied to the petrified pine stake. She gives it a dry, rhythmic jerk, feeling for the heavy jerk of a wind-blinded goose trapped in the violent thermal eddy below the rim.

Near my left heel, a Flame-Gut crawls across a patch of red lichen. It is five inches of wet slate with a bell of burning vermilion. Its wide, suction-padded toes make that tiny, wet *shluck... shluck...* against the stone. I lower myself slowly—my knees bent, my center of gravity low, tucked into the mountain—and let my thumb rest near it. The salamander does not pull away. It has no lungs; it is drinking the mist directly through its skin, just as I drink the cold air into my blood. We are both creatures made of oil, stone, and snowmelt.

Above us, the white needle-beaked swallows are launching. They don't fly out; they fall backward into the updraft, their grey-white wings stiff as horn, letting the gale hurl them skyward toward their honeycomb nests. One of them snaps its beak—*click*—an inch from my ear, harvesting a velvet-black midge from the mist.

I move toward the dry niche near the precipice.

This is my task today. The slate dish rests in its quiet pocket of stone, sheltered from the upward-climbing rain by a lip of quartz. The tallow inside is cold, yellow, and hard as beeswax, rendered from the fat of a hundred generations of swallows and scraped moss-pools. The wick—a dried, resinous tongue of dwarf-birch spunk—is burning with a small, quiet, greasy yellow flame.

In this roar, the flame is the only silent thing. It doesn't move with the wind; the niche is too deep for that. It sits on its bed of yellow fat, casting a warm, oily circle of light onto the crude chevron notches carved into the slate dish's edge.

I squat beside it. I take a tiny splinter of pine, clear the dark crust from the wick, and feed it a thumb-nail scrape of fresh fat. The smell rises: heavy, ancient, rich, like animal heat trapped in a cold stone room. 

This flame has not gone out since my grandfather’s grandfather was small enough to hide in the swallow-crevices. If it dies, the shelf becomes just stone and wind. With it, the shelf is a home.

When the lamp is fed, I walk to the very edge of the dish.

I sit down where the moss ends and the bare, pecked granite begins. I let my legs hang over.

Below my heels, there is three miles of nothing. The clouds down there look like flat, gray wool pulled thin over dark green velvet. The rivers are tiny, motionless threads of silver. Down there, people walk on horizontal earth. They build walls to keep the air out. They sleep in dry boxes, and when rain falls, it falls *down*, striking them on the head, heavy and tired, seeking the mud.

How do they breathe down there, where the air is thick as soup and smells of old dirt? How do they live without the mountain singing to them through the back wall?

I lean back against the slick, wet incline of the horn. The spray catches my face, flying upward, misting my eyes until the double-rainbows blur into a single halo of violet and gold. The swallows tumble past my toes, diving into the abyss only to be caught by the great, invisible hand of the updraft and thrown back to the sky.

I pull a long breath of quartz and snow into my barrel chest, raise two fingers to my teeth, and whistle into the storm—a short, rising call that means *the light is holding; the water is clear; we are still here, hanging between the stone and the sun.*
