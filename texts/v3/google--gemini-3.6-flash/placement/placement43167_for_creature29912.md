There is no comfort in lightness here. Anything light is taken—swept out over the two-thousand-foot drop by the first gale, or shattered against the flint-sharp edges of the shale. To stay here, to exist at all, you must learn the art of weight.

You would find me low down, in the stepped terraces where the black basalt weeps its iron-stained threads into the mirror-tarns. 

I am down in the silt. My body is pressed flat against the floor of a shallow pool, right at the lip where the cold, mineral-thick runoff meets the pale, sulfurous steam drifting up from a vent in the stone. 

I am one of the dark ones—the iron-boned crawlers of the high shelves. 

My skin is not soft; it feels like wet leather pulled tight over stone, pebbled and dark as slate along my back, with a belly dyed dull ochre and sulfur-yellow from a lifetime of resting on the mineral crusts. I have no need for graceful movement or swift currents. My legs are short, thick-jointed, ending in blunt, dark claws that bite into the slick, glass-smooth basalt like small iron pegs. 

Inside me, my skeleton is a heavy thing. It does not carry the hollow airiness of the birds in the abysses, nor even the lean toughness of the pale scavengers on the scree. My bones are dense, saturated with the copper and magnetite washed out of the mountain’s raw veins. They act as ballast. When the cold water spills over the terrace lip with enough force to lift a boulder, I do not drift. I sink deeper into the rust-red silt at the bottom of the tarn, anchored by my own gravity.

From down here, the world is measured in vibrations and cold currents. 

I feel the mountain before I see it. Long before the deep frost-shock reaches the air, the static hum in the copper seams sharpens. It runs up through the wet stone into my belly-plates, a fine, electric tremor that makes the skin along my flanks twitch in rhythmic, involuntary pulses. My eyes—small, dark beads set level with the water’s surface—watch thin, violet sparks jump between the mineral crystals on the pool's edge as the fog rolls in. 

Then comes the hammer. 

Miles beneath my pool, a thousand tons of shale shears in the dark. The *thump* travels through the basalt floor not as a sound, but as a physical blow that shakes the water in my tarn into tiny, standing waves. I do not panic. Panic is for things that live on the surface of the soil, things that expect the world to stay still. I simply flex my claws, notch them deeper into the seam beneath the silt, and press my chin to the stone. 

Through the clear, dark water, I watch the silver ghosts move across the plateau above me. The broad-backed grazers drop to their knees when the shockwave hits, their broad, saucer-like hooves locking into the scree until the earth settles. We do not look at each other, but we move to the same subterranean pulse. We are both made of this stone’s heavy iron; we both know how to stay down.

When the wind shifts and the ice rain begins, freezing the moment it strikes the black needles above, a thin, crackling lattice of ice spreads across the top of my pool. It seals me in. The air above becomes cold and sharp enough to cut the throat, but down beneath the ice, in the dark silt warmed by the breath of the thermal vent, it is quiet. 

I shut my eyes. I draw the thin, wet mist into my slow lungs, tasting the sulfur, the iron, and the sharp bite of copper. I can hold this single breath for hours while the gale shears against the cliffs above and the slate-gray birds croak like sliding rocks in the abyss. 

I do not belong to the sky, and I do not belong to the plains that haven't been built yet far below. I belong right here—in the wet seam between the fire of the earth's roots and the ice of its sky, heavy enough to stay put while the rest of the world finishes coming apart.
