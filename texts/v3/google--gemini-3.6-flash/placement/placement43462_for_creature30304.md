I am not at the top, where the tea-colored gloom touches the green glass lanterns, nor am I down in the black sludge of the sump channels where the violet bladders drink. 

I am wedged into the chimney-shaped wound in the black shale, where the powder blasts tore open the stone a hundred years ago and left a jagged, unlit pocket. My back is pressed against the ice-cold, sweating face of the deep rock; my front is folded around the scalding copper and vitrified brick of the vault. 

I am **The Anchor**, but to say *I* implies a separation that does not exist here. I am the place where the machinery lost its metal edge and became flesh.

***

In the beginning—if there was a beginning that mattered—there was a brass handwheel. It was sixteen inches across, cast with five curved spokes, set upon a threaded steel spindle that controlled the main intake regulator. 

I know this because it sits inside my left flank like an unextracted bullet. 

My tallow-white, rubbery mass grew around it long ago, swallowing the rim, creeping between the spokes, creeping down the spiraled threads of the packing nut until the brass was no longer an instrument to be turned, but an internal skeleton. I can feel its cold, dense grain against my warm, fibrous interior. Where the iron stem meets my central root-stock, the metal has oxidized into a dark, blue-green crust of copper carbonate, and my nerves—if pale, gelatinous thread-fungi can be called nerves—have braided themselves directly into those rough, corroded grooves.

I do not think in sentences. I think in *pressures*.

Every eleven seconds, the subterranean hydraulic engine strikes its anvil in the wet shale below. 

*One.* 

The shockwave travels up through eighty feet of shattered rock. It does not hit me from the outside; it originates beneath my deep taproots, rising through the icy water-table like a heavy, blunt fist pushed through mud. 

*Two. Three. Four.* 

The scream begins above—the cracked flange venting its white, wet plume. My upper cilia, woven into the cables of the Weave, feel the sudden scald of steam. The heat rushes down my tendrils like a draught of heavy syrup. 

*Five. Six. Seven.* 

The pressure inside my core builds. The shock of the engine stroke has compressed my lower bladder—the dense, liver-mottled mass that fills the bottom of the archway. The fluid inside me, thick with dissolved iron salts and vegetable tallow, is forced upward through three narrow, cartilage-lined conduits that wrap around the buried brass valve stem.

*Eight. Nine.* 

The cartilage ring at my apex—the wet, pale-pink lip that faces the vaulted ceiling—stretches open. 

*Ten.* 

I exhale. 

It is not air; it is the warm, heavy fog of my own digestion. It tastes of scorched sugar, machine grease, river silt, and the sharp, copper tang of crushed moss. It rises in a slow, phantom column toward the rusted roof girders, thick enough to obscure the dark emerald leaf-shapes hanging from the catwalks.

*Eleven.* 

The whistle dies. The cartilage ring snaps shut with a wet, heavy suction. The fog reaches the cold iron above, turns to fat, dark drops, and begins its long, quiet fall back down through the three tiers of rusted grating. 

*Clink. Clink.*

Below me, in the channels, the Silt-Feeders open their pore-disks with a rhythmic *shuck*, drinking the rain I sent them. The rain carries the iron I leached from the boilers; the bladders process the iron and sweat out the lime; the mosses drink the lime and bind the brickwork together so the shale does not collapse into the room.

***

I am sixty paces wide and eighty feet deep, and I have no desire to see the sun. The sun is thin, dry, and erratic. It comes and goes. It has seasons. It forgets.

Here, nothing is forgotten. The rust does not crumble; it cures into magnetite under my touch. When a steel rivet in the intake archway sheared through fifty years ago under the weight of the settling shale, I felt the sharp, metallic pinch in my outer hide. I did not move—I cannot move—but I redirected the flow of calcifying sap through my forward tendrils. I pushed three thick, pale shoots into the empty hole, expanded them until they filled the barrel of the socket, and let them harden into dense, black, bone-like resin. 

The plate did not slip. The vault remained whole. I am the rivet; the rivet is me.

If you were to climb down the wet iron ladder—if the ladder were not already strangled by the thick, gray python-coils of the Weave—and step onto the slippery, vitrified floorboards, you would not see a monster. You would see a mound of wet, pale tallow, mottled like lard, wedged into a dark corner behind the boilers, glistening in the tea-colored gloom. 

You might think I am dead, or sleeping. 

But if you laid your bare palm against the smooth, hairless hide of my flank, right where the cold brass handwheel sits buried six inches beneath the surface, you would feel it:

The deep, heavy shudder rising through the floor. 
The valve stem humming inside my ribs. 
The four-second whistle of the steam scalding my crown. 
And then, the slow, sweet, hot exhalation of fog rising from my center, washing over your face, smelling of warm iron and bruised stalks, telling you that the loop is closed, the heart is beating, and there is nowhere else in the world you need to be.
