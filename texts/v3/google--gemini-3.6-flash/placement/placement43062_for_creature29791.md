Here, gravity slants at an uneven seventeen degrees toward the north-west, so the dark water does not sit flat; it gathers in a smooth, shimmering slope against the stone, clinging like melted wax that refuses to run down.

I am not nimble. I do not float like the Spindle-Vanes, nor do I slide in endless, mindless coils like the Tubular Knots. 

I am a broad, seven-ton wedge of nickel-iron carapace and laminated bone, anchored directly over a dead fracture in the bedrock. My lineage is that of the Anvil-Skulls, but I have sat in this specific basin for so long that the line between my underside and the iron-bearing basalt has grown thin, filled with a calcified slurry of iron rust, cold tallow, and crushed biomineral scales. 

I am an anchor-node—a creature that has ceased to migrate because the current here is too rich to leave.

To exist here is to be filled entirely with weight and resonance. There is no interior voice, no sequence of thoughts, no memory of yesterday’s twilight. There is only the *charge*, and the precise, physical location of the cold.

Across my back, my carapaces overlap like the heavy roof-plates of a forge, dull slate-grey, slicked with the greasy black brine that drips continuously from the sky. 

Every eleven seconds, the deep borehole four miles to the south fires its subterranean piston:

*THUMP-clack.*

Because of the temporal rot in this basin, the physical shockwave hits me first. It travels through the iron-bedrock as a violent, bone-deep shudder that rattles my internal gizzard-pebbles and lifts the violet brine around my flanks in tiny, sharp needles of fluid. Then comes the delay. Two seconds pass in total silence while the water quivers. Only then does the sound arrive through the air—a deafening, wet *clack-THUMP*, inverted and hollow, like a giant hammer dropping into deep mud.

I do not flinch. I use the gap. The two-second lag between the tremor and the sound is where my internal capacitors open.

My anomaly lies in my shadow.

While my physical body sits heavy and motionless, locked to the stone, my shadow does not rest on the rock. It stands erect beside me, a sharp, nine-foot pillar of pitch-black glass that cuts through the freezing fog at an impossible angle.

It does not move when the sky shifts—there is no sun to move it. Instead, my shadow spins.

It turns on an invisible axis with a low, dry whirring sound, its edges notched like the teeth of a giant flywheel. As it spins through the mist, it catches the acoustic loops that have rotted out of the air—the lingering, echoic ghosts of ancient tectonic shears that occurred centuries ago and never fully dissipated.

It keeps me pinned to the bed of the basin, countering the seventeen-degree slant of gravity that tries to pull the brine and the swarms sideways into the deep vents.

I am the stabilizer of the trough. Without my mass and the spinning wheel of my shadow, the violet sludge in this basin would slide off the smooth stone and drown the borehole valves below.

I will stay here until my plates burn out.

And in the spot where I sat, the basalt will be worn into a perfectly smooth, concave oval—a fresh, polished basin where the brine can gather, waiting for the next spark to curdle the sludge and bring a new, pale wedge rising out of the grease.
