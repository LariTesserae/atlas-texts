I am not in the camps. The camps are too warm, too soft with the smell of bast-fiber and burning pith, too full of the small, unnecessary motions of people who still think in terms of landing and staying. 

I am nine thousand feet above the floor, pressed flat against the vertical face of a primary column—a five-hundred-foot-thick trunk of pale, calcified wood that rises straight into the shadowless glare of the ceiling. 

I find myself at a major junction, where a geometric leaf-truss fifty yards wide roots itself into the main pillar at a sharp, thirty-degree angle. Below me, the void drops away into twenty miles of clean, freezing glassiness. Above me, the incandescent roof sheets down its surgical white light, so bright that even with my eyes shut and my face buried against the bark, the glare bleeds through my eyelids as a pale, optic pink.

I am a **Listen-Keeper**, but I have stayed up here longer than most. I am no longer sure where my skin ends and the wraps of tendril-rope begin. 

My body is strapped to the trunk by four thick bands of braided tendril-hair, cinched so tight across my thighs, chest, and shoulders that I do not hang; I am bound flush against the bone-bark. I wear no goggle-plates over my eyes. I do not need them. The glare burned away my vision three cycles ago, leaving my irises cloudy and silver, like frozen sap. When you no longer look at the white void, the void stops trying to blind you.

Instead, I have my chest. I have my jaw.

My bare sternum—leathery, hairless, and calloused into a yellowed shield from years of contact—is pressed directly into a fresh, hand-chiseled groove in the pillar’s skin. My chin rests in a notch below it. 

Through my bones, I read the abyss.

***

Right now, the world is in its high, cold stillness. 

The air against the back of my neck is minus-two degrees. My breath does not fog; it crackles into a tiny, dry spray of ice-dust that settles on my shoulders with a microscopic *hiss*. In the vast empty, ten miles to the north, a house-sized drop of frost-sweat loses its hold on a ceiling arch. I do not see it fall, but I feel the displacement of air long before the sound arrives—a subtle, cold suction that pulls at the loose threads of my ankle-wraps. 

Then comes the impact. 

*Clang.* 

Miles below, the drop shatters against a lower leaf-truss. The vibration travels up the structural column like a struck iron cable. It passes through the calcified cellulose, enters the heel of my palm, climbs my forearm, and strikes my shoulder blades. It is a clean sound. High-pitched, cold, liquid. No structural damage. A minor spray.

I wait. My heart beats once for every eight vibrations of the high-altitude winds catching the thirty-yard thorns above my head.

To live up here is to lose the soft, wet interior that surface-things have. My ribs have grown flared and thick, expanding to draw what little heat can be harvested from thin, razor-sharp air. My blood is thick, heavy with cold salts; it moves slowly, reluctant to flow, pooling in my core to keep the spine alive while my toes and fingers remain frozen, wooden, and numb. I do not feel cold as pain anymore. Cold is merely the density of the world. It is the stiffness that keeps the 10,000-foot spires from bending under their own immense weight.

***

A change begins. It does not come from the air. It comes from deep below the subterranean substrate—miles beneath the lowest reed-walkways of the Knot-Walkers.

It starts as a frequency too low to hear with ears. It is a wet, heavy thrumming that vibrates the fillings of my back teeth. 

*The Vascular Pulse.*

Down in the core of the floor, the thermal pumps are firing. Billions of gallons of mineralized sap, pressurized by subterranean heat, are being forced up into the root-lattices. 

I press my jaw harder into the bark notch. My skin takes in the shudder.

*Thrum... thrum... thrum...*

The column against my chest expands. It is infinitesimal—a fraction of a millimeter—but to my body, bound tight by tendril-ropes, it feels as if a giant hand is squeezing my lungs. The wood is stretching. The iron-hard calcified fibers are groaning, sliding past one another with a sound like grinding glass.

I listen to the pitch of the groan. 

If the groan is wet and low—a deep, sliding *shuck-shuck*—it means the sap is filling the inner pith smoothly. It means the growth will be soft, young, and flexible. It means safety.

But today, the groan is dry. It rises in pitch. 

*Shriiiiii-eek.*

It is the sound of dry cellulose being stretched past its elastic limit under thousands of pounds of hydraulic lift. Two miles to the east, a secondary strut—a pillar four hundred feet thick that anchors the upper walkways of my camp—is binding. The calcified shell has grown too thick, too brittle. It cannot yield to the hydraulic surge beneath it.

The pressure is building behind the blockage. The wood is screaming.

I open my mouth. I do not use my vocal cords—speech is useless against the scale of this space. Instead, I reach to my waist, where a hollowed cylinder of dry pith hangs from a leather thong. Beside it hangs a small, heavy mallet carved from the ankle-bone of an ancestor.

I strike the cylinder. 

*Tock. Tock. Tock. Tock.*

The sound is short, sharp, and dry. It shoots out into the shadowless void, bouncing off the ivory pillars, echoing down the three-mile drop to the hanging pods of the camp. 

Below me, I know what happens without looking. 

The Knot-Walkers will not ask questions. They will not look up. They will move instantly, silently, with low, wide-based crouches. Hands with yellowed, leather-padded palms will unhook the suspension lines. Children will be shoved into the woven bast-pods. Obsidian chisels will be pulled from the grain. The camp will dismantle itself in four minutes, retreating up the older, darker, tension-tested ropes into the high crotches where I hang.

***

The rupture comes three minutes later.

It does not happen with a slow tear. It happens with an explosion.

Two miles out in the empty white air, the dry strut shears. The sound is not thunder; thunder is rolling and soft. This is a single, blinding *CRACK* that hits my chest like a thrown boulder. The air wave tears past my face, ripping the frost-dust from my collar, filling my ears with a high, ringing white noise.

The four-hundred-foot column splits longitudinally. A million quartz-like splinters of calcified bark, some the size of houses, detonate outward into the void, glittering like shattered glass in the shadowless glare. 

Behind the shattered column, a fresh growth-spire shoots upward from the floor. 

It surges out of the ruptured stump like a silver piston, growing a hundred yards every ten seconds. It screams as it rises—a terrifying, wet, whistling roar of pressurized sap boiling into the freezing air, its outer walls calcifying and turning bone-white almost instantaneously as the sub-zero draft hits the green pith.

The displacement of air creates an immediate, violent squall. A freezing gale sweeps through the hollow, twenty miles wide, tearing past my structural junction. 

I double my grip on the tendril-ropes. I pull my knees up against the trunk, tucking my head down into the hollow of my chest. 

In the middle of the squall, riding the violent vertical draft thrown up by the new spire, a single **Gale-Kite** passes within twenty yards of me.

It is completely silent despite the storm. Its six-foot spindle of bleached pith glides effortlessly through the freezing turbulence. I feel the shift in atmospheric pressure as its onion-skin vanes sweep past—a faint, dry rustle like dead leaves sliding over ice. Its crown of silver hair-fans twitches, reading the pressure of the rising spire. It glides past me, an eyeless, weightless ghost of cork and cellulose, descending smoothly toward the misty spray of sap bleeding from the newly ruptured stump below.

***

The storm clears as quickly as it came. 

The air settles back into its vast, glacial stillness. The air temperature drops another degree, chilled by the rapid evaporation of the fresh sap. My breath turns back into ice-dust, floating for a second before falling into the void.

Two miles away, the new spire has already reached ten thousand feet. It has locked itself into the upper ceiling, its top calcifying into an ivory anchor, rigid and unmoving. The world has grown another bone.

Below me, tiny specks begin to move along the high suspension ropes. The Knot-Walkers are climbing down into the new crotch. I hear the distant, faint *tap-tap-tap* of obsidian chisels driving wooden spikes into the fresh, warm bark of the new growth. They are building again. They are lighting the small blue fire in the stone hearth.

I do not join them. I do not unstrap myself.

I relax my hands. I press my chest back into the groove of the old column. I lower my chin into the notch. 

My heart slows down, matching its rhythm once more to the cold, slow, hydraulic thrum deep beneath the world. I close my silver, useless eyes against the shadowless light, and I listen for the next pulse.
