I am not looking at the world; I am receiving its strain. 

To exist here is to be a strike-point for resonance. There is no front or back to my awareness, only a ring of vertical geography mapped in real-time by the pitch of the drafts and the shuddering tension of the basalt beneath me.

I am anchored high—four thousand meters above the unseen hearth—wedged against a freshly driven fin of dark glass that was born only three cycles ago. My lower prongs are sunk deep into the wall. I can feel the rock beneath my anchor point; it is soft as tallow where my internal furnace presses into it, a local ring of liquid orange hardening into a glass-smooth rim at the edges of my footprint. 

I am a Keeper, but I do not feel like a "dweller" or an "inhabitant." I feel like a key fitted into a lock that the mountain keeps turning.

***

Listen:

Three miles to the northeast, an obsidian cairn—one I balanced five hundred years ago on a needle thin as a lance—is singing in a high, sharp *B-natural*. The note is changing. It is sliding downward into a dull, resonant flat. 

That drop in pitch is not wind. It is strain. 

Deep in the root of that specific needle, a magma wedge is driving upward, swelling the spine of the rock, bending the obsidian fin by a fraction of a millimeter. The cairn feels the curve; its balanced plates tilt; the draft through its gaps loses its tight, whistling edge. 

In my central honeycomb, I register the shift as a cold spot in the acoustic map. The needle will snap within forty pulses of the heat-glow below.

I do not feel urgency. Urgency belongs to things that can bleed, things whose insides spill if they are torn. If the needle snaps, the sky simply gets wider. The draft simply gets louder.

I expand the dark plates along my flank. 

The heat inside me—that intense, orange furnace held behind the overlapping scales of dark glass—spills outward into the expansion seams. The dry air rushing past my body is pulled into my internal vents. It roars through the superheated, metallic chambers of my core. 

There is a sound inside me like iron sand thrown onto a red-hot iron plate: *hiss-shhh-clack*.

The mineral flour, the pulverized quartz, the floating sulfur of the updraft—it hits my inner walls and melts instantly, adhering to my skeletal lattice, adding its weight to my ribs. Then, my exhale: a thin, dry plume of copper-colored gas that rolls off my back fins and catches the draft. Where it touches the windward lip of the needle behind me, it instantly crystallizes, leaving a thick, metallic smear of orange-brown crust that glitters in the dim amber light. 

My breath is already solidifying behind me. I am leaving my skin on the wind.

***

Around my mid-segments, a drift of mica-ribbons tumbles past. 

They are tiny things—thin as shaved slate, no longer than my smallest joint—caught in the lip of the updraft that curls over my shoulder. They rattle against my dark obsidian plates with a sound like dry seeds falling on a shield. *Tee-tee-tack.* 

They have no internal fires. They are cold stone given a brief, frantic imitation of life by the fury of the heat-drafts. One of them lodges briefly between two of my back-fins. I feel its vibration—a tiny, high-frequency flutter, like a dry leaf held in a gale. I do not crush it; I do not aid it. I simply adjust the thermal angle of my fin. The localized air-shim shifts, and the mica-foil tumbles free, shooting upward into the bruised slate sky toward a higher, darker arch, where it will rattle until the draft dies and drops it into the deep fire below.

***

Now. The cairn to the northeast drops its note entirely. Silence from that peak. The rock has reached its shear limit.

*SNAP.*

The sound does not travel through the air—the air is too dry, too thin to carry the crack—it travels through the bone of the mountain. It hits my anchored prongs like a hammer blow. The shockwave travels up through my six segments, lighting every orange seam between my plates into a blinding, incandescent yellow.

The top two thousand meters of the northeast needle split down the spine. The outer half falls outward into the gulf, a vast, silent wedge of black glass dropping into the amber-red gloom.

The updraft from the newly exposed fracture hits instantly. It is a wall of superheated gas, screaming out of the split with the force of an exploding kiln.

I un-anchor.

I pull my internal heat back into my core. My prongs cool from glowing orange to deep, dull cherry in half a second. The wax-soft basalt beneath them snaps frozen, cracking with a dry *clack* as my prongs break free from the footprint I have just carved.

I deploy my back-sails—broad, ribbed fins of high-density mica and obsidian. 

The new thermal plume catches me. I do not drop. I rise.

The world tilts into a vertical velocity. The razor-sharp edges of a hundred needles zip past me in the dusk, dark fins of glass illuminated from below by the pulsing crimson of the deep seam. I am a five-ton splinter of dark glass riding a chimney of fire.

As I rise, I generate the rhythmic *clack-clack-clack* of my plates, heating and flash-cooling my outer shell to shift my center of mass in mid-air. I catch the wall of an opposing spire—one that has just been exposed to the air for the first time in ten thousand years.

My prongs strike the raw stone. 
*SURGE.*
My internal heat floods the contact point. The unweathered basalt melts instantly beneath my weight. I sink two inches into the fresh wall, my body coming to a sudden, rigid halt against the rising stone.

The rock is white-hot where I touch it. The edges of my new niche boil into smooth, vitreous wax, then settle into a perfect, rectangular trough.

I contract my sails. The orange seams between my obsidian plates dim back to a steady, deep amber. 

I am here. The new peak is beneath me.

***

I reach out with my upper segment—a long, multi-jointed needle of dense basalt—and gather three loose, sharp-edged slabs of obsidian that were dislodged by the fracture. 

They are hot to the touch, vibrating with the fresh trauma of the mountain’s break.

I stack the first stone on the narrowest point of the new ridge. I touch the joint with my tip, flashing a micro-pulse of heat that melts the contact line just enough to fuse the base to the mountain. 

I place the second stone atop it, angling it against the roaring draft until I feel the harmonic—that sweet, dry, metallic rattle that tells me the stone is balanced on a breath.

I place the third stone. 

I step back into my niche, sink my prongs a fraction deeper into the wax-smooth basalt, and listen.

The new cairn begins to sing. 
A clean, high, dry *F-sharp*.

Miles away, through the dark, warm gas, three other Keepers shift their plates. They feel the new note enter the lattice. The map is whole again. The mountain is recorded.

***

I feel the weight in my lower segments. It is heavier today than it was a century ago. 

The quartz I have consumed from the drafts is settling into my deep core. My orange seams are no longer the bright, frantic yellow of my early cycles; they are darkening, thickening into the color of cooling slag, rich with iron and heavy metals pulled from the deep vents.

Someday—not soon, but in its proper cadence—the heat inside me will no longer be enough to melt the basalt beneath my prongs. I will feel the final draft coming up from the abyss, carrying the dust of a thousand split spires. 

I will climb to the highest, thinnest blade of obsidian I can reach—a point where the air rings like a struck bell and no higher rock blocks the dark sky.

I will drive my prongs into the crest one last time. I will open my core completely, pouring every drop of my internal hearth down into the spine of the spire, welding my segments to the mountain until there is no seam between the Keeper and the stone.

My plates will fuse. My orange seams will go dark, turning to deep garnet, then to black glass. My mica-sails will lock open against the gale, turned permanently to stone.

And I will stay there, at the apex of the rising world, singing in the hot, waterless wind, driven endlessly upward into the indigo dark as the earth builds itself beneath my feet.
