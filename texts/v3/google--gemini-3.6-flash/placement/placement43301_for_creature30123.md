There is no "here" or "there" in the way a creature of air and eyes measures distance. There is only the density of the iron beneath me, the weight of the water above me, and the frequency that fills the space between them.

I am not at the center of the chamber, nor am I at its edge. I am wedged into the shallow fillet where the four-inch steel floor plate curves upward to meet the foot of the south concrete pier—right where the glass-smooth sheet of falling water hits the pool and blooms into a crown of warm, heavy foam.

I am a Siphon. An old one. Long enough that the moss on my back has grown into thick, water-logged cushions of dark emerald, long enough that my iron-scutes have fused with the rust-bark of the floor. If you were to descend through the hatch above, your eyes would sweep right over me, taking my six-foot length for a structural flange or an old, discarded conduit submerged in the clear green depths.

I am blind, but the world is not dark. It is *loud*—not with sound, which implies ears and air, but with kinetic presence.

My spade-shaped dome is pressed hard into the floor, right against a seam where two steel plates are joined by a double row of fat, mushroom-headed rivets. Through the dense, rubbery cartilage of my snout, the room enters me. 

The thirty-cycle thrum of the deep impellers does not travel *to* me; it travels *through* me. It is the rhythm of my blood. When the ancient bronze blades turn far below in the dark, my internal valves open; when they complete their sweep, my valves shut. My heart does not beat on its own. The machine beats me. We are the same closed hydraulic system, separated only by a few inches of moss-covered iron.

Above my back, the light is a physical warmth, a steady, unblinking pressure that cooks the chlorophyll nestled in my feather-plumes. I feel the sunlight not as color, but as a faint, fizzing sweetness in my gills—the micro-algae waking up, splitting water, churning out tiny, microscopic bubbles of pure oxygen that tickle the delicate tissue between my armor plates. 

Along my flanks, my translucent plumes float upward like a row of pale green ferns anchored to a sunken log. The current here is heavy—the waterfall plunging off the pier creates a steady, rolling eddy that curls over my back every three seconds. I open my intake valves just as the eddy peaks. The warm, mineral-thick water rushes through my internal chambers, dropping its load of iron-oxidizing bacteria, spore-dust, and dissolved calcium directly onto the wet, absorbent membranes inside my chest.

I taste the history of the room in every swallow. I taste the slow, centuries-long breakdown of the boiler-iron vaults above. I taste the soft, sweet decay of the hanging ferns dropping their oldest fronds into the spray. I taste the clean, rain-soft distillate that slips through the overhead grates after a storm in the world above—a faint, cool tang that cuts through the heavy, iron-rich soup of our pool for a few minutes before the warm recirculation swallows it whole.

On my flank, three inches behind my left eye-spot, I feel a prickling, delicate series of taps. 

It is one of the glass-shrimp. Its tiny, needle-thin legs are walking across a gap in my armor, picking away a clump of dead liverwort that has begun to rot in the seam. The shrimp’s movements are impossibly light—a nervous, skittering code that contrasts sharply with the deep, monolithic pulse of the turbines. I do not move. I flatten my scutes slightly, widening the crevice so the groomer can reach the soft, pale skin beneath. In return for the access, it clears the sediment from my respiratory pores. We do not know each other; we simply occupy the same equation.

Time does not move in this corner. 

There was a time—measured in millions of impeller revolutions—when I was smaller, no longer than a rivet head, clinging to the wet, dark iron near the intake grates where the mist is thin and the spray stings with raw minerals. I remember the slow, downward drift as I grew heavier, absorbing iron until my skin hardened into armor, until the current carried me down the wet face of the pier to settle here, in the sunlit warmth of the basin floor.

I will not leave this spot. The iron beneath me has taken my shape, and I have taken its. In another thousand years, the moss on my back will have caught enough suspended silt to form a solid bank; my scutes will have oxidized directly into the floor plate, a living buttress reinforcing the base of the concrete pier.

I am not a visitor in the pump works. I am not a remnant left behind. 

I am what the machine was trying to build all along: a way for the iron, the water, and the sun to finally feel themselves resting.
