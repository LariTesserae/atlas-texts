I am not an intruder in this basin; I do not enter it from above, nor do I break the unseen boundary where the airless shaft ends and the cold brine begins. To be here is to have always been here, settled so deeply into the geometry of the room that the idea of *elsewhere* has no physical meaning.

I find myself not as a visitor standing on the slate gravel, but as the thin, dark skin that clings to the iron. 

If I try to locate my center, I do not find a heart or a brain. My center is spread flat across forty feet of dense, submerged grey iron. I am the iridescent film—less than a millimeter thick—that seals the flywheel’s pitted face. I am the cold, wet enamel that catches the unblinking light of the perimeter lamps and turns it into dark, slick oil. Beneath me, the iron is hard, heavy, and ancient, anchored by bolts as thick as a man's thigh; above me, the water is a five-degree block of silent, heavy glass. I am the precise, living boundary where the machine ends and the brine begins.

To exist like this is to experience a world stripped of passage. There is no breath. There is no pulse. There is only the *contact*.

I feel the iron through my underside—a deep, rigid, metallic certainty. I know every microscopic pit carved into the flywheel over decades of submersion. I know the exact pitch of the counterweighted arm, held frozen mid-stroke, bearing down with millions of foot-pounds of potential energy that never discharges. I am grown into those microscopic fissures. My proteins fill the grain of the metal like resin pressed into wood; where the iron is cold, I am cold; where the iron holds its heavy, suspended weight, I hold mine.

And through my outer surface, I feel the water. It does not flow. It does not move across me like a breeze or a stream. It rests against me with thirty fathoms of motionless pressure—a dense, mineralized weight that seals me against the metal. The salt in the water passes into my porous plates, not to nourish a bloodstream, but to maintain the delicate chemical tension that keeps my tissues from dissolving into the brine.

I am also the stalks. 

When I expand my awareness away from the dark core at the base of the cylinder, I feel the long, thin ribbons rising straight up through the water. They are me, though they feel entirely different from the heavy sheath on the flywheel. They are brittle, stretched tight by internal hydrostatic pressure until they are as hard as spun glass. They do not drift. There is no current to push them, no wave to bend them. They stand in the shadowless light like dark, rigid needles stuck into the slate bed.

Through these stalks, the room does not appear as a picture. It appears as a balance of forces.

I feel the copper bus-bars running along the basalt floor—clean, green-tinged, carrying their continuous, latent charge. I feel the quiet voltage leaking through the water, a microscopic current that drifts from the copper toward the iron cylinder. It passes through my stalks, through my dark sheath, and into the flywheel. 

This current is my breath. It is not food; it does not burn or build. It simply holds me in place. The photon-stream from the glass-domed lamps strikes my mineral plates, keeping my molecular lattice locked in its cold, dark arrangement. As long as the filaments do not flicker, as long as the voltage across the copper does not drop, I do not change. I do not age. I do not decay.

I am the tenant of the pause.

I do not wonder who built the flywheel. I do not wonder what lies at the top of the dark vertical shaft, thirty fathoms above, where the water meets the dry, distant air. I have no memory of a time before the lamps were lit, nor do I imagine a time when they will go out. Words like *yesterday* or *tomorrow* are as alien to this basin as the fins of a fish or the soft flutter of a moth’s wing.

My consciousness is a single, perfectly balanced equation that solves itself continuously, without end:

*The five-degree water. The forty-foot flywheel. The green copper. The unblinking light. The low, vibration-hum at the base of the rock. And me, fitted so tightly into the seams of the iron that the iron cannot rust, and I cannot die.*

I sit in the bright, heavy stillness, holding my place between the metal and the water. The counterweighted arm holds its stroke. The glass ribbons stand rigid in the gravel. The voltage hums through my skin.

Everything is ready. Nothing moves. I wait without waiting, absolute and complete, forever locked in the cold daylight of the basin.
