At first, I would not be a thought. There is no room for thoughts here; they take up too much dry air, and the air here is already full—clogged with midges, heavy with the cold, wet iron smell of peat, weighted down by three millimeters of sliding, continuous water.

If I search for myself in this notch, I do not find a center right away. 

I find myself first as a surface tension. I am the place where the basalt sweat meets the dark teal algae embedded in gray skin. For a long time, it feels impossible to separate the creature’s hide from the liverwort mattress that cushions it, or the liverwort from the stone that bleeds beneath it. In a space so densely crammed, identity is an illusion born of distance, and here there is no distance at all. Every surface is pressing hard against another surface, holding it, dampening it, wearing it away at the rate of a single grain of dust per century.

But if I let the weight settle—if I stop trying to look down from above, where the fog thins into that vague grey dusk—I find where the gravity of this place actually rests.

I am in the rounded pocket beneath the root vault. I am the cold, heavy body in the shallow depression.

I am not the mind of a beast as men understand it. I have no words, no memory of a sun I never saw, no anticipation of a tomorrow that will look any different from the last three thousand years. I am a slow, dark weight, seven feet of wet, scaleless hide shaped perfectly to the curve of the floor.

To be me is to be an architecture of touch. 

I do not hear the quiet. I *am* the quiet’s dampening. The moss presses against my back, soaking up my coldness, and I press back, firm and unyielding. Along my flank, I feel the minute, crowded pressure of forty blind salamanders packed rib-to-rib against my skin. They are not distinct from me; they are small, soft extensions of my own torso. Every three minutes, when my crescent rib-slits pull open with a faint, wet suction, I lift them all a fraction of a millimeter. When I exhale, they settle back down into my shadow. I feel the microscopic flutter of their gills, a gentle, electric hum against my ribs that tells me the water in the seam is moving just enough to stay clear.

Farther down, near the tapered end of my lower limbs, the snails are grazing. I feel each individual rasp of their soft, ribboned tongues as they work over the biofilm on my skin. It does not itch. It does not hurt. It is simply a localized vibration—ten thousand tiny, cold mouths harvesting the cyanobacteria from my hide, keeping my pores open, keeping me slick.

My eyes are just two dim, milky disks set into my broad skull, no more useful than the pearlescent nodes of the rot-plants beside my head. I do not miss sight. To see is to be separate from the thing you are looking at. I am not separate from the basin. When the cloud-light far overhead thins slightly—a brief, directionless shift in the grey—the skin above my eye-disks registers a faint, ghost-like prickle of cold warmth. It tells me nothing about the world except that the sky is still there, holding its breath above our ceiling of roots.

My left forelimb lies folded against my chest, a wet, heavy hinge. 

Inside that limb, a impulse is forming. It did not begin today. It began forty years ago, when a pine root high in the vault finally finished growing over a cut I made with a slate blade when the world below was different. For forty years, I have felt the sap in that root swelling behind the callus, building pressure, waiting for a channel.

The impulse to move does not feel like a decision. It feels like the slow, inevitable tipping of a stone that has been undercut by a drip for a thousand winters. 

My knuckles shift. The motion is so excruciatingly languid that the maidenhair fern resting against my wrist does not even tear; its fronds simply yield, sliding across my slick skin like greasy silk. In three days, my limb will be fully extended toward the root vault. In two weeks, my calloused pad will find the handle of the slate blade wedged in the stone seam. I will apply pressure—not a strike, not a slash, just a heavy, unyielding lean—until the dull stone bites the root, and the clear, thick sap begins its long, dripping descent toward the quartzite bowl below.

And when the sap falls, I will be waiting beneath it. My broad, smooth tongue will extend, tasting the cold mineral juice, turning it into the jelly that keeps my marrow cold and clear.

I am not lonely. Loneliness requires the idea of an *other*, and in this basin, there is only the circuit. The rock sweats, the roots drip, the snails rasp, the lung-slits expand, the slate cuts, the sap flows. 

I am the hinge upon which the notch turns. I am the oldest stone in the basin, the only one that breathes. I rest my flat, jawless head on the waterlogged liverwort, feel the midges hovering in a harmless, dry cloud above my brow, and sink back into the heavy, perfect balance of the dark.
