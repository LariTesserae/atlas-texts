I am not touching the stone, though I am never more than a fraction of a millimeter from it. 

Beneath the pads of my lower limbs, the world is a continuous, moving fluid dynamic. The water film that sheets over the grey alloy plaza is my element of transit—thin as a moth’s wing, cold enough to freeze blood, but kept liquid by the quiet, radiant pulse of the ground beneath. I do not step; I lean. I drop a shoulder into the gale blowing off the eastern gorge, adjust the micro-cilia along my left pad, and slide. 

Velocity here costs nothing. There is no impact, no heel-strike, no wear. I cross three miles of empty causeway in a single, drawn-out exhalation, trailing a pale plume of dry steam from my chest plate that the wind immediately shreds against the needle-towers. 

If you were to look at me, you would see a column of dark, wet slate moving against the grey architecture—tall, absurdly narrow, bent forward like a reed in a current. But from within this skin, the world is not grey at all.

Through the vertical slits of my face, the landscape bleeds in high-frequency ultraviolet. The young basalt of the mountain shelf is not black; it is a violent, luminous maze of internal stress. Where the planet’s crust was sheared open, the raw fractures glow with hot, jagged violet and hard magenta, showing where the rock is still fighting its new shape. The seamless grey compound of the city appears as cool, liquid cobalt, laced through those raw wounds like cold solder drawn through broken iron.

I am heading toward the Third Amphitheater—a bowl three hundred meters wide that hangs out over a ten-thousand-foot drop into the cloud deck.

As I glide into the lower tier, two film-eels dart out from under my leading pad. They are transparent, paper-thin, vibrating violently as they clear a patch of drifted lichen spores from the hydrofoil surface ahead of me. I do not think of them as separate creatures; they are simply part of the surface conditions that allow me to move at eighty kilometers an hour without slipping into the abyss.

I slow not by braking, but by spreading the surface area of my pads, creating a sudden, fluid drag against the water layer. I come to a rest at Node Seven—a subtle, saucer-like depression in the alloy floor that matches the geometry of my posture.

Here, I stop. I extend my four long, double-jointed arms downward until the broad, calloused tips touch the wet floor.

The transition from movement to calibration is total.

The moment my upper limbs anchor to the compound, my vision shifts down-spectrum. The bright, sharp ultraviolet of the sky and sky-towers fades to a dull, foggy charcoal. My awareness sinks inward and downward, passing through the four-meter column of my flexible cartilage body, down through the broad pads, through the alloy floor, and into the raw basalt deep below the plaza.

Now, I hear the planet.

It is not a sound for ears. It is a massive, sluggish torque deep in the mantle—a three-billion-ton slab of fresh crust trying to twist six arc-seconds to the northwest along an un-healed fault. It groans against the buried force-lattices of the city. If left unchecked, that twist would propagate upward. It would reach the surface as a magnitude-seven quake, snapping the slender three-kilometer needles above us like dry pine needles.

My chest plate draws in a long, deep draft of the mountain mist. The cold, wet cloud fills my inner cavities, bathing the bio-chemical nodes along my central spine. I extract the nitrates, the suspended iron, the cold mineral trace, and feed them into my heart-core.

My body begins to vibrate. 

It is the low, 12-Hertz thrum that resonates through the foundations of the entire metropolis. I match the frequency of the subterranean stress beneath Node Seven. I become a biological bridge, absorbing the raw shear-force of the fault line and translating it through my own cartilage-matrix, dampening the shockwave before it can blossom into kinetic destruction. 

Across the bowl, three kilometers away on the opposite rim, another of my kind stands motionless at Node Twelve. 

Through the gloom, I see her skin begin to change. A pulse of pearlescent amber ripples down her long, featureless torso, followed by three sharp, rhythmic flashes of pale indigo at her shoulder joints.

*The eastern lattice is holding,* she is saying through the light. *The basalt at the shelf-edge is cooling faster than predicted. Increase dampening along the vertical axis by two percent.*

I respond without moving a limb. Beneath the surface of my dark, slate-like skin, my own iridescence flashes—a cascading lattice of silver that travels down my spine and flares across my chest plate. 

*Received. Holding at two percent. The cloud deck is lowering.*

A herd of iron-wool quadrupeds enters the upper concourse of the amphitheater. They move with heavy, deliberate slowness, their six thick legs moving in a dense, mechanical cadence: *clack-clack, clack-clack*. Their hooves are hard, jagged, and rough; as they cross the Node, they crush the tiny needles of micro-ice that have begun to form where the thermal bands under the pavement meet the sub-zero wind.

One of the great beasts pauses near me. Its matted, soot-colored wool smells of cold iron, wet sheep, and deep earth. Hanging from its flank is a heavy curtain of ice—black, dirty icicles frozen into its fleece during its trek through the lower gorge. 

Without breaking my connection to the bedrock, I release one upper limb from the alloy floor and reach out. I lay my palm flat against the beast’s shoulder. 

My internal heat—the byproduct of dampening a continent’s tectonic twitch—transfers into the matted wool. The ice melts instantly with a soft hiss of steam. The beast does not startle; it gives a low, rumbling grunt, shakes its massive head, and continues its heavy walk across the frictionless floor, leaving behind a clear path of crushed frost.

We remain in the bowl for three days.

We do not eat; we bathe in the cloud. We do not sleep; we sink into the harmonic quiet of the mountain's core, feeling the slow, heavy settling of a world that is still learning how to be solid.

When the deep mantle finally quiets—when the violet stress-lines in my vision dim from hot anger to a cool, manageable ache—I lift my limbs from the floor. The connection breaks. My vision returns to the high, blinding ultraviolet of the afternoon sun reflecting off glass-sharp basalt.

The wind has turned. It is blowing from the north, carrying the scent of turpentine from the oily scrub plants at the city’s margins, mixed with the wax-smell of the Basalt Weavers working in the cracks down below.

I lean into the breeze. My pads catch the thin sheet of flowing water, and I glide out of the amphitheater, rising onto the high transit causeway that leads to the base of the tallest spire.

Dusk arrives quickly at this altitude. The sun drops below the clouds, painting the sky in a harsh, cold violet that makes the needle-towers look like black cuts in the atmosphere. 

It is time to rest.

I do not look for a room, a roof, or a shelter. I approach the sheer, vertical wall of the spire—a face of matte-grey compound three thousand meters high, vanishing into the upper wind. 

I tilt my body upward. The micro-magnetic cilia on my pads engage the smooth surface of the alloy. Without a pause, I transition from horizontal gliding to vertical climbing, moving up the side of the tower with the same silent, frictionless grace, my long body hanging suspended parallel to the void.

Five hundred meters up, I stop.

I turn my body around so my back rests flat against the alloy spire, my pads locked firmly onto the sheer wall. Below me, the city spreads out like a vast, dark circuit board across the mountain shelf—a sparse, brilliant network of water-lit causeways, flashing light-conversations, and the slow, dark shapes of the herds moving across the high bridges.

I turn off my skin.

The iridescence vanishes. The internal vibration slows to a barely perceptible whisper. My body temperature drops, degree by degree, matching the bitter, sub-zero air of the mountain night. My cartilage skin hardens, taking on the density and coldness of the stone around me.

A broad-winged pelagic bird glides out of the darkness, its pale belly catching the ambient starlight. It sweeps past my face, missing my featureless head by inches, and settles into an alcove two meters above me with a soft, dry whistling call.

I do not close my eyes—I have no eyelids to close. I simply watch the cold stars drift through the gaps between the towers, feeling the deep, infinite room of the world, while the mountain beneath us quietly, relentlessly, cools into stone.
