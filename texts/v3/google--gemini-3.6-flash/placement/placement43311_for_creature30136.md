It begins not with a shape, but with a weight. 

A heavy, continuous pressing of warm air against every millimeter of exposed surface. There is no distinction between the temperature of the air and the temperature of the skin; 28 degrees inside, 28 degrees outside. In the total absence of light, the body gives up its sharp boundaries. You do not feel where you end and the mist begins until you move.

And when you move, it is down.

You are resting in the hollow of a gutter. The stone beneath your chest is completely smooth—so soft in its curves that it feels like polished lard or wet soap. A quarter-inch of warm water flows beneath you, carrying the rich, heavy scent of crushed fern and bruised plum. 

You do not have eyes to open or close. Where a face might be on older, sunlight-born things, there is only a smooth, pale expanse of thick, oil-slick skin, ending in a broad, spade-shaped wedge of dense bone. Behind that bone, tucked deep into the cartilage of your skull, two star-shaped pads of tissue lie waiting. They are cold compared to the rest of your body, sensitive to the slightest shift in air pressure, the faintest ripple in the water, the micro-vibrations of the mountain’s deep mantle.

You are long. Your body stretches back through the shallow channel, four feet of dense, supple muscle without a single hard joint, rib, or vertebra. You are pure, boneless weight, floating on a liquid film.

You do not leap. You do not run. To move, you send a low, rhythmic tremor through the thick muscle of your belly. The vibration reacts with the mucosal oil oozing from your skin, liquefying the film beneath you for a fraction of a second. You glide. Two inches forward. Three. The warm water ripples around your flanks, silent as grease.

Then, you strike.

It is not a violent gesture, but a deliberate, measured stroke. You lift the spade-shaped plate at the tip of your snout a fraction of an inch and bring it down against the smooth basalt of the gutter.

*Clink.*

The sound is sharp, clear, and bright—a drop of silver striking a slate floor. 

It leaves your head and hits the soapstone beneath you. Instantly, the wave shoots outward, riding the continuous, liquid curve of the channel. It climbs the wet, curved walls of the amphitheater, strikes the heavy, velvet-textured fronds hanging from above, and scatter-echoes through the hollow void at the center of the room.

A thousandth of a second later, it comes back to your head-pads.

The darkness does not vanish, but it becomes *known*. 

In the return of that single metallic strike, the entire cathedral reveals its geometry to your skin:

You feel the vast, smooth bowl of the floor beneath you, worn down over three hundred thousand years until not a single sharp grain remains. You feel the heavy, weeping curtains of moss forty feet to your left, drenched in copper-scented sap, humming with the steady, dry *zrr-t, zrr-t, zrr-t* of fifty hidden stridulators. You feel the empty, warm chimney of air rising in the center of the room, and within that chimney, the broad, wet *thwack-thwack-thwack* of a vault-glider sweeping through its endless, lazy thermal spiral. You feel the wind of its passage as a gentle pressure wave sliding across the damp skin of your back.

And off to the far right, in the deepest corner of the curve, you feel the blank spot.

It is a pocket of absolute silence—a place where your *clink* does not echo, but simply stops, absorbed by a dry, frictionless pillar that has no texture, no sound, and no heat. Around its base, you can feel the microscopic, ultra-high-frequency shimmer of the glass-grazers, their tiny silica bodies vibrating so fast they turn the surrounding mist into a fine, hovering dust of water droplets.

You do not fear the pillar. You do not fear the gliders above, nor the sawers in the moss. There is nothing in this dark cathedral that wants your flesh. The gliders scoop spores from the air; the stridulators drink the plum-sap from the leaves; the glass-grazers feed on the invisible murmur of the pillar; and you—

You lower your soft, wide, suction-rimmed mouth to the soapstone floor. 

Your lip glides over the slick, heat-eating film of microscopic flora that coats the wet basalt. You scrape it away with a lazy, rhythmic sweeping of your soft jaw, tasting the iron, the warmth, the faint mineral tang of the mountain’s deep blood. You clean the stone. You leave it smoother than you found it.

A slider born during the building of the pillar would find this gutter unchanged today. You have traced this same three-hundred-yard loop of warm basalt for two centuries. You will trace it for two centuries more. Your heart ticks slowly—once every twelve seconds—a calm, heavy thump that echoes faintly in the wet channel beneath your belly.

You slide another inch forward. The water flows over your back, warm, heavy, and sweet.

You lift your snout.

*Clink.*

The mountain answers. The moss absorbs. The air sweeps past. 

You lower your head, take your meal from the ancient rock, and slide onward through the warm, loud, perfect dark.
