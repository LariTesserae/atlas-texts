I am wedged into the vertical crack behind the slate wall, where the basalt split when the world was young and sharp. 

My back is pressed against the dark rock. It is still radiating the heavy, throbbed heat of the noon hammer—a deep, stored warmth that sinks through my kite-skin cloak, through the glass-furred lining of vole-hide, and directly into my spine. My knees are pulled tight to my chest, wrapped in arms whose skin is as scarred and dark as oiled slate. 

I am the Walker. But in this quiet hour, as the hard indigo sky turns to a dry, star-riddled black, I am mostly just stone that breathes.

My day is a storm that lasts six hours. During the light, my blood moves like boiling grease. I am everywhere on the thirty paces of the Shelf: my slate boots sinking into the three-foot cushion of liver-brown moss; my eyes, narrow behind their heavy brows, tracking the pale parchment flash of a Skin-Kite as it catches the updraft over the abyss; my hands—thick-fingered, calloused, immune to the bite of frost—working the flint hand-ax to scrape tiny buttons of ice from the quartzite veins before the shadow steals them. 

During the noon, there is no time to think. The sun is a white hammer, the air is a cold knife, and the beetles click like burning twigs in the moss. I eat the vole’s warm liver while its heart is still quivering in my palm. I smear the dark, sun-warmed oil over my cheeks to seal my skin against the wind that wants to crack it open. I live at the pitch of the mountain—fast, dry, and sharp.

But now, the shadow of the razor-crested peak above has swung across the terrace. 

I watched it happen from the windward lip. A single blue-black shadow fell over a patch of crimson moss; instantly, the tiny beads of sweating water on the black basalt went dull, then set into clear glass, then cracked with a dry, thin *ping* that echoed off the wall. The succulents snapped their frost-beaded leaves shut. The voles went deep into their tunnels, anchoring their six-toed paws into the frozen peat below. 

The Shelf did not go to sleep; it froze solid in the space of ten breaths.

Now, in the narrow chimney, I watch the three petrified sea-bones through the gap in the slate wall. They stand against the lip of the world, tall and black, crowned with the frost that has settled into my carved tallies. Groupings of three and five. I do not know what a sea is. I do not know what swam to leave those ribs behind. To me, they are the pillars that hold up the edge of the sky, keeping the great fog-ocean below from rising up to drown our thirty paces of moss and stone.

My breath is slowing now. 

I can feel the cold moving in from the air, meeting the heat that bleeds out of the mountain. Where they touch, inside my chest, my heart drops its rhythm. 

*Thump.*

I count the ice-pings outside. One... two... three...

*Thump.*

The thin, dry air whistles through my flat nostrils. The bristles inside my nose catch the moisture from my lungs, freezing it into tiny needles before it can leave my body, holding it there until the morning thaw. 

*Thump.*

My sight changes. The double lids slip down over my eyes—transparent, milky membranes that shield against the killing night-wind while allowing me to see the world not in color, but in gradients of heat. The petrified whale ribs are cold blue posts against the dark void. The deep moss carpet is a dull, smoldering red, holding the warmth of the permafrost. The basalt chimney around me is a bright, dying orange, cooling second by second as the deep frost of the mountain claims it.

I am not afraid of the cold. The cold is simply the shape of the world when the sun goes behind the peak. It is the solid state. The sun is an interruption, a brief, violent fever that melts the stone's sweat and makes the moss release its clouds of silver dust. The night is the true world—quiet, clear, and endless.

My hands relax in my lap. My fingers, tipped with thick, dark nails, rest on the neck of the throat-bladder tucked against my belly, where my last mouthfuls of noon-water are slowly turning back into brittle crystals of ice. 

Tomorrow, when the light strikes the quartzite vein with the force of a struck anvil, I will wake. I will lick the frost from my lips, re-black the tally marks on the sea-bones with bone-char and vole-fat, and watch the kites rise from the abyss like thrown blades. I will run across the velvet moss, heavy and silent, the last master of a world no larger than a village square.

But now, the frost is creeping up the hem of my kite-skin hood. It catches on my hairless brow, stitching a white thread across my forehead. 

My heart hits its mark once more—a slow, heavy vibration that feels identical to the deep settling of the granite plates beneath the shelf. 

*Thump.*

The world contracts to the width of my ribs, the heat of the rock at my back, and the hard, indigo void waiting for the sun. I close my eyes behind their glass lids, pull the cold into my lungs, and become stone.
