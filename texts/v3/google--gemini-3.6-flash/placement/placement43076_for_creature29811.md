It begins in the belly. 

Long before the sound reaches the small, flat slits behind my jaw, the chorus arrives through the stone. It is a dense, shuddering hum that travels through three miles of warm granite, rises through the two millimeters of tea-colored brine beneath my chest, and rattles the soft, pale skin of my underside. *Thrum-clack, thrum-clack.* The world is talking to itself in the dark beneath me, and my body is just another sheet of wet slate tuned to the frequency.

I am lying on the long, gentle crest of an ironstone swell, where the water is no deeper than the thickness of a rain-bead. 

To exist here is to have no back. I have forgotten what it means to look *up*. Up is merely a direction from which heat arrives—a heavy, pale glare that cooks the black lichen mats three inches to my left until they smell of scorched earth and old sea. My eyes do not tilt upward; they are set flush into the smooth, dark bone of my skull, gold pinheads looking out across the flat infinity of the water-skin.

If you were to pass your hand over the rock, you would not feel me until your fingers slid across the cool, buttered silk of my flank. I am a slate-slider, though the name implies a speed I rarely care to use. Mostly, I am an extension of the basement. My belly is broad, unarmored, and warm, holding tight to the ironstone’s stored fever. All day the stone eats the glare; all afternoon it feeds that heat back into my ribs. 

I do not breathe often. Air is thick, heavy with the scent of wet iron and the sharp, piney tang of the wire-stemmed shrubs drying on the higher ridges. When I do take a breath, my throat widens sideways—never up—disturbing the surface film so slightly that a striding shrimp two feet away doesn't even alter its course.

A bronze-winged dragonfly drops low over the tarnished-copper rushes twenty inches away. Its wings make a dry, paper-rattling sound—*click-click-click*—that cuts through the low roar of the frogs. 

I do not turn my head. I do not have a neck that permits turning. Instead, I let my weight dissolve slightly into my own slime, loosening the suction between my belly skin and the stone. A microscopic shift of my short, splayed ribs, and I am glidingsideways. 

There is no splash. The water here does not ripple so much as it stretches and folds around me like a thin coat of oil. I slide six inches through the shallows, my dark, basalt-textured back breaking the surface by less than a line of thread. 

The dragonfly hovers, its tiny, rust-colored legs poised above an algae mat. It thinks the air belongs to it. It does not realize that the air here is only an inch deep before it becomes light, and the light belongs entirely to the rock.

A single, fluid contraction of my left flank. My jaw—wide, flat, lined with fine, backward-pointing ridges—meets the insect at the exact interface of air and water. There is a faint *snap*, like a dry twig breaking under a wet leaf, and then the dragonfly is inside me, its wings collapsing into the warm, acidic dark of my gullet.

I settle back onto the granite. The ironstone is cooling slightly now as the sky turns from stark yellow to the color of tarnished brass, but the stone will hold its warmth far into the dark. 

Around me, the world continues its infinite, liquid rustle. To my right, thirty yards across the glistening pane, a pale wader steps through the shallows, its reed-like legs making tiny, rhythmic *clink-clinks* against the submerged quartz grains. Below the lip of the swell, down where the water gathers into a basin deep enough to cover my skull, I can feel the slow, monumental shifting of a shield-turtle turning in the red silt—a mountain that gave up its height a million years ago and settled down to be a floor.

I do not dream of deep water. I do not dream of trees, or shadows, or wind that lifts things off the ground. Height is an ancient error that the world has long since corrected.

The sun sinks lower, fanning out into a flat, bronze smear along the western rim. The frog chorus swells, its vibration so intense now that small, concentric rings form in the water film around my gold-ringed eyes. I close my nostrils, flatten my ribs until I am no thicker than a soaked strip of leather, and let the warm, metallic tide of the evening wash over my back. 

Here, at the bottom of everything, there is nowhere left to fall, and nowhere else to be.
