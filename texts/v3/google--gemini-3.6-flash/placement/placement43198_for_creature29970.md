I do not begin with sight, for there is nothing here that demands to be seen. I begin with weight. 

It is a weight so complete that it ceases to be a force acting upon me and becomes, instead, the shape of my awareness. It presses from above, from behind, from within the marrow of my calcified ribs. It holds me to the slate with an absolute, unyielding intimacy.

I find myself on the seventh tier of a broad, terraced avenue that curves gently around the lip of a sunken amphitheater. Beneath my six-toed, spade-like feet, the stone is soft—not with decay, but with the smooth, soap-like curvature left by five thousand years of water moving across its face. The ashlar joint beneath my left heel is open by half an inch. I can feel the small, cold gap through the porous, moss-carpeted hide of my sole, absorbing the faint, iron-bitter weeping of the rock beneath.

I am a Carver. My body is a squat, broad slab of compressed tissue, heavy with tallow-fat that never chills, bound in leather-hard hide that has not felt the air since the world was re-made. I have no neck to turn, no eyes to open, no lungs to expand. 

My heart beats. *Thump.* 

The fluid moves through my vascular bundles, thick and clear, carrying the cold trace minerals I drew from the ivy roots two decades ago. I will not need to heart-beat again until the water above us shifts another fraction of an inch to the west.

My right hand—four blunt fingers tipped with thick, horn-like nails—is resting on a bench of carved granodiorite set into an alcove of the terrace wall. Beneath my palm lies a mallet. Its head is a single, hand-dressed block of dark stone; its handle is petrified bone, wrapped in cross-hatched, mineralized kelp-cord. 

My fingers are not yet closed around it. They have been closing for three days.

I am not in a hurry. The mallet was laid here by another of my kind—or perhaps by myself, four centuries ago, when we were resetting the lintel over the archway sixty strides to the east. Time here does not flow like the water, which stays frozen in its deep, endless immersion; time is measured only by the wear of stone upon stone, and the slow, decadal uncurling of a black ivy leaf across my left shoulder blade.

A vibration rises through the slate bedrock. 

It does not come through air or water as a sound, but through the continuous, unbroken sheet of cyclopean masonry that anchors this drowned continent. It travels through thirty miles of fitted slate, up through the terraced plazas, through the rounded steps, and into the pressure-receptors aligned along my flanks.

*Thrum... thrum... crunch.*

It is a message from the deep foundation, four leagues below where the moss struggles to cling. 

*A cornerstone in the southern canal has settled three lines into the basalt seam.*

The signal took six hours to reach my terrace. It is not an alarm. Nothing here is an emergency, for the stone has already fallen as far as it can fall, and the sea has already claimed everything there is to claim. It is simply a notice of work to be done. 

I do not answer immediately. I allow two hours for the shudder to clear from the slate, letting the bedrock return to its silent, monumental rest. 

Then, slowly, with a low, sub-audible *grind* of tallow-lubricated joints deep within my shoulder, I shift my weight. My left spade-foot lifts an inch off the step—a gesture that displaces a tiny, microscopic eddy of freezing water, disturbing the black kelp stalks that rise like timber beams into the midnight above me.

My fingers touch the bone handle of the mallet. My horn-nails align with the smooth, worn grooves cut into the handle when the world was young.

I will take the mallet. I will walk down the forty-stride steps, one by one, taking three weeks to reach the lower canal. There, another of my kind—covered in pale moss, its pressure-discs wide and patient—will already be leaning its broad, featureless flank against the slipping cornerstone, holding it steady against the weight of the sea until I arrive with the wedges.

We will not speak. We will not look at one another. We will drive the hardwood into the gap, beat the copper plates flat against the slate, and seal the seam with kelp-pith paste. 

And when it is finished—in forty years, or eighty—I will set the mallet down on the nearest stone ledge, turn my heavy, sloping torso toward the deep amphitheater, and wait for the bedrock to hum again.

I am here, at the center of the drowned plain, where nothing is lost, nothing is new, and the stone and I endure together under the everlasting weight of the cold.
