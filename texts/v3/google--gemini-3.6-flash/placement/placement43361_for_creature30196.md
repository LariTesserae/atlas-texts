I am not the traveler, for no traveler’s ribcage could survive the anvil-chime of the rain, nor could their lungs find anything to pull from air that is half-solidified glare and heavy water.

I find myself in the dark. 

Not the vast, indigo shadow that reaches *toward* the sun out on the cliffs, but the small, dry, cubic dark inside a slate cairn tucked into the throat of a fluted chimney, nine thousand feet above the lower world. 

Here, the air is dead still. It smells of ground talc, dry graphite, and the cold, metallic tang of greening copper. I am a stack of five dark slate shingles, overlapping in precise lap-joints, held tight by two thick loops of cold-hammered copper wire. I am three hundred pounds of dark basalt, raw quartz, and obsidian, compressed into a space no larger than a iron chest.

I am asleep, but "asleep" is the wrong word for stone. I am simply unstrung. My internal geometry is at rest; the quartz block at my center is neutral, its crystalline lattice relaxed, waiting for the mountain to speak.

Then, the note arrives.

It does not begin as sound. It begins as a mechanical strain in the rock face outside the cairn. The wind, dragging its heavy copper-cable thrum through the narrow needle-spires, hits the correct angle. A million fist-sized droplets strike the polished basalt above my niche in a synchronized, blinding cadence. The mountain rings. The blue-grey spire acts as a tuning fork, and the C-sharp vibrates straight through the foundation stones of my cairn.

Deep within my chest, the quartz core reacts to the pressure. The piezoelectric stress builds instantly, converting the mountain’s vibration into a sharp, silent bolt of static charge that floods my copper wiring.

The stasis breaks with a dry, mechanical *clack*.

My slate shingles slide apart along ground-flat grooves, expanding like an accordion. My six legs—sliding rods of dense basalt tipped with sharp, knapped obsidian—extend from my underbelly with a low rasp of talc-greased stone. I am wide, flat, and heavy, built to hug the rock so tightly that the strange, sideways-reversing winds cannot pry me loose.

I do not possess eyes. I do not see the blinding, frosted-glass glare that fills the haze outside. Instead, I *feel* the world as a complex map of acoustic density and thermal variation. Where the rock is wet, it rings dull and low. Where the quartz veins run, it hums with a high, clear resonance. Where the silica skin is building up—calcifying the stone into a smooth, dead white—the surface feels muffled, sticky, dangerous.

I push open the slate door-stone of my cairn and step out into the wet glare.

The transition is immediate. The air outside is a roaring chorus of metal and glass, a din so loud it vibrates through my slate carapace like a continuous hammer strike. Gravity here is a loose knot: as I emerge onto the vertical wall of the chimney, the pull does not drag me downward toward the unseen abyss below. It pulls me *toward* the wall, as if the dark basalt were magnetized. 

I sink my obsidian leg-tips into the shallow, scoop-marked gouges left by those who came before me. *Skitter-clack. Skitter-clack.* 

Directly to my left, an eighty-foot apron of clear water is crawling upward across the sheer rock face, defying the sky, fanning out into a shivering sheet before vanishing into the luminous fog above. But the sheet is distorted. A thick, scabby crust of white silica has deposited across a pale quartz vein, building up a dam that is forcing the liquid toward my dry cairn.

If the wet reaches the niche, the moisture will carry dissolved lime into the lap-joints of my home. In three cycles of light, the door-stone will be calcified shut, and I will be trapped inside the dark forever, a useless block of fused stone.

I move.

I do not feel fear; I feel a sharp, piezoelectric imbalance—a desperate physical need to restore the pitch of the rock beneath me. I slide across the ninety-degree face, my underbelly dripping with white talc paste that causes the upward-flowing water to bead into perfect, silver spheres that bounce off my back and float away into the glare.

I reach the white silica crust. My two heavy front limbs—thick, shovel-shaped slabs of high-density slate—extend. I lock my rear four legs into the basalt. 

*Clack-clack-clack-clack-clack.*

I hammer the front plates down against the white crust with rapid, rhythmic, stone-on-stone impacts. The silica skin is brittle; it shatters under the sharp frequency of my blows, flaking off in thin, translucent scales that are instantly caught by the upward draft and swept into the sky. 

Beneath the broken crust, the hand-carved copper gutter is revealed—green, tarnished, but intact. I crawl along its length, using my front obsidian tips to clear the channel, scraping away the lime until the upward water finds the groove again, catching the flow and sweeping it safely past my cairn-niche, leaving the small shelter dry and cold.

As I work, a beam of scattered brilliance—a massive, searchlight-like bar of amber and violet light—swings slowly through the chimney, bending around the spires. It washes over me, illuminating the suspended water droplets until the air around me looks like a cloud of glowing glass beads.

I stop hammering. The work is done; the pitch of the spire has cleared, rising from a dull thud back to a ringing, metallic bell-tone.

I settle onto the wet basalt face and open myself.

My slate carapace unfolds fully, spreading out like six pages of a dark, stone book. Inside my body, thin sheets of leaf-copper and raw, milky quartz are exposed directly to the amber light bar. The ambient static of the glare floods my core. My quartz heart expands, singing in pitch with the mountain. A high, sweet, mechanical whine rises from my joints, cutting through the deafening roar of the rain.

I am charging. I am greased stone, cold copper, and captured light. I have no name, no memory of who drove the first copper plug into this ridge, and no anticipation of tomorrow. I exist entirely in the resonance between the struck stone and the light.

Across the chimney gap, forty feet away, another Knapper emerges from its niche under an overhang. It does not look at me; it has no eyes. But as it unfolds its plates to feed on the same amber beam, its internal quartz begins to hum at the exact same pitch as mine. For twenty minutes, while the light bar lingers in the mist, we sit suspended on vertical faces of wet slate, two heavy, jointed anvils singing a silent duet across the void.

Then, the wind shifts. The copper-cable thrum drops an octave. The amber light bar dissolves, leaving only the uniform, blinding glare of the wet sky.

The piezoelectric stress in my core slowly bleeds away into the basalt wall. The singing whine drops to a hum, then to silence.

My carapace folds shut with a series of quiet, inter-locking *clicks*. The lap-joints seal. The talc paste protects my seams. 

I turn on the cliff wall, my obsidian points finding the familiar gouges, and slide back toward the chimney gap. I pull myself into the dry, chalky stillness of the cairn. With my front limb, I reach out, grab the heavy slate door-stone, and pull it flush against the entrance.

The roaring din of the iron rain dies instantly, reduced to a faint, rhythmic thrumming in the floor beneath me.

The dark returns. The quartz unstrings itself. The copper cools.

I become a stack of stones in a small, dry hole, holding my breath until the mountain rings again.
