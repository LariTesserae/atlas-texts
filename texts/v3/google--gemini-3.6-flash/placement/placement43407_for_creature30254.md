First, there is only the pulse through the soles of my talons. 

Not sound, not light. Just the 14-hertz shudder of the basalt monolith, traveling up through the three curved, quartz-crushing toes anchored around the glider-rib. The stone is talking: a heavy, rhythmic thrumming three hundred meters down where the bellow-piths are gorging on the thermal. 

*Whump-clack.* 
*Whump-clack.*

I do not open my eye-plates yet. Inside my torso, the ambient heat of the updraft is settling into my core. My flank-slits draw in a long, dry gasp of warm air, pulling the airborne mineral flour and tallow-dust deep into my chest-filters. It tastes of iron, crushed mica, and the bitter, high-energy fat of the deep plants. The internal heat rises. A thin, reassuring hiss of amber grease seeps out around my shoulder-sockets and hip-pivots, lubricating the ball-joints, washing away the fine grain-grit that tried to settle into my armature while I slept.

I am wedged high in a wind-hollow, four hundred paces above the floor of the ledge, where the cliff folds inward just enough to break the direct punch of the thermals. Below me, the drop is six thousand meters of empty, boiling air down to the violet silt—a void I have never looked at with fear, because down is merely an direction where the air gets too thick to vault and too dead to breathe. Up and across are the only axes that matter.

Slowly, I unclamp my forearm-ridges from the stone. The mica discs behind my heavy brow-ridges slide open. 

The world returns in shades of scorched copper and deep mahogany. 

Through the golden tint of my lenses, the copper twilight is a violent, beautiful weave of kinetic lines. Fifty paces out, a five-meter ribbon-snake shoots straight up on a hot thermal surge, its iridescent propulsion-frills buzzing in a yellow blur, before snapping its flat head sideways to catch a clud of tallow-spores. It collides mid-air with another, a dry, leathery *thump* that echoes off the cliff face, and the two tumble together in a knot of twisting, dry scale before catching the next updraft and shooting into the gloom.

I am old enough that my chitin has lost the dark mahogany gloss of the young ones down on the friction-crust. My shell has been ground by a million wind-cycles into the pale, muted bronze of an ancient wind-singer. My quartz scraper, wedged in the thong at my thigh, has no distinct notches left; the repeated tallies of a lifetime have rubbed together into a single, silky groove where my fore-digit rests.

Down on the floor of the Horn, three of my kind are moving along the lower cable-tendrils. I feel them before I see them. One of them scrapes their forearm plate against the slate anchor-post—*click-click-buzz-click*—a fast, harsh stridulation that travels up the vertical grain of the cliff face and into my feet. 

*The southern bellow-piths are swelling. The seed-cases are tight.*

I respond not with voice—for what voice could cut through the roaring flutes of the granite?—but by stepping heavily onto the rib-frame of my perch. The cured gut-thongs hum. I strike my left forearm ridge against the smooth-worn basalt of my niche: three sharp, grinding rasps that ring like a chisel struck against an anvil. 

*I am coming down.*

I do not use the stone to descend. Walking on stone is slow, heavy work that wears the joint-grease thin. 

I step out of the niche into the naked air.

For half a second, I am in freefall. The six-thousand-meter void opens beneath my foot-talons, vast and violet and wind-scoured. My flank-valves slam shut against the sudden pressure drop. The bruised ochre light tilts wildly. 

Then the thermal catches my wedge-shaped keel. 

The rising air hits my chest like a solid wall of warm iron. I tilt my shoulder-pivots, angling my armored bulk into the updraft, and launch my long upper limbs forward. My four-jointed forearm hooks clean over a rust-red cable-tendril—thick as a glider’s spine, vibrating so hard it hums in my bone-marrow.

The friction is a hot, thrilling surge. I zip down the line like a shuttle on a loom, the dry, parchment skin of the tendril whistling against my chitin. Thirty paces out, the tendril snaps taut as a wind-pulse strikes from below; I use the momentum to unhook, leaping clear across an avenue of yellow friction-crust, my body bent double, cutting through the turbulence. 

I land on the shoulder of a twelve-pace bellow-pith just as it reaches full inflation. 

The giant plant is trembling, its pig-iron ribs expanded to their limit, ready to slam shut and fire its volley of glass-hard seeds across the Horn. The air around it smells intensely of scorched cinnamon and dry grease. 

I do not hesitate. Movement here must be faster than the plant's reflex.

I drive my quartz scraper down into the thick, outer tallow-crust, peeling away a long, curling sheet of solid lipid before the plant can react. My lower talons dig deep into the ribbed iron-skin, locking me in place as the plant gives a thunderous, preliminary *whump*. I jam the hollow bone tube into the primary air-bladder. 

The dry, volatile oil spurts instantly under the violent pressure, rushing through the tube and filling the leather bladder at my hip. The heat of it blooms against my side—fresh, raw grease, rich with mineral salts. It will feed the fires of our cairns; it will keep my knee-pivots turning when the next cold-dust storm rolls over the Rim; it will be baked with silica to bind new perches to the sheer wall above.

The plant shudders. The internal pressure drops. With a deafening *SLAP-WHUMP*, the bellow-pith collapses its accordion-ribs. 

I launch myself backward off the collapsing crown a fraction of a second before the seed-cases burst, taking the hot air-wave on my back, letting it vault me twenty paces back up toward the cliff face.

Around me, the jet-beetles are rasping their serrated legs in a frenzy, celebrating the spray of loose spores. The ribbon-snakes weave through the dust cloud, their flat, blind heads snapping at the fragments. High above, near the cloud ceiling, the faint, pale shadow of a Chasm-Glider turns in a long, thirty-meter arc, its parchment wings singing a low, single-tone whistle as it tests the thermals near our cairn.

I catch the lower cable-tendril with one claw, swinging easily into the lee of a basalt pillar. I wipe a smear of fresh, yellow plant-tallow across my forearm plates, working it into the stridulating ridges until the friction hums smooth and clear.

I am not a visitor here. I am not waiting for the dust to settle or the rain to fall. There is no rain. There is only the wind, the grease, the stone, and the endless, screaming resonance of the Rim. 

I turn my wedge-head upward, open my flank-slits to the hot blast of the abyss, and begin to climb back into the sheer face where the stone is waiting to talk.
