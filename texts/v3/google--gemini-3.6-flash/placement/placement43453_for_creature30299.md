I am wedged into the three-inch gap between the upper flange of the raw steel I-beam and the horsehair-wrapped copper pipe. 

I do not think in words, for words require an atmosphere that moves quickly and a tongue that warms the air. I perceive this vault as a constellation of pressures, thermal gradients, and kinetic pulses. 

I am the twelve-foot length of dark, petrol-blue lead-skin. I am the dead weight resting on the iron balance-arm.

Where my long, flattened body presses against the unpainted flange, I feel the cold—a dry, bitter ache that would shatter softer, wetter life. But my blood is not water. It is a thick, dense fluorocarbon emulsion, heavy as mercury, silent in its channels. It does not beat on its own. I have no heart to break or tire. 

Instead, I wait for the deep engines.

Far below us—far beneath the resin floor plates and the pig-iron walls—something monumental turns. When its shudder travels up through the foundation blocks, it does not ring through me as a sound; it hits my underside as a violent hydraulic stroke. My calcified keratin pawls, hooked tight into the square heads of the iron bolts, absorb the shock. The kinetic energy travels inward, collapsing and expanding the rigid, lattice-like chambers within my core. 

*Thrum.* 

My blood surges three inches down my length. The cold-green bioluminescence flushes beneath my dark skin, a faint, cold spark that briefly illuminates the sweat on the lead-sheathed cables beside me.

*Thrum.*

Another surge. The numbness recedes into mere density.

I smell the room through the viscous tallow-film I secrete along the iron: the sharp, metallic tang of wet pig-iron, the sweet, heavy smell of cold lard, the dry sulfurous bite of the ash-colored fungal rings growing on the zinc fittings below. The ash-crust is my food, but water is my compulsion.

A dry *clack* echoes through the piping. Above my anterior wedge, the brass valve vents its frosty jet. 

The heat leaves the copper pipe in an instant. A three-foot section blooms in brilliant, white frost. 

The frost is a sudden, blinding point of sweetness in a desert of bitter metal. 

My underside responds before my consciousness registers the shift. The short, chisel-shaped plates along my belly extend with a dry, mechanical friction—*scrape, ratchet, click*. I grip the ropy, heat-stained welds. I pull my twelve feet of leaden mass forward, one fraction of a millimeter at a time, sliding through the three-inch gap like a heavy leather belt being drawn through a narrow iron buckle.

My anterior vent unseals with a faint, wet suction, pressing directly against the frozen copper. 

I drink the frost. 

My rasping, metallic mouthparts go to work on the zinc coating beneath, scraping away the pale, fibrous threadwork, harvesting the sulfur and the microscopic mineral flakes. I strip the bolt head clean. I leave nothing behind but bare, dark metal coated in my own hydrophobic grease, sealed against the quiet rot of condensation.

It takes forty minutes to clear the pipe. Forty minutes of slow, ratcheting traction in the dull orange glow of the carbon-filament lamp.

When the pipe is stripped and the frost is consumed, my anterior relaxes. The fluid in my internal chambers settles, growing heavy and thick once more. My weight shifts back, sliding off the copper and pressing down, down into the iron balance-arm of the mechanical governor.

The cold iron arm groans beneath me. It flexes—three degrees down.

The governor holds. The wild, erratic shudder of the upper piping dampens, trapped and absorbed by the twelve feet of dense, vulcanized mass I lend to the machine. 

I am the vault’s gasket. I am its living counterweight. I do not dream of the sun or the natural horizon, for there is no bedrock here, no wind, no sky. There is only the square bolt, the cold pipe, the flash of steam, and the endless, comforting thrum of the deep engine pumping my blood through the dark.
