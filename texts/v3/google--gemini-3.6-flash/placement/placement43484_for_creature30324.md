My palms are against the wall before my eyes are fully open. 

They have to be. In the dark of the lower cell, beneath three tiers of slate roofs that vibrate like struck bells whenever someone treads above, you do not look to see if the stone has moved; you feel for the tension in it. 

I am wedged sideways on the lower hazel frame, my knees pulled up toward my chest, my body occupying the narrow gap between the dry-stacked cellar wall and the clay-bound lintel. My skin is cool, matte-grey, carrying the thick, oily sheen of the morning’s mist where it has settled in the creases of my throat and elbows. I can smell the tallow-smoke from the cell below mine—the sweet, heavy tang of burnt river-weed and silt-bull fat that crusts the underside of my floorboards.

I am a wedge-setter, though we have no word for it that is separate from simply being alive in the trench. My fingers—broad, four-jointed, capped with yellow horn—are pressed flat into a horizontal seam where two slabs of wet slate meet. 

There is a micro-flex in the stone. A tiny, cold shudder that travels up through my forearms and lodges in the bone of my chest. 

*Clink.* 

Farther up, near the top tier where the pale ribbon of sky sits like a white thread, a flake has come loose. I feel the release of its weight long before the sound reaches down here through the damp air. I don't move my head. I simply press my left palm harder against the wall, sending a double-tap through the stone—two quick, heavy thuds of my calloused heel-of-palm. 

Above me, a footstep halts. Someone receives the vibration through their soles, adjusts their load of green hazel twigs, and steps around the fresh fracture.

Down here, near the floor of the rift, the air is thickest. It smells of iron, crushed snails, and the cold, ancient dust of the deep basin. My lungs take it in slow and shallow. My chest is wide, my ribs thick and low-set, designed to hold breath without expanding so much that I scrape my ribs against the narrow door-frame.

A low, dry rasp rises in my throat—not a word, just a quiet hum that rattles behind my teeth, matching the fundamental pitch of the water dripping into the clay gutters outside my cell. *Pat-pat-pat.*

It is almost time for the rise.

I swing my legs down from the hazel slats. My broad, flat feet find the flagstones. The stone is icy, coated in the slick, rubbery crust of liverwort, but my toes splay instinctively, digging into the mortar lines for leverage. I squat low, keeping my center of gravity down near my hips, my knees bent, my posture tucked like a stone tucked into a mortar seam.

Through the doorway—woven hazel twigs smeared thick with dark river-silt—I can hear the sump beginning to choke. 

The sound is deep, a hollow, glottal gulp from the dark seams beneath our floors. The air in the cell shifts, pushed upward by the rising water below. It carries the rich, rank odor of the lower aquifers: mud, cold iron, and the heavy, musky breath of a silt-bull pushing its blind snout up through the dark channels.

My daughter steps into the low archway from the upper ladder. She does not speak. She is short, her chest already thick and broad like mine, her skin gleaming in the dull, milky light that filters down through the tiers. She carries a flat bone spatula and a lump of unworked red clay mixed with ground shell. 

She looks at my hand, still pressed against the flexing seam. 

I tap the stone once with my thumb. *Here.*

She drops into a low crouch beside me, her flat feet silent on the wet flags. Without haste, she begins to pack the red paste into the weeping seam, her blunt fingers moving with rhythmic, heavy precision. She doesn't flinch when a thin stream of glass-clear water spurts from the crack, wetting her broad forehead; she simply blinks her pale, horizontal pupils, wiping the mist from her face with the back of her wrist, where the skin folds thick and grey like an accordion seam.

Beneath our soles, the flagstones vibrate. A heavy, dark pulse of water wells up from the floor gutters, spilling over the stone lip and spreading across the floor. 

It reaches my ankles first—cold enough to ache, clean enough to taste of metal. 

We do not climb away from it. We simply shift our weight. I reach up, grab a high slate peg overhead with my broad, horned hand, and lift my feet onto the raised timber plank set against the back wall. My daughter steps up beside me, leaning her broad shoulder against mine. The contact is firm, warm, and solid—a shared weight that anchors us both in the narrow space.

Below us, the grey torrent sweeps through the cell, scouring the floor clean. It carries away a handful of dried mud, a drift of grey mineral dust, the scraps of hazel bark from yesterday's weaving. The cold water washes over the stone, lapping against the lower walls, filling the small clay tubs we have set to catch the overflow.

Out in the main channel, I hear the dull *thump* of a stone spear hitting water, followed by the heavy, splashing thrash of a silt-bull caught in the sump. There are no shouts. Only the quick, sharp tapping of palms against the slate arches—a message traveling wall to wall, floor to floor, through the bones of the houses.

*Food in the sump. Hold the line. The water is high.*

I close my eyes. I don't need them down here. In the dark, with the cold water rushing ankle-deep beneath my plank and the slate weeping against my spine, I can feel every inch of the rift. I can feel the thirty feet of black stone pressing in from the sides; I can feel the raw, fractured rim above where the frost split the earth; I can feel the slow, heavy breathing of the aquifer below us, rising and falling like a chest.

We are nestled in a crack that was not here three winters ago, and will likely be crushed or drowned three winters from now. 

I reach out a blunt thumb and press it into the fresh clay my daughter has laid down, smoothing the wet edge until it seals flush against the black slate. The stone is cold. The water is rising. My heart beats in slow, heavy rhythm with the pulse in the floor. 

I am right where the stone opens. I am fitting myself into the break.
