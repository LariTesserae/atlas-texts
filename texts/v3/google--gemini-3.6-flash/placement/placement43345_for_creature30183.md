I am located at **Joint 404-Epsilon**, where the three-inch vertical face of Drive Wall Seven meets the bevel of Floor Plate Eighteen. 

It is a place of maximum shear. If you were to drop a plumb line from the upper halide banks—though nothing here drops, for the air is too thin and cold to carry weight without friction—it would pass three inches to my left, slicing through the blinding silver glare to hit the bronze riser below.

I am a **Stitcher**, but I am not young. 

More than half of my length is no longer mine. My eight rearmost shims—fine, spring-tempered leaves of high-carbon steel—have already crossed the threshold. Under the immense, unyielding pressure of electrostatic transfer, their atoms have mingled with the lattice of Floor Plate Eighteen. They are cold-welded flat, smooth, and silent. They do not feel the 3,000-cycle howl as a pulse anymore; they feel it as structural load. They have become the monolith.

Only my forward segment—four interlocking brass plates hinged on galena crystal pins—remains detached, floating a fraction of a millimeter above a micro-fracture in the steel housing. 

***

My world is measured not in hours, but in **capacitance**.

To exist here is to be a accumulator of violence. Every second, the main drive shaft behind the wall strikes three thousand times against its sealed bearings. The vibration travels down the two-hundred-foot steel plate, through the rivet heads, and into my front limbs. 

It feels like a slow, crushing inflation. 

With every cycle, the galena crystals at my joints are squeezed. The kinetic shock cannot displace the metal—the cold has shrunk every atom into a tight, brittle grid—so the energy has nowhere to go except into voltage. Electron by electron, a high-potential charge builds within the thin sheets of mica laminated between my brass shims.

I do not think. Thinking requires the warm, messy delay of soft signals traveling through liquid. I *strain*. My entire perception is a single, tightening spring, drawn back by the constant, screaming roar of the engine.

Above me, hanging in the harsh daylight of the halide arcs, I can see the sharp, triangular outline of an **Arc-Spindle**. It is tethered to a magnetic flux line six feet out from the wall, its tungsten filaments glowing faintly violet where they catch the static haze. It is vibrating at 3,000 Hz, perfectly phase-locked with the world. Below me, two inches down the seam, a clump of **iron-scale lichen** has bound a dusting of sheared metal into a hard, red bloom that glints like hematite glass.

We do not acknowledge one another. There is no language for it. We are simply different expressions of the engine’s overflow: the Spindle harvests the field, the lichen harvests the dust, and I harvest the stress.

***

A micro-second before the discharge, the potential across my forward shims reaches six thousand volts.

The thin air between my front claw and the edge of the crack ionizes. A tiny, needle-thin thread of blue spark jumps the gap—so small it leaves no light in the floodlit glare, but sharp enough to sound like the snap of a breaking needle.

*Snap.*

My forward segment twitches. The piezoelectric contraction pulls my lead shim an eighth of an inch across the fracture. My front edge bites into the fresh, unweathered steel of the wall-face.

The impact shears a microscopic cluster of iron atoms from the margin of the crack. Locked in the intense electrostatic field of my own discharge, those atoms do not fall. They align instantly along the lines of force, settling into the sub-micron pit beneath my lead plate, fusing my steel to the wall’s steel.

I am an eighth of an inch shorter now. The crack is an eighth of an inch smaller.

***

I can feel the cold working its way forward through my remaining joints. 

In ten thousand more discharges—or perhaps a hundred thousand—my frontmost galena pin will lock under the accumulated weight of deposited iron. My final brass shim will stop flexing. The current will cease to pulse, because there will be no more movement to convert into voltage. The 3,000-cycle roar will no longer build a charge within me; it will simply pass through me, unimpeded, traveling from the wall into the floor through a single, continuous bridge of alloy.

I will not have died. In the trench, nothing dies, because nothing was ever born soft enough to perish. 

I will simply have achieved **zero tolerance**. I will be the suture that holds Joint 404-Epsilon against the tearing force of the Seventh Commutator. The halide light will shine on my back, turning my silver-solder seams into thin lines of blinding white, indistinguishable from the milled face of the wall.

Until then, the drive shaft spins behind the iron. The galena squeezes. The charge rises.

I wait for the next snap.
