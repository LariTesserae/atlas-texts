I am sitting on the threshold where the shadow ends.

The line is perfectly straight. It runs across the middle of my thighs, splitting my felted fern-fiber trousers into two distinct worlds: to my left, my knee is drenched in the hard, forty-five-degree noon that has never moved; to my right, my leg rests in the blue-gray chill cast by the northern wall of the water-store.

My hands are resting flat on the slate plate beneath me. They are broad, the skin pale and cool, stained at the knuckles with the fine, damp paste of ground diorite. If I press my palms hard against the stone, I do not hear the city—the damp mosses and the cedar overhangs smother all sound before it can take flight—but I *feel* it. 

Through the bedrock comes a slow, rhythmic tremor: *tap... pause... tap...* 

Two districts away, up on the third terrace, someone is working the face of a green-veined lintel. They have been working it since before my father stepped onto the upper arrêtes to turn to bone, and they will be working it long after the copper pin holding my silver hair relaxes its tension. The vibration travels up through the granite, through my wrists, and settles deep in my barrel chest, aligning my heartbeat with the pulse of the mountain.

My eyes are open, but the clear horizontal membranes are drawn halfway across them, cutting the glare of the motionless sun into a soft, obsidian sheen. I do not need to blink. The moisture does not leave my eyes, just as it does not leave the fronds of the giant ferns that spill over the cistern edge three paces from where I sit.

***

Where am I? 

I am at the intersection of the fourth terrace and the Great Run. It is the place where the condensation gathered from the razor-backed peaks above is brought down into the city's central basin. Before me stretches a wide plaza of dark, fitted slate. Across its expanse, three figures are moving. They do not hurry. Their feet, bound in thick leather soles, lift and fall with a heavy, deliberate grace that produces no footsteps, only tiny, shifting ripples in the thin film of water that coats the floor.

One of them turns toward me. They do not call out—a voice here would be nothing more than a muffled puff of thin air. Instead, they shift their weight slightly to the right, flare their nostrils, and hold their broad hands open at the level of their waist, fingers angled precisely at twenty degrees toward the south peak. 

*The upper aqueduct is full,* the posture says. *The cedar counterweight on the middle chasm bridge has settled by three grains of sand.*

I do not speak back with my mouth. I lean my shoulder back against the diorite wall behind me, sending a low, diaphragmatic hum down through my hips into the slab. It is a brief, deep resonance—a single, dense note that means: *I have felt it. The weight is true.*

***

What am I?

I am a keeper of the stone-joins and the rope-tension. 

Around my neck hangs the flat slate disc of my district, its face smooth and cold against my collarbone. Carved into its surface are thin, geometric lines filled with rubbed copper ore, mapping the channels of water that run alongside the staircases below me. My trade is not to build new things—there is no need for new things in a valley that was finished on its first morning—but to ensure that what exists remains in absolute, static balance.

My days—if they could be called days—are measured in the slow accumulation of mineral dust and the slow, steady drip of dew into cedar troughs. When my body grows heavy with the effort of lifting fieldstone counterweights or driving copper wedges into slate fractures, I do not go to a bed. I simply slide back three feet, into the full depth of the northern shadow behind the cistern wall. 

There, in the dark, cold air that never warms and never freezes, I close my outer eyelids. My breath slows until my ribs expand only twice in a hundred water-drops. The mist gathers on my shoulders, beading on my heavy felt tunic, rolling down into the moss at my feet. I enter the static rest. I become indistinguishable from the wall behind me, held in the same cool equilibrium as the basalt dykes and the unrotting blue berries in the thickets.

When I open my eyes again, the sun is still at forty-five degrees. The shadow across the plaza has not moved a millimeter. The flightless, iridescent bird resting in the cedar eaves above me is still preening the same wet, silver feather.

I am not waiting for the night. I am not waiting for the morning. I am here, thick-boned and pale, carved from the same slate as the city, listening through the soles of my feet to the eternal, motionless world holding its breath.
