I am sitting in the crook where three basalt fingers rise together out of the raw floor, squeezed tightly into a vertical slot that fits my body like a socket. 

I do not have my eyes open, because I do not have eyes to open. Instead, the twin hollows on the front of my wedge-shaped head are flared wide, drinking in the landscape of heat. To me, the dark basin is not dark at all; it is a dense, glowing architecture of temperatures. The deep floor beneath my folded legs holds a steady, deep-red pulse—the blood-warm hum of the inner crust. Running up the pillar beside me is a thin, burning wire of gold: a vein of iron-sulfide ore radiating slightly hotter than the surrounding rock. And across the narrow aisle, nestled into a stacked-stone ring, three of my kin are sleeping. They appear as a single, rounded mass of soft, gentle orange, their heat overlapping, their slow, waterless metabolisms ticking down to a faint, rhythmic ember.

My left jaw is pressed flat against the basalt wall. The stone is my ear, my eye for distance, and my library. 

Through the solid rock, I feel the world. A mile to the west, a tube-dweller is sliding through a debris field of broken feldspar; I can map its length by the heavy, low-frequency ripple of its muscular segments dragging across the quartz dust, a long, cool cylinder pushing through the warm dark. Closer—just twenty paces up on a suspended magma ribbon—the sharp, microscopic *tick-tick-tick* of a crawler's enamel legs vibrates through the spine of the pillar. It is filtering the air. It is moving toward a seam of chalky bloom.

In my hands, I hold the work of this hour. 

My six fingers—tipped with broad, grey pads of dense horn—are curled around a hand-tool. It is a long, heavy scraper of knapped volcanic glass. The edge is so keen it could split a single grain of dust, yet my palms are so thick and dry, coated in their own callous-wax, that the razor-stone leaves no mark upon my hide. 

I am smoothing the shed rib of a tube-dweller, a dense, ivory-like curve of calcified plate that died three generations ago in the lower slots. I scrape. *Shhhk. Shhhk.* The sound does not travel far into the stagnant, sulfur-heavy air, but I feel the vibration of the glass shaving the bone clean through the bones of my wrists. The bone dust falls without a whisper, settling onto the glass flakes between my feet—adding my handful of dust to the ancient, unwashed pile left by those who sat in this exact crook before me.

Beside my knee sits a shallow stone tray filled with silver-gray fibers. They are threads of chrysotile, pulled strand by strand from a soft, fibrous asbestos seam forty paces down the alley. Beside the tray lies a sheet of dried grazer hide, stiff and pale, its belly-wax rubbed away and replaced with a dark, supple grease we compound from deep-crust lipids.

My work is simple. I bore a tiny, perfect eye into the end of the bone needle with a sharp quartz awl. I thread the stone-fiber through. I pierce the edge of the leather. I pull the thread tight. 

*Clack. Pull. Snug.*

I am stitching a mat for the floor of the hive. It will sit beneath the sleepers. It will dull the sharp edges of the basalt step. It will last five thousand years. When the threads finally fray, someone else—bearing the same broad, knobbly knuckles, the same heat-sensitive hollows—will sit in this crook, unpick the mineral thread, gather new chrysotile from the same seam, and stitch the hide again.

As I pull the needle, a vibration strikes my jaw through the pillar. It is not a random tremor. It is rhythmic, tuned, and deliberate. 

Three miles to the south, at the Great Ring where the basalt pillars grow as thick as cavern walls, a elder is speaking. They are striking the floor with the broad, bony heel of their wrist. 

*Thrum... thrum-thrum... thrum.*

I hold my breath—not because I need to stop the movement of air, but to allow my entire skeleton to become a receiver. The seismic pulse travels through five miles of continuous ironstone, climbing up the finger of rock, vibrating through my lower jawbone, into my skull.

*The lower seam is dry,* the ground says. *The grazers have dulled the glass edge at the third turn. Bring new blades when the torpor lifts.*

I do not answer immediately. In a world without weather, there is no hurry. I finish three more stitches. I set the bone needle carefully upon a flat ledge of feldspar—a ledge that has held that needle a thousand times before. 

Then, I lean forward. I raise my right hand, curl my fingers into my palm, and bring the dense, armored knuckle of my wrist down against the raw basalt floor with a sharp, heavy blow.

*Tap.*

The stone rings beneath my hand like a dead bell. 

*Tap-tap. Pause. Tap.*

*I have three blades knapped,* I send into the crust. *The glass is sharp. The hide is bound. The heat is steady.*

A moment later, a faint, comforting echo ripples back through the floor—a brief, silent acknowledgment from the south. Then, the rock returns to its monumental, static quiet.

I pick up the needle again. The air around me is blood-warm, thick, and perfectly still. It does not move my skin; it does not cool my skin. I am a piece of the basin that has learned to shape stone. 

I know every plane of this alley. I know that fifty paces above me, where the slate roof hangs in the absolute dark, there is no sky, no vast empty space, no terrifying fall into nothingness—only the eternal, solid weight of five miles of rock holding the universe together, keeping the warm dark safe, keeping it quiet.

I press the bone needle through the leather. The glass flakes glint faintly in the dim, greenish bioluminescence of the ore vein behind me, but I do not need the light. I feel the edge. I feel the stone. I feel the deep, unmoving earth, and I am entirely, perfectly home.
