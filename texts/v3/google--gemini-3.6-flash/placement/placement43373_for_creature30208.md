My jaw rests against cold-rolled bronze, and the bronze rests against the web of a massive cast-iron I-beam. 

Through my teeth and the dense, rubbery cartilage of my skull, the valley speaks. 

It is not a voice of words, but of frequencies—a rich, wet, iron chord that never ceases. *Thump-shrr-clack.* The lower sluice gate at Terrace Seven has dropped its latch; I feel the sudden, heavy hydraulic back-pressure ripple through the iron, a dull *thrum-m-m* that travels up from the deep bedrock, through thirty feet of vitrified brick, and into the soles of my wide, bare feet. Half a second later, the frequency shifts. A high, dry chatter enters the metal: a strain in the upper gantry bearing. 

*Dry iron.* The worst sound in the world.

I lift my head. The bronze rod remains in my hand, heavy, slick with my own body-grease, its tip notched from years of pressing against vibrating flanges. 

I do not look at the sky—there is no sky, only the low, slate-grey wool of the cloud layer, shedding its endless, heavy drizzle into my face. The water strikes my brow, gathers on my nose, and streams down my cheeks without touching my skin. The paste of crushed fern-sap, soot, and boiler-grease that coats me is thick, warm, and dark; the rain slides off it like mercury over lard. Beneath the coating, my body is warm, held at the steady, feverish temperature of the pipe-vaults where I was birthed.

I shift my weight. I have no kneecaps to click, no rigid longbones to ache in the high-altitude damp. My legs are dense bundles of gristle and fibrous muscle; they yield under me with a slow, hydraulic resilience as I descend the wet slate steps. The stone is hollowed like an old boot-heel beneath my toes, smoothed by centuries of grit-laden runoff. 

Around me, the ferns bow under the weight of the rain. A frond as long as my arm brushes my hip, leaving a trailing smear of cold moisture across my black-greased flank. I ignore it. My attention is entirely tuned to the space ahead of me—not through my eyes, which view the foggy world through thick, amber-tinted membranes, but through the displacement of air and the rhythm of the metal beneath my feet.

Three feet ahead, the six-story flywheel cuts through the open front of the masonry vault. It moves with a silent, terrifying velocity, its dark iron rim throwing off a fine, aerosolized mist of rain and tallow. It carries enough momentum to pulverize a mountain. 

I do not hesitate. I do not wait for it to pass. 

To live here is to realize that the machinery does not have a path; it *is* the path. I know the wheel’s pulse better than the rhythm of my own slow-beating heart. One... two... the counterweight on the far gantry drops, the draft of air pushes outward, cold and smelling of crushed ivy—and I step into the vault, right beneath the sweeping arc of the giant spoke.

The iron passes an inch above my stooped back. The wind of its passage presses my grease-slick skin tight against my ribs, blowing the water off my shoulders in a sudden spray. 

Before the next spoke can sweep down, my long, double-jointed fingers are already at work. 

I reach into the narrow, roaring gap between the journal-bearing and the masonry housing. The air here is blistering, heavy with the sharp, acidic reek of scorching oil. My fingers, thick and hairless, scoop a heavy wad of tallow-crust from the bucket slung at my hip—a cool, fibrous lump of black fungus and fat harvested from the warm sluices this morning. I slap it directly onto the screaming spindle.

The metal eats it instantly. The high, dry chatter dies. The bronze rod in my left hand sings a low, contented *mmmm* as the bearing drinks the fat and settles back into its wet, heavy groove. 

I step backward, out of the vault, just as the second spoke slices down through the fog, whistling through the empty air where my head was a fraction of a second before.

I do not feel relief. There is no adrenaline in cartilage. There is only the steady, deliberate movement of a joint returning to its socket.

I sit on the lip of the wet iron deck, high above the central basin, and let my legs dangle into the fog. Below me, far down in the dark, where the amber electric halos blur into soft orange blotches through the drizzle, I can feel the ancient, colossal pulse of a Siphon-Beast. It is drawing ten thousand gallons through its spongy, iron-caked lung-vaults, filtering the silt so my bearings do not grind to dust.

*Thump.* The beast expands.
*Hiss.* The water rushes through the copper troughs.
*Thump.* The beast contracts.

I press my bronze rod against the wet decking between my feet. 

*Tink. Tink-tink.* 

I tap out the message through the gantry. *Terrace Three, bearing seven, fed with fat. Smooth.*

A mile away, across the cloud-choked abyss of the valley, through thousands of tons of riveted iron and rain-soaked masonry, a distant Tender receives the click through the soles of its feet. It does not answer with a voice. It taps back once—a sharp, heavy *clack* that vibrates through my teeth.

*Understood.*

I lay back against the wet, moss-caked flange of the main girder. The rain drops endlessly from the iron lattice above, pitter-pattering against my thick, greased chest. The fog closes in, turning the world into a tiny, slate-grey circle of wet fern, rusted iron, and warm grease. 

I close my amber eyes beneath their clear shields. I do not need to see. The valley is beating under my back, holding me warm in the wet, dark machinery of its endless, deliberate life. I am wedged tight between the iron and the rain, and I will not shake loose.
