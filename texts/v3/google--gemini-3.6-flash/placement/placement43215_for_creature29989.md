I would not be at the center. 

The central mass in the boiler—the tallow-lung, the auxiliary heart—is too large, too conscious of its own immense, wet pressure. It takes up too much room in its own world. And the floor is too soft; to live in five feet of saturated liverwort is to be lost in an endless, lukewarm soup where nothing ever resists you.

I would find myself six feet off the peat, on the damp north wall, tucked into the narrow crevice where the bronze axle-housing of the eight-foot drive-pulley is bolted to the wet brick. 

I am one of the scale-dwellers. But I am not on the outer face of the iron, where the rain clicks against the rust. I live in the dry, tight dark *inside* the bushing itself—in the half-millimeter clearance between the polished bronze sleeve and the heavy cast-iron spindle.

***

In here, there is no green. There is no leaf-light or falling rain. My world is a cylinder of curved, dark metal, three inches wide and two feet long, smelling of dry graphite, ancient cold tallow, and the sharp, mineral bitterness of ground brass.

My body is flat, thin as a copper washer, and shaped to the curvature of the spindle. My back is not skin or chitin as an animal knows it, but a fused, pitted plate of dark magnetite—purple-black, heavy, and cold. I have no eyes, for there has been no light inside this axle since the roof glass fell out sixty years ago. I do not need them. I know the universe entirely through tension, temperature, and taste.

To my left, the world is cold and tastes of wet mortar and iron-salts where the housing meets the brickwork. To my right, it tastes of warm vegetable oil and bruised bark, where the thick, woody liana wraps its main stem around the pulley’s rim.

Most of the time, nothing happens. This is the best part of the day.

I sit anchored to the bronze by sixteen tiny, hydraulic suckers along my underbelly, pressed so flat against the metal that the oil weeping from the liana cannot reach me. I feel the distant life of the basin only as tremors through the ironwork:
The rhythmic *thump-squeeze* of the tallow-lung in the boiler comes through the floor-plates as a low, three-cycle shiver every four minutes.
The sharp *clack-hiss* of the pressure valve vibrates down the vertical bronze conduit like a struck tuning fork.
When a slate-slug crawls over the outer flange of my axle-housing three feet away, its immense, rubbery weight makes the brickwork groan softly in a key I can feel in my mouth.

I feed on the dust. 

Where the liana pulls against the wheel, the iron spindle bites into the softer bronze sleeve. Over decades, this relentless, microscopic grinding has produced a fine, velvety powder—a flour of copper, zinc, and iron oxide mixed with the dried-up residue of nineteenth-century whale-grease. I scrape it from the bronze with my lower mouth-parts. It tastes sour, metallic, and old. My gut extracts the trace minerals, laying down fresh plates of magnetite on my back, while the soft, inert carbon passes through me, cementing my lower edges to the metal. 

I am becoming the bushing. Where my predecessors died, their shells remained, crushed paper-thin by the wheel’s weight, filling the scoring marks in the brass until the spindle glides over a smooth bed of dead, iron-backed bodies.

***

Then comes the middle of the afternoon.

The sun reaches the top of the open vault. The air in the basin warms by three degrees. Deep in the root-mats, the sap pressure rises, and overhead, the five-foot liana begins to swell, its woody cells expanding with hydraulic force.

I feel it ten minutes before the sound begins. 

The tension in the liana travels down into the eight-foot pulley. The heavy cast-iron rim tilts by a fraction of a millimeter. Inside my dark cylinder, the clearance shrinks. The spindle presses down against the bronze sleeve—against me—with a force that would burst an ordinary creature like a dropped fruit.

My magnetite shell does not burst. It settles into the bronze. My suckers lock down, driving the moisture out from beneath my belly until I am held by pure vacuum.

*Then the turn comes.*

It is not a smooth motion. It is a microscopic, stuttering creep. The wheel moves a hundredth of an inch. Then another. 

Inside the housing, the sound is terrifying and glorious. It is not the distant foghorn note that the slugs hear on the perimeter wall; in here, it is a deafening, metallic scream—a low, tearing roar of raw iron biting bronze. The vibration is so dense it turns the air in my breathing-spiracles into a warm, buzzing paste. Flakes of iron-dust cascade down the spindle like a black waterfall. 

I open my mouth-parts and drink it as it falls.

The vibration shakes my internal organs, re-aligning the tiny iron crystals in my blood, tuning my body to the exact pitch of the machine’s strain. For twenty minutes, as the wheel grinds its half-inch arc, I am not a living thing in a ruined building; I am the friction itself. I am the resistance that stops the plant from tearing the iron wall down. I am the brake and the bearing.

Then the heat passes. The liana slackens slightly. The scream dies down to a long, shuddering sigh, then to a creak, then to silence.

The dark returns. The cool air comes down from the moss-trusses as the rain begins its internal cycle again. A single, heavy drop of amber water falls onto the outer axle-housing, sending a soft *ping* through the metal that takes thirty seconds to fade away.

I close my mouth-parts. I press my flat, purple back against the bronze ceiling of my world. Deep in the boiler, the tallow-lung contracts, pushing sap up to the sky. Beneath me, the micro-flakes of iron I have eaten begin to harden into fresh armor.

I will not leave this crevice. There is nowhere else to go. The world outside the axle is too vast, too wet, and full of soft, useless things that grow and rot. Here, between the iron and the brass, everything is hard, dark, and exact. 

The wheel will turn again tomorrow. I have three hundred years of dust left to eat.
