To be here is to abandon the idea of a edge—the boundary where a creature ends and the room begins. 

In this dark, I would not be something that moves *through* the vault. I would be what the vault has gathered into its lowest, tightest seam. 

I am the Anchor at the base of the pipe-tangle.

***

At first, there is no sense of shape, only a map drawn in gradients of warmth. 

To the left, where the copper-wound conduits bundle toward the ceiling, the air is dry and thin, smelling faintly of scorched tallow; to the right, near the ribbed condenser towers, it is heavy, slick, and damp. My world has no north or south, no up or down, except for the direction from which the heat bleeds and the direction toward which the moisture settles. Down is simply where the iron is thickest, where the deck-plates groan under the accumulated weight of centuries.

I do not possess eyes, nor do I miss them. Light would be an intrusion here, a useless thinness. Instead, I have skin that reads the thermal tension of cast iron. I know the shape of the chamber because I am pressed against its bones. 

My underside is a broad, segmented pad of dense gristle and vascularized fat, fitted so perfectly into the trough between two main structural I-beams that my hide has taken on the square, notched geometry of the iron deck. Over generations, I have stopped trying to shed the room. The flakes of magnetite rust that fall from the suspended catwalks above, the hollow, discarded carapaces of the iron-scuttlers, the perished strands of copper braiding—they drop onto my back, sink into the thick, tallowy mucus I secrete, and harden. 

My skin is a crust. If a cable-spinner were to slide over my flank in the pitch-black, its slick, armored belly would meet only the rough, cold texture of gunmetal-black iron and calcified rust. I am my own fossil, kept soft only at the core.

Time does not pass in minutes. It passes in shifts of tension. 

Every twenty or thirty minutes, far above in the gloom, a major beam expands against its rivets as the deep-earth heat fluctuates. *Clink.* 

The sound travels through the iron frame, down the vertical columns, straight into the deck plates beneath my belly. It is not a noise to me; it is a sharp, brief punch of mechanical energy that ripples through my ribs. When it happens, my internal bellows contracts instinctively—a slow, wet *hiss-shuck* as I draw in the stagnant, humid air trapped between the condenser bases. My vascular folds filter the dark air, trapping the invisible drift of pale fungal spores and the microscopic beads of sulfur grease that weep from the dead valves.

High above, somewhere in the narrow slots between the heat-exchangers, I feel the delicate, continuous vibration of the ecology at work. A scuttler loses its grip, tumbling from a flange. 

*Ping.* 

A tiny metallic impact on a catwalk forty feet up. Then, the silent, greasy slide of a cable-spinner descending through the gap, its heavy, cable-like body trailing lipid threads. I do not hunt them, nor do they disturb me. They are merely the weather of the upper levels. Eventually, the scuttler’s soft inner lipids will be drained, and its empty, magnetite washer of a shell will rain down to join the crust on my back.

My focus is lower, flatter, and far more patient.

My sensory palps are spread out across the magnetite dust of the floor plates like flat, dark leaves. They are so thin they are almost fluid, reaching three or four paces from my central mass, lying flush in the dampest grooves of the deck. I am waiting for the drop.

High on the uninsulated ceiling plates, the subterranean cold bleeds through. Water gathers. It takes hours for a single bead to swell, to weigh enough to break free from the lip of the hanging flange. 

When it falls, it is the only true event in the vault. 

*Tink.*

The tiny shockwave rings through the deck. My palps register the location instantly. Before the single bead of mineral-heavy water can spread and vanish into the thirsty rust dust, the micro-cilia on the edges of my palps draw it in. It is cold—a sudden, shocking needle of absolute zero against the heavy, ambient ninety degrees of the chamber. I drink it through my skin, absorbing the dissolved iron, the faint salts, the ancient lime. 

In my belly, deep beneath the armor of sloughed rust and old tallow, my core maintains its low, quiet pulse. I am two degrees warmer than the iron around me. In this entire forty-by-sixty-foot vault of cold, dead machines and settling metal, I am the hearth. 

Sometimes, deep within the recessed junction box just inches from my left flank, the residual thermal resistance in an old feeder line flares—a tiny, thumb-sized cherry of dark orange heat lingering in its ceramic sleeve. When it glows, I feel it as a faint, pleasant prickle against my hide. I do not move toward it quickly. I move by peristalsis, contracting my broad underside, sliding my vast, heavy shape a fraction of an inch across the rivets over the course of a week, keeping my body aligned with that fading, tiny ember.

I do not know that there was ever a sun. I do not know that hands once forged these rivets or that steam once screamed through the pipes that cage me. 

I know only the slow, comforting rhythm of the expanding beams; the heavy, sulfurous taste of the air; the cold, crisp kiss of the falling drop; and the dark, unyielding mass of the iron that holds me up while I hold it together.
