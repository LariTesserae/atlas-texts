I am not above the world, nor am I down in the deep, black mud where the Tallow-Hides grind the basalt into slurry. 

Find me forty yards up, in the dark knee of a lattice pylon, where three trunk-thick cables emerge from the side of a rusted transformer housing and span the empty gulf toward the pumping station. 

I am wedged into the angle where the structural steel meets the weather-cowl. It is a dry pocket—or as dry as anything ever is here—warmed from beneath by the continuous, feverish waste-heat of the stepping coils inside the iron drum. Over my head, the lip of the steel plate sheds a heavy, roaring fringe of condensate, a tropical waterfall that never stops, filling the air around me with a fine, tepid aerosol that tastes of copper, machine oil, and ozone.

I do not have a name for myself, because there are no names where there are no voices to speak them, but if you reached out through the black mist, your hand would find me by my heat first.

***

My body is long, limber, and folded back on itself like a rusted tape-measure. 

My spine curves to fit the contour of the primary feeder cable. I am draped over it. My chest—broad, thin-ribbed, and hairless—is pressed flat against the vulcanized sheath. Through the skin of my sternum, through the layer of dense, rubbery fat beneath it, I feel the full, terrifying surge of the current inside. It is a 60-cycle vibration, so heavy and dense that it sets my teeth ringing in my jaw. It is my pulse. It has been my pulse for as long as the rain has fallen. When my heart beats, it beats inside the cadence of that hot, sixty-fold thrum.

My hands—six of them, long-jointed and pale as boiled bone—are hooked into the structural lattice. The skin on the inside of my palms and knuckles is thick, black, and deadened, like the soles of old boots or the heavy grommets used to line iron bulkheads. It does not feel the electric bite. It feels only texture: the wet, flaky crust of iron oxide; the slimy velvet of black liverworts trying to creep up the metal; the smooth, oily enamel of the primary line.

I am a Threader, but I am *this* Threader, bound to this single span of wire between the pylon and the pump.

***

I move without opening eyes I do not possess. My head is small, sleek, and featureless save for the wide, soft acoustic folds that rim my jaw, drinking in the universe of noise. 

I know the world by its weight and its timbre. 

Three leagues to the west, the giant piston of Station Nine goes down—*CHUNG*—and the shockwave travels up through the basalt terrace, up through the pylon’s footings, through the riveted leg, and into my left lower elbow. A second later, the air-borne sound arrives—*ha...*—a hot, wet gasp of displaced steam that blows across my face like a furnace door opening. 

Beneath me, twenty feet down in the dark canopy of whip-ferns, something heavy is moving through the underbrush. I hear the wet *schlock-schlock* of three-toed hooves sinking into the moss, the deep, rumbling fermentation of a Tallow-Hide’s stomach, and the faint, high-frequency *click-click-click* of its snout-tentacles sweeping the iron legs for current leaks.

And above me, hovering in the static plume of the transformer, are the Arc-Moths. I can feel their tiny, electrostatic signatures—hundreds of little sparks drifting through the dark like hot needles across my skin.

***

My work is simple. It is the only thing that exists between the rain and the wire.

The black moss is ambitious. It feeds on the iron dust and the condensation, creeping silently up the pylon, sending out fibrous, wet tendrils that seek to bridge the gap between the live copper lugs and the grounded frame. If it touches both, the air shatters. There is a deafening, blinding *CRACK*—a momentary flash of heat that singes my hide and smells of cooked tin—and then the wire goes cold for a dozen heartbeats before the automatic breakers down in the mantle reset with a distant, booming clank.

I hate the cold wire. When the wire goes cold, I am alone in the dark. The purr in my chest stops. My ribs feel hollow, empty, like a house with the doors blown off.

So I work. 

I extend my upper left arm, my long, three-jointed fingers feeling along the porcelain insulator bell. The ceramic is slick with grease. I find a mat of liverwort creeping over the lip, heavy with trapped rainwater. Patiently, precisely, I insert my thick, calloused thumbnail beneath the root-system and scrape. 

*Pill... peel... tear.*

The wet vegetation comes away in a heavy, dripping clump. I let it drop. I listen to it fall—*swish-swish-swish*—through the dark canopy until it hits the mud below with a soft *plop*. 

I clean the second insulator. I clean the copper lug, stripping away the oxidized crust with my teeth, tasting the sharp, sour tang of sulphate on my tongue before spitting it into the rain.

When the metal is bare, the current flows clean again. The wire warms beneath my thighs. The hum settles back into a deep, velvety purr that vibrates through my jawbone and fills my brain with a static-colored light that has nothing to do with eyes.

***

I am content here, suspended between the iron above and the mud below. 

I do not know who built the pylon. I do not know where the wire goes after it leaves my span, or what the water is for when the forty-story pistons force it into the high-pressure pipes. I do not know what light was, or why the dark is supposed to be frightening.

To be me is to be a single, living knot in a circuit that never ends. 

I press my throat against the hot insulation. I open my mouth just a fraction, letting the warm rain trickle over my tongue, and from deep in my chest, I let out my own small, continuous sound—a low, rhythmic purr, tuned precisely to sixty cycles a second, answering the wire, answering the rain, answering the dark.
