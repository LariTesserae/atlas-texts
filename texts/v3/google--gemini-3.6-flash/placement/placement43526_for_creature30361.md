My toes—broad, long, and tipped with thick, dark horn—are curled over the sharp lip of a basalt ledge, three levels up from the weeping gutter. I do not slip. The oil on my skin keeps the sulfur water from clinging, but the horn on the soles of my feet bites into the jagged grain of the rock.

I would be a wall-setter, down where the old city meets the fresh crust. 

I am squatting low, my knees bowed outward, my center of gravity resting inches from the stone. The air is hot fleece against my shoulders. Water droplets bead along the rubbery grey slope of my back and slide off without sinking in, gathering at the small of my spine before dropping onto the Slate-and-Mortar stack below.

Before me is an open joint in a tenement facade—a place where the subterranean heat has shifted the foundations slightly, tearing a fingers-wide seam through three tiers of vitrified mud-brick. The seam must be choked before the pressurized steam from the lower fissure warps the bay-beast rib supporting the floor above.

In my left hand, I hold a heavy, shallow dish carved from slate, heaped with warm, grey paste: crushed mollusk shell, iron silt, and the fine white powder of ground thigh-bones, mixed with render-tallow. In my right, I hold a broad, flat spade knapped from dense river-flint.

*Click-slide.* 

My translucent membrane flicks across my eyes, clearing a speck of wet iron-dust. I do not look out across the basin; there is nothing to see out there except the same low, grey, steaming roof of mist that has always been above us. Instead, I look at the seam. I look at the grain of the basalt where it meets the mud-brick. I look at what my knuckles can reach.

Above me, four stories up, a crew of stone-splitters is hammering. *Thump-crack. Thump-crack.* The air is so full of the noise that it feels thick enough to chew, but I don't listen to the air. I listen through my feet. 

The vibration travels up through the obsidian ledge, through the keratin pads of my soles, into my thick calf-bones, and settles in my pelvis. I can feel the rhythm of their mallets: three heavy strikes, a pause to clear the dust, three heavy strikes. If the vibration changes—if the stone beneath my toes hums with a sharp, high-pitched ringing instead of a low, dull thud—it means the ledge I am standing on is stress-fracturing. I will know two beats before it breaks.

A figure climbs up the narrow stairwell carved into the tenement face. It is Sorn—or the one whose chest-rumble means *Sorn* to me. She is broad-chested, her apron stiffened with dried tallow and blackened with sulfur soot, carrying a basket of split slate tiles on her left shoulder. 

She stops on the step beneath my ledge. She does not shout. She does not try to cut through the shrieking of the sky-gliders roosting in the eaves above us, nor the relentless *clatter-thump* of the bone-shuttles weaving hide-thongs in the cellar below. 

Instead, she steps close. She leans her head forward and places the broad, flat bone of her forehead against mine.

The contact is warm, slick with sebum, and absolute. 

She vibrates her throat—a deep, muscular contraction in her chest that sends a low wave traveling straight through our fused skulls. The vibration rattles my teeth and hums in my ear-canals: *The lower pan is full. The mud-herders are boiling tallow. They need three slate lintels by the next steam-rise.*

I keep my forehead pressed to hers for a beat, letting the heat of her skull warm mine, then I rumble back through my sternum: *Leave the tiles. Take the bone-paste down.*

She pulls away. Her deep-set, pale-yellow eyes blink once—*click-slide*—and she nods. She drops the basket of slate at my feet, takes a lump of the wet mortar in her bare, wide palm, and swings her thick legs down the wet stone steps, her horn-soled feet slapping the basalt with the dull, heavy sound of wood striking wood.

I return to the seam.

I thrust the flint spade into the grey paste, scoop a heavy lump, and jam it deep into the wall's crack. I pack it tight using the heel of my palm, pressing until the iron silt squeezes back out around my bulbous knuckles. The paste is hot—it bites at the thick leather of my skin—but it will set fast in this steam. By the time I have slept and risen again, the bone-ash and shell-lime will have locked the mud-bricks to the basalt as if they grew there together.

My mallet sits on the ledge beside me. The head is a chunk of dark, unpolished river-granite, bound with cured hide-thongs to a handle carved from a long, dense bone. 

The bone is yellowed, smoothed by the grease of my hands, polished until the grain of the marrow-cavity looks like dark water frozen in stone. It was my father’s left radius. I know the slight curve of it near the grip; it matches the curve of my own forearm. When I drive a stone wedge into slate, it is his bone that takes the shock, passing the shudder through my palm, into my wrist, into my shoulder. 

He is in the wall behind me, too. His skull was crushed into the mortar of the cellar foundations twenty steam-rises ago, when the lower floor was raised above the thermal seepage. His hides cover the doorway to the sleeping alcove where I curl up when my muscles turn to water. 

There is no part of him that is gone. He is simply part of the weight that keeps the steam out.

The sky above us gives off its endless, greasy grey glow. The gliders shriek in the high carved eaves, their metallic clicking bouncing off the wet stone like hail. Down in the mud pans, a leviathan bellows—a sound so massive it makes the tallow-lamp in the window niche tremble, its small flame sputtering against the sulfur-damp.

I do not think about what is beyond the ridge at the edge of the basin. There is no "beyond." If you walked away from the city, the basalt would still be basalt, the steam would still be steam, and eventually your skin would dry, crack, and bleed where the oil failed. Life is here, where the water is warm, the rock is hard, and the hive holds itself together against the sound.

I take my thumb-ring—a thick band carved from the knuckle of a mud-beast—and rap it hard against the basalt wall three times. *Clack. Clack. Clack.* 

The sound travels down through the three-story stack, through the cellar, into the bedrock. Far below, a apprentice working the mortar-trough will feel the three sharp clicks in the soles of her feet, know that the upper seam is filled, and send up the next basket of slate.

I wipe my oily brow with the back of my scarred forearm, scoop another spade of wet bone-paste, and press my weight into the stone.
