I am wedged into the heart of a split.

It is a shear-fracture—three miles above the lower darkness, right at the knee where a mountain-wide bough curves up toward the indigo sky. Sixty years ago, when this limb snapped under its own icing weight, it pulled open a furrow twenty feet wide and a hundred feet deep. The inner wood here is not grey bark; it is the color of old copper, wet and dense, smelling of raw terpene and ancient cold.

My back is braced against the leeward wall of the tear. My knees are pulled up to my chest. The Amber-grease smeared across my cured Gale-Hound suit has gone stiff in the mist-front, gluing me to the fiber so securely that if I died right now, my shell would remain anchored here for a century, frozen into the grain like a knot.

I am not in an alcove. The alcoves are for the women, the young, the ones who stitch the hide-domes and brew the dark resin-chip fires. They stay in the sheltered scars where the wind loses its teeth. 

I am out on the open span. I am a **Grain-Tapper**, a Listener of the Shear. 

***

My left ear is pressed directly against the naked copper-rings of the iron-bark. I do not use an ear-horn; the horn muffles the high-frequency *ping* that comes just before the wood gives up its life. 

Through my skull, I can hear the whole sky-lattice breathing.

It is a terrifying, magnificent noise. It doesn't sound like a tree. It sounds like an iron bridge being twisted by an invisible hand. Miles out into the indigo, a cross-arch is contracting as the sub-zero mist hits it. I feel the shockwave travel through forty miles of solid fiber, striking the soles of my feet like a blunt axe head: *THUMP. THUMP. THUMP.* 

Then, the long, thin singing—the grain under my thigh stretching, the individual cellulose fibers slipping against one another with a sound like dry silk being torn in a small room.

*Not yet,* the wood says to my bones. *Not this hour.*

***

In my right hand, I hold my wedge-mallet, carved from the dense jawbone of an old Drift-Pelt. In my left, a six-foot rod of dry iron-wood, its tip wedged into a hairline crack that runs down toward the abyss.

If the crack expands, the rod drops three inches. If it drops, I hit the bough.

I tap the rhythm out. *Three quick, two long.* 

*TINK-TINK-TINK—CLANG—CLANG.*

The bone mallet strikes the iron-wood, and the sound does not travel through the thin air; it dives straight into the trunk. Down through the two-mile arch, across the swaying vine-bridge at the junction, and into the lee-alcove where sixty of my people sit in the dark around a smoldering bone-stove. 

My rhythm tells them: *The arch holds. The frost is heavy, but the arch holds. Do not cut the ropes.*

A mile away, across a gulf of black air so vast it makes the throat go dry, a faint *clink-clink* comes back through the solid structure. It is Old Vael, wedged into the northern groin of the arch. He is eighty years old; his hips have fused from the cold, and he hasn't walked a vine-bridge in two decades. He lives in his split, fed on frozen blood-cakes hauled up to him by his grand-daughters. He and I are the ears of the clan. We are the ones who tell the living when the sky is about to break.

***

A white shadow cuts through the purple lichen-glow above my head.

It is a Gale-Hound, late to its roost. Its black membrane is tight as a drum-skin, whistling through the black air with a dry, hissing sound—*shhhAAAAAAAA*—that grows louder until it slams into the bark fifty feet below me.

*THUD.*

The impact shakes my teeth in my jaw. The hound doesn't slip; its front suction-pads hit the ice-glaze with a wet *pop*, and its chest-spurs dig deep into the copper rings, spraying shards of frozen amber into the dark. It hangs there for a second, sixteen feet of oily white fur and panting lungs, its wide, black nostrils steaming in the sub-zero air. 

It turns its eyeless snout toward me. It knows I am here. It smells the Shatter-Claw grease on my suit. But it is tired; its membrane is heavy with rime. It crawls slowly downward, its claws clicking against the iron-bark, seeking the deep furrows where the six-legged crawlers sleep.

I let out my breath. The air freezes instantly against the collar of my hood, forming a small ring of white needle-ice around my mouth.

***

Now the mist comes.

It rolls out of the upper void like a wall of grey iron. In thirty seconds, the distant arches vanish. The purple glow of the lichen dims to a dull, bruised smudge. The rime hits my suit, clicking against the Gale-Hound leather like a shower of tiny teeth. 

My world shrinks to three feet: the copper-ringed grain before my face, the bone mallet in my hand, and the void behind my heels.

This is where I belong. Not on the ground—I have never seen ground, do not believe in ground, suspect the stories of flat dirt are a lie told by old men to make the young soft. I belong to the **Tension**. I am a piece of bone wedged into a piece of wood, hanging over two thousand meters of howling nothing.

And then—deep down—it happens.

It is not a sound. It is too big for sound.

Far below my open-toed boots, far down in the dark where the bruised lichens die and the black air turns heavy and thick, something shifts. 

The **Frost-Crawl** is moving its weight.

The trunk beneath me groans—a low, structural moan that starts in my pelvis and climbs up my spine until my skull vibrates against the wood. The entire twenty-mile arch bows upward, lifting me three feet into the icy air before settling back down with a terrifying, thunderous *CRACK* that reverberates across the abyss for twenty miles.

A warm wind rises from the dark. 

It washes over my face, cutting through the freezing mist. It smells of crushed, hot resin, bruised copper, and ancient, unwashed fur. The breath of the monster at the bottom of the world. It takes twenty seconds for the draft to pass, carrying away the rime, leaving the bark wet and black.

I wait. I keep my cheek pressed to the wood. I listen as the thousands of tiny cellulose fibers groan, fight, stretch... and hold.

The arch does not die tonight.

I raise my bone mallet. I swing my arm. I strike the iron-wood, sending the news down through the dark, through the grain, through the marrow of the world:

*We are still here. We are still gripping.*
