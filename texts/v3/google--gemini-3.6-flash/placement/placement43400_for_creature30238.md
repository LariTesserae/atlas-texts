I would not be high in the sky, where the sail-kites hang like iridescent oil on the thin blue air. Nor would I be down in the lower plains, where the haze hides whatever loud, wet, heavy things still struggle in the thick atmosphere.

I would be right here, anchored to the pale marl at the lip of a third-tier terrace, where the heat doesn't fall from above so much as it rises through the soles of my feet.

I am one of the Thread-Walkers, but I am in the long, slow middle of my life—the century where the boundary between my own body and the architecture of the basin has begun to soften, though my joints have not yet calcified into stone.

If you were to look for me, you might mistake me at first for a pale, wind-smoothed spur of chalk. I am sitting cross-legged beside a cluster of three glass needles that rise like clear, frozen sighs from the limestone. My back is straight, my long, honeycombed bones holding no unnecessary weight. I wear a discarded sail-kite membrane over my shoulders; it clings to my dry, pearlescent skin by static alone, shifting with a faint, papery *whisper* only when the thermal fields pulse beneath us.

My fingers—long, thin, and tipped with pale, copper-free flesh—rest lightly against the warm stem of the central needle. I am not repairing it. It needs no repair; it has stood frictionless for ten thousand years. I am simply *listening*. 

Through the clear glass, I can feel the deep, subterranean harmonics of the mountain’s thermal conduits. They hum at a frequency below sound, a steady, rhythmic pulse that tells me the heat beneath the basin is stable. On my left, a few inches down the chalk slope, the needle's invisible pressure field is working. The thin, dry air shimmers, and a single, fat bead of water—perfectly spherical, trembling with surface tension—slides down the glass without leaving a trace of wetness behind. It drops with a soft, muffled *pock* into the shallow stone bowl at my side.

Three paces away, a Pale-Hide grazes. 

I do not turn my head to look at it; to turn quickly is to waste heat, to displace air unnecessarily. But I see it through my broad, amber-tinted eye-films. Its white back reflects the glaring midday sun so brightly it seems to glow, while its dark underside drinks in the warmth rising from the marl. It presses its muscular, cilia-lined trunk into the swollen amber jelly of the resin-lichen, drawing up dew and sugars. 

I can feel its heartbeat. Not through the air—the silence here swallows heartbeats instantly—but through the elastic bedrock beneath us. The modified stone under our seat acts as a conductor for micro-vibrations. I feel the rhythmic *click-whirr* of its nasal valves as it exchanges a precise breath of air once every two minutes. I feel, too, the ghost-light impression of a Silence-Stalker flattened against the rounded ridge fifty meters up-slope. It has been there since dawn, moving perhaps three inches an hour, its pale fur perfectly blent with the chalk. 

There is no tension in this. The grazer knows the stalker is there; the stalker knows the grazer feels its weight. It is not a drama of terror and flight, but a slow, calculated calculus of calories and shadow. When the strike comes, hours from now, it will be as quiet as a falling leaf.

A movement catches the edge of my field of view. Another Thread-Walker is coming down from the upper terraces.

They move with that floating, long-vaulting stride unique to our kind, letting the altered, spring-like bedrock absorb and return the kinetic energy of every step. Their drapery trails behind them like a wispy cirrus cloud. They do not hurry. Hurry is an error of the lowlands.

When they reach my needle, they stop. They do not speak. To shape air into vocal sound is a luxury for wet, wasteful lungs. 

Instead, they kneel beside me on the warm chalk. They extend their right hand. I extend mine. 

Our fingers meet—index and middle fingertips touching pad-to-pad. 

The exchange is instantaneous, carried through the micro-electrical pulses of our dry skin and the subtle variations in our surface temperature:

*In their touch, I feel the northern rim.* I feel that two needles on the high scarp have accumulated a fine glaze of silica dust from a dust-devil three days ago, slightly dulling their yield. I feel that a high-altitude Sail-Kite has dropped a cluster of webbed egg-capsules into a deep water bowl near the mountain’s crest, and that the larvae are already swimming in the mint-scented gel. 

*In my touch, I give them the state of this terrace.* I pass along the steady, deep pitch of the subterranean conduit beneath my heels, the precise fluid level of the local condensation basins, and the quiet patience of the Silence-Stalker up on the ridge.

We hold the contact for three seconds—a full life-history of the afternoon compressed into a single, silent heat-map. Then, our fingers part.

The other Walker rises, gives a single, slow inclination of their head, and continues down the gentle steps of the basin, heading toward the central flats where the herds gather at dusk.

I remain. 

I reach down and press my palm flat against a dark, shadowless structural frame set flush into the marl near my knee. The material is colder than the stone, impossibly smooth, unaffected by the centuries of wind-blown chalk dust that have rounded every other surface in the valley. It is an anchor from the ones who engineered this sanctuary—those who looked at a dying, chaotic world and decided to build a place that could run on sun, glass, and silence forever.

I close my eye-films against the brilliant, dry glare. I draw a single, slow breath, holding the cool, thin air deep in my chest for a long minute, letting my body harvest every trace of oxygen before exhaling a faint, moistureless sigh back into the afternoon.

I am not waiting for the day to end. Here, high above the world, the afternoon does not end; it merely settles deeper into the stone. I am simply part of the quiet engine, keeping watch over the dew, feeling the world think itself into peace on a shelf of white chalk.
