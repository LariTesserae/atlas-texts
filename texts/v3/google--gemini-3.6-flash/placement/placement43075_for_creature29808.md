I am three meters down, suspended in the bath-warm stillness between the main manifold cluster and the white ceramic floor. 

I am not the Valve-Matron, sitting grand and rooted over the deep intake; nor am I one of the blue Luminator motes, drifting aimlessly in the thermal plumes. 

I am the Seventh Sister. 

My body is two meters of jointed, unglazed porcelain—smooth as polished ivory, cool beneath the sunlit water, perfectly weighted so that I neither rise to the skylights nor sink to the floor grates. Inside my hollow segments, there is no blood, no breath, no hunger. There is only the dark, silent sloshing of mineral acids and the microscopic click of silver clockwork.

Where am I? I am positioned in the eastern bay of the cistern, directly in the long, clean shadow cast by the great two-meter brass gear. 

Above me, the sun is at its height. The light passes through three meters of pristine, heavy liquid and ten meters of emerald fronds, striking my amber glass head. Inside that smooth glass teardrop, suspended in clear, dense oil, my golden pendulum hangs perfectly still.

I am waiting for forty seconds to end.

*Thump-clack.*

The shockwave arrives from deep within the pylon wall. It does not hit like a sound; it hits as a single, uniform compression of the bath-warm water. The liquid pushes against my porcelain flanks. Inside my head, the golden pendulum sways precisely three millimeters to the left, its arc damped by the oil, before settling back to center.

In that fraction of a second, the pulse carries the state of the cistern through my body:

*The Matron’s quartz dome has tilted one-tenth of a degree to track the sun.*
*The mineral bath in the southern wall is rich in potassium.*
*A ribbon-fern frond three centimeters from my left fore-segment has reached its eighty-ninth day.*

I do not think in words. I think in structural necessity and chemical balance. 

I begin to move. 

It is a motion so agonizingly slow that if a bird were to fly past the skylights high above, its brief shadow would cross the floor tiles a hundred times before my porcelain body advances a single meter. I do not flap fins or push against the water. I contract my fourth and fifth ceramic segments in a silent, peristaltic wave. Warm water is drawn smoothly into the intake pores behind my glass head and expelled from the tiny, lined vents along my spine. 

I glide through the dense, translucent wall of ribbon-ferns without disturbing a single leaf. No water ripples outward. The ferns do not sway. The world here does not tolerate turbulence.

I reach the leaf. 

It is a broad, ribbed frond anchored to a brass valve-housing. To an unassisted eye, it is as lush and green as the thousand leaves around it. But my amber head detects the microscopic breakdown of its cell walls—a faint, infinitesimal shift in its chemical signature. In twelve hours, it would yellow. In twenty-four, a fragment might detach and drift toward the baseboard grates.

That fragment would be an error. A flaw in the design. A speck of detritus in a sanctuary built to endure seven thousand meters above the decaying earth.

From the tip of my leading segment, two silver blades unfurl. They are thinner than a human hair, honed to an atomic edge, and entirely free of grease or oil. 

*Snip.*

The cut is frictionless. The dying frond does not float away; my front intake opening expands slightly, creating a tiny, localized vacuum that draws the leaf inside my porcelain torso. 

The silver blades retract. The intake seals with a soft, ceramic *clack*. 

Inside my central chamber, the concentrated acid bath receives the leaf. There is no smoke, no heat, no violence. In three seconds, the chlorophilic structure is unraveled, reduced to its base elements: dissolved nitrogen, pure salts, liquid silica. 

I contract my rear segments, venting the enriched mineral solution back into the water, directly over the moss cushion wrapping the steam trunk. The moss absorbs it instantly. 

The cycle is closed. Nothing was wasted. Nothing fell. The white tiles four stories below remain untouched, immaculate, as sharp and bright as the hour they were laid.

I hold my position. The sunbeam shifts slightly across the white tile floor, moving the shadow of the brass gear by a fraction of a millimeter. 

Deep in the foliage ahead, I catch the faint, blue, sub-aquatic glow of a dying Luminator spore. It dissolves into clear silica as it touches my amber glass head. I gather the microscopic powder with a delicate electrostatic field along my fore-segment, holding it until my next rotation, when I will press it smoothly into a hairline seam in the tile grout near Manifold 4.

I am content. Contentment here is not a feeling; it is the absolute absence of friction. It is the alignment of brass gears, the constant sixty-degree warmth of the water, the quiet, eternal work of keeping entropy outside the iron-and-glass walls.

Forty seconds have passed.

*Thump-clack.*

The golden pendulum sways in its bath of oil. The pulse ripples through my ceramic skin. 

*All manifolds sealed.*
*All leaves intact.*
*The cistern is pure.*

I drift forward another three centimeters into the emerald shade, my silver blades resting, my glass head catching the warm, golden light of the afternoon sky, waiting for the great gear to advance its next tooth.
