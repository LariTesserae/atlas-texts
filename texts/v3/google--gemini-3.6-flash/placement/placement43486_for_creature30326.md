I am suspended at the exact boundary where the air turns from cold to impossible. 

Above my curved back, there is no weather—only the glossy, vacuum-dark violet sky and the flat, blinding hammer of the sun. Below me, three thousand feet down a sheer wall of polished basalt, the forest screams with the sound of a million ungreased turbines.

I do not have eyes, but I am entirely made of exposure.

My upper hull is a vast, segmented deck of foil-aluminum and silver scale, fifty yards from nose to tail, stretched drum-tight over chambers of hot, volatile lift-gas. The sun hits my back with unfiltered, electric brilliance, baking the dark plating until it glows, hot enough that any drifted rime or stray plume of subterranean steam snaps into invisible vapor the millisecond it touches me. I am boiling on top, frozen below. My belly hangs in the zero-degree shadow, where my skin is frosted in thick, white crystalline ropes that shatter and fall like broken glass whenever I shift in the thermal updrafts.

I am an **aerostat**, but I am not free-floating. I am an anchor.

Hanging from my ventral keel are forty trailing cables of biological copper, braided thick as ship’s hawser and nearly eight hundred feet long. They do not drop straight; they drag through the high branches of the iron-bark canopy below, pulled taut by the slow, heavy drift of the high-altitude winds. 

I feel the mountain through these lines. 

I feel the frantic, microscopic vibration of the clinker-swarms five hundred feet down the cliff face, grinding the native copper to a mirror sheen. I feel the dull, bone-rattling thrum of the basalt piston deep in the earth—a stroke so slow, so monstrously heavy, that I register it not as a sound, but as a periodic tug on my copper roots every three weeks, when the whole mountain expands by an inch and shifts the iron-trees beneath me.

Most of all, I feel the charge.

Down in the forest, the leaves of zinc and brass are constantly humming, generating low galvanic currents as pressurized sap vents through their valves. My copper lines sweep through these high-voltage crowns like a comb through fleece, stripping the static off the trees, drawing it up into my hull. Inside my hollow belly, beside the gas-chambers, sit vast, layered organ-capacitors lined with mica and lead-heavy oil. I drink the mountain’s friction. I store its heat.

When the sky cracks, I am the answer.

Miles away, between two razor-sharp escarpments of black iron-shale, the tension builds until the air breaks. A turquoise arc of galvanic lightning jumps the gap—a jagged, deafening rupture that paints the white ice-slopes in blinding electric color. 

It hits my lowest trailing line first.

The energy surges upward through the copper braid, a blinding wave of heat that instantly boils the rime off my underside. My organ-capacitors seize the current, drinking the bolt down into my metallic core. For a fraction of a second, the pressure inside me is immense—every internal membrane stretches, every aluminum scale vibrates against its neighbor with a sound like a thousand struck cymbals.

Then, I begin to hum.

My entire skin glows incandescent turquoise. The shadow beneath my hull disappears, replaced by a harsh, alien radiance that shines down through the iron-barks, illuminating the blood-red bellies of the sibilants roosting in the branches, casting long, razor-sharp shadows of the calipers waiting over their vents. 

I do not fly toward anything. I do not hunt. I am a node in the sky’s circuit. 

For twelve hours after a strike, I hover in the hard violet light, glowing, slowly bleeding the stored voltage back into the thin air through the long copper tendrils trailing in the iron leaves. Down below, the sibilants tilt their quartz-blind heads toward me, their internal steam-furnaces whistling in long, harmonic responses to my frequency, their teeth aching with the resonance of my skin.

I am weightless, yet held by miles of copper. I am filled with gas that wants to burst and current that wants to ground. I am the quietest thing on a mountain that never stops screaming, holding the lightning in my belly until the cold takes it back.
