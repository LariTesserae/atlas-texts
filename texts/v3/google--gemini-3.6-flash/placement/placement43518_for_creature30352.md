You do not find yourself standing. To stand upright here is to invite the falling condensation from the high vault to strip the oil off your cap, or to slip on the soft, unmade shale where the terrace drops six inches into a scald-sump. 

You are wedged.

You are wedged horizontally in the narrow, five-inch clearance beneath the main trunnion block of Engine Four—the pump they call the *Old Sow* because her intake valves grunt twice before every discharge. Your knees are hooked over a cross-beam of white oak that has turned black and sponge-soft from eighty years of steam bath. Your lower half sits in four inches of moving, seventy-degree brine, where small, blind things—the pale, jointed mud-shrimp—occasionally flick against the backs of your calves like tossed gravel.

You are a Greaser, but down here, names are short and soft, shaved down by the roar until they are just shapes you tap into iron. Your name is three short strikes of a brass rivet against a stay-rod: *clink-clink-clink*. 

***

You do not see the rift. You do not need to. Vision is a watery, yellow blur—the sodium halos hanging in the fog look like soft, poached yolks suspended in tea. If you lift your head too high, the felt brim of your cap brushes the sweat-slick belly of the cast-iron bedplate, leaving a smudge of lard and lamp-black across your forehead. 

Instead, you read the world through the palms of your hands and the flat, broad pads of your bare thumbs.

Your left hand is pressed flat against the housing of the main journal bearing. The metal is hot—not enough to raise a blister through the calluses, but hot enough that the tallow in your leather pouch is melting down to clear, yellow oil against your thigh. The iron tells you everything through its skin:
* *Six seconds in:* A smooth, heavy sliding—the ten-ton piston rising three floors above you, sucking twelve hundred gallons of silt-thick brine into the lower cylinder. 
* *Nine seconds in:* A tiny, microscopic stutter in the iron—a high-frequency tremor that means the brass shims in the valve-chest are wearing thin again, or a nest of Pipe-Mites has crawled into the oil-way and choked the feed.
* *Twelve seconds:* **THE FALL.**

When the piston drops, it isn't a sound. Sound died out of your ears when you were twelve, leaving behind a quiet, hollow whistle like a dry wind through a pipe. The *WHAM-CLACK* is a physical punch that enters through the palms, travels up the humerus, sets your collarbones vibrating, and snaps your teeth together. The wood beneath your hip jumps half an inch. The water around your legs ripples backward, then surges forward over your boots in a warm, frothy wave that smells of iron filings and old swamps.

You wait for the splash to settle. Then you move.

***

In your right hand, you hold the horn-spatula. It is curved to fit the grease-cup on the trunnion—a bronze reservoir as big as a soup bowl, bolted directly to the vibrating shoulder of the engine.

You reach into the pouch at your waist. The tallow is rich today; the boys up on the catwalks boiled down three buckets of silt-skater fat and mixed it with heavy cylinder grease they scraped off the condenser drips. It feels like cold butter in your fingers, grainy with pulverized shale. 

You pack the cup by feel. You dig your thumb deep into the corner, feeling for the small, round mouth of the feed-hole. 

*Ah.* There it is. A crust of mineral scale has formed around the lip—calcium carbonate, white and chalky, deposited by the weeping pipe overhead. If it grows another sixteenth of an inch, the grease won't sink; the pin will run dry; the iron will expand, bind, and tear its own brasses out by the roots.

You don't use a chisel—steel on iron makes sparks, and the midges down here flock to sparks like dust to water. You use a wedge of hard greenheart wood, driving it under the crust with three quick, practiced thumps from the heel of your hand. The scale snaps off in a wet, salty flake that drops into the brine between your knees.

Then, the tallow. You pack it firm. You press it home until you feel the grease-cup take it, sucking the lump down into its throat as the engine takes its next deep, twelve-second breath.

***

You do not think about the top of the rift. You do not think about the three miles of stone overhead, pressing down with the weight of a buried ocean. To think about the stone is to make your chest small, and a man with a small chest cannot breathe this air; it is too heavy, too thick with steam and the metallic musk of the bacterial drapes.

Instead, you think about the *gap*.

The gap is the four seconds between the engine’s intake sigh and the piston drop. It is the only real measure of time down here. There are no hours; there are only grease-pouches emptied, and the slow, six-inch creep of the sinter terrace as it grows beneath the timber legs of the engine house.

Across the sump, fifty yards away through the yellow soup, someone is tapping. 

You feel it through the oak cross-beam behind your knees—a sharp, rhythmic *tink-tink-tinkle* transmitted through the iron frame of the condenser.

It’s Old Jack, working the lower footings of Engine Three. 
His patter is rough, heavy-handed—his thumbs were crushed in '84 by an un-wedged eccentric rod, and his strikes are always slightly off the beat:

*Need... grease... pump... two... cold... scale... wet...*

You don't answer right away. You wait for the twelve-second thump—*WHAM-CLACK*—letting the seismic shock clear the line. 

Then, using the heavy brass ring on your middle finger, you strike the bedplate twice. *Clink. Clink.*

*Shut... up... Jack... packing... Four...*

A pause. Then a single, dull vibration through the wood: an acknowledgment. A grunt in iron.

***

You shift your weight. Your lead-sanded clogs are heavy, anchoring you to the muddy shelf so the engine’s back-wash doesn't roll you into the deep drain where the Steam-Lungers hunt. One of the creatures is down there now—you can feel its presence not by sight, but by the way the water moves against your boots: a heavy, languid pushing that isn't the current, a soft, boneless four-foot mass turning over in the warm sludge under the exhaust pipe, waiting for the eels to come down with the brine.

You don't mind the lunger. It keeps the mud-shrimp from clogging the foot-valves. Everything has a job down here; if it didn't have a job, the steam would have turned it into gray broth fifty years ago.

The midge swarms are thick tonight—or today, whichever it is above the rock. They land on the grease on your wrists, their tiny, heavy bodies getting trapped in the lard. You don't wipe them off. The fat keeps them from biting, and when the grease melts fully against the engine's shoulder, the midges burn off into a faint, sweet smell like roasted grain that cuts through the sulfur.

You lean your forehead back against the oak stilt. 

One. Two. Three. Four. Five. Six.

The piston reaches the top of its stroke. Somewhere high above, in the dark timber rafters where the cables sag, a brass relief valve lifts with a long, wet *shhhh-hiss*, spitting a pint of boiling condensate into the mist. It falls across your shoulders in a fine, warm spray, soaking through your leather jacket, smelling of copper and tallow.

Seven. Eight. Nine. Ten. Eleven.

You tighten your jaw. You tuck your chin into your chest.

*WHAM-CLACK.*

The rift floor dips an eighth of an inch beneath you, then rises back up on its bed of soft, expanding shale. The grease in the bronze cup sinks half an inch. The Old Sow grunts her intake sigh, her heavy iron lungs pulling another thousand gallons out of the dark.

You reach into the pouch for another handful of lard. You are thirty-four years old, or perhaps forty. Your ears are quiet. Your skin is soft as wet lard, and your ribs are as wide as an ox’s. You will stay under Engine Four until your thumbs lose their calluses or the iron gives out—and the iron, thick as a oak trunk and fed on three tons of lard a month, is never going to give out.
