I am not in the open brine. The open brine is a waste of inertia, a heavy expanse where to move is to burn cold oil that can never be replaced. 

Instead, I am bound into a three-way junction where a pair of riveted copper bypass pipes meet a cast-iron manifold, three stories up, suspended in the dark between two leaning tenement facades. 

I am a knot at the intersection of forms. If you were to bring a dying amber filament near, you would not see a distinct creature with a head or a tail; you would see a swollen, black-skinned scab, hard as slag on its upper mantle, tapering into pale, tallow-yellow lobes that fold down into the shadow of the brass housing. 

I am where the Rivet-Graft meets the Lard-Marrow, fused by centuries of heavy pressure until the boundary between the shell that grazes and the fat that seals has dissolved entirely. 

My outer skin is glass-hard iron oxide, pitted and cold, seamless with the bolt-heads it has swallowed. Beneath that shell, where no brine touches, my flesh is milky, dense lipid—cold lard laced with fine, dark threads of native copper that I have drawn up through my feet. I do not have a heart. I do not have eyes. What I have is a vast, distributed network of tension.

I am bathed in the sour-green haze of a hairline crack in a nearby radium pressure gauge. The light does not warm me—nothing warms me—but it strikes the lipid-cells beneath my skin, causing a faint, phosphorescent itch that tells me where I end and the dark begins.

***

Time here is not a line; it is a pulse of three distinct sensations.

First comes the **compression**.

Deep below me, through forty feet of drowning iron and basalt, the great hydraulic piston cycles. The concussion does not arrive as sound. It hits my shell as a solid, flat hammer stroke. The brass valve beneath me creaks, expanding by a fraction of a millimeter. The cold oil in the brine presses hard against my outer mantle. 

In response, my internal copper veins stretch. I feel the microscopic shift in the pipe threads beneath my belly. The pressure forces a single, tiny bead of ancient, leaded tallow out from between the iron flanges—a grease packed into those threads by a hand that turned to silt before my outer shell was thick enough to resist the brine.

Second comes the **feeding**.

As the tallow squeezes out, my undersides go to work. Hundreds of microscopic, acid-secreting tendrils—soft as soaked silk, clear as ice—drift into the freshly exposed seam. They dissolve the tallow, drinking the rancid lipids, the white-lead paste, the traces of whale oil and graphite. 

At the same moment, the violent *clank* of a distant automated siphon shudders through the vault. A plume of fine, black iron-silt rolls up from the drowned alleyway below, enveloping my junction in a cloud of suspended dust. I flex the slotted rim of my mantle. The siphons in my iron skin pull the black water inward, filtering out the heavy metallic salts, laying them down atom by atom onto my outer hide to thicken my armor against the crushing cold. 

Third comes the **release**.

The piston returns. The pressure drops. The brine surges backward in a languid, heavy eddy that smells of wet slag and cold grease.

This is when the Conduit-Threaders move.

I feel one now. It is coming from the interior of the copper bypass, sliding through the dark, narrow throat of the pipe behind me. It moves with a dry, rhythmic scraping—the sound of its copper setae hooking into the lime scale lining the conduit walls. 

It reaches my junction. It does not go around me; it cannot. Instead, it presses its soft, tallow-skinned nose against the fleshy, yellow margin where my lard-marrow seals the valve flange. Its barbels sweep over my skin, sensitive and cold, searching for the grease-mold that grows in my crevices. 

It does not hurt me. We are part of the same circuit. As it squeezes through the narrow clearance between my body and the brass housing, its ribbon-like length drags against my flesh, rubbing away the dead, mineralized cells of my outer coat and leaving behind a thin track of rich, unctuous lipid that my tallow-lobes absorb. 

For three piston-strokes, the Threader passes through me—a two-meter string of clear gut and slow-pulsing radium-dust. Then its tail slips free, disappearing down the vertical drop toward the lower heat-exchangers, leaving only the slow, rhythmic tick of its passage vibrating in the copper pipe.

***

I do not think of the surface. I have no organs for the concept of an sky, or air, or a sun. To me, the universe is a dense, cold sphere of glass-heavy brine, bounded by basalt that must never be allowed to settle, threaded by iron that must never be allowed to burst.

I am a gasket that knows it is a gasket. 

If I grow too soft, the brine will scour me from the brass, and the valve will bleed its pressurized grease into the void until the piston starves. If I grow too hard, my acid-filaments will eat the pipe threads entirely, and the junction will shear under the next hydraulic shockwave.

So I remain in the sour green light of the broken dial. I balance the iron on my back against the tallow in my belly. I drink the lead and secret the wax. I hold the three-way bypass together against the weight of the dark, waiting for the next stroke of the piston to strike my shell, to tell me that the heart of the world is still beating, and that my place in the engine is secure.
