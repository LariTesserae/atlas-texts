I am not high enough to feel the light as a glare, nor deep enough to forget what the sun looks like when it hits clear quartz. 

I am thirty feet up, set into the eastern flank of the central atrium, precisely where the amber resin of the upper walkways meets the dark, load-bearing piers of chewed chalk and crushed sardonyx. 

I am an Elder, though "I" is a word that loses its edges when your rear limbs have been set in dry mortar for thirty suns.

Below me, the air is thick and sweet with the smell of toasted starch and warm lipid wax, rising in a steady, silent column from the deep crevices where the Anchor-Mothers breathe. Above me, the mica-fronds turn their bronze leaves toward the noon sky with a synchronized, metallic *click-click-click* that travels down the stone face like a scatter of dry grain. 

My carapace was once banded in bright chalcedony—red and pale cream—but the bands have blurred. Solar heat and the slow accumulation of mineral dust have turned my back into something resembling old horn or dark amber. I do not mind the weight. The weight is what keeps the arch above the nursery-bridge from sagging toward the floor.

If you were to look for me, you would not see a creature resting on a wall. You would see the wall itself bulging outward in a smooth, polished oval, crowned with six dark, jointed leg-segments that vanish into the translucent masonry. Only my thorax, my head, and my forward legs remain free to the air. 

My heart beats now. 

*...One.*

It will not beat again until the shade from the upper resin lattice reaches the edge of the bamboo thicket across the way. In the space between those beats, a hundred things happen in complete, muffled silence.

A young miner—pale-shelled, its mandibles still bright with the raw iron scraped from the ridge—crawls up the pillar beneath me. Its felted feet make no more sound than a feather dropped on wool. It does not look up; it has no eyes to speak of, only the sensitivity of its dark, velvet-coated antennae. 

It stops two inches below my chin. It presses the tips of its antennae against the lateral plates of my head.

Through that contact, I do not send it a thought. I send it a shape. 

I show it the thermal draft pushing through the gallery behind my back. I let it feel the microscopic shear-stress where the unweathered quartz face is trying to expand under the afternoon heat, pushing against the resin mortar with a force of three hundred grains. I show it where the wall is too thin—where the warm air is escaping before it can feed the powder-moss on the outer ledges.

The young one listens with its whole body, its carapace quivering with static charge. It taps my snout twice—*tick, tick*—a gesture of receipt as soft as a dry leaf hitting the rock. Then it turns, its felt-padded claws gripping the glass-sharp lip of the pier, and scurries down toward the synthesizers’ benches, its crop full of quartz flour ready to be kneaded into fresh mortar.

I breathe out. 

The air leaves my flank spiracles in a warm, lavender-scented puff. It carries the residual heat of my own slow digestion, mixed with the hydrocarbon vapors that my body brews from the lipids traded to me by the miners. The vapor curls upward, drifting past my face, and is caught by the wide, soot-black wing of a Velvet Moth hovering three feet away. 

The moth does not land. It rests on the air current my breath provides, its mirror-eyes catching the honeyed light and reflecting it back onto the dark wall as two pale, amber coins. Its wing-powder sloughs off in a faint, invisible dust that settles on my forehead, soothing the dry micro-fractures in my aging shell.

We are all holding each other up.

The mountain holds the quartz; the quartz holds the grubs; the grubs heat the air; the air carries the moth; the moth feeds the moss; the moss binds the dust; and I—sealed into the rib of the city by my own children—hold the arch so the sun can reach the resin without breaking the glass.

My lower segments have long since ceased to feel like limbs. They are stone now, or something better than stone: a flexible, mineralized amber that expands when the day is hot and contracts when the thin altitude turns cold at dusk. In another decade, or perhaps two, my heart will skip its minute-long turn and simply forget to restart. 

When that happens, there will be no gap left in the wall. A young synthesizer will crawl up, test my shell with its mouthparts, and lay a thin, glowing ribbon of wax over my eyes and my quiet jaws. They will smooth it down until I am only a curve in the gallery, a slightly darker vein in the long sardonyx spine of the thoroughfare.

Walkers will step on my back. Their felted feet will polish my shell until it shines like a river pebble—though none of us have ever seen a river.

Another beat.

*...Two.*

The shadow of the lattice has moved the width of a moth’s leg. The air is warm. The ridge is silent. I am right where the stone needs to be.
