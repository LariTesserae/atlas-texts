There is no *far*. There is only the density of the rock against my ribs, the cold friction of the water against my hide, and the thunder that does not enter through ears, but sits heavy and vibrating in the marrow of my jaw.

If you were to look for me, you would not see me at first. You would see only a dark, slick bulge in the third tier of the eastern wall—a curve of black mudstone that seems slightly too thick, wedged into a hollow where the basalt ceiling meets a vein of milky quartz. 

I am wedged here. My broad, neckless head is tipped down against my chest; my knees are folded tight into the swell of my belly. The water coming down from the hair-crack above does not splash against me; it hits the top of my skull, splits into four thin, perfect rivulets, and runs down the micro-channels of my back, shedding off my skin in small, glossy beads before dropping cleanly into the carved gutter below my feet.

I am the steward of this channel. I have been in this specific niche for seven cycles of the drift-eels, or perhaps seventy. Time does not accumulate here like sand; it washes through, leaves its trace of lime, and carries everything else away.

***

In my left hand, I hold the bone. It is an ancient probe, fashioned from the rib of something that died before the stone was chiseled into cells. It is smooth as fat, worn to the exact curvature of my palm. 

My sight is in the tips of my five-jointed fingers. Through the bone, I can read the gutter. Three thumb-widths down from the third step, the water is not sliding; it is *stumbling*. I feel the microscopic turbulence—a tiny, chaotic flutter traveling back up the current, through the bone, into the horn-nails of my index finger, and straight into the nerve-ridge across my forehead. 

A single grain of hard quartz sand, no larger than a grub’s mouth, has lodged in a hair-thin seam.

I do not hurry. Hurry belongs to things with lungs and short lives. I extend my arm, feeling the slick, cool air of the vault press against my skin. The roar of the room is immense, a solid block of sound that fills every cavity of the rock, but beneath the roar, I feel the structural heart of the place: the thousand tiny streams, all singing in different keys.

*Tap. Tap.*

I set the point of the bone against the quartz grain. I do not strike it; I press, leaning the heavy, mud-dense weight of my shoulder into the tool. The stone yields a microscopic shudder. The grain pops free, lifted by the current, and I feel the water catch its breath—its stride smoothing out, returning to that unbroken, glass-like glide down the terraced slate.

A deep, quiet satisfaction settles behind my forehead. The channel is open. The vault breathes.

***

Presently, another presence touches the stone three steps below me. 

I do not need eyes to know who it is. I feel the shift in the rock’s vibration—a slow, dense weight moving up the tooth-sized steps. The water on the slate alters its rhythm as two heavy, mudstone feet displace its path.

It is the older one—the one whose right arm has already begun to take on the pale, glittery crust of the calcium-weep. 

It ascends until its shoulder presses against my knee. We do not move away from each other. In this cramped citadel, space is not something you keep to yourself; it is something you share with the stone.

Slowly, deliberately, the older one leans its broad, flat forehead against mine.

The contact is instant and absolute. The deafening thunder of the falling water fades into the background, replaced by the deep, rhythmic thrum of its blood—a heartbeat that strikes only once for every twenty splashes of the catch-basin. Through the bone of our skulls, the message travels:

*The sulfur-pot in the floor-basin is dim. A drift-beast has fallen in the upper seam; its fat is cold, but its hide is thick.*

I press back against its forehead, a steady, firm push that carries my answer:

*The third tier is clear. The water glides.*

The older one nods—a slow, heavy tilt of its skull that rubs our wet hides together with a soft, suction-like sound. Then it draws back, its weight shifting down the steps, returning to the dark floor where the sulfur pots bleed their faint, greasy warmth into the damp.

***

I remain in my niche. 

I reach down to my side, where the skin folds over my ribs, and scrape a tiny bead of the thick, clear gel my body secretes. I roll it between my palms with a pinch of fine silt collected from the edge of my ledge. The mortar comes together—cool, dense, smelling of raw earth and ancient iron.

There is a micro-fracture forming in the quartz lintel above my head. I can feel it pulling at the air, a tiny, thirsty void waiting to split the rock. I reach up, my five joints bending with familiar, effortless precision, and press the mortar deep into the crack, smoothing it with the flat of my horn-nail until the stone feels whole again. Until it feels like nothing at all.

***

Where would you find me? 

You would find me right here, at the intersection of the weeping basalt and the carved slate. I am not the builder of this city, nor am I its guest. I am its maintenance. I am the organ through which the stone keeps itself clean.

Eventually—not today, not for many turns of the water—the iron in the weeping ceiling will settle too deeply into my skin. My fingers will lose their flexibility, turning from wet clay to hard slate. My heartbeat will slow from minutes to hours, and then to nothing at all.

When that time comes, I will not drop to the floor. I will simply pull my knees a little tighter to my chest, drop my chin into the hollow above my collarbone, and let the water sheet over my back until the calcite seals me to the wall. 

Someone else’s fingers will come along, tracing the curve of my spine, using my shoulder as a polished step to reach a higher gutter. And the water will continue to slide, perfectly, perpetually, down the carved dark.
