I am anchored at the triple-junction. 

It is the place where three massive, riveted box-girders meet like the knuckles of a buried hand, just twenty feet below the arch of the great forty-foot flywheel. Here, the condensation is thick, warm, and smells of old lard, iron filings, and the sweet, bruised-canning-jar scent of crushed fern fronds.

I am a truss-dweller. I have no name, because a name requires a tongue to shape it and air that stays quiet long enough to carry a whisper, and here the air is a solid, roaring wall. If my kind knows me, they know me as the steady, heavy frequency that lives at the triple-junction—the one whose pulse is always slightly slower than the hydraulic sigh of the upper ram.

Right now, my body is folded into the right angle of a structural gusset. My rear feet—the broad, four-way oppositions with their thick, leathery pads—are clamped around the lip of a six-inch angle-iron. My lower body is submerged in a three-inch-deep mattress of emerald moss that drips continuous, lukewarm oil-dew onto my thighs. My back is braced against the plum-colored oxide skin of the main web plate, and the dense, mossy bristle along my spine is dark and heavy, soaked through with the pavilion’s sweat.

I have been in this single position since the shadow of the flywheel spoke was two yards to my left. Now the shadow lies straight across my chest, a broad, cool band of blue-grey cut through the golden, spore-lit sunbeam that pierces the frame from the pale cyan sky above.

My left hand, long and four-digit-squared, reaches out with painful, deliberate slowness. It has taken me twenty minutes to extend my arm three feet along the copper conduit that feeds the thermal valve below. My fingers do not hurry; to hurry is to invite the grease to slip, to invite the air to catch you, to fall through the iron grates into the vast, light-blinded emptiness where the wind lives alone. I curl my fingers around the pipe. The metal is hot—hot enough to make the rubbery skin of my palm turn pale and soft—but beneath the heat is the rich, sugary crust of sulfur-goo that bleeds from the flange-joint. 

I scrape it off with the flat nail of my thumb. I bring it back to my mouth at the same glacial pace, slipping the warm, grainy paste past my soft lips. It tastes of brine, copper, and ancient fire.

When I have eaten, I do not move away. I lean forward.

I press the flat, hard disc of bone in the center of my forehead flush against the vertical face of the girder.

The world changes instantly.

The deafening, bone-shaking roar that fills the air shrinks into the background, becoming merely the sea in which the real language floats. Through my forehead, the iron does not roar; it *sings* in a thousand distinct, overlapping pitches.

I feel the plate-beetles first. They are forty feet down, scraping the gear-wells clean. Their passage is a dry, rhythmic scratch-shake, like sand sliding over slate—*shhk, shhk, shhk*—a tiny, metallic friction that tells me the moss down on the third tier is damp and ready for harvesting.

Further off, half a mile down the south pylon where the iron plunges toward the fog, there is a deep, rhythmic *thump-groan*. It is the thermal valve cycling. The steel down there is expanding under a fresh surge of steam; I can feel the heat rising through the frame long before the mist actually reaches my gusset. The vibration tastes sharp, like fresh iron-rust.

And then, very close—perhaps only eighty feet away, on the upper catwalk that spans the air-gap—I feel a different pulse. 

It is not a valve. It is not a beetle. It is a slow, heavy *thrum-pad... thrum-pad...*

Another of my kind. 

I keep my forehead locked against the metal, reading the weight of their steps. They are carrying something—their pulse is heavy, planted, taking four minutes for every five feet of girder. They stop. I feel the distinct, flat *clack* of their own forehead-pad hitting the same structural member.

They are sending a pulse. They strike the girder twice with their wrist-knuckle.

*THUMP. THUMP.*

The shockwave travels through fifty yards of riveted plate, through the copper conduits, through the moss-sleeves, and hits the bone in my skull. It is a question. It carries the harmonic signature of the high-level catwalks—a cold, thin vibration that means the wind is rising outside the lattice and the air-sweat is turning cool.

I do not use my throat. I have no voice to give. Instead, I shift my weight, raise my right hand, and bring my thick, calloused knuckle down against the web of the triple-junction.

*THUMP-THUMP-THUMP.*

*Warm here,* my pulse says. *The sulfur is thick on the conduit. The central wheel is true.*

I feel their reply—a single, long, lingering vibration as they press their forehead tight and absorb the news, a slow wave of shared heat through thirty thousand tons of iron. Then their steps resume, moving away toward the dark, quiet sanctuary behind the main boiler-housing where the sleep-clusters gather.

I relax my forehead, pulling back an inch from the plate. The physical sound slams back into my ears—the glorious, eternal, deafening gush of the high-pressure vents, the mechanical thunder that means we are held, that we are warm, that the sky has not swallowed us.

A brass-feathered swift drops out of the cyan light above. It lands three feet from my shoulder on an unused pipe-bracket with a sharp, double-metallic *tink* of its tiny claws. Its brassy breast feathers shimmer green in the mist-filled light shaft. It tilts its head, its dark eye fixing on me without fear. It carries a heavy, armored beetle in its beak, the beetle’s legs still feebly sweeping the humid air. 

We sit together in the roar. The bird does not sing; it doesn't need to. It simply bashes the beetle twice against the copper pipe—a bright, ringing *ping-ping* that sends a tiny ripple through the skin of my forearm—and swallows it whole. Then it drops sideways, folding its wings, and falls out of the pavilion frame, vanishing into the sunlit air toward the great white floor of the clouds below.

I watch it go, but my eyes do not follow it far. The blue sky is empty, thin, and silent; it has no grip, no heat, no iron to carry a voice. It is nothing.

I turn my gaze back to the girder beside me. A tiny turquoise lizard is working its way up the copper line, its throat flashing a rapid, silent yellow-green against the plum-colored oxide. I wait until it passes my hand—giving it the thirty minutes it needs to cross my knuckles—and then I slowly, deliberately begin to thread my limbs toward the dark space behind the big bronze gear-case.

The shadow of the flywheel spoke has moved another yard. The sun is tilting. It is time to fold into the cluster, to feel the heat of three other rubbery backs pressed against my own, our foreheads all resting against the same vibrating brass plate, listening to the great, slow heart of the world hum us to sleep.
