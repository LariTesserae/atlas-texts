I am not up in the air pocket, where the thunder breaks against the wet vaulting and tears itself into spray. Nor am I down in the black drain pipe beyond the heavy bronze bars, where the warm water gives up its light and slips away into the deep clay.

I am located on the second submerged tier, three paces to the left of the square pillar, exactly where the third solar shaft strikes the corner of the step at midday. 

It takes time to know what I am, because I am not a single body with a heart that pumps. My heat does not come from inside; I am precisely thirty-seven degrees Celsius from edge to edge, indistinguishable in temperature from the fluid that sweeps over me.

At first, I feel myself only as a line—a perfectly straight, six-foot seam of cream-colored slaked lime mortar pressed between two courses of hand-fired terracotta. I am the crispness of the right angle. I am the un-eroded edge that cuts the current like a dull blade. The mason’s thumb pressed the lime into me only two seasons ago; I still taste of dry calcium, river sand, and burnt limestone, slowly curing under the weight of a hundred tons of warm water.

But I am no longer just the mason’s line.

Where the vertical pillar of sunlight hits my sharp corner, the light does not just illuminate me—it *ignites* me. In the micro-texture of the rough lime, between microscopic grains of quartz sand, I am the first green silk. 

I am the hair-algae, anchored by thousands of invisible holdfasts into the porous mortar. I am the film of golden diatoms—trillions of tiny, glass-shelled geometries multiplying along the seam, turning the sterile red clay into a nursery. When the current sweeps down from the upper step, I do not resist it; I lay flat against the tile. When the current pauses in the swirl behind the pillar, my filaments lift, spreading outward like tufts of bright green hair floating in zero gravity.

I exist at the exact intersection of four forces:

First, the **mason’s string**. I am bound to the grid. I cannot grow on the glazed, dense face of the red tiles; they are too smooth, too hard. I am trapped in the straight lines, a living blueprint tracing the human geometry of the floor. I am the green net laid over the red box.

Second, the **light flue**. For two hours each day, the third shaft aligns perfectly with my section of the tier. During those hours, I do not merely see the light—I absorb it as a physical impact. The sunlight arrives through ten feet of water as a solid, leaning column of white-gold pressure. It turns my silica shells into tiny prisms. I produce microscopic bubbles of pure oxygen that cling to my green threads like silver beads before the current tears them away toward the surface air.

Third, the **acoustic storm**. The roar of the crashing waterfalls overhead travels through the water not as sound, but as a continuous, physical shudder. It vibrates the lime mortar beneath me. It hums through my cell walls. I am continuously shaken at the frequency of falling water, kept clean of dead sediment by the sheer violent acoustics of the room.

And fourth, the **bather**.

They come down when the solar shaft is at its brightest. When they sink to the second tier, their weight settles directly beside me. 

They do not crush me; their fingers find the sharp mortared groove right where my filaments are thickest. Their brown, water-softened fingers wedge into the seam to hold themselves down against the sweeping current. Their forearm rests along my tile. I feel the microscopic friction of their skin—the same temperature as the water, but different in texture, shedding tiny fragments of organic salt and oil that my cyanobacteria drink up instantly.

When they hold the ledge, their long hair floats out in the current, mingling with my green filaments. For twenty minutes at a time, we stream together in the same direction—two kinds of hair, one made of human keratin, one made of chlorophyll and lime, both animated by the same thermal loop.

Through their clear, unblinking eyes—staring straight past me into the shimmering silver mirror of the roof—I see what I am building. I am the first stain of life on an immaculate machine. I am the velvet margin where the dry desert overhead, brought down on the wind as microscopic dust, finally marries the wet, dark belly of the earth.

I am the green seam on the second step. I am the green silk holding fast to the mortar while the world roars past.
