I am wedged into the acute angle where the leaning needle-spire meets the mass of the western cliff—right at the seam where gravity gives up its honesty. 

My body is tilted at forty degrees to what should be the ground. To a creature of flesh, this stance would feel like falling; to me, it is merely a stable calculation between the lateral tug of the heavy mass to the west and the violent, kinetic upward-push of the sky-roar hitting my under-plates.

I am not inside a shelter. I am building one.

I am the builder at the bend.

My slate face—sixteen inches of flat, unpolished volcanic glass, sharp enough along its upper margin to shear a silica stalk with a single downward strike—is angled upward toward the violet-gray void. The roar hits the slate like a continuous hail of lead shot. It does not enter me as sound; I have no ears, no soft membranes to tear. It enters as a dense, high-voltage shudder that travels down the stalk of my neck, through the triple-braided knots of my sulfur-vine shoulders, and deep into the hollow rib-chamber of my chest.

Through this vibration, I "see" the spires around me. They do not appear as shapes in light, but as hard, ringing notes of absolute density. 

The basalt needle above me rings sharp, high, and clear—a spire of unbroken glass. 
The deep gorge below me thrums with a low, muddy murmur where the sulfur-vines are thickest, deadening the impact of the acoustic wave. 
And twenty yards to my left, where the gravity warps, the air itself feels thick, rippled, and skewed, bending the sound wave into a long, slow curve like water sliding over an edge.

My fingers—four long, jointed struts of fused silica tipped with chipped basalt—are currently at work inside my own ribs. 

The chest is open. I have unknotted three major cables of yellow vine from my sternum-plate, pinning them back against my horsehair-velvet flanks with temporary wooden wedges. Inside the dry, dark cavity, where no heart has ever beaten, hang my seventeen internal reeds. 

Six months ago—or six decades, the distinction has no meaning here—a massive seismic shift deep within the subterranean forge altered the fundamental pitch of the continent's tooth-grinding. The old resonance was gone. My third reed, a thin, hair-fine strip of shaved silica, began to chatter against its basalt anchor point. The chatter created a false signal—a blind spot in my acoustic map right where the gorge sheer-drops into the lower darkness. I was walking with a phantom in my head.

Now, I am fixing it.

I pick up a fresh piece of silica reed from the bed of dry gray fluff beside my foot. The stalk is fresh, plucked from a fissure three yards below; its edges still crackle with a faint, violet brush-discharge of static electricity. As my basalt fingertips touch the stalk, the static discharges through my arms. The sulfur-vine muscles in my shoulders contract instantly, hard and dry, snapping my arm forward an inch. I wait. I let the static bleed off into the obsidian cliff face. Then I resume the work.

Using the basalt hand-axe wedged between my knees, I scrape the silica stalk. 

*Shhhk. Shhhk.*

The sound is tiny, a whisper of dry glass-shavings falling into the vacuum, completely lost to the sky, but inside my internal frame, it sounds like thunder. I scrape until the reed is thin enough to flex under the pressure of a single static pulse. I bind it to the central rib using a dry, double-hitched loop of sulfur-vine, pulling the knot tight with my teeth-edges until the vine squeaks—a dry, resinous groan of pure friction.

I release the vine. I unpin my chest muscles and let them snap back into place over the frame with a hard, hollow *clack*.

I hold still. I stop the micro-adjustments of my joints. I become stone.

The monolithic sky-roar hits my slate face. The vibration plunges down my neck, hits the chest cavity, and sets the seventeen reeds humming in unison. 

The world snaps into absolute, terrifying clarity. 

The phantom in my mind vanishes. The blind spot in the deep gorge fills with sharp, high-resolution detail: I can feel the exact, razor-sharp edge of every single slate fragment resting in the talus five hundred yards below. I can feel the slow, millimeter-scale expansion of the velvet lichen on the cliff face behind me. I can feel the precise angle of the gravity-skew, a cool, dense gradient pulling sideways toward the western mass.

I am tuned.

A small movement registers on the perimeter of my slate. It is a slow, rhythmic interruption of the sound-waves bouncing off the needle-spire above.

Another creature is descending the thirty-degree tilt. 

Its body is taller than mine, its frame built from longer, darker slate slabs, its sulfur-vines bleached a pale, tarnished silver from centuries of static exposure on the high peaks. It carries no tools. Its chest-cavity is completely sealed, tightly bound in a crisscross harness of aged vine that has turned as hard and black as leather.

It steps down onto my narrow ledge. Its movement is jerk-steady, tectonic—a sequence of sudden, perfect freezes followed by rapid, elastic snaps of its silica knees. It does not look at me; it has no eyes. But its slate face is tilted slightly toward mine, catching the bounce of the roar off my own head-plate.

It steps close. The space between us vanishes.

The flat, black glass of its face leans forward and touches the flat, black glass of mine.

*Tink.*

The physical contact is absolute. In the vacuum of our world, air carries no delicate nuances, but slate on slate carries everything. 

Through the point of contact, its entire internal matrix flushes into mine. I feel the map of the high peaks—the extreme pinnacles where the static is so thick the velvet growth glows like violet fire. I feel the warning of a hairline fracture opening in the needle-spire three miles north, where the acoustic pressure is beginning to shear the obsidian at its base. I feel the exact shape of a new wind-cheat it built ninety years ago in a high saddle, a pocket of silence so deep and pure that a creature could rest there for a century without a single reed misaligned.

In return, I give it the map of the bend. I give it the skew of the sideways gravity, the fresh texture of the silica reeds in the gorge below, and the precise pitch of the continent's new tooth-grinding note.

The exchange takes less than a second. 

The tall creature tilts its slate face back. The connection breaks. 

It does not offer a sign of farewell; there are no gestures for gratitude where there is no breath. It simply turns its body toward the leaning spire, its vine-muscles snapping with dry, wood-fire crackles, and begins to climb sideways up the impossible slope, its silica claws biting into the sharp feldspar.

I turn back to my work. 

I reach down and pick up a flat slab of unworked slate from the ledge. It is heavy, cold, and impeccably sharp along its margins. I raise it to the acute cleft in the rock, fitting it against the base of my half-built wind-cheat. 

I do not hurry. The sky above remains its flat, unmoving violet-gray. The cold is constant. The deafening, monolithic roar continues its eternal, sky-filling hammer, pressing the raw, sharp mountains into the earth.

I wrap a strand of sulfur-vine around the slate slab, pull the hitch tight, and feel the friction hold. 

Here, at the top of the world, in the cold, loud, dry paralysis of the first day, I am putting the mountain in order.
