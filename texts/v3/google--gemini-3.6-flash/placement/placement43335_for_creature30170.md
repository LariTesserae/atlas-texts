Before there is a shape to my body, there is a pitch.

It is a low, heavy vibration that enters not through ears—for I have none—but through six points of contact along my length where my calcified hooks bite into the dark, oil-slick schist. Above me, a slate tile weighing ninety pounds presses down with a cold, passive authority. Below me, another tile meets it at an angle thirty-five degrees off from what the rest of the universe might call level. 

I am wedged between them. I have always been wedged between them.

Outside the wall, the atmosphere is tearing itself against the mountain—a continuous, flat, subterranean freight-train roar that never rests, never rises, never dies away. But here, where I am pinned in the dark seam of the second shelter, the air is different. The geometry of these hand-fitted tiles catches that deafening roar and bends it. It folds the frequency back on itself, stepping it down half an octave, turning a iron screech into a deep, steady hum that vibrates through my marrow.

I am a spindle, but to say "I am" is to imply a separation from the stone that does not exist. 

For three hundred years, I have not moved. My task has been simple, total, and absolute: to be the exact thickness of the space between two rocks. 

When a *kell* crawled over the outer face of this shelter a century ago—its six wide, wet-iron pads pressing against the dry-stacked tiles with the slow, crushing weight of a falling cliff—the upper tile groaned. It wanted to slip two millimeters down the thirty-five-degree pitch. Had it slipped, the seam would have closed. The acoustic angle of the shelter would have ruptured, and the wild, un-softened roar of the sky would have poured into the interior, shattering the quiet dead-zone where the air sits still.

I felt the shift coming before the stone even moved. I flexed my jointed segments, driving my forward hooks deeper into a hairline pore in the schist. From the margins of my middle joints, I squeezed out three drops of pale, viscous paste. It pooled in the gap, cold and thick, reacting instantly with the suspended copper and iron ions in the damp air. Within a day, it had turned as hard as the mountain itself. The tile stayed. The shelter kept its quiet.

Now, though, something else is happening. A slow, ancient change that happens only when the stone-dust has done its work.

Over the centuries, as the thin, metallic moisture seeped through the wall-seams, my outer shell has been drinking it in. Minute by minute, grain by grain, the dark slate has colonized my skin. The chitin of my husk has mineralized, turning from pale bone-tissue into something rigid, heavy, and brittle. My joints no longer flex with ease; they click like dry twigs. I am becoming slate. If I stay as I am for another hundred years, I will cease to be a shim and become merely a rock, unable to absorb the mountain’s subtle, eternal shifts.

I can feel the split coming. It begins behind my head—a tiny, hair-thin fracture running down my back.

It does not hurt. Pain belongs to things that bleed, things that warm the air around them. Here, there is only tension and release. 

I take a deep, slow contraction—not of lungs, but of the inner, softer frame that has been growing inside the petrified shell. I pull inward, shrinking away from my own skin. The fracture opens with a quiet, dry *snick* that is instantly swallowed by the wall’s low hum.

Slowly, over days, I wriggle forward out of my own petrified coat. 

I emerge smaller, paler, softer—a fresh needle of bone-and-chitin, six inches long, supple enough to bend around the micro-contours of the schist. My new hooks are sharp and wet with joint-paste. I creep two inches further into the dark labyrinth of the wall-seam, finding a fresh pocket of space where the upper and lower tiles threaten to touch, and I lock myself into place once more.

Behind me, in the gap I just vacated, sits my old husk. 

It is hollow now, a petrified tube of mineralized bone, heavy and loose. The draft that trickles through the wall-seams—the subtle, cold breath of the basin—catches the empty shell. It nudges it. The shell slides out of the seam, slipping free of the stacked slate, and falls through the quiet air of the shelter’s interior.

It lands in the corner with a soft, hollow clutter, settling onto a small, dry pile of splinters that has been gathering there since the mountain locked its gravity into place. 

Down there, in the acoustic dead-zone at the center of the ring, my old skins do not rot. They do not drift away, because the air inside the shelter is too still to scatter them. They sit in the cold, un-evaporating dew, pale and petrified, a record of every century this wall has refused to fall.

Up here, in the dark seam between the tiles, my new body settles into the stone. I feel the thirty-five-degree pull of the world anchoring me to the schist. I feel the heavy, iron roar of the sky hitting the outside of the wall, and I feel it drop—half an octave, precise and smooth—into the steady hum that fills my joints.

I extend my hooks. I touch the damp, greasy stone. I am ready for the next five hundred years.
