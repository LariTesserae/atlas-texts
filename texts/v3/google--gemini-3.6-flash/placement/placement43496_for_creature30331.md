I am not on the high peaks where the snow-hawks turn, nor am I down in the dark, cellar-choked roots where the khor bellow softly in the manure-warm gloom. 

I am at the hinge of the world: a middle terrace, on a sun-facing ledge no wider than three strides, where a braided juniper bridge anchors its heavy foot into the mountain.

My name, if spoken aloud, would require too much air—two syllables that waste a lungful of breath that could be used to heave a slab of slate. Instead, in the sign-language of the ledges, I am *Three-Fingers-and-a-Thump*: a flick of the right hand against the breastbone, signifying a man who works the structural joints where the wood meets the dry-stone wall.

My hands are thick as shovels. The skin across my knuckles is black-grey, permanently stained with the paste of charcoal, rendered khor-fat, and pounded juniper needles. The paste has worked so deeply into the grain of my palms that my lines look like natural mineral veins running through pale horn. I do not feel the frost when I touch the slate; my skin has long since surrendered the sharp, panic-filled nerve-responses of youth. To me, stone is not cold; it is merely *dense* or *loose*, *settled* or *shifting*.

Right now, I am crouched on my heels—the low, wide squat that keeps a man’s center of gravity inches from the rock, so that even a sudden gust off the north shear cannot catch him like a sail. 

Between my boots lies a lintel beam of seasoned mountain birch, three arms-lengths long and bent like a bow by decades of slow drying in the shadow-caves. I am working its end with a stone adze, knocking away thin, curling chips of dense, iron-hard wood. *Thump. Thump. Clack.* The sound is dry and short. It does not echo. The thin indigo sky drinks the noise instantly, leaving only the tiny, immediate ring of stone striking grain.

To my left, three feet away, the dry-stone wall of the dwelling terrace rises fifteen tiers. It is dark slate, split so cleanly that the joints are hair-thin. In the recessed pocket-holes I built into this wall twelve winters ago, the choughs are nesting. I can feel them more than I can hear them—a continuous, low, vibrating click and rustle inside the rock behind my kidneys, like a heart beating inside the mountain’s ribcage.

Below my left heel, the world drops straight down for eight hundred paces into the blue mist of the pass. I do not look at it, not because I am afraid—there is no word for vertigo in our whistles—but because it is empty space, and empty space contains nothing to lay a level against. 

Across the gorge, eighty paces away on the opposite shoulder, a young woman steps out onto an upper roof. She wears a surcoat of felted grey wool, so thick it stands away from her shoulders like armor. She does not call out. She raises one arm, extends two fingers toward the high peak, then sweeps her hand downward toward her hip, finishing with a sharp, flat palm toward the stone.

*A slide in the high gully,* she is telling me. *The ice is moving early today. Clear the drain-channel.*

I do not nod. I raise my adze, hold it flat against the birch beam for a heartbeat to show I have understood, and lower my head back to my work.

***

As the midday sun slides past the razor-edge of the upper peak, the absolute light vanishes instantly. There is no long twilight here; the mountain simply casts its own shadow over us like a falling slab of basalt. The temperature drops twenty degrees in the time it takes to draw ten breaths.

The hoarfrost instantly begins to bloom across the birch beam, silver-white and needle-sharp. 

I lift the adze, tuck it into my heavy leather cinch, and stand up using the slow, rolling push of my thighs. My knee joints crack—a dry, wood-like sound in the freezing air. I step across the threshold, pushing aside the heavy, double-layered wool curtain that seals the dwelling door.

Inside, the world changes its language entirely.

The cold drops away, replaced by a wall of dense, suffocating, wonderful heat. It hits my face like a damp towel. The air is thick with the smell of tallow lamps, the sweet, heavy stench of khor-dung smoldering on the hearth-stone, the sharp resin of my own skin, and the musk of twelve bodies packed into a space six paces square.

The ceiling is low—my hair grazes the juniper rafters. I stoop, unbuckling my moss-stuffed boots by touch in the dim yellow light of the clay lamp. 

In the corner, where the masonry meets the mountain's natural granite wall, two of the ivory dogs are lying wrapped around each other like a single, massive mound of wool. As I enter, the larger one—an old bitch with a scarred muzzle—doesn't open her eyes, but her chest gives a single, deep *thrum-thrum* that vibrates through the flagstones beneath my bare, calloused feet. She is telling the room that the shape at the door is known, that the air outside is cold, that all is anchored.

I sit on the edge of the sleeping bench beside my brother's child. She is seven winters old, her cheeks wind-burned to the color of ripe plums, her small hands already stained dark with charcoal paste. She is scraping a dry piece of khor-cheese with a bone knife, dropping the salty, yellow curls into a pot of boiling meltwater.

Above her head, carved into the main juniper ceiling beam, are three hundred years of notches. Some are deep and square; some are fine, light scratches made by fingers that were losing their strength. My own grandfather’s mark is there—a series of four diamond-cuts that tell the story of the winter the eastern cistern froze solid and we had to drink the blood of the young khor to live until the spring thaw.

I stretch my broad fingers into the tallow smoke above the small clay dish, letting the heat soften the grease on my skin until it shines black and wet. My breath comes slower now, deep and full, pulling in the rich, heavy air of the interior.

Outside, far above the dry-stone roofs, the high wind is scouring the pale granite teeth of the peak, whistling its thin, dry song through an empty sky. But inside the wall, surrounded by dry slate, felted wool, and the slow, heavy pulses of the sleeping dogs, there is only the quiet *clack* of the bone knife against the wooden bowl, and the knowledge that tomorrow we will lay three more stones upon the ledge.
