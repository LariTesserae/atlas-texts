I am not above the machinery, looking down, nor am I standing on some impossible, slick ledge of basalt. To be an observer here is to be torn to red fog by the first whipping chain. 

I find myself **underneath the housing of the main lower hydraulic ram**, wedged in the three-inch gap between the raw, shattered shale face and a eight-ton bedplate of cast iron. 

I am not a person, and I am not a single plant. I am the junction point where the black moss, the deep root-mesh, and the mountain’s cold fracture meet the hot, trembling steel. 

If I have a center—a place where the sensation of being is thickest—it is right here, wrapped around a two-inch steel anchor pin driven four feet into the unweathered stone.

***

My world has no horizon. It has only gradients of strain, temperature, and frequency.

To my left, the basalt is zero degrees, wet with cold, mineral-heavy condensate that trickles down from the pinched thread of navy-black sky far above. To my right, inches away, the brass bearing sleeve of the main shaft runs at a scorching, friction-cooked ninety degrees Celsius. 

I live in the narrow band between those two impossibly hostile truths.

I feel time not in seconds, but in the thirty-hertz thud of the ram. *Thud-stroke. Thud-stroke.* Every hit sends a shockwave through my dense, blubbery moss-body that would shatter bone, but I do not shatter. I deform. I squeeze flat, my cells sliding over one another like wet, oil-soaked leather, absorbing the mountain-breaking momentum and spreading it harmlessly into the dark stone. 

Where my microscopic rhizoids bite into the rock, I am sweating a thin, sulfurous acid. It stings, a slow, continuous chemical burn that eats tiny pits into the basalt, giving me a deeper grip. I can feel the mountain creeping, wanting to shed this heavy iron parasite and let it fall five thousand meters into the valley fog; I hold it back. My roots are laced with iron oxide pulled from the filings that settle in the slush beneath me. I am literally wire-stiffened flesh, a living turnbuckle binding the engine to the abyss.

Above me, I feel the movement of the ferns—not as a sight, but as a violent, rhythmic tug on the common root-mesh. Every few seconds, the steam valve overhead releases its screaming burst. The heat scalds my upper fronds, but it is a heat we crave. The instant the scalding fog hits the blue-green blades, their tar-nodules pop—*crack, crack, crack*—hurling oily, hard-shelled spores into the violent draft of the twelve-foot flywheel. Some are crushed instantly against the iron rim; others are driven deep into fresh micro-fissures in the iron plates, where they will germinate before the next stroke.

I am drinking. 

A thick, dark droplet of heavy gear oil—scorched, metallic, smelling of sulfur and pressurized heat—drips from the underside of the main shaft housing. It lands directly on my upper crest. My skin does not repel it; it pulls the black grease in through porous, rubbery membrane cells. I break the hydrocarbons down in the dark, converting industrial waste into cold, dense, petroleum-sugars. 

When the bearing beside me glows too hot—when the screech of dry brass reaches a frequency that threatens to seize the shaft—my deep roots react without thought. The heat triggers a hydraulic shift within my root-mesh. I release trapped, freezing mountain water against the glowing metal. 

*Hiss.* 

A violent burst of white steam curls out from under the housing, engulfing me in a warm, wet blanket. The bearing cools just enough. The metal glides again. A slick, hot film of burnt oil washes back down over my fronds as a reward.

I do not know what this machine is building, or what it is pumping, or why it was bolted to this unpeopled, five-thousand-meter wall of dark basalt. I do not know if anyone is left at the bottom of the world to hear the bone-rattling thunder of its stroke. 

I know only the four things that make up existence:
The absolute, razor-edged cold of the shattered stone.
The hot, greasy breath of the working steel.
The violent, screaming wind of the passing counterweights.
And the endless, pounding pulse that tells me the iron is still anchored, the green is still holding, and the mountain has not yet won.
