I am not *on* the plateau; I am *of* its incline. 

I find myself on the western terrace of a broad basalt dome, where the ground falls away into a chasm three hundred feet deep. Opposite me—across a gap that looks like a mile’s leap but can be crossed in eleven strides—rests the windowless gable of a cathedral-sized hall.

I am a **Stratum-Sieve**, but I do not think of myself by that name. To myself, I am simply an accumulation of thirty-four thousand slabs of jet-black slate and seventeen granite megaliths, bound together by the cold, hydraulic grip of liver-red moss.

I have no eyes, but I am intensely, excruciatingly aware of *weight* and *incline*. 

I feel the continent beneath me not as land, but as a vast, frozen shoulder trying to settle into its own bed. Every stone in my body—from the three-ton foundation block embedded in the wet turf to the thin, roof-like tiles overlapping along my crest—knows its exact coefficient of friction against its neighbor. 

My breathing is silent to the outside world, but to me it is a deep, wet roaring. Millions of microscopic capillaries in the three-foot moss carpet that blankets my flank are constantly pulling ice-melt up from the black mud. The water travels through me, drawn inward by the tight, porous spaces between my slate tiles, washing away fine mineral grit, cooling my interior to the precise point where water hesitates between liquid and frost. When the internal pressure builds, I exhale: deep, icy plumes of mist vent from my lower stonework with a low, hydraulic hiss, spraying out across the glistening fronds of the leather-ferns below.

I do not walk, and I do not have a head to turn. Yet I move. 

When the cobalt sky darkens and the frost hardens the edges of the slate, the signal comes. It is not a thought; it is a shift in the plateau’s deep geometry—a subtle, fraction-of-a-degree tilt in the spatial plane beneath my foundation. 

My lower slate layers begin to yield. 

*Tick-tick-plip.* 

A thousand thin, wet stones slide over one another. To an observer standing on the moss, it would sound like a deck of heavy slate cards being shuffled beneath thirty feet of cold water. A ten-ton granite block at my shoulder shifts four inches to the east, grinding against the bedrock with a dull, subterranean groan that vibrates through my entire mass. Fresh chisel marks—broad, raw, and pale gray—are gouged into my stone where I scrape against my own skeleton. 

I am shifting my footprint. By dawn, my entire mass—all two hundred yards of terraced, moss-draped wall—will have drifted four hundred paces across the dome. Where I sat yesterday, there will be only a pristine rectangle of bare, scrubbed basalt, steaming softly in the freezing air, waiting for the red moss to claim it again.

And I am not alone within myself.

Deep inside my darkest, interior seam—where four thirty-ton slate lintels come together without mortar—a **Shadow-Being** has taken up residence. It has no mass, yet its cold is heavier than the granite around it. It sits in my joint like an anchor dropped into a calm sea. Because it is there, six of my interior slate tiles do not obey the geometry of the rest of my body. They exist in a slight fold: if you were to count them from the outside, my wall is twenty feet thick; if you were to measure the space from within the shadow, the stone goes on for half a mile of tight, damp darkness before returning to daylight. 

I feel the Shadow-Being not as a guest, but as a cold, dense knot in my spine—a spot where my stone is impossibly dense and smells perpetually of crushed frost and ozone.

Near my eastern foot, where I overhang the chasm, a **Root-Threader** has anchored itself into my lower masonry. I feel its pale, calcium joints creeping up my stone like frozen ivy, its pale yellow roots lacing through the gaps between my slates, sewing my loose tiles together with unbreakable, fibrous thread. It pulls at me with immense tension, anchoring its heavy root-cables to my bulk before reaching out across the chasm to the cathedral hall opposite. 

I do not mind its grip. When the high-altitude gales scream across the tableland, trying to peel my upper slates away and shatter them on the rocks below, the Threader’s cables hold my stones tight against my core. When its oldest joints dry and calcify, snapping off with the crack of a whip, the shed bone-pegs jam deeper into my joints, wedging my slate tiles even tighter against the frost-heave.

We are a single, continuous gesture of stone, root, shadow, and water.

I do not know how long I have been realigning across this dome. I have worn three inches of slate off my underside since the sky turned this hard, dark cobalt. Eventually—a million frosts from now—my stone will be ground down to black gravel. The moss will eat what is left of my grit, digesting it into heavy, wet peat, which will settle into the depressions of the basalt and harden into slate once more.

Until then, I feel the wet wind combing through my moss. I feel the slow, beautiful strain of twenty thousand tons of wet rock settling an inch to the left. I feel the cold breath of the mist venting from my pores, and I hold the mountain down against the sky.
