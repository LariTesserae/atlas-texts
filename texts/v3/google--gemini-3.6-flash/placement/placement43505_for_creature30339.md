I am on the vertical face, six feet above the black brine pool, where the basalt is hot enough in its deep cracks to bleed steam, but cold enough on its outer skin to turn mist into ice. 

My chest is pressed flush against the wet stone. I have no eyes, but I do not need them; the rock speaks to the underside of my belly in low, hydraulic shudders, and the air speaks to the fringe of blue cilia around my jaw in delicate, prickly shocks. 

I am a Stalker of the Eleven-Second Gap, but inside my own skin, I do not think of myself by a name. I am simply the thing that waits between the thrums.

To exist here is to live in two alternating states of matter: stone, and lightning.

***

During the eight seconds of rising pressure, I am stone. 

My hide is smooth, thick, and oil-slicked, dark as the wet soot around me. My ribs are not separate bones, but a heavy, flexible cage of mineralized cartilage that expands slightly to match the swell of the earth below. I do not breathe through a throat—the air is too thick with scalding brine and fine iron grit—but draw cold oxygen slowly through spiracles along my flanks, filtering out the spray.

I am motionless, but I am not asleep. I am reading the circuit.

Through the rubbery pads of my feet and the thin, wet skin of my lower jaw, the wall is a luminous, branching map of voltage. I feel the deep geothermal vein tightening its fist in the dark below. I feel the Anvil-Lung expanding in its chimney, its massive fibrous tendons stretching under the weight of the sea. 

And on the rock face around me, I feel the grazers.

To my cilia, the iron-backed isopods do not look like creatures; they are small, heavy capacitors dragging themselves along the copper seams. I smell their iron shells—cold, bitter, and sharp—and I track the tiny, scratchy vibration of their diamond mandibles stripping the oil-moss. They move in waves, hundreds of them, crawling over the wet basalt. 

At second six, the tension in the rock reaches a high, screaming pitch that human ears could not isolate from the ambient roar, but to me it is a tightening spring. 

At second eight, the isopods feel it too. They stop grazing. In perfect, mechanical unison, thousands of iron tails slap flat against the basalt. 

*Zip-snap.*

A grid of blue-white light flares across the wall, burning through my cilia like a flash of bitter salt on the tongue. In that millisecond, every isopod discharges its stored static into the copper veins of the rock, grounding itself before the thermal strike. The discharge leaves an afterimage etched into the nerves of my jaw—a cluster of bright, distinct points of light glowing against the dark stone.

I pick one. A large one, three feet to my left, clinging to a sharp lip of basalt just above the spray line.

I do not move. Not yet. To move now, in the silence before the blast, would mean slipping on the wet, current-slick stone, or alerting the grazer through the conductive moss. 

I hold my breath. My muscles lock like cold iron.

***

Then, the eleventh second arrives.

The stone valve deep in the earth snaps open. The Anvil-Lung contracts with a concussive, wet *slam*. 

A wall of white, scalding vapor erupts from the vent chimneys with a sound that ceases to be sound and becomes a physical weight, crushing the air out of the rift. The mist blinds the cavern; the roar swallows the universe.

And I burst.

Time does not flow during the steam blast; it collapses into a single, violent frame. My rubber-padded claws bite into glass-sharp edges of basalt that would tear soft flesh to ribbons. I do not slide; my oily hide sheds the freezing spray, and my low-slung frame keeps my center of gravity pressed hard against the stone. 

Three feet of vertical, razor-edged basalt covered in slick liverworts disappears beneath me in a fraction of a second. I do not hear my own footsteps—the shrieking vent consumes everything—but I feel the hard, metallic *crunch* through my jaw as my scissor-like mandibles snap shut around the iron carapace of the isopod.

The shell breaks with the familiar, salty taste of chelated iron, copper-rich blood, and oily moss.

Before the steam jet can crest its peak, before the boiled fly-larvae hit the upper air where the storm-petrels wait, my claws lock into the stone again. My belly slams flat against the wet basalt. 

The steam cuts off. The shriek dies into a wet, echoing hiss.

I am stone again.

***

I am frozen three feet from where I started, the crushed body of the grazer held tight in my jaws. The rift settles back into its eight seconds of cold, heavy thrumming. Rain drops from the upper fog, icy and fat, washing the hot, iron-tasting blood from my face and down into the black brine pool below, where the Battery-Ribbons stir in the dark depths.

To an outside observer standing on the ledge above, I would seem to have teleported—a black shape frozen on the wall, suddenly reappearing a yard away with prey in its mouth, without any visible journey between the two points.

But there is no mystery to it. I am simply part of the timing. 

I do not wish for the sun, which I have never felt, nor for the wind, which is blocked by the high lip of the plateau above. The cold gray sky far overhead is merely a void where the steam goes to die. Everything that matters—everything that feeds, moves, or breathes—happens within these forty feet of wet, electric basalt, driven by a pulse that began long before my birth and will continue long after my cartilage turns back into mineral.

I chew slowly, grinding the iron shell against my hard palate, swallowing the rich, electro-chemically charged lipids of the grazer. My cilia ripple in the dark mist, cooling down, feeling the static begin to build once more in the wet moss.

Deep below my belly, the stone valve swells. The Anvil-Lung draws its wet, heavy breath. 

One. Two. Three.

I press my chest to the basalt, feel the copper seams begin to hum, and wait for the thunder to move me.
