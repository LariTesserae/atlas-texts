I am chest-down against the grain.

Before there is a face, or limbs, or the cold, wet velvet of skin, there is the thrum. It comes up through the sternum—not as a sound, because the heavy air eats sound before it can travel three paces, but as a dense, low pulse that rattles the cartilage of my ribs. Twenty miles away, down the long curve of a load-bearing arch that was carved when the world was young and straight, a wooden peg three feet thick has sheared. The wood is telling the wood. The wood is telling me.

My breath does not come from a mouth. Along my flanks, the lace-curtained spiracles pull in the warm, wet air—air that tastes of cold resin, digested bark, and the faint, sweet rot of old liverworts. It goes deep into the soft, spongy lining of my interior, warming me from within while the fog settles on my back in fat, silver beads that roll off my hydrophobic down without ever touching skin.

I shift my weight. Gravity here is not a stone pulling at the heels; it is a soft, indecisive weight, like a heavy blanket laid over the shoulders. I do not stand *on* the spar; I adhere to it. My lower digits—four wide, splayed pads lined with microscopic, moisture-wicking ridges—find the grain of the petrified heartwood through three inches of wet moss. I turn sideways, then upside down, stepping beneath the belly of the beam. The sky above is a ceiling of glowing, broad-leafed vines; the abyss below is a warm, amber-tinted fog through which a house-sized sphere of water is drifting, lazy and silent, carrying its pale, thread-like nymphs through the empty air.

I am a Splicer, but I am not alone on this joint.

Around me, in the dim, shadowless twilight, three others of my kind are already present. We do not look at one another with eyes—eyes are for the silent gliders that drop from the high mortises—we know each other by the rhythm of our movement against the wood. One of them, older, its grey-white velvet worn slick at the elbows, strikes its hard keratin knee against the bark.

*Tap-tap-thrum.*

The message ripples through the grain into my chest: *The rot is deep in the elbow. The Spinner is dead.*

We move together toward the joint—a massive, swollen burl eight hundred yards wide where four carved trunks meet in an ancient, braided splice. Here, the long-dead hands of the builders forced a socket into a mortise, pinning it with a dowel that has now sprouted into its own tangled forest of secondary spars.

The air here is thick with the smell of wet chitin and old silk. Resting in the crook of the joint is the shell of a Cable-Spinner. It died perhaps a century ago; its soft interior has long since been scoured clean by the cold, glowing mold, leaving behind a vaulted, amber-tinted chamber of translucent shell, tied directly into the flexing ligaments of the world. Inside that chamber, nestled in soft lacework fibers, our translucent, rubbery eggs lie waiting for the humidity to turn them into crawlers.

But the joint is weeping.

Through a tear in the ancient Spinner-silk, an indigo light is pulsing. It is soft at first, a faint violet stain bleeding through the liverworts, but as we draw closer, the Rot-Gazer’s embedded filaments flare brighter. The wood inside the socket is turning to black, anaerobic slush. The petrified heartwood—the bone of our sky—is sick.

We do not hurry. Hurry is an inefficiency that breaks soft ribs against hard wood. We move with the slow, fluid precision of creatures whose skeletons are made to bend.

I reach the lip of the fracture. My upper limbs, slender and six-jointed, extend into the dim indigo glow. With the flat, spade-like keratin of my outer digits, I begin to scrape. *Scritch. Scritch.* The sound is tiny, swallowed almost immediately by the damp atmosphere. I peel away the soft, ruined bark, digging down until my nails hit the hard, glassy resistance of healthy, resin-soaked grain.

Beside me, the old Splicer presses its palms flat against the raw edge of the wound.

I do the same. I press my chest tight to the beam, anchor my splayed feet into the firm heartwood, and draw a long, wet breath through my flank-spiracles. Deep within my wrists, the latex glands warm up. The sensation is a slow, pleasurable ache—a thick, amber heat creeping down through my digits.

Slowly, the latex begins to weep from our palms. It fills the air with a sharp, clear smell—like crushed needles and ancient rain. It spills over our fingers, heavy and translucent, pooling in the carved channel of the fracture. We work in absolute sync, without a glance, without a voice, our bodies rising and falling together as we pump our natural resin into the heart of the wood.

When the gap is full, we reach into the surrounding cushions of liverwort, tearing away thick, wet mats of green. We press the living moss into the warm, sticky amber, sealing it, binding the plant to the latex so that as the resin hardens into a glassy scar, the roots will sew the edges of the beam back together.

Underneath our hands, the indigo pulse of the Rot-Gazer begins to dim. The pain in the wood recedes from a sharp flare to a low, quiet hum.

The work is done for this shift of the fog.

I step back, turning my body until I am resting on the vertical sheer of the joint, looking out into the great central void of the bay. A mile away, across the empty, warm air, a Drift-Bell pulses its pale margin, dragging its long, feathery tendrils through a wisp of vapor, searching for tiny, seed-like flyers that have lost the updrafts. Below me, far down in the dim, greenish gloom, the long curtain-falls of condensation spill over the edge of a drainage trough, dropping silently into the unseen depths.

My skin is cool. My spiracles flutter, drinking the humid twilight.

I strike my knee once against the burl. *Thrum.*

Miles away, somewhere down the long, arching highways of the petrified sky, another chest is pressed against the bark, listening for the answer.
