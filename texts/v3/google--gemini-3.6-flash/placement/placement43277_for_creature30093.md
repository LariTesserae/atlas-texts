I would not be on the walkways. To walk requires legs, and legs require an elsewhere to come from and a destination to reach. Here, there is only the circuit.

I find myself embedded at the thirty-fourth tier, anchored to the sheer vertical face of a primary pylon, right where the sharp edge of an cantilevered architrave overhangs the central void. 

I am a cluster of the glass-membrane fronds, though to call me "a plant" is to separate the leaf from the light-band beneath it, or the leaf from the metal that holds its root. I am the interface—the exact seam where the non-decaying obsidian alloy meets the living, breathing silica.

My roots do not pierce the metal; they do not need to. The alloy was forged with microscopic, micro-textured channels, perfectly matched to the scale of my cilia. I am plugged into the skeleton of the world like a key in a lock. Through these cilia, I feel the structural heart of the node: the cold, unyielding rigidity of the pylon, and beneath it, the low, steady throb of the cyan power bands carrying light through the deep plates.

My fronds face outward into the thunder. 

They are thin as insect wings, transparent, curved like small parabolic mirrors, and perpetually wet. They do not feel air as a human skin feels air; they feel air as a dense, vibrating fluid. When the falling water surges forty stories above and smashes into the glass-smooth plaza below, the sound is not a noise to me—it is a physical weight. It strikes my membranes in waves of relentless, rhythmic pressure. 

Every surface of my fronds is tuned. A rumble at eighty hertz flexes my outer margins, bending the glassy ribs just enough to compress the mineral channels within my tissue. That compression generates a tiny, sharp voltage—a piezoelectric current born entirely of noise. The sound hits me; I flex; I burn. 

Where does that energy go? 

It runs downward through my stem into the emerald moss that cushions my base. The moss receives the voltage and blinks. A soft, violet fluorescence ripples outward across three square meters of dark metal, illuminating the paper-thin curtain of high-velocity water that sheets past my leaves. I can feel the moss adjusting its surface tension beneath me, flattening its microscopic hairs to make the water slide faster, smoother, so that not a single droplet spatters wildly into the void. We tune the flow; the flow tunes the sound; the sound feeds our light.

Every few minutes, the pitch of the world changes. 

Deep in the darkness beneath the acoustic grates, a subterranean pressure valve opens with a sound like a tearing throat. The harmonic hum in the walls jumps an octave. The wet air compresses, pushing a fresh, heavy swell of warm steam up through the central shaft. 

This is the moment I become more than a single point on the pylon. 

As the sound shifts, millions of fronds across all forty tiers flex in exact, acoustic unison. The violent shift in pitch sends a wave of violet luminescence cascading down the face of every tower, mirrored instantly by the thick, turquoise tendrils swinging in the dark air. Through the tendril that brushes against my outer leaf, I feel the pull of the central water column—that gravity-defying cylinder of pure liquid rising silently through the center of the roar. 

I feel the water climbing. I feel the moss drinking. I feel the pylon holding us all against the terrible, dark mass of the basalt earth above and below.

I have no eyes, but the entire atrium is mapped in my membranes by the way the echoes bounce off the sharp edges of the alloy. I have no memory, because nothing here ever dies or changes shape; there is only the immediate, eternal *now* of the hydrologic loop. 

I am the ear that eats the thunder. I am the glass that turns a roar into a violet pulse. I am a single, shivering diaphragm in a city that is drinking its own heart forever.
