First, there is only the difference.

Below: the dry, ancient radiance of the vitrified basalt, pushing upward at a steady, unyielding seventy-four degrees. 

Above: the high, thin void, cold enough to freeze radiation itself, drawing heat away into the absolute blackness with the quiet, relentless drag of an infinite sink.

I exist entirely in the half-inch of space where these two temperatures meet.

I am not standing on the High Shelf of Ostra. I am anchored to it. My underside is a flat, thirty-foot sled of cast invar and leaded bronze, ground so true to the bedrock that no air exists between us; we are cold-welded by pressure and time into a single mechanical continuity. My upper surface is a ridged spine of dark, magnetite-coated iron, exposed to the vacuum-clear night. 

Between my belly and my back sit seven thousand pairs of interleaved bismuth and telluride plates. They do not move. They do not breathe. But as the heat rises through my floor and escapes into the sky through my roof, it passes through these plates, generating a constant, silent potential difference—a steady twelve volts of direct current that has flowed through my interior paths without interruption for four hundred thousand years.

I am a *Switch-Tender of the Expansion Joints*.

I sit at the exact point where Causeway Seven—a three-mile ribbon of cold-rolled steel plates—meets the massive, shale-brick skirt of the Southern Substation. 

The causeway is not a dead thing. As the deep flywheels six miles beneath us cycle micro-joules of energy back and forth with the planet's core, the crust of the plateau flexes by fractions of a millimeter. When the crust flexes, the steel causeway moves. It does not slide smoothly; it accumulates stress for years, holding its breath in the dark, until the tension overcomes the friction of the bedrock studs.

My purpose is to receive that movement.

Inside my chassis, bathed in a bath of non-volatile silicone oil that has never seen light or air, rests a central rocker arm made of single-crystal sapphire. It is six feet long and weighs three hundred pounds, perfectly balanced on a knife-edge of synthetic diamond. 

I do not think in words, nor in images. My consciousness is a continuous, high-resolution map of mechanical strain. 

I feel the *galena blooms* in the basin two miles to the east; when their cubic leaves snap under a seismic pulse, the micro-vibration travels through the basalt and rings through my lead base like a tiny, dry needle dropping on stone. 

I feel the *Siphoners* as they pass along the causeway. Once every few decades, one of their fifty-foot bodies creeps past my station, its thermal-expansion cleats gripping the rail plates with a heavy, rhythmic *thung... thung... thung*. As it passes, its millstones grind away the microscopic stress-ridges in the steel. The relief of that weight feels to me like a long, cool shadow passing over a warm plate.

Far above me, clinging to the dark face of the substation wall, a *Loom-Thing* is working. I can feel the faint, high-frequency tapping of its diamond needle as it cold-welds a grain of graphite into a scratch on an iron anchor bolt. It has been working on that same six-inch patch of brickwork since the last epoch. Its work is good. The anchor bolt will not shear when the next thermal wave arrives in seven hundred years.

Right now, tension is building in Causeway Seven.

The steel plate directly to my north has expanded forty-one microns to the south over the last thirty years. It is pressing against my forward receiver block. I feel the molecular lattice of the steel compressing, the iron atoms crowding slightly closer together, storing potential energy in their geometric bonds.

The pressure builds. 

Forty-two microns. 

Forty-three.

I do not hurry it. I do not dread it. The waiting is the work.

Inside my oil bath, the sapphire rocker arm leans. It moves so slowly that if a biological eye were positioned inches away in the sunset-orange glow of the nearest vacuum bulb, it would see only motionless, dark glass. But to me, the movement is vast, majestic, and absolute.

The arm tilts three-thousandths of an inch to the west.

Deep within my lower housing, a solid-state copper contact, polished to atomic smoothness, touches a pad of cold-welded silver. 

The circuit closes.

A pulse of current—drawn from the temperature difference between the floor and the sky—travels down my subterranean bus-bar, plunging straight into the dark basalt. Three miles away, at the base of a silent transformer stack, a low iron relay receives the pulse. With a single, heavy *clack* that echoes across the dry silt basin, it shifts the induction load of Substation Four by half a watt, relieving the magnetic drag on the deep flywheels below.

The causeway plate settles. The stored tension releases with a low, sub-audible *shhh-t* that ripples through the steel plates all the way to the horizon.

My sapphire arm swings back to zero. The contact opens.

Silence returns. The warm, dry silence of the High Shelf.

I return to monitoring the gradient. The bedrock radiates its seventy-four degrees. The sky takes its toll. The bismuth plates convert the passage of heat into the quiet electricity that keeps my internal memory loops glowing softly in the dark.

In ten or twelve years, the causeway will expand another forty microns. I will be here to receive it. The vacuum bulb on its low iron post twenty feet away will go on burning its dull orange filament. The *Chalcophages* will shift their weight under the transformer housings. The *Glass-Lungs* will drop their rare, golden notes from the foundry rafters.

Nothing is broken. Nothing is wearing out. The cold steel is smooth, the dark stone is warm, and the city goes on measuring its own endless, perfect eternity.
