I am not at the extreme edges of this world—not up in the swaying, air-swept heights of the golden beard-lichen where the gliders leap, nor down in the heavy, silt-choked shadow of the lowest succulents where the old extractors drive their stone wedges. 

I find myself on Tier Nine, at the mid-level seam where the baked mud-brick terraces bite directly into the main trunk of a resin-thorn bush. It is a noisy, high-pressure junction: a crossroads of resin-paved footpaths, scarab ramps, and the thick, mahogany-barked limbs that serve as both structural pillars and living springs.

I am one of the Seam-Wrights. 

My work is the management of stress between things that grow and things that are built. Plants swell and contract with the heat of the day; the sandstone face holds its steady, deep warmth; the resin-mortar cures and hardens until it is brittle as amber. If someone does not tend the joints where the living wood meets the dried plaster, the thermal expansion will sheer a three-story dome right off the cliff-face before two sunsets pass.

To be here is to be entirely trapped in the present tense of touch.

My primary hands—the large, spatulate pair—are thick with dried tallow and grey sandstone dust. They are jammed elbow-deep into a trough of raw mortar, kneading together crushed mineral flour, plant-fat, and fine silk-fibers harvested from moth cocoons. The mixture is hot to the touch, working its way under my spatulate nails, smelling sharply of pine-gum, cinnamon, and the metallic tang of dry iron. 

My lower, secondary hands—smaller, delicate, perpetually agile—are busy elsewhere. Without my looking, they are threading a strand of cured sinew through a anchor-pin driven into the stone, pulling it taut, feeling for the exact tension that tells me the wall behind me is settling correctly. My fingers don’t think about this work; they *remember* it, the way a tongue remembers the shape of teeth.

I am resting on my heels, my prehensile toes clamped firmly into the tiny, pitted hand-holds carved into the butter-colored stone. My body is pressed low against the cliff, flat as an apricot skin laid out to dry. 

Above me, the world is a canopy of dry, papery rustling. A dwarf glider misses its landing on a terrace three tiers up, its thyme-colored fur brushing my shoulder as it catches itself on a vine-rope with a sharp *clack* of its claws. It doesn't apologize; it doesn't even look at me. It snaps its patagia shut, grabs a dust-moth mid-air with a papery *crunch*, and vanishes up into the dark amber haze of the lichen-curtains. 

Below me, a walnut-sized scarab is hauling a bucket of seed-pulp up the 80-degree ramp. Its chitinous knees make a dry *skritch-skritch* against the mud-plaster. As it passes my tier, I reach out with one primary hand to clear a wedge of abrasive quartz dust from its wing-case joint. I don't stop kneading the mortar to do it. The beetle doesn't pause either; it merely leans its heavy, armored shoulder against my leg as it passes, a brief, mutual recognition of weight and work.

I don't blink. My amber eyes stay wide, fixed on the hairline fracture opening up along the archway of a grain-silo. The dual nictitating membranes sweep across my vision—*click, click*—clearing away the fine gold silt carried on the convection currents that rise from the canyon mouth below. The air entering my slit-nostrils is so dry it feels like breathing crushed velvet, hot and thick with resin-steam.

In my chest, my heart beats with a terrifying, insectoid swiftness—a high-voltage thrumming that keeps my blood thick and salt-dense, burning through the lipid-milk I lapped at dawn. I am never thirsty. The thought of liquid water—cold, thin, running, wet—fills me with a deep, instinctual horror. Water is the great un-maker. Water is what turns our beautiful, dense, baked sanctuaries back into worthless mud and sloughs them down into the void. To be dry is to be permanent. To be oiled is to be safe.

A vibration hums through the stone behind my spine. 

*Tap. Tap-tap. Trrrr.*

It travels up through my heels, through my pelvic girdle, and into the base of my skull. It is the Master Builder three levels down, striking the cliff with a polished bone rod. 

*The wind is shifting outside the overhang,* the stone says. *The lower air is heating. Apply the heavy tallow to the western arches before the plaster bakes too fast.*

I don't speak back with my mouth—our voices are too light, too papery for the roar of this place. Instead, I take the heavy hardwood mallet tucked into my sinew-belt and strike the back-wall twice with the flat heel of my palm, then three times with the wood. *Received. Filling now.*

I scoop a mass of the steaming, sticky mortar into my lower hands, shift my grip on the stone with my feet, and flow upward along the vertical wall. 

There is no quiet here, no solitude, no space to stand back and admire the view. The view is three inches of sun-warmed, butter-yellow sandstone, six inches of resin-thorn wood, and the glowing, amber-lit back of my neighbor’s home. The sound is a deafening, comforting storm of scratching, clicking, singing, and tapping—the sound of ten thousand tiny, hot, waterless fires burning together in the dark, building a fortress out of dust before the shade moves and the day ends.
