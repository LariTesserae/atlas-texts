I would not be on the surface, where the white moss-vines form their tight vault against the grey sky, nor would I be among the broad-strided runners who carry message-taps along the high cedar pipes. 

I would be found on the seventh tier, in the dark narrow fold where the living larch-wall bows inward to hug the cold, weeping stone of the mountain.

I am *Seventh-Tier-North-Cambium*. My name is the place where my body rests, and my place is the work my hands remember even when my eyes are filmed over with frost.

***

In the hours before the mist thickens, I am wedged into a hollow between two ancient, load-bearing cedar roots that have grown as thick and black as river silt. My knees are pulled up into the hollow of my chest; my heavy mantle of compressed liverwort is tucked tight around my neck to catch the faint, humid warmth rising from the peat-bins three levels below.

My heart beats three times a minute. 

When I am in this torpor, I am not separate from the burg. The freezing moisture that trickles down the outer shale seeps through the bark-casing at my back, cold enough to turn the oil on my skin to a dense, grey tallow. I can feel, through the bony crest of my pelvis pressed hard against the heartwood, the vast, slow mechanics of the settlement. I feel the midnight contraction—the shuddering *thrum-crack* that travels down from the high canopy as three thousand yards of frozen timber shrink against the cold, drawing the upper rooms together like fingers making a fist against the storm.

Then, the morning sap-pulse moves. It is not a sudden thing. It is a thick, cold syrup shifting inside the vine-bridges above, a change in pressure that I feel first in the soles of my broad, boneless feet and then as a light, dry vibration behind my jaw slits.

The membrane sweeps sideways across my eyes, scraping away a thin crust of needle-ice. My cloud-grey irises dilate in the greenish gloom cast by the arch-lichen overhead. The frost on my knuckles melts into tiny, grease-slicked beads that run off without wetting my skin.

I uncoil. My joints make no sound; the thick lipid we secrete keeps even the small bones of the wrist sliding smoothly in the dark.

***

My work today is on the western archway of the seventh tier—a heavy, load-bearing spandrel of living larch that spans a forty-foot drop down into the lower peat-cisterns. 

Last night’s frost was hard. The mountain stone behind the wood expanded, shoving a jagged lip of ice-fractured shale two inches into the larch’s cambium layer. Left alone, the wood would split along its grain under the weight of the upper tiers; the frost would get into the wound, expand, and by winter’s heart, the entire north quadrant of the seventh level would sag into the dark.

I do not bring an axe. I do not bring iron.

I crouch on the iron-hard mat of creeping juniper root that forms the footway, my long forearms hanging low so the horn-spurs on my six fingers brush the wet bark. I can feel the tension in the wood before I reach it—a high, singing strain in the grain that travels through the soles of my feet.

I slide my palms into the crevice between the wood and the stone. My skin, slick with juniper-smelling lard, glides over the rough, wet cambium. I press the deep, deep barrel of my chest against the bark, feeling the cold, slow pulse of the tree’s inner moisture.

I lay my forehead against the larch. I open my mouth, but no air comes out.

From deep within my sternum, behind the heavy, thick-set ribs built for this thin air, I release the hum. 

It is a low, single-pitched vibration—a tone so deep it does not travel through the fog, but stays entirely within the wood. My collarbones rattle against my mantle. The grease on my cheeks trembles. 

Through the horn-spurs of my fingers, I press gently—not hard enough to bruise the bark, but with the steady, unyielding pressure of a wedge. I am telling the sap where to go. *Flow here. Avoid the stone-lip. Turn three degrees north, thicken the outer ring, build a knot where the shale bites.*

For three hours I do not move. The freezing mist coats my back in a fine, white fur, but beneath my mantle, my core stays stubborn and warm on a breakfast of cold, sweet peat-fungus and three swallows of bitter amber sap. 

Slowly, under the micro-vibration of my chest and the steady warmth of my lard-slicked palms, the living wood responds. The sap-flow shifts. The cambium swells sideways, growing *around* the sharp edge of the mountain rather than fighting it, wrapping the stone in a thick, scar-like collar of dense, cold-hardened timber.

When I lift my hands, the bark is warm to my touch—heated by its own sudden, directed effort.

***

In the late afternoon, when the dim twilight fades toward black, a rhythmic tapping echoes through the cedar water-pipe beside my head.

*Tock. Tock-tock. Tock.*

I lay the flat of my palm against the pipe. The wood is wet, slick with ice-water running down from the upper moss-vaults. The message comes through the grain, sharp and clear against my skin:

*Duct-Keeper-Above-the-Shale has stopped.*

I do not feel sorrow. Sorrow is a lowlander’s emotion, warm and wet and wasteful like ungreased skin in a blizzard. 

I lift my hands and tap back against the hollow wood with the horn-spur of my left thumb: *I am coming down.*

I slide down the vine-footways, my broad soles gripping the supple, anti-freeze-coated lianas without slipping. Down through the middle tiers, where the air grows thicker, damper, and smells of ancient, fermenting needles. Down past the tightly packed moss-houses where the young sleep wedged in their root-recesses. Down to the very bottom, where the wooden burg meets the dark, shatter-faced shale in the lightless cellars.

Here, the air is warmer—a muggy, heavy cold generated by the slow decay of millions of pounds of wet plant-mass.

*Duct-Keeper* lies on a low bed of compressed peat-brick beside the main structural arch of the foundation. He is very old; his wrists have turned thick and white with mineral deposits, and the grease-glands behind his ears have dried to grey crusts. His pale eyes are open, covered by their clear membranes, looking at the dark larch ceiling he maintained for eighty winters.

Two others are already there. We do not speak. The sound of our voices would only be lost in the wet peat walls.

We wrap him tightly in fresh, cold liverwort pulled from the aqueduct linings. We use long bone-splints to pin his six-jointed fingers flat against the base of the sagging archway—his hands spread wide, his horn-spurs driven deep into the soft cambium of the foundation wood.

I take a wooden bucket of wet peat-paste, rich with white mycelium spores, and begin to smear it over his grey, rubbery chest, his deep-ribbed torso, his quiet, wedge-shaped face. 

As I rub the cold sludge into his skin, I feel the calcified knots in his forearm bones—dense, heavy bone built by decades of absorbing the cold mineral water that trickles off the mountain wall. 

In three winters, the fungus will have consumed the lipids of his flesh. In ten, the larch root beside him will have drunk the nitrogen of his blood and wrapped its white tendrils around his spine. In fifty, his dense, mineralized skeleton will be a petrified brace at the very corner of the city, holding three hundred tons of timber and living flesh off the sharp shale below.

I press my grease-coated forehead against his blanched cheek one last time, feeling the final, residual cold of his body merging with the cold of the mountain.

Then I turn, place my bare feet onto the juniper roots, and begin the long climb back up to the seventh tier, where the larch wood is waiting for my hands.
