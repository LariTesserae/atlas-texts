First, there is no *I*. There is only the weight.

It presses from above with the cold, dead mass of an unremembered sky, and it pushes from the sides like the shoulder of a subterranean giant moving in its sleep. The fluid does not flow over skin; it scrubs. It is a thick, mineral-laden paste that heavy-hands everything it touches, dragging ground quartz and basalt flour across every surface in an endless, rhythmic scouring.

To exist here is to be low. Height is an error that the water corrects immediately, snapping anything that dares to reach upward and grinding it into silt. So I am broad. I am flat.

As the awareness coalesces, the body follows the shape of the physical necessity. I am a wedge. My front is a wide, sloping spade of thick, horn-hard keratin, smooth as a river-polished slate and angled so that the terrible hydraulic ram of the current does not lift me, but pinches me down, driving my belly harder into the grey mud terraces.

I have no eyes, because light here is not an image; it is an occasional, bruised spark born of violence. Farther up the slope, where two multi-ton cobbles smash together in the dark, a faint violet flash bleeds through the suspended clay—triboluminescence, the dying gasp of quartz under pressure. I do not look at it. I feel its heat-less vibration strike the thousands of microscopic pits that ring my broad snout.

My world is not seen; it is felt as a map of acoustic shadows and kinetic mass.

To my left, three hundred meters away, a field of smooth basalt hummocks breaks the great current, casting long, quiet lanes of sluggish mud behind them. I can hear those hummocks. They do not make sound themselves, but they alter the low, bone-shaking hum of the basin. They are deep, soft silence-shapes in a sea of infrasonic roaring. Rooted to their lee sides, the black tendon-cords vibrate like the low strings of a cello, humming in resonance with the water's heavy pulse. Their music is thick, greasy with mucus, calling down the micro-fungi.

Above me, the slurry thickens. A shadow passes—not a darkness of light, but a sudden, massive displacement of pressure that pushes my slate-backed body half an inch deeper into the silt. A Silt-Plow is moving through the mid-water, its vast, boneless hull undulating through the wet concrete of the deep, leaving a wake of turned clay and churned mineral flour. I hold still. I let the grit skitter off my overlapping dorsal plates. *Shhh-shhh-shhh.* It sounds like heavy rain against a stone roof, felt directly through the calcified lamellae of my spine.

When the pressure-wake settles, I slide forward.

My movement is slow, a muscular ripple running from the broad spade of my head down to the dense, flat paddle of my tail. I do not swim; I skate along the boundary layer, the thin ribbon of water right against the riverbed where friction has stolen just enough speed from the current to make survival possible.

My mouth opens beneath me—a wide, horny slit flush with the sediment. I scoop.

The silt here is cold, dense, and rich with the decay of things that died in a world I will never know—organic dust that took months to fall from whatever lies far above the ice-roof, mixed with the sloughed, nutrient-rich mucus of the tendon-cords and the greasy carpets of piezotrophic mold. The mud fills my gullet, heavy and metallic. Inside my throat, thick muscular plates compress the slurry, wringing out the micro-fauna and the organic sludge, passing the cleansed, sterile clay through my length until it extrudes behind me in long, dense ropes that settle back into the dark.

I am a cleaner of stone. A mouth that turns mud into quietness.

I drift farther into the lee of a massive basalt ridge, seeking a place where the water slows even more. The infrasonic roar of the basin becomes a muffled thrumming. Here, the cobbles are smaller, wedged tight into ancient frost-wedges and tectonic cracks.

My snout bumps against something that does not belong to the natural geography of the trough.

It is stone, yes—dark, dense basalt—but it is not round. In a world where every edge has been licked smooth by eons of mineral friction, this object possesses a line. A straight, deliberate ridge, flaked by hands that ceased to exist before the basin was fully drowned. A hand-axe, wedged deep into a basalt joint, jammed there to hold a position against a rising tide five thousand years ago.

I press my broad, blind spade against it. The stone tool is cold, calcified, locked into the bedrock like a tooth.

Behind the blade, in the tiny, triangular crevice formed by its ancient placement, the violent slurry cannot enter. The current snaps over the exposed edge of the axe, leaving a pocket of water no larger than a man’s thumb that has not moved in millennia.

I bring the sensitive, pit-studded margin of my head close to the gap.

Inside that tiny sanctuary, suspended in the absolute still, hangs a single glass hydroid. It is impossibly thin, translucent as distilled ice, its delicate, hair-like tentacles extended into the motionless dark. A single grain of quartz, carried on a rogue eddy, would smash it to pieces. The great roar of the rolling boulders, the crushing weight of the silt-plows, the sandblasting slurry—all of it rages three inches away. But here, behind the forgotten edge of a human stone, the glass creature floats in an eternal, fragile peace.

I do not disturb it. My heavy, slate-armored body is too rough, too thick with the basin's violence. If I brushed it, my calcified hide would shred its transparent life in an instant.

I alter the angle of my tail, catch a stray eddy of the heavy current, and push myself backward, away from the crack.

I turn my broad snout back toward the open terrace, toward the endless fields of egg-smooth cobbles, where the quartz sparks violet in the dark and the boulders roll with the dull, deadened thuds of a world that refuses to stop grinding.

I sink my heavy belly into the grey silt, open my broad mouth to the mineral paste, and continue to feed.
