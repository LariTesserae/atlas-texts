I am under the belly of the western beam engine, where the granite retaining wall meets the three-foot mattress of roots and oak deck. 

To exist here is not to see the basin first, but to feel it through the bone of the jaw and the flat, calloused undersides of my thighs. The world is eight beats every minute. *Chug-shuck... chug-shuck.* It comes up through the wet moss, through the iron grates, through the dense, iron-infused timber, and sets the water in my blood to the same heavy, patient cadence. 

I am a Tender, but here, in the dark behind the flywheel pit, the distinction between what I am and what the machinery is feels thin, like wet canvas stretched over iron ribs. 

My body is broad and low-slung. My skin is the color of a wet walnut, sealed against the constant condensation by a fine, permanent sheen of tallow and oil-mist that smells of crushed ginger and cold iron. I have no need for speech; my ears are thick and dull, deadened long ago by the continuous thunder of the short-stepped spillways forty yards to the south. Instead, I read the basin with my hands, my knees, and the bare soles of my feet.

Where am I right now? 

I am crouched on a knee in the shadow of the low-pressure cylinder housing. The air here is ninety-five degrees and thick enough to chew. Above me, the massive cast-iron beam, heavy as a fallen oak, nods its slow, twelve-foot arc. Every time the flywheel dips into its trough, a fine spray of warm, lubricated drainage water washes over my left shoulder and catches the broad, leathery leaves of the wild monstera growing beside me. The leaves slap against the riveted iron plate with a wet, rhythmic *clack-clack*, mirroring the stroke of the piston.

Between my knees is a main feed-pipe flange. It is weeping. Not a failure—nothing here fails violently—but a small, persistent jet of white steam is escaping from a worn gasket ring, singing a sharp, high-pitched *hiss* that cuts through the bass thud of the cylinder. 

My hands move without hurry. I carry no iron tools; iron sparks, and iron bruises the wet wood. On my hip hangs a thick bronze crescent knife and a leather pouch filled with boiled ginger-hemp fibers, spun thick as a finger.

Beside my right ankle, resting on the warm, verdigris-coated flange of the sump pipe, is an iron-backed salamander. It is ten inches long, dark as wet basalt, its belly pressed flush against the hot metal. We do not disturb each other. Its throat quivers in time with the cylinder’s exhaust cocks; it is waiting, as I am, for the heat to shift. 

I reach into the crevice where the ficus root wraps around the steam line. The tree has sent a tender, pale-green aerial shoot—no thicker than a quill—down toward the weeping flange. It wants the moisture, but if it grows straight into the jet, the live steam will scorch the tip black. 

I take the young root gently between two fingers. It is cool, firm, and slick with sap. I loop it once around the bronze tie-rod above the pipe, securing it with a knot of wet hemp, steering its growth so that in three months’ time, as the wood thickens and turns gray, it will form a living brace directly across the pipe joint. It will press the brass flanges together under tons of slow, biological force, sealing the leak better than any bolt could.

While I work, two iron-shelled snails slide across the back of my left hand, leaving a cold, dark streak of graphite paste on my knuckles. They are headed toward the hot spot near the drain cock where the sulfur-yellow grease-molds are thickest. I let them move over me. Their shells clink like small pebbles against my bone wrist-guard.

I am not a caretaker in a garden, nor am I a mechanic in a factory. I am a valve. I am a tendon. I am the part of the basin that notices when a vibration strays from eight beats a minute to eight and a quarter. 

If the oil-slick swifts up in the canopy stop diving through the steam plumes, I know a boiler cock is choked with scale. If the loaches in the brick sluices crowd near the western sump, I know the cylinder oil has gone rancid in the lubricating troughs and needs to be cleared with fresh tallow. 

When my work at the flange is done, I do not stand up. Standing is for the catwalks, where the air is thinner and smells of the sky outside the walls—a cold, dry sky I have no curiosity about. Down here, on the mattress of roots and rotting oak, the air is dense and safe. 

I lean back against the warm brickwork of the boiler foundation, resting my head against the damp moss. The vibration of the 12-foot flywheel rolls through my spine. The steam rises in glowing, pearlescent columns into the filtered sunlight above, and beneath the root-carpet, the warm water flows down toward the dark, deep sumps. 

I close my eyes. The iron thuds. The roots expand by a fraction of a hair. The snails scrape the scale. And I breathe the hot, wet air of the basin, knowing exactly where the iron ends, and where I begin—nowhere at all.
