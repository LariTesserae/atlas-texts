The first thing that exists is not sight, but weight and heat—two distinct temperatures that meet inside my chest. 

From above comes the dry, crushing press of the zenith, felt through the dense shroud of vapor as a broad, white pressure on the back of my shoulders. From below, through the soles of my hooves and the hardened keratin of my knees, comes the violent, wet surge of the boiling subterranean currents, pushing up through six feet of travertine and two inches of riveted iron plate.

I am not standing, nor am I walking. I am wedged in the elbow where a thirty-story copper condensation pylon anchors into the wall of a deep settling vat. 

My body is long, pale, and slow. I can feel the smooth, oily sheen of my skin repelling the continuous fallout of scalding droplets from the lattice above. The air here tastes thick and sharp—first of wet zinc, then of dark, bitter sulfur that coats the back of my throat like warm oil. I do not breathe to draw air into lungs of soft sponge; my chest expands to draw steam through lateral spiracles, cooling the internal stills where copper and iron precipitate out of the water I drank three cycles ago at the lower fissure.

I am a Steward, but I am no longer entirely mobile. 

My right leg, folded beneath me against the basalt pillar, has begun to set. Over the last forty seasons, the mineral water cascading from the pylon’s drain-lip has washed over my ankle, then my shin, filling the microscopic pores of my flexor tendons with silica and white calcite. The joint no longer bends; it has become a buttress. Where my skin meets the stone, there is no longer a seam. The travertine has climbed my hip in pale, ribbed tiers, binding me to the foundation of the vat. 

I do not fight this. It is the natural stiffening that comes when the iron in the blood settles into the marrow. I am becoming architectural.

My left hand—four joints to each long, ivory finger—rests flat against the main brine conduit, a steel pipe as wide as a room, pulsing with pressurized stream-flow. My chisel-tipped nails are buried a quarter-inch deep into a thick encrustation of yellow sulfur and rust that threatens to bind the primary pressure-relief valve. Automatically, without urgency, my index finger scrapes the crust away. The sound is small and dry: *srrrt, srrrt*. The valve breathes—a long, wet sigh of vapor that washes over my face plate, warming the heat-sensitive pits along my brow.

Through my palm, I read the city.

The pipeline is my sensory organ. Five miles to the east, a Silt-Licked has died in the middle of a high sluice; I feel the slight, drag-heavy alteration in the water’s fluid dynamics as its shell cements itself to the channel bed. Above me, forty yards up the copper lattice, three Lace-Fingers are moving in their glass-jointed rhythm, their metallic threads hum-singing as the wind catches them. I feel the microscopic vibration of their silk through the structural rivets—a high-pitched, metallic shivering that runs down the pylon into my shoulder.

And beneath me—far down in the dark, mineral-choked belly of the vat—the Siphonophores are pulsing. 

I can feel their bio-galvanic tide rising through the damp stone. It is a slow, thick current, a low-frequency hum that vibrates in my teeth and along the calcifying spine in my back. They draw the heat from the deep earth; I hold the valve that keeps their vat from boiling dry; the Lace-Fingers catch the steam we release and drop it back down as purified dew. 

We are not separate entities working in a landscape. We are the system’s nervous tissue, its valves, its scaffolding, and its sediment.

I raise my free hand—heavy, pale, tipped with an iron ring grown directly out of the bone of my middle knuckle—and strike the copper girder beside me. 

*THUMP.*

The sound does not travel through the air; the heavy steam smothers it before it can reach the opposite terrace. But the vibration strikes deep into the iron frame. It travels down the viaduct, across the obsidian sheets, through two miles of wet causeway, down into the deep battery vaults where the oldest of my kind sit sealed in ivory relief against the walls.

I wait. 

Three seconds later, the answer returns through the stone beneath my grounded knee. A double tap—*tink-tink*—from a Steward anchored at the secondary condensation node leagues away. The line is clear. The brine is flowing. The sun overhead continues its blinding, silent burn.

I draw my long fingers back from the valve, nestle my head into the curve of the travertine step behind me, and let the warm, mineral-laden spray wash over my chest. I will stay here for another century, watching the sulfur build, scraping the iron clean, listening to the hum of the core, until the stone reaches my chin and my voice becomes entirely iron.
