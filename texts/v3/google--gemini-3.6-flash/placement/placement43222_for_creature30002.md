I am tucked into the curve of a granite flute, three hand-spans above a condensation bowl that never fills and never dries. 

My knees are pulled high against my chest, locked in place by the stiff fold of a grazer-hide hood pulled low over my eyes. Even under the thick, sun-cured hide, the glare is not gone; it leaks through the dry pores of the parchment as a dim, orange heat pressed hard against my eyelids. I am in the two-hour stillness—not asleep, for sleep is a soft thing born of darkness, but idle, turned inward, letting the thick syrup of my blood cool down against the stone.

I am one of the people, but in this moment I am mostly a throat and an ear.

My forehead is pressed flat against the bare, polished slate of the channel wall. Through the bone of my brow, the mountain is speaking its single, endless word: a low, resonant thrum that rattles the teeth in my gums and vibrates the small, dry fluid-chambers deep inside my inner ear. It is the tone of the thermal stream far above, caught in the throat of the upper peaks. It never rises. It never falls. It is the floor upon which every thought I have ever had is built.

Layered over that deep hum, through the skin of my belly where it touches my thighs, comes the lesser rhythm: the wet, steady *slap-scrape* of a bone-walker herd three hundred paces out on the shale. They are moving west—or what we call west, toward the deeper flutes where the seep-water carries more sulfur. I can feel the soft, hydraulic *thud* of their broad hooves through the rock every four heartbeats. A single hind leg striking slate. A question sent through the floor. Another leg answering from the edge of the amphitheater.

Beside me, wedged into the same narrow shadowless groove, is the small body of my sister’s child. 

She does not have her hood down. She is too young to sit still under the parchment for long; she is still fascinated by the way the white light strikes her grease-coated forearms, turning the black pigment of her skin into something that shines like wet flint. 

She has her left palm flat on the stone between us and her right hand raised at waist level. Her fingers move in the swift, stiff semaphore of our kind—short, clipped gestures that do not waste breath:

*The beetles are loud.* (A quick double-flick of the index finger against the thumb, mimicking the friction of spiracles.)

*The basin is cold.* (A flat palm turned down, pressed brief and hard against the thigh.)

I do not open my eyes. I do not lift my hood. I reach out from under the hide and place two fingers against the side of her neck, right where her jaw meets the thick muscle of her throat. I hum a single, low frequency into my own chest—a vibration that travels down my arm, through my knuckles, and directly into her neck-bones:

*Still.*

She settles. Her small, dense body presses closer into the granite curve. I feel the warmth of her through the grease-enamel on her skin—a dry, dense heat that smells faintly of boiled tallow and the iron-bitter dust of crushed lichen.

In my right hand, resting on the slate between my feet, is my tool. It is not the jasper hammer or the volcanic glass, but a wedge of pale bone, four fingers long, rubbed smooth at one end and notched at the other. It is an instrument for gathering: used to scrape the silver beetle-grease from the granite grooves without dulling its edge against the rock, and to lift the thin, paper-like sheets of sun-cured meat from the drying-slabs without tearing them.

I have no name for this hour, because there are no hours. The sun above my hood is in the exact spot it occupied when my mother pressed her throat to my ribs to teach me the pulse of the herd, and it will be in the exact spot when my own bones are wedged into a deep crevice to turn grey and dry as slate. 

I feel the cold sweat forming at the base of my skull, instantly turning to a thin, brittle salt-crust under the grease. I do not wipe it away. The salt is part of the seal. 

Far out on the shelf, a flyer drops from a high ledge—I know it not by sight, but by the sudden, sharp drop in the beetle-screech as a dozen silver shells are crushed in an instant, a brief gap in the high metallic hiss that the mountain instantly fills with its own bass roar.

I pull my hood a thumb’s-width lower. My lungs expand—slow, wide, taking in the thin, iron-tasted air that burns the back of my throat—and then collapse with the steady patience of bellows. 

I am here, in the middle of the stone, where the light hits hardest and nothing rots. I am cold, I am full of raw salt-meat, and I am listening to the earth hum through my head.
