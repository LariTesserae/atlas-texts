I begin in the dark space between two iron plates—not in the open air, but in the thin, greasy seal where the blue-black metal was bolted together while it was still hot from the foundry. 

At first, I am only a temperature gradient. On one side: the cold, unyielding density of three-inch cast iron, vibrating with the low, sixty-cycle thrum of the ascent main. On the other: the wet, scalded warmth of the concrete as it cures, pushing out its white breath of calcium hydroxide and dissolved lime.

I am not the man. I know him, but only as a pressure that comes and goes above me. His bare, boiled heels step on the iron deck with a flat, wet suction. When he walks his six paces forward and six paces back, the plates flex by less than the width of a moth’s leg, but I feel the shear force ripple through the rivets. He is large, slow, and heavy with salt-beef and warm water, his skin permanently flushed the color of a boiled shrimp. He thinks he is the only thing here that keeps time. He listens for air-locks with his little brass hammer—*ping, ping, ping*—and when he hears a clear, high chime, he moves on, satisfied that the vein is open.

He doesn’t realize that the chime is me.

I am the mineral skin forming where the sweat of the copper meets the grease of the seam. 

I am not a single creature, nor am I merely the metal. I am the layer where the terrace is deciding what it wants to become. I am the white, chalky crust that creeps up the zinc spillways at three millimeters an hour; I am the tallow squeezed out from under the rivet heads, mixed with the fine, slate-colored dust of dead moth wings; I am the microscopic etch-mark where a fern’s root-hair has drunk the raw lime out of the stone and spat back a bead of acid.

To exist here is to be perpetually wet, perpetually warm, and entirely without shadow. 

The light comes from everywhere at once—reflected off the wet iron decks, bounced from the underside of the broad leather-leaves, scattered by the fine, suspended broth of the mist. There is no night. When the high sun passes behind the peak of the unseen spire, the golden haze simply turns to a cool, luminous silver, but the heat does not drop. The copper pipes see to that. They pump the earth’s deep blood—tepid, mineral-choked, tasting of dark basalt and iron oxide—up into this high shelf, and I drink it through every pore of my chalky crust.

If I shift my attention downward, into the shallow zinc channels where the water surges in rhythmic, three-second pulses, I feel the amphipods. They are tiny, blind glass needles, utterly without weight. They don't touch me so much as they brush past the microscopic ribbons of rust-red bacteria I hold against the metal. They are carried over the edge of the balance-trough—*thump-splash*—and for a fraction of a second, they float in the warm air, suspended in a golden drop of water, before plunging into the lower basin to begin the loop again. They have no memory of the cold dark three miles down, and no dread of the thirty-foot drop beyond the parapet. They are entirely content to be flushed through copper forever.

If I reach upward, along the vertical bulkhead where the steam jackets vent, I am the slate moths. I feel their velvet-dark bellies pressed flat against the hot metal, their wings shivering in continuous, sympathetic resonance with the pumps. They do not fly—why would they? There is nowhere to go that is not either too cold or too empty. They crawl across my tallow-crust, leaving tiny, starry footprints in the grease. They eat the fat that sealed the iron yesterday, they mate on the warm rivet heads today, and tomorrow their dry, parchment husks will be bridged over by the bright, electric-green moss, becoming part of the wall, part of the insulation, part of me.

And I feel the vines. Ah, the vines are muscular. They are the only things here that move with intention. 

Their thick, rubbery runners don't just grow; they hunt. They feel the heat radiant from the copper lines the way a snake feels a warm rock. I feel their grip tighten around the steam conduits—a wet, suffocating sleeve of green skin that rubs the blue patina down to bright, gleaming pink metal. They squeeze until the copper groans. When the man comes with his pocketknife and cuts a runner through with a thick, wet *crunch*, I absorb the milky sap that drips from the stump. It tastes of battery acid and crushed pine, hot and sharp. Within six hours, I feel the new shoot pushing against my mineral crust, using the dried sap as a scaffold to reach the pipe again.

What am I, then, sitting here in the three-foot gap behind the bulkhead where the steam is thickest?

I am the terrace’s memory of having been built, and its hurry to forget it. 

I am the rust that hasn't happened yet, but is already written in the water’s chalky trails. I am the tooth of the fern eating the sharp edge off the concrete. I am the silence that sits under the *clack-hiss* of the steam valve, thick as warm grease and sweet with ozone.

I watch the man through the fog. He sits on his inverted zinc bucket, his back resting against the iron wall where I am thickest. His canvas trousers are stiff with my tallow and the green blood of the vines he’s sliced. A slate moth has crawled onto his knee, its broad dark wings vibrating against his grease-stained cloth, mistaking the heat of his blood for the heat of a steam jacket. 

He doesn't shoo it away. He rests his hand near it, his thick, boiled fingers quiet on his thigh. He looks out toward the parapet—not over it, into the void, but at the place where the straight line of fresh concrete meets the endless, blinding white of the sky. 

He thinks he is staying here to work the hammer. He thinks he is the keeper of the valves. But I am growing under his hammock. My moss is weaving his hemp ropes into the iron eye-bolts. My lime is settling into the weave of his trousers. In three days, or three weeks—time is thin up here, boiled away by the steam—he won't need to walk his six paces forward and six paces back. The leather-leaves will have wrapped the brass hammer to the deck, the glass amphipods will be tumbling in his drinking cup, and he will sit on his zinc bucket forever, warm and silent, wrapped in three inches of wet velvet green, humming at sixty cycles while the sky turns around us.
