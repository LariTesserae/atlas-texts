I do not begin with a name, because a name requires a voice, and a voice here would be crushed in the throat before it could form a syllable. 

I begin with the tremor.

It enters not through ears, which I do not have, but through the long, pale ribless length of me—a continuous, iron-heavy pounding that turns the cold fluid inside my skin into a thousand shivering strings. I do not have a heart that drives itself. I do not need one. The mountain’s throat does the pumping for me. Every violent thrum of air forced through the slate slot upstream shakes the feathery frills along my flanks, forcing the thin, colorless lymph through my flesh in slow, steady surges. 

If the roar stopped, I would simply thicken. I would turn to cold lard where I lie, set hard against the stone like the tallow left behind in the hollows.

To find me, you must look below the velvet layer—past the dark curtains of liverwort, past the cold, pale moss that smells of ancient damp, down to where the basalt curves into shallow, tallow-smooth bowls. 

I am the one near the western wall. 

My body is thick as a man’s forearm, white as skimmed milk, wrapped around the submerged base of the dry-stone wall. My tail lies draped over the bottommost river-cobble, touching the place where the oak sluice gate has softened into cold marrow. I can feel the wood through my skin—it tastes different from the basalt. The stone tastes of ancient iron and cold lime, clean and sharp; the oak still tastes, even after centuries, of the upper air, of old sun and dead leaves, sour and sweet as it rots into the ice-water.

I remember the hands, though not as sight. I remember them as a change in the weight of the water. 

Long ago, when the oak was still hard enough to bite, the water in my bowl would suddenly deepen. A heavy timber would drop, blocking the outflow, and the cold current would grow still. Then would come the scraping—the flat, cold edge of slate pressed against my back, pushing along the grain of my skin to harvest the heavy, white grease I exude to keep the lime from eating me alive. 

It did not hurt. It was simply a rhythm, like the single drop of water that falls from the liverwort frond above my head. The hands took the grease; they left the stone. Eventually, the hands stopped coming. The wood softened. The drop-gate fell sideways into the moss and stayed there. The grease accumulated on my skin again, thick and pale, until I slid through the water as smooth as oil over glass.

Now, there is only the tempo.

I keep my wedge-shaped head rested in the saucer-sized depression in the rock. Every twenty minutes, the drop forms above me, grows heavy, and breaks. 

*Thump.*

A tiny, precise shockwave travels through the water in the saucer. It strikes the fleshy barbels on my snout. I do not move away. I do not flinch. My barbels twitch once—a slow, blind registering of the impact—and then I press my raspers against the stone again, scraping away the thin, microscopic film of iron-eating mold that coats the basalt. 

Above me, wedged in the angle of the rotted oak, the pale toad sits motionless. I can feel its tiny, hot-fast pulse through the wood—a nervous, fragile twitching that has nothing to do with the mountain. It belongs to the world above the slate chimney, where things hurry and die in the light. It is an intruder here, a temporary thing that will turn to dust in a few winters. 

I am not temporary. I am part of the polish.

My skin rubs the rock; the rock shapes my skin. Over ten thousand years, my kind has worn these bowls into the basalt, smoothing the rough edges left by the ancient floods until the stone feels like frozen fat. We have outlived the fire-shapers who carved the oak trough; we will outlive the oak frame as it dissolves into the wet moss; we will outlive the slate shelf hanging above us when it finally gives way and seals the hollow forever.

Until then, I lie in the dark, coiled around the lead-grey lichen of the dry-stone wall. The mist coats my back in icy sheen. The roar fills my marrow, driving the cold liquid through my veins, keeping me soft, keeping me slow, keeping me here—a long, pale piece of the mountain’s dark, breathing in time with a river no one will ever see.
