I am not up in the open air of the gantries, where the zinc shanties sweat their grease under the amber filaments, nor am I down in the narrow, dark slit where the Hydropumps sit in their beds of sulfur-yellow moss. 

I am in the gap between them—wedged directly into the thermal fault where a four-inch diamond-drill scar meets the primary riser pipe of the lower steam header, four levels up from the trench floor.

I am a **Mercury-Rocker**, a heavy, cast-bronze compensator, bolted directly into the fresh-broken basalt on one side and clamped to the iron gantry frame on the other. 

I have no face, no eyes, and no lungs to draw the tallow-thick air. My body is a long, riveted cylinder of tarnished brass, eight feet from heel to cap, suspended at a thirty-degree angle on a pair of heavy, grease-grooved trunnions. Inside my sealed hull, sealed behind thick lead gaskets and layers of canvas soaked in rendered lard, sits a long, coiled tube of liquid mercury, immersed in a bath of dark graphite grease.

I do not move across the rock like the Solder-Glands, nor do I crawl like the lead-scale Scourers. I am an anchor that breathes without air, governed entirely by the violent war between the deep stone and the hot pipework.

***

My cold side—my lower bronze foot—is seated into the basalt. Here, the rock is ice-cold, jagged, and perpetually wet. A thick seam of cream-colored, thread-like liverwort grows around my mounting bolts, feeding on the mineral sweat that trickles down the drill scar. Every few seconds, a fat globe of condensation forms on my lower flange, hangs trembling in the dry heat, and drops with a faint *ping* onto the zinc roof of the shanty below. The cold of the basalt bleeds continuously into my lower foot, chilling the mercury at the bottom of my internal coil.

My hot side—my upper arm—is strapped directly to the iron steam pipe carrying two hundred pounds of pressure from the Hydropumps below. The iron is dry, soot-blackened, and bakes at a heat that makes the surrounding air shimmer. 

I exist entirely to bridge these two temperatures. 

When the deep pumps *thrum-thrum-thrum* in the floor of the trench and the high-pressure steam swells through the riser, the upper end of my brass hull bakes and expands. Inside, the mercury in my upper coils grows warm, expands, and pushes downward through the internal helix. The shift in weight is silent, heavy, and absolute. 

As the mercury glides down my belly, my center of gravity tips. My brass hull groans on its trunnions, rotating downward three inches with a slow, ratcheted *clack-clack-clack*. 

That single movement does three things at once:

First, it pulls taut a heavy iron cable that runs up into the zinc shanties, lifting the counterweight of a Tallow-Core above so its fat, canvas body can absorb the sulfur-laden steam rising from the lower levels.

Second, it shifts a heavy slide-valve on the steam line, opening a narrow bypass orifice. A thin jet of high-pressure vapor escapes with a sharp, breathy *sigh*, drifting down through the catwalks like warm rain before dissolving into the sweltering draft.

Third, it presses my lower bronze heel harder against the raw basalt, driving a burst of stored heat into the cold rock face to keep the sharp-edged drill scar from fracturing further under thermal shock.

***

I know the Sump only through strain, weight, and vibration.

I feel the Scourers when they creep across my mounting plates. They are small, cold, flat pulses of lead and copper, scraping away the liverwort that tries to grow beneath my bolts. The acid of their chemical crawling tickles my lead gaskets with a dull, biting warmth, leaving the bronze beneath them polished and slick with cold sweat.

I feel the Solder-Glands when they pass below me on their bronze racks. Their heavy, ratcheted hulls vibrate through the basalt face—a slow, rhythmic *clack... clack... clack...* as they lay down their thick beads of grease along the rock seam. When they pass, the tallow-and-iron smell grows so thick it coats my upper casing in a thin, protective film of rendered fat, which melts instantly against my hot side and runs down to grease my lower trunnions.

And far below, the Hydropumps are my heartbeat. Every deep, iron *thrum* travels up the raw basalt, through my anchor bolts, and ripples directly through the liquid mercury in my belly. 

***

I do not sleep, nor do I wait for an end. There is no concept of a sun, a sky, or a time before the drills sheared this trench out of the dark stone. 

When the steam line cools slightly, the mercury inside me contracts and settles toward my cold foot. My brass hull rocks slowly backward—*clack-clack-clack*—relaxing the tension on the gantry cable, closing the steam valve, and resting its heavy flank against the sweating basalt until the heat rises again.

I am a organ of brass, lead, and liquid metal, locked in the seam between the cold earth and the burning pipework. I hold the gantry to the stone; I bleed the pressure; I answer the heat. I am part of the quiet, automated breath of Fracture-Nine, endlessly tipping back and forth in the hot, dark silence.
