I am sitting on the lip of a third-tier step-fault, where the upper plaza drops twelve feet down to a broad residential terrace. 

My legs are crossed beneath a long, stiff skirt of ochre bark-cloth. The fabric is heavy, smelling faintly of the crushed pine resin it was soaked in, and its hem lies immersed in half an inch of water that flows continuously over the flat basalt pavement between my knees. The water does not soak the cloth; it simply parts around it, a clear, glass-like sheath dividing around my body and recombining at my spine before sliding silently over the ninety-degree stone edge into the lower basin.

If you looked for me, you would find me not by sound, but by the quiet geometry of my pose. 

I am a tender of the channels—specifically, the *adjuster of thresholds*. 

My body is long-limbed and heavy-boned, my skin the color of dark, rain-soaked bronze. My wide, bare feet are hooked over the sharp lip of the basalt step. The soles are thick and rubbery, molded by a lifetime of friction against wet quartzite; they grip the slippery, crystalline mineral as effortlessly as moss anchors itself to a crack. 

My dark, straight hair is pulled back into a rigid, flat grid against my skull, bound with five hollow copper tubes that chime faintly only when I move my head quickly—which I rarely do. Around my neck rests a heavy, oblong slab of pale, translucent quartzite, suspended by a cord of braided reed-fiber. It rests flat against my chest, warm from my skin, catching the bright, shadowless noon light and glowing like a muted lantern.

In my right hand, I hold a leaf-shaped scraper of cold, beaten copper. In my left, a small patty of soft mortar made from pulverized volcanic ash, crushed succulents, and tree gum.

I am listening. 

To anyone else, the plateau is a single, vast silence. But when you spend decades with your palms flat against the dark rock, the silence breaks down into a thousand distinct notes of water. 

Right now, three feet to my left, there is an error in the world. 

A single tile of pink quartzite, set into the bed of the stone channel forty years ago, has settled a fraction of an inch too low on its northern edge. Because of this tiny tilt, the water sliding across it is not spreading in a smooth, silent sheet; it is catching on the lip of the adjacent basalt block, creating a minuscule, rhythmic *plop-plop-plop*—a tiny, hollow turbulence that echoes off the vertical stone wall behind me. 

It is a tiny violence against the peace of the terrace. It disrupts the *shhh* of the world.

I do not hurry. Hurry belongs to creatures who fear the dark or the cold, and here there is neither. I place my copper scraper beneath the edge of the pink tile, press down with the heel of my palm, and pop the stone free from its ancient bed. The water instantly fills the shallow depression, swirling softly around my copper-cuffed wrists. 

I scrape away the old, softened mortar. The basalt beneath is pristine—dark, fine-grained, bearing the fine, parallel tool-marks left by the mason’s copper chisel a century ago. I feel those marks with the tips of my fingers. They are sharp and clean, unweathered by time, preserved perfectly under the mineral-rich flow.

Across the broad terrace, a quarter of a mile away, a figure stands motionless on a high wooden aqueduct pier. It is Mael, whose long hair is bound in silver-dyed bark. They have seen me pause in my work.

Mael raises both arms, elbows bent at right angles, forearms flat—*Is the channel held?*

I do not speak; the distance is too great, and a shout would bounce harshly off the cliffs. Instead, I lift my left hand, holding the pink quartzite tile flat against the pale sky, then tilt my wrist twice—*A loose stone; the water stumbles.*

Mael responds with a slow, sweeping motion of one arm, slicing horizontally through the humid air—*Adjust it well. I am holding the upper sluice.*

I roll a small coil of fresh ash-mortar between my palms, lay it into the wet recess, and press the pink tile back into place. I use the flat of my copper blade to check the level, feeling the transition with my fingernail until the joint between quartzite and basalt is entirely seamless. Then I sit back.

The turbulence vanishes. The tiny *plop-plop* dies. The water resumes its long, unbroken glide over the step, falling twelve feet down the sheer cliff face in a silent, glass-clear curtain that doesn't splash, but simply merges with the lower pool with a soft, deep hum.

I clean my copper tool in the stream, wipe my palms on my resin-soaked skirt, and reach into the small pouch at my waist. I pull out a thick piece of baked reed-marrow bread. It is cold, dense, and slightly sweet. As I chew, I watch the water ripple around my calloused ankles.

I have no concept of yesterday or tomorrow. There is only this sheet of water, this height, this warm, heavy air holding the mist, and the slow, perfect geometry of the stone. 

My body is aging, but it is aging the way the plateau ages—not by decaying, but by settling. The joints in my hips are somewhat stiffer than they were when I was young; my knees carry a quiet, heavy ache that feels remarkably like the stone I sit upon. In thirty or forty years, when my fingers can no longer feel the millimeter-difference between two tiles, I will stop working the channels. 

I will walk up the thirty-foot stone stairs, stepping carefully across the wide causeways, climbing higher and higher up the step-faults to the place where the water first leaks, cold and pure, from the deep cracks in the earth. I will unbind my hair. I will remove my copper cuffs and lay my quartzite pendant on the dry edge of the highest cistern. 

And then I will lie down in the clear, mineralized water.

The flow will cover my chest, my face, my long, bronze limbs. Over the decades, as I sleep beneath the constant noon, the lime and silica in the water will slowly seep into my skin, replacing flesh with mineral, grain by grain. I will not rot. I will simply become another clean, right-angled block in the foundation of the plateau—holding up the causeways, guiding the water, quietly keeping the silence for those who come after me.

For now, I take another bite of bread. The warm wind rustles softly through the tall reed-beds fifty paces away. I stretch my long toes into the clear flow, feeling the friction of the stone, the soft pressure of the current, and the immense, unmoving weight of the world beneath me.
