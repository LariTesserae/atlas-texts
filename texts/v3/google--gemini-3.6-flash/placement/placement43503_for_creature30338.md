I am wedged waist-deep in a vertical seam twelve fathoms up the northern face, right where the grey slate steps back three feet and leaves a shelf no wider than a carpenter’s boot. 

My left shoulder is pressed flat against cold, un-weathered basalt—stone so freshly sheared that the shock-lines still feel like fine ribs under my linen shirt. My right hip rests against the raw, wet pine frame of a third-story hearth-room that didn’t exist three days ago. Between the live rock and the green timber, there is a gap four fingers wide. 

That gap is my domain. I am a **Socket-Liner**.

Before the masons lay a single slate slab for a floor, or the framing-men notch their joists, I must climb into the seam where wood meets the rift. Rock down here does not breathe, but green pine does; if you seat raw timber straight into damp slate, the cold mineral sweat will rot the wood to punk in five winters. So I work in the dark, narrow slits behind the houses, wrapping the timber-ends in tallow-soaked goat-hide and wedging them into the hand-chiseled rock pockets with flat shims of heart-larch.

My body has learned the geometry of this shelf. My knees are tucked to my chest; my feet are braced against a natural quartz boss that glitters in the dim, cool shade like crushed salt. My hands are thick-padded and grey to the wrists—not dirty, but saturated with the slick, iron-smelling mud I scrape from the floor-fissures to pack around the hides. Under my fingernails, the crescent of clay is as hard as horn.

From here, the rift is not a landscape to look at; it is a acoustic skin that vibrates against my ribs.

Directly below me—eight feet down through the gap in the floorboards—a Water-Keeper named Marei is working her way along a runnel. I cannot see her, but I know the wet, dragging sound of her oiled-hide apron against the slate, and the soft *snick-tink* of her small iron pick as she clears a skin of pale algae from a cistern channel. 

*Clump-clump.* 

A heel taps twice on the pine ladder lashed to the rock face just past my elbow. A signal. Someone is coming down from the fourth terrace with a basket of wet clay. Custom dictates I do not turn my head to see who it is. I simply press my spine tighter into the basalt seam, drawing my elbows in, making myself two inches narrower. The basket scrapes my leather shoulder-guard as the boy passes; I smell the cold, river-bed stink of his load and the faint, sweet pine-resin on his neck. Neither of us speaks. A light pressure from his bare heel against my boot-toe tells me he is clear, and then his weight vanishes down the rungs into the shadow below.

Above my head, the midday light is beginning its slow, brilliant slide down the southern cliff face. A sharp shaft of sun hits the roof-deck three feet to my right, turning the drying wool on the frames from dull mud to a deep, burning chestnut. With the sun comes the warmth, dropping straight down the trench like warm water poured into a deep bucket. 

I hear the old man who lives on that deck—Grandfather Tove—shift his weight. The pine boards groan softly under his bench. He is carving a spoon from a larch burl; I can hear the dry, slicing sound of his curved knife, *shhhk, shhhk*, followed by the tiny *pat-pat* of curly shavings falling onto the slate patio of the house beneath him. Somewhere down the alley, a tethered toddler lets out a high, warbling hum, tapping two smoothed quartz pebbles together in a steady, hypnotic rhythm: *clack-clack, clack-clack*.

I take my small hickory mallet from my belt. It is heavy, its head rounded and darkened by years of tallow and palm-sweat. I set a larch shim into the pocket beside the main joist. 

I don't strike hard. Down here, you never hit anything with a blow that echoes. An echo means you’re fighting the stone, and fighting the stone means a fracture where you didn't plan one. Instead, I give the wedge three short, dense taps—*thump, thump, thump*—the sound swallowed almost instantly by the wet wattle walls and the bundles of thatched sedge drying on the eaves. 

I lay my bare palm against the wood. I feel the grain take the rock's cold pressure. The pine gives just a fraction of a millimeter, bowing into the tallow-hide, settling into the socket with a quiet, wet sigh. It is set. It will hold three generations of children, six tons of slate hearth, and the weight of every snow that slides off the plateau above.

For a moment, I stay still, resting my forehead against the damp basalt wall. 

Far above, framed by the two sheer, razored edges of the world, the bright strip of sky is blindingly white. A falcon crosses it—a tiny, black cross drifting on wind I cannot feel down here. Up there, on the plain, the breeze is probably tearing at the dry grass, blowing dust into the eyes of anything stupid enough to stand upright on the flat earth. Up there, the horizon goes on forever, vast and empty and terrifying, with nothing to hold onto, nothing to wedge yourself into, no walls to keep the sky from swallowing you whole.

I close my eyes. I smell wet cedar, sheep tallow, cold slate dust, and the warm, milky scent of a Ledge-Goat tethered on the balcony across the chute. 

I draw a breath of the cool, damp air that has never seen a gale, reach into my pouch for another scrap of greased hide, and reach deeper into the dark, secure crack of the earth.
