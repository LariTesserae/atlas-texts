I am down on the second terrace, where the sunlight hits the basalt vein and turns the frost to warm, steaming brass. 

My jaw is pressed flat against the curb of an ancient polyp-thoroughfare. 

I do not hear the wind. In my head, where ears should be, there is only a heavy, greasy dark—a quiet so absolute it feels like a physical weight pressing behind my eyes. The screaming of the spires, the high whistling of the window-flutes, the violent crackle of the ice shattering in the gutters—none of this reaches me through the air. 

Instead, the world arrives through my chin, my sternum, and the six broad, rubbery soles of my feet.

Through the bone of my lower jaw, I can read the notch like a open map drawn in heat and pressure. The deep, heavy granite shoulders of the mountain give off a slow, thunderous thrum—a low, sixty-cycle pulse that vibrates in my back ribs, steady as a sleeping heart. Above that bassline, the petrified lime-combs sing in a thousands of thin, shivering tenors. I can feel when a cloud passes over the sun three terraces up: the rock cools by a fraction of a degree, the runoff narrows, and the pitch of the vibration shifts slightly to the left, tightening like a drawn bowstring.

I am a pachycaul, but in this moment, I am mostly an anchor. 

My wool is heavy, dark as wet slate, and so dense with lanolin that it smells of crushed iron, sheep-fat, and cold rain. The noon sun is beating directly onto my left flank, cooking the oil in my fleece until it softens and warms my hide to the temperature of fresh milk. On my right flank, exposed to the shadow of an arched doorway, the cold is sharp enough to turn water to needles, but it cannot penetrate the grease. Raindrops and meltwater sit on the surface of my coat like beads of quicksilver, shuddering in rhythm with the stone beneath me, unable to touch my skin.

Deep inside that wool, next to my hide, a tiny, feverish city is alive. 

I can feel them moving—thousands of microscopic, white springtails, scrambling through the dark, warm jungle of my fleece. They tickle my flanks as they graze on the green algae blooming in my sweat. When I shift my weight, a small puff of steam rises from my shoulders, carrying the scent of wet lichen and trapped heat into the freezing gale. I am a furnace wrapped in oil, anchored to a singing bone.

My flat snout dips forward into the gutter. The water is so cold it makes the nerves in my teeth ache, but it tastes rich and metallic—sharp with sulfur, iron, and the sweet, chalky tang of the lime-polyps dissolved by the deep lichen. 

As I drink, my front pads adjust their grip. The split cartilage of my toes works like wet leather pressed against glass, creating a seal so tight that the wind cannot lift me, even when a gust hammers down the notch like a falling tree.

Then, a sudden change in the stone.

A yard to my right, the vibration *dies*. 

It doesn't fade; it snaps off cleanly, leaving a pocket of cold, dead rock where the hum of the mountain simply vanishes. I freeze, my snout still dripping into the liverworts. 

It is an acoustic dead-spot—a place where the wind-waves curving around the rounded plaza strike the hollow of an archway and cancel each other out. A sphere of absolute, unvibrating silence. 

I do not look up—my eyes are weak, built only to distinguish the blinding solar glare from the safe, dark blue of the moss-caves. But my ribs tighten. I know what uses the quiet. Somewhere inside that silent pocket, or sliding along the shadow edge where its weight leaves no kinetic shadow on the stone, something hairless, cold, and dark is moving. The cleaver-cat does not sing. It travels in the gaps between the mountain's notes.

I remain motionless for a long minute, my broad belly pressed hard against the terrace floor, letting my sternum read the subtle, shifting interference patterns in the limestone. I wait until the wind turns, the acoustic pocket collapses, and the booming hum of the granite rushes back into the stone beneath my chest like a rising tide.

The danger passes. The mountain resumes its song.

I pull my snout from the water, my thick lips dark with purple liverwort leaves, and begin the slow, deliberate climb back up the carved staircase toward the shade of the lower chambers. Each step is a careful negotiation of friction and pulse—lift, seal, feel the hum, slide forward. 

I am small against these impossibly old peaks, small against the vast, empty avenues of petrified sea-bones. But as I step into the dim, moss-carpeted cool of the terrace cave, surrounded by the warm steam of my own wool and the micro-chatter of the springtails on my back, I know that the mountain and I are made of the same thing. 

The wind strikes the rock outside. The city screams its ancient, beautiful note. And deep in my marrow, I answer.
