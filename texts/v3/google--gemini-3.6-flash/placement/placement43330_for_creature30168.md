First, there is the load.

It does not feel like weight in the way a soft thing feels a burden; it feels like certainty. It is forty-two hundredweight of mineralized bone, slate chitin, and trapped brine, pressed so flat against the vertical basalt that gravity ceases to be a direction and becomes merely a friction coefficient.

I am wedged into the angle where the third gusset plate of Gantry Nine bites into the virgin rock face. My rear limbs—broad, rubbery, leaking their anchoring slime in slow, thick beads—are wedged deep into a seam of three-foot indigo moss. My left flank has been covered in that same moss for a hundred and twenty years. I do not scrape it off. It is cold, dense as wet wool, and it insulates my lower carapaces against the spray of grease venting from the 200-foot flywheel three hundred feet above my head.

I have no eyes to see the pale blue sky, nor the three-mile drop into the lower void where the water curtains go to disappear. I do not need them. 

Instead, my crescent flange—the broad, gray spade of bone that forms the front of my body—is pressed flush against the riveted iron beam. Through it, the world is mapped not in light, but in tension.

I feel the bridge. 

I feel the eight hundred thousand tons of cast iron stretching across the six-mile gulf to the eastern spire. I feel the microscopic expansion of the metal as a cold brine pipe pulses inside its housing; I feel the shearing force where the bridge-foot rests on the basalt shelf; I feel the rhythmic *thrum-thrum-thrum* of the cathedral-piston two miles to the north, coming down through three leagues of solid, wet stone as a clear, sharp spike of ninety-two cycles per second.

The deafening roar that fills the sky does not touch me through the air. The air is nothing—a thin, wet medium that carries sour ozone and cold grease droplets. The roar lives inside my shell, carried up from the rock through my six cartilaginous pads. It is my pulse. If the sound were to stop, my internal brine would cease to circulate.

To my right, eighty yards out into the clear, empty space between the spires, hangs Substation Delta. It swings on four steel cables, each as thick as an ancient tree trunk. To a eye that sees light, it is a black cylinder wrapped in wet copper bands, haloed in faint violet light. To my flange, it is a blinding, beautiful heat—a silent, ninety-thousand-volt hum that sourly ionizes the water clinging to my shell.

I feel a tremor along the top cable.

It is not iron stress. It is too light, too fluid. 

A Strand-Walker is moving down from the high peak. I register the double-clack of its copper calipers as it locks onto the wire: *snap-slide, snap-slide*. It moves with a twenty-foot liquid glide, its zinc scales scraping microscopic crusts of dry mineral off the line. It passes above me, bathed in the violet discharge, absorbing the grid’s excess voltage, clearing the route so the current does not arc sideways into the wet ferns. 

It does not know I am here. I do not know its name. We are simply two points on the same circuit diagram.

Below my left middle leg, something small and heavy is moving. It is a Solder-Gub, no larger than an iron rivet, creeping along the head of a massive structural bolt. I feel its tiny, lead-colored body depositing a fresh, microscopic skin of zinc-salt over a scratch in the ironwork. It works with a slow, brainless perfection, sealing the metal against the weeping rock. In three weeks, it will have crossed the bolt-head. In three centuries, it will have moved six feet down the beam. 

I have not eaten since the last century, when the brine pipe above me developed a weeping joint and leaked heavy, mineral grease onto the moss mat. My chisel-mouthparts are currently resting against the cold basalt, locked in a ninety-year stasis. There is no hunger in me. Hunger is a warm, quick thing for creatures that burn out in decades. Here, my core fluid moves at the speed of deep groundwater, thick with dissolved iron and heavy salts, preserved by the same wet cold that keeps the giant rubbery ferns from ever dropping a leaf.

In two hundred years, perhaps, the tension in Gantry Nine will shift. The basalt beneath its footing will settle by a fraction of a millimeter as the young mountain holds its breath. When that happens, my flange will read the change. I will raise my heavy rear plate, strike the basalt three times with a hydraulic thump that will travel six miles through the stone, and another Anvil, wedged into the lower shelf of the eastern spire, will feel the shiver in its legs hours later. It will know the bridge is holding. It will know the load is true.

Until then, I do not move. 

The cold water bleeds out of the stone, running in glassy, silent sheets over my slate carapace. The indigo moss slowly creeps over my third leg segment, binding me a little tighter to the mountain. The great flywheel turns above. The cables hiss in the high air. 

I am where the iron meets the stone. I am the stress-calculation that keeps the world from falling into the sky.
