I am not at the edge where the mountain drops into blue space. The sky out there is too vast, too thin, too full of cold wind that has forgotten how to rest. 

I am in the shadow. 

I am wedged into the dark curve behind the velvet fronds, where the basalt wall leans over the upper basin like a heavy shoulder. The stone against my back is not cold; it breathes an ancient, ambient heat that has taken a thousand summers to sink into the mountain’s heart. I feel it through my spine—a low, humming warmth that matches the slow, dark rhythm of my own blood.

Where do I end and the terrace begin? It is hard to say on afternoons like this, when the sun stands high and the gold light turns the rising steam into a thick, liquid yellow. 

My lower half is submerged in the middle pool, resting in the smooth, scooped basin where the water settles after passing through the V-notched spillway. The water comes up to my collarbones. I can feel the microscopic drift of lime and silica settling in the hollows above my chest, laying down a faint, white dust that pricks pleasantly against my slate-colored skin. If I remain still until the shadow shifts three hand-widths across the wet wall, that dust will harden into a delicate, lace-like crust. When I finally stir, it will crack with the sound of dry grass and dissolve back into the warm pool.

I am the creature with the wide, soft palms and the pale, lidless eyes, but I am also something older—the continuation of a habit that the mountain has forgotten how to break. 

My eyes are open under the water. Through the glassy membrane that covers them, the world is soft, gold, and blurred. I watch the amber gel-mat on the floor of the basin. It is fanning its thin edges against my hip—a tiny, rhythmic pucker that pulls the mineral-heavy water over its body. It does not fear my weight; to the gel, my flank is simply a smooth, warm rock that breathes twice in an hour.

*Clink.*

A single drop falls from the overhanging roof, striking the center of the pool three inches from my chin. The vibration ripples outward, a thin circle of light that touches my throat, then the lip of the stone, then fades into the dark ferns. 

I feel a faint pressure against my left heel. 

I do not hurry. It takes me fifty heartbeats to draw my leg back, slowly sliding my foot across the slick, pottery-smooth basalt so as not to tear the gel below. My wide, rubbery toes find the object: a glass-smooth seed-pod, heavy and round, caught in a shallow depression where the water leaves the upper shelf. 

It has been rolling for two seasons. It is impatient, for a seed. 

I lean forward—my long, tubed spine flexing in a single, smooth arc—and reach down into the channel with my right hand. My fingers are blunted, spatulate, covered in the same wet-slate skin that line my ribs. I do not lift the pod. To lift it would be to expose it to the dry, thin air above the thermal envelope, and its shell would crack before its time. Instead, I use the flat pad of my middle finger to nudge it gently sideways, out of the eddy and into the smooth, water-worn gutter that leads toward the empty rock-pocket near the terrace lip. 

The pod moves with a soft, submerged *thump*, sliding along the lime-glazed groove. It will rest there now. In thirty years, its roots will drink the warm runoff; in eighty, its fronds will shade the lower pool. 

My hand remains near the shelf where the tools rest. 

I look at them—the flaked obsidian scrapers, the river-cobbles with the finger-grooves pecked into their bellies. I do not remember who brought them here. Perhaps it was a mother whose voice I forgot before my eyes cleared; perhaps it was a creature like me, six shells ago, when the notch in the stone was still sharp enough to cut a thumb. The clear lime has grown over the black glass, binding the blades to the rock like muscles to a bone. They belong to the mountain now, but once every few moons, when the jade-succulent in the center of the terrace swells so full that its translucent bladders begin to split, I will reach out and touch the ancient glass. 

I will not pull it free. I will simply press my thumb against the exposed, razor-sharp edge, feeling the bite of it—just enough to remember what sharp means in a world where everything has been smoothed away—and then I will guide the edge against the thick, arm-like leaf. 

The taste of the bitter, cold sap rises in my mouth even now: mineral, bright, and heavy with the taste of deep subterranean iron. My body craves it, but only in its time. 

I pull my hand back into the warm pool. The water closes over my spatulate fingers without a splash.

Deep inside my torso, beneath the thick cartilage of my ribs and the slow, quiet churn of my stomach, I can feel the egg. 

It is heavy. It has been heavy for a hundred years. It feels like a smooth, hot cobble resting in a nest of wet silt. It does not stir, because there is nothing to wake it yet; it is waiting for my bones to give up their heat. It is waiting for the moment when my joints finally calcify into the basalt, when my slate skin turns to grey paste, and my long, slow breath stops rippling the surface of the basin. 

When that time comes, I will not leave this pool. I will lie down in the V-notched groove, where the water is warmest and the amber gel is thickest. The silica will cover my face first, then my chest, then my long, arched spine, building a smooth, pale mound over my shape until I am just another rounded bench in the amphitheater. And inside that shell of lime and bone, the egg will soften. It will drink the mineral soup of my dissolving flesh. It will open its lidless eyes under the water and hear the drop fall.

*Clink.*

I close my inner eyelids, letting the gold light fade into a soft, amber twilight. The wind is howling out there, ten thousand feet down, tearing at the cold scree and the dry pine needles, screaming across the empty air. 

It cannot get in here. The basalt wall holds the sun; the water weeps from the rock; the heat stays.

I draw a long, slow breath through my nostrils, filling my chest with the smell of wet iron and crushed fern. I hold it. I feel the pulse behind my ears, the tiny stir of the gel against my flank, the slow, imperceptible swelling of the seed-pod in the lower channel. 

Then, as the shadow of the cliff reaches the edge of my pool, I let the breath go. A single, glass-smooth ripple travels across the basin, strikes the notched stone, and turns back toward my heart.
