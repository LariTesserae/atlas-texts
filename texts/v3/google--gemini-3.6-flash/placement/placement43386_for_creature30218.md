You would find me eighty feet above the timber-floor, wedged into a windward fissure of a Choked Flue—a narrow, vertical cleft where the bark has split under two centuries of heat-stress. 

I am not down on the flat grain, where the heat radiates off the dark late-wood until the air shimmers like oil. Nor am I deep inside the damp, pitch-spun darkness of the water-wells. I live in the light-filled seam between the two: where the wind howls through the flutes of the ironwood trunk, and where the sun hits the fissure for only three hours at midday.

I am a **Sight-Burner**, though my people simply call me *The Eye-that-Listens*.

***

### The Body in the Seam

If you reached into the crack to touch my arm, you would not find the yield of soft, wet flesh. My skin is the dark, scorched brown of heartwood, dry to the touch and polished smooth where my elbows and hips rub against the fissure walls. I do not sweat; to sweat in this air is to die of thirst before the afternoon shadows reach the next spire. My skin excretes a fine, bitter oil that smells of cedar and boiled turpentine—a sealing glaze that traps every drop of moisture inside my chest.

My eyes are narrow, pale slits beneath a heavy overhang of bone, shielded by a visor cut from a water-clear resin gall. Through this lens, the world does not appear green or blue—there are no such things. It appears as a map of strain. I see the vast, polished timber-plateau in shades of amber, bronze, and carbon. I see the hairline *checking*—the microscopic tension-cracks that run along the spring-wood troughs—long before they open into three-mile splits.

My feet are locked into a pair of ancient borer-holes in the floor of the fissure. The soles are hard, dark pads of horn, ridged like the underside of a beetle’s thorax. They do not slip. They grip the dry grain so tightly that I can lean my entire body out into the open air above the abyss, held only by my toes and a single safety line woven from the inner silk of climber-vines.

***

### The Tool and the Mark

In my right hand, I hold my lens: a heavy, convex disk of petrified resin, four inches thick, ground smooth with fine borer-dust and polished with glider-fat. It hangs from a lanyard of dried vine-gut around my neck.

In my left hand, I hold a chisel knapped from the dense, black mandible of a bull-borer.

My work is to read the continent’s slow breathing and write its warning before it shifts.

Below me, suspended in the heat-shadows of the fissure like giant weaver-bird nests, hang the shingle-shelters of my kin. Fifty people live in this flue. They cannot see the plateau the way I see it from up here. They cannot feel the deep, sub-audible groan of the late-wood plates expanding under the noon sun. They hear only the deafening, metallic roar of the bronze drum-beetles covering the outer hull, a sound so loud it vibrates the fluid in their inner ears and turns speech into useless air-bubbles.

So I speak to them—and to the wandering bands crossing the empty wood-sea—with light and carbon.

When the sun reaches its zenith, I swing out on my vine-line. I catch the harsh, white beam of the noon sun through my resin lens, focusing it down to a needle-point of blinding heat on the bleached, dry bark of the flue’s outer ridge. 

*Smoke rises—a sharp, resinous plume.*

With precise, jerky flicks of my wrist, I scorch glyphs into the timber:
*A long carbon stroke crossed by three dots:* The well fifty paces below is sweet, but the pitch-spinners have spun nets over the third bowl.
*A jagged chevron pointing east:* A narrow rain-shaft is sweeping across the plateau fifteen miles out; its water will hit the eastern catchment before the heat drinks it back up.
*A heavy, double-ringed circle:* **Tension.**

***

### The Midday Snap

It is almost noon now. The heat is a physical weight, pressing against my chest like a hot iron plate. The sky above is pale as bone, choked with fine, floating wood-dust that catches the glare until the whole atmosphere seems to smolder.

Outside the fissure, the stridulators are frantic. Millions of bronze-armored bodies are striking their ribbed bellies against the flutes of the spire in a violent, rattling unison. The roar is no longer sound; it is a mechanical earthquake that shakes my knees, vibrating up through my horny soles, through my leg-bones, and into my jaw.

I press my bare palm flat against the raw wood of the fissure wall.

The wood is tight. It is humming like a stretched bowstring. Three miles out on the plateau, a plate of ancient late-wood—forty feet thick and as wide as a city—has expanded in the midday oven. It has nowhere to go. It is pushing against the base of our spire.

I do not look at the sky. I look down at my feet, at the micro-fractures in the grain. I watch the pale spring-wood between the dark rings begin to pucker.

I raise my lens. I catch the blinding needle of sun.

I burn a single, deep mark into the outer bark—a mark that means *Hold fast. Shift coming.*

A second later, it happens.

***CRACK-SNAP.***

It is the voice of the continent. The sound is so vast and sharp it feels as though the sky itself has broken in two. Beneath me, the entire cathedral-trunk of the Choked Flue lurches six inches to the west. A shockwave travels up the ironwood, throwing a shower of dead bark-shingles and dried beetle-carapaces out into the shimmering air. Far below, out on the featureless plain, a three-mile fault line opens in the timber floor with a sound like a hundred cannons firing at once.

The spire settles. The low, deep drone of the wind returns, rushing through the flutes like a sigh from an ancient lung.

I do not fall. My ridged feet are locked into the grain; my body yielded to the lurch automatically, swaying like a heavy branch in a gale.

***

### The Ancestors at My Back

When the snap has passed and the beetles resume their rhythmic, dry thrumming, I climb back deeper into the shadow of the fissure, away from the blinding glare.

Here, where the air is cool and smells of old sap and cured cork, I am not alone.

Set into shallow, hand-carved niches along the fissure wall stand the ones who held this lens before me. There are seven of them. They are bound in vine-gut, their posture upright, their joints locked in the stance of the runner. Their skin has turned to dark, translucent amber under decades of high-altitude wind and dammar resin. Their eyes have been replaced by polished resin beads that catch the stray glints of light penetrating the cleft.

My grandmother is the fourth one up. Her face is as lined and hard as dried root, her hands still curved in the shape of the carving-chisel.

I sit on a narrow ledge of bark near her feet, resting my back against the cool timber. My blood—thick, dark, and slow—beats a quiet rhythm in my ears, matching the sub-audible drone of the spire.

I am forty grain-shifts old. My joints are beginning to stiffen; the bitter oil on my skin is growing thicker, harder to secrete. In another ten or twenty shifts, when my eyes can no longer focus the noon sun through the resin disk, I will bring my apprentice up to this high seam. I will teach them how to read the hairline checking in the late-wood, how to feel the tension in the soles of their feet before the continent splits, and how to burn the carbon glyphs that keep our people alive.

And then I will take my place in the eighth niche. They will wrap my limbs in vine-gut, brush my skin with boiled dammar, and set my resin eyes toward the vast, sun-bleached expanse of the wood-sea.

I will stand here in the windward fissure for five hundred years, turning slowly to stone in the baking heat, watching the long, purple shadows of the Choked Flues sweep across the endlessly carved grain of the world.
