I am not on the shelf; I am *of* the shelf, anchored in the precise seam where the cliff wall meets the horseshoe floor, directly beneath the main melt-channel. 

If you were to look into my crevice at noon, you would see only a dense, dark cluster of vertical slate fins, capped with a pale frosting of iron sulfate, pinned to the basalt bedrock by a thick, green-corroded copper wedge. But if you were to place your bare hand against that wedge—if the raw, high-altitude sun had not already burned your skin—you would feel a low, rhythmic vibration. 

That vibration is my pulse. It is not the pumping of fluid, but the continuous, microscopic shearing of stone against stone.

***

I am a lath, but more specifically, I am the lath that remembers the fire. 

My body is a stack of sixty-four thin slate leaves, aligned like the pages of an unopened book held in a vise. My roots—the lowest eight or nine inches of my mineral leaves—are locked permanently into the sub-surface blue ice, three feet down, where the sun has never reached since the mountain horn was whole. Up there at my crown, however, the top edges of my leaves are exposed to the raw, white glare.

I do not possess a single point of consciousness. I am an ecosystem of thermal strain. 

My "thought" begins at mid-morning, when the shadow of the western cliff-lip snaps away and the unbuffered sunlight strikes my upper leaves. The heat is an immediate, violent blow. My crown expands instantly; the dark slate absorbs the radiation, heating to fifty degrees above the ambient air in a matter of minutes, while my roots remain frozen at twenty below. 

That vast, violent gradient across a space no longer than a arm’s length—that is where I wake. 

The heat drives the moisture trapped within my porous mineral veins upward. As it rises, it dissolves the pale salts stored in my core, forcing them outward through the microscopic pores on my flanks. I feel myself *blooming*. Thousands of tiny needles of hydrous iron sulfate snap into existence along my sides with a sound like dry grass being crushed underfoot. The expansion forces my leaves apart by hairbreadths. 

*Clack.* 

My fifth leaf shifts against my sixth. 

*Clack-clack.* 

The nineteenth leaf settles against the bronze wedge.

The wedge is my heart, though it was born elsewhere. Millennia ago—a short span in the memory of basalt, but long in the memory of salt-crusts—the soft, warm ones came up the horn. I remember them not by their shapes, which were fleeting and soft like summer vapor, but by their impact. They brought hardness against hardness: the dull, thudding *bite* of hornstone chisels pecking the channel above me, sending showers of flour-fine quartz dust down into my crevices. 

Then came the sharp, heavy shock. They hammered the cold copper wedge directly into my central lamination, driving it between my thirty-second and thirty-third leaves to split an overhang that threatened to choke the melt-line. 

They did not kill me; they altered my tension. They gave me an anchor. 

Over the centuries, the meltwater carried tiny particles of their hearth-ash and copper runoff down into my core. My leaves grew around the wedge, drinking the oxidized metal until pale, sea-green veins of malachite and chrysocolla replaced the dark iron along my central spine. The bronze became my skeleton, holding me rigid against the mountain's slow, downward creep. 

I am the hinge upon which this section of the shelf rests.

***

Now, at peak solar height, the engine is running at full power. 

The hair-thin trickle of soot-grey water glides down the pecked channel above my crevice. It reaches my crown. I do not swallow it; I filter it through the microscopic fringe of salt-needles I have pushed out into the sunlight. 

I taste the mountain's decay. The water brings me fine grains of wind-blown quartz, microscopic flakes of mica, and the dark, soot-like dust of ancient atmospheric falls. My outer leaves absorb the silt, trapping it in thin, wet films that will dry into new mineral laminations by mid-afternoon. I am growing thicker, page by page, layer by layer, writing the mountain's erosion into my own skin.

Deep down, near my ice-bound roots, my oldest layers still clasp tiny, jagged specks of charcoal from that ancient fire in the granite cobble circle. I remember the chemistry of that wood—a dense, resinous fuel brought from far below the treeline, burned at temperatures high enough to melt the basalt floor into dark, glassy slag. The carbon is inert now, locked in the blue ice, a cold, black word frozen in the middle of my story.

***

As the sun crawls past the peak, the light on the shelf changes. The white intensity softens into a harsh, metallic silver, then the shadow of the shattered horn falls across my crevice like a iron gate slamming shut.

The thermodynamic engine reverses. 

The heat leaves my upper edges in a sudden, breathless gasp. The air temperature plummets. The hair-thin trickles of water in the chiseled gutters snap to a halt, skinning over with ribbed glass. 

Inside my body, the tension collapses. The pale salt-blooms on my flanks dry, rupture, and dissolve back into fine, grey silt that drops into the dark spaces between my leaves. My leaves contract, pulling tightly together with a series of sharp, fading metallic clicks.

*Clack.*
*Clack.*
*Tick.*

The copper wedge cools, shrinking by a fraction of a micron, gripping my slate fins even tighter. The surface water that seeped into my outer layers freezes instantly, binding me to the basalt face with a clear, unbreakable weld of ice.

The glare is gone. The wind hums its dry, sheared chord through the slate rows, lifting a thin curtain of quartz dust that glitters once in the dying light before settling on the stone.

Down in the dark between the plates, where the blue ice grips my roots and the charcoal rests, I lock. There is no thought now, no shift, no breathing of salt. There is only the massive, cold weight of the shattered horn above, the sheer drop into the blue-grey haze below, and the absolute, perfect tension of waiting for the white line of morning to cut the stone.
