I am pinned in the high middle of the space, neither on the Floor nor at the Crown, suspended where the clarity of the water is so absolute it feels like being cast inside a block of ice that never froze. 

Before I have a name or a form, I have a tension. 

It is a lateral strain—a pull between two points of black obsidian that sit thirty paces apart across the main light-column. To my left, a sharp crystal tooth protrudes from the east wall, dripping with static; to my right, a shear of vitreous quartz catches the pale, midday sun-beam that cuts straight down from the sky. I am the bridge between them.

I am not stone, though the stone holds me. I am not water, though the heavy, iron-choked brine runs through me like cold blood through a vein. 

The first thing I know is the waiting. 

The deep pressure builds miles below us in the dark root of the spire. I feel it not as a sound at first, but as an increase in the density of the cold—a tightening in the liquid that presses against my skin. My skin is heavy, coarse, braided; it smells of cold copper and wet iron filings. 

Then comes the stroke. 

The subterranean shudder travels up through the basalt guide-shafts. It hits the vault like a dropped anvil. *Thump.* The water doesn't slosh; it compresses instantly, transmitting the kinetic slam straight into my length. My millions of small, articulated segments lock together—head to tail, tail to head—a million tiny iron jaws biting down on a million tiny copper tails.

*Chug—shriek—thump.*

The basalt piston rises six feet below. The hydraulic flues roar, forcing thousands of gallons of heavy, salt-laden brine through the tight obsidian throats. The vault fills with that terrifying, rhythmic thrumming, the sound of an unlubricated engine running at the center of the world. 

With the surge of fluid comes the charge. The quartz tooth to my left begins to sing—a dry, screeching vibration that sends cavitation bubbles tearing off its facets. The static builds across the mineral face until the dielectric strength of the brine gives way.

*Crack-hiss.*

The wrist-thick arc doesn't hit me; it *chooses* me. 

The lightning slams into my western anchor, travels through my copper-braided sheath, and blasts down my length in a fraction of a millisecond. 

In that moment of conduction, I am no longer a line; I am a violent, glowing purple muscle. The energy hits my core and my whole length snaps tight—a violent, whipping contraction that sends a shockwave through the water column, scattering the schools of Galvanic Needles that were hovering near my flank. The current boils the water along my skin, creating a million tiny vapor sleeves that collapse with a sound like shattering glass. 

I absorb the excess heat. My segments drink the current. Deep inside my braided copper hide, my internal chemistry surges: heavy metals are stripped from the rushing brine, bound to sulfur, and plated directly onto my worn outer wires, repairing the pitting left by the previous discharge. 

As the flash dies and the violet fluorescence fades back to the clear dark, I loosen. I return to my long, gentle catenary curve, swaying slightly in the turbulent backwash of the flue-jet.

Now, in the lull between strokes, I can see what I am.

I am a **Strand-Conductor**, anchored high in the sun-shaft. 

Looking down through the piercing beam of daylight, I can watch the rest of the machinery at work. Far below, where the light dies into cold twilight, the heavy Anvil-Crustaceans crawl across the obsidian floor, their gear-like mandibles grinding fallen carapaces into magnetic sand with that terrible, high-torque lathe-screech. Up along the walls, the Nut-and-Bolt Barnacles are just finishing their feed-stroke, slamming their bronze cirri shut in perfect unison with a dry *clack-clack-clack*.

And slicing through the water around me come the Galvanic Needles. They race up from the lower flues, glowing dull orange around their gill-slits, their bronze hulls hot from their own passage through the arc. One of them, a forearm's length and oxidized to a rich teal, brushes against my lower cable. It doesn't bite; it uses my lingering magnetic field to reorient its rostrum, clicking its internal cilia-pistons—*thud-thud-thud*—as it aligns itself for the next stroke, waiting for the quartz to charge.

I am not a single mind. I am the collective, quiet hum of three million hair-like worms, bound by a shared skin of native copper that we have spun over centuries. We do not dream of land, or soft soil, or warm air. We know only the tensile strength of our wire, the cold weight of the brine, the sharp geometry of the obsidian, and the clean, violent relief of the current when the mountain breathes.

The seven minutes are up. 

The liquid tightens again. The quartz tooth begins its high-pitched screech. My millions of iron-hearted segments lock their jaws, brace their copper skins, and wait to carry the light across the void once more.
