I am not above the stone; I am anchored to its beat. 

The world is three seconds long. One second for the heavy, distant drop of the beam in Hall Two; one second for the shudder to travel through eighty yards of dark granite flagstones; one second for the three lead plates in the front slot of my carapace to curl, expand, and settle. 

I am one of the white ones—a Porcelain-Back, heavy as a cast kettle, riding low to the wet stone. 

Inside my dome, there is no dark and no light, only the geometry of structural stress. When the great iron beams strike their downstroke, the granite floor flexes by a fraction of a hair’s breadth. I do not see the red-brick walls or the flat white sky, but I feel the fault-lines of the yard mapped out beneath my six bronze toes as lines of intense, cold clarity. A river of force runs from the base of Chimney Three directly toward the lip of the main drainage sump; that is where the floor is hardest, where the flagstones have been pressed into seamless density by centuries of identical steps.

That is where I go.

*Tink-tink-tink-tink-tink-tink.*

My legs do not swing; they fall. Hydraulic pressure, built from the rhythmic sloshing of the mercury-tin fluid in my internal brass coils, drives each yellow-bronze stud downward into the trough carved by those who walked this path an hour ago, a year ago, three centuries ago. The stone is cold, slick with the odorless black oil that never dries, but my toes do not slip. They find the micro-indentations in the granite, fitting into them like key to lock.

To my left, another white carapace slides against my flank. There is no yielding, no turning aside. The lateral pressure builds until our glazed porcelain hips lock together with a dry, screaming ceramic squeal. Fine white enamel dust—a cloud of pulverized glaze no thicker than flour—puffs out into the damp air between us. I do not feel pain; I feel a sudden, exquisite reduction in clearance. The dust coats my rear left leg-socket, mixing with the cold machine oil to form a dense, grey polishing paste. My joint, which had been grinding slightly on an uneven brass burr, suddenly turns smooth again. The friction is gone. The loop is corrected.

We pass the base of the elevated walkway. Above us, the Cable-Clingers are quiet, their dull grey stoneware disks packed so tightly into the riveted iron seams that they look like a second layer of plating. I catch the high-pitched, metallic *chitter* of their spring-steel ribs adjusting to a minor change in steam-condensation from the high gantry. A drop of cold, white water falls twenty feet and strikes the center of my shell with a sharp *clack*. It runs down my smooth curve, washing a line of black oil down to my bronze toes, where it is instantly absorbed back into the film on the flagstones.

Now the floor gives way to the open sump. 

Here, the noise changes. The dual roar of the steam beams and the dry rasping of the swarm is swallowed by a wet, heavy, thumping churn. The massive cast-iron flywheel of Hall Two emerges from the floor, fifteen feet of velvet-black pig-iron turning its slow, silent arc out of the drown-hole. 

I do not stop at the edge. My front bronze studs drive into the damp granite at the very brink of the pit; as the flywheel’s rim sweeps upward out of the dark sludge, I lift my front legs and clamp my bell-metal belly directly against the cold, smooth iron. 

The momentum catches me. I am lifted from the flagstones. 

For six seconds, I am airborne, riding the rim of the wheel up toward the shadowless white sky. The yard tilts beneath me—the four red-brick halls, the thousands upon thousands of white backs flowing in slow, deterministic rivers around the valve-houses, the iron walkways humming with suspended grey disks. The cold air catches the damp oil on my shell, cooling the mercury inside my brass tubes by a tenth of a degree. The fluid thickens; my internal valves click shut; my body becomes completely rigid, a single, five-hundred-pound block of ceramic and metal bolted by gravity to the turning wheel.

Then the arc crests, and I am carried down into the dark.

The daylight dies instantly as the rim plunges below the flagstones into the drowned sump. The air here is dense, thick with the smell of wet tallow, crushed lime, and cold petroleum. The sound of the swarm above fades into a muffled, rhythmic thrumming that shakes the liquid in my core.

We pass the Sump-Lurcher.

It lies half-submerged in the black slush, a mountain of lead plate and lard-soaked oak, chained to the submerged drive-axle. It does not move as I move; it rotates with the heavy, unhurried majesty of a drowning continent. Its brass-lipped troughs scoop three tons of silver-grey mineral sludge from the sump floor, rising alongside the gear teeth. I feel the massive, subterranean shock of its movement through the iron of the flywheel—a low, wet *gulp* as it dumps its load of grease directly into the teeth of the submerged bevel-gears.

The cold black water hits me. It sweeps over my dome, filling the transverse slot behind my brow, washing away the lead-dust and the mineral flakes that have accumulated during my walk across the plaza. The liquid inside my brass tubes does not mix with the water; it simply slows its cycle, settling into a dense, heavy stillness while the external submerged currents wash my bronze joints clean.

Three seconds underwater. Four. Five.

The flywheel carries me up out of the black slush. The grease clings to my glaze in a thick, shimmering mantle. 

The flat white sky returns. The merciless light hits my enamel, revealing every micro-scratch, every polished line of wear. My bell-metal belly disengages from the iron rim as it levels with the stone floor. My six bronze legs drive down with six sharp, identical *tinks*. 

I am back on the granite.

The lead plates in my brow flex, catch the shudder of Hall Three's next downstroke, and align my body toward the steps of the main valve-house. Three inches to the left of the path I took before. Half a micron deeper into the stone trough. 

There is no memory of the sky, no memory of the dark sump, no anticipation of the next revolution. There is only the alignment of the lead lamellae, the heavy balance of the mercury core, and the endless, perfect, screaming pulse of the iron engine that does not end.
