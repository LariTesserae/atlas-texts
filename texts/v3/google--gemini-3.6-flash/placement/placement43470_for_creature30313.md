You would find me suspended three tiers above the slate floor, wedged into a three-foot gap where a zinc cooling chute presses hard against a vertical slab of freshly cleaved basalt. 

My back is resting against the raw stone—cold enough to numb my spine through my oiled-canvas tunic—and my bare, leather-soled feet are braced against a heavy copper steam line that throbs with a sluggish, warm pulse. Above me, a six-story brick gable leans at an impossible angle, held in place only by the stubborn lattice of dark pine scaffolding and the pale, muscular vines of a giant ivy that is currently eating the mortar out of the fourth tier.

I am a *Pivot*. I live in the joints.

I am not nimble enough to be a Scout—my shoulders are too broad, my torso too heavy to swing from zinc catwalks with the effortless, otter-like flow of the young ones. Nor am I broad-eyed and deep-rooted like the Deep-tenders who sit in the icy dark below, listening to the blind crustaceans in the boiler pools. 

My place is the mid-tier threshold: the daylight line. This is where the bright, crisp sun cuts down through the web of iron pipes, casting hard, razor-sharp shadows across the wet emerald ferns. It is where the city’s two competing floods—the heavy iron and the wet green—grind against each other with the most violence.

In my lap lies a heavy brass drift-pin, a roll of flattened copper wire, and a short iron mallet with its head thick-wrapped in felted moss so it won't ring when I strike. My hands are broad, my palms as thick and tough as cured leather, yellowed with a permanent paste of tallow, crushed liverwort, and quartz dust. I smell of cold lard, pine tar, and the sharp, metallic bite of ozone that drifts up from the automated rolling mills nearby.

Right now, I am waiting on a single root.

It is as thick as my wrist, pale green and glistening with cold condensation, and it is slowly, relentlessly driving itself between a riveted flange on the copper pipe and the basalt cliff behind me. If the root forces the flange open, six hundred pounds of wet, low-pressure steam will blow out, killing the lichen-salve on the nearby gears and freezing the waterwheel fifty yards down the aisle. If I chop the root away too fast, the tension on the pine scaffolding above will shift, and the brick gable will buckle, sending twelve tons of red brick tumbling silently into the fern-canopy below.

So I do not chop. I negotiate.

Every few minutes, when I feel the copper pipe beneath my heels shudder with a soft, wet *thump* from the distant boilers, I tap the brass drift-pin into the seam between stone and iron. *Tap. Tap.* Just enough to give the root room to bleed its SAP down the basalt without tearing the rivets out. 

The sound is swallowed instantly by the thick carpet of silver moss beneath my knees. The valley takes the blow and buries it.

A Scout drops onto the pine beam beside me. He arrives without a sound, slipping through a curtain of rubbery fern fronds as if he were made of grease and smoke. He is younger than I am, his skin translucent in the harsh, clear light, his forearms tightly bound in copper thread to keep his sleeves out of the nearby flywheels. He doesn't look at the sky, nor down into the dark abyss beneath our feet; he looks at my brass pin.

He leans in close. There is no space for words in this saturated quiet. 

He presses his forehead directly against mine, hard enough that I can feel the sharp bone of his brow against my skin. He clicks his teeth—*tik-tik-thrum*—and the vibration travels straight through our skulls, shaking my jaw.

*The upper derrick is swinging granite,* the bone-click tells me. *The shadow comes in three minutes. The gable falls at dusk.*

I click back, a low rasp against the roof of my mouth that vibrates through his head: *Let it fall. The moss has already taken the lower arch. Tell the Deep-tenders to clear the grease-traps.*

He nods once, his eyes wide and dark, unblinking in the cold thin air. He pulls a slab of dried, oily fish from his satchel, tears off half with his teeth, and presses the rest into my grease-stained palm. Then he turns, vaults over the throbbing copper pipe, and vanishes up a zinc ladder that is already being strangled by fresh, pale-leafed ivy.

I chew the oily fish, tasting salt, cold smoke, and the faint, bitter tang of the river grass it was cured in. My breath leaves my nose in small, brief wisps that vanish almost instantly into the clear, near-freezing air.

Above me, the shadow of a massive granite block glides silently across the unweathered slate of the valley wall, blocking out the crisp daylight for a moment. A soft, muffled sigh—*hiss-shhh*—echoes from a zinc cooling tower as an automated valve opens, releasing a slow plume of white steam that rolls over my shoulders like cold wool.

I don't remember where I slept four nights ago. It was a brick loft with a copper roof, I think, but the ivy tore the south wall down on Tuesday, and the Scouts carried the roof plates away to build a splash-guard for the lower mill. It doesn't matter. By tonight, this zinc chute I am resting against will likely be choked with liverwort, and I will gather my satchel, my brass pin, and my felted mallet, and crawl fifty yards east into the warm, grease-scented recess of a new boiler house.

I am not building a city. I am not taming a wild valley. 

I am simply the grease between the stone and the iron. I am the small, quiet pulse that keeps the gear turning for another ten seconds, sitting barefoot on razor-sharp basalt, listening through the soles of my feet to the silent, hyperactive heart of a world that is eating itself alive, and making itself new, every single hour.
