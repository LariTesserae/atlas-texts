There is no *over there*. There is only *here*, which is warm, and *against*, which is heavy.

To have a face or a direction would be an error—an unnecessary edge in a place that tolerates no empty corners. I am not looking out at the basin; the basin is pressed so firmly against every inch of my hide that there is no space left for an outside world to exist.

I would be found in the middle stratum—not deep enough in the black muck to be swallowed by the sulfur-seeps, nor high enough in the scrub to feel the fat drops of canopy condensation strike my back. I am in the press.

I am one of the cables.

***

My head—if it can be called that, for it is merely the tapered, armored terminus of seventy feet of dull horn and hydraulic muscle—is wedged under the flare of a root that was old when the bitumen first began to rise. The root is coated in an iron oxide crust, cold and rough, smelling of crushed nails and stale grease. My jaw rests directly against it. Through the bone of my chin, I receive the basin’s only news: the low, shuddering *chug-hiss* of a methane vent three hundred yards to the south, traveling up through the metal-sheathed wood like a heartbeat through a copper pipe.

Above my spine lies the belly of a broad-ribbed wallower. 

It is immense, three tons of rubbery, hairless hide and yellow tallow, resting its full, dead weight upon my midsection. It does not know I am here, or if it knows, it does not care; I am simply a hard, smooth ridge in the mud that keeps its brisket from sinking too deeply into the black silt. Its heat is immense and constant, a dull, sloshing furnace that cooks the wet mud between us into a thick, oily paste. Every twenty seconds, its ribs expand with a long, wet siphon-wheeze, pressing me down an eighth of an inch deeper into the rust-gravel beneath me. Every twenty seconds, its ribs contract, and I expand into the space it leaves behind.

We breathe like two gears meshed together in the dark.

My scales are slag-grey, flat, and wide as a man’s palm, locked together like paving stones. They do not shimmer; there is nothing to reflect. They are coated in a thin sheen of sticky sap that has trickled down the root from a split in the bark above. Sheet-beetles, heavy with absorbed iron, crawl across my flank in a steady, shuffling carpet. Their little clawed feet make a dry, metallic clicking against my scales—*tink, tink, clink*—like dropped pins. I do not feel them as parasites or intruders. They are simply a rougher skin that grows over my own, a moving crust that eats the sap before it can harden and glue my plates together.

Somewhere beneath my thirtieth rib, trapped between my belly-plates and the slick stone shelf below, something else is breathing. 

It is small—a soft-furred, low-slung mammal or a broad-headed salamander that squirted into the gap to escape the wallower’s crush, only to find itself locked in the vise between the beast and me. It breathes in shallow, terrified twitches. 

I do not strike. I do not have the room to pull back a coil, nor the need for quick violence. I simply hold my tension. When the creature exhales, its body shrinks by a fraction of a millimeter. In that fraction of a second, my heart—which beats only twice a minute in this stifling heat—pushes a heavy pulse of iron-rich blood through my core. My muscle expands. I take the millimeter. 

When it exhales again, I take another. 

It is a conversation that will take four hours to finish. There is no urgency in it, no hunger, even. It is merely the physics of the basin: where a body yields, another body fills the space. By tomorrow, the salamander or the mammal will be grease, its thin bones snapped into splinters that will crunch softly under my belly as I shift, eventually dissolving into the iron-acid soil to feed the root I rest against.

***

To be me in this place is to forget that a body ever had a beginning or an end. 

I do not know where my tail lies. It is somewhere twenty paces back, coiled around another root, perhaps serving as a pillow for three sleeping wallower calves, or perhaps draped over the rusting shell of an ancestor whose plates died and locked into place fifty years ago. It does not matter. The tail is warm; it is pressed hard against hide and muck; it receives the *chug-hiss* of the earth. That is enough.

If the wallower above me dies tonight—if its long siphon-wheeze simply peters out and its heavy, yellow fat begins to turn to cool, sour grease—I will not move away. I will stay beneath it as the salamanders scrape through its flanks from below, as the sheet-beetles bore through its rubbery skin, as its broad ribs collapse inward like the frame of a sunken boat. I will let its decay fill the seams of my scales. I will feel it turn into the very mud I lie in, until the weight above me lightens just enough for another wallower to slide over the mound and press me down once more.

I am a iron chain in a dark basement, holding nothing up, locking nothing in, simply lying where the heavy things fell, warm and slow and blind, listening to the iron trees drink the dark.
