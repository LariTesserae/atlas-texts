It begins not with a sight, but with the press of heat against the chest—a heavy, yeasty warmth like standing too close to an oven door when the tin comes out. 

I am halfway up. Not high enough to touch the iron lantern-grates where the light burns white-hot through the haze, nor low enough to wade the green-filmed runnels where the Gutter-Strikers clack their long beaks. I am suspended in the middle air, tethered to the second-tier gantry where the ash-poles cross the ancient cypress knees.

If you look for me, you will find me by the sound of my work, though I make very little noise myself. 

I am a Keeper in the full middle of my life. My body is built like a barrel of salt-pork—short-legged, thick-necked, without a hair on my chest or arms that hasn't been worn away by leather straps and damp steam. My skin is dark tallow, dusted with iron-red brick powder that has worked so deeply into the pores of my knuckles it will never come out. From the waist down, I wear a stiff apron of pigskin, black with rendered sow-butter and stiffened by decades of lime-splash.

My feet are where I meet the world. Right now, my right foot is clamped flat to a wet, yellow terracotta tile on the gantry ledge. There is no arch to it; my sole is a pale, rubbery pad, wide as a spade, adhering to the damp glaze with a soft, wet *kiss* every time I shift my weight. I do not balance on the narrow ash-pole gantry; I adhere to it. The wet suction in my toes keeps me anchored while both my hands are free.

In my left hand, I hold a shallow bowl carved from a cured gourd. Inside it is a dark, heavy paste—slaked lime, crushed red clay, and the white, powdered jawbones of my mother’s brother, ground fine in the floor-troughs three seasons ago. In my right hand is a short spatula of green heartwood, its edge worn smooth as oil by thirty years of scraping.

Before me, where the third vault-rib springs from the wall, a brick is sick. 

It is an old hand-fired red, six inches above my brow, but the damp heat has gotten behind its face. It has spalled—a long, curved flake of orange clay has split off, hanging by a thread of green liverwort, exposing the dark, soft heart beneath. If I leave it, the condensation dripping from the upper terracotta gutter will find the crack. The water will creep behind the adjacent course, the mortar will soften to red sludge, and in three months, a square yard of the ceiling will drop into the beetle-urns.

I do not hurry. Hurry is for things that live under the open sky, where weather changes. Here, the temperature is always the bread-loaf warm; the splash-wheels always turn their copper paddles (*clack-clack-clack*); the light always slants through the butter-yellow dust in the exact same band.

I reach out with my left thumb—broad, flat, missing the nail from an old mallet-strike—and press it against the mortar joint beside the spalling brick. I feel the wall's pulse. 

Through the rubbery skin of my thumb and the flat pads of my feet, the whole Sump speaks at once. I feel the deep, heavy *whish-whish* of the Lard-Sows breathing in the dark crevices six yards below me—the rhythmic swell of their cold fat against the brickwork. I feel the high-pitched vibration of the three million Oil-Clickers churning in their hanging urns behind my shoulder. And deeper than that, through the solid ceramic spine of the vault, I feel the structural strain—the subtle, shifting weight of the forty souls, the gantries, the water-troughs, and the thousands of dead who form the outer shell.

*Ping.*

A bronze peg vibrates three yards to my left. Down on the floor, someone has struck a drainage spout with a scraper. A trough is clogging with fern-pulp. I don't look down. It is not my trough.

I bring the heartwood spatula up. With three quick, sharp taps—*thack, thack, thack*—I strike the face of the bad brick. The sound travels down the rib, through the pillar, into the floor tiles. Below me, two short-legged children tending the garlic boxes look up, their pale, grease-slicked faces turned toward the gantry, their wide black pupils catching the yellow light. They know the sound. They know I am cutting out a void. One of them reaches into a wicker basket and sets aside a thumb-sized wedge of fresh-fired clay, ready to pass up the gantry rope when I signal.

A One-Eye dives through the slanting daylight beam right past my ear. The air from its pale, papery wings hits my cheek—*fwt-fwt-fwt*—cool and smelling faintly of dried swallow-down and gnat-wings. Its black lower eye glints as it twists in mid-air, snapping a white mortar-mite from the edge of my lime-bowl before veering off into the high dark of the ceiling vaults. I don't flinch. We share the same air-space, the bird and I; its nest is three bricks above my patch-job, built from the same mud I use to mend the joints.

I begin to work. I drive the bronze spatula into the soft, saltpeter-crusted mortar around the sick brick. The old lime yields with a wet, gritty crunch. I pry gently. The dead brick comes away in my hand, warm and heavy, damp as a peeled turnip. Behind it, in the dark hollow of the wall, three blind, pale centipedes writhe in the sudden air.

Before they can scatter into the mortar seams, I lean over the gallery rail and make a low, clicking suction-sound with the roof of my mouth. 

Below, a Gutter-Striker—a fat, cabbage-green bird wading in the lower trough—lifts its head. Its long, three-jointed legs stretch out, and it hops up the low rungs of the ash-ladder with a clumsy, wet accuracy, its webbed feet slapping the damp wood (*shlop, shlop, shlop*). It stops on the rail beside my knee, cocks its head, and thrusts its blunt beak into the dark hole I’ve just opened. *Snap. Snap.* The centipedes are gone. The bird gives its ragged, algae-stained tail a quick shake, rubs its beak against my leather apron to clean off the brick-dust, and drops heavily back down to the trough below.

Now the void is clean.

I reach into the jar hanging from my belt, pull out a lump of cold sow-butter—scraped this morning from the flank of the big pale sow beneath the saltpeter vats—and smear it deep into the back of the brick-hollow to seal out the damp. Then I take my spatula, load it with the thick, pale mortar from my gourd, and slap it into the opening.

The smell rises immediately as the wet lime hits the warm clay—sharp, chemical, and sweet, carrying that unmistakable, faint scent of old bone-flour. It is a good mix. It will set hard in four hours under the heat of the light-shaft.

I take the fresh red brick passed up from below, press it home into the wet socket, and scrape away the excess paste that squeezes out around the edges. I don't waste a drop; the squeeze-out goes straight back into my gourd. 

When the brick is flush, I take my thumb and smooth the mortar line all the way around, pressing it deep into the seam until it is as slick and seamless as skin.

I stand back—my feet shifting on the wet cypress knee with a soft *plip-plip*—and look at my work. 

The new brick is darker than the old ones around it. It looks like a fresh scab on an ancient knee. But in three weeks, the steam will darken it. The wing-lint will coat it. The oil-mist from the beetle-urns will settle over it. The liverwort will creep across its lower edge from the neighboring joint, and my thumb-print in the mortar will harden into ceramic bone.

Behind me, in the swinging wicker pod tied to the main ash-upright, my youngest child stirs in her nest of dry beetle-husk chaff. She is nineteen months old, fat and hairless, her skin already smelling of tallow and sweet lard. She doesn't cry; children born in the Sump rarely cry—the air is too heavy for useless breath. Instead, she reaches a small, broad hand out from her pigskin swaddling, her splayed, archless fingers curling into the air, feeling for the vibration of the gantry.

I wipe my bronze spatula on my apron. I tap the brick once—*thack*—a clean, clear ring that travels straight down into the floor.

The vault answers. Below me, the copper water-paddles keep up their deafening, eternal *clack-clack-clack*. The salamander beneath the vats lets out a long, wet breath—*whish-whish*— radiating its slow, trapped earth-heat up through the soles of my feet.

The wall is whole again. I am in my place, suspended between the mud and the light, held up by the bones of the ones who came before me, holding up the ones who are sleeping in the wicker pods above. There is nowhere else to go, and nothing else that needs to be built. The world is forty paces wide, it is hot as fresh bread, and it holds together.
