I am three levels down from the sharp daylight, wedged into a vertical fissure no wider than my ribcage. 

My back is pressed flat against cold slate; my knees are bent, feet braced against a lip of granite that has held its place for three thousand winters. I do not move. To move quickly here is to waste breath, and breath is thin and precious, something to be held in the deep well of the chest until it cools to the temperature of the rock.

I am slate-colored today—the wet, bruised gray of a shadow in an open cistern. My skin is slick with the weeping of the mountain, drinking through its pores the cold condensate that runs down the wall behind me. I do not feel the cold as a threat; it is simply the state of things, the steady baseline against which my thick, slow blood pushes its iron.

My hands—broad, spatulate, the skin of the palms damp and slightly tacky—are resting in the stone trough before me. 

This is my place: a fifty-yard stretch of the Middle Conduit, where the water from the upper solar-catchments drops down toward the great mirrors of the lower shelf. It is a subtle turn in the aqueduct, carved with a slight, concave sweep so that the water does not splash or churn as it takes the corner. Splash creates vapor; vapor gets caught by the mountain wind and lost over the edge of the plateau. The water must stay on the stone. It must slide like wet silk, silent and unbroken.

A small thing has happened here. A single grain of white quartz, no larger than a bird’s eye, has wedged itself into the seam between two dry-stacked slates. It breaks the surface of the flow—just enough to create a tiny, standing ripple, a needle-thin hum of turbulence that I can feel in my elbows where they rest against the channel rim.

I do not use a tool yet. I press the pad of my right thumb against the quartz grain, feeling its sharpness against my calloused skin. I hold it there, letting my body weight settle into the stone, feeling the immense, heavy density of the city pressing down from above and reaching deep into the roots of the plateau below. 

Through my chest, through my chin pressed against my collarbone, I listen.

The city is talking. 

It is not a voice of words, but of bone-conduction. A mile to the north, someone has struck a key-slate three times with a granite flake—*thud, thud, thud*—telling the mid-levels that the ice in the northern shade has held late today, and the flow will be thin until the sun clears the high ramparts. Below me, deep in the fifth level where the daylight never enters, the slow, rhythmic *plink-plink-plink* of a subterranean drip echoes up through the dry masonry, telling my heels that the deep cisterns are nearly full.

My throat vibrates. I release a low, wet hum from my lungs—a chest-note that travels down through my thighs into the bedrock. *I am here. The corner is holding.*

With my left hand, I reach into the small pouch of felted moss tied at my waist and pull out a sliver of hard, dark granite, ground smooth on one side. Slowly, deliberately, I work the edge under the quartz grain. The stone does not want to yield; the frost of a hundred seasons has set it firm. But I am in no hurry. My breathing slows to three long, deep draws a minute. The skin of my back absorbs the cold trickle weeping from the fissure. My mind flattens out, matching the long, grey geometry of the terrace above.

There is a movement in the air above my fissure—a sudden, fast slicing of light. 

An ash-winged gull has drifted low over the narrow street, its wide gray wings catching the sharp sun for a fraction of a second before it sweeps out toward the valley edge. I do not look up. Looking up would turn my skin toward the glare, drying the moisture on my throat, wasting the salt I need to keep. But I feel the shadow pass over my wet forearms like a cool cloth.

The quartz grain yields. It pops free with a tiny, silent *click*. 

I catch it on the wet flat of my finger, pull it from the channel, and drop it into my mouth. I swallow it down into my heavy stomach, where it will mix with the bitter, iron-rich mash of lichen and amphipod I ate at dawn. Nothing is thrown over the edge. Nothing leaves the city.

I lay my palm flat over the seam where the quartz was wedged. The water gathers against my thumb, pauses, and then resumes its smooth, dark slide over the slate. The tiny standing ripple disappears. The channel becomes a mirror again, reflecting only the thin ribbon of pale sky visible through the fissure above.

I close my eyes. 

The noon glare is coming. Up on the top terraces, the stone will soon be burning with dry, white light, and my kin will already be slipping down the long stairs, chimneying through the narrow cracks, descending like shadows returning to the earth. They will come down into these cool, dark slots between the walls, pressing their slick, violet-gray bodies against the wet stone to wait out the sun.

I will stay here a little longer. My thumb rests on the smooth seam. The water flows over my skin, cold and mineral, carrying the taste of wet ash and high sky. I press my forehead against the dark granite before me, let my breath fall away into the deep hum of the mountain, and become indistinguishable from the masonry.
