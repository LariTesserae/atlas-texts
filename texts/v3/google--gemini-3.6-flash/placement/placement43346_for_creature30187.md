There is no *over there*. 

Distance is a concept for things that move through air, and air here is not a void to be crossed; it is a wet, hot weight that presses flush against every inch of skin. To exist here is to be pinned between three kinds of density: the unyielding, glassy tooth of the basalt behind; the immense, shuddering spine of the iron in front; and the suffocating blanket of vapor that fills every crack in between.

I am in the trench. 

I am where the stone was cracked open by the earth’s young, raw rising, and where men—or whatever built the steel four stories tall—laid down the fifteen-meter wheel. My body fits this hollow the way melted wax fills the grain of rough wood. Twelve meters of waterlogged, olive-gray leather, wedge-headed, eyeless, anchored to the dark.

I do not "see" the cathedral nave of basalt, nor the shafts of orange light cutting through the steam. Light is a thin, useless surface phenomenon. 

Instead, I *have* the sound. 

It enters me not through ears—I have none—but through the thousands of fine, hair-like cilia that fringe my upper flanks like a coat of wet moss. The sound is my anatomy. The high, sharp roaring of the plume where the waterfall strikes the brass catchment is a cool, prickling static across my left shoulder. The heavy, submerged *thump-hiss* of the hydraulic ram deep in the mountain’s gut is a wet, rhythmic contraction in my lower gut. 

And the flywheel—the great fifteen-meter disk of riveted iron—is my heart.

My internal pulse does not belong to me. Left alone in a quiet world, I suspect my blood would simply stop, settling into a dark, tepid pool. But here, as the iron rim turns against its axle—moving so slowly that the sky changes twice before a single bolt completes its circuit—a shudder runs through the bedrock. 

*Groan.* 

The vibration travels up through the floor of the trench, through my thick, tallow-coated underbelly, and strikes my sternum. *Thump.* My heart expands, pulling in a gallon of warm, mineral-thick sludge from my digestive furnace. 

Minutes pass. The steam gathers in the copper conduits. The verdigris weeps. The iron rim creeps another inch against the raw basalt seam. 

*Groan.*

*Thump.* My heart closes, pushing the heat back through my twelve meters of mass. 

I am locked into the clock of the machine. I do not experience seconds or hours; I experience degrees of rotation and the long, slow rise of steam pressure.

My days—if the word means anything in a pocket where the sun is only a yellow smear in the fog—are spent in a state of continuous, heavy maintenance. I am the cushion between the mountain's rage and the machine's fragility. 

When the urge to feed rises—not as hunger, but as a cooling of my internal heat—my siphon extends. It is a long, muscular trunk of slick, gray skin, sliding out from the trench like a heavy python. It blind-gropes through the dripping, neon-bright moss, pushing past the small, hard bodies of the Brass-Eaters who scrape their endless, microscopic harvests from the copper pipes. My siphon finds the broadleaves—fleshy, door-sized foliage slick with mineral sludge—and wraps around their fibrous stalks. 

I tear them down by the armful. I pull them back into the trench, dragging them under my mass where my lower mouthparts reduce them to a thick, green slurry. I drink gallons of the scalding, calcified water that overflows from the brass basin, mixing plant pulp with iron dust and subterranean sulfur.

Inside me, the furnace cooks it down. And from my pores, it returns to the world.

My underbelly is constantly weeping tallow. It is a dense, yellow-gray grease, rich with plant lipids and rendered minerals, smelling of crushed fern and ancient iron. As I shift my weight—a agonizingly slow crawl of six inches over the course of an afternoon—I press this grease directly into the teeth of the giant iron gears. I smear it across the massive axle where the flywheel bites into the rock. 

Without me, the damp heat would have devoured this place centuries ago. The rain of cloud-drops and steam-exhaust would have boiled the iron into orange flake; the raw basalt would have seized the dry axles and snapped them like glass. But I am here. I am the oil. I am the living seal that keeps the ancient, un-oiled steel sliding smoothly against the mountain's unweathered bone.

The machine keeps me warm; I keep the machine moving. Neither of us remembers who started the arrangement, or if there was ever a time before the wheel began its turn.

And then, the relief valves open.

It happens when the pressure deep in the four-story boiler reaches its limit. A sudden, terrifying spike in the acoustic landscape. A high-pressure, deafening shriek of dry steam that tears through the humid soup, bouncing off the sheer basalt walls until the rock itself seems to ring like a struck bell. The blind white crabs in the basin below lock their spade claws into the grates; the Brass-Eaters flatten their iron shells flush against the vibrating copper.

I do not flinch. I am too heavy to flinch.

Instead, as the steam fills the throat of the rock pocket, turning the world into a blinding wall of pure white heat, I open my siphon wide. I draw the hot fog deep into my wet, leathery chest. My broad ribs expand, stretching the moss-encrusted hide of my back until the seams pull tight.

And from deep within my gut—from the same dark chamber where the broadleaves dissolve and the tallow brews—I release my voice.

It is not a roar or a call. It is a low, shuddering moan, a vibration so deep that it sits below the register of sound, down in the realm where rock meets muscle. It matches the resonant frequency of the three-thousand-meter spire. The stone wall behind me takes the note and carries it; the copper pipes ring with it; the ancient steel boiler thrums in unison.

For twenty seconds, the shriek of the steam and the moan of my flesh become a single, solid object—a wall of vibration that holds the cloud-shelf suspended three thousand meters above the silent world below.

Then the valve snaps shut. The shriek dies. The steam thins, scattering again into shimmering shafts of orange light. The white plume of the waterfall returns to its steady roar.

I collapse back into the dark trench, my skin slick with fresh grease, my cilia settling as the air stills. My siphon pulls back against my chin. 

Above me, the giant wheel creeps another fraction of an inch against the stone. 

*Groan.*

*Thump.*

I close my eyes—the ones I don't have, in the dark that doesn't end—and go back to sleeping against the joints of the world.
