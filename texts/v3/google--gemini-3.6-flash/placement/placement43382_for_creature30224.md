It begins not with an image, but with a buzz in the teeth.

It is a hum that never stops, a thick, mechanical vibration that travels up through the heels, through the heavy marrow of the shins, and settles in the joint where the jaw meets the skull. If I were to unplug my ear canals—to pull out the plugs of cold lard and bone-ash—the world would instantly shatter into a roaring, iron-grinding violence that would split my eardrums like dry bladders. But with the tallow in place, the universe is quiet, heavy, and felt entirely through the skeleton.

I am down on all fours in two inches of streaming, salt-biting brine.

My knees are resting directly on the black slate, calloused so thickly that the sharp edges of the stone cannot bite through. Over my back, the rain falls—not as drops, but as that perpetual, atomized silver grease that paints everything in the basin. It collects on the overlapping shingles of my coat—cut from the glossy, charcoal carapace of a third-year scraper—and rolls off my shoulders into the tide with a faint, steady ripple I can feel against my thighs.

Where am I? I am three miles out from the northern hummock, on the bare, slick dish where the rock bows lowest. I am an island of soft, thick flesh in an expanse of cold stone and grinding iron.

To look at me from above—if a Vane-Glider were circling in the low, weeping cloud-bank—I would look like nothing more than another piece of dark detritus left by the surge: a low, rounded humph of black carapace, slick with lard, immobile in the gray expanse. My skin is pale as tallow, coated in the rendered fat of the beasts we harvest, smelling faintly of old sulfur and ocean salt. My lungs are slow; we do not breathe fast here. The air is too dense, too wet, too filled with the fine, sulfurous steam rising from the rock pores. Every breath is swallowed like lukewarm broth.

In my right hand, I hold a short, heavy stave carved from the thickest rib-arch of a dead *Radulath*. The bone has been seasoned in eel-oil for years, turned yellow and dense as basalt. Its tip is wedged deep into a fine, hairline fissure in the slate—a seam where the bedrock folds like skin.

My hand rests on the stave. I am waiting.

Through the bone stave, the basin speaks to me in a language of shocks and shivers. 

Two miles to the west, the herd is feeding. I can feel the massive, rhythmic *scrape-slide-scrape* of forty thousand iron-chitin radulae stripping the yellow scum from the stone. It arrives in my wrist as a dry, shivering tremor, like a gravel slide caught in slow motion. The beasts are moving in an arc, turning their heavy, drop-shaped bodies toward the center of the dish. 

Why are they turning?

I press my bare chin to the top of the stave. The bone vibrates against my lower teeth, ringing through my jawline. 

There. A different note. Deep down, miles beneath the slate, something is moving. It is a slow, hydrostatic groan—the deep subterranean lung of the world expanding. The underground reservoirs are filling. The brine is coming.

In five minutes, the cold water will force its way up through the microscopic vents in the slate. The ankle-deep sheets we sit in will swell to knee-depth. The tallow-scum will bloom, sweating out fresh sulfur, and the herds will go mad with feeding, their heavy bodies thrashing through the surging current.

I do not move quickly—quickness on the slate is death. A sudden slip, a twisted ankle, and you are down in the water sheets where the blind *Grief-Worms* slide. I have seen a man fall, numb his leg on a hidden eel jaw, and drown in three inches of water while his kin stood ten paces away, unable to hear his splash over the thunder of the stone.

Instead, I lift my maul—a smooth, weight-heavy block of dark basalt bound to a glider’s thigh-bone with braided intestine—and bring it down once, hard, against the rib-stave.

*THUMP.*

The shockwave travels out through the seam, radiating through the slate.

Miles away, sitting on the high dry-mound of our hummock, my daughter feels the strike through the soles of her eel-skin boots. She will feel the sharp, singular pulse of bone against stone. *Surge coming.* She will tell the others without a word, using the quick, gleam-talk of wide palms and dark fingers in the yellow hearth-light. They will pull the drying lines inside the rib-huts. They will stake down the fat-rendering pots. They will secure the bone needles and the basalt axes.

I lift the stave from the crack and stand up slowly.

My body is short, wide, built like a low buttress. My center of gravity sits down in my hips, my wide feet spreading inside the soft, down-lined soles of my throat-hide boots. The wind here doesn't blow you over; it presses you down against the wet slate, pushing the cold rain into every seam of your armor.

As the hydrostatic tide begins to bubble up—turning the black stone into a foaming, pale yellow froth—I see them emerging through the gray haze.

The lead bulls of the herd.

They are colossal, low-slung mountains of dark ironwood chitin, six feet tall at the crest, twenty feet long, pushing a bow-wave of yellow-tinged water before them. Their massive, arched backs glisten with the silver rain, marked with the concentric rings of a century’s growth. Beneath their armored skirts, I can feel the terrifying, rhythmic crunch of their feeding plates, pulverizing the stone, chewing the very floor of the world.

A Needle-Stilt skitters past my leg, its needle-thin legs clicking like hail against the slate, fleeing the path of the titans. In the water, a dark, lateral shadow flickers—a Slate-Eel, six feet of blind muscle, tracking the turbulence. It brushes against my heavy boot, finds no soft flesh, and glides on toward the herd's flanks.

I do not run from the beasts. There is nowhere to run on a flat that stretches to the horizon. Instead, I step into the shallow depression of a dry-swell, turning my profile to the herd, leaning my weight into the cold wind.

The leading bull passes twenty feet from me. The force of its displacement sends a knee-high wave of brine crashing over my thighs. My carapace-coat sheds it instantly, the water rolling off in heavy, fat droplets. I feel the sheer, mindless weight of the creature in my gut—a living siege engine of chitin and sulfur, deaf and blind, driving forward on its single, ancient errand to grind the stone and eat the earth.

And behind it, trailing in its churned, bloody, mineral-rich wake, is what I came for.

An old one. A cow, her shell scarred by lightning and glider-claws, her rhythm staggering. I feel it through my boots before I see her: her radula is skipping, grating unevenly against the slate. A tooth-plate has broken. She is starving, falling behind the wall of iron backs.

She will not survive the swell. The Grief-Worms are already gathering in the froth behind her, their narrow heads lifting from the water like slick gray reeds.

I tighten my grip on the basalt maul, adjust the bone-stave at my belt, and shift my feet on the grease-filmed rock, finding the tiny, imperceptible pitted grain of the ancient stone.

I am *Un-Grushed*. I do not grind the stone; I live on the marrow of those that do.

The rain continues to fall, atomized and cold. The low sky presses down like a bruised eyelid. The world is nothing but stone, water, noise, and fat. And as the tide rises to my knees, I step out into the wake of the titans to do the quiet, heavy work of staying alive.
