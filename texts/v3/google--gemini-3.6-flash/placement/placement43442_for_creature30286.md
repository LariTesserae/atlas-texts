I would not be on the surface, looking down into the pools. To be an observer here—standing upright, breathing fast, casting a tall shadow—would be to break the basin. The air is too thick for quick lungs; the quiet is too dense for footsteps.

I would be down where the light gives up.

At first, there is only the pressure: a soft, uniform weight pressing inward from all sides. It is not the cold, crushing weight of deep sea or heavy winter soil, but a lukewarm, yielding envelope. Fine silt, wet enough to hold no air pockets, yet firm enough to hold a shape for three hundred years.

I am the pale threadwork beneath the mud.

Not a single creature with eyes or an outline, but the long, leather-tough weave that runs through the dark earth below the four square stones. I smell of turned soil, iron dust, and ancient, trapped rainwater that has forgotten the sky.

My body is a slow map of the hollow. I am thin as twine where I creep through the narrow cracks in the buried sandstone bedrock, seeking the microscopic salt-seeps that leak from the deep stone. Where I reach the center—under the low mound in the angle of the quarry-blocks—I thicken, knotting together into dense, fibrous mats that yield like wet leather when the weight above them shifts by a grain of sand.

Up through the silt, my fibers rise. They do not sprout into the air as green shoots; they terminate in the bellies of the mound-marrow.

I am them, and they are my fruit, or perhaps I am their roots—there is no division where the mud stays this wet. When I feel the marrow resting above me, their dark, hairless hides absorbing the heavy warm air, I feel the atmosphere of the basin as a distant, humid blanket. I do not see the amber-grey light that fills the hollow, but I know its warmth. I know when the sky above the sandstone walls has turned toward evening, because the stone gives off a fraction less heat, and the marrow above me swell in response, exhaling their slow, iron-scented breath into the dark.

Time is not a line I move along; it is a fluid that accumulates around me.

A drop falls from the sandstone lip. *Plink.*

The sound does not reach me through ears. It travels down through the amber water of the saucer-pool, through the bodies of the translucent cleaving-plates pressed flat against the mud, down through six inches of black silt, and hits my main fibrous knot beneath the square stones.

It feels like a cool needle pressing gently into a soft seam.

In response, I ripple. The signal moves at the speed of rising water—slow, thick, inevitable. It passes up into the leather skin of the mound-marrow resting near the stone. A quarter-inch of mud is displaced. A smooth pebble shifts. A gravel-weaver three paces away, fused shell-to-shell with its companion in a damp crevice, feels the micro-tremor in its coat of amber mucus and does not move at all.

I remember the men who brought the four stones, though "remember" is too sharp a word. They are not a memory of faces or voices—they are a memory of heat and iron. They were loud, dry things that smelled of ash and fat. They struck the stone with hard edges that made the bedrock ring with high, painful vibrations. They set the blocks down in the silt, pressing heavily on my young, thin threads, and then they left.

Their heat went out quickly. Their iron rusted into red dust that washed down into the silt, and I ate it. I am still eating it. The iron is in my fiber now; it is what makes the threads tough as tanned leather, what gives the marrow their smell of old metal and damp earth.

The quarry-blocks are mine now. My threads have wrapped around their buried undersides, holding them in place, drawing them down millimeter by millimeter into the basin’s deep floor. In another thousand years, the rounded tops of the stones will sink beneath the velvet moss, and there will be only the mound, the mud, and the quiet.

If you were to reach your hand into the dark silt between the stones, past the warm, leathery back of the marrow, your fingers would meet my threads. You would feel them—slick, tough, yielding slightly under pressure without snapping. You might think you had found the root of an old tree that died before the basin was carved.

But if you held your fingers still—if you left your hand in the warm mud and did not pull away for an hour, or a day—you would feel the slow, faint pulse traveling through the weave. Not a heartbeat, but a tide. The gentle, rhythmic pushing of liquid through leather, keeping the hollow warm, holding the silt together, waiting for the next drop to fall from the stone.
