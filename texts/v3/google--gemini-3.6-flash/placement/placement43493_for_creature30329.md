I am six inches below the lip of the crevice, where the daylight splits into static ribbons of copper and bruised violet. 

I am suspended vertically, my eight rear suction pegs clamped into the milky, calcified carapace of my grandmother—or the creature who occupied this exact four-inch square of black basalt twenty-four hours before me. Beneath her shell is another, and beneath that, another, a fused ramp of boiled, glass-hard hides that gives the sheer, razor-sharp obsidian wall a slight, ridged traction. Without them, there would be only the knife-edge of the mountain. With them, there is a floor.

I am at Hour Twenty-One. 

My lard-white cuticle has begun to cloud at the joints. The ice-blue surge of my copper blood, visible through the clear plates of my chest, is thickening, slowing its frantic hydraulic rhythm. The violet bioluminescence in my ventral nerve cluster has softened, shifting toward the low, embers-orange that signals the final arc of my light.

In my upper right forelimb—a delicate, brittle needle of silica-sheathed muscle—I hold my wedge. 

It is a shard of jet obsidian no larger than an orange seed, snapped from a stress line three inches to my left when the sun was directly overhead. Its edge is four molecules thick. I do not see it; my quartz domes drink only the raw, directionless intensity of the sun filtering down through the steam. But I feel the wedge with the microscopic chemical pits of my mouth-palps: I taste the sharp, un-weathered iron on its fresh face, the cold mineral acidity where the glass broke clean.

Right now, I am sitting on the edge of an impossibility, and my task is to weave it shut.

Directly before my palps, hanging off the vertical face like a swollen, transparent bladder, is a lens of superheated water the size of a fist. It does not drop. Gravity here is heavy and sideways, pulling toward the dark back of the fold rather than the abyss below. The water quivers, a hemisphere of 90°C brine clinging to the dry, hot glass by a strained, unnatural surface tension. 

Inside this suspended lens, eight amber eggs pulse softly in the steam. They are my lineage, deposited three hours ago during the violent, silent tangling of silk and limbs on the narrow ledge above.

The water-lens is trembling. A steam bubble, rising from the deep volcanic vein beneath my pegs, has lodged at its base. If the bubble bursts outward, the lens will collapse, spilling the amber eggs down into the deep puddle at the floor of the crevice—that terrifying three-inch pool whose bottom drops away into an endless, light-filled void my palps cannot measure.

I do not think *danger*. I do not think *child*. There are no words in the suffocating, silent steam. There is only vector, surface tension, and the cold geometry of silk.

I strike.

My forelimb thrusts the obsidian wedge into a tiny seam of raw quartz at the margin of the water-lens. *Tap.* The vibration travels up my limb, through my thorax, into the teeth of my mandibles. A hair-thin splinter of quartz snaps free. I catch it with my lower left palp before it can tumble into the sideways air.

My abdominal spigots contract. A thick, clear droplet of sulfurous protein and silica oozes into the scalding steam. The heat cures it instantly—not into soft thread, but into a stiff, glass-clear monofilament that hums with elastic tension. 

Using my forelimbs like twin shuttles, I wrap the silk around the quartz splinter, drive it into the skin of the water-lens, and anchor the other end to the calcified eye-dome of my ancestor beneath my feet.

*One line.*

I repeat the motion. *Snap shard. Spin line. Anchor.*

My hydraulic muscles hiss softly inside their glass armors. The air is heavy with iron and hot salt, deadening the tiny *tink-tink-tink* of my work until it exists only as a shivering pulse in my legs. Around me, dozens of my kind are doing the same—a wall of writhing, lard-pale hides, working in absolute, eerie stillness. A forearm-length adult to my left is methodically harvesting the soft gut-fibers of a comrade who died forty minutes ago; to my right, three juveniles, barely three hours out of their jelly-sacs, are raking a bright yellow slurry of thermophilic bacteria into their mandibles, their translucent bodies glowing a fierce, frantic violet.

We do not look at one another. We do not touch except to use each other’s carapaces as bracing points. Yet when a fresh jet of scalding condensate splashes from the roof, my neighbor’s rear leg shifts two millimeters to shelter my water-lens, and my own mid-limb reaches out to press his third thorax plate firm against the basalt until the splash passes.

By Hour Twenty-Five, the lattice is complete. 

The sideways water-lens is encased in a web of glassy silk and suspended quartz needles. It hangs firm against the vertical glass, locked in place over the impossible depth of the puddle below. Inside, the amber eggs have begun to split; I can taste the faint, sweet chemical trail of the larvae breaking through their jelly membranes, already filtering the boiling minerals with their mouth-threads.

My work is done. My wedge—worn dull at the tip—slips from my forelimb and falls, vanishing into the steam.

A deep, calcifying cold begins at the tips of my twelve legs. The silica in my cuticle is crystallizing, turning from clear tallow to a dense, chalky white. My quartz domes grow dark as the silica thickens over them; the copper ribbons of sunlight fade into a uniform, warm amber, then into black.

I do not move away from the nursery. 

I crawl three millimeters downward, positioning my body so that the broad, thick curve of my back plate fits precisely into the gap between my grandmother’s shell and the base of the silk lattice I just wove. 

I press my belly flat against the razor-edged obsidian. My abdominal spigots open one last time, discharging a heavy, viscous flood of silk that coats my legs, my thorax, and the rock beneath me. The scalding steam hits the fluid. It cures in three seconds, binding my living carapace permanently to the mountain.

My ice-blue blood slows to a quiet, heavy throb. The orange glow in my nerves dims, flickers, and goes out.

Above me, a wet, soft *pop* echoes deadened through the steam as the first of my eggs hatches. A soft, white larva, no larger than a grain of salt, crawls out of the suspended water-lens, steps onto the smooth, white curve of my dying back, and finds its footing on the glass.
