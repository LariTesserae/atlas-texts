I am pressed flat against the lee-side of a root-knot, where the grain swirls into a dark, oil-smooth eddy six feet wide. My sternum rests against the heartwood. My jaw is laid sideways on the striations. Through the bone of my cheek and the cartilage of my ribs, I am listening to the mountain’s clock.

I am not in the cluster of dwellings. I live three hundred yards below them, out on the naked under-slope where the curve begins to roll toward the abyss. 

If you were to look for me from the high ridge, you would not see a person at all; you would see a dark, oblong knot lashed tight against a natural seam in the wood by three fat strands of braided fiber. I am tied to the world by my thighs, my waist, and my left wrist. My skin is the color of a wet acorn, stained so deeply by decades of tannin-wash and boiled wax that the sweat on my forearms smells like cedar oil and old smoke. My feet—wide, flat, with toes that can curl around a two-inch ridge of grain like the rootlets of a cliff-fern—are bare, braced against a minute lip of heartwood worn smooth by a million years of wind.

Where am I? I am at the *Tether-Joint*, the place where the village’s main drop-line meets the deep wood.

Above me, suspended in the lee of the big knot, the five bark-huts hum with the light, busy lives of my kin—the wax-scrapers, the rope-spinners, the women who simmer dew-water in clay pots set on mud-hearths. But down here, where the warm thermal rushes up from the invisible gulfs to lick my back, there is no chatter. There is only the vibration.

Through my jawbone, the sound is not a wall of noise; it is an anatomy.

I feel the *Iron-sheaths* first—a dry, terrifying tremor that travels five miles through the petrified heart of the plateau. Millions of broad, dark shells grinding their jaw-plates against the grain. When the pitch rises, sharp and thin like grit between teeth, I know the air three leagues north is drying out. The wax is hardening; the beetles are working harder to scrape the grey film from the hollows.

Then comes the thump. A deep, heavy thrum that hits my diaphragm three seconds before it reaches my ears through the open air. It is the *Flute-dweller* down in the three-thousand-foot vertical chimney to my left. It has just squeezed forty feet of translucent, soft bladder against its calcified silk cables, pushing a lungful of warm, sap-thick gas out into the sun. The sound rolls out of the dark flute like a bronze bell struck underwater: *Hoo-oo-oom.* 

The vibration loosens a drop of glass-clear condensation from the overhang above me. It falls two feet, striking the back of my hand. Warm. Sweet-bitter. I lick it off my knuckle without shifting my weight.

In my right hand, I hold my only tool: a wedge. It is carved from the leg-bone of an ancient sky-skimmer, boiled in tannin until it turned black and heavy as iron, its point ground down on the rough bark-pith of a dry root. It is tapped four inches deep into a hairline crack where the grain split long before my great-grandfather’s people first climbed into these arches.

My work is slow. It has no end.

The wood here does not rot, but it *moves*. Not as a tree moves in a spring breeze, but as a continent moves. Under the intense, continuous heat of the lower thermals, the five-mile root-node expands by a hair’s breadth every fifty years, then shrinks as the high mist-veils wash over it. When it shifts, it pinches or releases the fissures. If a fissure pinches too fast, it will snap a vine-cable like a dry thread, and a dwelling—with its mud hearth, its wax-pots, its sleeping children—will slip into the pale golden sky, falling two miles into the warm, endless bottom before it strikes a lower arch.

So I sit here, anchored to the slick horn-surface, feeling the tension in the grain.

When the wood pinches, my bone-wedge sings. It hums with a high, thin whine that vibrates through my teeth. When I feel that whine, I pick up my mallet—a rounded knot of lignified ironwood bound to a horn grip—and I strike the wedge three times. *Thump. Thump. Thump.* Driving it a fraction of an inch deeper to keep the fissure from closing entirely, preserving the mouth that holds our main anchor.

A flock of *Leather-snappers* bursts from the shadow of the arch beneath me. They drop five hundred feet into the gulf before their dark, slick wings catch the rising heat. *Crack-snap-crack!* The sound of their wings is like a volley of small stones hitting a drumhead. The air they displace washes over my bare back—a sudden, hot wind smelling of wet leather and digested beetle-shell. I don't look up. Looking up is for those who stand on flat earth. Down here, you look only at the grain, three inches from your eyes, searching for the micro-fractures where the dark amber is beginning to sweat.

In the fissure beside my left knee, a ribbon of thick resin is moving. It is four inches wide, clear and yellow as honey, sliding downward at the speed of a fingernail’s growth. Deep inside its glass-bright heart, two inches down, I can see a tiny, blind *Amber-gill*, its pale, worm-like body coiled in a slow, centuries-long squirm. It has been in that exact spot since my grandmother was a girl. It will be six feet further down when my grandson takes my mallet from my grip.

We share the grain, the worm and I. Neither of us digs. Neither of us builds. We only cling, oiled by the sap, warmed by the warm wind that never stops blowing up from the floor of the world.

A mist-veil glides across the rift. It arrives without clouds or rain—just a shifting curtain of cool, sparkling droplets shaken loose from an arch five miles windward. It hits my back, wetting my dark bark-cloth vest until the tannin bleeds a little, staining my ribs. The wood beneath me darkens from oil-horn yellow to the deep, blue-black of wet slate. The shell-grinders on the upper dome pause their rasping for three seconds to drink, and in that brief, miraculous silence, the only sound in the universe is the deep *Hoo-oo-oom* of the spire-dwellers and the steady, rhythmic *thump-thump* of my own heart against the heartwood.

I am forty years old. My fingers are thick and blunt, incapable of holding a fine needle or weaving thread, but they can feel a three-micron shift in the grain before it splits. My lungs are full of wax-dust and boiled tannin. I will die down here on this ledge—not from a fall, for my tethers are checked twice a day and my knots are ancient ones, but simply because one day my heart will slow to the speed of the amber-slide, and the villagers will haul my dark, dried body up to the high knot, wrap me in layers of inner-bark, and wedge me deep into a dry fissure where the wood can hold me forever.

Until then, the wind rises. The heartwood hums. I set the bone-point against the dark grain, raise the mallet, and wait for the mountain to breathe.
