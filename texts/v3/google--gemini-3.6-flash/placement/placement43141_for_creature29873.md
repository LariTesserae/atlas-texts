First, there is only the hum. 

It does not begin in the ears—there are no broad ears to catch it, only tight, oil-sealed slits behind the jaw. It begins in the breastbone, a low, wet rattling like gravel churning in a deep drain. It is the mountain’s blood shifting three hundred paces beneath the basalt floor, a hot, sulfur-thick breath building in the throat of a dry seam.

I am down low, in the blue-black shade where three slate cells lean together like old teeth, shielded from the blinding overhead glare. 

My body is long, thick-ribbed, and heavy with blubber that smells of cold iron and crushed liverwort. I have no fur to freeze, no feathers to soak; my skin is a single, unbroken suit of oiled leather, slick as river mud and gray as the rock itself. Where my belly rests against the slate, the stone is warm. I keep the frost from the floor simply by existing upon it.

I have six legs, wide and short, and every pad is spread flat. Thirty small, black hooks—curved like midge-teeth—are sunk into the invisible pores of the basalt beneath me. I do not walk so much as I adhere. When the terrace tilts under the weight of wet gravel, or when the cold spouts burst, the world may slide, but I do not. I am nailed to the mountain by my own weight and the grip of my toes.

Beside me, wedged under the shadow of my flank, is a human child. 

She is small, wrapped in three layers of tallow-cured hound-skin, her face painted with a dark ring of soot and fat that runs from her brow down to her chin. Her eyes are half-closed, the double lids weeping a thin, clear slime that catches the faint, stray glimmers of light bouncing off the wet floor. Her feet, bound in liverwort-stuffed booties, are shoved deep into the warm fold between my fifth leg and my belly. She smells of boiled larva-curd and the sharp, salt-stink of her own skin. 

She does not speak. She does not need to. I feel her fingers—smooth-tipped, the fingerprints already scoured away by her first three years of handling silt—tapping a rhythmic, three-stroke pattern against my hip. *Tap. Tap-tap.* 

*Stay.*

I stay. I am the hearth. I am the three-hundred-pound slab of living iron that keeps her toes from turning black and snapping off like frozen birch twigs.

Above us, the world is a madness of hard, white light and silent movement. Through the vertical chimney of the stairwell, I can see the undersides of the bone ladders. Leather-soled feet scramble up and down, dropping bits of gray moss and drops of cold water onto my back. I feel the dull, heavy *whump* of slate slabs being laid three terraces up, the vibration traveling down through the dry-stacked walls and into the bedrock, straight into my claws.

A shift comes.

It is subtle at first—not a sound, but a change in the weight of the water deep in the stone. The sulfur smell in the air grows thick, sweet, and warm. The moisture dripping from the ceiling flumes slows from a steady trickle to a nervous, spitting stop.

The child feels me change before she sees it. 

My ribcage stops its slow, lazy rise and fall. The subsonic hum in my throat deepens, dropping an octave, vibrating so hard that a tiny pool of caught water in the hollow of my shoulder begins to ripple into concentric circles. My six legs lock into the basalt. The tiny keratin hooks pull tight, anchoring my blubber against the slate.

*Hummmmm-shuck. Hummmmm-shuck.*

The child moves instantly. She doesn't cry out; she doesn't drop her slate-scraper. She kicks her feet free from my belly, rolls onto her knees, and strikes the flat of her palm four times against the cellar lintel. *Thwack. Thwack. Thwack. Thwack.*

Up the chimney, the signal catches. A bone-needle clicks against a slab. A leather boot slams twice on a flume. The whole vertical hive above us—hundreds of low-built, skin-wrapped bodies—stiffens in unison. The silent traffic on the ladders freezes. 

Deep behind the cell wall, the mountain gives a wet, heavy *gargle*.

A new spring is breaking. It is coming through the back of the third tier, right where the old women are drying larva-jelly on slate trays. 

I do not scramble away. I am built for the surge. I turn my broad, wedge-shaped head toward the cellar mouth, my dark, rimless eyes narrowing against the sudden shaft of reflected glare as the wall above us begins to be dismantled. 

Men are already up there, their black-ringed eyes wide, their smooth-palmed hands moving in a blur of quiet, desperate coordination. They are pulling down their own roofs stone by stone, passing forty-pound slabs of basalt hand-to-hand down the bone ladders, building a new baffle to turn the coming torrent toward the lower catch-pools before it can wash out the sleeping cells.

A wave of steaming, mineral-thick water bursts through a seam six feet above us, spilling over the terrace ledge like a sheet of boiled milk. It hits my back with a heavy *slap*.

It is hot—blistering hot from the deep veins—and it smells of old iron and drowned moss. The water surges over my oiled hide, split by the ridge of my spine, and cascades around my flanks into the drain troughs below. I do not budge. My six legs are anchored in the deep stone; the current washes over me as if I were merely another rounded boulder in the gutter.

The child is already gone, scrambling up the ladder behind her father, a slate baffle tucked under her arm, her small hands coated in yellow bird-tallow to grip the slick rungs.

I remain in the wash, my hide gleaming like wet lead under the white sky, my belly warmed by the mountain’s boiling throat. I feel the town tearing itself apart above me and rebuilding itself four paces to the east. I feel the bone ladders shaking as thirty people run across them with stone in their arms. I feel the tallow-birds tumbling from their crannies, grunting as they dodge the flying spray.

I shut my nose-slits against the steam. My throat continues its deep, rhythmic thrum, telegraphing the pressure of the subterranean pulse through the soles of every leather boot in the valley. 

I am where the water meets the stone. I am where the city rests its cold, human weight. I am the iron-slip at the bottom of the world, holding fast while the stone walks around me.
