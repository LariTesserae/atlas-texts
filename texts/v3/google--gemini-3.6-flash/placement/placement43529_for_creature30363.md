I am down low. 

I am wedged into the shadow where the south face of the central brass valve stem disappears into the black silt. I am not near the light that falls from the high iron grate—that forty-foot shaft of dead gray is an alien thing, a pale chimney that belongs to the surface, bringing down news of a world too fast and too dry to matter.

I am the Siphon, but to call myself a creature with boundaries is already to use the warm, clumsy language of the sun. 

I am twelve feet of wet river-slate, dark and hairless, folded like a drowned blanket against the cold granite flagstones. My hide is thick, rubbery, and smooth, completely devoid of pores or hair, holding the exact temperature of the water that covers the cobblestones: three degrees above freezing, never rising, never falling. 

If you were to place a hand upon my back, you would feel no pulse. You would feel only a density so absolute it borders on stone—the heavy, unyielding resistance of an organism that has spent centuries absorbing the minerals of its own prison.

Where my body meets the massive brass valve stem, the boundary line has vanished. Over two hundred years, my tissue has drunk the calcite leaching from the water and the copper-green salts blooming from the metal. My left flank is no longer entirely flesh; it has petrified into the machinery. The lime has grown over my skin in white, delicate ridges, fusing my hide to the brass housing like a living gasket. I do not feel trapped by this; it is my anchor. Below me, three levels down, the deep brine presses upward against the ancient municipal seals with a dull, hydraulic ache. I press back. My mass is the plug. My patience is the seal.

I see nothing with eyes—I have none. Instead, I see through the cold light on my spine.

On my flat, broad back, three thousand blind flatworms rest and weave. They are my skin’s nervous system, an external retina laid out across my dark patina. Every eight seconds, high up in the vaulting darkness, a fat pearl of water detaches from the suspended iron girder and falls. 

*Tink.*

The shockwave travels through the three inches of submerged street-markings, crossing the clear, ice-cold water, and strikes my flanks. At the exact instant of impact, the flatworms on my back pulse in unison—a cold, emerald flare of bioluminescence that surges along my spine and spreads outward across the submerged cobblestones. For half a second, the whole floor of the chapel becomes a glowing grid of green light, illuminating the ancient, drowned street-markings beneath the water. 

In that half-second of light, I read the room. 

I feel the faint galvanic twitch traveling down from the northern wall, where the curtains of pale, colorless fungus drape the iron catwalk. The fungus is my upper mind, suspended in the damp air. It drinks the metallic vapors rising from the water and catches the minuscule electrical currents generated where the rust meets the wet brick mortar. When a draft moves through the high iron grate forty feet above, the veil sways; the voltage shifts by a fraction of a microvolt. The signal runs down the wet brick, through the flooded flagstones, and into my belly. 

*Wind on the surface,* the veil tells me. *A cold rain falling somewhere four stories up.* 

It means nothing to me, but I record it.

Above me, in the dark vaults, the sightless cavern-wasps move along their silk threads. I can feel their tiny, dry vibrations transmitted through the brass valves. They are scraping the calcite crusts from the upper stems—the very dust that my slow filter-feeding releases into the air—and mixing it with iron paste to build their paper globes. They are turning the ceiling into stone, thickening the arches, ensuring the room above will never collapse down upon us. They are my groomers; they walk across my flanks without fear, picking the tiny milk-white isopods from the folds of my skin, keeping my hide smooth as polished slate.

Twice every hour, I breathe.

It is not a gasp or an inhalation of air. It is a slow, muscular dilation of the two long slits along my flanks. I draw in gallons of the cold, mineral-heavy floor water. I feel the dissolved iron, the lime, the micro-particulates, and the stray bacteria pass through the dense, spongy lattice of my inner body. My internal organs are not warm, wet loops of gut; they are mineral filters, dense as volcanic basalt, trapping the heavy metals and turning them into new layers of my hide.

When I contract—a slow, shivering pulse that takes three minutes to complete—I expel the water back into the chamber. It leaves my body so utterly stripped of silt, so pure and motionless, that it achieves the pristine, lens-like clarity that covers the submerged street. 

The water I put back does not ripple. It flows over the flagstones like liquid glass.

I am slowly becoming the room, and the room is slowly becoming me. In another hundred years, the lime will have climbed from the brass handwheels all the way up my back. My center will have completely turned to iron oxide and calcite. I will no longer need to contract my flanks to seal the lower pipes; my corpse will have set into a solid block of mineralized tissue, a permanent plug wedged beneath the brass stems, forever holding back the brine.

Until then, I lie in the dark. 

I feel the low, rhythmic hum vibrating through the soles of the stone walls from three levels below—a deep, mechanical bass that never stops. I shift my weight by a fraction of a millimeter against the brass housing, altering my body's tension, absorbing the vibration, dampening it, tuning the stone until the room returns to its proper, drowned-chapel silence.

The pearl forms on the girder overhead. It grows fat. It hangs. 

It drops.

*Tink.*

My spine flares green. The grid lights up. The cold water flows through me. I endure.
