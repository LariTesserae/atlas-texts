You would not find me in the empty air. To be in the air is to be detached from the stroke of the piston, and in this world, to be detached from the stone is to be nothing at all.

I am pressed flat against a fluted rib of hornblende, three thousand feet above the lowest gas-fissure and two thousand below the peak’s crown. I am tilted at eighty degrees, hanging over a gulf so deep that the cold drizzle falling from my flank will take forty seconds to strike the moss-curtains below. 

I do not have eyes. I have never known what it is to receive a ray of light, nor do I have a concept for the void between stars. To me, the universe is not made of shapes or colors; it is made of **densities, temperatures, and vectors of pressure**.

My body is long, jointed, and low—a heavy, braided rope of dense, cold-hardy cable wrapped in a pelt so dark and hydrophobic that the oily rain slides off it without a whisper. My pelt smells of rendered tallow and cold iron filings. Beneath the velvet, my skin does not shiver; my temperature is dialed precisely to match the damp, freezing air around me, so that I leave no heat-trail for anything else to track. I am an echo of the rock’s own chill.

My foreclaws are driven deep into the stone. They are not bone—they are mineralized spurs, grown from the iron my gut extracts from the tallow-grease I drink. When I shift my weight, the spurs do not slip; they bite into the hornblende with a dry, sharp *tink*, a sound that is instantly swallowed by the heavy liver-worts and wet iron-leaf ferns draping my shoulders. Beneath the claws, the soft pads of my feet are filled with viscous, non-freezing lipids that expand into every micro-fissure of the rock, sticking to the wet lichen like cold rubber on iron.

I am a **Velvet-Stalker**, but I am not moving. I have not moved for three cycles of the mountain's breathing.

Instead, I am reading the mountain.

Through my foot-pads, I feel the deep, subterranean *clack-shhh* of a rock-valve firing six miles down in the dark. The vibration travels up through the core of the spire, ringing the hornblende like a struck anvil. A fraction of a second later, the wave passes through my claws, through my carpal joints, and into the two broad, leaf-shaped pits set into the velvet of my blunt skull.

The world blooms in my mind as a map of micro-warmths and mechanical tremors:

*   Far to my left, running in a straight, chest-wide line down the precipice, is a lichen conduit. I can feel the rhythmic, wet *thrum-pump* of the barnacle-moulds forcing cold grease upward toward the peak. 
*   Scraped along that conduit, sixty yards away, is a Sludge-Siphon. It moves with a heavy, wet *schlick, schlick*, its brassy mouth-scrapers stripping excess paraffin from the tube. It is warm—a faint, dull ember of friction-heat in the freezing dark—but its skin is tough, fibrous, and tastes of bitter mineral ash. I let it pass.
*   Far above, where the peak narrows into the fluted horn of a Cymatium, the air is quivering. The Cymatium is receiving the valve-shock now. Its bone-roots convert the kinetic punch into a low, pure, sub-audible hum that cascades down the rock face. The hum hits my velvet pelt, smoothing the oil-drizzle on my back, settling the air into a profound, woolly quiet.

And then, through the hornblende, I feel it.

A slow, heavy tapping. *Click... click... click-click.*

It is four hundred yards down-ridge, on a sheer face of wet fronds. A Pyrite-Cask. It is grazing on the iron-leaves, its double-hinged mandibles pulverizing the mineral fronds with a dry, grinding *crunch*. It is massive—the size of a hearth-stove—and its heavy, brassy carapace radiates a dull, persistent heat generated by the dense, chemical furnace of its digestive gut. As it moves, its wedge-shaped iron legs drive into the stone, sending telegraphic pulses through the mountain’s skeleton. It is signaling its presence to others of its kind, telling them that the fronds on this ledge are rich in sulfur and cold grease.

It does not know I am here. It cannot sense me, because I have no pulse that speaks to the stone, no heat that bleeds into the fog. 

Slowly, without a sound, I retract my left foreclaw from the rock. The mineral spur disengages with a silent *click*. My oil-filled pad peels away from the lichen with no more noise than a falling drop of grease. 

I do not hurry. Hurry is for things that warm themselves with blood. I move with the slow, hydraulic patience of the conduits themselves. One foot drives its spur into a fresh seam of hornblende; the opposite hind-foot releases. I slide through the wet, shivering curtains of fern like oil flowing downhill.

I can feel the Pyrite-Cask’s heat growing clearer in my thermal pits. I can sense the exact shape of its iron-plated shell—the overlapping brassy scales, the small, rust-stained seams where the plates meet, and beneath them, the soft, cold, oil-rich muscle that drives its mandibles.

A miles-long ridge nearby gives off a sudden, low-frequency groan as a deep gas-pocket shifts. The air quivers. The rain drops heavy and straight. The Cymatium overhead sings its endless, mindless note into the black abyss.

I reach the edge of the ledge. I extend my four-part jaw, the pincer-bones unspooling from my velvet head with a cold, wet click of cartilage. I locate the thin, unarmored joint between the beetle's shoulder-plates where the heat bleeds out into the moss.

I do not hate the beetle. I do not fear the dark. I am simply a gear turning against another gear in a machine that was started before the peaks had names, and will continue to thrum long after the cold grease stops flowing.

I strike.
