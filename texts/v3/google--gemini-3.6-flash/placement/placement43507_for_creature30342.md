I am wedged into a vertical seam where the black basalt meets the wet, orange rust of the lower gantry. 

It is not a choice to be here, in the sense that a soft thing chooses a shadow or a roost. It is simply where my weight settled when my carapace hardened out of the mineral brine three thousand cycles ago. 

I am one of the bound. My shell is narrow, four inches across, ribbed like a river stone that has been carved out of slag and tempered in salt. I have no eyes to see the plain, blinding daylight that strikes down into the trench, nor do I need them; light here is just another form of heat, a flat weight pressing down on the dorsal plates of my back.

Instead, I feel the world through the velvet brush of micro-cilia that carpet my seams. 

Through them, the air is not empty space. It is a solid, scalding fluid—a dense, shifting tapestry of steam plumes, mineral spray, and the sharp, dry whistle of the relief valve tearing through the wet air above. I know the position of every needle-jet of boiling water without looking; I feel their white arcs as narrow bands of intense, localized pressure that hum against the air. I know the shear-mats below me not as a color, but as a dull, thick dampness that tastes faintly of iron oxide and dissolved silica when the mist washes over my belly.

For fifty-nine seconds, I do not exist as a creature that moves. I am a mineral fixture. A knot of glass and iron glued to a wall.

In this long interval, my body is entirely open to the trench. The heat penetrates my dark shell, soaking deep into the stagnant bladders beneath my plates. The scald does not hurt; it is the fuel. It cooks the thick, thermal bacteria riding in my gut vents, fermenting the tough kelp fibers I scraped from the rust during the last stroke. But my blood does not move. It sits still and thick in its internal channels, heavy with dissolved iron and sulfur, waiting.

I can feel the engine preparing. 

I feel it not through sound—sound is too thin a word for what happens here—but through the stone itself. Deep within the trench walls, the basalt is straining. The eight-foot iron piston is at the peak of its long, agonizing inhalation, holding thousands of tons of displacement in suspended potential. The air in the trench grows imperceptibly tighter. The whistle at the relief valve shifts its pitch up a fraction of a hertz, screaming against the pressure.

Then, the release.

The fall is total. The air disappears for a microsecond as the iron weight plunges, displaced by a mountain of dark metal dropping into the floor.

Then comes the *thrum*.

It hits my shell like a sledgehammer wrapped in wet leather. The shockwave travels straight out of the iron gantry, through my suction-pads, and into the core of my body. It is violently, magnificently loud—a sound that shatters the air and turns the moisture in my cilia into a violent spray.

In that exact instant, my life happens.

The shockwave strikes my fluid bladders, collapsing them instantly under the kinetic surge. The static, heavy brine inside me is forcibly driven through my vascular valves in a single, brutal pulse. Oxygen and hot sulfur slam through my tissues. My nerves flare white-hot with sudden, mechanical life.

Before the echo can bounce twice off the razor-sharp glass of the opposing wall—while the air is still dense and shaking like gelatin—my front left joint snaps forward.

*Two millimeters.*

The tip of the fused iron-glass leg bites into a microscopic notch in the basalt. The rear pad releases, slides, and clamps down again onto the slippery, vitrified scale of the shear-mat. My mouth-rasp darts out, shears three tough threads of pale, scalded vegetation from the iron boot, and pulls them into the gut vent.

And then, the pulse is gone.

The shockwave dissipates into the deep rock. The fluid in my channels slows, settles, and stops. My leg locks into its new notch, rigid as a iron rivet. The long, high whistle of the steam valve reasserts itself, washing over my shell in a steady, white roar.

I am frozen again. 

Down in the dark slot beneath my ledge, far below the surface of the boiling mirror-pools, I can feel the great tallow-mass shifting in its sleep. The same shockwave that drove my leg forward just drove three hundred gallons of scalding brine through its pale, parchment-like folds. I feel its deep, low reaction through the rock—a slow, hydraulic shudder as it filters the iron precipitates out of the surge. We are tied to the same stroke, that massive, pale lung in the dark and I on my sunlit ledge. 

Up here, the fat, scalding drops of condensation continue their metallic rhythm, striking my backplate with hard, bright *ticks*. The heat sits heavy and thick in my shell. The kelp-fiber begins to break down in my vents, releasing its bitter, sulfurous gas into my static blood.

I do not think of the next step. I do not think of the top of the wall, fifty feet above, where the steam dissolves into the dry, empty sky. There is nothing up there for a body made of basalt and boiled salt. 

There is only the long, slow rise of the iron boot. 

Fifty-eight seconds.
Fifty-seven seconds.

I hold the rock. I soak in the scald. I wait for the heart of the world to strike again.
