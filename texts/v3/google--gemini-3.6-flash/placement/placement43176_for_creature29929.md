My lower jaw rests against the grain of the central spire, four meters above the silt line. 

I do not hold myself there with my hands. My left thigh bears a teardrop of carved basalt, bound into the waist-loop of my hyacinth apron; my lungs are half-emptied, held at precisely the volume needed to make me weightless in this cold, iron-dense water. I hover without drifting, my webbed toes brushing the lip of a archway that was opened before my grandmother’s frills had lost their juvenile pink.

Through the bone of my jaw, the city speaks before it reaches my eyes. 

A quarter-mile to the north, where the canopy leaves catch the last pale-green shafts of the setting sun, a weaver strikes her ring against a cross-girder—*tap, tap-tap*—a signal that the evening’s kelp-line has been stretched. Lower down, deep in the dark sediment beneath my tail-edge, the giant mussels snap their valves. *Thump. Thump.* The vibration travels up the timber-reed like a breath through a throat, rattling softly in the roots of my teeth.

I am an arborist of the lower spires. My skin is the dark, slick grey of a river-pebble, rubbed thick with rendered char-fat to keep the lake’s mineral chill from settling into my joints. My gill-frills—wide, fern-like sheets of dark moss-green—bloom lazily beside my neck, fanning the heavy liquid twice, three times a minute, before collapsing back against the folds of my throat. My heart gives a single, heavy knock in my chest. Then it waits. 

In my right hand, I hold a broad-bladed chisel of black obsidian, its edge ground so fine it can shear a single fiber from a water-hyacinth stem. In my left is a weighted mallet of elk-antler, its butt-end smoothed by forty years of my own palm.

I am not building a room. I am coaxing one.

The stalk before me is three hundred years old, as thick as eight men standing chest-to-chest, its outer heartwood black and iron-hard from centuries of immersion and oil-curing. Two winters ago, the living vascular bundles inside this column shifted, pushing out a dense, heavy knuckle of growth that threatened to pinch closed the secondary causeway to the granaries.

I move my obsidian blade against the swell. The water dampens the strike before it happens, so the mallet falls with a muffled, heavy *thut*. A thin ribbon of black timber curls off the wood, dark as a dried leech, and drifts slowly upward through the green light, wavering as it ascends toward the sunlit canopy where the children play.

I wait three beats of my heart before I strike again. To rush the wood is to bruise it; a bruised stalk grows sour, its sap turning cloudy and thick, attracting the burrowing amphipods that riddle the lattice with rot. 

My brass-colored irises contract as the sunlight above turns from green to deep, luminous cobalt. The lake is cooling now, its heavy water settling into the night-density. Around me, the synchronized rasp of the freshwater barnacles sharpens—millions of tiny, armored legs kicking in the dark, a metallic hum that vibrates in the skin of my chest like a struck wire.

I am old enough that the tips of my gill-frills have turned pale and calcified, dusted with the white mineral crust of the deep vents. In forty or fifty years, when my bones become too dense for even the basalt weights to balance, my children’s children will bring me down here, to this very stalk. They will carve a niche precisely three fingers wider than my shoulders. They will place me inside, my hands folded over my teardrop stone, and they will tie fresh, wet shoots across the opening.

I can feel the spot already. It is just below where my jaw now rests—a dark, quiet density in the core of the timber that has not seen the green sun since the mountain was young. The wood will lip over the frame. The grain will tighten. I will become a knot in the pillar that supports the weavers' loom three stories above.

For now, I work.

I strike the antler against the stone. *Thut.* Another ribbon of wood rises, twisting lazily in the cold stream. 

A school of bone-plated char sweeps past my shoulder, their broad fins brushing my hide with the texture of wet gravel. I do not flinch. I shift my weight slightly, exhaling a thin trail of silvery bubbles that wobble upward into the dark canopy. 

I press my jaw back against the living heartwood, close my outer eyelids, and listen to the city breathe.
