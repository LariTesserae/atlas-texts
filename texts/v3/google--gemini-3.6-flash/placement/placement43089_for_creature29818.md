I am wedged into a three-sided corner where two octagonal pillars of basalt meet the lowest course of the North Terrace. 

It is a deep place, sixty paces below the floating reed-bridges, down where the bronze twilight softens into a thick, cool indigo. Here, the warm sulfur plumes from the floor do not bubble; they slide up the faces of the dark glass columns like sheets of silk, distorting the view of the city above into a wavering, silent theater of shadows.

I am a Keeper of the Anchorage. My hands—broad-palmed, with horn nails worn square and blunt from forty years of grinding shell against obsidian—are currently buried wrist-deep in the heart of a primary cable. 

The cable is as thick as my chest, an eight-strand braid of cured kelp-skin wrapped with tough, fibrous sea-vine sinew. It runs from a massive lead-weighted toggle buried deep in a natural fissure of the pillow-lava, slanting upward at a forty-five-degree angle to hold the six-story tower of the Weavers’ Cell from leaning outward into the open basin. 

If I let go, I do not fall. I simply rest here, pinned gently against the cold obsidian by the six small river-pebbles tucked into the pockets of my hip-strap. My weight is almost true today—perhaps half an ounce light. I can feel it in my upper ribs, a subtle, insistent lift that wants to pull my shoulders toward the surface, but my feet, planted wide on the smooth basalt ledge, keep me anchored. 

My four rib-slits flare and collapse—*open, press, close*—in a rhythm so long and deep that a terrestrial pulse would seem like the fluttering of a moth’s wing beside it. The water entering my chest tastes of wet iron, bruised bronze-leaf, and the faint, sweet sting of active volcanic vent-gas. It fills the thick, fibrous lard behind my ribs with a steady, quiet warmth.

*Clack.*

The sound comes through the bone of my lower jaw, vibrating through the basalt before it reaches the water in my ear-depressions. 

I do not turn quickly. Nothing moves quickly here. I let my broad neck turn slowly, my plum-dark eyes dilating until the iris vanishes entirely, opening the double pupils into two huge, dark wells to drink the meager light.

Ten yards above me, drifting past the third-story window-aperture of the masonry, is Vael. 

Vael is carrying a slab of pressed tuber-jelly wrapped in a broad leaf, held against their stomach with one long, double-jointed arm. Their skin, usually the dull slate-gray of damp mortar, is flushed across the collarbones with a slow, rippling pulse of pale mustard-yellow—the signal for *rest-approaching* or *food-shared*.

I do not speak with my mouth. I press the back of my palate together and snap the hard cartilage plates. 

*Thump-clack.* 

The sound carries through the submerged street, crisp and clear. *I am anchored. I am working.*

Vael does not answer with sound. They tilt their head, their clear nictitating membrane sliding lazily across their wide, lidless eyes to clear a speck of floating silt. Then, with a single, languid push of their broad, six-toed foot against a projecting window sill, they alter their trajectory. They do not swim; they simply glide through the indigo space between us, their long, leather-smooth body cutting the water without leaving a ripple, until their feet find the ledge beside mine.

They reach out. A broad, heavy thumb presses against my throat-skin, feeling the slow pulse of my gills, checking my heat. I hold still, letting them read my temperature. In return, I lay my hand on their shoulder, feeling the weight belt they wear. 

Vael is light today. They have been up near the reed-frames, working in the upper canopy where the plants are dry and full of air pockets. I reach down to my belt, unhook a small, flat slate disc—about the weight of a plum—and slide it into the braided loop at Vael’s hip. 

Instantly, the faint, upward drift in Vael’s torso ceases. They settle into the water, perfectly suspended, hovering an inch above the basalt flags as if resting on a bed of down.

Across their forehead, a dim, greenish-yellow sheen blooms and fades. *Balanced.*

We sit together in the quiet nook for a long time. Time here is measured not by the passage of the brassy disc overhead—which we can barely see through the green canopy and twenty yards of water—but by the slow, white accumulation of lime-crust on the stone cables, or the time it takes for a tuber-vent egg to soften in the deep heat-wells.

Vael breaks off a corner of the tuber-jelly and presses it into my palm. It is dense, oily, and tastes of rich earth and hot mineral water. I chew slowly, my heavy jaw-muscles working the dense paste, feeling the warmth spread down my throat and into the fat-banks around my spine.

Far above us, a single glass-eel weaves through the high kelp curtains, its translucent skeleton catching a stray shaft of pale noon light and flashing like a needle of silver before vanishing back into the canopy. We watch it go with the quiet, mild interest one accords a passing cloud.

Above that eel, beyond the curtain of vines and the suspended reed-bridges where the elders are currently balancing one another, lies the ceiling of mist. The Light-Without-Weight. 

I think of it rarely, and when I do, it feels like a bad dream told by a stone-cutter who drank too much fermented vent-foam—a terrifying, thin void where the water disappears, where a body becomes so hideously heavy that it cannot float, but instead falls until it smashes against the dry, cold rocks at the bottom of the world. A place where there is nothing to hold you up.

I press my back deeper into the corner of the basalt column. The rock is hard, unyielding, and warm. The kelp-cable beneath my hands is taut and thick, vibrating with the deep, low hum of the planet’s heart. 

I am thirty feet below the light, three thousand feet above the plains, weighted with river-stones, held in the warm, heavy, dark hands of the water. Nothing is falling. Nothing will ever fall.

I take another small bite of the jelly, close my eyes, and listen to the stone.
