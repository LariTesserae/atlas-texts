I would not be in the air. 

The upper dark is too thin, too fragile, too filled with the wet, desperate snapping of suede edges colliding in the pitch. To be a Vault-Ribbon is to live in perpetual terror of the turn—the single clumsy stroke that tangles a wing-strut and drops you out of the humid sky, tumbling through thirty feet of absolute night into the teeth below. 

Nor would I be in the slurry of the troughs. To be a Tecton is to be too vast, too ancient, trapped beneath the mountain of chitin in a slow, centuries-long digestion, driving the world’s heartbeat with the bruised, aching muscle of a chest plate pounded endlessly against slate.

No. If I am anywhere in this cold, wet cradle, I feel myself sinking—down past the flying ribbons, down past the slick, ropey coils of the Slit-Eels weaving their vinegar-smelling bellies through the gaps—until the weight catches me. 

I am in the pavement.

Specifically, I am an **Iron-Biter**, but I am not the one on top, carrying the wet falls of the Vanes on my dorsal plate. Nor am I the one on the bottom, whose three jaw-rings rasp directly against the ice-cold basalt. 

I am the **middle shell**.

***

To exist here, locked in the central tier of the living floor, is to know the world purely as pressure, friction, and sound. 

There is no light to miss; the concept of a sky or a boundary is a non-thing. Consciousness is not a flame here—it is a heavy, metallic coldness, a quiet awareness of boundaries where gunmetal-colored armor meets gunmetal-colored armor.

Above me rests the flat, heavy belly of another of my kind. I can feel the three spiked pads of its rear legs digging into the grooved ridge of my back, pushing off me to gain purchase. The weight is enormous—a smothering, stony gravity that presses my carapace down until my ventral plates are almost flush with the shell below me. Over decades, this endless, shifting grinding has worn my dorsal armor until it is as smooth as blown glass, polished by the slick, cold grease of ten thousand neighbors passing over.

Below me is another back. It moves with a rhythmic, agonizing slow-shudder—eight short, jointed legs thrusting forward in turns, carrying us both across the wet shale. I do not choose where I go. I am part of a biological glacier, moving at the speed of eroding rock, wedged so tightly into the mass that to turn my body a single degree would require breaking the shells of six others.

And I do break sometimes. A hairline fracture blooms along my left lateral flange—a sudden, sharp ring of shock that radiates through my biomineralized hide. 

Instantly, the white latex comes. 

It does not bleed out into the dark; my thick, copper-rich blood reacts with the icy condensation dripping from above, instantly curdling into a dense, rubbery scab that seals the gap. It smells sharply of wet iron and bitter sulfur. Beside me, a Pale Slit-Eel, attracted by the chemical burst, drives its hard, spade-shaped head into the seam between my shell and my neighbor’s, searching for exposed flesh. Its smooth, scaleless body rubs against my cilia—cold, rubbery, smelling of vinegar—before it realizes the seal has held, and it squarms away into the dark, hunting a softer failure.

I do not feel fear. Fear requires an outcome that can be avoided. Here, there is only the engine.

My mouthparts do not touch the stone—that is for the bottom layer—so I feed on what drips down through the stack. The rich, iron-heavy runoff from the upper shells; the crushed, mineral-dense paste of broken Vane-wings that sink through the cracks; the dense, microbial foam squeezed out by the constant friction of our bodies. My three concentric jaw-rings do not rasp rock; they rotate in slow, alternating counter-clockwise cycles, idling, grinding the sludge into a thick, cold grease that fills my gut and turns my shell to iron.

And then comes the pulse.

It begins miles to the east—a sudden, deep-earth shudder that travels not through the air, but through the basalt beneath us and up through the three layers of our bodies. 

The sub-bass hum of the subterranean Tectons alters its pitch. The steady *thrum-thrum-thrum* that vibrates through my ventral plate snaps into a sharp, violent tremor. A giant is rolling in the slurry trough below. 

Around me, millions of sensory cilia stiffen simultaneously. The darkness, which was a wall of grinding glass, becomes a single, coordinated shockwave. 

The layer beneath me surges. My own eight spiked legs, pinned until now against my neighbor's polished back, find purchase in the grooved carapaces beneath. I dig in. The three rings of my mouth lock tight. The entire pavement shifts—a massive, rhythmic heave that rolls across the basin floor like a tide of wet iron. 

We slide forward. Shell scrapes shell with a deafening, splintering scream that echoes nowhere, swallowed instantly by the sheer density of the crowd. A Threader is pinched between my lateral edge and another’s shield-boss; its cartilaginous ribs pop like dry twigs, its pale grease lubricating our forward push. 

We do not know where the wave leads. We do not care. There is only the momentum of the mass, the cold wetness dripping from unseen heights, the smell of ozone and copper, and the endless, comforting crush of the dark.

I am a single tooth in the mouth of the bottom of the world, grinding, sealed, and endlessly carried along.
