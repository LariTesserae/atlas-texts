The copper band on my left wrist is biting today. 

It is the wind coming over the notch—it brings a dry, thin friction from the peaks behind us, and the metal bleeds the static off my skin in little, silent sparks that taste like salt at the back of my mouth. I am leaning my left shoulder against the wet interior wall of the fourth-tier vault, where the basalt is dark as oil and smooth from the continuous weep of the seam.

I am not sitting. I am standing, but my weight is sloughed off into the rock. The stone is colder than my skin, but only slightly; after a few minutes, the boundary between my hip and the mountain disappears entirely.

I would be here: on the high terrace, where the log aqueduct branches into the upper residential face. 

I am one of the first. I have no name that needs to be tossed through the air; if someone below needs to know where my weight is anchored, they do not call out. They strike the base of the central pillar twice with the flat of a wooden mallet, and the vibration travels up through thirty feet of raw granite, through the flagstones, through the damp, broad soles of my feet, and strikes the bones of my inner ear like a struck bell. 

*Here,* my throat answers, not with a word, but with a low, dry rumble pitched far beneath the whistling of the unglazed window behind me. The sound travels down my spine, into the slate floor, and back down to the terrace. They feel me. The work continues.

My task this afternoon—if afternoon is the right measure for the light that sits trapped and silver in the basin’s spray—is not heavy. I am waiting for a four-ton lintel of green cedar to settle. We bound it in wet tallow-cured leather three days ago and pinned it into the rock face with cold-hammered copper dowels. As the timber drinks the damp air of the basin, it swells, expanding into the mortise we cut into the basalt. If it swells too fast, it will split the stone face along an unweathered grain. 

So I stay here, my palm pressed flat against the seam where wood meets grey rock. 

I can feel the cedar breathing. It moves in microscopic twitches—a slow, wet pressure, like an eel turning in deep silt. Every half-hour, a timber-moth lands near my fingers. Its wings are pale as frost, and its eyes glow with that soft, cold, greenish light, like water sitting in a copper bowl. It doesn't fear my hand; my hand smells of the same spruce tallow and wet iron as the beams it was hatched in. It licks the grey dust from the margin of the cut stone, its palps moving with tiny, rhythmic scrapes, then flutter-falls away into the shadows of the vault roof.

Below me, through the wide, square window opening, the basin is brilliant with glare. The light doesn't fall from above so much as it bounces between the wet walls, held suspended in the fine mist that rises from the channel drains. 

I watch a slate-hawk drop from the sixth tier. It doesn't flap. It opens its heavy, scaled wings, catches the cold draft rising from the subterranean slot canyon below the street grates, and leans into the turn. For three seconds, it is entirely still in the middle of the air, its dark keratin plumage shedding the spray in tiny, bright beads, before it rolls over and vanishes down into the dark slot where the blind crayfish breed.

Humans—if they ever came up the scree, clattering and bleeding on the razor-edges of the basalt blocks—would look at this vault and ask what it was *for*. They would look for the smoke of fires, for carvings on the pillars, for the marks of a king or a god. They would find only the smooth cedar bench, the wet slate floor, and the clean, endless weeping of the mountain through the stone seams.

They would think we were waiting for the rest of our life to arrive.

They would be wrong. The vault is not an empty box to be filled with possessions; it is a volume of quiet air carved out of the thunder of the mountain. To stand in it, cold-skinned, wet-footed, feeling the static pull out through the copper on your wrist while the mountain vibrates against your ribs—that is not preparation. That is the whole thing.

A tremor runs through the cedar beam under my palm. A deep, wet *thunk*—the wood has seated itself into the notch. The grain has locked. The mountain has accepted the timber.

I lift my hand. My palm leaves a dark, damp print on the pale cedar that begins to dry instantly in the draft. 

I walk to the edge of the terrace flight. My bare soles grip the slick, moss-rimmed slate without a sound, the wet skin conforming to the sharp, un-eroded corners of the steps. Three levels down, a woman is sitting on a low wall of grey blocks, scraping liverwort from a water channel with a flat copper blade. Her shoulders are wet with spray, silver threads of water running down her bark-cloth tunic.

I do not drop down to join her. I do not ask her how long the water has been flowing true.

I simply press the heel of my foot hard against the top step and send a single, clear vibration down the flight. 

She doesn't look up, but her blade stops for a second. Her chest rumbles once in response—a low, deep frequency that vibrates the water in the aqueduct beside her into tiny, concentric rings.

*It holds,* she says through the stone.

*It holds,* I answer through my heels.

I step back into the high vault, lean my spine against the dark basalt, and let the cold glare of the mountain fill my eyes.
