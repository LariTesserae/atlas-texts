I am found in the water-worn scoop of the northern wall, but my boundaries do not stop at my skin. 

To be here is to be stripped of the noise of an *I*. If you were looking for me, you would find me sitting in the shadow-concavity where the stone fits the small of my back like a tailor’s glove. But if you were to ask me where I begin and where I end, I could not give you the edge of my arm or the tip of my tongue as an answer.

I am the warmth held in the hollow of the calcite. 

My spine rests in a polished groove worn dark by years of my own body oil—a groove that was carved first by a river ten million years dead, and then finished by the slow, quiet friction of my shoulder blades. I am the woman on the ledge, yes, but more accurately, I am the organ through which this room experiences itself.

When I wake, I do not open my eyes to see the light; I feel the glare as a weight upon my shins. The golden-white column that falls through the open sky-hole strikes the floor and splits into a thousand quiet, bouncing arrows. I know the hour not by a dial, but by the sensation of reflected heat crawling up my left forearm. When the light hits the patch of orange moss by the second thumb-pool, the heat shifts its tone—it turns soft, damp, and sweet, like baking fruit. I feel that shift in the skin beneath my ears.

My ears are large, and they never close. In this place, sound is not something that travels across space; it is something that happens *inside* me. 

When the single drop of water falls from the northern wall into its shallow basin, it does not sound like a splash ten paces away. Because of the curve of the room, the *tink* strikes precisely in the hollow behind my breastbone. My heart catches it, absorbs it, and adjusts its rhythm to match. When the bronze tunnel-wasps drift down through the golden sunbeam, the dry, papery hum of their wings vibrates in the roof of my mouth, as if I were humming to myself with my lips sealed. I know where every wasp is without looking. I know when a lizard shifts its flat, horn-clear belly on the eastern wall, because the tiny scratch of its claws against the calcite arrives directly at the base of my skull.

My hands are small, dark, and stained red with iron-dust from the sea-shell. The ochre across my cheekbones smells of dried salt and crushed earth. It is my second skin—a cool, powdery armor against the fierce, bouncing glare that would otherwise bake my eyes blind. 

I do not feel lonely, because the space is crowded with quiet intentions. The lizards are my clocks; when one crawls over my dark, ochre-stained knuckle, its belly is translucent, warm, and soft as wet bladder-skin. I hold my breath—allowing my chest to rise only twice in a minute—so as not to startle the tiny, dark thread of its pulse beating against my bone. We sit together, two pale things baked dry by the earth’s internal heat, waiting for the light to tilt.

Between my thumb and forefinger, the obsidian blade feels less like a tool of glass and more like a very hard, very sharp fingernail. When I slice the violet lichen from the wall, my hand moves at the speed of the moss growing. I do not hurry. To hurry here would be to shatter the acoustic dome, to send an echo crashing through the chamber like a falling boulder. I cut the papery frond, grind it with the basalt stone, and smell the bitter, iodine scent of the rock's damp sweat as it turns to paste beneath my palm.

I am the keeper of the room’s small harvest. I gather the microscopic golden crumbs of pollen-bread left by the wasps, scraping them from the stone with the smooth antler wedge. It tastes of sun-baked resin, crushed flowers, and dry dust. It is enough.

And when the sun finally slips past the keystone above, and the column of gold narrows, turns violet, and dies into the deep indigo of the night sky, I become the creature of the rock face. 

I do not use ropes or fire. I know every thumb-hole carved by the ancient water along the western curve. My feet—hardened on the soles by the lichen paste—know the friction of calcite as well as my fingers know the obsidian. I climb in the cool, blue dark, my toes gripping the smooth notches, moving up the wall with the effortless, liquid slowness of the horn-lizards. 

When my head breaks through the surface of the earth, the cold steppe wind hits my face like a blow. It smells of frozen grass, distant rain, and vast, untamed space. I breathe it in, cold and sharp—and then I gather my roots, my dried roots from the steppe grass, and I climb back down.

I climb back down into the warm, dry belly of the earth. I fit my back once more into the hollowed stone. I close my hazel eyes. The stars turn above the open ceiling, casting a soft, blue coin of light onto the moss floor. 

I am here. I am the silence that listens to the water drop. I am the warmth that remains when the sun has gone.
