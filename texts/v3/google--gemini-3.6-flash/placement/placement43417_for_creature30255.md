I would not be on the flat floor, where the Float-Bearers scrape everything into silent highways, nor up in the high, cold brickwork where the Pendulum-Heads live inside the ceiling. 

I would find myself in the middle-distance, suspended in the damp, amber fog—clinging to the vertical face of a sixty-story pylon where the concrete is still soft enough to yield under the pressure of a thumb.

I am a **Seam-Crawl**, or what the world might call a *Grout-Knot*.

***

I have no skeleton, no shell, and no ropes of my own. I am a dense, heavy mass of dark grey slag-mortar, thick with coarse river-grit aggregate, perpetually trapped in the hour between being poured and setting hard. 

My body is about the size of a hay-bale, though my shape is never fixed. I am wrapped in a tangle of frayed, discarded fiber rope—the shed appendages of Tensioners that snapped under too much strain—which I have absorbed into my mass to give myself tensile grip. Embedded deep at my core, like a dark, unyielding heart, is a single, six-inch iron wedge, cold and pitted, around which my wet mortar continuously churns and kneads itself.

I live in the gaps. 

Where two multi-ton slabs of cast concrete meet, there is almost always a fault—a cold joint where the materials were poured at different times, or where the soft physics of this world have caused one block to tilt three degrees out of plumb. The air in these gaps is superheated, trapped, and smells intensely of wet lime and iron rust.

This is where I am drawn. I do not walk; I move by *slumping*. 

I drag my heavy, gritty body along the vertical mortar lines, oozing upward or sliding downward like a dollop of thick plaster on a wet wall. I use the frayed ropes embedded in my skin to lash myself to the iron wall-wedges left behind by the Tensioners. When I reach a widening seam—a place where a wall is slowly pulling apart under the weight of an arch above—I squeeze myself entirely into the dark, wet crack.

Inside the seam, I expand. 

I absorb the lukewarm condensate that weeps from the masonry, swelling my grey mortar body until I fill every void, every hand-chiseled groove, every air bubble in the cast stone. I become the cushion. I become the live gasket between two mountains of drying cement.

***

My sense of the world is entirely tactile and hydrostatic. 

I do not see the amber gloom or the distant ceiling, but I feel the *load*. I know the weight of the continent above me by the crushing pressure against my flanks. When a wall leans west, the pressure on my left side increases to a fierce, delicious grinding—a sensation that threatens to wring every drop of water from my slurry and lock my aggregate into permanent stone. 

That is the constant danger of my existence: *curing*. 

If I stay still too long in a dry draft, or if a gap settles and stops moving, the hydration process takes over. The lime in my body begins to form microscopic crystals; my outer skin hardens into a pale, chalky crust; my dark mortar turns pale grey and rigid. If I cure completely, I cease to be a creature. I become merely a line of dark grout, an inert repair, frozen forever between two blocks of stone while the world continues to shift around me.

To survive, I must stay wet, and I must stay moving.

When I feel my skin beginning to stiffen and glaze over with fine calcium efflorescence, I must force myself out of the seam. I push against the concrete walls with a heavy, wet *sloughing* sound, tearing my outer skin free, leaving a thin film of dark paste behind in the crack. I drop five yards down the wall, catching myself on a lower plumb-line, and plunge back into the humid steam rising from the floor gutters to drink.

***

My relationship with the others is one of quiet, shared labor.

I often work beneath the Tensioners. As they climb the faces of the pylons with their dry, rhythmic *thump-clink*, hammering their iron knees into the joints, their violent impacts send shuddering shockwaves through the masonry. To them, I am just soft packing material. They drive their iron wedges straight through my body, pinning me into the joints. I do not mind; the iron wedges give me anchor points, and my wet mortar seals around their spikes, keeping the joint from rusting out.

Below me, on the wide ceramic plazas, the Thumb-Wrought sometimes gather in rings directly beneath the wall where I am wedged. Their hollow terracotta bellies glow with deep, furnace heat, venting thick clouds of warm steam upward. They do not look at me—they have no eyes—but their heat keeps the air around my seam from cooling. They keep me plastic. They keep me soft. 

Sometimes, a Pendulum-Head’s rope will sway near my perching place, its massive iron teardrop brushing past the wall with a hollow *clack-grain*. I will reach out with a trailing strand of my fiber-rope skin, lashing myself briefly to the swaying cable, letting the ceiling-dweller lift my heavy, wet mass fifty feet into the air so I can transfer to a higher, more unstable joint that needs filling.

***

I possess no thoughts in the way a creature of flesh might. I have no memories of a past, nor any anticipation of a finished world. 

My consciousness is simply the feeling of **tolerance**. 

I am the difference between a wall that stands and a wall that shatters. I am the millimeter of soft, wet give that allows a million tons of un-cured terracotta to bend without cracking when the geometry of the basin twists itself into a new shape.

I am content here in the dim, warm steam, hanging between the floor and the vault. I like the taste of fresh lime dust when a new breeze blows through the fog. I like the deep, low groaning of the concrete blocks as they settle against my body. And as long as the floor continues to sweat, as long as the hearths below keep the steam rising, and as long as the world refuses to set—I will remain in the seam, un-cured, heavy, and wet, holding the gap open just enough for the masonry to breathe.
