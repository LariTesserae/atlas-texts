It begins not with a sight, but with a weight—the cold, unyielding pressure of dark slate on my left flank and the cold, unyielding curve of cast iron on my right. 

I am lodged in the narrow gap between the basin’s central engine casing and the bedrock, four feet of pale, slate-grey flesh contorted to the contour of a long-dead gear-train. I have no eyes; the faint green phosphorescence drifting on the stagnant pools beneath me is meaningless to my skin. What I know of the world is a map of textures, chemical gradients, and acoustic shocks: the greasy velvet of iron-oxidizing bacteria, the bitter salt of copper runoff, the thick smell of cold mutton tallow that rises from my own calloused ridges, and the endless, shivering hum of water traveling through metal.

I am a lung-slug, moving through the middle of the basin at the speed of settling silt.

My forward end rests inside the drowned mouth of a three-inch gate-valve. My barbels—eight soft, pale fingers of cartilage and nerve—are fully extended into the valve’s flooded interior, fanning the dark water. They are sweeping for the chemical bloom of an egg-mass: those gelatinous, cold-veined ribbons anchored deep within the pipe by the siphon-sacks above. The scent is here, faint and iron-sour, laced with the tallowy tang of fresh jelly.

Along my flanks, my pulse-holes open with a slow, wet *schlub*, drawing in the clammy mist that hangs beneath the ceiling, then closing tight to press the cold moisture through my internal gills. With every breath, my heavy body expands against the rusted teeth of the gear-bar. I do not tear; my outer skin is thick as boot-leather, scarred and calloused by decades of dragging myself across slag, bolt-heads, and the glass carapaces of the isopods that crunch softly beneath my underside like river-gravel.

I leave behind a trail of grey, tallowy slime. It fills the pitted iron of the machinery, smoothing the sharp edges left by the long-vanished builders, greasing the stalled teeth so that my passage sounds like a wet leather strap being pulled through a dry wooden pulley.

Then, overhead, the clockwork shifts.

I feel it first in the bone-structure of my belly, which rests upon a stone ridge in the mud. The upper cistern has reached its tipping point. A sudden, heavy *thnk-clack* reverberates down through the iron framework, vibrating through the copper flues, through the slate walls, and straight into my muscle. 

The surge comes—a rush of cold water tumbling down the sloped trough. The half-submerged paddlewheel turns. *One. Two. Three.*

Then the chiming. *Clink, tonk, chime.*

The vibration strikes the water like a physical blow. The chord travels through the flooded channels, setting every molecule of cold copper-salt in my tissue ringing at once. 

I freeze. My barbels lock in place, rigid as pale rootlets in the dark valve-housing. Around me, the basin goes absolute in its quiet. I feel the distant, collective pause of the others: thirty feet away, the hundreds of lantern-shaped siphon-sacks hold their breath, their inner cartilaginous valves locked tight; down in the shingle-pools, the glass-shelled isopods cease their dry sand-rasping, their tiny brass mandibles frozen mid-scrape, their clear bodies catching the faint amber spark of a dying junction-box.

For three seconds, the basin is a single, held breath of iron and lard.

Then the paddlewheel comes to rest in the mud with a soft, final squelch. The counterweight swings back. The pressure releases.

My pulse-holes open again—*schlub*—and I draw in a long, cold draft of wet coal-dust and sulfur. My barbels loosen, drifting forward into the dark valve until the tips touch something soft, yielding, and thick with eggs.

I do not hurry. Hunger in this cold is a slow, steady pilot. 

As I pull my rear quarters through the gear-teeth, my tail scrapes over the stone ridge beneath me. It is not slate. It is the petrified flank of another lung-slug that died in this same channel two centuries ago. Its flesh did not rot; the mineral-heavy water replaced its grease with iron-carbonate and lime, turning its breathing orifices into dark, stone rings over which the runoff now spills. 

My tail fits perfectly into the curve of its calcified back. When my own grease runs dry and my pulse-holes finally stiffen in the cold, I will remain right here, wedged between the engine casing and the rock. The velvet orange skin of the bacteria will coat me; the water will deposit its iron in my skin; and I will become another step in the stair, another gasket in the machine, holding the current true for whatever comes after me to creep across the dark.
