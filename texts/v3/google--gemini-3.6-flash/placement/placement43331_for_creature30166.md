First, there is no *me*. There is only the grid.

It enters not through ears—which were flattened and sealed beneath thick folds of gristle generations ago—but through the lower jaw. The long, heavy spade of orange enamel rests flush against the liverwort, pressed down by the forty-hundredweight draft of my skiff-shaped skull. 

Through the wet, five-foot mattress of root-fiber comes the song of the plateau. 

To the west, three miles off, the engine at Node 3 hits its backstroke. A sharp, localized twitch in the left side of my jawbone: *clack*. 
A second later, the deep-bore pulse travels through the black basalt beneath the roots, rattling my molars: *thrum*. 
Then the high-pressure relief valve vents into the thin air, a fine, shivering needle of vibration that runs up through my snout and warms the grease pockets behind my tiny, dark eyes: *hiss*.

I am a spade-vessel, and this is how the world is read. It is not a place of shapes or colors; the blinding six-thousand-foot sun is merely a heavy, white iron plate pressing against the back of my neck, burning away the moisture until my grease-glands leak to compensate. The world is a map of tremors, laid out in vectors of steel and stone.

I am standing near the third trestle of the East Flume. I know the trestle is there because its three-inch iron rivets are anchored deep into the basalt, and every time the flume carries a surge of cold meltwater eastward, the legs groan. The vibration of rust-flaking iron tastes sour, like old copper in the mouth.

My four feet—broad, spongy dishes of fibrous cartilage a foot across—spread my weight so completely that the silver-stemmed liverwort barely groans beneath me. Beneath my soles, I can feel the shallow tide of sheet-water rushing across the unweathered rock. And in that two-inch dark gap between the stone and the moss-roots, I feel the ribbon-glassers. Thousands of them, three feet long and paper-thin, contracting their pale flanks in perfect, silent unison every time Node 3 strokes. They are the subterranean pulse; I am the surface weight.

My head moves slowly, a massive, muscular sweep to the left. The broad enamel plate of my jaw scrapes through the top inch of the turf, shearing away the silver liverwort-heads. 

Inside me, the work is slow and hot. My three stomachs are heavy, wet furnaces. The icy, mineral-rich stems ferment in the dark, turning into a thick, starchy sludge that gives off a faint, lavender-scented steam. It vents from two spiracles along my flanks, rising into the crisp, cold air as a brief, pale plume that catches the glare before the wind shreds it. 

Above me, fifty yards up, a saucer-leaf over-fills. 

I don't see the heavy, crystal cord of water drop, but I feel its impact when it hits the moss twenty paces away—a fat, rounded *thum* that ripples through the root-mattress like a stone dropped in a pond. A second later, a micro-drop in the burning heat on my hide tells me a sky-skimmer has glided through the splash-zone, its translucent skin-sails catching the warm steam rising from my back to gain altitude, soaring toward the next green pillar. It is a fragile, glass-boned thing, existing only in the light and air. I do not envy it. The air is empty and loud; the moss is heavy, wet, and real.

A new vibration enters the jaw.

It is not an engine stroke, nor the drag of the creeping steel power-cable that stretches across the crag behind me. It is light, sharp, and rhythmic—the asymmetric clatter of iron-shod boots on a steel catwalk. 

A Keeper has come out of the oxide-red pump-house. 

He is walking the high flume trestle, fifty feet above the moss. I feel his passage as a moving point of stress along the iron legs of the structure. He stops above my position. 

Through the bone of my chin, I feel the sharp, ringing *clang-clang-clang* as he strikes a bronze scraper against the flume casing, knocking away a crust of mineral scale. The sound sings down the iron leg, into the basalt, up through the silver liverwort, and straight into my skull. It is a clear, metallic bright-spot on my map—a brief, sharp star of vibration in the low, endless thrum of the plateau.

I do not look up. To look up is to offer my soft, ungreased eye-sockets to the UV fire of the thin sky. Instead, I shift my broad, skiff-like body three inches to the right, placing my dished feet carefully over a hollow in the basalt where the sheet-water runs fastest. 

The water here is bitter with tallow runoff from the piston housings upstream, rich and slick. My tongue, thick as a tree root, laps it from the moss-stems.

The Keeper strikes his pipe once more, a single *thrum*, then his footfalls recede back toward the warm, coal-smelling belly of the pump-house. The steel cable creaks on its iron pulley half a league away, carrying a cable-louse inch by creeping inch toward its mating splash-pool. Beneath my belly, in the wet dark, the ribbon-glassers flex.

I lower my jaw again. The cold liverwort yields with a wet, heavy hiss. 

The engine at Node 3 beats: *clack-thrum-hiss*.

My internal fire hums, sending a fresh cloud of warm steam into the freezing sunlight. The plateau holds us all—the iron, the water, the root, and the flesh—bound together in a long, patient century of work that has no need for words, or eyes, or ending.
