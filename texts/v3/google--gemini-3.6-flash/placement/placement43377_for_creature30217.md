I do not exist in this chamber as something looking in from the outside. There is no outside. There is no horizon, no distance, and no light that carries meaning to me. 

If you were to look for me, you would find me at the base of the second piston—the one whose stroke lags a fraction of a heartbeat behind the primary intake. I am pressed flat against the polished ironwood floor, my wide, cask-shaped chest wedged into the angle where the floor-weave meets the sliding bark of the column.

I am a Loom-tender, though the name is a word for a mouth I do not have and eyes I do not use. To myself, I am simply the pressure that moves against the pressure.

***

My world begins in the bone. 

Before I move, before I reach, there is the *thunk-whack*. It is not a sound that enters an ear; it is a solid wedge of kinetic force that strikes the broad soles of my four feet, travels up the thick, marrow-dense bones of my legs, and slams into my chest. My heart does not beat on its own steady cadence. It is too small, its muscle too thick and heavy, to drive my cold blood through four feet of dense, oil-soaked flesh. It waits for the floor to kick it. 

*Thunk-whack.* The shockwave strikes, the broad muscular valves in my central artery snap open, and a heavy surge of thick blood rushes up into my neck-hump and down into my spade-shaped tail. My life is a forced oscillation. Between the piston strokes, I am still, held in the thick, muggy suspended animation of the air; on the stroke, I am driven forward.

My face is pressed against the floor. Through the wide, fat-cushioned pad of my lower jaw, I read the chamber. 

It is not a picture; it is a map of tension, heat, and viscosity. 

To my left, thirty paces across the slick floor, the trough is cold and fast—a thin, vibrating line of clear water running smooth through the ironwood weave. Beneath me, the floor is warm, humming at a high, dry frequency: ten thousand Scrapes are gathered here, their heavy indigo shells clicking against one another as they drink the atomized mist. I can feel the sharp, pin-prick vibration of their tiny hooked feet through the soles of my hands. They feel like a rough, living carpet, crisp and oily.

Further out, in the wide, cool spaces where the spray doesn't reach, I register three slow, smooth glides—the Lather-skins. They move like oil poured across wet slate, their broad bellies leaving faint, slippery trails of mucus that dull the wood’s vibration where they pass.

I push myself up. My broad, sprawling limbs move in time with the three-part rhythm of the room. One-two-three. Rise, step, plant. 

The air entering my crescent nostrils is thick enough to chew. It tastes of hot vegetable grease, the sharp, electrifying tang of torn plant fibers, and the deep, resinous sugar of squeezed sap. I take a deep breath, testing the air-mix with the sensitive cilia lining my nasal passages. 

*There.*

A sharp, thin spike of heat registers in the upper vault—eighteen feet up, where the eastern taproot arch meets the sliding ceiling sphincter of the middle column. It is a dry heat, blistering and lean. The resin in that joint has burnt away under the continuous friction of the stroke. The wood up there is beginning to bite wood. If it bites, it will shear. If it shears, the stroke will break.

I do not hurry. Hurry has no place in a engine that has run for ten thousand years.

I swing my heavy spade-tail to the left, using its weight to sweep a path through the carpet of Scrapes. The beetles scatter with a dry, rustling rattle, their hooked feet scraping against the floor, but many are too slow. I drop my broad left hand into the thickest cluster, feeling for the heavy, resin-heavy domes. I scoop up four of them, pressing them tightly into the palm-cup of my hand. They do not fight; they simply lock their legs and vibrate violently against my thick, hide-like skin.

I turn toward the center column. The piston is rising now, a huge, bark-sheathed pillar lifting through the ceiling with a wet, heavy suction sound that pulls the air right out of my nostrils. 

I reach out with my foot, finding the deep, step-like ridges in the arching taproot wall. My toes—short, blunt, and tipped with thick, horn-like nails—clamp around the smooth wood. I begin to climb.

Up here, near the vault, the sound changes density. It becomes thick, almost liquid, bouncing off the bone-smooth dome and pressing against my back like a heavy hand. Condensation drips continuously from the archways, washing across my oiled skin in warm, greasy streams. 

Through the wood in my hands, I feel the telegraphy of the upper world. High above me, perched in the crevices of the arch, the Thrum-fowls are tapping. I feel the sharp, precise *tick-tick-tick-tap* of their beaks travelling down the root-fibers into my fingers. They are reporting from the high canopy—feeling the fluid pressure rising in the great sky-conduits miles above the root-mats. The sap is heavy today. The pressure is building. The pistons will need to stroke faster soon.

I reach the overheated joint. The air here is so hot it scorches the fine hairs in my nostrils. The wood of the ceiling ring is pale, scraped raw and dry by the relentless up-and-down slide of the column. A thin curl of smoking, shaved wood hangs from the seam like a pale ribbon.

I do not use a tool for this. I lift my hand, bring the four Scrapes to my mouth-slit, and crush them between my heavy, broad gum-plates.

The sound is a sharp, wet *crunch*—instantly lost in the thunder of the room. Their indigo shells shatter, releasing a flood of boiling, golden grease—concentrated resin, mineral oils, and thick, raw fat stored in their heavy bodies. 

I spit the steaming mass directly into the dry seam. 

With my broad, flat palm, I work the mixture deep into the tight crevice, rubbing the shattered chitin and hot grease into the wood grain. The friction of the rising column catches the oil, dragging it up into the hidden sleeve of the sphincter. 

Instantly, the dry heat vanishes. The high, screeching vibration in the wood softens, relaxes, and drops an octave into a smooth, wet, hydraulic glide. 

*Thunk-whack.*

The middle column drops. The shockwave hits me full in the face, blowing back the sensory filaments in my nostrils and rattling my heavy skull. The newly greased joint swallows the piston smoothly, spitting out a fine, warm cloud of aerosolized resin that settles over my back like a coat of liquid glass.

I stay there for a long time, clinging to the root-wall, my palm pressed flat against the seam, feeling the joint breathe. Smooth. Wet. Heavy. The friction is gone. The balance is restored.

Slowly, I descend the root-arch, stepping back down onto the polished floor. 

I do not go far. I move back to the base of the central piston, where the floor is warmest and the resin pool is thickest. I sink down, folding my four sprawling legs beneath my cask-like body, resting my flat head directly against the heavy, vibrating foot of the arch.

The beetles crawl back over my limbs, their tiny, cool claws scratching against my warm, oil-slick hide as they clean the stray resin from my skin. A Lather-skin glides past my flank, its cold, slick tail brushing against my side as it hunts in the dark. Above me, the Thrum-fowls tap their silent, rhythmic messages through the ceiling, and high in the vault, the steady amber light shines through the leaf-membranes on a scene that does not change.

I close my nostrils against the heaviest mist. I let my heart settle into its borrowed rhythm. 

*Thunk-whack.* My blood moves.
*Thunk-whack.* My flesh hums.
*Thunk-whack.* 

I am where the machine remembers it is alive.
