I would be found in the deep, wet curve where the highest stone step retreats beneath the umbrella of a canopy plant—a place where the emerald shadow is so thick that the midday sun loses its glare and becomes a cool, liquid green. 

I would not be moving. In a world that has perfected the art of quiet efficiency, movement is an indulgence spent mostly by those who must gather or dissolve. I would be anchored.

I would be a **Tendril-Keeper**, though I would have no concept of that name, nor of any word at all.

***

At first, I am only weight and moisture. 

My body is a broad, pale mound of soft, rubbery tissue, folded into a fissure of the dark gray rock like tallow poured into a groove and left to set. I have no eyes, for there is nothing in this hollow that needs to be seen from a distance; the light here is not an image to be captured, but a warm, heavy blanket that presses against my skin, telling me precisely where the sun rests in its luminous bowl. I have no limbs to lift me, no mouth to open, no lungs to expand. I breathe through the continuous, microscopic seepage of the wet moss that coats my flanks, drinking the mineral-heavy water directly through my outer membrane.

Where my body meets the stone, I am deeply rooted. Microscopic threads of my own flesh have worked their way into the hair-fine cracks of the bedrock over centuries, feeling the ancient, slow heart of the ridge-line—a mountain that forgot its peaks eons ago and settled into this smooth, waxy trough.

In the dark beneath my body, I feel the water entering the hollow. It bleeds from deep fissures, rich with iron and silica, cold from the stone’s core before the sun warms it in the upper basins. I can feel its pressure rising. By contracting the dense, muscular rings hidden deep within my lower tissue, I widen or pinch those stone veins, letting the water spill fast or slow across the terraces below. I do not decide to do this through thought; I do it as a heart beats, or as a leaf turns toward heat. When the basins down-terrace grow shallow, their thermal signature shifts, becoming sharp and hot; my skin reads that rise in temperature across forty feet of humid air, and my submerged roots relax, releasing another cool, mineral-rich pulse into the light.

***

My true extension, however, is not into the stone, but into the spire.

Rising from the shallow pool just two inches from my northern flank stands one of the ancient needles—that thin, seam-free spire of unnamed material that bends the light around its tip. It has stood there longer than the canopy plant above us, longer than the species of glass-reeds that tip and spill in the lower basins. It is cold to the touch, yet it boils the water at its base without heat, sending up a perpetual, ghost-like plume of cool vapor.

From the crest of my pale, moss-encrusted back, I have grown a tendril. 

It is not flesh, not entirely. It is a hollow, vitreous tube of organic glass, as thick as a finger, drawn out from my own secretions over a hundred years of patient, unhurried growth. It reaches across the damp gap of air, curving slightly to meet the needle’s smooth, non-metallic side. Where the glass of my body touches the surface of the spire, there is no joint, no scar, no seam. My cells have mimicked the spire’s molecular grid, growing into it, fusing with it until the boundary between living tissue and ancient artifact has vanished.

Through this glass bridge, I do not drink water or gather light. I receive the hollow.

The spire is an antenna for things that make no sound. When a school of blind, ribbon-thin swimmers orbits a spire down-terrace, their synchronized, rhythmic displacement of the water sends microscopic bio-electric ripples through the ground-grid; I feel it as a faint, rhythmic tingling along my dorsal ridge. When a multi-limbed amphibian on the upper steps vents a thermal pulse to signal its readiness to spawn, the air's density shifts, and the spire registers the change in pressure; the signal travels down the glass tendril into my nervous system as a sudden, pleasant wash of internal heat.

I feel the leaf-shadow when it glides across the slick rock twenty yards away—not as a predator, but as a subtle change in the surface tension of the stone’s moisture film. I feel the heavy, breathy *kiss* of the bladder-grazers’ vacuum-feet as they climb the lower steps, their water-filled bellies sloshing gently against the wet wax of the terraces.

I am the point where the forgotten technology of the past and the silent biology of the present meet and hold hands.

***

In the middle hours of the day, when the mist suspended in the air turns the hollow into a blinding, shadowless room of silver light, a bladder-grazer wanders up to my alcove. It is old; its clear parchment skin is scarred with thin, white lines where glass-reed nodes have scraped it, and the green pulp in its digestive sacs moves with a slow, sluggish tide.

It does not see me. It feels my mass through the warmth radiating from my lichen-covered skin. 

Slowly, heavily, it presses its pale, water-filled flank against mine. 

Through the skin-to-skin contact, a vast, silent conversation takes place in an instant. I feel the chemical density of its internal fluid—it is low on silica, rich in calcium. I feel the steady, four-part contraction of its dorsal heart-sacs. In return, my skin releases a microscopic surge of warm, mineralized oil into the film of water between us, anointing its parchment flank with nutrients that soften its aging hide.

For a long time, we rest together under the green umbrella of the canopy plant. The grazer’s body is warm and heavy; my tendril hums with the cool, steady voltage of the spire. Between us, a midge with triangular, iridescent wings lands silently on the glass tube that bridges my back to the machine. Its wings flicker once, twice, splitting the diffused sun into two blue needles of light, before it folds them and stays.

The grazer does not move. I do not move. Below us, a glass-reed fills with water, grows heavy, tips, spills its clear burden into a shallow pool with a silent splash, and springs back up to catch the mist again.

There is no memory here of what the spires were built for. There is no memory of the voice that once spoke words, or the hand that ground the first stone into dust. There is only the light, the wet rock that has forgotten its sharp edges, the cold spire that boils without fire, and the slow, warm pulse of my body, holding the hollow in balance until the sun begins to dim.
