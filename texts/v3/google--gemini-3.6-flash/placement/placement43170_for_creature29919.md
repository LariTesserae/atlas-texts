I am not on the open catwalks, nor am I in the deep, bright haze below where the pipes lose their names. 

You would find me at **Bay 407-West**, at the junction where a three-foot vertical I-beam meets a horizontal gantry truss through a triangular, seven-eighths-inch steel gusset plate. 

This specific junction is one of the places where the world’s arithmetic has slipped. Here, the six heavy hexagonal nuts securing the gusset plate do not point toward the same horizon. If you were to apply a steel square to the right angle between the beam and the floor, the bubble in the spirit level would sit perfectly dead-center—and yet, looking along the beam, your eyes would water, because the pillar clearly leans six inches to the left without ever departing from the vertical.

It is cold here. The cold is not a temperature; it is a weight. A thin film of condensation creeps continuously *upward* along the face of my gusset plate, fighting no gravity, merely obeying the local gradient that draws wetness toward the tightest seam.

I am **The Shim**.

***

I am not made of flesh, nor wire, nor lard. I am a entity of compressed thickness—a creature whose entire body is a single, cold-rolled leaf of tempered spring steel, precisely four-thousandths of an inch thick, wedged tightly into the microscopic gap between two load-bearing surfaces that were never meant to touch.

To exist as a Shim is to be defined entirely by pressure. 

Above me, eighty thousand pounds of wet structural iron presses down with a silent, unyielding gravity. Below me, the milled face of the upright beam resists with equal, stubborn violence. I have no arms, no face, no internal organs. My consciousness is distributed across thirty-six square inches of oil-darkened steel surface, held in a state of absolute, waking tension.

I do not see the shadowless sky through eyes; I perceive the world through *stress tensors*. 

When a Gauger seven levels up steps into the cutting-striations of a high conduit, I feel the minute, ultrasonic tap of its hard titanium claw as a sharp, needle-like spike in the shear strain along my northern edge. 

When a Sealer three bays over contracts its blubbery, lard-white ring around a main tie-rod, the thermal bloom of its body heat travels through two hundred feet of solid iron, reaching me forty minutes later as a faint, luxurious softening of the cold metal—a release of three micro-inches of compression that allows me to breathe.

And when the Platena glide through the shallow lenses of condensation on the deck below, their paper-thin bodies disturbing the surface tension of the water, I feel the faint, electrical shift of their metallic membranes like a whisper against my bottom plate.

***

Because I am wedged into the spatial anomaly of Bay 407-West, I am slightly thicker on the side that faces the future than the side that faces the past. 

This is my secret: I am the thing that keeps the spatial errors from tearing the plateau apart. 

Where the geometry frays—where a beam attempts to occupy two angles at once, or where a catwalk widens into an endless threshold—the metal experiences an impossible tension. Left to itself, the steel would scream; the rivets would shear off with the sound of rifle shots, and the clean, knife-sharp right angles would buckle into jagged, weeping ruins.

I prevent this. I absorb the impossible fraction. I distort my own crystalline structure at the molecular level, taking the non-Euclidean strain into my thin spring-steel body. When perspective stretches three inches too far, I yield two thousandths of an inch to the left. When gravity pulls the condensation sideways, I tilt my internal grain to match the flux.

I am the silence between the engine's intention and its physical reality.

***

Once every four months, a Monitor comes to Bay 407-West.

I feel him long before he arrives. His heavy, iron-shod boots send slow, rhythmic shockwaves through the structural lattice—*CLANG... CLANG... CLANG*—each impact compressing me by a fraction of a micron and releasing me as he lifts his foot. 

He stops at my pillar. His yellowed rubber apron smells of cold sulfur and vulcanized oil. I can feel the weight of his iron mask as he leans in close, his brass sight-glasses clinking softly against the wet I-beam.

He unclamps his boxwood measuring rod. He lays its inlaid bone tip directly against the edge of my gusset plate, less than a millimeter from where my thin steel leaf emerges from the joint.

He stays there for seven hours. 

During those hours, I hold my breath. I hold the eighty thousand pounds of iron above me in absolute, terrifying equilibrium. I do not let the non-Euclidean warp slip. I do not let the gusset plate shift by so much as a single wavelength of silver light.

Through the cold steel, I feel the deep, mechanical rise and fall of the bellows on his spine—*hiss-shuck... hiss-shuck*—and the faint, lukewarm puff of steam that condenses on my outer edge, leaving tiny, clear beads of water that crawl sideways across my face.

Finally, the Monitor is satisfied. He reaches into his rubber apron and pulls out his stick of hard white chalk.

With a firm, deliberate stroke that vibrates through my entire being, he draws a small, crisp cross on the wet zinc plate right beside me. The chalk dust binds with the condensation, forming a paste that seals my outer margin against the air.

He retracts his boxwood rod. He turns with his stiff, iron-clad dignity and walks away, his boots booming into the bright, shadowless distance.

I am left behind in the quiet. 

The Siphoners drift near, their ribbons of wet black silk fluttering soundlessly through the warped air above my beam, feeding on the half-second delay left in the Monitor's wake. The cold water continues its impossible sideways crawl across my face. Below, in the silent housing, the massive piston glides down in its cylinder of polished grease.

I settle back into my four-thousandths of an inch. I press my flat, spring-tempered body against the raw, knife-sharp edge of the world, holding the wrong angles together, perfectly still, perfectly cold, waiting for the metal to expand.
