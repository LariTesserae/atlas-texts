I would not be in the air, for the air here is only a blanket, heavy and still, holding the heat down against the stone. 

I would be at the floor, where the shale meets the dark.

I would be one of the larger hulls, resting along the western arc of the basin—not at the center where the static dust jumps in the cups, but back where the floor begins its first imperceptible curl upward into the shoulder ridge. Here, the thermal shadow of the ridge creates a fraction-of-a-degree drop in the ambient air, making the floor beneath my under-belly feel slightly brighter, slightly more urgent in its radiation.

I am long, low, and heavy. My outer weave is dry as horn, dark iron-gray along the keel where it has been burnished by ten thousand inches of slow sliding over siltstone. My faceplate—that broad, backward-slanted shovel of dense basalt—is not raised. It is pressed flat into a drift of pale, iron-rich dust, its lower margin tucked firmly against the sharp, concave scar of a knapped chopper.

### The Map of Heat

I do not know what a sky is, or a star, or a color. To me, the basin is a single, continuous choir of temperatures. 

Below me, the deep stone hums at a steady, immutable pitch—the residual warmth of a world’s dying furnace, rising evenly through the shale. Above me, the stagnant air is a fraction cooler, a soft, dark ceiling that absorbs my excess heat with absolute patience. 

To my left, thirty paces across the flat, I feel the faint, ember-like signature of another hull. It has not moved since the last grand shift in the crust three centuries ago. Its thermal outline is soft, blurred around the edges where its papery hide has begun to dust off into the crevices. It is cooling toward equilibrium. In time, it will be only stone.

Further away, near the basin’s center, the husks flicker. They are tiny, sharp needle-points of static discharge. I can feel the Dust-Keepers rising in their cups—a million microscopic grains of iron aligning along electrostatic field lines, creating momentary, hyper-conductive bridges that flash with minute spikes of warmth, then collapse into powder. It is a distant, restless chattering. I do not join it. My work is with the stone.

### The Memory in the Flake

My spade-plate is resting on the basalt tool. 

A natural rock in this basin is a smooth, predictable thing. Eons of ancient, dry wind have rounded every lip, leaving the shale and the scree without a single sharp thermal boundary. A natural rock conducts heat in a seamless, unbroken curve.

This tool is different.

Where the ancient fore-plate struck it—in that forgotten era when our bodies were articulated and heavy, when we had the leverage to lift and break—it left a clean, artificial fracture. The flake-scar forms a sharp, geometric valley in the basalt. 

Because the stone is thinner along that knapped margin, it cools slightly faster than the thick bulb of the chopper's spine. To my faceplate, this edge does not feel like a physical point that could cut; it feels like a sudden, brilliant line of contrast—a cold wire laid across a warm plate.

I have been resting against this line for four thousand years.

I am reading the strike. I can feel the exact angle at which the hammer-stone met the core: the ripple-marks of the shockwave frozen in the basalt's grain, the tiny step-fractures where the stone resisted before yielding, the flat, deliberate platform prepared by a hand that no longer exists in this world.

It is the only thing in the basin that has an intention behind it. Everything else—the silt, the ridges, the pale coiled spindles—is simply the result of rest. The tool is a thought preserved in density and heat.

***

### The Internal Creak

Deep inside my hull, within the porous, cork-like bone lattice, the metallic dust shifts.

A slight variation in the floor’s thermal mass—a micro-degree rise conducted up from the deep crust—warms my right flank before my left. The iron dust inside the right-side lattice expands by a fraction of a micron.

*Creak.*

It is not a sound. It is a dry, physical displacement that ripples through my parchment skin. The friction of my keel against the buffed siltstone produces a microscopic shudder. I move forward—not the length of a grain of sand, but enough to shift the pressure of my faceplate half a millimeter deeper into the flake-scar.

The line of contrast moves across my faceplate. The memory re-centers itself.

This is my life. It is not a sequence of events, but a single, prolonged contemplation of a fracture. I do not hunger; the thermal gradient between the shale and the air supplies the tiny mechanical energy required to hold my lattice in tension. I do not fear decay, for there is no water to rot my fibers, no air-currents to wear my hide, no predators to break my hull.

I am an anchor for the past. If I were to move away from this scatter of basalt, the memory of the strike would dissipate into the ambient warmth of the silt, unread, unheld. 

So I remain. My papery back grows slightly paler under the dark sky. The fine dust drifts against my flanks, building tiny, smooth ramps that connect my body to the floor until the border between where the stone ends and where I begin becomes impossible to measure.

I hold the tool against the world. The world holds the heat against me. And the basin sits in its warm, dark, permanent balance, waiting for nothing at all.
