The world does not enter through the eyes. Eyes are small, wet, pink-rimmed things, good for nothing but the dim, mustard-yellow smear of a sodium bulb or the pale glimmer of rime on a root-edge. 

The world enters through the chin.

My lower jaw is flat, bare of fur, and pressed hard into a shallow trough of cold heartwood where a sixty-foot root snaps against an iron mounting plate. The timber here is dense, tight-ringed, and frozen as granite. When I press my throat and mandible into the grain, the rift opens up inside my chest like a map drawn in iron and shockwaves.

To the south, three miles through the dark wood-vaults, the main piston of the Third Vent is descending. It is a long, heavy slide—a three-minute groan that travels up through my ribs, vibrating my spleen and loosening the ice frozen into the fur behind my shoulders. I can feel the rust flaking off its guides three miles away; I can feel the microscopic hitch in its second stroke where the guide-rail bowed twenty years ago. 

To the north, higher up in the crotch of a shear cliff of white timber, a small, light vibration steps across a root-bridge. *Click-click. Click-click.* Iron spikes in canvas boots. One of the two-legged grease-things is moving. It is light—hardly two hundred pounds of water and felt—and its pattern is jerky, cautious, lacking the grand, forty-minute cadence of the flywheels. It carries a warm tin of tallow; I can smell the scorch of its charcoal brazier drifting down on the cold fog, smelling of burnt lard and wet ash.

I do not move. My heart gives a single, heavy *thud* in my chest, then waits. Ten seconds pass before it beats again.

I am wedged into the angle where a pale, cork-like root, as thick as a house, meets the bottom flange of a steam-trap. The air here is bitterly cold, so dense it feels like breathing crushed glass, but beneath my belly, where my thick mat of hair is caked with frozen sap and coal-dust, my skin is tough and hot. 

Above me, the gasket-crust is thick. It grows in greasy, ivory-colored shelves across the seam where the iron bites into the timber. The continuous, bone-deep thrum of the engines has been shaking the sap out of the heartwood for decades, mingling it with the orange powder of rusted bolts. I raise my head—just an inch, lifting my chin from the acoustic deck—and drive my snout into the underside of the lowest shelf.

The fungus yields with a dull, rubbery tear. It tastes of cold tallow, pine oil, and the sharp, metallic tang of iron scale. As the skin breaks, a thick, amber sap oozes out onto my whiskers, freezing almost instantly into tiny, glass-like beads that rattle against my chest. I chew slowly, my flat, heavy molars grinding the frozen resin and dormant grubs into a thick paste. 

Around me, the roar is complete. It is not something heard in the ears; my auditory canals are solid wedges of pale cartilage, quiet and dark as a stone in a well. The sound is a physical mass. It sits on my spine. It presses my heavy ribs down against the wood. It fills the air so entirely that the fog itself seems to pulse in tiny, rhythmic jumps against the amber halos of the gantries.

The grease-thing above me stops. Through my paws, I feel the sharp *thump* of its knee hitting the iron ladder, then the slow, rhythmic trickle of hot mineral oil being poured into a grease-cup overhead. A stray drop of the oil falls three hundred feet through the dark and strikes the fur of my flank—a tiny spot of searing, unnatural heat that smells of deep earth and crushed mineral. I do not flinch. 

The piston three miles south hits the bottom of its stroke. 

The impact is a flat, terrible shockwave that rolls through the floor, lifting my chest a fraction of an inch off the timber. The shear cliff behind me shrieks as a new fracture line opens three inches deeper into the heartwood, spraying a cloud of fine, white wood-dust over my back. 

Then comes the hold. Twenty minutes of stillness from the great piston, filled only by the high, thin vibration of the distant flywheels and the slow, icy drip of condensation from the dark canopy above.

I sink back down into the trough. I lay my jaw flat against the grain once more, feeling the long, slow cooling of the iron plate, the faint twitch of a tallow-eel in the slush-pool forty paces below, and the steady, glacial growth of the pale wood beneath my belly, expanding by a hair’s breadth against the cold iron that binds it. 

I close my milky eyes against the amber light. I am thick, cold, and wedged deep into the grain. The world hums through my bones, and I am full.
