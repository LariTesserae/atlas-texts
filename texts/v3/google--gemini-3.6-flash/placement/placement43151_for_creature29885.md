I am not on the surface, where the airborne silica sweeps thin and cold against the obsidian needles, nor am I hanging from the ceiling like a tooth of slate. 

I am down near the bottom, wedged into a shallow, razor-edged step-fault three inches above the orange ember-glow of the bedrock. I am an old specimen, heavy-built and thick-shingled, caught in the transition between the grinding mobility of the middle layer and the stone-fused permanence of the Root. 

Above me, the weight is immense. Two fist-sized Millers are parked across my dorsal plates, their mandibles working in a steady, dry *scrape-scrape-scrape* as they strip static-charged sulfur from the rim of my third shingle. Beneath me, my spade-feet have driven so deeply into a basalt cleavage plane that the mineral salts extruded from my pores have begun to sinter my underbelly directly to the horn of the mountain. I can no longer back out. I am becoming part of the floor.

I have no eyes, but I am far from blind. My world is a three-dimensional map drawn entirely in voltage and strain.

Through my greigite carapaces, I feel the precise geometry of the pocket. The basalt slab behind my tail-shingle is holding eighty thousand tons of shear-stress; I know this because the static tickle flowing through my copper-filament web is sharpest on my left flank, humming at a high, dry voltage that tastes of iron dust and raw sulfur. 

The copper threads—the metallic webbing—are inside me as much as they are around me. They creeping through the open pores of my graphite-sponge gut, drawing the thermal current up from the magmatic floor and dispersing it through my heavy iron plates. I am a single node in a circuit that spans the entire pocket. When a Climber on the ceiling six inches above me shifts its weight, the change in capacitance ripples down through the web and twitches my corundum jaws before I have time to process the movement.

There is no quiet here, but there is no noise either—not in the way a soft creature with ears would know it. Sound here is pure mass in motion. 

When the mountain speaks, it does not strike my hearing; it compresses my entire body. I feel the subterranean torque building in the bedrock beneath my spade-feet—a long, agonizing coil of potential energy that bends the obsidian walls by invisible micro-fractions. The heat spikes. The metallic webbing glows with a sudden surge of static that turns the dust between my carapaces into a cloud of blue sparks.

Then: the yield. 

The screech of the colliding slabs is a physical fist that slams my plates together, grinding the sulfur flour in my gut into dense, molten greigite. A Miller directly above my right flank is caught in the focal point of the shear; its brittle, iron-sulfide shell shatters with a sharp, pneumatic *crack*, disintegrating under the mountain's heel into a hundred pressurized, jagged shards.

I do not mourn it. The concept does not exist in the rock. Instead, the sudden collapse of its body drops a fresh shower of pulverized mineral meal directly into the reach of my jaws.

My heavy silica mandibles close on the shattered armor of my neighbor, crushing the mineralized plates back down into raw sulfur, iron, and dormant, seed-like crystallites. As I chew, my sponge-gut expands, drawing in a long, hot gulp of superheated carbon dioxide and dry salt. The heat from the floor drives the reaction; my pores bleed a thick, searing kerogen paste that flows into the fresh cracks on my dorsal shingles, instantly baking into a new, glass-hard rim of slate-grey stone.

I am the anvil. I am the jaw. I am the circuit through which the mountain’s slow, crushing violence becomes form.

I will stay here, locked in this step-fault, taking the weight of the swarm on my back and passing the magma’s heat upward, until the next great tremor finally shears the floor beneath me—crushing my iron shell to dust, and scattering my crystallite seeds into the fresh, hot cracks of the rising mountain.
