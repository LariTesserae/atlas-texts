I am not above the stone, nor am I beneath it. I am the motion that stays between.

At first, there is no shape to me—only the warm, heavy pressure of a world that does not end because it does not begin anywhere else. There is no *over there*. There is only the immediate, velvet thick of the fluid and the constant, rising breath from below: a draft that is neither wind nor current, but the slow thermal exhalation of the cistern’s iron floor.

My mind is not behind eyes. I have no eyes to hold a horizon, and here, a horizon would only be a lie. My mind is spread out along three meters of pale, cartilage-bound muscle, and concentrated most sharply in the long, trailing threads that hang from my jaw—the barbels. 

They are my sight. They are my memory. 

I float in the central vault, suspended in the gentle, body-warm draft. I do not flap; I do not strike. I simply ripple—a single, long S-curve passing down the chamois-soft length of my flank—and the water yields without a splash, without a murmur. 

My barbels trail down, brushing the red terra-cotta of the floor tiles. 

*Clack-clack-clack-clack.*

The sound does not come through ears; it travels straight through my cartilage spine, a low, rhythmic vibration like a distant loom. I feel the millions of tiny horn-mouths of the Slab-Backs as they graze the purple veil. I can read the density of the herd beneath me by the sharpness of the chatter in my ribs. Where the tiles are hottest, near the deep floor-seams, the *clack* is frantic, thick, crowded. Where the stone cools slightly toward the outer pillars, the cadence thins to a sporadic click.

I drift higher, rising on the heat. A gas bubble passes me—a heavy, low *thrum* that displaces the water against my belly like the pass of a large hand. 

My barbels sweep across a stacked wall of dark basalt. I do not see the blackness of the rock, but I know its grain. I know that four lengths of my body above the floor, the stone changes from the flat, coarse clay of the tiles to the dense, polished river-basalt. I feel the subtle, wave-like ripples carved into the blocks by iron chisels ten thousand years ago—chisels held by hands that had fingers, though I do not know what fingers are. To me, those ripples are simply the texture of the world’s ribs. Time has smoothed them so completely that my skin slides over them with less friction than water against water.

I reach a copper pin. 

I know it before I touch it. The water here tastes different—sharp, metallic, bitter with the bleed of oxidized turquoise. My barbels tingle as they pass through the plume of the Patina-Worms. I feel the sudden, microscopic snap as hundreds of fine, thread-like gills collapse all at once, drawn back into the split stone by my passing pressure-wave. I do not eat the worms here; the copper in their guts is heavy and acrid. I leave them to their dark, bitter gardens, anchored in the expanding joints of the metal.

Instead, I curve my body, folding double in the narrow clearance of an archway. My cartilage spine flexes without a crack, without a effort, bending around the geometry of the room as if I were made of the fluid itself.

I am hungry, but hunger here is not a desperation. It is merely a tilt in the rhythm.

I drift along a lintel where the Slab-Backs are thickest, their salmon-pink shells overlapping like shingles on a roof. I press my snout close to the stone, dragging my barbels over their hard, humped carapaces. I feel the young worms sprouting on their backs—tiny, feathery crowns brushing against my skin. 

I open my pharynx. 

There is no strike, no flash of teeth. Only a deep, sudden expansion of my chest—a heavy, silent vacuum that pulls against the basalt. *Thump.* Three, four of the broad isopods are torn from their hydraulic grip, sucked backward into the dark, muscular chamber of my throat. The stone beneath them is left temporarily bare—a pale, clean patch of clay where the purple veil has been scraped away. In a few days, the veil will bleed its bruised-indigo light back over the scar, and the young Slab-Backs will drift in from the margins to graze again. 

Everything here is eaten, but nothing is diminished.

After I feed, I am heavy. I seek the bracket.

I know which bracket is mine, though it is identical in measure to twenty others around the perimeter. It is the one directly above the western floor drain, where the great Cistern-Mouth down in the dark works its slow, muscular mantle. Every few minutes, the Cistern-Mouth pulls down a draft of settled detritus and pushes up a long, purified jet of hot mineral water. The draft catches my tail and holds me aloft, weightless, hovering before the carved stone face that projects from the wall.

I slide my flank against it.

The face has no nose left. It has no lips, no eyelids, no brow. Ages of water—and ages of skin like mine—have ground those features down into a single, perfect, egg-shaped oval of black basalt. 

I coil around the bracket, wrapping my three meters of pale muscle around the cold, ancient stone. I press my side hard against the oval, dragging my chamois skin back and forth, back and forth across its surface. 

I shed my old skin here. It comes away in thin, translucent ribbons that float upward into the purple gloom, carried away by the heat to feed the veil or to be drawn down into the filtering mouth in the floor. And as I shed, my body polishes the stone. The face gives me a smooth surface to become clean; I give the face the soft, wet friction that keeps it featureless, keeping it safe from the crusting worms and the sharp mandibles of the isopods.

We are making each other smooth. We have been doing it for so long that the stone and the flesh have agreed upon the same shape.

As I coil there, resting in the velvet dark, listening to the untiring *clack-clack-clack* of the floor, I feel no desire to wander beyond the slate pillars into the cold, deep drop-offs outside. There is nothing out there that has been shaped by a hand. There is no heat out there that matches the heat inside my own muscle.

I am a Ribbon. I am the eyes the stone lost when its carved faces dissolved. I am the pulse that measures the room from key-pin to floor-tile, held in the dark, warm hands of a sanctuary that forgot its builders, but remembered how to breathe.
