It begins in the lower jaw. 

Before there is sight, before there is a sense of weight or light, there is the hum. It travels up through the temporal bones, vibrating the teeth in their sockets with a frequency so deep and ancient it feels less like sound and more like the tension that keeps the skull from coming apart.

I am lying on my right side in the triangle of dark behind the western pump. My forehead is pressed hard against the raw concrete wall. 

The concrete is where the two worlds meet, and I am the line between them. Through my skin, through the narrow, unyielding bone above my eye, I can feel the vast, cold, suffocating weight of the lower bottoms—miles of black, saturated silt and ancient marsh mud pushing inward, trying to crush this hollow square. And against my back, radiating through the burlap and the oiled hemp, is the sweltering, iron-scented fever of the node: two thousand pounds of green-painted cast iron thrumming at a single, unvarying stroke, pushing the wet heat back out.

I am the slice of soft tissue caught between the cold earth and the hot engine. 

I do not open my eyes right away; there is no need to. The air is a thick, yellow-grey blanket that tastes of sulfur, wet slag, and the sharp, mineral tang of cylinder oil. I reach up with a thumb—thick-skinned, flat-tipped, the nail worn down to a blunt shield—and press the tallow-soaked cotton deeper into my left ear. The world outside my head is a silent, violent shaking. The cotton swallows the scream of the high-pressure return line and leaves me only the pulse, steady as a sleeping heart. *Thwack-thwack-thwack.* The vulcanized drive belt. Three inches above my hip. I can feel the displaced air of its passing sweep across my bare flank every second, cooling the grease on my skin before the steam scalds it warm again.

When I sit up, my wooden soles clatter softly against the ironwood planks of my shelf. My body knows the clearances better than my eyes do. I duck my head beneath the brass grease-line that weeping-bleeds a bead of fat onto my collarbone, swing my feet down, and touch the wet, smooth-troweled floor-slab.

I am small, but I am dense. My skin is hairless, grey-pink, and smooth as a scalded turnip, slicked with a permanent mantle of oil-mist and condensed water that keeps the heat from baking me dry. I do not bleed easily; the small cuts I get from the razor-edges of the pine-form concrete don't fester—the oil seals them, turning the scars into dark, graphite-colored lines that match the creases of my knuckles.

I pick up the brass oiler. Its long spout is warm to the touch, heavy with the thick, yellow lubricant I scoop from the settling pans. 

I move toward the sump, my feet sliding in the rhythmic, low-slung gait that keeps me balanced on the algae-skinned concrete. I do not look for the drive belts; I feel the heat of their friction against my cheeks as I glide between them. To step wrong would be to be caught by sixty pounds of lashing rubber and thrown into the churning teeth of the intake grate, but there is no stepping wrong. The floor-slab has its own geography of vibrations: near the pedestal, the concrete shudders in short, sharp needles; near the pit edge, the thrum broadens into a slow, rolling heave.

At the edge of the pit, the midges swarm in clouds so thick they feel like wet wool against my mouth. I don't brush them away. I lower the copper-wire basket into the black water where the four pipes disgorge their endless, lukewarm torrent. 

Through the clear oil-film on my eyes, I see them: the white fish. They are thin, sightless ribbons of tallow and bone, circling the suction throat. They do not know the sky; they do not know the marsh; they know only the current and the midges. I jerk the wire frame up. Three of them thrash silently in the mesh, milk-skinned and translucent, their tiny red hearts beating like sparks behind their ribs.

I do not use a fire. Fire would die in this damp soup anyway. 

I walk back to the western pump, reach out, and slap the three white fish flat onto the blistering top curve of the main drive cylinder. The iron is painted dark green, but the paint has long since burned away on the high housing, leaving bare, blue-black metal that runs hot enough to spit steam. 

The fish sizzle instantly. The smell rises—sweet, oily, hot fish-flesh mingling with the rich, heavy perfume of heated tallow. I flip them once with my thumbnail, scrape them off with the edge of my steel file, and fold them into my mouth whole. The tiny spines are soft as boiled thread. They taste of iron, river silt, and engine grease. It is good food. It keeps the fat on my ribs and the oil in my joints.

Above me, high beyond the tall, grey walls, a shadow passes. It is the blunt, dark geometry of an elevated railway gantry, carrying something heavy and dry through the upper air. A faint, distant tremor passes through the upper lip of the concrete box—a foreign vibration, thin and rattling, belonging to the city of light and dust. 

I do not look up. The sky is only a yellow sheet that vents steam. The city above is an abstraction, a place that exists only to produce the black water that feeds my sump, the rags that catch in my intake screens, and the copper wire I twist into fish-traps.

I take the brass oiler and press its spout to the main journal bearing. I feel the warmth of the shaft spinning inside its babbit sleeve—too dry, the vibration tells me; it’s rising three beats above its normal pulse. I press the thumb-pump once, twice. The thick, golden grease flows in. The vibration smooths out, dropping back into the heavy, chest-thumping cadence that fills the basin.

The pump is pleased. The basin is balanced.

Later, when the yellow light dims to its perpetual twilight, I will take my scraper and clear the intake screen of a drowned bird and a clump of saturated tar-paper. I will drink two mouthfuls of sweet, cold condensate caught in the tin funnel from the return line. 

And then I will climb back down into the triangle of shadow behind the green pedestal. I will lay my pink, hairless forehead against the crisp, sliver-sharp imprint of the pine board in the raw concrete wall. I will feel the cold, endless mud of the world outside trying to seep through the four-foot pour, and I will feel the roaring, boiling heart of the machine pushing back against it.

I will close my eyes in the warm, wet dark, plugged with tallow, wrapped in oiled burlap, and I will let the machine beat my blood for me until the water moves again.
