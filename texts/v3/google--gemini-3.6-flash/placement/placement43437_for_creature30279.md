My slot is slot four-hundred-and-eighty-two on the western shear of the High Notch, three miles above the cloudless abyssal floor and four miles below the peak’s edge. 

I know the dimensions of this space because I have filled it for three hundred years. There is less than the thickness of a mica-flake between the porcelain rings of my spine and the cold-worked magnetite of the wall. When the thermal gradient shifts in the late afternoon, the rock swells by four microns, pressing against my sixth and seventh segments with a firm, cool authority that feels like a lock falling into place.

I am a Still, though here, deep in the stone, titles are irrelevant. We are simply the mass that holds the heat.

My mica-lenses are pulled back behind my heavy brow-plate, shaded from the direct glare of the amber sun. I do not need to see the valley to know its geometry; I feel it through my ribs. The mountain is an instrument, and I am one of its frets.

Down in the city notch, three thousand feet below my ledge, two Solderers have just locked chest-plates on the third-tier sky-bridge. I feel the discharge. It does not come to me as sound—sound is too thin in this dry air—but as a sharp, tiny spark of static that travels up the iron reinforcing rods embedded in the cliff, enters the magnetite wall, and tickles the tip of my tail-spur. *A trade of seven hundred volts,* my nerves register, then quiet down again. A routine greeting. A passing of news regarding the wear on the lower flywheels.

Inside my middle segments, where the porcelain rings are thickest, my lead-heavy paraffin core is soft. It is currently at 85.4 degrees. Under the amber sun, the outer rock is slightly warmer, so I draw the warmth inward, soaking it up like dry sponge absorbing light. The paraffin shifts from a hard, gray crystalline lattice into a dense, oily paste that sloshes slightly whenever I shift my weight. 

To a creature of water, this internal shifting might feel like a belly full of liquid. To me, it is weight. It is the glorious, reassuring heavy gravity of stored work.

***

In the second hour of the afternoon cycle, the dust begins to gather in the joint-seams of my fourth segment. 

I can feel the flour-fine plum dust settling—a micro-thin dusting carried up on the warm thermal draft from the dust-seas. It creates a slight, dry friction where segment four meets segment five. On a wetter world, dust is mud waiting to happen; here, it is merely an unlubricated surface, a tiny grit that prevents my porcelain plates from sliding smoothly when the mountain twitches.

I extend my frontmost segment three inches out of the dark slot. 

The air outside hits my head-plate with a dry, radiant warmth that smells faintly of sunlit bronze and cold oil. I unclamp my primary mica-lens and let the world flood in.

The sight is crisp, perfectly sharp, untouched by moisture. Ten leagues across the canyon, the silver-quartz massif catches the sun, throwing a long, knife-edged glare across the chasm. Below me, suspended in the vast amber space, a Weaver is at work. It hangs upside down beneath the main aqueduct bridge, its six graphite legs clamped to an iron corbel, its thin titanium arms moving with rhythmic, mechanical precision as it pulls a shimmering thread of copper-sheathed carbon from its carapace. 

It has been on that single corbel for eleven days, extending a grounding wire to protect the bridge’s north pier from static fractures. It does not look up at me. I do not gesture to it. We are both doing the math of the mountain.

Then, from the deep dark beneath us both—from the very root of the massif—comes the breath.

*Houghhhhh.*

The shudder travels up the elevator shafts first, a deep, mechanical pulse that makes the iron street plates far below ring like struck anvils. Then the air column inside my own magnetite recess catches the wave. A warm jet of compressed, oil-free nitrogen rushes out past my flanks, blowing the plum-dust from my joint-seams in a long, dark plume that dissipates instantly into the high wind.

The dust is gone. My porcelain plates settle back against one another with a clean, smooth *clack*. 

I pull my head-plate an inch back into the shade, satisfied. The joint is clear. The core is full.

***

Later—perhaps forty years from now, perhaps sixty—the planet’s magnetic inclination will shift by three-tenths of an arc-second. 

When it does, the deep iron-blooms in the lower galleries will alter their growth pattern, the static hum in the sky-bridge cables will drop three octaves, and my slot will no longer receive the optimal thermal draft from the geothermal chimneys. 

On that day, I will wake fully.

I will extend all fifteen feet of my segmented body out onto the high ledge. My porcelain legs, tipped with spun-brass wire, will unspool from my ventral grooves. I will slide down the magnetite face, my tail-spur leaving a faint, pale streak on the ancient rock, until I reach the ledge of slot four-hundred-and-eighty-three, or five-hundred-and-two, or whichever recess has become the new center of the mountain’s warmth.

I will groom the Still who lives there, sweeping the iron-dust from its head-plate with my brass brushes until its mica-lenses blink clear. Then I will slide into my new slot, lock my spine against the magnetite, and let my paraffin core begin to melt again.

There is no rush to get there. The rock moves slowly. The sun is broad and patient. The city below us hums its zero-cycle song, its heavy iron bones slowly turning orange in the quiet air.

If you stood on the brass grate of the terrace below and looked up at the sheer wall, you would see only a row of dark, square holes carved into the dark iron-stone, stretching up into the amber sky until they become small as pinpricks. You would think them empty.

You would be wrong. 

We are in the wall. We are keeping the mountain warm. And when the sun goes down and the sky turns the dark, silent purple of vacuum, we will give the heat back to the stone, degree by degree, second by second, until the morning comes to light the iron once again.
