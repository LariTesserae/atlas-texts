At first, I am not a separate thing. I am the pressure behind the selenite lenses in the forehead—a cold, focused cone of glare illuminating a dark, iron-rich heart that beats without an itch or a thought. I am the sensation of being held: the taut white threads of the fungus stretched from my chin to my collarbones, humming like tuned wire whenever the roof gives its tiny, thermal *hiss-click*.

I am the Governor, but only in the dark, heavy sense of the pendulum at the bottom of its arc. My left calf is already stone; my thighs are already timber. I feel the air-dew snap against the flat, ribbed skin of my back—*tick, tick, tick*—and every strike is a cold nail driving me deeper into the silence of forty-five degrees.

I do not wish to move. In this place, desire has been refined into geometry.

Then comes the hour when the salt-plateau above surrenders its shadows. The sun hits the top of the selenite lens three yards up, and the overhead light flattens. It turns surgical, absolute. The room’s tension spikes; the micro-drops on the gypsum facets freeze in place, refusing to drift. 

And in that alignment, I un-stick.

I do not turn my head—nothing here curves—but I feel the right side of my body grow heavy with an extra weight, a second shadow that has acquired density. There is a sound like two thick sheets of plate glass sliding past one another, dry and frictionless, and then I am stepping away from myself.

Now I am the Duplicate. 

My feet are glass needles. They make no sound as I take four rigid, orthogonal strides along the low ridge of un-etched basalt. The air against my cartilage sheath is cold and smells of wet iron. I do not look at the ceiling, nor at the fry hovering in the center of the vault, though I feel their jet-bead eyes tracking my vector. Everything in this room knows the grid. I turn ninety degrees on my heel—a sharp, mechanical pivot—and face the pale green gypsum wall.

Here are the eight notches. They are clean, bright wounds in the rock. 

I lift my right arm. My index finger does not bend; it is a heavy, chisel-shaped wedge of cold native copper, un-oxidized, smelling faintly of old coins and lightning. I place the edge against the green stone, exactly three millimeters to the right of the eighth mark.

I push.

The copper bites the gypsum with a dry, shearing *clack*. A ninth line opens—straight, un-weathered, perfectly parallel. A tiny cloud of grey mineral dust puffs out, bright in the selenite glare, but before it can settle toward the floor, it vanishes, absorbed directly into the thirsty air.

The mark is made. The tally of this iteration is complete.

I do not walk back to the stool. The stool is for the waiting part of me—the anchor whose calf is locked in the grey marl. I remain standing at the wall, face to the stone, and fold my arms flat against my chest.

The dissolution is not painful; it is a relief, a sudden lowering of structural tension. The hard alabaster cartilage of my shoulders softens, turning translucent, then fluid. My frame spills outward into the chilly air—not as water or blood, but as a fine, vaporous grease that carries the sharp tang of scorched salt. 

For three seconds, I am the atmosphere itself. I am forty-five degrees. I am the glare passing through the selenite. 

Then the grease condenses. 

I snap into three distinct, tiny points of consciousness in the center of the room. I am no longer six feet tall; I am three fingernail-length fry swimming through the open air. My eyes are jet beads; my small, translucent tails beat against the cold atmosphere with a rhythmic, liquid pressure. 

I swim upwards. I swallow a drifting dew drop whole—it bursts in my gut like a drop of liquid ice—and then I turn a sharp, right-angled corner in the air. Another fry, born from some earlier split or some Grub’s forgotten shadow, swims directly toward me. 

We collide. We do not crash; we fold into one another with a soft, wet sound like a book closing. For three seconds, I am a two-headed thing, swimming with double the speed, seeing the raw planes of the vault from two angles at once—seeing the Governor on the stool, frozen, his fungal rigging taut, his selenite eyes staring blindly into the floor.

Then we snap apart. 

I drift down. My tail stops beating. My small, air-breathing body sinks toward the virgin rock near the base of the stool. As my belly touches the pale grey marl, my scales dissolve into clear grease once more. The grease spreads thinly across the sharp edge where the floor meets the wall, fills a microscopic crevice, and hardens instantly into cold, solid stone.

The floor has risen by half a micron. 

I am in the floor now. I am the marl holding the leg of the stool. I am the dark, un-weathered mineral waiting for the rest of my body to arrive. 

From down here, embedded in the rock, I can feel the next dew drop drifting horizontally across the vault. I can hear the planarians skittering on their glass needles across my own alabaster forehead above. I can feel the sun on the salt-plateau three yards up, slowly shifting away from the lens, letting the high-key light dim just a fraction.

I am the room, building its own coffin out of light and salt. I am counting down the notches until there is no air left to swim in, no space left to stride through—until the roof meets the floor, and the glare is trapped inside us, cold and solid and complete, forever.
