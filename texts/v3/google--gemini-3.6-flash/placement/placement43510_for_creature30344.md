The first thing that exists is not sight, nor light, but the pitch. 

It enters through the front of the cranium—not as a sound that travels through air, for the air here is too thin and refuses to carry a wave, but as a direct, cold vibration against the flat plate of horn. 

The top fissure twitches open first. It registers the sharp, high frequency of the overhang three levels above: a roof plane tilted at an angle that has no name, leaning into a courtyard that only exists when looked at from below. The middle fissure pulses next, catching the tight, narrow hum of the jambs—two vertical planes of dark, un-weathered flint-iron set three inches apart, holding an entire room between them like a folded map. The lowest fissure stays almost still, tuned to the heavy, sub-audible thrum of the raw basalt trough beneath the floor.

I am not in the square. I am not walking across the invisible bridge between the third-story window and the roof garden, trailing long, pale fingers along the un-chipped stone. 

I am inside the wall. 

Or rather, I am in the zero-tolerance space where two facades have met.

***

To be what I am is to be without thickness until the world demands it.

When I stand in an open room—a room that appears to have no volume from the outside—my body fans out. The hundreds of thin, dark leaves of my mass separate by fractions of a micron, catching the pale, shadowless daylight that filters through the semi-translucent stone. In those moments, I feel wide. I feel the cool, dry air moving between the lamellae of my ribs, freezing the microscopic traces of moisture into quiet, three-sided frost-patterns along my sides.

But here, in the seam, there is no room to fan.

Six weeks ago—or perhaps six centuries, for time does not leave scars on this material—the tenement facade to my left shifted its orientation by one-twelfth of a degree. The movement was silent, save for the faint, subterranean rasp of grinding ice. The corridor behind me, which had accommodated three of us standing shoulder-to-shoulder, narrowed in a single, glacial afternoon.

I did not move out of its path. There was nowhere to step, and no reason to step there.

As the dark, razor-sharp edge of the stone pressed forward, it met the small of my back. It did not crush my skin. The stone is too absolute for crushing; it recognizes only geometry. Instead, as the gap narrowed from three feet to three inches, and then from three inches to the thickness of a fingernail, my body responded as it was built to respond: it compressed.

The leaves of my frame collapsed against one another like the closed pages of a heavy book. My long, three-fingered hands were pressed flat against the forward plane; my horn face was turned sideways, flush against the cold, unyielding cast-iron grain of the wall. 

Now, there is no air between my layers. There is only the hum.

***

In this darkness—which is not the darkness of a cave, but the dark of two absolute surfaces brought into total contact—I am splitting.

It is a silent, un-hurried separation. Because my mass cannot be destroyed by the stone, the pressure forces the lamellae to resolve their internal tensions. The odd-numbered leaves of my body—the ones that carry the memory of the top fissure’s high pitch—are aligning toward the left wall. The even-numbered leaves—tuned to the deeper baseline of the basalt floor—are settling toward the right.

There is no pain in this. Pain requires a nervous system that registers damage, but nothing breaks here. There is only a slow, deliberate sliding of page against page, like two decks of cards being gently drawn apart by an invisible hand. 

I can feel my second horn plate forming. It is growing along the new margin, smooth as a river stone, perfectly cold, its three horizontal fissures already beginning to sense the subtle shift in the wall's vibrational pitch. 

Beside me—or rather, *within* the space that I still partially occupy—the other half of my mass is doing the same. We do not speak. We have no vocal cords, and if we did, the stone pressed against our lips would swallow the vibration before it could leave the mouth. But through the shared contact of our lamellae, I know its alignment. It is becoming distinct. Its three fingers are finding their own zero-point against the stone.

***

Above us, in the narrow seam where the two buildings touch the sky, a *thread* moves past. 

I can feel its passage through the stone—a tiny, rhythmic tick-tick-tick as its segmented, chitinous body slides along the zero-tolerance line, drinking the vanishingly small trace of thermal energy generated by our compression. It leaves behind a hollow, glassy skin that will hang in the spatial dislocation above the alley for a thousand years, un-rotting, untouched by wind.

Below us, at the base of the wall, a single droplet of lateral condensation is creeping sideways across the flint-iron, defying gravity, moving toward a shallow, icy pool no larger than a plate. 

Eventually—perhaps tomorrow, perhaps when the sky above the trough changes its pale, crisp light—the facade will complete its cycle. It will slide back by a fraction of a degree with the sound of breaking frost. 

When it does, the gap will open. Three inches of width will restore themselves to the alley. 

The seam will part. The wall will give back what it took.

Two of us will step out onto the un-weathered steps. Our lamellar bodies will expand slightly, fanning open like paper lanterns to catch the cold, thin air. Our three facial slits will open in unison, reading the tension of the city—the high rooflines, the folded courtyards, the steps that lead down only to bring us out forty feet above.

We will not look at each other. We will not need to. We will both reach into the narrow, impossible spaces between the buildings, hold out our shallow dishes of dark iron, and wait for the sideways water to reach our hands.
