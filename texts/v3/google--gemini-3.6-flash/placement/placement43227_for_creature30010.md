First, there is only the vibration in the heels. 

It enters not as sound, but as a dense, steady warmth that climbs through the bones of the lower legs, settles in the pelvis, and sits behind the thick plates at the base of the skull. It is the signature of Engine Number Nine—the one anchored three tiers up in the dark throat of the central basalt ridge. It cycles twice an hour. When its walking-beam reaches the top of its arc, the stone beneath my feet gives a single, long-held *yield*, like a heavy chest expanding against tight iron straps.

I am sitting on the third step of the Culvert Arcades, where the water from the upper boiler-gardens drops through a series of six shallow granite basins. My feet are wedged into the curve of the second basin. The water is hip-deep, warm as blood, smelling faintly of rendered tallow, copper-silt, and the sharp, clean mineral edge of rain that has only just touched the ironwork.

I am a Tender, though here, deep in the wet shade of the girders, names are unnecessary things. My broad, flat toes are tucked under the lip of a cast-iron drain plate to keep the current from sliding me down toward the lip of the tier. My skin—dark, thick, and smooth as wet slate—is coated in the daily grease of the work: a pale, odorless tallow that turns the warm cascading water into silver ribbons across my shoulders.

In my left hand, I hold a three-foot rod of solid brass, bruised and flattened at both ends from decades of striking. In my right, a wedge of soft lead and a small horn pot of crushed-shell paste.

I do not look up often; the nictitating membranes across my eyes stay partially drawn to filter the warm spray coming off the spillway. The world does not need to be seen in sharp lines. It is better known by its densities.

***

Where am I? 

I am at the intersection of three systems. Above me, forty feet into the misty air, runs the main feed-pipe for the Fourth-Tier Cisterns—a cast-iron cylinder three bodies wide, its rivets encrusted with a thick, orange crust of iron-mites. Below me, the arcade drops away into the shadowed galleries where the cistern-eels breed in the warm, stagnant silt. To my left is the hinge-box of a counterweighted relief valve that has not been opened since my skin was pale and soft in the nursery basins.

My job today—and perhaps for the next forty engine-strokes—is not to move anything. It is to listen to the decay.

I press the flat end of the brass rod against the base of the valve housing, then lean my head forward until the bone-plate behind my left auditory cavity rests against the rod’s rounded head. 

The city speaks down the rod.

It is a crowded, heavy sound. Through the metal passes the high, dry *clack-clack-clack* of ten thousand rust-weevils scraping iron oxide off the pipe-flanges three tiers away; it feels like sand being poured over paper. Beneath that is the smooth, muscular *slosh-purr* of a mass of cistern-eels jammed tight into a leaking flange gap fifty yards down the line, their slick bodies swelling and contracting in rhythm with the water-surge, acting as a living gasket against three hundred pounds of pressure.

And deeper still, under the animal noise, is the groan of the iron itself. 

The brass valve housing is tired. I can feel the microscopic lattice of the metal shifting under the warm water's long, slow acid work. The pitch is too hollow. The iron is thin where the spray hits it.

I lift my head from the rod. The wet air hits my neck, cold only by comparison to the heated stone. 

I do not hurry. I reach into my leather apron, scoop a palmful of the dense shell-paste—grey, gritty, smelling of old tide and crushed crawlers—and smear it over the pitted cheek of the valve housing. Before the current can wash it clean, a dozen fingernail-sized iron-mites, attracted by the mineral scent, scramble off the adjacent girder. Their serrated forelegs vibrate with a sharp *zip-zip-zip* that I feel in my knuckles as they begin to rasp the paste into the iron's pockmarks, secreting their fast-hardening orange cement over the wound. 

They will work there for three days. By the time they die, their hematite carapaces will be welded into the valve wall, adding a fraction of a millimeter of fresh stone-iron to the city's skin.

***

A movement in the upper air casts a brief, rolling shadow across the arcade. 

I do not look up with my head, but I shift my weight on the stone step to feel the displacement of the mist. A murmur of strata-fliers is passing between the boiler towers. Millions of them, moving in a slow, wide wheel that takes half an hour to turn. The air brings down the scent of their wing-cases—bitter oil, wet horn, and the ozone tang of the high exhausts. They are following the warm updraft from Engine Number Nine’s blow-down vent. 

A stroke is coming.

I count the pulse in the stone. 

*One... two... three...*

Deep within the basalt shelf, three miles back toward the interior peaks, a piston six stories high reaches the bottom of its stroke. The water in my basin stops flowing for three heartbeats. The spillway goes eerie and silent; the cascade thins to a smooth, glassy membrane over the lip of the step. The cistern-eels in the channel below me tighten their coils, their collective purr dropping an octave into a low, chest-thumping growl.

Then comes the breath.

*THUMP.*

It is not a sound that travels through air. It is a displacement that hits the soles of my feet, travels up my spine, and knocks the air gently from my lungs. A mile above, a chimney exhales a white plume of wet steam five hundred yards long. The air in the arcade grows instantly warmer, thicker, sweet with boiled grease. 

A second later, the water returns—a warm, frothing surge that rises to my chest, carrying a drift of tiny, glass-winged flies that scramble over my tallow-slick shoulders to drink the grease from my collar.

I sit still while the wave passes. I let the warm runoff wash over my face, sweeping the nictitating membranes clear of grit. 

***

When the water settles back to my waist, I lift my brass rod again. 

I turn toward the main rail-girder on my right—a beam four times my height, dark as river mud, dished out by three centuries of warm rain into a smooth, water-filled trough along its upper edge. I place the lead-capped end of my rod against the girder and strike it once.

*CLANG.*

The note is low, flat, and carries far. It travels down the wet girder, through the brick tenement walls stacked beneath the rail line, down through four tiers of arches, until it reaches the Valve-Keepers at the Spillway Gate.

I wait. 

Five seconds pass. Then six.

From two miles away, down in the blue mist where the shelf drops toward the lowland haze, comes the answer. It reaches me through the stone beneath my thighs—three short, vibrating taps, like a heavy heart beating against granite.

*Tap. Tap. Tap.*

*The gate is clear. The water is moving. The weight is held.*

I lay the brass rod across my knees. My fingers, broad and blunt, trace the shallow, water-carved bowls of the step where generations of Tenders have sat before me in this exact culvert, watching the same rust-weevils build their tiny cement ramparts against the long decay of the world.

There is nothing else to be done here until the next stroke.

I lean my head back against the rounded brick wall of the arch. The wall is warm—a steam conduit runs somewhere behind two feet of wet masonry—and the brickwork vibrates with the soft, wet rubbing of a vault-paddler shifting its bulk in the dark space above my head.

I close my eyes. The dark behind my eyelids is the same color as the slate around me: deep, wet, and filled with the low, slow music of a continental machine that has forgotten how to stop, and has no need to hurry.
