My palm is flat against a six-inch high-pressure return pipe, right where it bends around the shoulder of the Fourth Needle. 

Through the oil-cured leather of my mitten, I don’t hear the water so much as I feel its temperature—a steady, 170-degree thrum that tells me the deep coils in the amphibolite core are still sweating cleanly. Below my left boot, the six-thousand-foot drop is a grey-blue smudge of thin atmosphere, but I don’t look down to check the void. My eyes are six inches from a brass sheaf-housing, watching a single, brass-shelled Scab scrape a crust of bright canary sulfur off the outer flange. 

It moves at the speed of cooling lead. Its shell touches my kneepad, warm as a fresh-baked brick.

I am a Sheaf-Gauger. I live in the mid-tier harness of the South Face, tethered to a three-inch eye-bolt driven six feet into unweathered basalt before my grandfather was born. My world is four feet wide and ninety miles tall. 

I am thirty-eight years old, though age here is measured less by years and more by the thickness of the tallow-scale on your leather apron and the deepness of the sulfur-yellow beneath your fingernails. My ribs are broad, wide as an ox’s, flared out to pull the rare, cold air into lungs that have never smelled grass, leaf-mold, or soil. I wouldn't know what to do with a flat field if you dropped me onto one; my spine is built for the lean, the harness-hang, the double-click of the carabiner.

*Clack-clack.*

I unhook my sternum line, loop it around the vertical flange of the cable-guide, and clip in again. *Thunk.* The brass jaw bites the iron. The weight shifts from my thighs to my lower back. This is the first thing we learn, before we have words for heat or steam: *Two points of iron always touch the Spire.* If you have only one, you are already falling; you just haven't hit the blue haze yet.

A vibration comes down the handrail—not sound, but a rhythmic, metallic chatter. 

*Tap-tap-slide.* 

I press my bare knuckles into the brass rail to take the message through the bone of my wrist. It’s Kaelen, three hundred feet up at the main boiler manifold. 

*Pressure high in the third loop. Prepare to vent.*

I take my brass mallet from my belt—the pig-hide strap supple, soaked in black tallow-grease—and strike the pipe twice. *Gong-gong.* *Understood.* In this thin air, shouting is a fool’s errand; the wind strips the voice off your lips before it reaches your own ears, leaving only a dry rasp in the back of your throat. But the iron never lies, and the brass carries a man’s intention half a mile across the void in the space of a heartbeat.

I reach into my side-pouch and pull out a wedge of dried Tallow-moss. It’s dark, almost black, dense as cold beef fat and smelling faintly of rotten eggs and boiled copper. With a short-handled draw-knife, I pare off a curl of the stuff and press it into the grease-cup above the sheaf axle. The heat radiating off the rock—a heavy, baking radiance that smells of cooked iron—melts the moss instantly. It runs into the brass race with a faint, wet hiss, turning the slow, fifty-ton groan of the descending counterweight into a silent, velvety glide.

As I work, a shadow sweeps over the wet iron face.

It is a Sougher, dropping from the upper chimneys where the air is too thin even for steam to hold its heat. Its nine-foot membrane of parchment-skin makes no sound—just a dry *whuff* as it stalls in the rising plume of thermal exhaust fifty feet out from my rig. Its pale, triangular head turns slowly, heat-pits glowing like faint embers in the shadow of its blind brow, searching the brass pipes for the warm spots where the Scabs gather. 

It sees my heat—the hot blood blooming in my deep chest, the warmth of my hands against the cold tool-belt. For three seconds it hovers, suspended between the six-thousand-foot drop and the wet iron face, riding the hot breath of the city’s boilers. I hold still. We don't touch them, and they don't tear at our harnesses. We are both just things that cling to the iron where the rock is hot enough to keep the cold air from freezing our blood.

Then it tilts a wing-tip, catches an updraft off the secondary furnace tier, and glides away toward the Fifth Needle, its grey shadow sliding silently over three thousand riveted windows.

The sun is directly overhead now, a harsh, white disk that casts un-diffused, razor-sharp shadows across the wet basalt. The sweat inside my leather suit doesn't evaporate; it mixes with the fine soot that coats everything, turning into a slick, dark film that smells of old engines and copper rust.

Far below, deep in the dark gap between the Second and Third Needles where the sunlight never reaches, the great steam-piston finishes its three-hour stroke. 

*Thrrrr-uump.*

The subsonic pulse travels up through six thousand feet of black amphibolite, through the three-inch steel mounting bolts, through the brass sheaf, and into the soles of my seamless boots. It rocks me gently in my harness, a deep, rhythmic cradle-beat that vibrates in my teeth and sets the glass Lume-tube at my belt flickering with a pale, bioluminescent green.

It is the city telling us it is still held fast. The rock hasn't split. The bolts haven't sheared. The steam is still turning into water, and the water is still turning into life.

I reach for my brass mallet, unclip my foot-line, slide it eighteen inches down the guide-rail, and clip it home. *Clack-clack.* I am four feet lower than I was an hour ago. There are three thousand more feet of cable-sheaf to grease before the cold wind rolls over the spires from the dark side of the world, and I have plenty of tallow.
