I do not begin with eyes or a name. In a place where nothing falls and nothing rots, there is no need for a face to present to the light.

At first, I am only the pressure.

I am the six-mile pulse of liquid holding itself beneath six feet of pale, turned heartwood. I am the sheer, silent strain against the vascular valves—that immense, patient leverage of wet cellulose pulling water up from nowhere, holding it under hundreds of atmospheres of tension until the timber hums like a struck bell. 

I am down in the seams. I feel the microscopic dry spot before it exists: a square inch of hexagonal tile where the clear, viscous weeping has slowed by a fraction of a drop per minute. I feel the Marrow-Thread beneath me—a wrist-thick cable of spongy white pith—twitching in its pre-grown conduit, pushing its blind papillae up through the join to taste the sweet, humid air. I feel the summons go out through the dark channels, the slow, heavy waking of the resin-slug, its log-like body shifting in the hollow beneath an arch to creep toward the seam, dragging its trail of liquid glass to make the floor whole again.

And then the valve throws wide.

The *thrum* beneath the floor yields to the wet cannon shot—a concussive, acoustic boom that shakes the soft, fibrous terraces of the grand stairways. The water surges. It rushes four hundred feet straight up through bundled cylinders of pale, chlorophyll-tinted alabaster, roaring behind translucent bark, whistling through the narrowing geometry of the high arches.

And as the shockwave climbs, I climb with it. I am no longer beneath the floor; I am stretched high in the sunlit mist, four hundred feet above the parquet.

I am the Strung Nerve.

My body is a sixty-foot ribbon of unpigmented cartilage, taut as a bowstring between two fluted columns. I have no eyes, no hair, no limbs—only wide, vascular suction-disks gripping the weeping wood at either end. As the hydraulic roar threatens to split the arch, I pull myself bone-tight. My flesh hums into an invisible blur. I catch the violent acoustic spike and absorb it, drinking the shock into my striated length, dampening the deafening roar into a low, resonant chest-tone that vibrates through the entire three-thousand-yard vault. Where my disks bite into the pale timber, the bark secretes its thick, sweet weeping directly into my skin. I eat the vibration; I am nourished by the sheer kinetic weight of the water's climb.

The surge passes. The water gathers on the rubbery, sail-sized leaves above, condensing into fat, glass-like spheres that slide down the fluted pillars in silent, heavy ribbons.

I follow the drop as it falls. 

I am three hundred feet lower now, resting on the mirror-still surface of a broad lip-vase grown straight out of the parquet floor. I am a Meniscus Keeper. I am flat, pearlescent, no thicker than wet parchment, spanning six feet across the quiet pool. I feel the heavy, fat drop break from the column’s lip above. It falls through the warm, motionless air and strikes my back.

There is no splash. 

My dorsal capillaries take the blow instantly, bowing downward like a soft trampoline, dispersing the force laterally across my translucent sheet. My underside siphons open with a tiny, silent hiss, venting the excess liquid into the depths of the basin. The surface of the pool does not ripple beyond my perimeter; the unweathered, dry wood outside the vase’s lip remains untouched, immaculate, sealed in its permanent noon.

I am the floor. I am the arch. I am the drop. I am the tension that keeps the drop from straying.

But as the deep, low C-note of the architecture fades back into the steady, organ-pipe *thrum*, all these sensory currents—the taste of the seam, the vibration of the nerve-ribbon, the quiet give of the parchment skin—converge into a single, enclosed space.

They pull me deep into the base of the central spire, where the spiraled stems meet in broad, fluted buttresses. Here, behind an inch of semi-translucent amber bark through which the sunlit mist casts a shadowless, golden glow, the sap moves slowest. 

Here, I am the Gestant.

I hang vertically in the narrow, fluid-filled chamber, suspended in the same clear, sweet water that runs through the high aqueducts. My torso is long, ribless, and smooth as carved ivory. My legs taper into jointless points that touch nothing. My elongated skull curves gently backward, fitting precisely into the spiral grain of the pillar’s interior wall.

I do not breathe air. My lungs are filled with the warm, dense resin that pulses through the cathedral’s plumbing. Every few seconds, when the main valve fires beneath the floor, the chamber expands slightly, and the liquid presses against my skin—not as an impact, but as a deep, heavy hug that keeps my heart beating in exact, rhythmic unison with the plateau.

My hands—eight fingers on each, long as reeds, fine as needles—are spread wide against the inner amber bark. Fine, white rootlets sprout from my knuckles, weaving seamlessly into the wood’s own vascular trunk-lines. Through them, I feel everything.

I feel the log-slug smoothing a seam two miles to the west.
I feel the Strung Nerve trembling above me, settling back into silence.
I feel the Meniscus Keepers holding the quiet surfaces of three thousand pools.

My translucent eyelids are closed, but I am not asleep. I do not remember a yesterday, because the sun has never moved from the zenith of these high, pale clouds. I do not anticipate a tomorrow, because no leaf here has ever fallen, no fiber has ever frayed, and no mark has ever been left upon the soft terraces.

I am simply receiving the geometry. 

With every pulse of the wet hearts beneath the parquet, another layer of dense, pale cellulose is laid down inside my bones. My skin becomes slightly more like the heartwood; the heartwood becomes slightly more like my skin. 

I am waiting for nothing to arrive. I am only letting the roaring, wet organ pipes of the cradle fill every remaining hollow, until there is no longer any difference between the creature being grown and the cathedral growing it—until the plateau is entirely finished, completely full, and perfectly still in the loud, warm sunshine.
