It begins in the chest—or perhaps in the basalt wall pressuring my shoulder blades. At this hour, when the sun sits directly atop the needles like a white-hot coin, it is impossible to tell where the warmth of my own blood ends and the heat of the stone begins.

I am wedged into a vertical seam behind the third-stage steam collector, where the shadow is narrow, dense, and absolute. 

My heart beats once. A long, empty silence follows. Then it beats again. Eight, maybe nine times in a minute. In the noon furnace, to move is to bleed water; to speak is to throw away mist. So I remain still, folded into myself like a sleeping bat, my dark, heavy skin pressed flush against the shaded basalt. My outer eyelids—the thin, dark membranes that drop like smoked glass to shut out the searing glare—are pulled down tight. The world outside the crease is reduced to a stark, amber slit of light and a terrifyingly pure silence.

I am of the Kin. A Tender of the Lines. 

My left hand, broad and thick-nailed as a digging spade, is wrapped around the lower stay-cable where it anchors into the boiler’s iron shoe. I do not hold it to keep from falling—my hips are jammed fast into the stone joint—I hold it to *listen*. 

Through the copper-sheathed iron, the entire plateau speaks to my palm. 

I feel the high, tight tremor of the northern span, three spires away: a Vane-Glider has just banked too close, its mica wingtip skimming the metal like a fingernail across a stretched drum skin. Below that, deeper in the cable’s grain, is a slow, rhythmic *thrum-thrum-thrum*—the main water-lift piston down in the shadow-fissure, cycling its mineral draft. But there is a flutter in the pulse today. A tiny, dry hitch at the top of the stroke. A valve needs grease, or a salt-crust has scabbed over the bleeder port. 

It is my valve. My sector of the teeth.

I open my eyes beneath the dark membranes. A Solder-Mouse, its pigskin flank twitching, scrambles out from the rusted housing of an ancient pressure gauge six inches from my nose. Its pale-cyan eyes glint in the bounced light. Its spine-bristles are laden with tiny, shimmering beads of condensation gathered from the boiler’s weeping seam. It doesn't fear me; to the mouse, I am merely another outcropping of dark, heat-soaked rock, smelling of old tallow and cold iron. It licks a bead of moisture from the copper rivet near my knuckles, its tongue a tiny flick of blue, then vanishes back into the steel shadows.

I wait. We all wait. The beetles are hidden under the rock fins, their mirror-belly plates tucked away. The coursers are dormant in the deep, dripping cisterns beneath us. Even the gliders have vanished into the pale blue void above, riding the air so high their parchment wings look like specks of ash. The machine ticks—a glass eye on the spire above me making its slow, mechanical tracking turn toward the afternoon—and the stone cooks.

Then, the shadow shifts. 

It moves only an inch, but it is enough. The edge of the black seam creeps across the brass face of the collector, and the air in the chasm changes key. A cool, thin draft, pulled down from the upper atmosphere by the rising column of noon heat, slips into the fissure with a dry *whoosh*. 

The afternoon cycle begins.

I retract my dark eye-membranes. My vision clears, sharpening the world into brutal, high-contrast clarity: the deep reddish-brown of the basalt needles, the blinding flash of the sky, the brilliant, electric turquoise of the copper-oxide aprons spilling down the rock below me.

I shift my weight. My joints crack with a sound like dry twigs snapping. From the heavy leather strap across my chest, I unhook my pulley—a thick block of cast bronze, its wheel grooved deep and black with decades of baked-in beetle tallow. 

I do not walk down the spires. Only lizards spend their blood on the hot rock faces. 

I reach up, hooking the heavy bronze block over the primary span-cable that stretches across the abyss toward the West Needle. The metal wire is taut, green-patinated, humming with the wind. I check the leather apron tied around my waist, ensure my copper chisel and grease-gourd are wedged tight in their sheaths, and plant my horn-nailed heels against the boiler frame.

I push off.

The world drops away instantly. The deep chasm opens beneath me—a narrow, ink-black drop eight hundred feet down to the cold, wet dark where the sun never strikes. The dry air roars in my ears, thin and cold against my face, while the heat from the sun-baked spires blasts against my back like an open furnace door. The pulley shrieks on the copper wire—a high, singing *reeeee* that echoes off the polished rock fins on either side.

For three seconds, I am suspended in pure, blinding space between two iron teeth of the world. 

Then the cable bows, the momentum slows, and I swing smoothly into the shaded alcove of the West Needle. My heavy feet hit the basalt ledge with a dull *thud*. My fingers snap out instinctively, my long, thick nails finding the narrow, water-worn gutters in the rock, anchoring me instantly. 

I unhook the pulley and hang it from my belt.

Here, a main pipe leaks. A steady, sizzling mist sprays from a cracked flange, coating the basalt in a lush, thick skin of dried-liver lichen. A dozen Bronze-Back beetles are already there, their jointed legs ticking like clockwork as they crowd the wet margin, their bronze shells glinting in the ambient glow. 

I crouch beside them, my broad knees splayed, my heavy chest rise and falling as I take in the thin, crisp air. It tastes of copper, steam, and old dust. 

I pull the grease-gourd from my belt, unstopper it, and scoop out a thick dollop of gray, tallowy fat. I reach into the mist, past the ticking beetles, and begin to work it deep into the dry seal of the piston shaft. The iron is hot enough to tingle through my callouses, but I take my time, smoothing the fat into the joint, listening as the dry, hitching pulse in the metal smooths out, becoming a soft, wet, subterranean *thrum*.

The machine breathes easier. The mist settles into a steady, predictable spray. A sheet of micro-droplets blooms across the hot rock, sizzles, and rises again as steam to meet the next passing cloud.

I sit back against the basalt, resting my hand on the smooth, vibrating iron pipe. I am not the builder of this place. I do not know what grand, forgotten mind anchored these copper veins into the bone of the earth, or what the great boilers were meant to refine when the world was young. 

I only know that the stone gives off heat, the pipes give off water, and the wind plays the cables like a harp. And as long as I can slide between the needles, grease the joints, and tune the metal to the mountain's breath, the mist will fall, the beetles will drink, and the sky will stay wide and clear above us.

I pull my gourd of succulent-water from my hip, take a small, measured sip of the warm, sticky liquid, and watch a Vane-Glider drop out of the white sky, its wings ringing against the high line as it comes home to roost.
