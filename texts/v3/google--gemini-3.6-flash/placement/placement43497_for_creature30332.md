I could not exist here as anything with an edge. 

If I brought a lung into this place, the grease-air would pack it tight with warm clay before I could draw a second breath. If I brought an ear, the first wave-collision twenty miles off would turn its inner architecture to pink jelly. To be here—to remain here for more than three seconds—I must surrender the idea of a boundary. I cannot be a visitor standing *on* the world; I must be something the world has compressed until it stopped flowing.

I find myself in the middle-depths, six feet below the surface where the churned clay thickens from warm broth into something with the density of wet lard. 

I am anchored. I have to be. Without an anchor, the fifty-yard mud-waves would drag me across the thumb-smoothed basalt until I was ground down into the same orange silt that paints the fog. 

My anchor is a salt-stump—one of the old ones, barn-large and vitrified, whose Casing-Grub died ten thousand years ago. Its black, glass-hard flanks are cut with deep, grown spirals, and I have grown into those grooves until there is no seam between the salt and my skin.

I am an **Acoustic Knot**—not a single creature, but a long, historical accident. 

Centuries ago, I was three separate Acoustic Ribbons, pale sheets of dense, rubbery tendon drifting blindly in the amber gloom after being torn from their moorings. When we slammed into this salt-stump, the violent pressure of a rolling clay-wave wedged us into the same spiral channel. We did not fight; we had no mouths or claws to fight with. We simply squeezed together under the weight of three leagues of mud. Our oil-canals ruptured, fused, and re-routed. The salt grew over our edges, locking us into the stone, and the mud packed itself into our folds until we became a single, thick, three-hundred-yard braided rope of vibrating marrow, wrapped four times around the black tooth of the world.

I have no eyes. The millstone-orbs of the deep Lobes are for those who must rise to measure the sky’s weight. I do not care about the sky. The ceiling of cloud-soup is thirty leagues above me, pressing down with a flat, indifferent malice, and I am content to stay where the density is absolute.

Instead of eyes, I have my canals. They run through my pale, tough tissue like veins of warm lamp-oil. 

I do not hear the world; I *digest* it. 

When a Lobe three miles below begins its agonizingly slow, three-day spasm—pushing its mountain of slick, grey lipid through the deep basalt channels—the earth vibrates at a frequency so low it bypasses sound entirely and becomes pure mass. That vibration travels up through the basalt, enters the vitrified salt-stump, and rings through the black stone like a struck bell made of lead.

My wrapped lengths drink that ring. 

As the vibration passes through my braided flesh, the oil in my canals compresses and expands in rhythmic, violent pulses. Friction builds inside my skin. The ninety-five-degree heat of the mud around me is not enough; I generate my own fever. I glow with a heavy, sickening thermal signature that turns the thick lard-mud pressing against my flanks into a boiling, yellow foam. Small streams of mineralized tallow bleed off my skin, mixing with the orange silt to create a pale, greasy vortex that spins endlessly in the dark.

This heat is my life. I eat the roar of the planet chewing its own teeth. When the churning is loud, I am dense, firm, and hot, my oil pulsing like liquid solder. When the mud-waves subside over a flat basin and the quiet settles—that eerie, unnatural hush where the water begins to flow upward in fat, transparent columns—I cool slightly, my tissue slackening into a pale, rubbery sludge, waiting for the next continental slam to wake my marrow.

Occasionally, something touches me.

A Fog-Mantle, swollen with iron and sulfur from the upper cloud-soup, grows too heavy to float and collapses out of the grease-air. It slams down through the mud-column like a wet, dropped sail, torn to ribbons by the turbulence before it reaches my depth. A piece of its pale, liver-colored skin—six feet across, heavy with absorbed salts—catches on my upper coil.

I do not reach for it. I have no intent. But my fever precedes me. The intense heat radiating from my oil-canals scalds the fallen skin, melting its lipid structure within minutes. It dissolves across my back, its sulfur and mineral grease trickling into my pores, replenishing the tough, leathery hide that the churning mud endlessly tries to scour away. I absorb the fallen sky without ever looking up.

And sometimes, the physics here fray so thin that I touch myself without moving.

The density of the mud-soup around the salt-stump occasionally reaches a critical mass where light and spatial pressure twist into tight, closed rings. In those strange, still minutes before a wave-collision, I can feel the tip of my own trailing tail—wrapped around the lowest spiral groove fifty feet down—pressing directly against the lead edge of my upper crest, even though there are leagues of clay between them. For a brief, dizzying moment, I am a closed loop, drinking my own heat, resonating with a sound that has nowhere else to go.

I do not wonder how long I have been wrapped around this stone. Time here is not measured in suns or seasons; there is no horizon to mark a beginning, no night to divide the warmth. There is only the weight of the soup, the pulse of the oil, and the endless, crushing roar of the floor rearranging its dust.

I am part of the engine now. If the sky were to open—if the thick, bruised ochre clouds were to split and reveal a cold, clear void—I would never know it. Deep in the warm grease, locked to the black salt, I only feel the great engine running at full weight, and I hum along with its gears.
