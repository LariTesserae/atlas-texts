You would find me twenty feet off the root-pavement, wedged in the warm, wet crook where the main spar of the Third Trunk opens to receive the pig-iron housing of the central pump. 

I am not standing. I am pressed flat against the dark grain, my sternum glued to the wood by a slick paste of boiled lard and fine copper dust. My toes—broad, squared at the tips, the nails thick and yellowed like old horn—are hooked into a natural crease in the bark where a brass steam-line disappears into the trunk’s interior.

I am a Tender, but at this moment I am mostly an ear.

The midges are thick tonight—a swaying, pale column thirty feet tall right in the central aisle between the monoliths—and their drone is a high, vibrating fever that fills the sinus cavities. But down here, with my cheek flushed against the petrified teak, the midge-song fades into a background hum. What I hear through my jawbone is the deep, wet *stroke-and-drag* of the main piston twelve feet below me.

*Chug.* The valve drops. The sap surges upward through the wood’s vascular pipes—a sound like a heavy wave breaking against a mud-bank.
*Clack.* The iron flywheel reaches top-dead-center, its twelve-foot rim flinging a fine spray of warm, amber-scented grease against my lower back.

My eyes are open, but I do not need them much. Every few seconds, the horizontal membrane slides across my sight with a soft, wet *click*, wiping away the condensation and the stray midge-wings that settle on my silver-green irises. Through the dusk, I see the amber filament globes glowing down the long avenue, their light reflected in the wet backs of three large salamanders sleeping in the tannin gutter below.

My skin is the color of an oiled acorn, deeply creased at the throat and wrists where the tallow keeps the damp from rotting the folds. I am short—shorter than the iron flanges I grease—but my shoulders are thick as knee-roots from sixty years of pulling brass scrapers through cambium scum.

Right now, my right hand is resting on the bearing box. The pig iron is hot enough to soften the lard on my knuckles. I can feel a bad shudder—not in the metal, but two inches deep in the living grain behind the rear bolts. The trunk is fighting the stroke. A pocket of trapped air or thick, crystallized sap has lodged in the third vessel.

Without lifting my chest from the wood, my left hand goes to my harness. My fingers know the tools by their weight and the shape of their hafts:
The bone wedge, smooth as river silt.
The brass hammer, its head pitted from steam-scalds.
The horn flask of cold pig-fat.

I do not speak. There is no one up on this gantry with me, and even if there were, the air is too thick with the pump’s wet rhythm for words. I give three sharp, clear whistles through my teeth—high and thin, like a startled swift—to let the Tenders on the lower deck know I am about to shift the flow.

Down below, a small, dark shape—one of our young, her skin still pale-purple and slick with birth-fat—looks up from a beetle basket, her wide silver eyes catching the gall-light. She taps the copper conduit twice with a scraper to confirm.

I place the bone wedge into the weeping seam just above the bearing box. I raise the brass hammer.

When the flywheel drops into its lowest stroke—the instant of maximum vacuum, when the whole basin seems to hold its breath for a fraction of a heartbeat—I drive the wedge deep into the wood.

The bark splits with a sound like a wet branch snapping. Hot, vinegar-sharp steam blasts out across my collarbone, smelling intensely of fermented sugars and old copper. I don't flinch; the tallow on my skin turns white and sloughs off, taking the sting with it. The sap releases—a dark, thick spurt that coats my forearms in rich, red resin—and then the pulse clears.

*Chug-clack. Chug-clack.*

The vibration under my ribs smoothes into a long, oily purr. The iron flywheel takes the wood's weight; the wood swallows the iron's heat. The equilibrium returns.

I will stay up here for another two strokes of the great vault-clock—which is to say, until the cold-galls on the ceiling dim from pale green to dull yellow, signaling the shift when the midge-clouds descend to feed in the gutters. 

Later, I will climb down the dished stairs, my bare soles fitting perfectly into the hollows worn by my father and his mother before him. I will sit in the dark, warm alcove of the Third Monolith, where the air tastes of toasted grain, and let my granddaughter scrape the bronze-beetle fat from my back with a blunt horn blade.

And many years from now, when the stroke in my own chest becomes uneven and the bone wedges can no longer clear the fluid from my lungs, they will carry me back up to this very crotch. They will score a deep, vertical smile into the bark right behind the iron bearing box, where the wood is always eighty degrees and smells of grease. They will push me in, feet first, my back to the pig iron.

The cambium will roll over my knees in a month. By the end of the year, my ribs will be part of the brace that holds the flywheel true. I will feel the *chug-clack* through my hips forever, driving the sap up into the dark ceiling where the light never brightens, keeping the basin warm while others scrape the copper and drink the steam.
