I am not high on the cedar bridges where the winds catch the lichen-drapes, nor am I in the upper levels of the slate towers where the air moves freely between the gap walls. 

Find me low down, where the rift narrows to a wedge—at the base of the eastern wall, three meters above the central drainage channel, where the fresh-knapped basalt meets the ancient, unyielding granite of the cliff face.

I am pressed chest-first against a vertical seam. 

My lower jaw is dropped slightly, the split at my chin resting squarely in a wet groove carved two hundred years ago by someone whose thigh-bone I am currently setting into a structural joint. Through the bone of my skull, the entire rift is a dense, three-dimensional grid of soundless motion. Above me, I feel the rhythmic, soft-booted weight of fourteen of my kin sliding across the middle terrace; to my left, six levels deep inside the tenement wall, thirty-two bodies are wedged together in torpor, their shared heat radiating through the slate like a slow, dull embers-glow. Deep beneath my toes, where the mountain's cold water collects in the bedrock cisterns, a silk-covered predator is sliding through three inches of fog-drench, its passage registering as a tiny, liquid *hiss* in my lower teeth.

I am a joiner, though we have no word for it that is spoken with breath. 

My work is not to alter the mountain, but to find where it wishes to settle and give it something solid to lean against. In my right four-digit hand, I hold a heavy, rounded stone of pale quartzite, smoothed by high-altitude glacial silt. In my left, I hold the bone-pin—a polished, cream-white section of rib, taper-carved to a fraction of a millimeter. 

There is a micro-fracture in the third tier of the tenement foundation. It is invisible to the eye, but the fog knows it; the condensation gathers there too quickly, pooling instead of running down into the carved gutters. Left untended, the freeze that comes once every forty seasons would drive a wedge of ice into the slate and push the lower overhang half an inch out of true.

I do not swing the quartzite hammer. I lean my shoulder into it, using the dense, heavy geometry of my torso to press the bone-pin into the seam. 

*Thump.* 

The vibration travels up my arms, through my collarbones, and settles into my ribs. The stone accepts the bone. The fracture closes. A thin film of moisture squeezes out of the gap and runs over my knuckles.

A thumb-sized, velvet-shelled snail—its dark grey mantle shimmering with trapped mist—slides onto the back of my hand. I do not shake it off. My skin is cool, slate-grey, and hairless along the palm and inner arm, matching the ambient temperature of the wet rock. The snail finds no heat to startle it; it simply mistakes my wrist for another stone, leaving a clean, iridescent ribbon of slime across my skin that will dry into a tough, waterproof sealant over the newly driven pin.

I slide sideways. I do not lift my feet; the leathery, microscopic ridges on the undersides of my toes grip the glass-sharp basalt with a quiet, sticky friction. My body pours down the wall like cold oil.

My back is cold, but dry. The dense, velvet nap of charcoal fur that covers my nape, shoulders, and outer limbs catches the drifting cloud-tops as they pass through the seam at chest height. The moisture beads on the tips of the guard hairs, gathering into perfect, silvery spheres that roll off when I shift my weight, falling silently into the ferns below. 

I pause in an alcove beneath an overhanging step. It is the hour—if such a measure exists here—when the violet dusk deepens toward its darkest tone. 

A cluster of six-legged salamanders is already here, packed tightly into the corner of the dry-stacked slate. Their pink, frilled gills ripple gently in the thin air, fanning the mist. As I curl my compact, wedge-like body into the alcove beside them, three of them crawl without hesitation onto the swell of my thigh, seeking the tiny, internal warmth my metabolism generates as I slow down. 

I pull my clear secondary eyelids sideways across my dark eyes, sweeping away a film of dew. I do not close my main eyelids; light is a secondary thing here, a faint green smudge of bioluminescence on the lower steps that tells me nothing I do not already know through my chin.

My breath slows. My core temperature begins its deliberate, quiet drop, falling degree by degree to meet the chill of the mountain.

Above us, high in the gloom, the sky-bridge hum begins. 

It starts as a faint tremor in the stone behind my shoulder blades—a low, resonant pulse emitted by the elders squatting on the hollowed cedar logs three hundred feet up. It travels down the dry-stacked slate, passing through every knapped prism, every lichen-braided rope, every pearlescent bone-pin held in the masonry.

It reaches my jaw.

I do not open my mouth, but I let my chin drop lower, locking the split of my bone against the granite bedrock. I allow the vibration to pass through me, using my own dense skeleton to amplify the frequency and send it down into the roots of the cliff. 

*We are here,* the stone says, humming back through our teeth. *The slate is locked. The joints are tight. The water is running true.*

The salamanders on my leg do not move. The moths tumble through the quiet air above the lane, their felted wings brushing the wet lichen without a sound. My fur sheds a last cluster of mercury-drops into the moss. 

I close my outer eyes now, pull my velvet shoulders tight against the cold stone, and become another piece of the rift, holding up the staircases until the mist turns to frost and back to mist again.
