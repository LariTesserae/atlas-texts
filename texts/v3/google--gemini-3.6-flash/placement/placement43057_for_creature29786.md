I am not looking at this place. I am being pressed into it.

At first, there is only the heat—not a temperature you measure, but a weight that sits on the back of the neck and fills the lungs like warm, liquid silt. Then comes the skin. 

I am wedged between two plates of young basalt, my knees drawn up into my chest, my spine curled into a curve that fits the sharp V of the rock-crease. I am between skins. The old hide along my back has split down the spine—dry, stiff, pebbled like boiled leather, thick enough to have turned a glass splinter two hunts ago—and the humid steam rising from the vent beneath my heels is working its way under the tear. It hums with a fierce, deep itch. Beneath the dead layer, my new skin is pale, slick, and raw, so sensitive that the mere drift of warm condensation dripping from a fern-stem overhead feels like a series of small, needle-sharp taps.

I press my left palm—bare, broad, unarmored—flat against the wall of the fissure.

The stone speaks before anything else. It doesn't use sound; it uses density and tremble. Through the pale pad of my palm, I feel the basin’s deep engine. A mile down-fault, the earth is shifting—a slow, grinding groan of basalt sliding against basalt. Closer, much closer, there is a rhythmic, wet thumping that rattles through the rock into the bones of my arm: six heavy legs, a spade-snout crushing glass. An Iron-Borer is wallowing in a sulfur-pocket, its massive bulk grinding obsidian down to reach the wet tubers below. 

From deep in my throat, I let go a single, dry *click*.

The sound leaves my chest, hits the walls of the crease, spills out into the dense, wet gloom of the thicket, and bounces back. In the concave hollow where my eyes would be if I were a creature of light, the world flashes into sharp, three-dimensional geometry. 

I don't "see" the basin. I *feel* its shape against my face.

*Click.*
The edge of the basalt crease is two hand-spans to my left, razor-sharp, weeping condensation. 
*Click.*
Overhead, a three-tiered canopy of fleshy, heavy leaves forms a thick, acoustic ceiling that mutes the sky. 
*Click.*
Ten paces out, a cluster of Vire-striders hangs suspended in the vines, their long, hairless bodies dripping grease, their pit-organs glowing in my mind as cold, blank spots where sound dies in soft flesh. They are waiting. They know the Borer is nearby, but they cannot smell me. I am slathered in old Borer tallow—rancid, cold, thick as mud—plastered over my shoulders and into the folds of my joints. To their heat-sense, I am just another patch of cooling mud wedged into the stone.

Down on the floor of the fissure, a soft, dry rasp begins. *Scritch-scritch-scritch-scritch.*

The Scours are coming through. Millions of tiny, bronze-plated legs scraping over the glass. I lift my feet an inch higher into the crease, wedging my elbows into the rock. Below me, the living liquid sweeps through the bottom of the crack, licking the stone clean of every scrap of moss, every drop of spilled blood, every fragment of dead leaf. They pass over my discarded tool—a heavy, teardrop flake of obsidian I knapped three hours ago to butcher a leg-joint. It is already dull. By the time the swarm leaves, the glass will be licked bare, smooth, and lost to the dark.

I don't mourn the tool. There is always more stone.

I reach out with my right hand, feeling along the wall of the crease for a cold spot. The basalt around me is warm from the vents, but young glass—glass that cooled quickly in the wet mist—holds a distinct, icy density. My fingers find it: a fresh knot of obsidian jutting from the wall like a frozen bubble.

My left hand finds a rounded hammer-stone resting in the mud near my hip.

I don't hesitate. I don't measure with a straightedge. I strike.

*THWACK.*

The sound is a blinding flare in my face-concavities—a sharp, acoustic explosion that illuminates every leaf, every vine, every joint of my own broad, crouched body for a microsecond.

*THWACK.*

A heavy, crescent-shaped spall breaks free and falls into my waiting palm. It is heavy, cold, and atom-thin along its fresh edge. It bites immediately into the soft leather of my thumb, drawing a single bead of thick, dark blood. I lick the blood away. It tastes of iron, wet ash, and crushed sap. The edge is good.

I peel the last strip of dead skin from my forearm, letting it drop into the lingering tail of the Scour-swarm below. The tiny bronze plates swerve, tear the old hide to ribbons, and consume it in seconds. 

Now I am soft. Now I am sharp.

I draw my knees down, uncurling from the rock-crease. My center of gravity stays low, my broad chest almost brushing the wet stone as I shift into a spring-loaded stance. I bring the ribbed bone-plate on my left forearm against the hardened ridge of my flank.

I give it one hard, grinding drag.

*GZZZT.*

A loud, metallic rattle cuts through the deafening roar of the basin—a signal that carries thirty yards through the thicket before the wet air swallows it.

Two creases over, answering rattles scrape back through the dark. *Gzzzt. Gzzzt.*
My pack-mates. They are wedged in their own splits in the world, greased in tallow, holding fresh glass, listening to the same thudding heart of the Borer down-fault.

We don't have a plan. We don't have a map. We have only the heat of the beast ahead, the razor in our hands, and the roaring, dark warmth that cradles us as we drop from the stone and slide into the green.
