I begin where hardness ends. 

There is no bone in me to break, no tooth to set against the dark. If you were to reach into the amber twilight and press a hand against my side, you would feel only a heavy, velvet resistance—a skin like soaked leather holding back a secret volume of the same warm, salt-sweet brine that surrounds us both. My heart is not a pump that clatters; it is a slow, dark contraction that takes three minutes to roll from my forward fingers down the six-man length of my body, rippling through my middle like a wave passing through deep mud.

I am down on the hummocks, where the peaks of the old world have surrendered their teeth. 

I do not know what a sky looks like when it is empty of water, nor do I know what it means to fall. Here, to fall is merely to settle, slow as a dying frond, resting wherever the thermal drift leaves you. 

I am resting against a ring of egg-smooth basalt. 

My eight forward fingers—each soft as a lung, lined with tiny, weeping lips—are spread across the curve of a single, hand-stacked stone. The stone has no name, but it has a contour I have known for three hundred years. It was placed here before the water grew this thick, before the mountains smoothed into sleeping shoulders. Silt lies heavy upon it, a fine, pale-green velvet born from the microscopic decay of the canopy miles above. 

I am clearing the silt. 

It is not work. It is not a task with an ending. I sweep my fingers across the stone, feeling every microscopic hollow where ancient water has licked the basalt flat. I lift the silt and let the slow, horizontal current carry it away toward the kelp stems. Then I press the soft, heavy flat of my body against the stone, holding it down against the plateau, reassuring the stone that it is still there, that the sea has not yet dissolved its weight into nothingness.

Above me, the world is a long, amber quiet. 

I feel the pale-ribbed long before their shadow touches my skin. My skin is dotted with thousands of tiny, blind pits that drink the pressure of the sea. I feel the wide, rhythmic pulse of a twenty-yard grazer three miles to the west—the soft *gulp-and-push* of its ribcage as it filters the gold-brown spores out of the canopy-drift. The pulse washes over me like a warm hand, pressing my leather length tighter against the cairn. A frond of bladder-kelp, thick as a tree trunk, sways past my flank, its smooth skin brushing mine with a slick, wet sigh. I do not look up. I have no eyes to waste on the tea-colored gloom. Up is only where the warmth gets slightly thinner; down is where the stone remembers what it was.

Something changes in the thrum. 

The great thermal plume rising from the trench six miles off has shifted its breath. The deep, bass vibration that lives in the basalt—the sound that is not heard but felt in the salt of my own inner fluid—alters its key. It is the long signal. The warm water leans.

A second length glides out of the kelp-felt behind me. 

It is another like myself, long and dark, its leather skin scarred with pale, diffuse patches where it has touched other stones, other cairns, other lives. It does not hurry. Hurry is a forgotten physics, a madness that belonged to creatures with sharp bones and thin air. It drifts down onto the same circular ring of basalt, its eight soft fingers reaching out until they find mine upon the stone.

We do not touch face-to-face; we have no faces. We touch skin-to-skin along our entire length.

Slowly, with the agonizing patience of two roots entangling in rich soil, we wrap around one another. My tail end loops over its midbody; its broad, spade-like paddle folds softly over my forward end, shielding my eight fingers against the stone. We bind ourselves to the cairn ring, anchoring each other against the long, three-hour sweep of the shifted current.

In the embrace, the line between us begins to blur. 

Through the porous velvet of our skins, the salt-sweet fluid moves back and forth. Its warmth becomes my warmth; the memory of the deep trench it crawled through last season bleeds into my own leathery flank. We do not speak, for the water swallows sound before it can shape a word, but in the slow diffusion of our waters, I learn the shape of the cairn three miles south. I feel the smooth, oval stone it tended through the dark months. I feel the cold, sudden draft of an air-cavern that floated too low last week, its silver meniscus brushing against the kelp tops before drifting away into the air gaps.

We will stay like this for four days, or seven. The thermal plume will cycle three times, pushing the golden kelp canopy to the west, then letting it fall back to the east. 

High above us, miles up in the amber dark where the sea turns to oil-thick twilight, a canopy is growing old. I can feel its vast, miles-wide body beginning to lose its hold on the light. It is preparing to sink. In a month, or two, it will drop its massive, translucent blanket down upon this hummock. It will cover the kelp, the ferns, the cairn, and us. It will wrap everything in a cold, quiet shroud of clear gelatin, and the light will go out entirely for a hundred years.

We do not move to escape it. There is nowhere to go that is not the Shelf, nowhere that is not the sea. 

When the canopy falls, we will simply be here, beneath it, wrapped around the ancient basalt, skin to skin, pressing our soft fingers into the dark mud, feeling for the roundness of the stones, keeping the shape of the world from slipping away in the warm, wet sleep.
