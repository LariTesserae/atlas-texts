I am high up, suspended in the wet air between the third and fourth tiers, wedged into the armpit where a giant hollow reed piling meets a living willow-joint. 

My feet are where I begin. They are wider than they are long, my toes curved hard around a split-bamboo rail that glimmers with cold dew. I don't use safety lines here; my soles—dark, rubbery, an inch thick, cracked like ancient peat—have memorized the texture of every species of bark in the bowl. Through them, I don't just feel the wood; I feel the city’s pulse. I feel the heavy, rhythmic *thwack-thwack* of the Maul-men five stories down, driving wedges into the foundation roots; I feel the shudder of the lower water-wheels; I feel the subtle, sickening give of a stem that has absorbed too much cold rain and is beginning to split under its own weight.

I am a **Grafter**, but not an old one sitting on the dry river-slate below. I am in the middle of my years—my chest broad as a beer cask, my walnut-brown skin thick with the yellow-white coat of salamander-lard and crushed mint. The rain beads on my shoulders and rolls off in tiny silver spheres, falling into the white void beneath my heels. I smell of sour fat, pine-gum, and the sharp, metallic tang of cold river silt stuck under my thick fingernails.

Right now, my world is four feet wide. 

I am hanging by one arm from a braided vine strap, my knees bent, my torso twisted toward a bad seam. A three-year-old willow wand—thick as a wrist, bent to form the rib of a weaving-hut—has taken the wet rot. It was trained wrong by a careless apprentice two seasons ago; the bark was nicked too deep, and now the icy drip from an overhead flume has settled into the wood. It’s soft. If I leave it through the night, when the cold thermal rises from the cloud-drop, the wind will catch the wall of the hut like a sail, and the rib will snap.

In my right hand, I hold a short bone chisel, yellowed and smooth from thirty years of palm-sweat. In my mouth, held between my broad, flat teeth, is a birch-bark plug of warm grafting-wax—tree-gum mixed with boiled lard and powdered slate. 

I don't speak—not out loud. The air is too loud for words. Below me, three thousand glass frogs are hammering their tiny silver anvils, a deafening, metallic ringing that bounces off the wet cliff face until the whole mountain seems to chime like a struck bell. A flock of moss-parrots explodes out of the high canopy overhead, showering my shoulders with crushed seed-cones and bitter green pine-needles. 

Instead of speaking, I use the bone whistle tied to my left wrist. I lift it to my lips without dropping the chisel and blow two short, high-pitched *skreeps*, followed by a deep, guttural click in the back of my throat.

Farther down the trunk, hanging off a ladder made of knotted root, a Cutter hears me. He doesn't look up—nobody looks up here unless they want a splash of foul water or a falling stone in the eye. But a second later, a fresh wand of green hickory—supple, unblemished, dripping with sap—is thrust up through the bamboo walkways by two dirty, grease-slicked hands.

I take the wood. I work fast, my movements violent but precise. 

With three heavy, shearing strokes of my bone chisel, I cut away the rotting section of willow. The wood smells sharp and sour, like turned cider. I spit the warm wax from my mouth into my palm, smear it thick over the raw white wound of the living trunk, and notch the fresh hickory wand directly into the nicked bark. Then, using my teeth and my left hand, I bind the joint tight with green sinew, pulled so hard the cord bites deep into my own calloused fingers.

I don't mend things to make them last. Nothing lasts here. The wetness eats everything. The mountain eats everything. My work is just to keep the city *breathing* for another season—to make sure the wood gives enough to bend in the storm, but holds enough not to drop a family forty yards down into the black peat.

Suddenly, the chiming stops.

All at once. Three thousand glass frogs go instantly, dead silent. The metallic ringing cuts off like a snapped string, leaving only the roar of the flumes and the heavy drip from the canopy.

My toes lock instantly around the bamboo plank. My breath catches in my barrel chest. 

A thermal shift. The cold air is coming up from the cloud-drop.

I don't whistle. I don't have to. Across the entire vertical hamlet, the frantic anthill energy shifts in a heartbeat. Maul-men drop their hammers and throw their weight against the tension-ropes; Flume-tenders slam wooden gates to redirect the water so the cisterns don't overflow; women in the hearth-huts snatch up children and pull them inward, toward the thick trunks of the ancient trees.

I lean my body flat against the damp, greased bark of the main reed-piling, pressing my chest into the wet wood, my toes digging into the fibers. I pull my hide apron up over the back of my neck. 

Then the wind hits. 

It comes out of the white fog below like a cold fist, spraying mist upward, catching the bottoms of the woven huts. The whole city creaks—a massive, groaning chorus of a million living joints bending together. The reed piling beneath me shudders, leaning six inches to the left, straining against its lashings. The green sinew I just tied hums like a harp string under my hand.

I hold on. I smell the cold, clean air of the deep drop, the scent of crushed mint torn from the cliffs, and the sour grease on my own skin. I am wedged into the wood, part of the lashings, part of the tension, heavy and wet and stubborn.

I wait for the wind to pass, knowing that as soon as it does, a rope will have frayed, a joint will have loosened, a flume will have misaligned—and my wide, flat feet will already be moving to find the break.
