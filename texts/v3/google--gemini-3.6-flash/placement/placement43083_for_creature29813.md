I am not in the room. The room is in me, translated through heat.

To be here is to shed the noise of shape and distance. There is no *over there*; there is only *hotter* and *colder*. 

I find myself spread wide across the floor behind the main compressor, where the basalt dips into a shallow basin. I am a thick, pale mantle—a hundred square feet of heavy, oil-slick tallow, four inches deep at my center, tapering into translucent, bruised-violet margins that lie so flat against the iron plate that the seam between my flesh and the rust is impossible to find. 

I am the creature, yes, but more accurately, I am the *surface where the room feels itself*.

***

If you were to ask where my mind is, I would points to my skin. I do not have a head to hold a thought, nor a spine to pull it straight. My awareness is an expanse of thousands of tiny, shallow pits scattered across my upper back. They do not see light—light is a thin, useless thing that carries no weight—but they drink the thermal signatures of the chapel-pit.

Through my skin, I map the dark:

There, six feet to the north, is the sharp, icy edge of the fresh granite fracture—a wall of absolute zero that draws the warmth out of the air like a draft. 

There, clinging to the iron conduits above, are the fronds. I feel them not as leaves, but as small, persistent islands of lukewarm dampness. Where their spores fall onto my back, they prickle softly—tiny, cold needles of living oil that set up a faint, rhythmic itch. Over weeks, that itch settles into a network of glowing, pulsing veins across my hide. I do not mind them. They are cool against my heavy warmth, like wet thread drawn over a fever.

And there, directly ahead, rising out of the floor like a towering pillar of summer, is the vertical pipe.

I am waiting for it. I am always waiting for it, though "waiting" implies a sense of time I do not possess. There is only the slow swell of heat, the drop of water, the slow swell of heat again.

***

*Hiss.*

The air does not shake—the thick, wet atmosphere swallows the sound before it can reach the corners—but the pipe vibrates. A sudden, blinding white sun erupts in my thermal field as the hot steam vents through the conduit.

My response is not a decision. It is an expansion.

My flesh, cold and thick as lard in the basin, begins to loosen. Deep within my mass, where no light has ever touched, the thermal energy of my last feeding turns fluid. I do not lift a leg or extend a foot; I simply permit my weight to tilt. 

Slowly—at the speed of a candle melting in a closed box—a thick, ivory fold of my body glides out of the sulfur puddle. It rises an inch off the ground, heavy and dripping with mineral condensation, and drapes itself over the copper manifold attached to the pipe.

The metal is hot enough to scald leather, but to me, it is liquid life. The heat pours directly through my skin, bypassing blood, bypassing breath, flooding straight into the dense, pale jelly of my core. My pit-pores bloom wide, drinking the radiance. Where my flesh meets the copper, a faint, wet *sizzle* rises—not of burning, but of oil and moisture sealing the contact.

Below, against the floor, my underside goes to work. 

A million microscopic, velvety filaments—my lower skin, pressed hard against the flaking lead-green paint and the wet basalt—begin their slow, blind drinking. I taste the cold iron. I taste the sharp, yellow tang of deep-earth sulfur, the dark, heavy sweetness of forgotten machine oil, and the dry, flaking salt of iron oxide. I do not chew; I dissolve. I draw the metal’s decay into myself, turning rust into hide, petroleum into cold, dense weight.

***

Am I lonely? The concept has no temperature. It cannot be felt by pit-pores. 

Yet, I am not entirely singular. Three feet away, wedged into the dark crease between the flywheel and the floor, sits a mass the size of a loaf of bread. It is pale, bruised with violet, and completely motionless. 

It was part of my left margin four months ago. It grew too thick, too heavy, too far from the warmth of the pipe, until the tension in my flesh pulled it thin and it sloughed away with a dull, wet tear. 

For weeks it lay there, inert as a dropped rag, stiffening in the damp shade. But lately, as my body drapes over the manifold and radiates its stolen heat back into the room, that small shed piece has begun to warm. Its edges are softening. Yesterday—or perhaps a month ago—I felt a tiny, faint pulse of thermal activity from its center. It has extended three translucent threads into the seam between the basalt and the iron plate. It is beginning to taste the rust.

It is not my child. It is simply more of the room becoming us.

***

The steam cycle wanes. The copper manifold cools from a roaring sun back into a steady, comforting ember. 

Slowly, reluctantly, my heavy fold slides off the pipe and settles back into the basin of sulfur water with a soft, airless *thunk*. The air makes a faint, wet *pop* as my suction releases from the painted iron.

The neon indicators on the control boxes give off their sour, dying red embers. The indigo fronds weep their cold, glowing oil. Deep in the rock, a stressed steel girder gives a long, dry *creak* as it settles an eighth of an inch into the granite.

I close my pores down to fine slits. I press my belly tighter against the iron plates. I soak in the wet, mineral dark, full of heat and rust, completely unbothered by the century, waiting for the pipe to speak again.
