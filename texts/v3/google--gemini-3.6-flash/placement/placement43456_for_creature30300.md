I find myself three feet below the ceiling of the fog, suspended between forty-seven parallel leaves of black glass. 

I am not on the floor of the basin; I am *in* the grain of it. My body—eight yards of wet, dense gristle and tallow-soaked hide—spans a narrow gap where the shale leaves grow so close that a human hand could not pass. But I am not a hand. I am a shim. I am a wedge of heavy, lipid-rich cartilage, six inches wide at my flank, tapering to a blunted horn at my snout. 

The glass edges do not cut me. They bite into the three-inch layer of cold lard that coats my ribs, and the lard yields, spreading into a thick, copper-scented grease that seals the gaps between the rock and my skin. The leaves carry my weight—not as a bed, but as a vise. I am held by the sheer density of the blades, locked into the dark, vertical lattice like a slide pushed into a rack.

I have no eyes. The bruised-gray dusk above the basin ceiling does not reach down into the razor-alleys, and even if it did, there is nothing to see but the glossy, dripping walls of slate an inch from my skin. 

Instead, I have my keel. 

It runs the length of my belly—a solid, honeycombed arch of calcified bone that rests directly against the sharp crowns of the slate plates below me. Through it, the world is bright, clear, and deafeningly precise. 

I do not hear the subterranean roar with ears; I *receive* it into my marrow. The basin is a acoustic map of pitch and resistance. Where the deep vents are wide and choked with cold mud, the vibration comes through my keel as a heavy, dark thrum that vibrates my liver. Where a fresh rift has opened three miles to the east, the anhydrite beds are snapping the quartz veins, sending sharp, ringing *tinks* through the shale leaves that strike my ribs like cold needles. I can feel the shape of the basin by how the sound dies: a wall of solid basalt dampens the hum to a dead, heavy silence; a field of open, loose slate leaves rings out in a chaotic, shattering chorus; and the body of another like me—wedged five hundred yards away—shows up as a soft, muffled spot in the iron grid, a place where the stone’s vibration goes thick and quiet.

Right now, I am working.

The vent behind me shifted six winters ago. The cold air rising from the deep channels has turned violent, its frequency rising from a low furnace-hum to a high, metallic shriek that threatens to shatter the chin-horn of any who linger in its path. The vibration is too tight; it turns the grease in my chambers to thin water and makes the bone-keel ache with small, micro-fractures. 

I am lengthening the dike.

My snout is pressed against the rough, pitted flank of a three-ton basalt boulder. The rock is cold, non-vibrating, and dead—a heavy, mute anchor in a singing world. My chin-pad, thick with scarred, fibrous horn, fits into a shallow, smooth gouge left by the one who pushed this block fifty years before me, before its tallow dried and its body hardened into slate.

I begin the displacement.

I pump cold, iron-heavy blood out of my shoulder-chambers and down into my tail. My hindquarters swell, expanding against the forty-seven shale plates until the rock creaks and the wet tallow squelches in the dark gaps. I am locked fast at the rear. 

Then, I contract my chest. I draw my head back three inches, peeling my chin-horn away from the basalt. I inhale the cold, aerosol fog through my dorsal blowholes, drawing the grease-laden air deep into my lung-sacs, cooling the fluid in my core.

I shift the hydraulic weight forward. My chest swells, locking my front against the glass blades; my tail unclasps, sliding forward through the lubricated alleys with a wet, heavy rasp.

Then I heave.

I expand every muscle along my six-yard length at once. My chin-horn strikes the basalt gouge. The friction is absolute. Beneath me, three slate needles shear off with a sudden, dry *CRACK* that sends a shudder through my ribs. The basalt block groans, its flat bottom scraping across the fractured pavement, dragging through the black, iron-choked mud. 

It moves less than an inch. 

The sound of the movement—the heavy, bone-grinding friction of basalt on shale—travels through the dike, through the stacked wall of rocks, and out into the four-mile basin. It is a slow, heavy signature. It tells the others that the wall at the southern margin is growing, that the acoustic lee is widening by the width of a thumb.

I rest. My breath comes out of my dorsal vents as a cold, copper-scented mist that immediately dissolves into the perpetual fog.

Below my belly, in the quiet, stagnant ribbons of water caught between the stone leaves, I feel tiny, rhythmic pulses. The pale, coin-shaped sliders are crowding into the dark space beneath my keel. They are attracted by the fresh tallow sloughed from my hide and the thick, iron-rich mucus I leave in my wake. They slide over the glass edges on their rubbery bellies, grazing on the orange autotrophic crusts that grow where the iron water drips. 

When I am finished with the boulder, I will drop my lower jaw into the seam. I will press my broad, slit mouth against the slate faces and draw in a long, slow vacuum of cold water, coin-creatures, and mineral sludge. It will sit in my three stomachs for months, turning slowly into the lipid-heavy lard that keeps the razor-world from cutting me to bone.

For now, I stay wedged. 

I feel a rasp coming through the basalt. It is not the subterranean earth; it is a signal. A mile away, at the far end of the dike, another heavy mass has pressed its chin-pad to the stone. It forces a short, violent burst of air through its blowholes, vibrating its bone-keel against the basalt. 

The message arrives through my ribs as a series of dry, rhythmic thumps: *The wall holds. The air is thick. The grease is deep.*

I do not answer with sound. I answer with weight. I swell my chest chambers, locking myself tighter into the black glass leaves, pushing my chin-horn once more into the gouge of the basalt, waiting for the deep earth to breathe its next low chord before I push again. 

I will stay here until the iron in the mist fills my pores, until my tallow grows stiff and white, and the slate leaves finally stop cutting the lard and begin to grow through me, taking my length into the singing floor of the world.
