First, there is no sight. The fog lid hides nothing from me, because I have no eyes to miss the sky. 

There is only the *pitch*.

The world is an acoustic horn carved from baked clay and cold, volcanic rock, and I am set into its throat.

I find myself wedged in the upper throat of the south-facing drain, four feet below the surface flags, where the broad oak timbers are still square and hard, though their edges have been rounded by eight hundred years of water and sliding shell. 

My hide is slate-violet along the ridge of my spine, pale as boiled fat beneath my belly. I am four feet of dense, wet muscle, but I do not hold myself in the current by strength of limb. I hold myself by breath. 

When the sluices above surge and the cold mountain water piles up against my snout, I open my throat and drink the gloom. My lungs—great, thick-walled sacks that stretch from my shoulders to the curve of my pelvis—expand until my ribs bite into the wet oak frame. I swell until there is no gap between my body and the timber. I am the plug. I am the dam. The river shoves against my wedge-shaped head, cold and heavy with the taste of sulfur and wet lime, but I do not move. I am locked into the architecture.

Through the rubbery hide of my flanks, I read the basin.

It speaks to me in scratchings and impacts. Above me, on the stepped orange tiles, a thousand tiny, chalky feet are tapping out their frantic, blind harvest: *tick-tick-tick-tick-tick*. I can feel the exact weight of a crustacean three tiers up as its short spatulate pincer rasps the black tar from a mortar seam. I can feel the tiny, hair-like stitch-worms wriggling beneath the tile glaze, their sticky mucus holding the brick-dust together against the current. I know the basin’s wear. I know which bronze pegs are loose; I can taste the sour, green tang of their verdigris in the stream that washes over my nostrils.

A drain two paces to my left clogs. I feel the water rising across the central flags—the slow, heavy shift in pressure as the shallow pool deepens, spreading cold weight across the terracotta heart. 

Above, the clicking grows frantic. The crustaceans are being lifted, dislodged by the swell, their hooked dactyls losing their grip on the polished orange clay. 

I wait. My pulse is slow—one thick, heavy beat for every twenty slaps of the waterfall against the basalt wall.

Then, a sudden, blinding tremor in the water. A cluster of isopods has lost the stone entirely. They come tumbling down the stepped runnel, swept toward my shaft in a frantic, rolling knot of chalk-grey armor and thrashing legs.

I do not move my body; I release my breath.

A sharp collapse of my lungs—just enough to unwedge my ribs from the oak—and I drop backward three inches into the dark throat of the drain. The sudden suction draws the current down, and with it, the tumbling shells.

My jaws snap shut. There are no teeth to break, only the broad, hard ridges of bone that grow straight from my skull. *CRACK.* The slate-grey carapace yields with the sound of a dry branch snapping underfoot. Inside is the bitter taste of iron, crushed micro-algae, and cold water. I swallow it whole, feeling the sharp fragments slide down my throat, and immediately fill my chest once more, wedging myself tight against the wood before the full force of the backed-up pool can wash me down into the dark.

Then, because the food is good and the water is cold, I call.

I pull the air deep into my throat-sac, press my snout against the oak timber, and let the muscle contract.

*THUMP.*

It is not a sound that travels through air; it is a stroke laid directly onto the bones of the basin. The wood shakes. The basalt slabs ring like struck iron. Up on the stepped tiers, the vibration travels through the water and hits the lower carapaces of the feeding isopods, stunning their tiny, nervous hearts. A dozen of them drop their scrapers, unhook their legs, and go tumbling into the main runnel, washed down toward the waiting throats of my kin in the neighboring wells.

My call echoes back to me—first from the angled basalt walls above, multiplying into a low, metallic roar, and then, much later, from the deep below.

*Thump.*

A answer from the drown-chambers beneath my tail. 

Down there, where the oak timbers are no longer square but rotted into black, spongy tatters, lives the mother of my drain. She is twice my length, blind, lard-white, and so wide her flanks touch both walls of the subterranean sump. She has not seen the grey fog lid at the top of the basin in a century. She does not wedge herself with breath; she rests her great, pale chin in the accumulated silt, opening her wide, toothless maw only when the gurgling clearings flush the dead down to her floor.

I feel her egg-masses swaying against my tail-tip—hundreds of leathery, plum-sized spheres anchored to the rotted pegs, bathed in the ice-cold draft that blows out of the deep earth.

Someday, my lungs will grow too large for this upper square. My ribs will widen until the hard oak can no longer hold me against the autumn surges. The water will take me, pressing me past the bronze pegs, past the square throat, down through the dark, splinters of rotted wood scraping my purple hide, until I drop into the silent, stagnant silt of the lower sumps. I will lose the taste of the grey fog; I will lose the sound of the falling rain. My skin will turn to milk, and I will become the weight at the bottom of the world.

But not today.

Today, the water is cold and loud. The sluices are surging. I expand my chest, feeling the rough grain of the ancient timber bite deep into my flesh. I set my jaw against the current, close my blind snout, and listen to the endless, beautiful, rain-like drumming of ten thousand tiny stone-pincers scraping the dark mortar clean.
