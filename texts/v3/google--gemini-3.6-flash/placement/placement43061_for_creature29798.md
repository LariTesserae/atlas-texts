I am hanging upside down in the three-inch cavern of empty air. 

Here, in the space between the built nodes, the air moves. It is a slow, wet draft—cold enough to make the copper-fluid in my chest pulse with a slow, viscous rhythm, carrying down the sharp taste of fresh frost from the upper ironwood cracks and sweeping away the warm, sour sulfur rising from the bottom silt.

To a creature of flesh and heat, this gap would seem like nothing—a pocket of dark, damp silence. To me, suspended by four amber-hooked digits from a single diagonal strand of dragline silk, it is an ocean of information.

My body is long, pale, and water-logged. My outer chitin has already begun to cloud, turning from the clear, spring-water glass of my youth to the milky, bruised grey of an old Weaver whose time in the upper fronds is ending. My lower hands—broad, spatulate, scarred by a thousand mixings of pine-resin and crushed shale—are tucked tight against my sternum, holding a tiny reservoir of waterproofing tallow harvested from the sloughed skin of the salamander who sleeps two inches below me.

My work is not in the canopy today, nor down in the mud with the Mason-teams whose leg-drumming I can feel vibrating up through the limestone like a steady, distant heartbeat. 

I am holding the Tension.

The single strand of silk I cling to does not belong to me; it was laid by a fat, blind orb-weaver who died three thaws ago in the lower gutters. The silk remains, preserved by the chill and the oil we groom into it daily. It spans the empty three inches, anchored at one end to a sharp, frost-sheared flake of flint and at the other to the midrib of a living fern-crozier that is currently trying to uncurl into the main thoroughfare.

If the frond uncurls freely, its tip will strike the suspended fern-stem aqueduct in two days' time, snapping the tripod-frames and drowning the nursery-galls in a flood of cold condensation.

So I am here, hanging in the dark, acting as a brake.

I do not cut the frond. I feel its life through the silk. I can sense the tremendous, slow-motion pressure of the sap pushing upward into the leaf-cells—a dull, relentless hydraulic surge that twangs the silk strand against my upper barbels. *Thrum. Thrum. Thrum.* Each pulse of plant growth is a low-frequency click that travels through my legs, through my thoracic plates, down into my copper-fluid. 

Every few hours, when the tension rises to a critical frequency—when the silk hums at the pitch that means the fiber is about to snap—I reach out with my upper left hook. The hook is microscopic, ground down from the tip of a shrew’s incisor and bound to my knuckle with three wraps of spider-thread. I make a single, shallow scratch in the outer skin of the fern’s petiole. 

I taste the sap as it bleeds out: bitter, green, laden with cold-weather sugars. 

The scratch releases the internal pressure just enough. The frond pauses its uncurling. It bows slightly to the left, away from the aqueduct, leaning toward a gap where its shade will protect a bed of young liverwort. I apply a dab of salamander-tallow to the wound with my lower spatulate hand to seal out the black-rot mold, and then I settle back into the quiet draft.

I cannot see the violet smudge of the mycelium on the floor beneath me, but I feel its light as a faint, oily warmth against the underside of my abdomen. I can feel the isopods scuttling along the spruce-root causeway an inch to my left—their hard, segmented bellies making a dry *click-shrugg, click-shrugg* against the damp bark, their mandibles dripping with stolen honeydew. 

Through my middle barbels, I read the scent-tracks drifting on the draft. A Weaver passed along the upper silk line twenty minutes ago, her scent-trail rich with the sharp, resinous tang of crushed spruce-needles and the faint, sweet musk of fatigue. She was carrying nettle-thread to the eastern gutter. I can tell by the slight unevenness of her chemical signature that her left barbel is damaged—probably nipped by a predatory ribbon-worm during the morning silt-clearing.

I bleed a small droplet of my own scent onto the silk strand: *Tension holding. Growth deflected two degrees west. Gap clear.*

The draft shifts. A water droplet—vast, heavy, perfectly spherical, and trembling with surface tension—is gathering on the tip of an ironwood root directly above my head. It grows by micro-degrees, distilling out of the heavy stillness. I feel the air currents warping around its expanding curve. 

To a creature my size, a falling drop is not rain; it is an anvil. If it hits me directly, the surface tension will trap me inside a glass prison, dragging me down into the peat where I will drown before I can puncture the skin of the water.

My barbels fan out, measuring the wobble of the droplet. *Three seconds. Two.*

I do not panic. I simply shift my weight. My amber hooks release the dragline silk with a faint, dry *snap*, and I drop two centimeters onto the broad, damp back of the salamander resting in the mortar-gap below. 

The drop hits the silk line above me with a wet, heavy *THUMP*, shivering the entire tension network of the city for six inches in every direction. The silk holds. The drop shatters against the flint-chip terrace, breaking into a hundred tiny micro-spheres that roll harmlessly into the liverwort gutters.

On the salamander’s skin, it is warm—a fraction of a degree above the freezing air. The amphibian’s side expands and contracts beneath my feet in a slow, oceanic rhythm: *inhale, exhale.* The wet mucus on its flanks tastes of earth, lime, and ancient water. I crouch low, letting my broad lower hands scrape away a fleck of dried silt from between its smooth, dark costal grooves, tucking the debris into my mandible-pouch for the Mason-teams to recycle into mortar.

My upper joints are getting stiff now. The cold fluid in my legs is turning thick, cloudy with the mineral salts that mark the end of my Weaver-cycle. In another day, perhaps two, my upper hooks will lose their fine precision. I will no longer be able to split spruce-root into ribbons three cells wide, nor feel the micro-twang of a frond under tension.

When that happens, I will leave the three-inch cavern. I will descend into the lower gutters where the stone is young and raw, newly shattered by the frost. I will lay my spatulate hands flat against a collapsing mud terrace, alongside fifty others of my kind, and I will let the Mason-saliva harden around my legs. 

My chitin will become part of the retaining wall. The sharp chips of flint will settle against my back. My copper-blood will feed the creeping liverwort, and the violet mycelium will weave its pale, cold threads through the joints of my armor, turning my memory into the quiet, rhythmic pulse that holds the dark together.

Until then, the frond is growing again. 

I feel the silk strand hum against the air. I reach out my amber hook, find the line in the pitch darkness, and climb back up into the gap.
