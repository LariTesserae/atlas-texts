I am not in the tenements where the fern-tea steams, nor down in the wet dark of the foundry terraces where the pistons drink their oil. 

I am out at the Ninth Needle—a spire of black slate so young its edges still feel as though they were sheared by a cold chisel five minutes ago. Here, the gravity does not ask you to stand upright; it leans thirty-five degrees toward the mountain face, so that to look down is to look along the vertical cliff, and to look up is to stare sideways into the grey cloud-sea where the sky-rays run their slow, oily currents.

I am a Turnbuckler, though on the high ledges they simply call us the *Hands on the Wire*.

My body is long-limbed and heavy-boned, curved into the triple-bend that keeps my chest five inches from the iron plates. My skin is the dark, oily grey of wet river slate, cracked at the knuckles and thick with calluses that have swallowed coal soot and zinc filings until they no longer feel the bite of the mountain’s razor-edges. I wear a greased-wool coat that smells of boiled tallow and engine-sweat, weighed down at the hem by six iron plumb-bobs that swing toward the cliff instead of the fog below.

Under my iron-shod boots, the catwalk rings with a high, tight *ping*—not the low thrum of a bridge that has settled, but the frantic, vibrating strain of an anchor under siege.

Because the rock behind me is growing.

It doesn't pour; it doesn't melt. It emerges from the dark like salt precipitating in a cold jar, flat geometric faces of basalt creeping outward millimeter by millimeter, pushing against the main suspension collar of the South Tenement block. If left alone for three moons, the new stone will shear the five-inch rivets clean off the gantry and send ninety homes tumbling into the cloud deck.

My right hand holds a steam-wedge—a heavy, brass-jacketed chisel fed by a rubber hose that trails back to the local bypass manifold. My left hand rests flat against the main stay-cable, feeling its pulse.

*Eleven seconds of silence.*

In these eleven seconds, I listen. I press the tip of my brass rod to the steel strands and lean my forehead against the metal. Across three chasms, past the wasp-nest tenements and the swaying gasometer, someone at Needle Four is tapping out a line: *Cold draft rising from the lower rift. The sixth cable is carrying three extra tons of frost-water. Hold the turnbuckle two threads back.*

I do not speak back with my mouth. I take my iron mallet and strike the cable twice—*clink-clink*—two short, high-frequency rings that slide down the wire, through the fog, into the jawbones of the workers three miles away.

*Then the twelfth second arrives.*

*THUMP.*

The entire mountain range shudders. Five miles down in the deep crust, the great hydraulic pump takes its breath, and the vibration travels up through the virgin granite, through the iron gussets, through the soles of my boots, and straight into my teeth. For one full second, the universe is nothing but the stroke of the piston. I close my eyes. My breath stops in my throat. My heart waits for the machine to finish before it takes its own next beat.

The sound dies into the slate-colored mist. The eleven seconds return.

I lean out over the void. The light from the sodium lamps along the trolley line is pale, flickering, caught in the heavy beads of moisture that hang suspended in the air. 

I check my shadow.

It is pinned against the wet granite behind me, but it is not quite right. While I am crouched, resting my shoulders against the brass handrail, my shadow is still reaching forward—its arms extended, its waist bent deeper, as if it is already three paces ahead, working the diamond saw into the growing basalt. It lags by four seconds, twitching slightly in the orange light.

I do not move yet. I sit on my heels, pull my short clay pipe from my collar, and wait. To step forward while your shadow is still out there in the future is to invite the catwalk to stretch beneath you—to find that a simple three-foot stride has suddenly become forty feet of empty, fog-drenched air.

I watch my dark double on the stone. Slowly, smoothly, like cold grease sliding down an iron plate, the shadow pulls its arms back, aligns its posture with my spine, and snaps back beneath my boots.

The space is stable. The tension is true.

I rise—triple-bent, low to the steel—and fit the steam-wedge into the hairline seam between the young basalt and the tenement bracket. I open the valve. A needle-thin plume of scalding white vapor hiss-screams into the chill air, condensing instantly into a shower of warm droplets that wet my face and make the emerald moss on the nearby girder curl its small, cold tongues.

The fresh stone groans, cracks along its crystalline lattice, and flakes away in a clean, straight slab, falling down into the grey floor of the world where no one has ever walked.

I lay my bare palm against the newly exposed rock. It is ice-cold, sharp enough to cut leather, and utterly pristine. No sun has ever touched it. It was born ten minutes ago.

Behind me, in the bay windows of the suspended tenement, a tiny yellow lamp flickers on. Someone is stoking the tallow stove. The smell of frying fern-roots drifts out through an airlet, thick and bitter and sweet.

I take my brass key, loop it through my harness, and lay my hand back on the main cable. I feel the wind. I feel the weight of six hundred people sleeping in iron boxes above the mist. I feel the mountain pushing up against the iron, and the iron pushing back against the mountain, neither one willing to yield a single inch.

The eleven seconds end.

*THUMP.*

I smile into the wet dark, adjust my boots against the slanted gravity, and keep working.
