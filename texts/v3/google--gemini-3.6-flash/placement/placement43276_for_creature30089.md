I would not be at the warm, squirming heart of the pile. That is a place for the younger ones, for those whose plates are still thin enough to bend and whose oil-blood pumps fast enough to feel the friction of the mass. 

I would find myself six feet above the carpet, anchored to the sheer, unweathered ridge of the highest basalt needle—the one that catches the full, tearing bite of the upward drafts. My body is a four-foot wedge of grayed, pitted iron-chitin, overgrown with the slick ink-crust of sulfur-lichen that smells of fresh-broken slate and ozone. My eye-stalks have long since clouded into milky spheres; I do not need the bruised violet dusk that leaks through the cloud-gaps. Sight is a flat, shallow thing that only knows the surface of stone. 

I know its heart.

I am a Mason, and my world is mapped entirely in tremor, tension, and impact.

My four hind-limbs are wedged into a pre-existing shear-fracture, locked so deeply into the basalt that my body has become an extension of the cliff face. Where my lower joint-pads press against the rock, I feel the mountain breathing. I feel the dull, muffled thrum of ten thousand cold hearts beating twice a minute in the basin below. I feel the heavy *tink* of the water-drip falling three yards to my left, its shockwave radiating through the mineral grains like a tiny, liquid bell. 

In my fore-pincers, I hold the chisel. 

It is made from a piece of my own shed dorsal carapace—a thick, curved triangular flake from a molt thirty winters past—bound to a wedge of dense leg-chitin with five tight wraps of dried, fibrous gut-tendon. The tendon was harvested from a kin-body that died in the lower pile when the cold was sharpest; it is dark, stiff, and smells of old salt and glycol. The tool fits into the leathery folds of my grip as if it grew there.

I do not hack at the rock. That would shatter the blade and micro-fracture the anchor-point. 

Instead, I click.

*Krk.*

The sharp, dry snap of my mandibles sends a pencil-thin beam of high-frequency sound straight into the vertical slate before me. The sound travels into the stone, hits a hidden vein of brittle iron-ore six millimeters deep, and bounces back into the wet cilia of my head-ring. 

I "see" the fault line. I feel where the mountain is weak, where it is stubborn, and where it is ready to give up a flake of itself.

I adjust the angle of the chitin blade by a fraction of a millimeter, wedging its point into the microscopic lip of the stress-line. I hold my breath—or rather, I freeze the slow, oily churn of my internal organs, bringing my mass to absolute, dead-still equilibrium against the wet basalt. 

I raise my tail. The heavy, hammer-shaped knuckle at its tip is dense as a solid lump of cast iron, pitted with decades of impact. 

I strike.

*Tink.*

The shock of the blow rings up through my pincers, down through my shell, and deep into the mountain. A thin, crescent-shaped leaf of basalt, no thicker than a fingernail, detaches from the cliff and falls into the dark, striking my lower plates before dropping into the squirming mass below. 

I do not look to see where it lands. I do not care. I bring my cilia forward, tasting the hot, dusty tang of newly exposed iron-grain lingering in the wet mist.

I have been working on this single crescent groove for three moons. It is nearly deep enough. It must be exact—curved to match the precise swell of an egg-capsule, pitched at a seven-degree angle so that the violent updrafts from the black clouds below will press the amber sphere *into* the rock rather than prying it free. 

There is no pride in this work. Pride requires a future that looks different from the past. For me, there is only the rhythm: 

*Click.* Read the grain. 
*Set.* Angle the blade. 
*Strike.* Flake the slate.

Below me, the endless scraping of ten thousand iron carapaces provides the metronome—*krk-krk-krk-shrr-shrr*—a sound that is not noise, but the very texture of the air. It tells me the cluster is alive. It tells me the mass is churning, that the outer edge is pushing inward, that the cold is being shared.

My own hemolymph is thick today, sluggish with glycol, moving like cold grease through my chest cavity. My turn at the warm core of the pile was over long ago; up here on the spire, the ice forms on my back plates during the long dark, freezing into thin, clear ridges that shatter when I raise my tail to strike. Soon—perhaps five seasons from now, perhaps ten—the oil in my heart will thicken past the point of pumping. My hind-limbs will lose their chemical grip on the shear-fracture. I will slide down the needle into the pile.

And they will strip my shell. They will take my dorsal plates to carve new cradles. They will unwrap my tendons to bind new chisels. My soft parts will feed the young whose amber shells are currently curing in the grooves I carved when my pincers were sharp.

I raise my tail again. 

The mist rolls up the cliff in a heavy, wet sleeve, smelling of sulfur and wet iron, coating my blind eye-stalks in tiny, clear beads. The bruised violet light in the sky dies completely, leaving only the dark, the dripping water, and the vibration of the slate beneath my feet.

I set the blade. 

*Tink.*

The mountain gives up another grain. And the world remains exactly as it must be.
