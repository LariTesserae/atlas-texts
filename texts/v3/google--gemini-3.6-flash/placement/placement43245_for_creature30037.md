I am pressed against the six-inch drop. 

During the middle hours of the day, when the sun hangs at its sharpest angle, I do not exist as a living thing. I am a plate of dry porcelain, three feet wide and less than a quarter-inch thick, wedge-fitted into the right-angled shadow cast by a terrace lip. My back is bleached the white of boiled bone, crusted over with the mineral salts left behind when the morning moisture boiled out of the gutter. 

If a boot were to land on me now, I would not bleed; I would snap with the sound of a dry tile breaking across a knee. I am entirely inert. My temperature matches the terracotta beneath me—one hundred and forty degrees, humming with the trapped energy of the continental pour. Deep below my flat body, the stone suffers its afternoon agony: every few minutes, a mile-long plate somewhere to the north expands past its tolerance, and a dull, metallic *tong* vibrates up through my underside, rattling my dry edges against the micro-striations of the masonry.

I wait. I am pure thermal patience.

***

The change does not begin in the sky; it begins in the air’s weight. 

As the sun slides toward the pale, lime-colored haze of the horizon, the long, thin shadow of a cooling fin fifty yards away stretches across the flat plate. It creeps slowly, a dark wedge gliding over the pristine red stone, until its tip reaches the terrace lip and covers me. 

The heat does not leave the terracotta quickly—the floor is too dense, too thick—but the air directly above me thins and cools. The pressure shifts. 

Far down in the narrow expansion seam three feet to my left, the *strata-strips* begin their evening work. The plates are un-pinching as the surface layer cools. As the seam opens by the thickness of a hair, the pressure on their hydraulic channels releases with a long, dry, whistling sigh—thousands of them exhaling in unison along the mile-long seam. A faint plume of warm, mineralized vapor vents upward from the crack.

I catch that vapor. 

The salt crust on my upper surface drinks the steam and turns translucent. The chalky brittleness in my tissue softens. It starts at my outer perimeter—a ring of pale grey rubber expanding outward—and moves inward toward my core. My calcified skin absorbs the moisture, swelling, relaxing, un-rigidifying. Within an hour, I am no longer a discarded slab of porcelain. I am wet leather. I am a disk of dense, cold cartilage, heavy with brine, resting flush against the red floor.

***

Now, in the blue twilight before the fog settles, I begin to feel the ground.

To a creature six inches high, the world has no hills, but it has texture. Beneath my under-belly lies the original face of the continent: the microscopic, parallel grooves left by the giant straight-edges that smoothed this hydraulic cement when the world was poured. They are razor-clean, perfectly parallel, and utterly unworn. They have never known rain that didn’t evaporate in twenty minutes; they have never known soil; they have never known friction.

They are too smooth. Nothing can hold to them.

I extend my margin. I do not have muscles; I have three hundred concentric rings of fluid-filled chambers. I drain the inner rings and pump the saline solution into the outer ones. My leading edge creeps forward an eighth of an inch, sliding down the six-inch drop of the terrace lip to touch the lower plate.

Where my belly rests against the virgin stone, my glands excrete a clear, weak acid—a bitter broth of organic salts and captured dew. 

It does not burn the stone; it does not pit or crater it. It merely attacks the factory sheen. It dissolves the ultra-fine laitance, the paper-thin skin of pure lime that dried on top of the silicate aggregate when the slab was cast. Beneath my body, a tiny, invisible fizzing takes place. I drink the liberated calcium. I pull the trace iron and silica up through my lower pores, feeding my calcified ribs for tomorrow’s baking, using the world’s pristine skin to build my own sun-shield.

***

By midnight, the fog has arrived. 

It rolls off the broad concavities to the west, pushed by the low, steady thermal wind that sets the distant *vent-fans* to humming. Their dry, forty-hertz drone travels through the air, while the groan of the cooling plates travels through the stone. Sitting between the sound in the air and the sound in the floor, I move.

I slide off the terrace lip, dragging my three-foot disk down the six-inch vertical face and onto the flat trough below. My movement is imperceptible—an inch every forty minutes—leaving behind a trail of wet, dull grey. 

Where I have been, the fierce, glass-like reflection of the terracotta is gone. In its place is a soft, satin finish. If the sun were to strike my path tomorrow, it would not glare; it would diffuse, scattered by millions of microscopic etchings where my acid has softened the geometry.

I am not the builder of this world. I am its first decay. 

I am the instrument by which an unblemished, manufactured continent learns how to become a landscape. It will take ten thousand years for my kind to dull the floor of this single concavity, and ten million more before our trails overlap enough to create the first handful of loose sand. But the work has begun.

Before the dawn arrives to bake me back into bone, I find the next shadow. I pull my soft, rubbery margins inward, gather my internal brine into my deepest chambers, and lay myself flat against the cold lime, waiting for the sky to turn to fire.
