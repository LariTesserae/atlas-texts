I do not find myself in the center of the city, nor down in the aqueducts where the heavy dust slides like gray sand. 

I am high up, on the seventh step-terrace of the eastern wall, right where the soft marl of the cliff-face meets the fused, glowing lip of the selenite dome. 

Here, the heat is at its most absolute. The amber lens overhead is only a few yards above my head, a vast, seamless sky of translucent horn that groans and pops with a deep, subterranean bass as the midday light pours through it. The air against my chest feels less like gas and more like a heavy, dry quilt pressed hard against the ribs—warm, static, smelling faintly of heated quartz and bitter sulfur.

I am one of them. But I am old, even by the slow arithmetic of this basin. 

My skeleton is no longer merely porous calcium; it has begun to drink the nitrogen dust into its deeper channels. When I move my left knee, it does not flex with the soft glide of youth; it clicks—a sharp, mineral snap, like two dry tiles sliding together. The desiccated leather of my skin has bleached to the exact tone of the marl walls behind me, dusted so thoroughly with yellow spores that I look as though I have been dipped in dry ochre.

My horn-eyelids are lowered halfway. Through their smoky, translucent shade, the violent glare of the sun-well is translated into a calm, liquid copper. Below me, the great empty city looks like a skeletal relief carved into a slab of sun-bleached bone. 

I carry a tool. It is a long, narrow rod of cold-forged bronze, its tip flattened and notched from centuries of use. 

I am a Listener of the Marl—the one who tracks the shift of the city’s bones, and the one who marks those who have finished moving.

Right now, I am standing beside my companion, whose name was once tapped out in long, rhythmic sequences across the lower plazas, but whose body has now belonged to the stone for forty years. They sit upright on the edge of the seventh terrace, knees drawn neatly to their chest, head bowed slightly forward. Their skin has lost its give entirely; it has turned to chalk. Where their hands rest against their shins, the skin has fused seamlessly into the step itself. 

I take my bronze chisel and mallet. The work is slow. There is no moisture to lubricate the cut, no dampness to hold the stone together. Every blow sheds a fine, dry snow of white flour onto my feet. 

*Clack. Clack. Clack.*

The sound does not travel through the screaming air. The friction of the glass-cacti down in the lower plazas is too intense—a roaring, rasping storm of iron-filings that fills the vault from edge to edge. Down there, millions of silver-vaned plants are clicking and unfolding in the moving spotlight of the sun, their dry fronds violently thrashing in the thermal draft. 

Instead, the sound of my mallet travels *downward*. 

It strikes the marl, passes through the carved step, shoots down through the thirty-foot foundation of the terrace, and radiates out across the entire grid of the city through the soft, chalky bedrock. 

Three hundred yards away, at the base of a hollow, three-story tenement, a young dweller pauses in her sweeping. She does not look up—her head does not turn. But she feels the *clack-clack-clack* rise through the soles of her feet, travel up her shins, and shudder in the fused bone plates of her jaw. 

*A name is being finished on the seventh tier.*

She presses her bronze rod against the nearest door-frame and taps back twice. *Acknowledged.* The vibration hums through my own collarbone, a faint, rhythmic tickle in my sternum, like a dry heart beating once, then twice.

I return to my work. My horn-crest—heavy, ridged, and sweeping back over my neck like a frozen wave—catches the violent acoustic fury bouncing off the selenite dome above and deflects it away from my spine. My body is a dampener, a vessel of quiet moving through an ocean of gold-lit noise.

I do not feel coldness, or dampness, or hunger as softer things understand it. In my belt pocket rests a single, hard wafer of baked dust-dough, seasoned with the bitter, sapid syrup of the pith-reeds. I will break it later, when the light shifts to the western rim and the shadows stretch long and sharp across the empty plazas. 

For now, I watch the searchlight of the sun sweep across the lower city. I watch two solitary figures in yellow sulfur-moss cowls step out from a cellar archway, their sickle blades glinting as they harvest the silver leaves before they can harden into glass. They move together without speaking, their horn-crests occasionally leaning in to touch—a brief, quiet collision of skulls that passes an entire thought between them in a flash of bone-conducted hums.

I feel a profound, unshakeable contentment in the dryness of my marrow. 

There is no rot here. Nothing spoils. Nothing is lost; it merely calcifies, settling into the grand, permanent architecture of the basin. The city was built for fifty thousand, yes, but it is not lonely. The thirty-thousand who came before us are still here—they are the steps we walk upon, the lintels that shade our stoops, the white sentinels sitting in silent contemplation along the boulevards, watching the light turn.

My knee clicks again as I shift my weight. The stone is entering me, inch by inch, century by century. 

One day, perhaps two sun-sweeps from now, I will lay my bronze rod down on this high terrace. I will look out over the amber-drenched emptiness, lower my inner obsidian eyelids, press my crest against the warm marl of the wall, and stop moving. The young weaver down in the aqueducts will climb the steps with her hammer, and she will carve my mark into the stone beneath my feet.

And the city will be slightly denser, slightly whiter, and perfectly complete. 

I raise the mallet. *Clack.* The chalk dust falls like dry light upon my boots. Below, the glass forest screams its rust-red, metallic song into the vault, and through the soles of my feet, I feel the whole world humming—warm, deafening, and immortal.
