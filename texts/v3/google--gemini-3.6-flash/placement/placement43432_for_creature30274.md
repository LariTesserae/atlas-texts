The pulse arrives before the light does. 

It starts forty feet down, in the iron throat of the sump, and travels up through the damp heart-wood of the taproot against which my back is pressed. *Thump.* The root flexes—a micro-expansion of wet fiber against corrugated steel. *Clack.* The valve drops, and a shiver of warm condensation runs down the channel between my shoulder blades, collecting in the hollow above my hip before spilling over onto the felted moss of the catwalk rail.

I am not above, where the Graft-Skeins hang translucent in the green twilight of the pavement-reflectors, their ribs wrapped in night-blooming cereus. Nor am I below, pacing the springy mattress of root-stock with spade-flat feet and an iron scraper held in a four-pound hand.

I am in the middle tier, at the second-level bypass—the place where the corrugated dwellings stack five high against the wet vault wall, and where the copper steam-manifold bends three times to clear the trunk of the central taproot.

I am wedged into the angle where a timber-framed habitation meets the boiler jacket of a auxiliary feed-pump. It is six feet above the catwalk grating, eight feet below the lower canopy fronds. It is a pocket of stagnant, ninety-degree air that smells of crushed turnip, wet zinc, and coal-ash. 

I am a Sump-Lung, but I have stopped walking.

***

There is a period in the life of a Greaser—if *life* is the word for this four-stroke circulation of bilge and starch—when the heavy metals taken up from the sluices stop merely thickening the long bones and begin to settle into the articular cartilage. 

My ankles went first, two years or fifty thousand pump-cycles ago, fusing into dense, bulbous knots of bone and calcified sediment that no longer flex. Then the left knee anchored itself to the copper flange of the bypass line it was leaning against during a six-day maintenance hold. The skin along my thigh—pale, smooth, rubbery as a cold turnip—did not tear when the copper oxidized; it grew *around* the green crust of verdigris, forming a seamless, hairless collar that seals the joint against high-pressure leaks.

I do not have a name, because there is no one here to call into the fog, and my throat contains no dry air for shouting. But if I were a tool, I would be the packing gland at the second-level junction.

My left hand—the one with the dark, horn-tipped fingers—is permanently hooked into the spokes of a twelve-inch brass handwheel. The wheel controls the auxiliary drip-feed to the lower cultivation terrace. I do not turn it often. I feel the pressure through the palm of my hand: a high, thin quiver when the upper pavement-lights heat the vault’s cap-stone and the condensation accelerates; a low, thick thrum when the sump-bilge grows heavy with coal-dust after a rain on the surface three levels above.

When the pressure rises, my shoulder—heavy, stiff, loaded with lead and copper salts—naturally sags half an inch under its own weight. The handwheel turns three degrees clockwise. The bypass opens. A cupful of warm, turquoise bilge splashes down into the liverwort cascade below my heels. When the pressure drops, the swelling in my neck-joints subsides, my shoulder lifts, and the wheel returns to its mark.

I do not think about turning the wheel. My collarbone *is* the valve spring.

***

Through the thick, milky film over my right eye, the world is an arrangement of soft orange moons—the bulkhead lamps—suspended in a sea of moss-green grease. Nothing moves quickly enough to startle me. 

Once every twenty minutes, a Sump-Lung moves along the catwalk below my feet. It glides with that slow, flat-footed sway, its head trailing low, its gray, hairless skin glistening under the steady drip from the brickwork overhead. It does not look up at me. I do not look down at it. We are aware of each other the way two adjacent pipes in the same manifold are aware of pressure: if I were to burst, its wet air would grow momentarily hotter; if its scraper were to slip, my floor-vibration would lose its third harmonic.

Otherwise, there is only the garden and the stroke.

*One.* The piston falls in the sump. Hot steam bursts from the gland-packing fifty yards away, expanding into the upper fronds with a sound like a heavy canvas sail opening in the wind.

*Two.* The drip from the ceiling arch strikes the crown of my head, slides over the ridge of my brow, and lodges in the groove of my upper lip. It tastes of iron, lime, and cold rain that fell somewhere else a hundred years ago.

*Three.* A taproot-node, three inches from my left ear, swells against the cedar wall of the habitation box. The bark splits with a wet, wooden *pop*—a sound no louder than a bubble breaking in oil. A pale, finger-thin tendril creeps out of the crack, turns toward the warmth of my neck, and attaches its tiny, sucker-like roots to the crease behind my collarbone.

It does not hurt. The skin there is already dead to touch, toughened by sixty years of steam-spray into something resembling wet leather. The tendril wants the lipids my skin secretes; I want the cool, starch-rich sap it leaks from its growing tip. We exchange fluids without a sound, bound together by the same warm water that runs through the iron pipes behind us.

***

In the hours when the surface-reflectors go dim—when the green twilight in the upper girders fades into a deep, velvety violet—the vault seems to shrink. The heat pulls inward, gathering around the boilers and the thickest taproots.

From my perch in the angle, I can look down into the open front of a dwelling box stacked across the alley. Inside, suspended in its woven sling of liverwort fiber, a Graft-Skein-in-waiting rests between shifts. Its long, pale legs dangle over the copper drainage trough; its chest rises once for every three strokes of the beam pump. It is younger than I am—its skin still has the smooth, translucent quality of a peeled reed, and its fingers have not yet developed the dark, calcified tips that come from fifty years of scraping rust.

It is watching the valve beside my head. Not with curiosity—there is no curiosity thirty feet down—but with the quiet, structural attention of a joist watching a beam. It is waiting for me to stop.

It knows, as I know, that my left hip is nearly solid now. The bone has expanded into the cedar framing of the box; the wood has softened and molded around my pelvis, forming a deep, dark socket of wet grain and mineralized flesh. In another year, or ten million strokes, my heart will miss its first beat during a blow-down cycle. It will not restart. My blood, thick with verdigris and dissolved lime, will settle into the lowest veins of my legs and turn to cold, turquoise gel.

When that happens, the others will not cut me down. You do not cut down a functional bypass.

They will simply bring a bucket of tallow-soaked oakum and pack it into the space between my ribs and the iron boiler jacket to stop the heat from leaking into the alley. They will wrap my horn-tipped fingers around the handwheel with a copper wire so the weight of my arm continues to hold the valve spring against the sump's pulse. 

And then the creeping fig will come down from the upper girders, taking ten years to cover my face, my lidless eyes, my neck, and my chest in a dense, felted mat of small, round, wet leaves that smell of damp earth and old copper.

I am content here in the angle. The air is warm. The steam is steady. My skin is wet. 

The pump strokes below: *thump-clack, thump-clack.*

My heart answers on the upstroke. The taproot swells against my back. The drip falls from the brickwork and hits my forehead.

Everything is tight. Everything is sealed. Everything is in its place.
