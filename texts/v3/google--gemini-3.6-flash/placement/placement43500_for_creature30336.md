My jaw is pressed flat against the papery, bone-white sheath of a ninety-foot column. 

I do not use my ears to listen. The air is too full of the deafening, continuous roar—the cannon-crack of bursting seed-galls, the abrasive grinding of the Gristle-Crushers six hundred feet below, the high-harmonic scream of millions of mica-winged fliers shearing through the warm amber dusk. To open the mind to that acoustic wall is to lose all sense of direction. 

Instead, I listen through my mandible. I lock the horn-plates of my lower face directly into the calcified grain of the wood, and I feel the column’s pulse.

I am a *Glass-Listener*. Among my kin, we are the ones who stay on the pillars past the hour when others have packed their reed-baskets and dropped their hide-lines to safer canopy-strata. We live in the twilight of the wood—that hyper-dense, volatile three-hour window between when a giant column stops expanding and when its internal chemical engine exhausts itself, turning the living cellulose into brittle, stressed glass.

Right now, beneath my cheek, the pillar is singing. It is a sharp, staccato vibration—like sand falling onto a stretched skin. *Micro-fractures.* The sap inside is thickening, turning from warm, gold lipid into cloudy, razor-sharp resin crystals. The harvest window is open.

I pull my head back. My inner eyelids—thick, transparent sheets of horn—click shut for a fraction of a second to wipe away the fine dust of pulverized silica that floats endlessly through the warm vault. My spine-crest, the wide, fan-like array of thin bone that rises from my back, flexes outward. It catches the dry, hot updrafts pushing through the canopy, flushing dark, tallowy heat away from my core. I have no sweat to cool me; if I held this heat inside, my blood would thicken to grease and lock my joints in minutes.

With my lower left hand, I reach for the tallow-pot tied to my thigh. The pot is carved from a single, un-cracked tuber-gall, lined with heavy, non-volatile crawler-fat. I scoop a glob of the cold, grey grease and smear it thick over my forearm and the edge of my jasper chisel. 

Every motion must be slick, heavy, silent of sparks. 

A single dry stroke of hard stone against stone—a single glint of static friction on a dry vine—and the terpene-saturated air in this fork would go off like an explosive charge. The firestorm would leap from pillar to pillar at the speed of a tearing sail, cooking the canopy to ash before we could even drop into the root-tunnels. The Taboo is not a law written in words; it is the physical law of our lungs. We move as if the air itself is sleeping gunpowder.

I set the jasper blade against a swell in the pillar’s flank—a bulging resin-node where the yellow sap has pooled under fifty atmospheres of internal hydraulic pressure. My claws, four on each hand, curved and heavy as iron pitons, lock into the fibrous bark above the node. 

I do not swing a mallet. A strike might cause a fracture to propagate too fast, shattering the node into a million flying shards of yellow glass. Instead, I use body weight and levered force. I wedge the jasper blade into a natural seam, set my shoulder-plates against the handle, and push.

The blade bites. The wood gives off a sound like dry bone snapping—*crr-rrk*. 

Instantly, the resin seeps. It is not water-thin; it is a heavy, boiling amber lipid, rich with toasted-seed scent and sharp with the tang of pulverized mineral. It bursts through the seam in a thick, pulsing ribbon. 

I drop my mouth-plate open and press my gullet directly over the wound in the wood.

The liquid hits my tongue-ridge—hot, viscous, tasting of bitter earth, dry oil, and ancient sunless heat. I swallow in long, rhythmic gulps. The lipids flood into my second stomach, where my body extracts the chemical calories, converting it instantly into the thick, protective grease that coats my nerves and keeps my dry, scaled skin from cracking under the kiln-heat of the basin. What I do not drink, I direct with a carved wooden trough into the bladder hung beneath my platform—a deep, leather pouch cured in crawler-gall that will feed my basket-group for three cycles of canopy growth.

As I drink, the pillar gives a sudden, heavy *THUM*.

The vibration travels straight through my ribs, rattling my breastbone. Down in the shadows beneath my feet, a basalt rib has just split again, driven upward by the deep earth’s shifting iron. The yellow tendrils gripping the rock have snapped under the sudden shear, and the shockwave ripples up the pillar like a stroke on a massive gong.

Above me, a flock of Chaff-Swarms erupts from a papery sheath. They roll out into the amber haze in a blinding, chaotic wheel—thousands of segmented, mica-winged bodies reflecting the gold light of the vault. The sound of their wings shearing the thick air washes over me like a slide of crushed gravel.

The pillar under me tilts two inches to the north. 

It is time. The resin I am drinking is turning white, clouding over as it hardens into solid amber against my chin. The internal wood is crystallizing. The column has five minutes before its base gives way under the weight of its own canopy-strata.

I pull my face back from the seam. The remaining resin hardens instantly in the dry air, sealing the wound in a thick, glass-hard scab of yellow stone. 

I do not panic. Panic is a luxury for creatures with soft skin and wet lungs. I reach for my hide-line—a thick, braided strap cut from the armored skin of a canopy-crawler, rubbed so heavily with leaf-tallow that it slides over the corky bark without a sound, without a whisper of heat.

I cast the line out. The weighted bone-hook at its end arcs through the dust-mote haze, catches the elbow of a younger, greener pillar twenty feet across the void, and bites deep into its soft, un-calcified sheath.

I check my tallow-pot one last time, seal its wooden lid, and dig my rear claws into the dying wood beneath me.

Behind me, deep within the core of the ninety-foot column, the first long, clear crack echoes—a sound like a sheet of ice snapping across a winter lake. The paper sheath turns silver. The glass is giving up.

I step off into the warm, roaring air.

I drop through the amber twilight, suspended by a single greased strap, my crest flattened against my back, my inner eyelids locked against the silica wind. Below me, the undergrowth thrashes and roars; above me, the vault glows with its pale, immortal twilight. I am moving, sliding, swinging from a dying bone to a living one, woven entirely into the hot, dry, roaring engine of the dark.
