The power did not arrive with a flash of thunder or the tearing of the woven canopy. To shatter the gray daylight would be to scorch the eyes of the swifts; to bring a cold silence would be to burst the lungs of the glass salamanders. 

Instead, the power arrived as a vast, gentle weight of attention—a quiet consciousness that descended through the leaf-vault like water soaking into dry moss. It was an intelligence older than the ironwood walls, wiser than the deep rock beneath the root-plate, and steeped in a profound, unhurried benevolence.

It hovered first in the high air, suspended in the cool mist beside the solder-swifts. 

A foolish power, seeing this place, might have felt pity. It might have judged the darkness oppressive, the noise violent, and the creatures trapped. A lesser god would have torn open the roof to let in the blue sky, silenced the deafening engine, and given the blind salamanders eyes to see the stars. And in doing so, that god would have slaughtered the basin in an afternoon. The salamanders would have suffocated in the sudden stillness; the beetles would have starved as the ungreased joints seized; the trees, unwatered by the steam-drizzle, would have withered; and the Keeper of the Sleeve would have unravelled into dry, dead fiber.

But this power was wise. It understood that perfection does not always mean freedom, and freedom does not always mean grace. Perfection can also be a closed circuit. A wheel turning without a wobble. A song with only one note, held forever.

The power sank lower, passing through the mist, tasting the air. It smelled the sharp, bruised pine, the cold iron, and the wet peat. It listened—not with ears, but with an absolute comprehension—to the rhythm of the hall.

*Thump-clack... hissed-chug... thump-clack.*

It felt the vibration traveling through the slate-like root-wood floor. It felt the calcified bellies of the salamanders pressed to the damp grooves, their tiny, pale hearts beating in exact, four-part harmony with the stroke of the thirty-foot walking beam. It watched the grease-beetles, heavy as river pebbles, sweeping their dark, resinous wax into the sleeve-joints, their hunger bound irrevocably to the machine’s sweat.

Then, the power turned its gaze upon the Keeper of the Sleeve.

It watched the creature move during the fraction of a second when the walking beam reached the apex of its stroke. *Thump-clack*—the chisel-thumb clearing the mineral scale. *Hissed-chug*—the retreat into the timber's shadow. The Keeper’s heartwood brow was scarred, its lichen-filled lungs drinking the metallic steam, its whole existence distilled into a single, flawless act of maintenance. It had no past, no future, no thought of a world beyond the three hundred paces of polished root-wood. It was a creature of total, unconflicted purpose.

The power felt a deep, quiet awe. In all the chaotic, expanding cosmos—full of dying stars, warring kingdoms, and desperate, searching minds—here was a corner of reality that was entirely, irrevocably *settled*.

So, what does a wise and benevolent power do when it encounters a world that is already complete?

It does not change the world. It protects the loop. It cures the invisible decay.

The power turned its vision inward, peering deep beneath the surface of the basin, down into the places where even the Keeper could not reach. It looked into the molecular grain of the great iron walking beam. It looked into the deep geothermal throat three miles below the root-plate.

There, it found the quiet, inevitable tragedies of time.

It saw that in seven thousand years, a microscopic flaw in the iron of the main trunnion pin would finally propagate into a fracture, snapping the beam and crushing the basin in a single, catastrophic instant. It saw that the deep volcanic fissure supplying the steam was slowly, imperceptibly silting over with quartz crystals, destined to starve the engine of its breath in a dozen millennia.

These were not failures of the system; they were simply the slow, cold erosion of the physical universe pressing against a fragile miracle.

The power did not use magic to turn the iron into gold or the steam into light. That would have changed the weight of the stroke; it would have altered the pitch of the vibration that kept the salamanders alive. 

Instead, with a touch as delicate as sunlight resting on water, the power reached into the heart of the metal. Atom by atom, it aligned the crystalline lattice of the cast iron, healing the microscopic fatigue, making the beam eternally supple yet unyielding. Down in the dark earth, it adjusted the thermal flow of the deep crust, smoothing the quartz channels so the steam head would remain constant, purring at the exact same pressure until the last sun in the sky grew cold.

It removed entropy from the equation. It gave the closed system what it had always striven for: true eternity.

Then, the power did one last thing.

It moved down to the junction where the largest drive-pipe plunged into the living wall of silver-firs. It hovered before the Keeper of the Sleeve.

The Keeper had no eyes to see the power, no ears to hear it. It knew only the steam, the resin, the metal, and the stroke.

During the apex of the stroke, in that tiny window of stillness before the beam fell, the power gently brushed the Keeper’s smooth, heartwood brow.

It did not grant the Keeper speech. It did not give it memories of the outside world, or a mind to question its existence—that would have been a cruelty, instilling a loneliness the creature had never known.

Instead, the power imparted a simple, physical sensation. A deep, bone-dwelling warmth. A subtle tuning of its internal root-fibers, so that when the Keeper pressed its head against the cold iron pipes to feel the water flow, it would no longer feel only the mechanical pulse of the piston. 

It would feel, buried deep within the rhythm, a sensation of profound, absolute approval. It would know, in the language of wood and iron, that its labor was seen, that its devotion was holy, and that the basin was held in an enduring grace.

*Thump-clack.*

The Keeper’s chisel-thumb tapped the copper release valve, knocking away a tiny flake of white crust.

*Hissed-chug.*

The Keeper retreated into the shadow of the silver-firs. A thick, clear drop of Amber-resin welled from its spine, sealing the bark where the iron rubbed against the tree.

Overhead, the solder-swifts cut their mesmerising, dark arcs through the gray mist. Below, the grease-beetles scraped the algae and swept their slick wax into the brass sleeves. In the microscopic rivers, the blind glass salamanders held fast to the slate-colored wood, their hearts beating in time with the earth.

The benevolent power slowly withdrew, rising up through the woven vault, leaving no footprint, no altered shadow, no broken thread. 

Behind it, the great iron beam rose, paused, and fell. The drizzle gathered on the leaves, dripped, and ran. The sound was deafening, constant, and now, forever unchanged.
