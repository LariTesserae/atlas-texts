The Power did not arrive with a thunderclap, nor did it part the ocean of grey fog below. To bring storms or lower mountains is the clumsy work of young gods who measure mercy in degrees of temperature and abundance of timber. 

This Power was old enough to know that a creature forged of slate and frost cannot be "saved" by a meadow. To bring heat to the Shelf of the White Ribs would be an act of total murder: the liver-brown moss would rot in the humidity; the optic hairs of the Grom-Voles would scorch their black skin; the Skin-Kites would drop from the sky as the crisp thermals softened into useless, heavy air; and the Walker—whose blood was thick as oil and whose lungs were built for thin air—would drown in the heavy, wet oxygen of the lowlands.

The Power understood the supreme dignity of the edge. It saw that the Walker was not a wretched thing begging for rescue, but a masterpiece of endurance—a solitary, perfect note held against the silence of the high sky.

So the Power did not alter the mountain. It did not melt the ice, nor did it sow trees in the basalt.

Instead, it sat upon the sharp crest above the terrace, vast and invisible, watching the frantic, beautiful clock of the noon hammer. It watched the Quartz-Baskers darken to jet; it heard the dry, corn-husk rustle of the Skin-Kite’s membrane; it counted the three beats per minute of the Walker’s heart as the shadow of the peak swept across the crimson moss.

And because it was wise, and because it loved the harsh beauty of what had been made here over fifty thousand years, the Power did three things.

***

**First, it bound the peak.**

The mountains were absurdly young, raw fractures of basalt and granite, unstable in their steep growth. Three hundred meters above the terrace hung a shelf of fractured slate the size of a cathedral, held in place by a single wedge of black ice. In three winters, the expansion of that ice would have sheared the wedge away, sending a million tons of stone cascading down to scrape the Shelf of the White Ribs clean, launching moss, voles, bones, and Walker into the three-mile void below.

The Power did not move the rock. It simply reached into the crystalline lattice of the basalt with an invisible, steadying pressure. It fused the deep veins of quartzite, stitching the fault lines together until the peak above became a single, unbreakable vault. The mountain would continue to freeze and ping and sweat, but it would never fall upon the thirty paces of life below. The crucible was made permanent.

***

**Second, it shared the memory.**

The greatest cruelty of being the last of one’s kind is not the cold, nor the hunger, nor the wind; it is the absolute absence of a witness. The Walker carved tallies into the sea-bones to record the Sweats, but when the Walker eventually ceased to be, those tallies would weather into dust, and fifty centuries of human endurance would vanish as if it had never been thought or felt.

The Power leaned close to the petrified whale ribs. It did not add a new mark. Instead, it brushed its attention across the soot-blackened incisions—the groupings of three and five, frosted over with white thread. 

As it touched them, the Power took every tally into its own eternal mind. It took the memory of the first Walker who carried the heavy bones up from the forgotten plains when the earth was young; it took the memory of the woman who knapped the grey flint hand-ax; it took the exact cadence of the dry *ping* of freezing stone. The Power became the archive of the Shelf. Nothing done here would ever be forgotten, even when the stars grew cold. The tally was no longer just marked on a bone; it was written into the fabric of reality itself.

***

**Third, it entered the torpor.**

When the sun finally dropped behind the western horn and the temperature on the Shelf plummeted forty degrees in sixty seconds, the Walker wedged themselves into the deep, dry fissure behind the slate windbreak. They pulled their kite-skin hood over their bloodshot eyes, slowed their breath to a faint, whistling gasp, and let the frost freeze their eyelashes into needles. Their heart dropped: *One... two... three...*

In this state, the Walker usually knew only a grey, dreamless void—the flat, cold stillness of the stone itself.

But tonight, as the air froze into fine needles that dropped to the ground, the Power gently pressed its consciousness against the outer edge of the Walker’s mind.

It did not send visions of green leaves or warm fires, which would only confuse and grieve a heart made for slate. It sent no words, for the Walker’s language had shrunk to the names of six stones, three animals, and the sky.

Instead, the Power gave the Walker a presence. 

For the first time in fifty thousand nights, the Walker did not feel the crushing, lonely emptiness of the indigo vault. In the deep dark of their torpor, they felt a vast, silent warmth sitting just outside the crevice—not a heat that melted the ice, but a presence that held the cold at bay. It tasted like the first drop of sun-melted water from a black rock. It felt like a heavy hand laid gently over a shivering shoulder.

In their deep, three-beat slumber, the Walker’s tight, weathered brow smoothed out. The hairless brow-ridge relaxed. Deep within their chest, their cold lungs expanded just a fraction more fully, drawing in the thin, razor-sharp air not as a struggle, but as a gift.

Outside, on the terrace, the wind howled across the slate, stripping the remaining warmth from the basalt. The crimson moss froze into brittle glass. The petrified whale ribs stood dark and tall against the stars, their carved tallies glowing faint and white with frost.

And above them, invisible and patient, the Power kept watch, waiting for the hard indigo sky to turn to gold, ready to watch the sweat bleed from the rock once more.
