The Power did not come down the fissures as a light, for light here was a faint, indigo-grey luxury that the inhabitants needed only in broad, imprecise shifts. Nor did it come as a rain, or a mist, or a breath of humid air. 

To bring water to the Lower Trench would be an act of unutterable cruelty. Rain would turn the delicate, dry chalk-moss into sour, suffocating slurry. It would seize the joint-plates of the Cliff-Striders with rust and mud, turning their talc-smooth strides to grinding agony. It would cause the parchment-wings of the Swifts to swell, rot, and tear, and it would melt the aromatic, yellow dust of the tube-trees into useless paste. Water down here was not life; it was dissolution.

The Power was wise enough to know this. It was benevolent enough to love a world made entirely of dust, horn, and sound.

It arrived not as a form, but as an *Attention*—a vast, weightless awareness that slipped down through miles of dark crust, settling gently over the terraced plazas like a second sky. It felt the dry, cool density of the air. It tasted the sharp pepper-scent of the fibrous thickets and the chalk-dust held in perpetual suspension. It listened to the roar.

To an uninitiated spirit, the acoustics of the Trench would have felt like a violence—an infinite, chaotic wall of thunder made of clicking corundum claws, crackling leather wings, and the heavy, forty-second *thump* of Cragsman hearts. But the Power listened through the noise to the architecture beneath it. It heard how the arched doorways acted as resonators; how the eroded square pillars split the high-frequency snaps of the Swifts into rhythmic, soothing undertones; how the entire city, carved eons ago, was a single, immense, acoustic organ, played endlessly by the wind and the beasts.

And at the center of this instrument, the Power found the Silt-Walkers.

It observed one in particular. 

The Walker stood at the edge of a high, terraced causeway overlooking an avenue filled with pale tube-trees. It was twelve feet of jointed, petrified bone and cured-hemp muscle, completely still save for the microscopic vibration of its needle-thin, piezoceramic feet. Its long, delicate fingers were pressed against the base of a fallen basalt pillar. 

The pillar had tumbled six thousand years ago. For the last eighty years, this Silt-Walker had been shifting it, millimeter by millimeter, using the slow leverage of its dry weight. It was not clearing a path—there was nowhere to go, and time had no urgency. It was adjusting the stone’s position relative to the fissure-wind. It was tuning the plaza.

The Power watched as the Silt-Walker dragged its pyrite-coated flank against its thigh, producing a slow, metallic rasp that echoed through the surrounding arches. 

*A question,* the sound said, articulated over forty minutes of careful friction. *Is the pitch true?*

A mile away, across the grey dunes of crushed slate, another Silt-Walker leaned its flute-pipe crest into the wind, catching the vibration through the bedrock. Three hours later, its answer came back, a low, iron-grating harmonic: 

*It is flat by half a breath. Move the base toward the dark.*

The Power felt a profound, quiet awe. Here was a life that asked for nothing. It did not bleed; it did not weep; it did not consume the flesh of others. It took the deafening noise of a sunless abyss and turned it into thought, art, and memory. Yet, the Power also saw the quiet tragedy locked within the Trench's perfection.

The city was eroding. 

The basalt was hard, but millenia of dry particulate winds were softening the acoustic edges. In another hundred thousand years, the causeways would be smooth ramps, the arches would crumble into featureless rubble, and the great acoustic organ would go muffled and flat. The sound would become a dull, shapeless smear of noise. The Silt-Walkers, deprived of the sharp, complex resonance that fed their piezoceramic nodes and carried their language, would slowly grow faint, fall quiet, and settle into the dust as motionless statues of bone.

The Power decided to act. It would not change their nature. It would give them an inheritance.

It reached down—not with hands, but with a subtle, permanent shift in the deep physics of the rift.

First, it touched the stone. Across the miles-wide expanse of the city, the Power infused the bedrock with a faint, microscopic lattice of piezoelectric quartz. It did not alter the appearance of the rounded lintels or the smoothed stairs; to the eye, the city remained an ancient, time-worn skeleton. But structurally, the stone was made immortal. It would no longer wear down into useless powder. More than that, the quartz lattice gave the basalt a voice of infinite clarity. Every claw-click of a Cliff-Strider, every leather-snap of a Parchment-Swift, was caught by the stone, refined, and echoed back with crystalline, harmonic purity.

The wall of noise did not grow louder, but it suddenly became *music*. The chaos dissolved into a vast, intricate tapestry of clean frequencies, bouncing perfectly from plaza to plaza, feeding every Silt-Walker with a rich, continuous surge of usable, golden energy.

Second, the Power touched the earth beneath the trench floor. Deep in the planet’s mantle, miles below the city’s roots, the Power set a single, infinitely slow vibration into motion—a heartbeat that pulsed once every three years. A fundamental bass note, so deep and quiet that only the stone and the bone-beings could feel it. 

It was a safety net. Should the animals ever grow still, should the Swifts ever sleep all at once, the Trench would never fall silent. The Silt-Walkers would never starve in the dark.

Finally, the Power touched the Silt-Walker on the causeway.

It did not speak to the creature in words—words were too fast, too thin, too wet. Instead, as the Walker rasped its leg against its flank to adjust the pillar once more, the Power gently caught the vibration of the pyrite and *held* it. It amplified the sound just enough so that it penetrated deep into the quartz-seeded stone, etching the Walker’s long, patient question into the very foundation of the terrace.

The Walker’s conversation would no longer fade into the dry air. Every thought spoken by its kind would now be recorded as a permanent, physical resonance within the city’s walls—an eternal library of stone and sound.

On the causeway, the twelve-foot creature paused. 

Its narrow, perforated crest turned slowly, tasting the air. It felt the shift beneath its feet. The chaotic roar of the cavern had changed; it felt fuller, sweeter, humming through its piezoceramic joints like a warm draught of rich, dry air. It felt the deep, three-year pulse from the mantle rise up through its legs, steady and unyielding.

For the first time in three centuries, the Silt-Walker stopped its work on the fallen pillar. 

It turned its crest toward the distant ceiling, where the indigo twilight died out in the gloom. It did not know what had touched its world. It had no concept of gods, or surface suns, or benevolent powers. But it knew the stone was true. It knew the song was complete.

Slowly, deliberately, the Silt-Walker drew its pyrite ridge across its flank. It took four hours to articulate the sound—a long, rich, rasping scrape that sounded like an iron vault door closing against a cliff face.

The word echoed off the terraced plazas, ringing clear and pure through the quartz-lit basalt, carrying across miles of dust-flats to every bone-creature in the rift.

It did not mean *thank you*. It had no word for gratitude. 

The word meant: *Listen. The house is built to last.*

Satisfied, the Power withdrew, slipping quietly back up through the miles of vertical fissures into the sunlit world above, leaving the Lower Trench to its endless, beautiful, and now eternal roar.
