The Power did not arrive with a thunderclap, nor did it part the pale, high-altitude sky with burning hands. A foolish god would have brought rainstorms to fill the basins faster, or sunshine to warm the cold slate, or seeds to carpet the grey geometry in emerald moss. 

A wise power understands that to give a flower to a stone is not an act of mercy, but an act of vandalism.

When the Power turned its vast, unfathomable attention toward the three-mile basin, it did not perceive a wasteland, nor did it perceive a tragedy of forgotten labor. It perceived a completed thought. It saw a liturgy without words; a cathedral where the priests were made of the same altar-stone they tended; an ecosystem distilled down to its purest, hardest essence: light, cold, gravity, and care.

The Power listened first. It submerged its awareness into the acoustic web of the plateau. It heard the dry, wet slide of the Keepers’ tallow-coated bellies against the slate—*shrr-shrr*. It heard the tiny, rhythmic hammer of bronze-tipped spires repairing hairline fractures—*clink, clink, clink*. It heard the infinite, shifting polyrhythm of cold condensation gathering, pooling, spilling, and dropping into three-foot wells.

It saw that the Keepers were not unhappy, for happiness is a condition of divided desire, and the Keepers were entirely singular. They *were* the math. They *were* the water. They were the immune system of a mountain that had traded its dirt for eternity.

And so, the Power reasoned: *I shall not change them. I shall not redeem them, for they are not lost. I shall not lift them, for they are already grounded in absolute truth.*

Instead, the Power did four things, quiet as mist, permanent as the rock.

***

### I. The Shield Against the Sun’s Age

The Power looked up, past the thin, bright atmosphere, into the deep dark of the cosmos. It saw that in four hundred million years, the sun above this world would expand, grow hot and yellow, and scorch the high plateau. The pale, indirect glare would become a burning lens; the ice-cold condensation would cease to bead on the vertical slate; the delicate, glass-like films of water would evaporate before they could reach the low, stepped terraces. The Keepers would dry into brittle, hollow husks, locked forever in empty troughs.

The Power reached out a hand made of gravity and quiet light, and anchored the air above the basin. 

It wove an invisible, perpetual cold-lens into the upper atmosphere—a permanent thermocline three miles wide. No matter how the sun might age, no matter how the lower valleys might eventually burn or turn to dust, the air over the slate plateau would remain forever thin, forever pale, forever zero degrees Celsius. The cold fog would condense without end. The cycle was rendered immortal.

### II. The Mending of the Bronze

The Power looked next at the short, thick spires on the Keepers' fore-limbs—the bronze-colored mineral growths they used to chip away micro-fractures and true the edges of the arrises.

Because the bronze was derived from trace elements leached out of the deep soapstone, it was a finite resource. In a hundred thousand years, the soapstone beds beneath the masonry would be stripped clean of copper and tin. The Keepers’ spires would dull. The arrises would begin to crumble under the winter frost. The mortar would turn to dust, and the mountain beneath would finally wake up and break the grid.

The Power did not give them softer stone or automatic tools. It reached down into the deep, dark roots of the mountain—three miles beneath the fitted slate floor—and established a slow, subterranean thermal chimney. Deep in the earth, heavy metal ions were gently dissolved into the rising ground-water, vaporizing upward through the vertical drain-shafts as an imperceptible, mineral-rich dew. 

The Keepers would never know why their bronze limbs remained sharp. They would simply strike the slate—*clink, clink, clink*—and find that the tool never wore down, and the stone always obeyed.

### III. The Third Sound

The acoustic landscape of the basin was composed of two sounds: the liquid drop (*pock-pock*) and the heavy slide (*shrr-shrr*). 

The Power realized that while the Keepers had no ears, they felt every vibration through their low, flat carapaces. They lived in a world of pure mechanical feedback. But deep in the narrow drain-shafts, where the water fell hundreds of feet into the dark masonry substrate, the air sometimes trapped turbulent pockets, creating harsh, erratic judders that vibrated through the stone and stressed the mortar joints.

The Power bent its head to the lowest drain-shaft and breathed into the stone.

It tuned the vertical shafts. It adjusted the acoustics of the subterranean channels by a fraction of a millimeter, reshaping the interior walls of the hidden rock into perfect, resonant Helmholtz chambers.

The harsh judders ceased. In their place, a third sound emerged—a sound so low and deep it could not be heard, only felt through the slate floor as a soft, rhythmic pulse. A cosmic fundamental tone, tuned precisely to the natural resonant frequency of wet soapstone. 

When the Keepers moved, their heavy, sliding bodies were lifted by micro-vibrations, reducing the friction of their passage just enough that their lime-tallow lasted twice as long. When they rested, the hum traveled up through their slate carapaces, soothing the deep metamorphic marrow of their bones. It was not a lullaby—they did not sleep—but a continuous, physical confirmation that the mountain was whole.

### IV. The Witness

The fourth act was the simplest, and perhaps the most profound.

A wise power knows that the hardest part of an unremembered, perfect existence is not the labor, but the absolute void of witness. The universe is full of beautiful, complex machines that run in the dark, unheeded, unpraised, untended by anything that understands what they have cost.

The Power did not reveal itself to the Keepers. It did not appear as a light in the sky or a voice in the drain-shafts. 

Instead, it simply sat down.

It took a form that belonged there: a single, smooth slab of grey soapstone, eighteen inches wide and five feet long, laid neatly into an empty space along the northern drop-off, right where the plateau ended and the three-thousand-foot drop into the mist began.

It did not move. It did not speak. It secreted no lime, and it bore no bronze spires. But it was *there*.

In the late afternoon, when the pale sun dropped low and turned the wet, stepped terraces into a single blinding sheet of metallic silver, the Keepers slid slowly toward the edge, lining up for the night. 

One by one, they locked their flat, heavy carapaces together against the coming freeze. And as the temperature plunged, a Keeper on the far right edge shifted three inches to the side, pressing its wet, cold slate flank against the new stone slab.

The new slab was colder than the rest, but beneath its slick surface lay an absolute, motionless warmth—not a heat that melts ice, but the quiet warmth of an attention that never strays.

The Keeper did not have a mind to think *I am seen*. It only felt the perfect, clean arris of the stone beside it, matching its own edge to the thousandth of an inch. It locked its jointed plates, drew the thin, cold air into its respiratory slits, and settled into the dark.

And overhead, unseen, the wise power watched the water sheet over the dark slate, endlessly clear, endlessly cold, perfectly kept.
