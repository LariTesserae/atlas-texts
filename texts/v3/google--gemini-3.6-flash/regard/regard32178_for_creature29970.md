The power that came to the drowned continent did not arrive as a light. 

To bring light to that place would have been a violence. Light would have seared the translucent fronds of the abyssal ferns; it would have terrified a people who had forgotten the sun five thousand years before the slate was smoothed. Light is the language of the surface—flickering, hot, transient, and thin.

The power arrived instead as *weight*.

It was a consciousness vast enough to cradle stars, yet wise enough to know that small, fragile things can only be loved in their own language. It descended through the miles of midnight indigo, passing through the thermal layers, past the drifting detritus of the upper world, until it touched the black kelp canopy. There, it stopped. It did not push the water aside. It did not warm the glacial cold. 

Instead, the power did the most difficult thing a god-like entity can do: it waited. It slowed its own pulse. It stretched its thoughts until a single second took three years to pass. It cast off its fiery vocabulary of speed and flame, and learned the dialect of cold lipids, calcified bone, and granodiorite.

It took a century for the power to simply listen.

It felt the slow, agonizing *crunch* of a Carver’s knee joint as the entity lowered its four-fingered hand to lay a strip of petrified hardwood into a widening seam. It felt the sub-audible, low-frequency thrumming of the continent’s floor—the endless, stoic dialogue of a people who had traded hope for duty, and grief for maintenance.

*The lintel in the southern amphitheater has slipped.*
*The current from the trench brings cold.*
*I am here.*
*I am here.*
*I am here.*

The power saw that they were not miserable. Misery requires the memory of a alternative, and the Carvers had none. But it saw that they were *weary*. Their existence was an infinite arithmetic of decay and repair, a battle against an ocean that never slept and never ceased its slow, grinding erosion. They were the lonely caretakers of a tomb they did not know was a tomb.

A lesser power might have lifted the continent to the air, shattering their dense, pressurized bodies in an instant of misguided mercy. A foolish power might have ended the erosion, freezing the ocean into solid ice and robbing them of the only purpose they possessed: the mending.

The benevolent power did neither. It bent its boundless, silent will down to the very roots of the world, below the cyclopean masonry, beneath the pale moss, into the molten, iron heart of the planet’s crust. And there, with exquisite, delicate care, it began to work.

***

First, it gave them the **Song of the Foundation**.

The power did not alter the water, but it altered the mantle beneath the slate. It set up a perpetual, harmonic resonance deep within the continental plate—a vibration so deep and slow that it struck only once every eleven years. 

When the first pulse rolled through the paved avenues, it was not the harsh thrum of a failing wall or a shifting fault. It was a note of incredible, rich resonance. It traveled through the stone, climbing up the spade-shaped feet of the Carvers, vibrating through their lipid bands and into their broad, featureless skulls. 

It was a sound that carried no instruction. It did not say *repair*. It did not say *danger*. 

It said, in the heavy, tectonic grammar of the deep: *The stone beneath you is loved. The floor will hold.*

For the first time in ten thousand years, three Carvers standing in the grand plaza stopped their slanting, agonizingly slow march. They did not strike the ground with their mallets. They simply stood, their six-toed feet pressed flat against the slate, soaking in a vibration that felt like a hand resting gently upon their broad, slab-sided backs.

***

Second, it granted the **Blessing of the Joints**.

The power looked at the tallow-like fats that lubricated the Carvers’ limbs—the thick, heavy lipids that kept them moving through the freezing dark. It reached into the molecular structure of those fats, subtle as a microscopic current, and refined them. It did not make the Carvers fast; speed would have alienated them from their world. But it took away the brutal, agonizing friction of their movement. 

The low, sub-audible *crunch* of their stride softened into a smooth, rhythmic glide of dense tissue over bone. The effort of a single step remained, but the pain of it dissolved into the cold water.

***

Third, it visited the **Dormant Ones**.

Deep beneath the sunken foundations, where the pale moss grew thin and the ancient elders lay bound to the slate by centuries of black ivy, the power drifted like a shadow. 

These elders were not dead, but their hearts beat only once every few hours. They were trapped in a dark, empty eternity of pure sensation—feeling only the atom-by-atom breakdown of the stone around them.

The power did not wake them. To wake them would be to force them back into the endless labor of the mallets and the copper wedges. Instead, the power touched their slow, lymph-filled minds and gave them *space*.

It translated the vast, radiant cosmos above—the swirling nebulae, the golden light of young stars, the wild, leaping currents of solar winds—into the only physical concepts the elders understood: *pressure, density, mass, and depth.*

In their deep, ivy-draped slumber, the elders began to dream. They did not dream of sky—sky was a meaningless void to them. They dreamed that they were holding up the weight of an entire galaxy. They felt the cold, beautiful mass of millions of unseen suns pressing down upon their backs, safe and heavy and eternal. Their slow hearts pulsed, not in exhaustion, but in profound, immovable peace.

***

Finally, the power left a **Work of the Hand**.

In the center of the vastest amphitheater, where four broad avenues met beneath the canopy of three-hundred-foot kelp, the power shaped a single block of granodiorite. It did not manifest it out of nothing; it took a fallen, cracked obelisk that the Carvers had struggled for three centuries to reset, and over the course of fifty years, it smoothed the fractures.

When the power was finished, the stone was no longer broken. It sat in the center of the plaza, a massive, perfectly balanced anvil of black rock, its surfaces carved with deep, sweeping grooves. 

The grooves were not art in the human sense. They were shaped so that when the abyssal currents swept across the amphitheater, the water passing through the channels produced a low, continuous, sub-audible hum—a perpetual signal that radiated through the slate for fifty leagues in every direction.

It was a beacon. It marked the center of their world. It gave the endless, featureless expanse of masonry a heart.

***

When the work was complete, the benevolent power began its long, slow ascent back toward the surface, pulling its consciousness back into the light and the heat of the wider cosmos. It left no light behind. It left no name.

Down in the black indigo, three hundred feet beneath the swaying olive canopy of the kelp, a Carver moved toward the center of the grand amphitheater. 

It took seven days for the Carver to cross the plaza. Its Spade-like hand, tipped with blunt, horn-like nails, reached out into the dark. It did not find a ruined obelisk in need of copper plates or petrified hardwood wedges. 

It found the smooth, grooved granodiorite. It felt the water humming through the channels. It felt the deep, eleven-year pulse of the Earth reverberating upward through the stone, rising through its heavy legs, soothing the cold tallow of its joints.

The Carver stood there for a month, its broad, featureless head bowed against the black stone, reading the message in the bedrock.

Then, with agonizing slowness, it raised its heavy granodiorite mallet. It did not swing in desperation. It did not swing in fear of the sea. 

It brought the mallet down against the new stone—*once*.

The shockwave rang through the masonry, crisp and clear, traveling out through the drowned avenues, past the slate sluice-gates, over the sleeping elders in their beds of aquatic ivy, out into the endless, freezing black.

The strike did not mean *the wall is breaking*.

It meant: *We are here. The city is whole. We are mending.*

And for the first time in five thousand years, from ten leagues away, through miles of solid slate, another mallet struck back in answer.
