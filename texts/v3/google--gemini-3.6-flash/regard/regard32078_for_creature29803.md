The Great Attention arrived not as a light—for the rift had light enough—nor as a voice, for a voice would have been a catastrophe in a place where sound went to die. 

It arrived as a weightless, infinite stillness hovering above the hairline crack in the earth. 

To a lesser power, the rift would have looked like a tragedy requiring correction. An unwise god, full of loud compassion, would have looked down into that cold, damp slot and felt an urge to "save" them. Such a god would have cracked the earth wider to let in the dawn and the sunset. It would have sent down a shaft of warm, golden sunlight to melt the chill, or dropped fire into their shale hearths to cook their food, or sent a howling wind to sweep away the cellar-damp.

And in doing so, that unwise god would have murdered them all.

The warmth would have dried the ironwood until it snapped. It would have withered the jade moss that cushioned their steps, causing their slate tiers to slide into the abyss. The fire would have choked the narrow throat with ash and burned their lungs. The wind would have shattered their acoustic silence and blown their plum-sized birds into the sky, never to return. The moving sun would have blinded eyes that had evolved for ten thousand years to read the shadowless glare of a permanent zenith.

The Power that stood above the rift was not unwise. It was old, patient, and deeply, terribly discerning. It understood that perfection is not a single, universal state, but a fragile, local agreement between rock, water, and intent. 

It looked down into the twenty-man span. It saw the root-weavers with their fine bone needles. It saw the grey-skinned children feeling the vibration of a timber-tap through the soles of their slippers. It saw the blind, pale crayfish in the stone troughs and the slate-colored salamanders pulsing on the lower tiers. It saw a civilization that had solved the problem of existence by becoming as quiet and enduring as the mountain itself.

The Power felt a profound, aching reverence. It did not want to change them. It wanted to *deserve* them.

So, it did what wisdom always does in the presence of a finished masterwork: it stepped back, and it became a wall.

First, it turned its attention to the world **above**.

The lowland shelf above the rift was chaotic and careless. Centuries of rain sat in heavy, unstable pools on the plateau. A mile to the north, a loose gravel moraine was slowly creeping toward the cleft; in fifty years, a heavy thaw would send a mudslide down the vertical stone throat, smothering the ten tiers in grey sludge. To the east, the wind was carving away a limestone crag that would eventually collapse and seal the narrow opening above, plunging the city into absolute, airless darkness.

The Power did not reach down with thunder. It worked at the speed of the rift itself.

Over the course of three human generations—a mere blink to the Power, but a quiet eternity to the people below—it gently nudged the deep geology of the plateau. It tightened the micro-fissures in the limestone, locking the crumbling crag into an eternal arch. It tilted the shelf above by less than half a degree, directing the future mudslides away from the fissure, sending the runoff into a distant river valley. It smoothed the lip of the crack so that no loose pebble, dislodged by a passing beast, would ever fall thirty yards to strike a shale roof.

It made the rift immortal from the outside.

Then, the Power looked **within**. 

It understood that even the most benevolent god must not make its presence known through miracles that break the world. If it was to give a gift to the people of the slate, the gift had to speak their language: the language of vibration, density, and time.

The Power lowered its consciousness down into the damp, vertical throat. It did not touch the air. It did not touch the plants or the birds. It slid directly into the wet slate and the deep, saturated quartz of the rock walls.

There, in the bones of the earth, the Power began to hum.

It was not a sound. It was a frequency below the threshold of hearing—a deep, resonant, rhythmic pulse that vibrated through the bedrock at the exact tempo of a resting human heart. It was a chord of absolute structural integrity.

Down in the rift, on the fifth tier of stacked shale, a root-weaver named Kael was kneeling on a dripping ledge. Her bone needle was poised above a delicate, white root-tip of cold-ash, preparing to guide it into a hairline fracture between two granite blocks. 

Suddenly, her thick, splayed toes—gripping the basin-worn step—felt something shift. 

It was not an earthquake. There was no grinding, no shift of mortar, no settling of lard-wood beams. It was a sensation in her heel, a subtle, rhythmic *thump-thump... thump-thump...* rising from the deep earth. It felt as though the mountain itself had developed a second, deeper pulse.

Along with the pulse came a strange, density in the stone. The cold shale beneath her palms felt suddenly... supported. As if the mountain had gathered its strength beneath their tiny city, locking every block, every peg, every root into an unbreakable, timeless grip. The stone was no longer just holding them; it was *holding on* to them.

Kael did not look up at the zenith-light, for there was nothing new to see. The light remained stark, brilliant, and heatless.

She slowly withdrew her hand from the rock face. She reached out and struck the lard-wood balcony railing three times with the flat of her palm—a sharp, rhythmic vibration that traveled down the ten tiers.

Across the five-foot gap, her neighbour stepped onto his slate roof. His wide, silver irises met hers in the glare. 

Kael did not speak. She tapped her forearm twice with three fingers—*The foundation is deep*—and then tilted her right shoulder slightly inward—*It is pleased with us.*

Her neighbour looked down at his feet, feeling the soft, rhythmic hum vibrating through his moss-soled slippers. He nodded once, a slow, deliberate drop of his chin. He tapped his own chest—a low, resonant click that barely disturbed the damp air—and returned to his work, rubbing crayfish tallow into the grain of an ash lintel.

Above them, thirty yards up, the Power watched. 

It saw that they understood. They did not build temples to it. They did not sing praises, for praises required noise, and noise was a sin against the cold velvet air. They simply accepted the gift as they accepted the wet iron smell and the drip of the fern tips: as a fundamental law of their small, perfect universe.

Satisfied, the Power folded its infinite presence around the fissure like a cloak. It sat above the hairline crack in the grey earth, silent and invisible, catching every stray leaf, diverting every threat, and holding the deep stone steady, content to be the unremembered floor beneath the feet of a quiet people.
