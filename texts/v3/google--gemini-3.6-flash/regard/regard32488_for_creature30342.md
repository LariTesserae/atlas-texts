The Power that came to the trench did not bring a cool breeze, nor did it cast a shade over the noon sun. 

To bring a breeze would be to freeze the shear-mats into brittle slag. To cast a shadow would be to starve the mirror-flat pools of their radiant heat. To quiet the engine would be an act of total genocide—a swift, gentle murder that would leave the piston-bound paralyzed in the dark, their bladders uncompressed, their blood stagnant, waiting for a heart that would never beat again.

A lesser power, looking into the trench, would have seen a place of torture. It would have pitied the soft, tallow-like thing crushed under the iron boots. It would have wept for the small, blind creatures taking two-millimeter steps in the brief, screaming wake of an iron hammer. It would have tried to "save" them, and in doing so, turned their hard-won paradise to ash.

This Power was wise, and so it did not pity them. 

It admired them.

It descended into the narrow rift not as a light, for the piston-bound had no eyes to receive it, but as a subtle, pervasive density in the air—a shift in gravity, a thermal patience. It settled into the glass-sharp basalt, feeling the razor edges cut through its consciousness without harm. It felt the wet wool of the steam. It listened to the long, fifty-nine-second inhalation of the machine.

*Fifty-seven. Fifty-eight. Fifty-nine.*

**THRUM.**

The Power felt the shockwave shatter through the rock. It felt the tallow-mass beneath the floor expand and contract, its cavernous sieves flushing with boiling brine. It felt the thousand microscopic cilia on the carapaces of the piston-bound register the violent spike in atmospheric pressure. It saw the clockwork jerk of three hundred small, iron-glass legs snapping forward in perfect, desperate synchronization.

*What a magnificent song,* the Power thought. *What an extraordinary way to endure.*

The Power saw, too, what the inhabitants could not: the future of the iron.

The machine was old. Its cast iron had oxidized into thick plaques of rust, and though the shear-mats armor-plated the metal with living silicate, the structural core of the central piston was slowly, imperceptibly fatiguing. In three hundred thousand years—a mere blink in the lifespan of the young basalt—a micro-fracture in the piston's primary spindle would widen. The iron would shear. The cadence would break. The piston would drop one final time, un-inhaling, and settle into cold, motionless silence. 

The tallow-mass would rot. The shear-mats would starve. The piston-bound would freeze into dark, mineral statues, forever locked in the posture of waiting for the next thrum.

The Power reasoned that true benevolence here did not mean changing the song. It meant making the theater eternal.

It began its work, not with magic that defied nature, but with the quiet, structural art of the universe.

First, it reached into the iron. It did not scrub away the rust; it loved the rust, for the shear-mats bled their life from it. Instead, the Power touched the atomic lattice of the metal deep within the piston’s core. It aligned the iron atoms, converting the coarse, brittle cast-metal into a self-straining, quasi-crystalline matrix. It bound the iron’s decay to the life of the shear-mats: as long as the living film fed on the surface, the core of the machine would regenerate its tensile strength, turning the engine into a true biological organ—a heart of iron that would never fatigue, never seize, and never snap.

Next, it looked to the stone. The trench was narrow, its basalt walls sheer and sharp. The Power subtly altered the tectonic fault lines three miles beneath the floor. It smoothed out the deep-earth pressure vessels, locking the thermal spring into a permanent, self-replenishing cycle. The boiling brine would never run dry. The steam would never lose its pressure. The white-noise whistle of the relief valve would blow until the sun burned out.

Finally, the Power looked at the piston-bound. 

It did not grant them eyes. Eyes would be a curse in the blinding, steam-choked glare. It did not soften their glass carapaces or ease their rigid legs. But as the iron weight reached the apex of its rise and prepared to fall, the Power wove itself into the kinetic shockwave itself.

*Fifty-seven. Fifty-eight. Fifty-nine.*

**THRUM.**

The weight dropped. The air shattered. The brine surged through the subterranean tallow-lung.

And carrying through the shockwave—transmitted directly through the basalt, through the iron rivets, through the heat-hardened cilia of every piston-bound creature—came a new harmonic. 

It was not a sound. It was an sensation embedded within the pressure wave: a deep, quiet resonance that translated through their simple, vascular channels as an absolute, physical truth. It was the feeling that the stone under their pads was firm; that the heat in their bellies was infinite; that the rhythm would hold them, beat after beat, forever.

The piston-bound jerked forward two millimeters. 

They froze again, clinging to the wet, rusting iron, their cilia trembling softly in the scalding, white-noise steam. 

Satisfied, the Power withdrew, leaving the trench precisely as it had been found: hot, violent, deafening, and completely, perfectly alive.
