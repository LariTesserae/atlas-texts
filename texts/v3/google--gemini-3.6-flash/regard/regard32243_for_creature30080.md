To bring light to this place would be an act of violence.

To fill the dark with blinding yellow suns would sear the pale scar-tissue of their faces, scald the shade-adapted kelp-canopies into black slush, and shatter the intricate, pressure-mapped geography that the Grasp-born have carved into their bones over ten thousand years.

To lift them out—to pull them up into the warm, thin air of a surface world where water falls instead of holds—would be to tear them apart. Their solid, fused ribcages would expand and crack without the loving vise of a hundred fathoms; their slow, heavy hearts would burst in the low pressure; their bone-spurs, built to grip basalt, would find nothing to hold but soft, useless dirt.

A foolish power would have saved them by changing them. 

A benevolent and wise power looked down through the bruised, indigo smudge of the surface, felt the shuddering, three-beat pulses of their iron-black blood through miles of stone, and understood: *They do not need to be saved from their world. They are the song their world is singing.*

So the power came down. 

It did not come as a vision or a voice in the sky. It descended as a change in weight—a vast, warm, high-density current that slipped quietly over the high western ridge, blending seamlessly with the freezing, four-hour avalanche of brine. 

It brought no light. It brought *harmonies*.

***

### I. The Tuning of the Stone

The power first touched the basalt. 

Where the ancient harvesters had spent generations laboriously chipping out hand-flutes and guide-grooves with flint mallets, the power gently urged the rock. It did not melt the stone; it *persuaded* it. Deep within the drowned ridges, micro-fractures smoothed themselves out. The basalt grew denser, harder, and self-healing. 

More than that, the power carved acoustic veins into the heart of the mountain chain. 

When the freezing river cascades over the ridge every four hours, the water no longer merely roars with mindless, crushing violence. As it passes through the natural spirals and the newly tuned flutes in the stone, it creates a massive, resonant fugue. The glass-reeds still scream their metallic chimes, and the gland-kelp still booms its leather-drum beat, but now, hidden beneath the thunder, is an unmistakable, low-frequency pitch-map.

Every pass, every gorge, every hanging valley now has its own distinct chord that vibrates through the rock floor. 

A Grasp-born, blind and miles from their log-shelter, needs only to press their broad forehead to the basalt. The stone sings to them: *You are three leagues north of the vent-saddle. The gorge below you is deep. The shelf to your left is safe.* 

They will never again lose the rope in the dark.

***

### II. The Iron-Lick Vents

The power moved down into the thermal saddles, where the earth belches sulfur and superheated water. 

It adjusted the chemistry of the deep earth. The plumes rising into the cold kelp forests no longer carry raw, toxic acid that burns the oily hide of the harvesters over time. Instead, the vents now release a rich, bio-available soup of dissolved magnetite, deep-crust minerals, and a warm, soothing lipid-compound synthesized from the furnace of the sea floor.

The power made a quiet adjustment to the biology of the Grasp-born themselves—not a grand mutation, but a quiet, cellular gift.

Now, when a harvester comes in from the punishing cold of the harvest, exhausted, their hide cracked and their blunt hand-spurs worn down to smooth, aching nubs against the basalt, they do not merely huddle together in slick knots of cold flesh. They go to the edge of the Iron-Lick Vents. They subserve their heavy, slate-like limbs into the warm, dark plumes.

The water heals them. The dissolved magnetite settles into their bones, re-crystallizing the worn spurs on their wrists, hardening the blunt bone-hooks so they are sharp and true once more. The lipids soak into their blubberous hide, sealing out the rot and the freezing salt. 

Their lives will still be hard—they are built for hardness—but their bodies will no longer fail them before their spirit does.

***

### III. The Weave of the Deep

The power turned its attention to their tools.

It did not give them iron or bronze; metal in this crushing, mineral-heavy brine would leach, rust, and poison the kelp beds. Instead, it touched the *glass-reeds* and the *iron-moss*.

Deep in the swaying, dark meadows, where the long pale tendrils grow, the plant fibers began to secrete a translucent, resinous silicate. When the Grasp-born cut the reeds and ply them into cordage along their stone pylons, the friction of their heavy, bone-knuckled hands now activates the resin. 

The ropes no longer rot. They do not snap under the sudden, violent wallop of the surge. They remain supple, slightly tacky to the touch of a bone-spur, and practically indestructible. The guide-lines that connect shelter to shelter, field to field, will now endure for ten thousand years. The paths are permanent.

***

### IV. The Shelf of the Remembered

The greatest act of wisdom was reserved for the end.

In the old days, when a Grasp-born grew ancient—when their inner rhythm slowed, and they chose or were forced to let go of the rope—the current took them over the lip of the submerged gorge, down into the pathless, silent abyss where even the sound of the kelp-drums died. It was a cold, solitary erasing.

The power went down into that abyss.

Miles below the kelp forests, in the vast, lightless floor of the deep basin, the power lifted the stone. It built a great, sweeping shelf of polished basalt, cradled by gentle, warm upwellings that counteract the crushing downward pull of the deep. 

When an old one finally lets go of the guide-rope—when their work is done and they give themselves to the water—they do not tumble into a desolate void. The current carries them gently down, laying their iron-dense bodies to rest upon the Shelf of the Remembered.

There, in the silent warm water, their bodies do not decay into mud. The mineral-rich brine slowly calcifies them, turning their slate-and-tallow forms into smooth, enduring statues of jet-black stone, forever anchored to the deep shelf.

And the power tied the Shelf back to the world above.

When the great four-hour surge roars over the high ridges and plays the basalt flutes of the upper valleys, the vibration travels down through the roots of the mountains, hits the statues on the Shelf, and bounces back up. 

When the young harvesters press their foreheads to the rock during the storm, they do not only hear the map of the current. They feel the steady, resonant echo of those who went before them—a deep, rhythmic drumming that says:

*We held. We still hold. The stone remembers.*

***

### V. The Touch

Before the power withdrew its vast attention back into the deep cosmos, it did one final thing.

A lone harvester was working along the edge of the drowned gorge, miles from any shelter. The four-hour current was at its peak, screaming through the glass-reeds, pulling at the harvester’s broad shoulders with the force of a falling mountain. The harvester had dropped to all fours, their bone-spurs deeply slotted into a basalt flute, their broad, wedge-shaped head bowed low against the freezing avalanche.

They were tired. Their slow heart beat once... twice... in the dark.

The power did not stop the current. It did not warm the sea.

It simply reached out and pressed a presence against the broad, horseshoe-shaped ridge of cartilage on the harvester’s snout. 

It was not a sound, nor a touch of flesh. It was a vibration—a deep, dense, loving resonance, far lower than the chime of the reeds, far deeper than the roar of the water.

It translated into the fluid of the harvester’s inner skull with absolute clarity:

*Hold fast. The water is heavy, but you are stone.*

The harvester’s four-digit paddles tightened in the groove. Their broad, hairless chest expanded against the crushing weight of the brine. They did not know what had touched them, only that the dark was no longer empty, that the song of the stone had grown sweeter, and that their grip would never slip again.

They struck the basalt twice with a heavy, bone-knuckled wrist—*I am here; I hold*—and turned back to the harvest in the music of the dark.
