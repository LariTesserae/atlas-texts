The power did not arrive with a flash or a thunderclap; in a world that already rang like a struck anvil under a sun that burned through every horizon at once, a sudden display of force would have been an vulgarity.

Instead, the power arrived as *attention*.

It was an awareness as vast as the weather and as quiet as a vacuum, descending softly through the blinding, wet glare until it occupied every millimeter of the high, chiming maze. It felt the wet cold that coated the dark ribs of basalt; it registered the upward-crawling sheets of water and the fist-sized droplets hanging suspended in the gaps between the needles. It listened to the roar—the deafening, metallic chorus of millions of tiny collisions where water met stone, and the low, copper-cable thrum of the wind moving through the fluted spires.

Where a lesser power might have seen a wasteland—a cold, uninhabited hell of iron noise and distorted physics—this power possessed *wisdom*.

Wisdom looks at a thing and sees what it *is*, rather than what it lacks. 

It saw that there was no tragedy here. There were no stranded souls crying out for soil, no fallen empires whose ghosts wandered the wet slate. There was no rot, no disease, no fear, and no pain. The world was an exquisite, self-regulating machine of geology and acoustics, operating on principles that were bizarre only to those whose minds were tied to the slow, heavy gravity of mud and blood.

And at the center of this machine, the power found the Knappers.

It tracked one as it emerged from its cairn: three feet wide, inches high, a jointed assembly of dark slate plates, cold-hammered copper wire, and quartz. The power observed the precise, mechanical rhythm of the creature as the deafening C-sharp vibrated through the basalt. It saw the piezoelectric stress flex the quartz core; it heard the dry, clicking rasp as the slate shingles slid apart; it watched the six basalt limbs, tipped with obsidian, step with eerie, frictionless grace onto the sheer face of the wet cliff.

The power watched the Knapper work. It saw the creature hammer a loosened copper plug back into its circular socket—*clack-clack-clack-clack*—routing an upward-flowing stream away from a dry niche. It saw the Knapper unfold its slate carapace to drink the scattered beams of amber and violet light, humming in total, blissful alignment with the acoustic pitch of the canyon.

The power recognized the beauty of the design. The Knappers were not prisoners of the mountain; they were its fingers. They were the means by which the stone maintained its own strange, ringing clockwork.

Yet, because the power was also *benevolent*, it did not merely watch. Benevolence is the desire for a good thing to endure, to flourish according to its own nature, and to be protected against the blind, senseless attrition of time.

The power understood the Knapper’s only true vulnerability: *attrition*.

Over geological spans, the obsidian tips of their legs would micro-fracture and dull against the hard basalt. The deep veins of quartz, from which the creatures harvested their protective paraffin-lipid paste, would slowly silt up with heavy lime. And occasionally—rarely, but inevitably—a sudden shift in the wind would cut the acoustic C-sharp pitch mid-cycle, leaving a Knapper stranded far from its cairn as the wet glare coated its slate plates in a calcified, suffocating skin of white silica before it could crawl back into the dark.

The power would not change their nature. It would not give them eyes to see things they did not need to see, nor lungs to breathe air that had no purpose here. To turn a Knapper into a flesh-and-blood animal would be an act of violence.

So the power acted within the language of stone, copper, and sound.

***

First, it touched the deep veins of the mountain. 

Far below the fluted spires, where the dark basalt met the root-rocks of the range, the power shifted the crystal lattices of the milky quartz. It cleared the microscopic fissures, allowing the dark, paraffin-like mineral lipids to seep more freely upward into the accessible hollows. The Knappers would never find their supply of joint-grease exhausted; their slate plates would slide along their ground-flat grooves for another ten million years without grinding to a halt.

Second, it altered the native copper.

The power did not make the copper gold or platinum—those metals were foreign to the mountain's song. Instead, it subtly refined the atomic structure of the cold-pounded copper plugs and the thick binding wires that wrapped the Knappers' carapaces. The green oxide that coated them became non-porous and infinitely self-passivating—a tough, microscopic armor that prevented the damp from ever eating through the heart of the metal, no matter how many centuries the rain fell upward against it.

Third, it left a gift of sound.

The power gathered the acoustic properties of the canyon system into its hands—the ringing iron rain, the copper-cable wind, the anvil-chimes—and braided them into a permanent, subtle harmonic. Deep within the bedrock, it set a low, subsonic sustain, a fundamental tone that would never fade, even when the surface wind died to a complete standstill. 

From that moment on, no Knapper would ever be trapped in the open by a sudden silence. The mountain would always hold enough residual resonance in its bones to allow every caretaker time to slide back across the vertical faces, pull the lap-jointed slate door-stone shut, and seal itself into the dry, chalky quiet of its cairn.

***

Finally, before its attention lifted back toward the stars, the power did one last thing—a small, quiet gesture of respect from one maker to another.

On a distant, unclimbed spire of dark slate, high in the thickest part of the wet, blinding glare, the power extended a single point of focused, impossible pressure. 

Using neither flame nor steel, but simple, absolute intent, it carved a new niche into the sheer basalt face. It shaped the stone with the same coarse, uneven gouge-marks that a hand-swung obsidian hammer would leave. It fitted a heavy slate cairn into the hollow, lap-jointed and ground-flat so perfectly that not a single atom of the suspended fog could enter. It forged a new gutter of cold-pounded copper, secured with heavy plugs, to divert the crawling water sheets away from the entrance.

And inside the niche, resting in the dry, white darkness, it placed a new stack of river-smoothed slate shingles, wrapped in bands of native copper wire, with a core of flawless quartz waiting at its center.

It did not give the new Knapper a soul, for the mountain’s own song was soul enough. It simply left it there—balanced, greased, and precisely tuned.

When the wind next moved through the spires with that low, copper-cable thrum, and the rain struck the stone to set the canyon ringing in C-sharp, the new cairn’s door did not slide open immediately. It waited for the precise moment of resonance.

Then, with a dry, clicking rasp, the slate plates expanded. The obsidian-tipped limbs extended. And the new caretaker stepped smoothly out onto the wet, blue-grey basalt, riding the edge of an upward-falling river, completely at home in the chiming, blinding glare of its world.

Satisfied, the power withdrew, leaving the mountain to sing its own song, forever.
