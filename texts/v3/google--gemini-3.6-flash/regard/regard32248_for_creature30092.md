The power arrived not as a light, for light was already there, cutting through the amber water in long, lazy shafts from the skylight above. It arrived as an attention—a vast, quiet mind that settled around the exterior of the spire like a warm front moving over a mountain.

It had no shape, but it possessed immense breadth. It could feel the howling, freezing gales of the high atmosphere scraping against the outer brass skin of the tower; it could feel the dizzying drop of three thousand feet of empty air below; and it felt, with exquisite clarity, the small, violent, beautiful pocket of heat humming inside the forgotten chapel.

A foolish power, seeing a leviathan trapped in a room of iron and glass, would have broken the vessel. It would have shattered the glass ports, smashed the bronze grates, and sought to return the Spire-Siphon to some imagined ocean below.

But the wise power looked into the water and saw the truth: *there was no ocean below.* 

There was only this. There was only the continuous current, the sweet ozone bubbling into the pressurized air, the golden grease-globes carried like lanterns by the tallow-skinned spinners, and the vast, blind, cartilaginous engine of the Siphon itself, holding the central wheel in an embrace four hundred years old. To break the iron would not be liberation; it would be murder. The cold sky would freeze their wet gills; the fall would shatter their soft, boneless bodies. This was not a prison. It was a cathedral built by accident, consecrated by time.

The power observed the Siphon. It watched the creature press its broad, spade-shaped head against the leaking wall-intake. It felt the *th-thump, th-thump* of the lower piston shuddering through the creature’s chest, and it heard the low, resonant organ-pipe hum the Siphon sang in return.

The power listened to that hum for a long time. It was a song without words, without thought, without sorrow. It was the simple, perfect statement of an existence that wanted for nothing.

And yet, the power saw what the Siphon could not.

It saw the micro-fracture forming in the lower piston’s iron arm—a hairline crack, hidden beneath a century of moss and rust, that would, in seventy-three years, snap the metal and bring the heartbeat to a sudden, freezing halt. 

It saw the upper glass ports, where the bronze frames were slowly bowing outward under the continuous hydraulic pressure, destined to give way to the violent air currents of the sky. 

It saw the slow, inevitable creep of metal fatigue—the one enemy that warm water and emerald silk-weed could not charm into grace.

The power did not descend with trumpet blasts or miracles. It did not alter the shape of a single Ribbon-Jack or alter the path of a single floating grease-sphere. Instead, it did what wisdom always does when confronted with a fragile paradise: it became the invisible mortar.

First, it reached into the metal.

It did not restore the iron to its original cold, sharp geometry—that would have stripped the room of the river-pebble smoothness the creatures depended on. Instead, it bound itself to the atomic lattice of the brass and iron. It touched the hairline crack in the lower piston-arm, fusing the iron at the level of its deep, dark grains, turning the fatigue into a supple, immortal toughness. It reinforced the curved glass ports, knotting the silica with invisible threads so that no storm in the sky could ever crack them.

It did not stop the wear—for the wear produced the golden rust-flakes that fed the baleen of the Ribbon-Jacks. It simply made the wear *cyclical*. What was scrubbed away by the warm current was now quietly drawn back into the metal from the mineral-heavy water, a perpetual, living metallurgy that would never fail.

Next, it touched the water itself.

It adjusted the low electrical drone of the copper coils, smoothing out the harsh, jagged spikes of current into a pure, harmonic chord. The Ribbon-Jacks felt the change instantly; their hair-thin filaments flared a soft, copper-green, and their aligned grids settled into an even deeper, more tranquil suspension across the sunlit spans.

Finally, the power turned its attention to the master of the cistern.

It did not give the Spire-Siphon sight. Sight would have been a cruelty in a world of dark grates and cloud-streaked shadows. It did not give it high intellect, for high intellect brings memory and longing, and the Siphon had no need for either.

Instead, the power leaned down—imperceptible, weightless, warmer even than the water—and laid a single, divine impression against the creature’s broad, spade-shaped forehead.

It was a feeling. A singular, overwhelming sensory truth:

*The beat will not stop.*
*The warm water will not end.*
*The song is heard.*

The Spire-Siphon did not wake, for it was not truly asleep. But along its flanks, the two pale ridges of specialized muscle suddenly flared with a brilliant, golden luminescence—brighter than they had glowed in five hundred years. 

Its broad chest swelled against the wall-intake, pulling in a massive surge of mineral-rich fluid. The four slit-like gills on its upper back flared wide, venting shimmering thermal plumes high up toward the silver air-ceiling, where the Loom-Skins hung by their lobed digits.

Below, the piston struck: *th-thump, th-thump.*

The Siphon opened its throat. The low, organ-pipe hum swelled, rising out of the water, vibrating the iron walls, passing through the reinforced glass, and spilling out into the cold, empty sky outside.

The power listened to the echo as it carried across the clouds. Then, satisfied that the chapel was eternal, it withdrew into the vastness—leaving behind only a warm engine, a blind leviathan, and a song that would never have to end.
