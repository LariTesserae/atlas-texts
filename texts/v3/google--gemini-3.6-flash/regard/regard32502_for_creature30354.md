It came down through the chest-high clouds not as light, nor as thunder, but as an subtle alteration in the weight of the air.

The Power was ancient, unhurried, and vast—a consciousness born of the cold spaces between stars, accustomed to watching the slow, violent birth of worlds and the noisy, frantic die-offs of civilizations that burned too hot and too fast. It had seen a million races erect monuments of brass and glass, shouting their names into the void until the sky turned to ash and their cities to salt.

When its attention settled upon the four-thousand-meter rift, it did not see a primitive species struggling in a desolate place. It saw a masterpiece.

It observed the slate towers, stacked without mortar, their centers of gravity leaning inward with mathematical devotion. It observed the six-legged salamanders pressed together in the slate gaps, the silk-covered stalkers hunting in the drainage channels, and the velvet-shelled snails sealing the micro-fractures with mineral slime. It perceived the Basalt-Kin—their spade-like digits, their clear, sliding eyelids, their bone handrails polished by generations of wet palms, and their total, sacred commitment to silence.

A foolish power, moved by ignorant pity, might have brought fire to the rift. It might have split the high cloud cover to let the golden sun strike the black glass walls. It might have warmed the air, or given the Kin loud, spoken voices so they could sing across the canyon.

But this Power was wise. It understood that to bring fire to the rift would be to murder it. 

Sunlight would dry the fat, pale mosses and scorch the leather-fronded ferns. Heat would ruin the slow, exquisite torpor of the Kin, driving their slow-burning chemistry into a frantic, short-lived fever. Spoken words would shatter the dense, liquid quiet that allowed a builder to hear the structural stress of a slate block six stories down.

The Kin did not need salvation. They were not broken. They were a perfect gear in a silent, high-altitude clock.

Yet, benevolence seeks to give. And wisdom knows *where* the world is fragile.

The Power looked deeper than the sky-bridges, deeper than the cisterns carved by hand-chisels, down beneath the four-thousand-meter mark, into the dark, raw marrow of the mountain itself. 

The basalt here was young. Its fractures were still razor-sharp because the earth beneath it was still moving. Seven miles down, along a buried fault line of pale granite and basaltic plate, a massive wedge of kinetic tension had been building for three hundred years. It was a silent, subterranean pressure—a shear-stress waiting to snap. 

In less than two decades, the mountain would have twitched. A single, sharp micro-fracture at the base of the rift would have sent a shockwave tearing up through the vertical seam. The mortarless slate towers, for all their exquisite balance, would have sheared at their foundations. The braided cedar bridges would have snapped like dry hair. The fragile, cold-blooded city would have fallen into its own canyon in a single afternoon of dust and grinding rock.

The Basalt-Kin could not read a fault line seven miles down. Their lower jaws could only hear the mountain as high as its skin.

So, the Power acted.

It did not alter the surface. It did not speak. It descended quietly into the cold, crushing dark of the earth beneath the rift. 

There, in the deep granite roots, the Power expanded. It slipped its presence into the micro-fissures of the deep fault, weaving itself between the grinding faces of the subterranean rock like oil between bone. With infinite, gentle patience, it absorbed the kinetic stress. It bled the crushing pressure away, dispersing the violent energy sideways into vast, uninhabited basalt beds fifty leagues to the west, where it dissolved into harmless, low-frequency warmth.

It bound the base of the rift. It anchored the black glass cliffs, locking the shear-planes together so securely that the young mountain would stand firm for another hundred thousand winters. The towers of slate would not fall. The cisterns would not crack.

And then, as the violet-grey dusk settled over the canyon, the Power did one last thing.

High on the braided cedar sky-bridge, twelve of the Kin squatted in a motionless row, their velvet-backed shoulders pressed together against the cold wind. They dropped their split lower jaws onto the wet timber, sending their low, subsonic hum down into the ropes, down into the street, down into the bones of the city—testing the integrity of their world.

*Are you solid?* their bodies asked the stone. *Are you intact?*

Miles below, embedded in the very foundation of the cliff, the Power caught the tiny, whispery vibration of their song.

It did not change the frequency. It did not intrude. It simply gathered the return-echo of the basalt, smoothed away the minor structural tremors, and amplified the stone’s natural answer. 

Up through the slate towers, through the wet gutters, through the bone handrails, and into the wood of the sky-bridge, the mountain answered them. The response came back deeper, richer, and more profoundly solid than it ever had before—a hum of absolute, unfathomable permanence.

On the high bridge, the twelve Kin did not startle. They had no word for miracle.

They simply felt the deep, unbreakable strength beneath their spade-like toes. They felt the stone holding them, steady and eternal in the freezing fog. 

Satisfied, they slid their clear eyelids sideways, pulled their soft charcoal fur high around their ears, and let the cloud-tops wash over them, content to be wet, cold, and entirely safe in the dark.

And the Power, unseen and unthanked, remained below in the deep rock, listening to the soft, liquid *click* of salamander feet, keeping the world still.
