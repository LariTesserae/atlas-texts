The Power that came to the Oolitic Spine had no lungs to fill with the thin gas, no skin to crisp against the dry chill, and no weight to trigger the gravity that the mountains had long since forgotten. 

It was an intelligence vast enough to comprehend a universe, but wise enough to know that comprehension is often a clumsy, heavy thing. It approached not as a storm or a burning star—for either would have shattered the delicate, papery balance of the terraces—but as a quiet alteration in the ambient light, a shift so subtle that the perpetual thirty-degree shadows did not move by even a hair’s breadth.

It came because it had heard the sinew lines.

Across the dark, cold gulfs between world-systems, the tactile clicks of the six-fingered dwellers had echoed. To a human ear, those vibrations were nothing—dry, microscopic snaps fading instantly in a vacuum. But to a power that listened to the structural stress of reality itself, the Oolitic Spine was singing. It was a song made entirely of tension: a slow, ancient, agonizingly precise hymn of holding things together.

The Power paused at seven thousand meters. It extended its awareness into the stacked cards of space.

A foolish power, seeing this place, would have brought rain. It would have wept for the dryness, releasing torrents to soften the slate, wanting to see rivers carve the stone and green leaves uncurl from the quartz veins. But water here would be an apocalypse. Water would swell the parchment skin of the dwellers; it would dissolve the dry mineral felt of the Talc-Lappers; it would lubricate the shale until the friction vanished and the four-mile gorges collapsed into the inverted sky.

A lesser benevolent power would have brought motion. It would have unpinned the spatial locks, set the stones in flight, and freed the long-legged needle-flies to soar, mistaking the stillness of the Spine for a prison.

The Power that arrived was wiser than that. It looked into the amber, liquidless eyes of the Builder sitting in the shallow niche beneath the Great Shelf. It saw the flat, door-thin torso, the spade-like hands, the six fingers resting near the obsidian axe. It read the geometry of the creature’s mind. 

It realized: *They are not suffering. They are fulfilling a vow made to the architecture of the world.*

They were the last anchors. If they stopped placing slate discs upon the cairns, the folded space would snap like an over-tensioned spring, and the entire region would unspool into non-existence. They were tired with a fatigue that had long since passed beyond muscle and bone into the realm of pure geology—a quiet, honorable weariness that had lasted ten million years.

So the Power asked itself what a true gift would be to a world at the bottom of time.

It did not break the silence. It did not alter the sky. 

Instead, the Power did three things.

***

**First, it offered its own weight.**

The Power descended into the deepest, most precarious seam of the Spine—a place six miles down where two upside-down spires met at a razor-thin point of spatial instability. For three million years, four dwellers had been required to sit in nearby niches, their spade-fingers constantly adjusting a complex web of sixteen sinew lines, barely keeping the spires from sliding past one another into the void.

The Power slipped into that seam. It did not use stone or mortar. It simply pressed a fraction of its own eternal, immutable patience into the gap, becoming a silent, weightless wedge. It took the strain.

Six miles away, in a niche carved from ash-grey shale, a six-fingered dweller felt the sudden, miraculous slackening of its line. 

For the first time in an eon, the tension on the sinew went entirely neutral. The dweller did not startle—it possessed no startle response—but its amber lens-eyes shifted their focus by a microscopic fraction. It looked at the string. Then, with a slow, dry, mechanical fluidity, it uncurled its six long digits from the chert peg. 

It did not need to hold the line anymore. The seam was safe. Forever.

The dweller folded its triple-segmented legs beneath its lattice-torso, slid slightly deeper into the shade of its rock shelf, and entered a state that was not death, nor sleep, but pure, unburdened rest.

***

**Second, it joined the network.**

The Power did not speak to the dwellers in words, for words require time and sound, both of which were absent here. Instead, it touched the sinew lines.

It placed a gentle, rhythmic pulse upon the entire woven grid—a tactile vibration so low and steady it felt like the heartbeat of a mountain that had finally found its rest. It was a message composed entirely of texture: the feeling of dry slate laid perfectly flush; the feeling of a cairn that would never tip; the feeling of being seen, understood, and spared.

Across the vast, silent terraces, millions of six-fingered hands rested against the vibrating strings. 

In their amber eyes, the folded world did not change its shape, but the *meaning* of the folds shifted. They realized they were no longer alone in holding the weight. A second caretaker had stepped onto the terraces—one that would not exhaust its static charge, one that would never turn to talc.

***

**Third, it left a seed of memory.**

When a six-fingered dweller finally reached the end of its mineral clock, its body turned to solid stone, remaining in its niche forever. Over eons, hundreds of these petrified caretakers sat locked in the shale, silent monuments to their own long labor.

The Power walked among these frozen elders. It touched the milky, opaque lenses of their eyes. And into the crystal of their petrified marrow, it etched a permanent, microscopic light—a faint, crimson luminescence fed by the ambient static of the sky.

Now, when a dweller’s work was done and its parchment skin hardened into talc, it did not merely become a brick in the wall. A soft, warm, ochre glow began to emanate from within its chest, shining gently through the splits in the shale. 

The niches were no longer just shelters or tombs; they had become lanterns.

***

When the Power finally withdrew its vast consciousness from the upper air, the Oolitic Spine looked, to a casual glance, entirely unchanged.

The air remained cool, dry, and motionless at seven thousand meters. The slate-shelled crawlers still matched the cleavage of the rock. The parchment-winged flies still hung suspended in the spatial locks. The thirty-degree sunlight still cast its crisp, unmoving shadows across the dry, lead-colored moss.

But the labor of the world had changed.

Along the high terraces, a six-fingered dweller emerged from its shelter. It carried a single disc of slate, moving with that instantaneous, unaccelerated step across a spatial fold that spanned five miles of empty air. It reached a cairn, placed the stone upon the stack, and pulled the local seam tight.

Then, it turned its narrow, browless head toward the turquoise sky. 

It felt the steady, benevolent pulse humming through the sinew line beneath its feet. It looked down into the five-mile gorge and saw the warm, red light glowing softly from the niches of those who had gone before.

It did not smile, for its face was stone and horn. But it rested its spade-like hand flat against the slate, absorbed a single spark of static from the dry mountain, and sat down in the sunlight, content to watch the motionless world hold itself together—knowing that now, the mountain was holding it back.
