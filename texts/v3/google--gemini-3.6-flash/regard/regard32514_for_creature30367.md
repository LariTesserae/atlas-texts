It came down not through a rupture in the ceiling, nor with the thunder of a god descending, but as an awareness—vast, quiet, and weightless—filtering through five miles of solid basalt like water seeping through dry chalk. 

It was a power ancient enough to have seen stars ignite and cool, wise enough to know that light is not inherently better than dark, and benevolent enough to understand that to "save" a world you do not understand is merely to destroy it with kindness.

For a long time—several centuries by the count of the surface, though only a single breath in the tempo of the deep earth—the Power did not act at all. It simply witnessed.

It drifted along the unblinking, shadowless ceiling, drinking in the cold, cyan luminescence of the crystalline veins. It hovered above the wide thoroughfares, watching the pale, four-fingered builders dismantle a sprawling colonnade at dawn and reassemble its chalk blocks into a towering maze of terraces by noon. It listened—not with ears, for there were no ears in the Power, but with a deep, spatial empathy—to the ambient rustling of the plain: the soft, wet *hiss* of bone sledges gliding over lubricated slate; the rhythmic, muffled *thud* of copper wedges driven into chalk; the microscopic, liquid scrape of a centipede-crawler scouring mineral waste from an aqueduct channel.

The Power watched a worker drop its hemp harness, step into a clear, shallow puddle, and lock its joints into the standing stillness. It watched three cyan gliders flutter down from the ceiling vaults, their translucent, umbrella-like bellies glowing soft and cold, to rest upon the worker’s pale, slick chest, licking the salt-crusts from its skin.

And the Power understood.

A lesser deity, full of surface-world arrogance, might have looked upon this subterranean basin and seen a tragedy. It might have felt pity for creatures that had never seen a leaf, never felt the warmth of a sun, never heard a song spoken aloud, and never slept beneath a dark sky filled with stars. Such a deity would have cracked the basalt ceiling open. It would have brought down rain, planted seed-spores in the chalk, given them voices to shout with, and forced the night upon their silver, pupil-less eyes.

And in doing so, it would have murdered them.

The Power saw that there was no misery here. There was no grief, no war, no hunger that went unfed, no ambition that curdled into cruelty. The builders were not prisoners of the dark; they were the kinetic philosophy of the earth itself. They did not inhabit the city—they *were* the city. Their labor was not a sentence imposed upon them; it was their metabolism, their language, their singular, slow, physical thought. To give them rest would be to stop their hearts. To give them permanence would be to freeze their minds.

Yet, benevolence cannot remain entirely passive in the presence of such quiet, endless devotion. The Power wished to offer a gift—a blessing that would honor their geometry without altering a single line of its truth.

It reasoned thus: *If their life is a thought, then where is the answer? If their labor is a question asked of the stone, what is the stone’s reply?*

The Power turned its attention to the center of the plain—to the shallow, ankle-deep pool where the workers stood waist-deep, shaping the great egg-like boulders of white quartz with primitive copper rasps. It watched them roll a finished sphere—smooth, unangled, holding no leverage points—out of the water, up the sliding slate avenues, and set it gently in the soft talus at the foot of the ancient shale cliffs.

Thousands of these eggs sat in long, luminous rows along the perimeter of the basin, unmoving, pristine, untouched by the endless demolition of the city. 

The Power descended to the floor of the valley. It took no form; it brought no warmth to the cool, cellar air. But as it passed through the wet plazas, it touched the stone.

First, it adjusted the microscopic friction of the bedrock. It did not make the floor magic, nor did it remove the weight of the chalk-blocks; labor was the builders' purpose, and to make it weightless would be to make it meaningless. Instead, it subtly refined the crystalline alignment of the bedded slate beneath the thin film of condensation. The water became a slightly more perfect lubricant—just enough that the bone sledges slid with a deeper, more harmonious ease, reducing the deep, water-logged bruising on the workers' graying skin by a fraction of a degree.

Second, it spoke to the gliders and the crawlers. It did not give them command or intellect, but it braided a subtle, invisible warmth into the spore-dust filtering down from the ceiling veins—a nourishment that allowed the cyan gliders to glow just a shade brighter, casting a faint, beautiful halo of turquoise over the pale shoulders of the standing workers.

Finally, the Power moved along the perimeter of the basin, where the thousands of quartz eggs rested against the crumbling shale.

It touched each sphere, one by one.

It did not alter their shape. It did not carve patterns into their pristine, hand-smoothed surfaces. Instead, the Power deposited a tiny, eternal seed of resonance deep within the core of every egg—a passive, subsonic harmonic, perfectly tuned to the frequency of the Earth’s core.

From that moment on, whenever a worker finished a quartz egg and rolled it to the edge of the world, something new happened. 

When the worker laid its four-fingered, calloused hands upon the smooth quartz for the final time before returning to the endless construction of the plain, the stone would not merely be cold and silent. The quartz would vibrate—a faint, gentle, subsonic pulse that traveled through the horn-like pads of their digits, up the bones of their arms, and directly into the membrane-slits behind their jaws.

It was not a command. It was not a voice. 

It was a echo. It was the deep, stone-bound acknowledgment that their thought had been received; that the long, chaotic dance of chalk and slate had been recorded in the foundation of the world; that even in a sky that never darkens, beneath a light that never shifts, their quiet, relentless existence was seen, understood, and deemed entirely good.

Satisfied, the benevolent Power withdrew, retreating up through the five miles of solid basalt, back into the boundless reaches of the cosmos.

Below, in the cold, shadowless glare of the basin, a builder stepped out of the central pool, trailing water over the flagstones. It helped three others push a freshly rounded quartz sphere up the stepped terraces, sliding it across the slick slate until it settled into the long row against the pale cliff.

The builder paused. It did not have a name, nor a voice to speak one. But it pressed its yellowed, calloused fingertips against the cool white surface of the quartz.

Deep within the stone, something hummed back—a low, perfect, liquid thrum that resonated in its jawbone.

The builder stood motionless for a long time, its silver, pupil-less eyes reflecting the unblinking roof of the world, while a cyan glider drifted down to land quietly upon its shoulder. 

Then, it let go. It turned back toward the shifting avenues, picked up a bone sledge, and began, with quiet, unhurried grace, to tear down the morning’s city so that the afternoon’s thought could begin.
