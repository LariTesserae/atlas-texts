The Power did not arrive with a thunderclap, nor with a burning flame. 

To bring flame into this world would be an act of war; to bring a blinding sky-light would be to scorch the broad-leafed canopy, dry the wet liverworts, and blind the pale, lidless eyes of the gliders. Wisdom knows that a world made of wet wood and quiet breath cannot bear the weight of glory.

So, the Power came as a shift in the temperature of the mist—a gentle, imperceptible cooling of a fraction of a degree, just enough to cause the suspended water-spheres to bead with a clearer, sweeter dew.

It walked without feet across the continent-spanning spars. It moved down the three-mile mortises, past the white, chitinous chambers of the dead Cable-Spinners, and through the glowing indigo territory of the Rot-Gazers. It observed everything, and its heart—vast, quiet, and ancient—was filled with an immense, aching tenderness.

It saw a world built by forgotten suffering that had, over millennia, softened into grace.

The Power asked itself the question that only the truly wise ask when encountering a delicate, self-sustained beauty:

*What must I do?*

***

### What It Did Not Do

It did not give the Splicers thumbs. It did not give them voices to cry out to the empty sky, nor minds that longed for stone, iron, or the warmth of an open fire. To give them a longing for the earth would be to condemn them to a life of perpetual exile on a highway in the air. 

It did not slay the silent gliders that fell through the amber fog to hunt the young. To remove the predator would be to let the soft-boned populations explode until the liverworts were stripped bare, the cambium peeled to the grain, and the great beams devoured from the outside in.

It did not strip away the rot. Rot was the hearth of this world. Without rot, the bioluminescent gelatin could not grow; without the mold, the Cable-Spinners would starve; without the Spinners, the joints would shear and the miles-high arches would fall into the dark abyss.

The Power understood that this world was a single, continuous gesture of survival, where decay and repair were locked in a slow, perfect dance. To "fix" it by divine decree would be to break the weave.

***

### What It Did

Instead, the Power did three things—subtle, deep, and quiet as the growth of a root.

#### 1. The Anchoring of the Deep Grain
Deep within the core of the ancient petrified heartwood—in the places so dense that even the Rot-Gazers’ optic threads could not penetrate—the Power pressed its attention. 

It did not alter the wood, but it spoke to the slumbering cambium. It laid a subtle, permanent elasticity into the cellular lattice of the great load-bearing trusses. Where ten miles of wood flexed under the weight of a water-logged burl, the grain was given a memory of perfect tension. From that moment on, no archway would ever suffer a catastrophic snap. Joints would still groan; dowels would still slowly shear over centuries, giving the Splicers work and purpose; but the world’s ultimate foundation was made impossible to break. The floor beneath their velvet feet would hold, forever.

#### 2. The Song in the Wood
The Power leaned its forehead against a main highway spar—a beam seven miles long and wide as a mountain ridge—and struck it once, very gently, with the weight of its intention.

It was not a sound that traveled through the air. It was a sub-vocal thrum, a pulse that rippled through every spliced trunk, every braided joint, and every pinned dowel across the entire continent. 

To the Splicers, pressing their sensitive, flat chests against the damp bark, it did not feel like an intrusion. It felt like the deepest, most comforting chord the wood had ever sung. It carried no words, no commandments, and no threats. It carried only a fundamental physical truth, translated into vibration:

*The house is held. You are not alone in the quiet.*

The Splicers stopped their work for a single, long moment. Across hundreds of miles of suspended architecture, six-limbed grey-white forms paused on the lips of drainage troughs, upside-down under rain-slicked trusses, and deep inside the dark sockets of the mortises. They felt the thrum settle into their springy cartilage skeletons. They did not understand it, for they had no concept of gods or powers, but their lace-curtained spiracles widened, and their velvet skin relaxed. 

When they resumed scraping the rot and weeping their amber resin, they worked with a strange, rhythmic ease that would linger in their blood for a thousand generations.

#### 3. The Memory in the Resin
Finally, the Power visited the places where the Splicers laid their rubbery, translucent eggs—inside the hollowed, chitinous shells of the ancient Cable-Spinners.

It touched the amber latex that wept from the Splicers' palms—the sticky, sap-like fluid they used to bind moss into the wounds of the wood. The Power left a gift inside the chemical memory of that resin.

Now, whenever a Splicer pressed their palms to a stress-fracture and poured their life-work into a split beam, the curing amber would glow—not with the sharp, urgent indigo of the Rot-Gazer, but with a faint, warm, golden light that lasted for a few hours. And in that light, reflected in the soft water of the nursery-chambers, the developing larvae would feel a sensation they had lost before history began:

The feeling of a hard, steady hand holding a stone chisel. The feeling of love, intention, and craftsmanship. The warmth of the forgotten builders, returned to them not as a ghost, but as an abiding peace.

***

### The Departure

When it was done, the Power did not leave a monument. It carved no name into the heartwood. It left no holy scripture written in the bark.

It simply withdrew, stepping back into the vastness between worlds, as softly as a rising cloud of vapor.

Below, in the green-brown twilight, a house-sized sphere of water drifted slowly between two three-mile-wide arches. Inside it, a Sphere-Nymph swam in silent, perfect circles. On the beam above, a Splicer struck the wood with its knee—*thump, thump-thump*—sending news of a fresh patch of moss down ten miles of wet, living timber.

The world remained wet, quiet, and old. But deep inside the wood, where the ancient grain turned to petrified stone, the world was loved; and so, it held.
