It came without the tearing of the sky or the thunder of heralds. 

To bring a violent glory to such a place would have been a cruelty, for the basin was already balanced on the edge of a feather. A single loud gesture from a god could have shattered the acoustic glass of the amphitheater, startled the swifts into abandoning their honeycomb cliffs, or boiled the precious, gelatinous sap within the waxy leaves of the *wax-flesh*.

Instead, the Power arrived as an intention—a vast, cool mind descending through the bruise-colored sky, settling over the rim of the basin like a layer of dry, weightless air. 

It was an entity that had seen the birth of stars and the drowning of continents. It understood the mathematics of empires and the decay of iron. Yet, as its gaze sank into the tiny, warm tooth of the mountain, it paused. 

It felt the rhythm of the place before it saw it. It felt the *thump-thump* of the heavy wooden pestles, a heartbeat striking through the basalt. It saw the wide, luminous pupils of the compact people, turning toward the soft copper twilight. It smelled the heavy, resinous perfume of wood-gum, the sharp tang of swift-guano, and the clean, greased skin of children playing with knuckle-bones in the dust.

The Power listened. It heard a mother on the lower tier pitch her voice into a shallow concavity in the basalt—a sound like a low, vibrating bell—and watched as the wave of sound rolled across two hundred paces of open air, reflecting perfectly into an alcove on the seventh tier, where an elder nodded and hummed in reply.

*A perfect machine,* the Power thought. *A living pearl, carved by wind and held in the palm of the empty sky.*

The Power’s benevolence was matched by its formidable wisdom. And wisdom, above all things, knows the danger of the gift.

To a lesser deity, the desire to "bless" this cradle would have been overwhelming. A lesser god would have struck the basalt floor with a staff and summoned a gushing spring of sweet, cold water. But the Power knew what water would do here. Rain or a stream would drown the low-slung, sun-baked clay houses. It would rot the delicate, thread-like lichen hanging from the roofs. It would wash away the dry, precious guano before the shrubs could drink it. It would make the clackers slick and cold, killing their appetite for the grubs that threatened the walls. Water, the ultimate gift of the lowland gods, would be a deluge of death at the crown of the world.

To offer them iron, or fire, or broad-leafed crops from the deep valleys would be to break the fragile, closed loop of their existence. If they did not have to scrape the morning lichen with swift-bone spatulas, their days would lose their rhythm. If they did not rely on the dark, scythe-winged swifts returning at dusk, their gaze would turn inward, and the ancient, quiet pact between the mountain and the sky would dissolve.

The Power realized that these people were not suffering. They were not waiting for a savior. They had reached that rarest of cosmic states: a species that had found its final, harmonious shape. They were complete.

So, what does a wise power do when it finds something that is already whole?

It does not build. It does not speak. It becomes the shield between the perfection and the entropy of the universe.

Silent as a shadow moving over sunlit rock, the Power reached down into the mountain's heart.

First, it addressed the **Stone**. 

The grand, overhanging brow of granite that shaded the village was ancient. Millions of years of temperature swings had left microscopic fractures deep within its spine. In three thousand years—a mere blink to the mountain, but sixty generations to the people—a major frost or a sudden deep tremor would have sheared that massive overhang away. It would have crushed the western terraces and left the basin exposed to the blinding, scorching light of the high sun, turning their cool twilight into an oven.

The Power slid its consciousness into the hairline cracks of the granite. It did not use magic that glowed or hummed; it simply pressed the mineral grains together, fusing the quartz and feldspar at an atomic level. It knitted the mountain's bones back together, anchoring the brow so deeply into the roots of the world that no earth-shaking or age could ever dislodge it. The shade would remain. The twilight was secured for ten thousand generations.

Second, it touched the **Swifts**.

The Power turned its gaze outward, sweeping down the sheer, featureless cliffs, past the cloud deck, into the dense, humid lowlands miles below. It saw that in the forests below, a strange, ash-colored blight was beginning to take root in the marsh-reeds—a rot that would, within a century, starve the high-altitude insect drafts upon which the swifts fed. 

Without moving a single rock in the basin, the Power altered the wind. It established a perpetual, invisible thermal current—a gentle, warm conveyor of air that would forever sweep up the eastern face of the massif. This draft would carry a endless, rich drift of high-flying midges and draft-bugs up from the healthy highlands further south, ensuring that no matter how the lowland forests shifted or withered, the swifts’ crops would always be full when they tumbled back over the stone lip at dusk. The gardens in the sky would never go hungry.

Third, and finally, the Power gave a gift to the **Dead and the Living**.

High under the granite brow, where the mummified ancestors sat side-by-side in their narrow niches, the air was dry and peaceful. But because the stone was cold, the voices of the living below could not quite reach them.

The Power reached out a thumb of pure, benevolent warmth and touched the high rock behind the dead. It infused the basalt of the upper cliffs with a subtle, permanent resonance—a natural acoustic memory. From that moment on, the rock beneath the ancestors would gently hum with the vibrations of the village below. Every low-pitched chant, every *thump* of the mortar, every wet *crrr-crrr* of the mountain fowl would travel up through the stone like a warm current. The ancestors would not just sit in the quiet wind; they would feel the living pulse of their children pressing against their dried, leathery backs forever.

And for the living, the Power left one tiny, imperceptible grace.

It touched the air trapped within the bowl, altering its chemistry by the fraction of a degree. It made the moisture extracted from the lichen just a fraction sweeter; it made the jelly of the *wax-flesh* leaves cool the throat a little longer; it made the skin-salve rendered from the old clackers hold its oil against the dry wind for a few hours more.

It did not change their struggle; it simply made their mastery of it feel effortless.

When it was finished, the Power did not leave a mark. There was no altar, no commandments written on the granite, no dream sent to a priest. To be known would be a burden to them; they would spend generations trying to understand a mind that was not meant for their small stone world.

The Power slowly drew its presence back up into the dark, bruise-colored blue of the high atmosphere. It looked down one last time.

Below, a woman with skin the color of wet basalt stepped out onto a high ledge. She carried a dried gourd filled with sweet, grey lichen-sludge. She looked up toward the sky—not because she felt the god leaving, but simply because the morning air was warm and the swift-wings were already whistling overhead as the flock dropped out of the bowl into the clouds.

She took a breath of the thin, warm air. It felt good in her flared, barrel-like chest. She picked up a sliver of flint, cut a leaf of *wax-flesh*, and held the clear core between her teeth, letting the cool sap bleed down her throat.

She tapped her thick, calloused heel against the stone step—a step dished out by her great-grandmother's feet—and pitched her voice into the rock. 

The low, resonant sound bounced across the basin, warm and smooth, carrying no prayer, no plea, and no fear. Just the simple, loud, permanent news that she was there, that the day was warm, and that the mountain was holding them fast.

Far above, the Power smiled, and turned its gaze toward the cold, quiet stars.
