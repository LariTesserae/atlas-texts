The Wise Power came to the high wood not on wings, nor in a chariot of thunder, but as a subtle shift in the light—a sudden, deep gold that settled into the mist-veils floating between the three-thousand-foot spires.

For a long time, the Power did not move. It simply *listened*.

It felt the deep, bone-shaking hum of the five-mile root-node beneath its perception. It took in the deafening, mechanical roar of the billion Shell-Grinders rasping the iron grain; the low, resonant foghorn booms of the pale Flute-dwellers expanding in their dark chimneys; the sharp *crack* of leather wings catching the thermal currents; and the slow, century-long slide of the Amber-gills pushing through honey-thick resin.

A lesser power—an ambitious, impatient divinity—would have looked at this world and seen a tragic ruin. It would have thought: *There is no soil here. There are no rivers of sweet water, no meadows, no soft beds for sleep. I shall bring rain, I shall rot the heartwood, I shall lay down green grass and give these poor, clinging humans iron and silk.*

But the Wise Power understood the cruelty of unthinking pity.

To rot the wood would be to collapse the cathedrals. To bring heavy rain would wash the fragile bark-dwellings into the two-mile abyss. To give the humans steel would be to teach them to destroy the iron-grain that supported them; to give them soil would be to smother the micro-mosses that fed the clingers, unspooling the entire, intricate clockwork of the ecosystem.

This world was not broken. It was *finished*. It was an ancient, perfect machine running at its terminal equilibrium. The creatures were not suffering; they were optimized. The humans were not wretched; they were exquisite.

The Power watched the single human sitting on the carved ledge. It saw the cracked, bark-like soles of their feet, gripping the polished wood with three points of contact. It saw the careful, rhythmic movement of the rib-scraper shaving dark wax from the giant tendril. It saw the thin lanyard of braided vine tied tightly to the human’s wrist, anchoring the rib-bone tool to the flesh, and the flesh to the mountain.

The Power felt the human’s mind. There was no despair in it. There was only an immense, quiet focus, a profound intimacy with the grain, and a deep, instinctual reverence for weight, wind, and friction.

The Power smiled—a gesture that manifest across twenty miles of open sky as a brief, gentle lull in the turbulent thermals.

*I will not change your world,* the Power thought, speaking into the quiet space behind the wall of sound. *I will not make it soft. Softness would kill you. But I will give you grace within the hard places.*

And so, the Wise Power performed four acts of quiet, invisible tending.

***

### First: The Law of the Second Chance
The Power looked down into the two-mile drop beneath the human settlement. It knew the terror of the falling thing—how a single missed grip or a frayed vine meant a tool, a gourd, or a body was claimed forever by the warm abyss.

The Power did not remove gravity, for gravity was what kept the clingers ratcheted to the slopes. Instead, it spoke to the rising thermals. It wove a permanent, subtle eddy into the warm air currents that washed up from the deep rifts. 

From that moment on, if a scraper, a mallet, or a water-gourd slipped from a human’s hand, it would not plummet straight down into the void. The warm air would catch it, tilting its fall into a long, floating arc that would gently deposit the lost object onto a lower ledge or into a soft pocket of tea-colored moss a hundred feet below. 

It was not a miracle that broke the world’s physics; it was a merciful drift. The humans would still have to climb down to fetch what they lost, maintaining their caution and their skill, but the absolute, unforgiving finality of the drop was softened.

### Second: The Song of the Ropes
The Power leaned down and touched the braided ropes of inner-bark that held the suspended dwellings to the root-knots. 

It did not turn the bark to steel. Instead, it imparted a gift of prophecy to the plant fibers themselves. From that day forth, when a rope was nearing the end of its strength—when the bitter tannins were dry and the inner strands were about to snap under the stress of the wind—it would begin to sing. It would absorb the friction of the grinding beetles and resonate with a clear, bell-like ring that could be heard even over the deafening wall of noise. 

The humans would never again be surprised by a snapping line in the dark of night. The wood would tell them when it was tired, and give them time to weave a new strand.

### Third: The Lanterns of the Fissures
The Power peered into the six-foot-wide dark fissures where the resin trickled an inch a day and the Amber-gills swam their century-long laps. It saw that the humans sometimes leaned over these terrifying gaps to harvest dark wax or ancient, hardened amber, working in dangerous obscurity.

The Power reached into the slow-moving rivers of golden resin and touched the trapped air bubbles left behind by the primordial trees. It breathed a faint, cold bioluminescence into them. 

Deep within the dark splits of the plateau, a soft, eternal, amber light began to bloom. It did not burn; it did not melt the resin. It simply cast a warm, golden glow from beneath the slick wood, illuminating the foot-holds for the humans and revealing the slow, silent ballet of the Amber-gills moving through the depths like stars trapped in honey.

### Fourth: The Aegis of the High Void
Finally, the Power rose high above the canopy-mountains, up to the boundary where the soft, warm, sap-scented atmosphere met the thin, freezing void of the true sky. 

It saw that, once every few thousand years, a cold solar wind or a shift in the high atmosphere would threaten to drop a frost onto the root-nodes—a frost that would freeze the saucer-pools, crack the iron-hard wood, and kill the delicate micro-mosses.

The Power stretched its vast, invisible hands across the sky, weaving a permanent, warm envelope around the high-wood world. It became a silent shield, ensuring that the thermals rising from the unseen roots below would never fail, and that the temperature on the five-mile domes would remain forever thick, warm, and smelling of boiled sap and old wax.

***

When its work was finished, the Power returned to the ledge where the single human sat.

The human had just finished shaving a thick ribbon of wax. They paused, wiping their brow with the back of a tanned, scarred forearm. 

The Power did not manifest as a giant face or a burning voice. It knew that to reveal itself fully would shatter the human’s quiet, self-reliant dignity. Instead, as the human picked up their gourd of warm dew to drink, the Power allowed a single, momentary sensation to pass through the human’s heart:

*A profound, baseless feeling of safety.*

The human did not know where it came from. But suddenly, sitting on the edge of a two-mile drop, surrounded by a deafening, metallic roar and the snapping of leather wings, the human felt as though the entire five-mile mass of petrified heartwood was holding them up on purpose. They felt that they were not an accident clinging to a dead skeleton, but a beloved child resting on the knee of a giant.

The human took a sip of the sweet, tannin-stained dew, let out a long, quiet sigh, and leaned back against the iron grain of the root-knot. 

They looked out across the twenty-mile gulf to the pale golden sky, smiled at the sheer, beautiful vastness of it all, and went back to work.

And the Wise Power, satisfied that the world was safe, whole, and unchanged in its perfection, withdrew into the light—leaving the engine of the high wood to turn, booming and grinding, for a million years more.
