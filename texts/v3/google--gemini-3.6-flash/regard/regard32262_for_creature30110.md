The Power did not come down as a footstep, for a footstep would have crushed the quartz-glass and turned the micro-city into a smear of copper dust and wet slag. 

It came down as a shadow that contained no darkness—a vast, quiet bending of attention, like a lens focusing on a lens. 

For a long time, the Power simply watched. Wisdom begins with the refusal to interrupt what is already working.

It observed the cog-backs ratcheting across the flaking iron, their brass-plated carapaces ringing like microscopic cymbals against the conduit. It smelled the sharp, comforting plume of nutmeg and tallow wafting from the junction box as the blue-bellied bees caught the spark. It watched the three-legged spider, anchored in the rust-pocks of the silent flywheel, lower its feathered limb into the thimble-basin with the patient, rhythmic gravity of an old clockwork weaver.

And, deep in the socket of the sheared axle, the Power felt the slow, twelve-second expansion of the purple-black heart—the child of slime and salamander, breathing through steam-cracks, pouring its pale milk down the spokes to feed the crimson worms.

The Power felt no pity for this place. To feel pity would be a failure of sight. This was not a prison; it was an altar built by accident and inhabited by grace. It was life doing what life has done since the first lightning struck the first warm soup: taking the broken edges of an indifferent universe and weaving them into a home.

Yet, because the Power was wise, it saw the single, fatal flaw woven into the pocket’s warmth.

This little world was built upon an illusion of permanence. Its sun was a discarded observation port that a single stray rock from the surface could shatter. Its heat was the waste bleed of a human steam-pump whose iron pistons would eventually seize, or be dismantled, or be abandoned to cold rust when the factory above went bankrupt. Its electric tick—the rhythm that kept the bees singing their nutmeg scent and clearing the air—was bound to a copper ground-wire that would one day corrode through or go dead.

The human world above was a shifting, chaotic sea. Eventually, it would move on. The steam would stop. The light would go out. The pocket would freeze, the pale milk would dry in the spokes, and the cog-backs would become nothing more than silent, metallic dust at the bottom of a dark, cold fracture.

The Power did not wish to remake the pocket into a grand cathedral. To grant these creatures human intelligence, or vast wings, or freedom from their tiny stone crate would be an insult to the exquisite precision of their adaptation. The slug-newt did not dream of the open ocean; its paradise was the wet basalt corner and the sulfur-fire in its belly.

So the Power did not reach in to change what *was*. It reached in to change what *supported* it.

It laid a single, invisible finger against the foundation of the world.

First, it touched the quartz-glass port in the ceiling. It did not alter the glass itself, but it wove a subtle, permanent warp into the stone around it. No falling beam, no shift of masonry, no settling of the foundation above would ever press upon that thick slab again. And beneath the glass, it anchored the light: even if dust and coal-soot covered the upper surface, the focused beam passing through would remain constant—a warm, golden midday that drew its brightness not merely from the sky, but from the quiet, luminous memory of the sun stored in the deep stone.

Second, it touched the steam-crack behind the Hub-Entity’s gills. It bypassed the human pipes above, sinking a hairline filament of heat down, down through miles of granite, straight into the quiet, thermal veins of the earth’s mantle. Should the steam-pumps above burst or grow cold, the deep earth would gently take up the burden. The crack would go on breathing its moist, sulfurous warmth at the exact temperature the purple-black heart required.

Third, it touched the stripped copper ground-wire in the junction box. It unhooked the wire’s subtle current from the noisy, flickering generator on the surface, and tuned it instead to the slow, electromagnetic hum of the rotating planet. 

*Click... click... click...*

The tick remained. It did not change its tempo by a microsecond. But now, it was no longer the pulse of a human engine; it was the rhythm of the iron core beneath the crust. The blue bees shimmered in unison. A fresh, warm puff of nutmeg vapor drifted out across the wet slate, slightly richer now, carrying a hint of crushed clove and rain.

Finally, the Power bent its attention toward the Hub.

The purple-black organ in the axle-socket did not possess eyes, but it felt the presence. It did not experience fear; it had no concept of danger outside the drop of temperature or the drying of steam. But as the Power’s thought touched its braided nerve-fibers, the creature’s twelve-second pulse paused for a fraction of a heartbeat.

The Power did not give the creature a mind. It gave it a *promise*.

It touched the pores that secreted the pale milk, infusing the fluid with a subtle, self-correcting memory. If the cog-backs ever over-grazed, the milk would grow slightly richer in iron-salts to speed the hatching of the crimson worms. If the slug-newts grew sluggish, the milk would thin, sharpening their appetite. The creature in the hub was no longer just an accidental thermostat—it was made immortal in its stewardship, bound to the pocket as long as the stone stood.

The purple-black tissue swelled slightly, drawing in a deep, wet breath of the subterranean steam. Its pale gills rippled like feathers in a breeze. It began to pulse again: *one... two... three...* steady and calm, driving the pale milk down the rusted flywheel.

The Power withdrew. 

It did not leave a sign. It left no holy altar, no burning bush, no glowing runes etched into the granite. The iron conduits remained flaking and orange; the slug-newt continued to pin a cog-back against the basalt; the three-legged spider dipped its feathered palps into the thimble-basin and drew out its knot of writhing crimson worms.

The world above would rage, build, decay, and fall into ruin. Great iron cities would rise over the steam-pump array, crumble into dust, and be replaced by forest or ocean or ash. 

But down here, in a space no larger than a shipping crate, locked beneath the earth, the light would remain unblinking. The water would bead on the glass and drop onto the rust. The bees would catch their sacred, electric spark. And in the dark, warm hub of the broken wheel, the nameless heart would go on beating, keeping its tiny, perfect creation alive in the dark skirts of eternity.
