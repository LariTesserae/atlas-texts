The Power came out of the cold, deep silence between the stars—a presence vast enough to cradle a sun, yet so subtle that its arrival did not press down upon the amber atmosphere by a single feather’s weight of pressure. 

It was a consciousness born of ancient light and deep understanding. It had seen worlds of liquid blue that wept in endless tides; worlds of iron that shrieked as they cooled; worlds where intellect burned like a fever, building empires of glass and regret, only to tear them down in tears.

It hovered at the thermospheric fringe, unfolding its attention like a vast, invisible net, and it looked down upon the fractured plain.

It felt the heat rising off the pale chalk and dark volcanic glass. It listened to the tapestry of dry, quiet sounds: the soft *click-click* of twelve porcelain feet; the paper-thin rustle of burgundy foliage; the heavy, subterranean *churn-thrum* of a Marrow-Mason digesting stone in the dark.

A lesser power—a young god, or a clumsy spirit of creation—might have looked upon this place and felt a profound, tragic pity. It would have said: *Look at them. They are parched. They do not know the joy of a cool spring. They do not know the smell of wet soil, or the sky-shattering glory of a rainstorm.* And that lesser power would have summoned clouds. It would have cracked the sky with thunder and poured millions of gallons of sweet, fresh water onto the dry city.

And in doing so, it would have committed an absolute genocide.

The rain would have turned the mineral towers into boiling white slurry. It would have dissolved the lime-paste and eroded the subterranean nurseries. The water would have washed into the spiracles of the lungless Vellum-Runners, drowning them in their tracks. It would have converted the petrol-nectar of the waxy blooms into a foul, ruined emulsion, and turned the hard, amber eggs of the Masons into soft, rotting pulp.

The wise Power knew this. It understood that benevolence is not the act of imposing one’s own definition of paradise upon another; it is the art of recognizing a paradise that is already complete.

This world was a hymn of dry perfection. It was an engine that burned without smoke, a life-form that spanned a continent without a single thought of war, cruelty, or greed. Nothing here suffered, because nothing here fought its environment. The creatures *were* the environment, a self-repairing, self-expanding tapestry of horn, silk, and vitrified dust.

So the Power did not bring rain. It did not drop down from the sky in a flash of thunder to demand worship from creatures that had no eyes to see it and no ears to hear a voice. 

Instead, the Power sat in the high, silent vacuum, and it considered what a true gift would be.

After a long time—a century on the plain, measured in the slow, rhythmic glazing of ten thousand gallery-towers—the Power acted. It made three interventions. They were so small, so deeply woven into the physical constants of the world, that not a single insect paused in its work.

***

First, the Power looked outward, into the dark void that surrounded the world's amber sky. 

It saw that in three million years, a stray, iron-heavy asteroid—a dead mountain traveling at forty miles a second—was destined to strike the center of the fractured plain, shattering the volcanic foundations and vaporizing the continent-city in a flash of apocalyptic heat. 

The Power reached out a single, invisible thread of gravitational intent. Millions of light-years away, it gave that drifting iron mountain a microscopic nudge. The asteroid shifted its trajectory by less than the width of a hair. Three million years from now, it would pass harmlessly through the black sky, a faint golden shooting star that would burn out in the upper air without disturbing a single leaf of bronze foliage.

Then, the Power looked at the sun—the steady, bright eye that baked the terracotta walls day after day. It saw the stellar core, calculating the natural life of the star. It saw that the star’s nuclear furnace would eventually swell, turning hot and blue, drying even the resin and scorching the wax to ash.

The Power reached its mind into the star’s churning heart. Gently, with the delicate care of a Sun-Glazer polishing a seam of obsidian, it smoothed the stellar convection currents. It balanced the fusion of hydrogen, locking the star into a state of permanent, golden middle age. The day would remain warm, bright, and amber for ten billion years. The city would never freeze; the city would never burn.

***

Second, the Power turned its attention to the dust.

Every few days, the continental silt-fronts swept across the plain, bringing the raw lime and silica that the building-fauna gathered to reinforce their galleries. But these fronts were born of chaos; occasionally, a violent atmospheric anomaly would generate a tempest so fierce it would shear away the high gallery-bridges before the Sun-Glazers could seal them.

The Power breathed upon the dry atmosphere. It did not alter the weather, but it tuned the thermal harmonics of the plain. It arranged the air currents so that the dust-fronts would always arrive with a predictable, gentle rhythm. The winds would roar, but they would never exceed the precise structural tolerance of a fully cured resin arch. The silts would fall like a steady, generous snowfall of pale powder, settling softly into the waiting mandibles of the builder-fauna.

***

Finally, the Power did something that was purely for itself—an act of quiet, divine appreciation.

It did not grant the creatures intelligence. To give a Lacquer-Clipper a soul or a self-reflective mind would be to give it doubt, fear, and the agonizing awareness of its own mortality. The Clipper was perfect as a vessel of pure, unthinking instinct.

Instead, the Power placed a single, microscopic artifact at the exact geometric center of the continent, buried ten miles down within the crystalline bedrock beneath the deepest Marrow-Mason.

It was a prism of pure, concentrated attention—a divine "eye" that did not interfere with anything around it. It possessed no mass, radiated no heat, and emitted no light. It was simply a point of permanent, loving awareness embedded in the stone.

Through this prism, the Power would forever feel the world. 

It would feel the rhythmic, soft *shhh-shhh* of the Chalk-Paws’ leathery bellies as they shuffled through the terracotta arcades. 
It would feel the precise, surgical click of a Lacquer-Clipper harvesting petroleum-nectar. 
It would feel the cold, parchment grace of the Vellum-Runners flowing like shadows through the jade foliage. 
It would feel the quiet, warm, sulfur-scented nurseries where the amber eggs lay stacked in the dark, waiting to hatch into a world that was safe, dry, and eternal.

The Power understood that beauty does not require a audience to be real, but it is made richer when it is witnessed.

With its work complete, the vast, benevolent presence withdrew its focus from the immediate skies, scattering its consciousness back into the starry deep. It left no footprint on the chalk, no sign in the amber sky, and no legend in the minds of the inhabitants.

Below, on the sharp edges of the volcanic glass, a Sun-Glazer flattened its broad porcelain disk against a gallery wall. A warm, dust-laden breeze swept out of the golden horizon, carrying the sharp, sweet smell of warm wax and dry cedar. The Glazer extruded a bead of clear, vitreous varnish, smoothed it over a hairline crack in the clay, and kept moving—slowly, steadily, perfectly—across the face of a world that would never end.
