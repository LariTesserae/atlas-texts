The Power did not descend as a flash of light, for light in that place was already a diffused and sacred bronze, and a sudden brilliance would have burned the broad, un-eyelidded eyes resting in the moss-lined towers. 

Nor did it speak in a voice of thunder. Thunder belongs to the air—to the cold, thin, violent realm of the *Light-Without-Weight*, where sounds shatter against dry stone and vanish into nothingness. Here, where sound was a heavy, physical pulse carried through warm liquid, a loud voice would have struck the wide ribs of the stone-cutters like an iron mallet, tearing the delicate cartilage behind their jaws.

The Power came instead as a presence felt only by the deep stone beneath the pillow-lavas—a consciousness as large as the mountain range, yet as gentle as the rising of a heat-plume.

***

### I. The Perception

For seven hundred years of the city’s slow time, the Power simply watched. 

It watched from the deep earth and from the green mist above. It listened to the rhythmic, distant *thud-clack* of river-cobble against granite. It watched two weavers at a reed-frame, their skins shifting in subtle, rippling waves of slate-gray and muted ochre as they passed bone needles between them, exchanging thoughts without a breath of air or a tremor of haste.

It observed their children—the amber eggs wedged into the sulfur-sweet fissures, washed by the slow churn of heated earth-water, tended not by a single anxious pair but by every broad-handed traveler who drifted past the vent.

It observed their single, central preoccupation: *balance*. The careful addition of a lead stone to a waist-strap; the subtracting of a pebble when the belly was light; the communal hovering on the reed-bridges at twilight, where no one stood higher than another, where no one fell, and where no one was dragged into the dark.

The Power had seen ten thousand worlds. It had seen empires carved from brass and oil, driven by the frantic terror of their own brevity—beings who built towers to escape the earth, only to fall from them; beings who burned their forests to warm their hands, only to choke on the ash. It had seen gods who demanded altars, who split the sky with laws and doctrines, leaving their children weeping in the dust, trying to measure up to an invisible height.

Here, there was no height to reach. There was only the bath, the stone, the kelp, and the slow, beautiful weightlessness of a people who had asked for nothing more than to endure together.

***

### II. The Decision of Wisdom

A lesser power—a foolish god, young and full of vanity—would have sought to "raise" them. 

It would have parted the ceiling of mist to show them the stars. It would have drawn them up out of the warm water onto the cold, wind-scoured basalt, shouting, *Look what you might become! Look at the vastness above your ceiling!* And in doing so, it would have killed them. Their heavy, un-jointed bones would have cracked under their unmitigated weight; their vents would have dried and split in the fierce mountain gales; their large, plum-like eyes would have gone blind in the raw, unfiltered sun.

A clumsy god would have brought them metal. It would have taught them to smelt iron in the thermal vents, filling their clear, warm basin with slag and soot, turning their forty-year tasks of cable-braid into frantic decades of competition and war.

The wise Power knew that perfection is not a ladder to be climbed, but a state of equilibrium to be preserved. The city was not broken. It was a rare, low-entropy jewel—a place where life had made peace with its cradle.

Yet the Power saw what the city’s people could not see, for their eyes were turned inward, toward the masonry and the moss.

It saw that the mountain was young, and young rock is treacherous.

Three miles beneath the basin floor, a deep tectonic fault was slowly shifting. In less than twelve generations, a sheer basalt column holding up the southern wall of the sky-sea would fracture. The table of young rock would tilt. The vast, lukewarm basin would drain in a single catastrophic afternoon, pouring millions of tons of water down the jagged cliffs into the dry, burning lowlands below. The city would crumble. The kelp canopies would dry into black crust. The people, heavy and helpless, would drop to the bare rock and be crushed by their own unbuoyed flesh.

And beyond the fault, the thermal magma chamber that fed the vents was slowly cooling. The sulfur-sweet plumes would turn cold within a millennium, leaving the water icy, the eggs inert in their stone mortar-wedges, and the bronze-leafed sea-vines to rot in the dark.

This was the work for a benevolent power: not to change the song, but to ensure the floor under the dancers never fell away.

***

### III. The Quiet Work

The Power did not reveal itself. It took no shape. It left no footprint on the basalt flags. 

Instead, over the course of three centuries—a duration so gradual it felt to the city like nothing more than the natural settling of the world—it went to work beneath the stone.

1. **The Rooting of the Basin:** Deep within the mountain’s gut, where the rock was still red and molten, the Power wove vast, crystalline tendons of quartz and unbreakable granodiorite through the fault-lines. It welded the southern basalt shelf to the core of the continent. It braced the holding tank of the sky-sea so that no tremor, no quake, and no shift of the world’s crust could ever crack its lip or drain its waters. The sea would stay suspended, thousands of feet high, for as long as the planet spun.

2. **The Eternal Hearth:** The Power reached into the mantle below, gently redirecting a deep, perpetual thermal artery. It anchored the magma pocket beneath the city, ensuring that the sulfur-sweet vents would bubble at their exact, comfortable temperature—neither too hot to burn the amber eggs, nor ever cold enough to let the olive-bronze twilight freeze—for a million years to come.

3. **The Harmonic Gift:** Finally, the Power did one small thing within the city itself—something so subtle that even the longest-lived stone-cutter could not be sure it hadn't always been there.

It touched the lowest floor of the deepest basalt alley, where the unworked pillow-lavas emerged from the earth. There, it tuned the resonance of the thermal vents. 

The low-frequency hum—the engine idling at the bottom of the well—changed its pitch by a fraction of an octave. It became a perfectly stable, rich harmonic pulse that echoed through every stacked obsidian block, every reed-bridge, and every moss-lined alcove.

To the beings, the sound was not a voice; it was simply the voice of the home itself. But carried within that vibration was a subtle, comforting property: it reduced the brittle strain in their heavy bones; it rendered their amber eggs slightly more resilient against silt-rot; and it filled their long, silent periods of resting with a deep, dreamless ease that kept their minds free from panic or despair.

***

### IV. The Continuation

In the late afternoon of a day five hundred years later, two stone-cutters hung suspended from a fat, braided kelp-cable six stories above the street floor.

One of them—an elder whose matte skin was shaded in the dark bronze of aged leather—held a chisel of knapped obsidian. He was adjusting the fit of a slate roof-slab that had shifted a fraction of an inch over the last forty years. 

Beside him, a younger being hovered, adjusting a lead pebble on her weight strap. She tapped her chest hide, sending a faint ripple of pale, sulfur-yellow light across her chin—a question: *Is the cable holding?*

The elder did not answer with words. He reached out with his broad, five-toed foot and pressed his tough, calloused sole against the octagonal basalt face of the tower. He felt the heavy, vibrating pulse of the earth coming up through the stone—the warm, perpetual, low-frequency hum that filled his broad chest with a steady, quiet warmth.

He snapped his palate plates together—*clack-clack*. 

*It holds,* the sound said through the water. *It has always held. It will always hold.*

Above them, the brassy disc of the sun drifted behind its veil of greenish mist, lightless and weightless, utterly forgotten. Below them, in the warm, dark, supporting water, the city rested in its eternal twilight, perfectly balanced, completely safe, and wonderfully alone.
