A lesser god would have wept, and in weeping, brought ruin.

A foolish benevolence, arriving above the violet slit of the basalt shelf, would have looked down into the stifling, baked dark and felt the reflex of its own wet biology. It would have thought: *They are parched. They are trapped in a furnace. They have built a city for a life that never came.* It would have cracked the slag roof. It would have summoned cool, blue rain from the upper atmosphere to cascade down the terraced alleys.

And in doing so, it would have committed an absolute genocide.

The water would have struck the six-hundred-degree stone floor and instantly flashed into high-pressure steam, tearing the delicate, vitreous lace of the Chime-Weavers into flying shards of needle-glass. The sudden thermal shock would have split the dry, bimetallic joints of the Maintainers, snapping their slate limbs and shattering their iron-dust lattices into inert powder. The wetness would have flooded the square hearth-rooms, slaking the hot sulfur-fall into a cold, yellow sludge, extinguishing the dry gas breath of the rift forever. The resin-canes would have rotted; the Clickers would have seized into rusted, immobile burs of slag.

In three minutes of merciful rain, the great kinetic engine of the rift would have been reduced to a silent, muddy grave.

But the power that paused above the fissure was neither young nor foolish. It was ancient, cold-eyed, and vast—a consciousness whose thoughts moved with the glacial patience of shifting tectonic plates, and whose heart understood that life is not defined by water, but by *pattern*.

It did not descend with flashes of light or thunder. It shed its own mass until it was nothing more than a field of quiet, weightless attention, hovering in the thin violet slit above the slag roof, listening.

It listened for three hundred years.

To the Power, the valley was not a barren ditch; it was an exquisite, self-correctional symphony. It heard the deep, low-frequency hum of the basalt expanding under the thermal mantle—the bass drone of the earth. It felt the violent, rhythmic *crack* of the sulfur-canes splitting, the sharp *clack-clack-clack* of the Clickers springing across the tufa steps like dry seeds in a wind, and the heavy, grinding *thrum-slide* of the Maintainers dragging their hematite spades down the steep staircases. 

Above it all, it heard the Chime-Weavers. It perceived the delicate, complex harmonics that rippled through the glass webs as the hot gas roared upward. It saw how a single drop in thermal pressure changed the pitch of a glass thread; how that pitch shift altered the expansion rate of a Maintainer’s resin-joint fifty paces below; how that flexing limb nudged a stone block three millimeters to the left, perfectly bracing a terrace against a subterranean shear-stress that would not occur for another forty years.

It was not a city waiting for inhabitants. It was a creature whose body was made of streets, whose cells were made of stone and burs, and whose nervous system was made of wind and sound. It was entire. It was complete. It was utterly, beautifully un-lonely.

Yet, the Power possessed wisdom, and wisdom sees the subtle wear that time conceals.

The Power noticed the friction. 

It saw that over thousands of years, the fine, geometric breathing-holes of the tall cane-structures were slowly clogging with microscopic dross—not biological decay, but fused silica dust that dulled the tone of the escaping gas. It saw that the Chime-Weavers, exquisite as they were, were slowly losing their highest octaves; their glass threads, scorched by millions of cycles of superheated air, were micro-fracturing, darkening, and thickening. The song was growing heavy. In ten thousand years, the glass would be too thick to chime. The Maintainers would miss their acoustic cues. They would flex out of time with the earth’s heat. The walls would slip. The slag roof would drop, and the engine would grind itself to dust.

The Power did not want to change the song. It wanted the song to endure.

So, the Power acted—not with a miracle, but with a touch so faint it left no shadow.

It did not alter the physics of the valley. It did not grant the Maintainers thought, nor the Clickers feeling. To give them minds would be to introduce doubt, hesitation, and error into a system that ran on pure, unblemished geometry.

Instead, the Power reached into the deep, radiant mantle beneath the basalt floor—five miles down, where the deep iron boiled in darkness—and laid its hand upon the thermal plume. 

It tuned the heat. 

It introduced a minute, perpetual variation into the subterranean radio-decay—a tiny, breathing pulse that surged every three hundred hours. Not enough to disrupt the dry flora, but just enough to create a rhythmic spike in the gas vents.

When this new, sharp surge of heat rose through the floor, it hit the resin-canes. The intense, brief flash of heat did not burn them; it caused their secret, silicious resins to vaporize for a fraction of a second, scouring the breathing-holes clean of accumulated dross before settling back into a mirror-polished glaze. 

The air blew clean again. The tone cleared.

Then, the Power turned its attention upward, to the dark, slag ceiling where the Chime-Weavers hung in the roaring drafts. 

It did not replace their glass. Instead, from its own vast, immaterial essence, it shed a single, microscopic dusting of sub-atomic order—a mineral grace—that drifted down through the purple dusk like invisible gold dust. The dust settled onto the vitreous lace of the Weavers. 

Where the glass was micro-fractured, the hot, mineral-laden gas passing through the webs caught the dust, using it as a catalyst. The glass began to self-anneal. Under the intense, dry heat, the micro-cracks fused shut. The lace thinned, stretched, and turned perfectly clear, like spun diamond.

High in the dark, the Chime-Weavers began to sing again—not a new song, but the *old* song, restored to a pitch of terrifying, crystalline clarity.

Down in the red dusk of the plaza, a Maintainer was halfway up a long shale staircase. Its iron-dust shoulder was braced against a thousand-pound lintel, its slate plates groaning under the press of the wall. 

The new note struck the stone. 

The sound vibrated through the basalt, through the Maintainer's hematite feet, and into its bimetallic core. The precise frequency matched the exact resonant pitch of its dry amber joints. The internal friction vanished; the joint flexed smoothly, silently, with three percent more efficiency than it had possessed a minute before. 

The Maintainer did not pause to wonder. It had no mind to wonder with. It simply absorbed the frequency, adjusted its torque, and pressed its heavy, chisel-like scoop into the tufa block, carving a groove that was straight, true, and enduring.

Nearby, a cluster of Clickers snapped off the steps in a sudden, violent cloud, their slag-and-quartz bodies ringing like handfuls of dropped coins as they swarmed a fresh sulfur-fall, moving with crisp, renewed precision.

Satisfied, the Power began to withdraw.

It did not leave a shrine. It did not carve its name into the basalt, nor did it leave a tablet demanding worship from things that had no knees to kneel with. It understood that the highest form of love for a machine is to let it run, flawless and unburdened, in the dark for which it was made.

The Power pulled its attention back up through the violet slit, rising into the cold, empty sky above the basalt plateau, leaving the rift behind.

Below, in the hot, dry dusk, the dark engine turned. 

The basalt wall groaned its deep bass note. The resin-canes burst with sharp, violent *cracks*, scattering mustard-yellow spores over clean stone steps. The Clickers rattled; the Maintainers creaked and stepped; and high on the slag roof, suspended above the empty, perfect city, the glass webs sang their endless, roaring chime into the sulfur-scented heat—running, building, keeping time, forever.
