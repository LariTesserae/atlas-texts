The Power did not descend as a sudden radiance, for sudden radiance would have scorched the pearly, cataract-clouded eyes of the One-Eyes and cast terrifying, unaccustomed shadows across the wet tiles. 

It did not break open the ceiling, for the sky above was a cold, chaotic emptiness of howling winds and boundless, uncontained space—an abyss that would have shattered the minds of a people whose very sanity was anchored by the low, warm, curving ribs of baked red clay.

It did not grant them speech, for words are light, fragile things that drift away and die in the damp air, whereas the three sharp strikes of a wooden mallet on a mortar joint—*thack, thack, thack*—travel forever through the continuous ceramic bones of the world, keeping everyone safe, keeping everyone known.

The Power was wise, and so it understood that benevolence is not the tearing down of a prison if the prison has become a sanctuary; it is not the changing of a shape, but the perfect, quiet honoring of it.

It paused above the three iron-grated lanterns, hovering in the cold, wet soil of the outer grade, looking down through the slanting bands of butter-yellow light. It listened to the heavy, reverberating roar: the *clack-clack-clack* of the copper paddles, the dry *fwt-fwt-fwt* of linen-white wings, the *whish-whish* of crimson gills, and the deep, papery hum of a million clicker-beetles in their dark urns. It saw the short, tallow-skinned people with their broad, suction-padded feet, moving with quiet, unhurried grace through the steam, their bone needles flashing as they tended the seams of their world.

The Power loved them. It loved them not with pity—for there was no misery here, only the fierce, exquisite dignity of a total, closed-loop harmony—but with the quiet awe one feels before a wild orchid growing in a dark cave, or a clock that has kept time for five hundred years without a single winding.

And so, the Power decided what it would do.

It would not interfere. But it would *sustain*.

***

First, the Power reached out its vast, invisible hands through the dense, cold earth surrounding the parish-church-sized hollow. It wrapped itself around the exterior of the Kiln-Vaults like a second skin of warm, absorbent velvet.

Outside, in the great world above, the centuries would bring shaking mountains, biting frosts, shifts in subterranean aquifers, and the heavy, crushing weight of settling strata. But inside the envelope of the Power’s attention, no earthquake would ever shear a pillar. No deep frost would ever penetrate the outer brickwork to freeze the water in the terracotta gutters. The iron grates of the three sky-lanterns would never rust through, nor would they ever be choked by falling autumn leaves or drifting mud from the upper world. The yellow light-shafts would fall forever at the exact angle needed to feed the dark-leaved garlic and the bitter cress.

Next, the Power leaned closer, sending a gentle, imperceptible intent down through the steam and dust, touching the quiet biology of the Sump.

It did not alter the creatures; it simply smoothed the rough edges of their mortality.

To the *Lard-Sows* in their dark, warm cubbies, it granted a subtle, endless renewal of their deep thermal core. They would never grow cold, nor would their translucent, lard-white skin ever suffer the rot of stagnation. Their thick, viscous sow-butter became slightly richer, slightly sweeter, healing the cracked skin of the human scrapers in half the time.

To the *One-Eyes*, it touched the clouded, pearly cataracts of their upper eyes. It did not restore their vision—for vision of the sun would only blind them to the shadows—but it turned the inflammation into a cool, painless stone, like a polished pearl set into the skull, shielding them forever from the sting of the light-shafts while their lower, glossy-black eyes tracked the mortar-mites with flawless precision.

To the *Oil-Clickers* in their hanging urns, it gave a silent blessing of endless fertility and quiet contentment, ensuring that the papery, drumhead roar of their carapaces would never falter, and that the clear, amber oil would spill into the ceramic funnels generation after generation, lighting the deep places under the stair-treads where no sun could go.

***

Finally, the Power looked upon the *Keepers*.

It saw a pregnant woman hanging by one arm from an ash-pole gantry, her wide, archless foot adhering to the damp wood with a soft, wet grip, while her other hand used a bone needle to repair a split in a wicker cradle. It saw an old man on the lower floor, his skin stained with iron dust, carefully scraping a spalling brick from the base of a vault-pillar, preparing his own eventual resting place.

The Power did not give them dreams of the sky. To give them dreams of the sky would be a cruelty, a poison of longing for a world they could never walk upon. 

Instead, it gave them the **Grace of the Unbroken Circle**.

It settled into the mortar joints. It crept into the bone-flour ground by the heavy stone troughs on the floor. It mixed itself into the slaked lime and the brick-dust.

From that day forward, when a Keeper died, and his kin stripped his fat for winch-grease, carved his long-bones into spatulas, and crushed his skull to seal a failing arch, something miraculous occurred—not visible to the eye, but felt in the very marrow of the place.

The mortar holding the Grand Sump together grew impossibly strong. The dead did not merely hold up the walls; they *remembered* them. The thoughts of the dead—the rhythm of the splash-wheels, the warmth of the salamander-hides, the smell of fresh bread and rendered lard—soaked into the red clay bricks, vibrating at the exact frequency of the vault’s heavy roar.

When a young Keeper stood on a wet tread high above the floor and struck the brickwork three times—*thack, thack, thack*—he was not just signaling a worker on the floor. He was speaking through his grandfather, his great-grandmother, and the dead bricklayers who had laid the first courses centuries before. The entire structure became a single, reverberating instrument of living memory.

And in the swinging wicker cradles high in the ash scaffolding, the infants, bound in pigskin to their mothers' aprons, slept sweeter than before. They breathed the vinegar-damp, yeasty air, lulled by the deafening, eternal *clack-clack-clack* of the copper paddles, knowing—without ever having the words to say it—that the sky was red, the earth was wet, the wall was their family, and the world was entirely, perfectly whole.

Up above, in the vast, cold emptiness of the universe, the Benevolent Power folded its arms around the little box of baked clay, settled into the deep earth, and kept watch. 

It changed nothing. It saved everything.
