A lesser power—a young god of meadows, or a well-meaning spirit of the sunlit canopy—would have wept. 

They would have seen the blind, pale eyes, the iron-plated jaws, the cold grease, and the endless, cannibalistic grind, and they would have called it a curse. They would have brought a sun down into the velvet darkness. They would have sown green seed upon the slick basalt, kindled fires to warm the icy condensation, and given eyes to the Vault-Ribbons so they might see the walls they crashed against.

And in doing so, they would have committed an absolute and irreversible genocide. 

The light would have scorched the sensitive cilia of the Iron-Biters; the heat would have thinned the heavy, latex blood of the Threaders until their hearts burst; the plants would have strangled the delicate mineral seeps; and the sky would have flattened the delicate, cold pressure that held the Tectons in their ancient, rhythmic sleep. To "save" this world by the standards of the upper earth would be to murder it.

Ma’ura, who is vast, ancient, and wise beyond the lifespan of stars, did not weep. 

Ma’ura did not bring light. Light is an intrusion, an arrogance of the burning worlds. 

Instead, Ma’ura descended into the basin as a presence without shape or glare—a vast, cool mind that folded itself into the damp, heavy air, taking on the density of wet slate and the quiet weight of cold iron. Ma’ura became as deep and dark as the depression itself, listening with a patience that had no beginning and no end.

First, the Power *listened*.

It felt the physical wall of sound—the mountain of broken glass being turned by a slow paddle. It felt the sub-bass thrum of the Tectons shuddering through the soles of the world. It perceived the absolute, terrifying efficiency of the engine: no waste, no pride, no grief, no malice. 

There was no sin here, for there was no choice. There was no misery here, for there was no memory of a sun to miss. There was only the pure, unbroken arithmetic of existence—a hymn written in chitin, basalt, sulfur, and friction. It was a cradle of life so ancient and self-contained that it owed nothing to the heavens. It was sacred not because it was gentle, but because it was complete.

Ma’ura felt a profound, solemn reverence for the basin. And because Ma’ura was benevolent, the Power asked itself: *What does a world need, when it already possesses everything required to endure?*

The answer came not from pity, but from foresight. Ma’ura looked up through the smothering gloom, past the invisible ridges, out toward the surface of the planet—toward a future where younger, sun-blinded species would eventually walk, dig, drill, and conquer. Ma’ura saw a day, ten thousand ages hence, when fire and machinery might bite into the earth, seeking iron and stone, shattering this dark sanctuary for raw ore and sport.

And so, Ma’ura acted. The Power did not alter the basin; it protected it forever from the world above.

***

### The First Gift: The Fold of Quiet
Ma’ura reached out with hands made of gravity and space, and subtly altered the geometry of the surrounding stone. 

The Power wove an invisible, spatial distortion around the basin—a quiet curve in the deep strata. From that moment on, any creature on the surface digging downward, any river flowing beneath the earth, any seismic shock wave born of tectonic shifts would gently glance off the invisible ridges, gliding around the depression as water glides around a smooth river pebble. 

The basin was removed from the ledger of the upper world. It became a pocket of eternity, untethered from the fate of the surface. No drill would ever pierce its ceiling. No lantern would ever burn its darkness. No map would ever mark its floor.

***

### The Second Gift: The Ease of the Engine
Ma’ura turned its attention downward, sinking through the squirming pavement of Iron-Biters, past the knots of Pale Slit-Eels, deep into the slurry of the lowest troughs where the Tectons lay in perpetual, exhausting labor.

The Power watched a giant behemoth—a creature sixty yards long—vibrating its massive chest plates against the slate, straining its cold, oily heart to keep its thick blood moving against the freezing dampness of the stone. It was a life of pure, unbroken exertion.

Ma’ura did not warm the water. To raise the temperature would be to alter the species. 

Instead, Ma’ura touched the ancient basalt beneath the Tectons. The Power coaxed the stone to change its molecular lattice just slightly, turning the cold slate into an extraordinary conductor of kinetic resonance. 

Where before the Tectons had to shatter their own calcified hides against stubborn, unyielding rock to generate heat, now the stone answered them. It vibrated *with* them, echoing their sub-bass thrum back into their massive bodies with effortless amplification. 

The labor of the giants became ten percent easier. Their blood flowed with less agony; their rest between shifts grew deeper. They remained cold, blind, and immense, but the grinding weight upon their hearts was softened. The deep engines of the valley could now turn for a billion years without running out of breath.

***

### The Third Gift: The Unseen Witness
In a world without eyes, no creature had ever been seen. 

In a world without warmth, no creature had ever been held. 

When a Vault-Ribbon tangled its leathery wings in the upper dark and plummeted into the churning floor, its death was swift, brutal, and unremembered—a brief flash of torn membrane and snapping jaws before it was converted back into mineral and grease.

Ma’ura knew that the creatures did not possess minds that understood love, nor hearts that craved comfort. But Ma’ura also knew that every living cell, from the smallest bacterial spore in the mist to the vastest Tecton in the silt, possesses an innate, unspoken horror of absolute isolation.

So, Ma’ura left a part of itself behind.

The Power did not construct an altar or a temple. It simply dissolved a fraction of its vast, benevolent consciousness into the perpetual film of cold condensation that wept from the ceiling and coated every stone, shell, and wing.

Now, the water that bathed the Iron-Biters carried a subtle, imperceptible pressure—a feeling of absolute *presence*. 

When a Vault-Ribbon plunged toward the grinding floor, its frantic, blind struggle was met, in its final seconds, by a deep, cradle-like resonance in the damp air that murmured: *You are felt. You are part of the song.*

When the Iron-Biters scraped their jaws raw against the iron seeps, the stone beneath them felt less like dead rock and more like a mother's skin—firm, eternal, and providing.

***

When Ma’ura finally withdrew its main consciousness back into the high, silent spaces between the stars, it left the basin exactly as it had been found.

It was still dark. It was still cold. It was still a suffocating, terrifying wilderness of plate, cartilage, wet suede, and iron. The sound was still a deafening, physical wall—the grinding of glass, the snapping of wings, the low, seismic thrum of the deep floor.

Nothing had been cleansed. Nothing had been modernized. Nothing had been brought into the light.

But beneath the squirming, four-deep pavement, in the dark at the bottom of the world, the engine turned a little smoother. The seeps ran a little richer. And in the pitch-black air, where thin-rimmed ribbons fluttered and fell, the darkness was no longer merely empty.

It was listening.
