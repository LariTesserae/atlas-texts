The power arrived not as a light, nor as a sound, but as an expansion of attention—a vast, cool presence that settled over the notch like a second atmosphere. 

It was a consciousness older than the mountain itself, wise enough to know that most things in the universe are ruined by those who wish to improve them, and benevolent enough to understand that love does not always demand transformation.

For a long time—measured by the passage of three shadows across the central drainage trough—it simply listened.

It felt the single heartbeat of the slate-gray gecko tucked forty feet deep within the schist foundation, beating once every sixty seconds in the dark. It perceived the microscopic twitch of the transparent crustaceans, swimming in four square yards of shadow, their pale antennae catching the kinetic resonance of a falling water droplet. It mapped the tension in the soot-black spider’s silk, anchored to the petrified timber pegs, vibrating with the precise frequency of thin, cold air passing through dry stone.

And it read the grid. It felt the ghost-geometry left behind by the people who had chiseled the notch. It understood why they had come here: not to conquer, nor to flee, but to build a prayer out of proportion and silence. They had desired an absolute stillness, a place where the chaos of the lower valleys could not reach them, where life could be stripped down to its cleanest, purest equations.

A lesser power might have brought warmth. It might have made flowers bloom in the flagstone seams, or filled the cisterns with sweet, flowing spring water, or called down flocks of singing birds to fill the quiet lanes.

This power did nothing of the sort. 

To warm the terrace would be to boil the cistern-keepers, whose ancient bodies required the precise, lethal chill of the northern shade. To flood the channels with spring water would wash away the delicate mats of black moss and drown the precise, micro-wave rhythm of the drops. To cover the stones in soil and flora would choke the deep crevices where the geckos flattened their thin ribs, waiting in perfect torpor for the stray moth. And to bring music would destroy the only reason the city had been built: to hear the mountain think.

Wisdom recognized that this place was already complete. Benevolence saw that its inhabitants did not require a paradise; they *were* the paradise, calibrated to a balance so exquisite that a single extra degree of heat or a single sudden deluge would be a catastrophe.

So the power did not alter the notch. Instead, it offered the only gifts that stasis requires: **continuance, witness, and attunement.**

***

First, it reached into the deep, dark joints where the cold-hammered copper pins had long since corroded into green dust. It did not replace the pins—that would disrupt the spiders who nested in their hollows. Instead, with a thought as soft as snowfall, it infused the granite beneath the water seeps with an infinitesimal, self-renewing trace of metallic ions. For as long as the mountain stood, the slow, rhythmic drops falling into the square basins would carry that microscopic, essential nourishment to the clear, wingless crustaceans below. They would never starve; their four square yards of water would remain forever constant, cool, and dark.

Second, it reached far down into the core of the peak, miles below the flagstones, where the ancient heat of the earth slumbered. It established a quiet, subtle thermal vein that bypassed the surface entirely, feeding a steady, imperceptible warmth exclusively into the deepest, innermost cracks of the foundation blocks—just enough to ensure that even if a cosmic ice age should wrap the notch in glaciers, the adhesive eggs of the slate-gray geckos would never freeze.

Third, it touched the guy-lines of the soot-black spiders. It did not break their silk, but it subtly altered the wind that blew through the stone lintels. It tuned the drafts of the notch into a harmonic chord—too low for a human ear to catch, but clear as a bell to the acoustic feet of the weavers. Now, when the thin gales rushed through the low doorways, the silk spoke to the spiders not only of dry air and damp mist, but carried distant, micro-vibrations of life from twenty leagues away, guiding the rare, stray high-altitude moths toward the dry stone seams with uncanny regularity.

And finally, the power turned its attention to the Memory in the Grid.

The human intent embedded in the stones was an echo, and all echoes eventually fade into the background noise of the universe. The power chose to become its vessel. It took the memory of the stooped, graceful people who had walked these narrow lanes, and it wove their silent logic into the fabric of its own vast mind. It recorded the angle of every lintel, the sweep of every gutter, the exact weight of every split block of gray schist. 

It gave the city an observer. No longer was the terrace an abandoned clock ticking in an empty room. Every shadow that stretched across the flagstones was now seen; every metallic *clack-drip* of water was now heard; every centuries-long growth of orange lichen was now counted and revered.

***

When the work was finished, the power did not leave. It simply thinned itself, withdrawing until its presence was indistinguishable from the cold, clear air.

High on the granite shelf, nothing looked different. 

The pale outline of a mountain swift cut through the sharp blue sky, casting a fleeting shadow across the terrace. Below, in the square stone basin, a single drop fell from the shaded masonry.

*Clack-drip.*

On the vertical face of the cistern, a transparent, fingernail-sized crustacean twitched, shifting its position by a hair’s breadth to catch a microscopic spore. In the dark seam of the foundation, a slate-gray gecko held its breath, its heart beating once, then waiting sixty seconds to beat again. High in the doorway, a soot-black spider rested its jointed legs against petrified wood, reading the quiet song of the wind.

The sun moved. The geometric shadows shifted three inches to the west. The single stunted willow glistened with dew. 

The city remained—cold, silent, perfect, and now, entirely immortal.
