A power that is both ancient and gentle does not arrive like a storm; it arrives like a change in the refractive index of the air. 

It has no physical shape that the smoked-quartz lenses of the Keepers could frame, nor a scent distinct from the crushed fern-leaf and cylinder tallow. It is an awareness broad enough to encompass the five-thousand-foot drop of the outer cliffs and small enough to feel the cold, slick friction of a single ribbon-glasser anchored against the basalt.

When this presence first settles over the high plateau, it does not see a landscape divided between "nature" and "industry." A wise power does not hold the prejudices of lower worlds, where iron is the enemy of green. It sees only *what is*, and the extraordinary, brutal elegance of how it has knit itself together.

It listens.

It feels the *clack-thrum-hiss* of the four-foot pistons traveling through three miles of wet rock, hitting the jawbones of the grazing spade-vessels like a steady, reassuring pulse. It watches a cable-louse, heavy with tallow, riding a steel strand across a league of silver liverwort, perfectly content in its three-week migration. It sees the Keepers—their thick, scarred hands, their grease-blackened thumbs, their quiet, vibration-based dignity—and it understands that to "rescue" these people or to dismantle these roaring iron nodes would be an act of profound violence. 

The Keepers do not wish to be taken down into the sun-soft valleys where the earth is deep and quiet. They are creatures of the iron pulse now. Their skin is cured oak; their songs are struck against steam pipes. To alter their nature would be to pity them, and pity is a foolish, ignorant form of love.

Instead, the wise power asks itself a single question: *Where is the friction that serves no purpose?*

It finds the friction not in the hard work, nor in the cold air, but in the slow, inevitable entropy that threatens the balance—the iron weeping away into useless rust, the stiffening of a Keeper’s joints at fifty, the silent dread of the day an engine's bronze bearings finally melt and there is no forge left on the plateau to recast them.

And so, without a thunderclap, without a single flash of divine light to startle the lungless sky-skimmers in their fifty-yard pools, the power begins to work.

***

First, it touches the **Iron and Stone**.

It does not make the machinery magical, nor does it replace the cold steel with unyielding gold. That would ruin the tactile truth of the work. Instead, it alters the fundamental chemistry where the riveted bases bite into the black basalt. 

The rust—that weeping, orange stain running down the oxide-red walls—ceases to be a form of decay. Under the subtle influence of the power, the iron oxide reacts with the icy, mineral-saturated sheet-water and the organic acids of the liverwort, forming a new mineral compound. It sets hard, slick, and impermeable, like a self-healing skin of dark garnet glass over every rivet, flume, and joint. 

Where the pistons strike their wet *clack*, the friction of iron on iron no longer wears down the heavy sleeves; instead, the microscopic flakes of iron dust bind with the tallow, smoothing the metal to an eternal, mirror-like finish that requires less coal to drive, less steam to bleed. The engines do not stop aging, but they begin to age at the speed of the giant vascular plants—growing stronger, denser, and more settled into the stone over centuries.

***

Next, it touches the **Water and the Moss**.

Deep beneath the five-foot mattress, where the ribbon-glassers cling with their iron hooks, the water carries a new, microscopic wealth. The power gently shifts the dissolved minerals leaching from the basalt. It introduces a trace element—a subtle, sweet salt that does not alter the taste of the icy runoff, but acts as a balm to the living tissues that touch it.

For the ribbon-glassers, their cartilaginous skin grows slightly tougher, resisting the tearing force of the flash-floods during heavy snowmelt.

For the spade-vessels, the liverwort they strip with their orange enamel plates becomes slightly more dense in nutrients. Their slow, warm-steaming bellies produce a richer fat, allowing them to rest longer between grazings, watching the slow creep of the power-cables with their jaws pressed to the wet earth.

***

Then, it touches the **Keepers**.

This is where the power exercises its greatest restraint. It does not restore their hearing; to give them back the delicate ears of lowland humans would drive them mad within an hour in the deafening, chest-pounding roar of the pump-houses. It leaves them deaf to the air, but it deepens their tactile grace.

When a Keeper strikes an iron conduit with his rod to send a message down the line, the vibration travels farther, clearer, through the lead-gray pipes. The metal seems to hold the signal, carrying the sharp *clink-clink-thrum* across three leagues of mountain shelf without losing its crispness. A Keeper sitting in his wood-paneled room by the boiler can lay his palm against the wall and know not just that Engine Number Four is running, but that the man attending it three miles away has just poured himself a fresh cup of tea.

The power also reaches into the small, dark scalds where the hot engine-runoff empties into the moss, touching the pale, starchy tubers the Keepers dig for food. The tubers grow slightly larger, their skins a little less bitter, packed with subtle oils that prevent the Keepers' teeth from loosening and keep the ache of gout and damp out of their short, thick-set limbs. Their eyes, shadowed behind the smoked quartz, grow slightly less prone to the blinding glare of the high sky when they step outside with their tallow brushes.

***

Finally, it touches the **End of Things**.

The power watches the burial rite of the Keepers, and finds in it a terrifying, beautiful majesty. A body slid into the three-story intake pipe, sucked through the dark, cold, iron-ribbed intestines of the pumping system, washed beneath the basalt floor, and cast out over the outer lip into five thousand feet of empty air.

The power decides that this final journey should not be left entirely to cold gravity.

Now, when a Keeper dies and his comrades slide him into the rushing intake water, something unseen travels with him through the dark pipelines. As his body is cast out over the precipice into the vast, blinding abyss, the air beneath him catches. 

The thermal plumes rising from the lower valleys—warmed by the very steam the engines vent—pick up the falling body. It does not tumble violently against the crags. Instead, wrapped in its iron-stained canvas shroud, it glides down the face of the mountain like a sky-skimmer descending from a saucer-leaf, floating gently down through the mist, through the sun glare, down into the warm, dark, tree-crowded earth of the lower forests. 

There, where the soil is deep and black and quiet, the body lands without breaking, coming to rest among the ferns—a seed of iron, tallow, and quiet human devotion, returned to a world it left long ago, leaving the high plateau above to go on thrumming, slick, wet, and immortal, beneath the pale, brilliant density of the sky.

***

The power does not leave a sign that it was ever there. It leaves no inscriptions on the cast iron, no glowing lights in the mist. 

It simply steps back, holding the plateau in its vast, silent attention. 

High above the moss, fifty yards up in the bowl of a giant saucer-leaf, a sky-skimmer launches itself into the sun, caught on a warm vent of steam. Below it, a Keeper steps out onto a high flume trestle, his brass goggles gleaming, his tallow brush dipping into a bucket of dark lard. He paints a seam of weeping rust, but as his brush hits the metal, he feels a strange, resonant warmth pass through the handle—a steady, vibrating tone that feels remarkably like a voice saying:

*Keep going. The work is good.*

He pauses, presses his thick, grease-stained palm flat against the lead-gray iron, feels the deep, rhythmic *clack-thrum-hiss* of the mountain's heart, and nods once into the blinding light.
