The Power came from the old worlds. 

It was an awareness heavy with history, accustomed to places where mountains had already sloughed their shoulders into dust, where cities rested on seven layers of their own forgotten dead, and where every river ran thick with the silt of memory. It was used to prayers—the sharp, desperate, high-frequency cries of short-lived creatures begging for fire, for sun, for rain, for the postponement of the rot that waits in all soft things.

When its gaze fell upon the high notch, it halted. 

The Power did not land; it had no mass to strike the razor-edged scree. It simply expanded through the cold, spray-laden air, filling the basin like a sudden drop in barometric pressure.

It felt the sharpness of the granite. To an entity that had watched continents turn to sand, the sheer, un-eroded arrogance of these basalt columns was astounding. There were no ghosts here. The stone held no impressions of past suffering or ancient triumph. It was a place without a past, utterly consumed by the physical weight of its own *now*.

The Power observed the inhabitants. 

It watched a woman—tall, grey-skinned, her wide, damp feet gripping a slate ledge slick with glacier-weep—hammering a cold-beaten copper pin into a joint of green cedar. She did not hurry. Her heart beat with a slow, heavy rhythm, like water dropping into a deep cistern. She did not look up at the sky. She had no concept of the sky as a home for gods; the sky was merely where the wind came from before it struck the hollow tiers to make the low, whistling tone she used to measure the passage of the afternoon.

The Power listened to her throat. It heard the sub-vocal rumble pass down through her ribs, into her legs, and out through the slate to a man three terraces down, who shifted his weight and set a fresh basalt block into place without a word.

An unwise god would have pitied them. 

An unwise god would have seen their bare feet on the sharp stone and given them leather shoes; would have seen their cold, dark vaults and brought them fire; would have heard their silent, sub-vocal hums and given them songs, poetry, longing, and the agonizing gift of ambition. An unwise god would have sought to "uplift" them, turning their slow, perfect maintenance into a frantic climb toward some distant, imagined zenith.

But this Power was wise. It recognized that to give fire to those who do not shiver is to scorch them. To give words to those who speak through stone is to deafen them. To give a future to a people who exist in a perpetual, pristine beginning is to murder the present.

The Power realized that these were not broken things waiting to be made whole. They were a rare, unblemished equation, balanced perfectly against the cold iron, the wet moss, and the endless thunder of the slot canyon below.

So, the Power did what only the truly benevolent can do: it refrained.

It did not break the silence. It did not send a voice from the clouds or cause the aqueducts to run with wine. Instead, it leaned into the mountain, sliding its vast, quiet consciousness down through the structural fissures, deep into the un-eroded roots of the basin.

There, far beneath the drainage grates, where the glacial torrent roared in the dark, the Power performed three small, invisible acts:

**First, it anchor-bound the ice.** It subtly altered the deep thermodynamic currents of the upper peak. The ice-seams that fed the aqueducts would never vanish in an unexpected warm age, nor would they freeze solid in a deep winter. The weeping of the rock face would remain true—a steady, eternal dripping, precise to the drop, ensuring the log flumes would never run dry nor overflow their copper bounds.

**Second, it laid a veil of indifference over the notch.** Down in the lower valleys, where the world was old and soft and full of hungry empires, men were beginning to forge steel and dream of conquest. The Power drew a slight, perpetual bend into the light and wind above the mountain’s edge. To anyone looking up from the lowlands, the high notch would appear only as an impenetrable cliff of barren grey shadow—a wall of sheer rock unworthy of an army’s breath. The world below would age, decay, burn, and rebuild, but it would never find the path up the razor-scree.

**Third, and finally, it offered a single gesture of communion.** 

The Power did not speak, but it matched its own vast, ancient frequency to the sub-vocal hum of the mountain. For a fleeting second, it vibrated in harmony with the roar of the slot canyon, the whistle of the wind through the four-story window apertures, and the cold strike of copper pins against basalt.

High on the third terrace, the woman holding the copper pin stopped her hammer. 

She did not feel a divine presence. She did not drop to her knees. But through the soles of her feet, she felt the mountain respond. It felt deeper than usual—as though the granite beneath her went down forever, resting on something impossibly dense, calm, and eternal, something that agreed entirely with the setting of straight stone blocks.

A small, clear stream-lizard shifted on the moss beside her ankle, its glass-skin catching the bright alpine glare. High above, a slate-hawk dropped from an unglazed balcony into the updraft, its interlocking feathers shedding a spray of cold water as it banked into the notch.

The woman leaned her weight against the cedar beam, felt the true, perfect alignment of the wood against the rock, and brought the hammer down. 

*Clang.*

The Power withdrew, slipping quietly back into the deep spaces between the stars, leaving the high basin exactly as it had found it: sharp, bright, loud with the voice of water, and beautifully, perpetually unbegun.
