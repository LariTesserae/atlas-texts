The Power came down not as a light—for the void was already choked with light—nor as a voice, for a voice in that ringing glassiness would have been an intrusion, an ugly violence against seventy miles of sacred quiet.

It arrived as an awareness. A vast, cool attention that slid through the continental roof-crifts, unspooling through the miles of dark stone above, until it settled into the freezing, shadowless air of the abyss.

It saw the world below. It saw the five-hundred-foot columns of ivory cellulose, straight and taut as harp strings. It saw the house-sized droplets of frost-sweat shattering on the leaf-trusses with the sound of struck anvil-iron. It saw the Gale-Kites, blind and cork-light, soaring on the squalls of displaced air. And it saw the Knot-Walkers—the long-limbed, milk-skinned human sparks clinging to the crotches of the giants, their eyes hidden behind bone slits, their thumbs calloused like horn, their hearths burning blue in rings of ancient, stolen stone.

A lesser power, moved by ignorant pity, would have sought to destroy the cold. It would have sent down a warm sun, unsealed the roof, softened the iron-hard leaves into lush, green velvet, and flooded the void with thick, damp air.

But the Power was wise, and wisdom knows that to alter a world's nature is to murder the things born of it.

Had the air grown warm, the calcified cellulose would have rotted. The five-hundred-foot pillars would have softened, lost their tensile strength, and collapsed under their own oceanic weight, crushing the camps in a deluge of decaying pulp. Had the light been softened into dusk, the Gale-Kites’ pressure-fans would have grown dull, and the Knot-Walkers’ silver-grey eyes—magnificently forged by forty generations of glare—would have seen only useless fog. To sweep away the razor-sharp flints of the floor would be to starve the Vein-Cutters, who needed those flint edges to shape their obsidian and reap the pith-marrow.

The Power understood: *These people do not suffer their world. They are its voice. They are the only part of this severe, magnificent machine that knows it is magnificent.*

So the Power did not break the machine. It tuned it.

It looked closely at the friction points—the places where the world’s severity ceased to be a forge for human dignity and became merely a senseless, wasteful tragedy. It saw three things.

First, it saw the **Listen-Keepers**. It saw the elders, blind and frost-bitten, pressing their bare ribs against the shrieking bark, straining through the chaos of micro-vibrations to hear the single, subtle tear that presaged a catastrophic growth-surge. It saw how often the warning came too late—not from a failure of skill, but because the wood’s internal acoustics were chaotic, filled with static and false harmonics. It saw whole camps swept into the abyss by a spire that ruptured without a clean acoustic signature.

Second, it saw the **Hearths**. The ancient basalt stones brought down from the roof in forgotten epochs were wearing away. They were chipped, ground to dust by centuries of heat and movement. Without stone to ring the fires, the blue pith-flames would char the cellulose floors, setting off localized vascular panics in the plant-body, or simply dying out in the draft, leaving families to freeze in their suspended bast-pods.

Third, it saw the **Tears**. The oil produced by the Knot-Walkers' tear ducts was thick, but the dry, floating ice-dust was relentless. By their thirtieth year, most Knot-Walkers suffered from constant, agonizing corneal scarring—a silent, gnawing pain that made even the quietest evening in the pod a trial of endurance.

The Power did not speak. It did not reveal itself in a burst of fire. It simply reached into the deep, subterranean hydraulics of the world—into the dark, high-pressure pumps five miles below the plant-substrate—and made three minute, permanent adjustments.

***

The first shift happened during the shift of the third watch.

On a high strut overlooking a ten-mile drop, an old Listen-Keeper named Vael sat strapped to a primary column. Her blind, silver-grey eyes were closed; her chest was bare, pressed against the frozen, whalebone-hard bark. For hours, she had been drowning in the usual static: the dull, wet thrum of deep sap, the random snapping of surface-hairs, the thermal groaning of the upper arches. It was like trying to hear a single reed-flute in a gale.

Then, the wood beneath her ribs changed.

It did not grow softer. It did not grow warmer. But the internal grain of the calcified cellulose—the microscopic, crystalline tubes that ran from the abyssal roots to the glowing roof—aligned. They swept into a perfect, uniform acoustic geometry.

Vael gasped. The static vanished.

Suddenly, the column was no longer a wall of random noise; it was an instrument of absolute, bell-like clarity. She could hear the sap rising six miles below, not as a muddy roar, but as a clean, rhythmic pulse—a low *C-note* that resonated in her sternum. And three miles to the west, where a structural leaf-truss was beginning to fatigue under the weight of a hanging frost-sweat drop, she did not hear a chaotic crackle. She heard a distinct, high-pitched *ping*—a warning so clear, so precise in its location and timing, that she knew to the minute when the truss would shear.

She did not know why the wood had begun to sing. She only knew that the wood was now honest. She struck her hollowed pith-cylinder three times—a sharp, confident signal—and the camp moved with effortless, unhurried grace hours before the floor buckled.

***

The second shift occurred deep in the concavities *underneath* the great structural leaves—the pitch-black spaces where the Frost-Moss grew in the shadows of the water-mirrors.

A young Vein-Cutter named Keth was crawling through the dark, his obsidian blade held between his teeth, searching for the velvet-dark fungus to feed his pod. His hand brushed against something lying in the slush of a dry concavity.

It was not wood. It was heavy, cold, and smooth.

He brought his bone-slit goggles close. In the faint, blue bioluminescence of the moss, he saw a nodule the size of a man’s skull. It was dark, dense, and fine-grained—a piece of subterranean slate-basalt, brought up from the mantle by the high-pressure hydraulic surges, carried through the vascular channels, and deposited gently in the shadow-pockets like a seed.

He touched it with the tip of his obsidian blade. It did not scratch. He laid his palm against it; it absorbed the faint heat of his flesh and held it, radiating a deep, slow warmth.

He did not shout—loud speech was a fool’s indulgence—but he let out a low, double-click of his tongue that brought his climbing-partner scrambling through the dark. Together, they bound the stone in tendril-rope and hauled it up to the camp.

It was the first new Hearth-Stone found in three hundred years. And as the hydraulic surges continued, every few months, in the dark concavities where the Vein-Cutters worked, another stone would be found. Not thousands—for a flood of stone would have made them careless—but just enough. One for every new camp. One to replace every hearth that cracked. A steady, sacred communion between the deep earth and the people of the lattice.

***

The third shift was the smallest of all, so subtle that even the Knot-Walkers’ finest carvers did not notice it for a generation.

A new species of Frost-Moss began to bloom in the cold shadow-pockets. It looked identical to the old moss—dark, velvet-soft, bitter to the tongue—but its spores carried a microscopic, oil-rich lipids synthesized from the mineral-bark.

When the women boiled the moss into the daily broth, the oil was absorbed by the body. Within months, the Knot-Walkers’ tear ducts began to produce a fluid that was slightly thicker, perfectly clear, and completely immune to freezing. The dry ice-dust no longer bit into the cornea. It slid away on a slick, frictionless film.

The gnawing, unseen pain that had haunted their quiet hours vanished. For the first time in memory, when the families gathered around the blue hearth-fires in their swaying bast-pods, the elders could close their slits, rest their bare faces in the firelight, and look at one another without tears of ice forming on their cheeks.

***

The Power stayed for seven cycles of the vascular pulse. It watched the Knot-Walkers adapt to these subtle mercies.

It watched them carve new foot-notches in the fresh, whalebone spires. It watched the Gale-Kites slide through the shadowless glare on papery wings, their pressure-fans registering the clean, tuned acoustics of the air. It watched a mother in a suspended pod press her hand to her child’s cheek, signaling *safety* with a soft, rhythmic tap of her knuckles—a signal that no longer carried the tremor of pain or the dread of sudden collapse.

The void remained freezing. The air still burned the unaccustomed lung. The leaf-edges remained sharp as knapped flint, and the house-sized water drops still shattered like falling iron. The world was still a place of magnificent, terrifying severity.

Satisfied, the Power withdrew. It slid back up through the twenty miles of continental crust, out into the silence between the stars, leaving the abyss behind.

It left no temples. It left no holy texts. It left no name.

Down below, on a ledge overlooking five miles of brilliant, empty air, a blind Listen-Keeper sat with her bare chest against the singing wood. She felt the clean, deep pulse of the world rising through her bones—a song of pressure, cold, and flawless strength.

She smiled, notched her wooden pole to mark the rising floor, and let the quiet hold her.
