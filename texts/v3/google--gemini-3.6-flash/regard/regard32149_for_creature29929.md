The Power did not drop down like a falling star, nor did it break the surface of the lake with a blinding light. To break the mirror of that cold, high dish would have been a violence. 

Instead, it arrived the way deep pressure arrives at the bottom of a trench: without motion, present all at once, filling the spaces between the water molecules, the grain of the iron-hard timber-reed, and the slow, nine-beat hearts of the sleepers in the heartwood.

It was an intelligence so ancient that its thoughts were shaped like geological epochs, yet so fine in its attention that it could count the individual cilia of a feeding barnacle without disturbing the rhythm of its sweep.

For three turns of the mountain’s shadow, the Power did nothing but listen.

It listened to the *click-click-rasp* of the armored legs against the trunks. It registered the deep, hollow *thunk* of the giant mussels, feeling the vibration move through the basalt bowl of the crater. It felt the precise, patient strike of an obsidian chisel two hundred feet down, where an arbor-carver was shaping an arch that her great-grandchild would be the first to walk through. It traced the slow, cold blood coursing through the Oul-Vat, noting how their flesh was free of the fevered, burning haste that consumed the air-breathing world below the peak.

The Power searched the city for a flaw. Not a flaw of design—for the city was as natural as a honeycomb—but a point of vulnerability. Where did this world bleed? Where was it thin?

It found three things.

First, it felt the deep root of the mountain itself. Two miles beneath the silt floor, the ancient magma chamber that fed the thermal vents was shrinking. In three thousand years—a mere breath in the life of the timber-reed—the vents would cool. The lake would lose its micro-currents. The cold would become absolute, freezing the water-hyacinth to brittle ice, and the Oul-Vat would sink into a torpor from which their slow hearts would never wake.

Second, it looked up through the ninety feet of clear liquid to the sky. Far to the south, beyond the dry lowlands, the races of smoke and iron were growing. In time, their eyes would turn toward the high peaks. They would see the blue eye of the crater lake. They would bring nets, iron axes, and the loud, violent sound of voices struck through air. They would not understand that a city could be grown rather than built; they would see only timber to burn and strange, silent beasts to pull into the burning sun.

Third, it looked into the dark heartwood at the base of the central stalk, where the ancestral dead lay sealed in living wood. It saw that while the Oul-Vat knew how to remember, they had no way to preserve their memory if the reed should ever die. If the tree fell, the ledger of ten thousand lives would rot into the silt, unread forever.

A lesser power—a god of pride or intervention—would have revealed itself. It would have parted the water, spoken in a voice of thunder, and demanded altars. It would have given the Oul-Vat metal, or fire, or laws written on stone.

But this Power was wise. It knew that to give fire to a creature of cold water is to murder its soul. To speak aloud to a people who talk through the resonance of wood is to deafen them. To make oneself known is to transform the worshiper into a beggar, replacing their quiet, self-contained mastery with an aching dependency on the heavens.

The Power chose the path of the true gardener: it touched only the conditions, leaving the living things untouched.

***

In the fourth night, while the cobalt light faded into the pitch-black density of the deep basin, the Power began its work.

It reached downward, past the silt, past the roots of the timber-reed, into the red, pressurized heart of the mountain. It did not ignite a volcano; it bound the deep earth. With a subtle, gravitational stroke, it sealed the fault lines that threatened to leak the lake’s water into the dry caverns below. Then, it nudged a slow, deep plume of liquid mantle beneath the basin, locking the thermal vents into a perpetual, steady pulse. For fifty thousand years, the vents would not fail. They would breathe their warm, sulfurous, mineral-rich drift into the basin at the exact temperature required to keep the timber-reed supple and the char abundant.

Next, it touched the sky above the crater. It did not raise a wall of glass or a dome of force. Instead, it altered the high-altitude thermal currents around the peak. It bent the light and the clouds just so. To the eyes of those who looked up from the lowlands, the mountain peak would forever appear wrapped in a perpetual, jagged cap of impassable ice and dry, barren cloud. The lake would remain hidden in plain sight, a secret dish held in the palm of an unapproachable sky.

Finally, the Power turned to the Oul-Vat themselves.

It did not alter their bodies. It did not make them stronger, or longer-lived, or wiser than their nature permitted. But as an Oul-Vat arbor-carver slept, suspended in her woven hammock of kelp-line, her hand resting lightly against the dark, oiled wall of her chamber, the Power pressed a single, microscopic change into the sap of the timber-reed.

It was a quiet gift—a memory embedded in the biology of the plant.

From that night forward, whenever an Oul-Vat died and was sealed into the heartwood, the living fibers of the reed would not merely enclose the body; they would take into their own grain the subtle, mineral resonance of the dead one’s bones. The wood would remember the frequency of their life. 

Centuries later, when a child pressed their jaw or their webbed feet against the pillar to feel the news of the city, they would not merely feel the current strokes of their neighbors. They would feel, beneath the surface hum of the barnacles, a faint, sweet, foundational chord—the combined, harmonious vibration of every ancestor who had ever slept within the stalk. The city would become a living instrument, playing its own history into the bones of the living, continuous and unbroken.

***

When the work was done, the Power did not wait for thanks. It required no songs, no sacrifices, no names carved into the bark.

Before it withdrew, it left only one mark of its presence—a gesture so small that only the lake itself recorded it.

In the center of the broad, open shaft where the sunlight hit the floor at noon, the Power touched a single grain of basalt silt. The grain vibrated once, a tiny, perfect pitch that resonated at precisely eight beats per minute—the speed of an Oul-Vat heart at rest.

Then, the Power receded, sliding out of the basin as silently as it had entered, returning to the vastness between the stars.

Below, ninety feet beneath the surface, an elder Oul-Vat shifted in his sleep. His leaf-like gill-frills bloomed outward, fanning the cool, mineral-dense water, then collapsed against his dark neck. His fingers, resting on the smooth lintel of his doorway, caught a sudden, deep vibration from the wood—a sound like a distant drum, but softer, warmer, as if the entire mountain had just exhaled a long, peaceful breath.

He did not open his broad, brass-colored eyes. He did not know that a god had visited his home.

He simply pressed his cheek a little closer to the heartwood, listened to the ancient, steady hum of the barnacles, and sank deeper into the cold, green sleep of the world.
