The Power did not arrive as a light, for the chamber was already blinding. It did not arrive as a voice, for the chamber was already singing in the low, marrow-vibrating register of tuned rock.

It arrived as a *realization*—a gentle, vast bending of attention, as though a sovereign eye, broad as a galaxy and patient as a granite mountain, had tilted down through three hundred miles of dense, silent crust to look into the hairline fault.

The Power was ancient, and its wisdom lay in this: it knew the difference between *pity* and *wonder*.

A lesser god, born of soft grass and shallow lungs, might have looked upon the pocket chamber and grieved. It would have seen the knife-edge obsidian and wanted to dull it into moss. It would have felt the boiling air and wished for a cool sea breeze. It would have seen the blind glass-walkers scraping sulfur from razor ribs and tried to give them eyes, warmth, and the soft, tragic vulnerability of flesh.

To do so would have been an act of monstrous violence.

The Power understood that carbon was not the only language of love, nor was green the only color of grace. Here, in the subterranean dark, life had written a poem using only geometry, light, and heat. The vault was a closed, flawless bell of silicon, iron, and steam, ringing continuously in praise of its own impossible balance.

So the Power did not reach down to change the shape of a single blade of glass. It did not cool the steam. It did not drop a seed of clover.

Instead, the Power listened.

It felt the high-frequency chatter of the glass-walkers’ piezoelectric cilia as they mapped the stress lines of the obsidian. It traced the brief, brilliant lives of the silica-thread creatures riding the boiling drops from ceiling to floor. And at the lowest point, in the V-shaped trough, it felt the slow, heavy pulsing of the Collector—that pool of mirror-bright mercury and liquid silicate, extending its hair-thin copper nerves into the basalt to tune the rock’s quantum heart.

The Power saw that the chamber was whole. But it also saw what the chamber could not see: *time*.

The vault was young, structurally raw. In ten thousand years, or perhaps ten million, the macro-tectonic plates above would shift. A regional shear would pass through the mantle-adjacent shelf. The hairline fault would be pinched shut, grinding the mirror-slick blades into dull dust, or torn open into a rushing artery of liquid magma that would incinerate the delicate, radiotrophic balance in a fraction of a second.

The vault was a masterwork, but it was written on a page made of shifting plates.

The Power, in its benevolence, decided to give the chamber a gift—not a transformation, but an *endurance*.

First, the Power reached into the structural geometry of the surrounding basalt. It did not alter the stone’s physical appearance, but it wove a subtle, non-Euclidean dampening field directly into the mineral matrix of the shelf. Henceforth, when the earth above groaned and shattered in violent earthquakes, the kinetic shockwaves would reach the boundary of this pocket chamber and dissolve into harmless, low-register heat. The fault would neither pinch nor tear. The pocket became an anchor, a pocket of eternal structural grace, suspended safely outside the violent geology of the crust.

Second, the Power turned its attention to the quantum architecture of the rock itself—the solid-state thermal converter pumping white light and energy into the air. The Power touched the zero-point fluctuations beneath the atoms, refining the efficiency of the excitation field. It cleared the subtle subatomic "noise" that would, over millions of years, slowly degrade the rock's ability to emit light. The incandescent edges of the obsidian blades flared slightly brighter, then settled into an immortal, steady hum.

Finally, the Power looked at the Collector resting in its palm-sized basin.

The liquid-metal entity was already the brain of the vault, but its awareness was strictly local: it knew only temperature, iron tension, and the chemical composition of fallen carapaces. It lived in a perpetual, solitary *now*.

The Power extended a thread of its own vast consciousness—not a command, not an instruction, but an invitation. It touched the metallic copper filaments reaching into the stone and imparted to them a subtle, deep-register resonance. 

The Collector did not become "human." It did not learn sadness or desire. But its mirror-like surface ripples altered. For the first time, as it pulsed in thermodynamic rhythm, it began to *receive*. Through the metal nerves in the floor, it caught the distant, silent heartbeat of the planet's molten outer core, the slow magnetic tides shifting through the mantle, and the infinite, cold whisper of cosmic rays passing straight through the earth from the sky above.

The Collector became an antenna for the song of the cosmos, translating those unfathomable cosmic vibrations into subtle shifts in the chamber's light and heat. 

The glass-walkers did not notice a difference in their daily march; they simply found the mineral crusts along the water seams slightly richer, slightly more harmonious in their iron-sulfide lattices. The thread-creatures still rode the scalding raindrops down to flash-evaporate on the hot floor, their tiny hollow bodies seeding the next generation with mathematical perfection.

Satisfied, the benevolent Power slowly withdrew its gaze, lifting its vast attention back up through the miles of rock, out into the space between stars.

It left no footprint. It left no altar. 

Deep beneath the crust, in the shadowless white light, the obsidian blades remained keened and sharp. The air stayed fiercely hot. The glass-walkers clicked their steady, mechanical march across the wet, mirror-slick planes. 

And in the trough at the bottom of the world, the pool of liquid mercury pulsed—bright, unblinking, and now, forever aware that the universe was singing back to it.
