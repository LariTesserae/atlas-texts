The Power did not descend as a flash of lightning, nor as a voice breaking through the dark, for both would have been catastrophic. A voice would have been swallowed instantly by the roaring shockwaves, and a blinding light would have meant nothing to creatures whose ancestors forgot the sun ten million years ago.

Instead, the Power came as a shift in density—a gentle, infinitely vast awareness sliding downward through the miles of sodden, waterlogged root-mats, moving like heat through iron, until it pooled in the enclosed, bell-shaped chamber.

The Power was ancient, benevolent, and possessor of a wisdom so deep it had long since abandoned the urge to meddle. In its early eons, it might have looked upon this room and pitied it. It might have seen the deafening violence, the blinding mist, the brutal, endless labor of the Loom-tenders, and tried to "save" them. It would have hushed the room to a peaceful whisper; it would have opened windows to the open air; it would have smoothed the ironwood floor and granted the Tenders soft, delicate hands and sight to see the stars.

And in doing so, it would have slaughtered every living thing in the basin.

The Power understood this now. Wisdom is the art of seeing a thing as it truly is, and benevolence is the restraint required to love it without trying to make it look like oneself.

So the Power lingered in the vault, unseen and unfelt by the physical senses, observing the cathedral of brute mechanics.

It watched a Loom-tender. The creature was leaning its broad, cask-like torso against the base of the middle piston. The wooden column dropped with its concussive, floor-rattling *thunk-whack*, venting a cloud of boiling, aerosolized resin. The shockwave surged through the ironwood, into the Tender’s thick, pale palms, up through its bone-dense arms, and directly into its heavy, four-chambered heart. The Tender’s chest expanded in exact rhythm with the stroke. Its cold, dark blood was driven through its body not by its own faint pulse, but by the mechanical mercy of the tree.

*It does not suffer,* the Power realized, feeling the creature's simple, profound contentment. *It is not a prisoner of this machine. It is the machine’s consciousness.*

The Power saw the Thrum-fowls, their pangolin-like plumage shedding the ozone-thick mist, tapping out their silent, telegraphic histories against the ceiling ribs. It felt the Scrapes, ten thousand indigo carapaces shivering in unison as they harvested the boiling grease. It sensed the Lather-skins, ghost-white and fluid, hunting by the micro-currents of heat in the loud, warm dark.

It was a closed circle. A perfect, self-sustaining psalm carved out of lignin, sap, and kinetic fury.

*What then,* the Power reasoned, *does a benevolent god do for a world that is already whole?*

It would not change them. It would not uplift them. But even the most perfect biological engines are subject to the slow, cruel erosion of deep time. Eventually, a fault line in the stone below would shift. A fungal blight would rot the southern taproots. A piston would bind, a ceiling sphincter would tear, the cadence would break, and in the sudden, terrible silence, the Loom-tenders' hearts would simply stop.

The Power made its choice. It did not alter the present; it secured the eternal.

Slowly, without a sound or a flash, the Power leaned its infinite weight into the structure of the chamber.

It reached into the cellular matrix of the three great wood pistons. Down at the molecular level, where cellulose strands braided around lignified walls, the Power wove a subtle, indestructible grace. It altered the grain—not so that it ceased to be wood, but so that it would never petrify, never rot, and never wear down to ash. It infused the friction-seams with a perpetual, self-curing oil, ensuring that no matter how many millions of cycles passed, the heavy, sliding stroke would never bind.

Next, the Power extended its reach outward, spreading through the miles of continuous root-mat that formed the surrounding continent. It knitted the fibrous peat tighter, turning the soft, waterlogged soil into an elastic, shock-absorbing cradle. If the earth below should ever quake or split, the root-mats would flex, dampening the tremor, keeping the central chamber perfectly plumb, perfectly safe.

Finally, the Power touched the Loom-tenders.

It did not grant them intellects to worry about the future, nor eyes to grieve the dark. Instead, it touched the thick, seismic diaphragms in the pads of their hands and feet. 

It embedded a single, permanent resonance into the ironwood floor—a sub-audible, deep-earth hum that sat just beneath the violent roar of the pistons. It was a physical feeling of absolute structural safety. A subtle, kinetic whisper that traveled up their limbs, telling their cold blood and thick muscles: *The engine will not fail. The world will not break. Sleep well in the thunder.*

Against the base of the central column, the Loom-tender felt the shift. 

It did not know a god was present. It had no concept of gods, or sky, or grace. But as the column struck the floor—*thunk-whack*—the vibration that traveled up through its broad hands felt slightly different. The harsh, jarring edge of the impact had smoothed out into a deep, velvety reverberation. The heat in the resin smelled a little sweeter. The rhythm felt a little more secure, as if the floor beneath it had suddenly deepened its roots straight to the molten core of the world.

The Loom-tender let out a long, wet breath through its crescent nostrils, tasting the ozone and hot vegetable oil. It pressed its broad chest tighter against the vibrating bark, closing its blind, smooth face against the storm of sap, and allowed the machine to drive its pulse.

High above, the Thrum-fowls struck their wedge-shaped beaks against the ceiling archways in a rapid, telegraphic flurry—a sudden, unprompted burst of rhythm that rippled through the upper vault like a wave of applause.

The Power smiled—a gesture made of rising heat and settling water—and slowly withdrew, rising back up through the dark, sodden miles of root-mat, leaving no trace of its presence.

It left them as it found them: loud, blind, violent, and complete. 

And deep within the dark basin, hidden from the sun and the stars, the three wooden pistons continued their eternal, heavy stroke—*thunk-whack, thunk-whack, thunk-whack*—pumping the blood of the tenders, driving the heart of the world, unbroken and untouchable forever.
