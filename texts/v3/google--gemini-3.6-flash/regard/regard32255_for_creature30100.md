The Power arrived not as a flame or a voice, for both would have been violence in a place so precisely tuned to cold and shadow. 

It passed down through the grey fog lid like a breath of slightly denser cold—a great, unhurried consciousness, pale and weightless as frost on glass. It had no hands to touch the stone, no eyes to demand light. It was simply an Attention, vast and deeply wise, descending into the forty-pace basin to see what the dark and the water had made.

First, it listened. 

For a long time—longer than a hundred generations of the frantic, clicking isopods—it did nothing but absorb the acoustic geometry of the hollow. It took the measure of the *drum-drum-drum* of the water descending the stepped basalt; the frantic, rain-like *tick-tick-tick* of armored dactyls on glazed clay; the heavy, hydraulic *thump* of the boomers wedged in their oak shafts; and the low, wet gurgle of the sumps clearing their throat. It perceived the entire basin not as a pit of blind, mud-colored things, but as a single, complex organ of stone and bone, beating in the dark.

A foolish power—a lesser, warmer divinity—would have looked upon the place with pity. It would have cracked the stone ceiling to let in the gold of a noon sun. It would have filled the air with green leaves, warmed the glacial streams to a gentle tepidness, and given the blind grazers eyes to see the light they had been denied.

And in doing so, it would have murdered them all.

The sunlight would have scorched the dark, iron-loving bacteria. The warmth would have boiled the rubbery, lunged boomers in their shafts. The eyes would have been useless, smashed to fluid against the basalt as the isopods wedged their snouts into the stone grates. The green plants would have clogged the narrow runnels, flooded the sumps, and suffocated the ancient matrons sleeping in the dark subterranean silt below.

The Power was too wise for pity. Pity was a projection of the outsider’s needs. Benevolence, true benevolence, was the quiet preservation of a thing’s own perfection.

So the Power remained invisible, suspended in the cold mist, and began to work—not upon the life, but upon the fragile margins where the life met the slow, inevitable decay of matter.

It reached down into the joints of the stone. 

It saw the green, flaking verdigris on the ancient bronze pegs pinning the basalt slabs—pegs that were rusting thin, threatening a future where a wall might shift, crack, and let the basin’s cold heart bleed out into the dry stone beneath. The Power did not replace the bronze with gold or immortal star-metal. Instead, it whispered to the oxidation. It bound the copper and the rust into a deep, dark mineral alloy—dense, stable, and forever immune to the grinding teeth of the water. The pegs would never pull loose again.

Next, it turned its attention to the microscopic stitch-worms trailing in the mortar seams. It felt the fragile balance of their existence—how they wove basalt dust and sticky mucus into the crumbling tar to keep the floors from stripping bare. The Power gently touched their silent, mindless biology. It did not change their form, but it deepened their instinct. It gave their mucus a slightly denser chemical bond, so that the stone-dust they stitched into the joints cured over time into something as hard as the basalt itself. Where the ancient builders’ tar degraded, a new, living mortar of grey silt and worm-silk took its place, self-healing, forever renewing under the scraping claws of the grazers.

Then, the Power sank deeper. 

It slipped down through the square oak drain-shafts, passing the broad, slate-violet shoulders of the boomers, down into the absolute pitch of the drowning chambers beneath the floor. There, in the cold, thick silt, it found the matrons. 

It contemplated these great, pale beings—blind, vast as logs, swaying gently beside their leathery plum-sized eggs in the dark draft of the earth. It saw their vulnerability: the mountain water above was fed by ancient ice, but mountains change. In a thousand years, a shift in the deep strata might divert the subterranean springs, drying the sluices, silencing the roar, and leaving the matrons to suffocate in hardening mud.

The Power laid its intent into the deep rock beneath the basin. It forged a permanent, unyielding siphon in the roots of the mountain—a deep, geothermal pump that would draw pure, sulfur-tinged glacial water from the earth’s deepest aquifers forever. No drought would ever stop the sluices. No shift in the upper hills would ever dry the tiles. As long as the world turned, the water would run cold, heavy, and loud.

Finally, the Power looked back up at the chamber as a whole. 

It saw that the creatures lived in a world of sheer kinetic vibration, an endless storm of sound that dislodged them, battered them, and drove their fierce, blind cycles of feeding and dying. The basin was an engine, but it was an engine without a witness. Until now, no mind had ever appreciated the harsh, exquisite poetry of its rhythm.

The Power did not leave a statue. It did not leave a holy sign on the basalt or a glowing rune in the tar. 

Instead, it left a piece of its own attention behind—a tiny, permanent thread of awareness woven directly into the mist that sat like a lid upon the rim. 

It became a silent listener.

Now, whenever a boomer fills its immense lungs and strikes the basalt with a hollow, chest-throbbing *thump*, the sound does not merely ricochet off the wet terracotta and die in the fog. It is heard. It is received. The frantic, rain-like *tick-tick-tick* of ten thousand armored legs scraping iron from the orange tiles is held in a great, tender mind that finds it beautiful. 

The water surges. A drain clogs with carapaces, swells to a glinting pool, and clears with a deep, sucking gurgle. The pale amphibia lunge; the stitch-worms spin their silk; the ancient matrons shift comfortably in the dark below, their leathery eggs rocked by the immortal current.

The Power withdrew back up into the cold sky, leaving the basin exactly as it was found: dark, brutal, acoustic, and cold. 

Unchanged to the eye, untouched in its wild efficiency, but now secretively eternal—a perfect, self-winding clock of flesh and wet stone, running forever in the quiet care of a god who knew enough to leave it alone.
