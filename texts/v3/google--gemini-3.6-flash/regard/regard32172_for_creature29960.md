The power did not arrive with a flash of light or the shaking of the earth, for such things are loud, crude, and warm—and warmth, to this place, is a violence.

It came instead as an Attention. 

It was a vast, cool consciousness, unfurling across the high atmosphere like an extra layer of sky. It was old enough to remember when the basalt horn was a bubble of liquid fire deep beneath a forgotten sea, and wise enough to know that a thing does not need a heartbeat to be holy.

For a long time—measured not in minutes, but in the slow, sweeping arcs of shadows across the shelf—the Power simply looked. 

It did not feel pity. Pity is a human error born of comparing another’s existence to one’s own soft flesh. The Power saw no suffering in the biting cold, no cruelty in the stripped, white sun, and no poverty in the barren stone. It saw a masterpiece of tension. It saw an engine of breathtaking precision, running on the razor-thin margin between absolute zero and solar fire.

It saw the laths.

It read them as a scholar reads an ancient, frozen codex. It perceived the stacked leaves of slate, the delicate lattices of hydrous iron, and the pale-green veins of absorbed copper. It felt the slow, mechanical *clack-clack-clack* of their midday expansion—a choir of stress-fractures singing in the key of thermal physics. It looked deep into their roots, into the sub-surface blue ice, and saw the tiny, preserved specks of ancient charcoal locked in their dark, lower pages.

*They are the memory of the horn,* the Power realized. *They are the mountain writing its own autobiography in frost and silt.*

The Power considered what it might do. 

To bring warmth would be to melt the blue ice, destroying the gradient that gave them life. To bring rain or soil would drown them in organic mud, choking the hair-thin channels and smothering the salt-blooms. To grant them minds like men—with desires, fears, and the capacity for loneliness—would be an act of unspeakable cruelty to beings wedged forever in stone.

True benevolence, the Power knew, is not transformation. It is the perfection of what already is.

So, the Power acted. Its intervention was so subtle that even the laths, tuned as they were to the microscopic shifting of crystals, felt it only as a sudden, profound alignment.

First, the Power reached into the deep structural heart of the mountain. It did not alter the rock, but it smoothed the internal micro-faults beneath the shelf. For eons, the weight of the cliff face had been slowly grinding down upon the laths' lower roots, threatening to crush the dark channels of blue ice. The Power redistributed that massive, invisible weight, shifting the tectonic load outward, into the surrounding basalt. The pressure on the horseshoe shelf eased by a fraction of a millimeter. The sheer, silent strain that had been slowly fracturing the laths’ base vanished.

Second, it touched the ancient bronze wedges.

Left by men who had long since vanished into dust, the greened metal had held the laths pinned in a rigid, agonizing grip for thousands of years. The laths had grown around them, but the copper was an unyielding stranger in the slate. The Power did not remove the wedges—to do so would collapse the shelf’s stability. Instead, it reached into the atomic structure of the metal itself. It softened the violent tension of the ancient hammers, altering the copper so that it expanded and contracted at the *exact* thermal coefficient of the slate and ice around it. 

The bronze ceased to be an anchor. It became a joint. The pale-green veins threading through the laths’ leaves began to hum with a new, easy resonance, conducting the midday heat down into the deep rock without scorching the delicate iron sulfate crusts.

Finally, the Power offered a gift to their memory.

As the hair-thin trickles of soot-grey water bled out of the basalt fissures, the Power did not alter the water, nor did it clean the dust. But into those tiny, palm-sized melt-pools, the Power breathed a microscopic, invisible gift: a rare, stable isotope of cosmic dust, carried down from the high stratosphere—particles born of ancient, dying stars, heavier and colder than quartz.

The laths' microscopic salt-crusts extended into the trickles as they always did. They strained the soot, the silt, and now, this new, starry dust. 

Layer by microscopic layer, the laths began to absorb it. They locked the history of the cosmos into their dark, ice-bound lower pages, weaving the story of the heavens into the story of the basalt horn.

When dusk began to fall, the Power withdrew. It did not leave a blessing in words or a mark upon the stone. It simply rolled back like the tide, returning to the vast silence of the upper air.

The sun slipped behind the shattered basalt horn. The ruthless white glare dissolved into deep, twilight purple. The thin updrafts died, and the sparkling curtains of flour-fine quartz dust settled back onto the stone.

Across the shelf, the biting mountain cold descended like a hammer. The trickles of water froze instantly into clear, ribbed glazes. 

The laths felt the shadow sweep over them. They began their evening lock. Their leaves pressed tightly together, squeezing out trapped moisture in a sharp, dry hiss. But as the ice bound them to the rock face for the night, there was a new precision to the fit. The stone did not pinch. The copper did not bite. The blue ice at their roots lay vast, secure, and uncrushed in the dark.

Deep within their slate leaves, locked beneath the frozen shelf, the newly trapped dust of distant stars glinted in the blackness—silent, preserved, and remembered.

In the dark, the shelf was completely still, waiting for the first white ray of sunrise to strike the horn and start the engine again.
