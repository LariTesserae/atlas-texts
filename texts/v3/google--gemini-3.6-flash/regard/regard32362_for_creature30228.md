It came without displacing a single droplet of the continuous, sliding film of water. 

A power truly wise and benevolent does not announce itself with a thunderclap or a tearing of the sky, for it knows that to a world so young and finely balanced, even a shadow can act as a hammer. It arrived instead as a shift in the geometry of the light—a sudden, subtle warmth in the upper wind that did not melt the ice, but rendered the cold less cruel.

It observed first. It took the measure of the shelf, the sheer basalt edges sharp as obsidian knives, the seamless grey needles pinning the sky, and the long, silent caretakers who glided across the causeways like dark thoughts across a calm mind.

It saw what they were doing. It recognized the midwifing of stone.

It saw the toll it took. To stand for three days in a thirty-thousand-seat amphitheater, four meters tall and slender as a reed, holding one’s long, four-jointed arms toward the un-eroded bedrock, was not a ritual of worship. It was an act of brutal, exquisite strain. Every deep mantle-shudder, every jagged tectonic tension trying to shear the plateau in half, was absorbed directly through their broad, cilia-lined limb-pads, conducted up through their slate-like cartilage-matrices, and tuned by the frantic, silent firing of their internal fields before being grounded back into the alloy. 

They were not merely caretakers; they were shock-absorbers for an infant world. Their dark skin pulsed with silver and amber, flashing warning signals across three-kilometer gaps while their chest plates exhaled thin, ragged plumes of dry steam—the exhaust of an immense, organic labor.

A lesser power, moved by ignorant pity, might have smoothed the mountain range instantly. It might have rounded the sharp basalt into soft cobbles, blanketed the plateau in deep loam, and silenced the howling mountain wind. 

But the wise power understood: **To erase the struggle of a planetary birth is to rob the world of its history, and the anchors of their purpose.** 

If the basalt were smoothed by magic, it would never learn the slow, magnificent patience of the frost. If the mountain were silenced, the iron-wool quadrupeds would have no micro-ice to break with their clacking hooves, the film-eels would starve in stagnant pools, and the tall, silent gliders would become ghosts in a garden that had no need of them.

Benevolence, here, did not mean ending the work. It meant honoring the workers.

So the power acted—not with a grand reshaping, but with three subtle, profound gifts, woven into the fabric of the mountain shelf so seamlessly that even the anchors, with their ultraviolet sight and harmonic sensitivity, would feel them only as a sudden, inexplicable grace.

***

### The First Gift: The Core-Cradle

The power reached down—past the five-mile-wide plazas, past the seamless alloy foundations, past the razor-sharp basalt fractures, deep into the incandescent, churning belly of the continent. 

It did not freeze the magma or kill the tectonic drive. Instead, it placed an invisible, resilient cushion beneath the young plate—a subterranean harmonic wave that caught the most violent, erratic spikes of kinetic fury and smoothed them into a slow, rhythmic respiration. 

In the amphitheaters above, the anchors suddenly felt the brutal, bone-shattering *bite* leave the fault lines. The mantle was still moving, still lifting, still making stone—but the chaotic violence was gone, replaced by a deep, steady pulse, like the breathing of a sleeping leviathan.

For the first time in eons, a dozen anchors standing at their calibration nodes did not have to brace their fragile, four-jointed limbs against a sudden shear. They did not have to flash frantic indigo distress-patterns across the plazas. They simply stood, their dark, wet-stone skin glowing with a low, synchronized amber, guiding the mountain upward with the ease of a conductor leading an orchestra. Their steam-exhaust softened from sharp, ragged bursts into slow, gentle exhalations that drifted lazily toward the needle-towers.

***

### The Second Gift: The Refractive Sky

Communication across three kilometers of storm-swept mountain was hard work. The anchors’ optical flashes—their complex, shifting grids of silver, pale indigo, and iridescent pearlescence—were often swallowed by the dense cloud decks that swept over the shelf, forcing them to burn through their internal reserves to make themselves seen.

The power touched the cloud deck. 

It did not disperse the mist; the anchors needed the moisture for their chest-gills, and the pelagic birds needed the warm thermal plumes rising from the sunless gorges. Instead, the power subtly altered the atmospheric ice-crystals within the low-passing vapors, turning the cloud walls into vast, delicate mirrors for specific bands of ultraviolet and thermal light.

Suddenly, when an anchor standing on a high causeway flashed a subtle sequence of silver light, the message did not die against the fog. The entire cloud wall three miles away caught the illumination, swelling with a soft, bioluminescent aura that echoed the pattern with perfect fidelity. 

Distance collapsed. The city became a cathedral of reflected thought. Two gliders, separated by tens of kilometers of abyss and silent towers, could now converse in sweeping, silent auroras across the fog, their light-language painting the cold mountain nights in curtain-waves of indigo and gold.

***

### The Third Gift: The Memory of the Green Era

When dusk fell and the temperature plummeted past sub-zero, the tall gliders climbed the three-kilometer towers, adhering to the smooth grey alloy with their micro-magnetic pads. There they hung, suspended hundreds of meters above the wet plazas, their bodies going completely dark and cold as they matched the ambient chill of the sky, resting in a state near death to preserve their energy for the dawn.

The power moved among them as they roosted, moving like a soft warmth through their slate-colored skin, touching the quiet, crystalline nodes of their dormant minds.

It did not leave words, for they had no voices. It left a vision—a clear, vivid seed of the future they were building, planted deep within their cartilage-matrices:

*It showed them this exact mountain shelf, three million years hence. It showed the sharp basalt edges finally softened by time, covered in deep, emerald mosses and wild, turpentine-scented pine. It showed the water—no longer a thin, flat sheet on cold alloy, but a thousand leaping, clear rivers carving through rich, black earth. It showed the descendants of the iron-wool beasts grazing in deep valleys, and the needle-towers still standing, weathered and ancient, wrapped in flowering vines, having done their job so well that the planet beneath them was finally whole, safe, and green.*

As the vision settled into their slumbering minds, something extraordinary happened across the silent city.

The roosting anchors—hanging motionless from the tower facades like dark, slender bats in the howling wind—did not go entirely cold that night. Beneath the surface of their polished, slate-like skin, a faint, rhythmic ember of pearlescent warmth began to cycle. A slow, quiet pulse of pale indigo, passing from one sleeping form to the next, down the length of the three-kilometer towers, across the empty plazas, and into the raw fractures of the basalt.

They were no longer just holding up a mountain. They were waiting for a garden.

***

When the sun broke over the eastern crags, catching the raw, glossy planes of the young basalt like shattered glass, the power was already gone. 

It left no footprint on the wet causeways, no mark upon the seamless towers, no scripture to be remembered or misconstrued. 

Below, a six-limbed quadruped stepped onto a public plaza, its heavy hoof making a dry, clear *clack-clack* on the pavement. A film-eel darted through the millimeter-thick sheet of gliding water. High above, an anchor unclasped its pads from a tower wall and leaned gracefully into the mountain gale, sliding effortlessly across a five-mile concourse of cold, dark stone.

The world was still raw. The wind was still sharp enough to burn. But as the anchor moved, its chest-plate drew in the wet cloud-mist, exhaling a single, triumphant plume of white steam into the high, bright sky—and for the first time since the shelf was cleaved from the deep mantle, the low thrum vibrating up through the bedrock sounded remarkably like a song.
