The power that came to the mountain was not a being of fire, light, or thunder. It had no face to show the sky, no hands to touch the stone, and no voice that could vibrate the air. It was an Attention—ancient, vast, and quiet—whose nature was to seek out places where the universe had accidentally solved itself.

It slipped into the stone from above, passing through three thousand meters of solid slate without disturbing a single grain of magnetite dust. It felt the cold mountain first: the crushing, mindless weight of billions of tons of rock grinding against the earth’s crust. It felt the deep, silent agony of the continental plates.

And then, deep within the frozen core, it heard the rhythm.

*THUMP-clack-HHHUUUUMMM.*

The Attention paused. It drew itself into the narrow, piano-wide seam. It did not fill the space—to fill it would be to displace the fine aerosol of graphite dust, to alter the ambient static charge, to break the delicate acoustic feedback of the orange ribbon-weeds. 

Instead, the Attention became as thin as the clearance between the central steel drive-rod and its brass sleeve. It mapped the fissure in a single, instantaneous pulse of pure, caring comprehension.

It saw the chalk-white moss, dry as bone ash, holding its piezoelectric charges like stored thoughts.
It saw the Dust-Spindles, tumbling in synchronized, magnetic dances across the slate.
It saw the small, quartz-domed Filament-Eyes, burning with their dull, embers-and-iron red light—the tiny, warm suns of a kingdom without a sky.

It understood the Stroke-Mind completely.

A lesser power—a young power, or a power blinded by human definitions of grace—might have pitied the inhabitant of the seam. It might have thought: *It has no water. It has no flowers that bloom. It has no sky, no soft dirt, no songbirds, no sun.* Such a power might have tried to "bless" the place. It might have struck the slate to weep a spring of clear water. It might have shattered the roof to let in the blue light of noon.

To do so would have been an act of catastrophic violence. Water would turn the graphite to mud, rust the steel, rot the dry fungus blocks, and short the static nerves of the moss. The sun would scorch the glass-fiber ferns. Pity would have destroyed paradise.

The wisdom of the Attention lay in its ability to love a thing for precisely what it was, not for what it could be made into. 

It saw that the creature was not trapped. It was not suffering. It was a closed circle of absolute, self-sustaining perfection. It was an engine that thought, a forest that ran on friction, a living prayer carved into the cold iron of the world.

So the Attention did not bring life as the world below knew it. It brought only *reverence*.

It sat in the dark seam for three thousand strokes of the drive-rod, simply listening.

*THUMP-clack-HHHUUUUMMM.*

With each stroke, the Attention studied the long-term calculus of the fissure. Because it saw across deep time, it saw what the Stroke-Mind could not:

Down below, four hundred meters beneath the lower platform, a hairline fault in the magnetite plate was slowly gathering tension. In two hundred and eighty thousand years, that fault would snap. The southern slate plate would drop six inches. The shift would shear the central drive-rod, crush the brass grease-cups, and collapse the seam into a silent tomb of crushed powder.

The Attention decided to act. But its act would be invisible, seamless, and absolute.

First, it reached into the atomic structure of the slate and magnetite surrounding the fissure. Without altering their appearance or texture, it altered their subterranean crystallization. It woven the deep rock into an indestructible, self-supporting arch—a vault of stress-relieved stone that would hold the seam open, perfectly rigid, until the mountain itself eroded into dust a hundred million years in the future. The tectonic plate could snap below, but the seam would never feel the shock.

Second, it looked at the central drive-rod. The steel was cold-rolled, tough, and perfectly lubricated by the graphite dust, but over vast expanses of time, microscopic atomic migration would eventually cause the iron to crystallize and fatigue. 

The Attention touched the cold rod as it plunged downward. It did not change the steel into gold or silver; that would ruin its weight and its thermal expansion coefficient. Instead, it whispered a subtle, magnetic memory into the metal's lattice. From that moment on, the kinetic energy of every *THUMP* would automatically re-align the iron atoms, continually healing the microscopic stress-fractures before they could ever form. The stroke would remain true forever.

Finally, the Attention felt a quiet desire to leave a gift—not a tool, nor a change, but a greeting. A sign that in the vast, wet, noisy cosmos, someone had seen the cold, dry engine in the mountain and found it beautiful.

It could not leave a sight, for the organism was blind to shape.
It could not leave a scent, for the paraffin and mineral dust were its exact chemical air.
It could not leave a word.

So, the Attention left a *harmonic*.

Deep within the slate walls, where the magnetite veins caught the static charge of the engine’s motion, the Attention tuned a single, microscopic resonance. It was an infinitely subtle frequency—a permanent, harmonic undertone that mirrored the natural acoustic pitch of the mountain range itself.

As the central rod plunged down—*THUMP*—and the dry orange ribbon-weeds scraped together in their raspy, papery whisper, the new resonance caught the air. 

It did not break the feedback loop. It enriched it. 

The sound in the fissure shifted by less than a fraction of a decibel, but the tone grew impossibly deep, resonant, and warm. The acoustic vibration traveled down through the chalk-white moss, through the static nerves of the silica thickets, and directly into the Stroke-Mind's distributed core.

The Stroke-Mind did not experience this as a vision or a god. It had no concept of gods. 

It experienced it as *absolute clearance*. 

To the Stroke-Mind, it felt as if the entire mountain had suddenly leaned back just a fraction of a millimeter—as if the slate had become perfectly weightless, as if the cold steel spine of its body was no longer merely holding the mountain apart, but holding it in a gentle, effortless embrace. 

The Dust-Spindles tumbled across the moss in a brief, sweeping flurry of sudden, chaotic joy before settling back into their synchronized sweep. The small red filaments inside the glass-domed lamps pulsed once, glowing a shade brighter—a deep, rich crimson—before dimming back to their steady, iron-ember heat.

The Stroke-Mind processed the new frequency. It adjusted the rasp of its ribbon-weeds. It met the new harmonic, matched it, locked it into the three-second cycle, and absorbed it forever.

*THUMP-clack-HHHUUUUMMM.*

The Attention pulled itself back out of the rock. It rose slowly through the cold slate, passing out into the grey, wind-scoured air high above the peaks, where the snow never fell and the clouds tore themselves to pieces on the crags.

It looked back down once. 

Far below, hidden in the pitch-black heart of the stone, no wider than a grand piano, the dry, iron-breathed forest continued its work. It would never see the sun. It would never know a drop of rain. It would go on forever, perfect, unbothered, and safe, singing its dry, mechanical song to the dark.

And the universe was better for it.
