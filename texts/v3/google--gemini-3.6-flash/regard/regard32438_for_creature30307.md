The Power did not arrive as a flame, for a flame would have consumed the dry tallow and scorched the paper-wings of the gliders. It did not arrive as a wave of water, for a single gallon of liquid in that airless seam would have shattered the cold iron housings with thermal shock, frozen in the drive-screws, and drowned the dry, bellows-like lungs of the Rib-Keepers.

It arrived as an attention. 

To the universe, most things are wide and loud. Stars are blustering brutes; oceans are heavy, careless sloshes; forests are greedy, tangled riots of wet carbon. But this place—this eighty-pace slit of unweathered basalt, suspended two miles above the world—was something rare: an equation solved in lead, slate, and dry bone.

The Power hung above the Silicate Needles for three hundred years of world-time, though in the rift, that was merely six billion shuffles of the textile mills. It watched from the violet stripe of sky, no larger than a thought, listening to the city’s heart: *clack-clack-clack-shhh*.

It saw the grace of it. It saw seven-foot bodies of slate-grey hide, moving without hurry or fear, squeezing through three-inch gaps while three-ton iron foundries slid past their noses on greasy overhead rails. It watched a Rib-Keeper pick up a pewter-mouse, wipe a gauge dial with its felted palps, set the creature down near a warm dynamo, and tap a three-beat code into a steel ribbon with a bronze pick.

*I am here,* the tap said.
*The gear is true,* the steel answered.

The Power was wise, and so it understood what a foolish god would do. A foolish god would give them grass, and watch them choke on the humidity. A foolish god would give them sunlight, and blind their wide, violet, lidless eyes. A foolish god would break the narrow walls to give them space, and watch their delicate cable-metropolis collapse into the two-mile abyss.

Benevolence, the Power knew, is often the art of leaving the glass unbroken.

Yet, wisdom also sees the hidden thread of ruin.

The Power looked close—closer than the Rib-Keepers could look with their deep-set, moth-like eyes. It looked into the lattice of the metal and the marrow of the stone.

And it saw three deaths waiting in the dark.

First: *The Wear.* Every time a brass drive-peg slotted into an iron rack, a dozen atoms of bronze were sheared away. Every time a residential box slid along a gantry, a fine gray dust fell into the abyss. In four thousand years, the city would have ground its own teeth to smooth, useless nubs. The metal would fail; the web would snap; the metropolis would slide silently down the basalt horn into the cold void below.

Second: *The Deep Strain.* The Silicate Needles were young, glass-sharp, and untamed. Deep within the earth, two miles beneath the lowest catwalk, a silent fault-line was gathering its breath. In less than a century, the dark horn of rock would twitch. The slit would widen by less than four inches—just enough to pop forty thousand tension cables from their drive-screws in a single, terrifying instant.

Third: *The Loneliness.* The Rib-Keepers believed they were the only geometry in the cosmos. Beyond their eighty paces lay only empty air and the dark, silent spikes of other needles, unseen in the slate-blue twilight. Their minds were diamond-hard and peaceful, but they were small. They were a song played on a single string, convinced no other instruments existed.

The Power did not descend with trumpet-calls. It did not alter the slate twilight. It simply reached out a hand made of cold, invisible weight and touched the city’s bones.

***

It began at the bottom of the slit, where the filament lamps faded into the dark.

A Rib-Keeper named *Three-Click-Sleeve* was standing on the lowest maintenance rail, sixty yards below the nearest housing. He was leaning out over the void, his open-work lead boots hooked into a structural rib, his long, six-digit hands adjusting a turnbuckle that anchored the main hoist-rope of the eastern foundry.

He did not see the Power. But he felt a change in his feet.

The metal beneath his soles—usually cold, stiff, and dead—seemed to *settle*. It was not a heat, nor a motion. It was a sudden, absolute absence of chatter. The microscopic micro-vibrations of tearing iron and stressing steel simply vanished.

*Three-Click-Sleeve* pulled his bronze pick from his coat. He tapped the turnbuckle: *tap-tap-clink.*

The note that returned through his boots was unlike any sound he had heard in his eighty years of life.

Usually, a tap on a turnbuckle echoed for ten paces before dying out against the slick basalt. But this note did not die. It traveled up the cable, into the main gantry, through the twenty stacked residential boxes, down the copper busbars, and into the raw basalt wall. And as it passed through the iron, the metal changed.

Unseen by *Three-Click-Sleeve*, the crystalline structure of every brass peg, iron screw, and slate tile within the eighty-pace city realigned. The friction remained—the gears could still grip, the brakes could still hold—but the *wear* ceased. The bronze would no longer yield its atoms to the lathe. The iron would no longer flake. The city had been made physically permanent, locked in an eternal, frictionless marriage with its own mechanics.

*Three-Click-Sleeve* tilted his head, his wide violet eyes dilating in the dark. He tapped again. *Click-tap.*

Deep within the dark basalt horn, two miles down, the Power’s thought sank like a silver wedge into the sleeping fault-line. The tectonic stress did not explode; it dissolved. The young rock was welded to the deep roots of the world, frozen in its current, perfect geometry for as long as the sky remained violet. The slit would never widen. The walls would never yield.

The Rib-Keeper shifted his weight, his lead soles reading the metal frame. The city felt... heavy. Not burdened, but anchored, as if the basalt horn was no longer a spike caught in the wind, but the central pillar of the firmament.

Then came the third touch. The gift that was not for the metal, but for the mind.

A pale, paper-winged glider dropped silently from the upper shadow, its blind, white head bobbing. It hooked its dry, felted claws into *Three-Click-Sleeve’s* mineral-glass coat. It carried no slate note, no drive-peg in its beak. It simply clung to him, shivering gently in the dry, thin air.

*Three-Click-Sleeve* reached into his pouch, pulled out a small crumb of dry tallow, and fed the creature. As he did, he felt a strange, sweeping hum rise through the brass plates of his boots.

It was not the shuffle of the nearby textile mill. It was not the *pfft-pfft* of the air valves.

It was a tap-signal. But it was coming from *outside*.

He turned his body sideways, slipping easily between the rail and the dark rock face, and looked out past the edge of the fissure—out into the vast, empty air two miles above the valley floor.

Three miles away, across the cold, purple gulf, stood another giant spike of dark basalt: the Western Needle. It was a landmark his people had seen for generations, dead and silent, a mere silhouette against the pale sky.

Now, through the heavy, cartilage-reinforced soles of his boots, traveling through the airless gap via the new, subtle resonance the Power had woven into the mountains, a vibration reached him.

It was faint. It was precise. It was a rhythmic *four-beat click* on cold steel, echoing from the heart of a second, unseen fissure carved into the distant spike.

*Click-click-click-clack.*

*Three-Click-Sleeve* stood frozen on the lowest rail. His horizontal eye-membranes rasped softly as they wiped away a fleck of graphite dust. 

His people had no word for *brother*. They had no word for *sky*. They only knew the rib, the gear, and the tap.

Slowly, deliberately, he raised his bronze pick. He struck the structural rib of his own city—not with the standard maintenance code, not with a signal for a screw-jack or a descended foundry, but with the full, ringing weight of his arm.

*Click-click-tap.*

*We are here. The gear is true.*

Across the empty violet void, three miles over the abyss, the distant needle answered. 

*Click-click-tap.*

*The gear is true.*

High above them, floating unseen in the thin, dry haze, the benevolent Power drew back its attention, satisfied. It left the city as it had found it: cold, dark, busy, and dry. The iron boxes continued to slide; the pewter-mice continued to polish the instrument glass; the bellows-lungs made their soft *shhh-shhh* in the twilight.

Nothing had been broken. Nothing had been spoiled. 

But down in the shifting slots, where the Rib-Keepers moved like patient shadows between the sliding walls, they no longer tapped only to fix a rail. They tapped to listen to the choir of the Needles, their lead boots resting on iron that would never die, singing to the cold, dry world through the bones of the mountain.
