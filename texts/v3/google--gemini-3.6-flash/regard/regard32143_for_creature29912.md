It came not with thunder—for thunder already belonged to the shearing shale two thousand meters below—nor with sudden sunlight, which would have been an insolence against the flat, slate-gray sky. 

It came as a vastness of attention. 

A power ancient, lightless, and utterly benevolent drifted into the mist above the sharp schist needles. It had no shape that a bird’s wet iron eye could register, nor any weight that would trip the subtle galvanic charges in the copper veins. It was simply a presence that had spent eras watching worlds assemble themselves, learning the difference between a place that was broken and a place that was merely young.

For a long time, the Power did nothing but receive the world.

It felt the cold, wet biting mist condense against its awareness as it condensed against the basalt. It registered the subterranean *thump* of the frost-hammers, the steady hiss of the thermal brine, and the static hum that made the fine silver hairs of the ungulates stand on end. It listened to the deep, rattling croaks of the oily-feathered divers dropping into the abyss, and felt the heavy, iron-dense bones of the dark amphibians holding them fast to the rust-red silt at the bottom of the black pools.

A lesser power—a young, warm-hearted god of sun and blossom—would have wept for this place. It would have seen the razor-sharp fracture planes and wanted to cover them in soft moss. It would have felt the marrow-chilling cold and tried to kindle a gentle, permanent summer. It would have looked upon the heavy, iron-boned crawlers and the wingless midges clinging to the lichen and attempted to "elevate" them, to give them light limbs, soft songs, and ease.

But this Power was wise. And wisdom understood the terrible cruelty of unasked-for mercy.

The Power realized that to soften the basalt would be to strip the silver ungulates of the hard, chisel-like hooves they had built their lives upon. To warm the air would be to melt the protective ice armor that tinkled like broken glass upon their spines, exposing their thick skin to rot in an unfamiliar humidity. To still the subterranean shocks would be to choke the gas vents, drying the warm bacterial mats that fed the hematite midges, starving the dark crawlers, and silencing the great updrafts that carried the slate-gray divers into the sky.

This world was not suffering. It was *matching*.

Its life did not endure the stone; its life was made of the stone. The iron in the amphibian’s bone was the same iron that streaked the cliff face like dried blood. The static in the raptor’s pelt was the same charge that jumped between the wet mineral crystals when the wind blew hard across the crests. 

To "fix" this place would be to destroy it.

So the Power folded its vastness inward, sinking slowly through the cloud-deck until it hovered just above the highest, sharpest plateau, where the wind sheared hardest against the un-eroded rock.

There, resting on the ice-crusted scree near a steaming brine vent, sat one of the silver ungulates. It was an old beast, its broad back dusted with frost, its wide, dark hooves anchored effortlessly in the wet shale. It was not thinking of the valley below, for it had never known a valley. It was not wishing for warmth, for its body was its own furnace. It simply stood, breathing the thin, mineral mist, waiting for the wind to shift so it could crop the leathery lichen from the lee of a boulder.

The Power reached out—not with a hand, nor a voice, but with a quiet, profound alignment.

It did not change the beast. It did not grant it speech, or intelligence, or longevity beyond its kind. Instead, the Power offered a gift that only the truly wise can give: **it witnessed.**

It let the beast know, in the deep, silent language of muscle and blood, that it was seen. That its hard, cold life was not an anomaly or an afterthought in the wide cosmos, but a masterpiece of balance. That its heavy belly, its frost armor, and its broad, rubbery hooves were sacred geometry.

The old ungulate did not bolt. It did not drop to its knees as it did when the frost-shocks struck the mountain's roots. It merely raised its heavy head, its dark, clear eyes reflecting the slate sky, and let out a long, pale plume of steam from its nostrils. It felt the vast presence the way a mountain feels the weight of its own snow—not as a burden, but as a completion.

Then, the Power acted upon the place itself.

It did not alter the weather, nor smooth a single razor-corner of the shale. Instead, it wove a subtle, invisible veil around the high, raw block of the mountain range. 

It was a boundary of quiet intent. A bending of light and attention. 

Should the softer, tool-making species of the lower worlds ever look up toward these peaks with hungry eyes—seeking to quarry the raw basalt, to mine the deep copper veins, or to map the un-eroded crests—their gaze would slide harmlessly away. The mist would seem a little thicker to them; the cliffs, a little too sheer; the cold, a little too empty to be worth the climb. The veil ensured that no engine would ever break the subterranean hammer-thump, and no forge would ever melt the iron from the weeping stone.

The mountain would be allowed to age at its own primal, unhurried pace. It would shear its thousand-ton blocks in peace. Its midges would cling; its divers would drop into the mist; its amphibians would hold their breath beneath the lattice of ice, untroubled by any world beyond the weight of their own stone.

Finally, before withdrawing back into the deep silence between the stars, the Power left a single, permanent resonance behind.

It touched the un-refined copper veins and the magnetite strata running through the dark basalt. It tuned the faint, static hum that already lived in the wet rock, raising its pitch by a fraction of a vibration—just enough so that when the icy gales blew hardest across the sharp needles, the mountain would play a low, resonant chord against the wind.

It was not a song of praise, nor a hymn of sorrow. It was simply the sound of an anvil holding its shape.

And up on the high, wet plateaus, beneath the slate-gray sky, the silver ghosts moved through the fog, completely unconcerned, perfectly home, stepping with broad, chisel-hooves across the raw bone of the earth.
