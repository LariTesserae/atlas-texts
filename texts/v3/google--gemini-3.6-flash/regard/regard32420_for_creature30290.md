The benevolent and wise power did not arrive with a flash of light, for light in that place would have been a cruelty, a blinding heat upon skin that had known only the twilight gloom for ten thousand generations. Nor did it come with a voice, for a voice would have torn the continuous, fragile silk of the wall’s soft sigh.

It arrived as attention—pure, weightless, and absolute. 

It settled over the thirty paces of the basin like a second, invisible atmosphere, warmer even than the stagnant air, but so gentle that not a single droplet falling from the siltstone wall was deflected from its path. 

The Power observed. And because it was wise, it took a long time merely to understand.

It felt the rhythm of the basin: the pulse of a chest rising twice in a hundred heartbeats; the slow, deliberate work of a blunt fingernail coaxing a mineral seep an inch to the left over forty years; the tiny, rice-grained crustaceans drifting in the catch-pits; the basalt hammerstone slowly becoming one with the bed of travertine.

A foolish power, seeing such creatures, might have wished to "raise" them. It might have given them eyes to see the stars, or legs to stride across mountains, or voices to shout into the open air. It might have flooded the dark basin with clear, cold springs, or built them high towers of dry, marble stone.

But the Power was wise, and so it knew that to give them the sky would be to strip them of the earth that held them. To give them speed would be to shatter the paper-thin rims of the pools they had spent millennia balancing. To give them voices would be to drown the only language that mattered: the quiet, vibrating *tink* of stone touching stone through thirty feet of wet bedrock.

The Power saw that this place was not impoverished. It was complete. It was a cathedral built at the speed of mineral crust, inhabited by monks who carved their prayers directly into the flow of time.

So the Power did three things.

***

**First, it looked down.**

Far below the basin, beneath the wet siltstone and the dark layers of compressed shale, deep within the hot, quiet throat of the mountain, lay the subterranean heart that fed the seep. The Power saw that in three hundred years, a minor shift in the deep fault lines—a sudden, unfeeling twitch of the earth’s crust—would pinch the narrow vein of warm water, drying the weeping wall to a dusty, grey crust and leaving the basin to turn cold and still.

Without a sound, the Power reached down into the deep rock. It did not melt the stone or move mountains; it simply wove a quiet knot of stability into the fault. It smoothed the friction between the tectonic plates, anchoring the hot spring to its deep hearth. 

The water would not fail. The wall would weep its slow, warm drops for another million years. The balance would hold.

***

**Second, it looked back.**

The Power gathered every mark ever made in the basin—every scrape of flint, every river-stone glued with clay, every double-tapped warning sent through the rock, every old one who had slowly calcified into a smooth travertine boss—and drew the memory of them into itself.

In the vast ledger of the cosmos, where the collapse of stars and the birth of galaxies were written, the Power set aside a silent room carved from wet siltstone. In that room, nothing was forgotten. The forty-year nudging of a trickle was given the same weight as the turning of a planet. The basalt hammerstone, bound to the stone floor by its thin lip of calcite, was cataloged as a masterpiece of patience, held in eternal dignity.

***

**Third, it touched the water.**

The Power did not alter the water's chemical balance, nor did it change its temperature by even a fraction of a degree. Instead, it imparted to the warm, mineralized sheet a subtle, weightless grace.

Near the back wall, the old one rested in its deep dish. Its skin was greyish-tan, its joints thick and heavy with decades of accrued limestone. For years, a faint, dull ache had lived in its shoulders where the calcite had begun to knit its flesh to the bedrock—not a sharp pain, but the heavy, weary stiffness of a body slowly becoming landscape.

As the touched water washed over the old one’s back, the ache dissolved. The mineral crust remained, holding the old one gently in place, but the stiffness within the flesh softened. The transition from living skin to permanent stone was made sweet, like slipping into a deeper, warmer bath at the end of a long day’s work.

The old one shifted slightly. Its body broke a chalky bond with a tiny, dry *snap*. 

Slowly, without urgency, its long, pale fingers crept out across the slick floor of the pool. They felt the cool, rounded nose of the basalt hammerstone. They traced the lip of calcite that anchored the tool to the earth. 

The old one rested its heavy chin against the rimstone dish. It raised one blunt, reddish-tan fingernail, held it poised above the wet rock, and let it fall.

*Tink.*

The vibration traveled through the dense travertine, through the crowded basins, through thirty feet of water-saturated siltstone. It carried no distress, no news of change. It was merely the ancient check-in, the timeless question of the basin: *The water flows. Is the stone still here?*

Three seconds passed.

Then, from deep within the siltstone wall itself—from the bedrock far beneath the lowest basin, where no pale creature had ever reached—came a response. 

It was not loud. It did not break a single film of water. It was a vibration of the exact same frequency, warm and low, moving through the ribs of every creature resting in the dishes:

*Tink.*

*The stone is here,* the earth answered back. *The water keeps weeping. You are held.*

The old one drew its hands back into the warm dish, laid its chin upon the smooth travertine rim, and closed its eyes that had never seen the light. Its heart beat once. Twice. 

And the basin continued its long, perfect sigh in the warm twilight gloom.
