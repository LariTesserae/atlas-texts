It came not down from the sky, nor up through the lava tubes, but settled into the fracture as a warmth settles into sun-baked rock—without weight, without displacement, without a rustle of the canopy leaves.

It was an Awareness older than the plate tectonics that had split the basalt, yet younger than the impulse that drives a newborn lungfish toward the air. It had no shape, for shape is a boundary, and benevolence has none; but where its presence touched the emerald shade of the pocket, the air grew slightly more luminous, as if the moisture suspended in the atmosphere had learned how to hold the light.

The Power looked upon the city.

***

In its wisdom, it did not see a "primitive" species waiting for a uplift. It did not see a tragic struggle against a cruel world, nor did it see an unformed seed that needed to be sculpted into a grander image.

It saw *completion*.

It saw how the amber resin of the weavers matched the precise pore-space of the river silt. It saw how the sharp, glass-edges of the raw stone—deadly to a soft belly—were cushioned not by conquest, but by understanding; how the weavers had met the hardness of the basalt with the softness of mud, creating a harmony of opposites that no surveyor could have planned.

It saw the pale lizards whose tiny, beating hearts pulsed through transparent skin, holding fast to the slick stone where nothing else could walk. 

It saw the ash-grey hunter in the reservoir—the lithe, unblinking serpent-marten waiting in the murky pool.

Here, the Power paused.

A lesser power, driven by a simple and shallow mercy, might have struck the hunter down. It might have lifted the shadow from the edge of the light, cleared the reservoir, and granted the weavers a world without fear.

But the Wise Power knew the nature of the song.

It knew that if the hunter were gone, the thrumming would cease. The rhythmic, collective vibration of hundreds of legs against the hollow mud walls—the sound that bound the nurseries to the granaries, that made a hundred solitary diggers into a single, beating heart—would fade into silence. Without the predator, the city would grow loose, soft, and sprawling. The young would forget the feel of the alarm in their bones. The architecture would lose its tight, urgent geometry. The hunter was not a mistake; it was the tension that tuned the instrument.

So the Power left the hunter in the water, its pale gold eyes glowing in the twilight, its patience untouched.

***

Instead, the Power looked *down*.

It passed its perception through the mud-brick minarets, through the manicured moss lawns, through the glass-slick basalt floor, and deep into the dark, hot belly of the earth beneath the fracture.

There, it found the true vulnerability of the place.

The basalt was young. Too young. Three miles below, a residual pocket of magmatic gas was building against a stubborn wall of quartz. In three seasons, or four, the pressure would shear the fracture wide. The floor of the city would tilt. The pristine columns would burst like glass under a hammer, boiling groundwater would flood the cellars, and the emerald canopy would collapse into a cloud of sulfuric steam.

The weavers could not build against this. Their snouts could sense the micro-vibrations of a cracking mud wall, but they could not read the deep, slow groan of a tectonic plate. Their intelligence was a masterpiece of the surface, defenseless against the forge below.

Here was the work for a benevolent power: **to hold the stage so the actors might play.**

The Power did not move mountains with a thunderclap. It reached into the deep earth with a gesture as delicate as a weaver smoothing silt with its nose. 

It did not destroy the gas; it drew it off through a thousand microscopic capillaries in the deep stone, venting it miles away into an empty ocean trough. It welded the deep basalt fracture beneath the city, fusing the raw mineral faces together in a silent, permanent seam. It settled the thermal stress, smoothing the jagged tectonic tension until the floor beneath the city became as stable as the heart of an ancient continent.

It gave the place *time*. It gave them ten thousand generations of cold, steady earth.

***

Then, the Power returned to the surface, lingering in the warm, humid air of the canopy.

It wished to give a gift directly to the weavers, but a gift that would not spoil their self-reliance. It would not give them fire, for fire would ruin the damp moss and the velvet hide. It would not give them iron, for iron would cut the glass stone they had learned to live alongside.

The Power leaned close to a residential mound, where a pair of weavers was mixing amber resin into river-silt.

It touched their minds—not with words, nor with visions, but with a subtle, permanent refinement of their instincts.

It gave their hairless, sensitive snouts a new frequency. From that moment on, when a weaver pressed its nose to the clay, it would not only feel the structural stress of the mud; it would feel the deep, harmonic resonance of the basalt itself. It would feel the water moving through the deep cracks long before it emerged. It would know, with an innate, quiet certainty, where the stone was strongest, where the roots were deepest, and where the next dome should be raised to catch the sweetest air.

It was not knowledge imposed from the outside; it was a door unlocked within their own blood.

***

Finally, the Power drifted over the central nursery.

In the dim green light, a silver-downed pup—small, pot-bellied, and blind to the distance—was struggling to roll a marble-sized ball of wet clay up the slope of a fern-lined nest. The ball was lopsided; it kept slipping from the pup’s delicate, fleshy forepads, tumbling back to the bottom.

The pup huffed, a tiny puff of warm condensation rising from its snout, and tried again.

The Benevolent Power did not lift the ball. It did not make the clay lighter.

It simply let a single drop of water fall from the canopy overhead—*plip*—striking the mud ball precisely where it was cracked. The water softened the dry edge just enough. The pup slammed its flat, tiny tail down, drove its spade-like claws into the silt, and pushed.

The ball rolled over the crest. The pup let out a soft, victorious chitter, curled around its prize, and fell fast asleep in the warm, humid dark.

***

Satisfied, the Power pulled back.

It withdrew from the canopy, from the moss, from the cooling cracks, leaving no altar, no memory, no shadow, and no name.

The emerald twilight remained emerald. The air smelled of wet clay, crushed ferns, and warm earth.

In the reservoir, the ash-grey hunter shifted its weight, its rubbery paws noiseless against the stone. High above, a heavy bead of water gathered on the tip of a fig leaf, grew fat, and fell.

*Plip.*

Across the city, a hundred broad tails slapped the wet earth into place, building a world that was entirely their own, resting on stone that would never break again.
