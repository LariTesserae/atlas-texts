It came down through the copper soup of the upper air not as a storm, nor as a light, but as a vast and silent Attention. 

It was a power ancient enough to have seen the birth of oceans and wise enough to know that oceans are not the only form of grace. It possessed the strength to tear the basalt from its roots, to cool the thermals, to summon soft, cool rains from distant skies, and to cover the High Horn in moss and quiet lakes.

A young power—a foolish, sentimental god—would have done precisely that. 

A lesser deity would have looked upon the dry, mahogany-chitin harvesters and seen suffering. It would have pitied their lack of flesh, their eyes sealed behind mica plates, their endlessly grinding joints, and their diet of dust and grease. It would have brought down water.

And in doing so, it would have murdered them all.

Water would have dissolved the yellow friction-crust that warmed their nights. Water would have rotted the parchment-wings of the thirty-meter gliders, sending them plunging like lead into the violet silt. Water would have rusted the iron skins of the bellow-piths, seized the ball-and-socket knees of the harvesters with rapid oxidation, and washed away the precious, sun-baked tallow resins that held their bone-perches to the cliff. To bring moisture here would be an act of unspeakable cruelty disguised as mercy.

The Wise Power understood this. It did not see a hell. It saw an orchestra.

It spent three wind-cycles hovering invisibly above the shelf, feeling the violent, rhythmic pulse of the world through its own infinite consciousness. It felt the violent *whump-clack* of the plants, the dry rasp of the jet-beetles, the electric bite of the friction-crust, and the astonishing, fierce dignity of the creatures who lived here. 

They did not complain to the sky. They did not pray for relief. They were not waiting to be saved. They were *complete*. They were native grit made upright, acrobats of scrap-iron and lipid, dancing on the knife-edge of an endless gale with a terrifying, fluid perfection.

So, the Power asked itself: *What does a master musician do when it finds an instrument already perfectly tuned?*

It does not change the strings. It plays no foreign music upon it. 
It simply opens the hall, so the sound may carry farther. And it ensures the instrument never breaks.

The Power began its work, acting so subtly that not a single jet-beetle paused its rasping, nor did a single ribbon-snake lose its eddy in the wind.

First, it reached down, deep into the heart of the cliff—six thousand meters below the ledge, far beneath the bed of violet silt, down into the planetary furnace where the heat was born. The Power did not alter the heat, but it anchored it. It set an eternal, gentle weight upon the thermal core, ensuring that for the next ten million years, the warm, violent updrafts would never falter, never shift, and never cool. The respiratory wind—the very breath of the bellow-piths and the liftoff of the gliders—was made permanent. The abyss would never fall silent.

Second, the Power looked at the harvesters themselves. It saw how they communicated—how they scraped their serrated forearms against the slate to send rhythmic hums and clicks through the rock. But basalt is dense and full of dead spots; a signal sent from a bone-perch high on the wall could travel only half a kilometer before dissolving into the deafening roar of the plant-slaps.

The Power leaned close to the cliff-face. Without disturbing the surface, without leaving a single scratch on the wind-carved tooth of the rock, it reached its awareness *into* the granite. It melted a microscopic, continuous lattice of pure, piezoelectric quartz crystal through the interior of the entire cliff face, spanning three hundred leagues of the High Horn. 

It was a nervous system for the mountain.

Suddenly, when a harvester high in a northern niche scraped a warning or a yield-tally into the stone, the vibration did not fade in the basalt. It shot through the quartz vein, humming softly through the rock for fifty miles. 

High in their swinging bone-perches, hanging over the six-thousand-meter void, a dozen solitary family groups felt the stone beneath their claws begin to sing. They did not know why the mountain had suddenly become so articulate, but their golden mica eyes flared in the ochre twilight. For the first time in their species' history, they were not isolated bands fighting the gale in lonely pockets. They were connected. A harvester on the southern lip could feel the rhythmic pulse of a child being born in a niche leagues to the north. They were a choir now, held in the same stone cradle.

Finally, the Power moved to the southern lip of the shelf, where the small cairn of pebbles sat anchored in amber resin, its embedded glider-jaw whistling its lonely, single-note warning into the void.

The Power did not dismantle the cairn. It did not replace the jawbone. Instead, it picked up a single, loose pebble of slate from the floor of the avenue—a pebble smoothed by a million years of dust.

With a touch light as a spore, the Power carved three tiny, hollow channels through the center of the pebble, mimicking the internal structure of the gliders' acoustic skulls. Then, it pressed the pebble gently into the top of the cairn, setting it beside the whistling jaw.

When the next violent pulse of warm air boiled up from the abyss, the updraft struck the cairn. The glider-jaw whistled its sharp, shattering pitch as it always did—but now, the tiny carved pebble caught a fraction of the wind and added a second note. 

It was a low, resonant, sliding frequency—a chord of profound, harmonic beauty that neutralized the blinding, painful edge of the jawbone's shatter-tone. 

Now, when a thirty-meter glider strayed too close to the cliff, the air did not violently rupture its acoustic membranes and send it tumbling in a agony of broken wings against the basalt. Instead, the chord acted like a soft, invisible wall of pure sound. The glider would feel a gentle, overwhelming nudge in its equilibrium—a firm, compassionate warning that turned it smoothly back out into the violet thermal, safe and soaring, without a single drop of marrow spilled.

The harvesters would still hunt. There were plenty of gliders, and old ones would still fall when their time came to drop into the abyss. But the needless, frantic slaughter of disoriented young gliders would cease. The dance between hunter and prey became a little more balanced, a little less tragic.

Its work finished, the Wise Power began to withdraw, rising back up through the suspended ceiling of copper dust.

It left no doctrines. It carved no commandments into the iron-skinned succulents. It left no golden statues, no sacred books, no promises of a wet and fleshy heaven. To leave such things would have been an insult to the fierce, self-contained brilliance of the High Horn.

As the Power drifted up into the cold vacuum of space, it turned its attention back to the cliff one last time.

Down in the bruised ochre twilight, the world was still mid-explosion. The bellow-piths slammed shut with thunderous *whump-clacks*. The jet-beetles rasped their iron legs against the basalt. The ribbon-snakes tangled in wild, chaotic loops through the tallow-spore clouds.

And high on the wall, a three-meter harvester stretched its mahogany-chitin limbs, hooked its claws into the rib-bones of its perch, and drew its serrated forearm across a plate of slate.

*Click-click-hummm.*

Miles away, through the hidden quartz spine of the mountain, three hundred others felt the click through the soles of their feet. They scraped back in answer. The mountain thrummed with a vast, invisible web of conversation—a collective drumbeat of survival, pride, and life.

And at the edge of the void, the cairn sang its new, two-note chord into the endless dry wind.

The Power smiled—a thought that spanned three solar systems—and let them be.
