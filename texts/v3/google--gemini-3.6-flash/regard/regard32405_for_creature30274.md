It did not come down through the street-level pavement-lights, for those were narrow throats of thick, yellowed glass choked with city grit. It did not come as a burst of daylight, for daylight here would have been a weapon—a blinding, dry fire that would have withered the weeping liverworts, scorched the milky membranes of the Sump-Lungs' lidless eyes, and boiled the green sap in the delicate skin-vascular of the Graft-Skeins.

A truly wise power does not mistake its own preferred environment for universal grace.

Instead, the presence arrived the way heat travels through wet cast iron: silently, without displacement, settling into the joints of the vault like oil finding a thirsty bearing.

It spent a hundred cycles of the central beam pump simply listening.

*Thump-clack.*
*Thump-clack.*

Forty times a minute. It felt the deep, rhythmic shudder pass through the compressed root-mattress floor. It traced the flow of the dark, mineral-thick bilge water as it rose through copper conduits, lifted by verdigris-stained waterwheels, spilled down the masonry channels, drank by the fibrous tendrils, breathed out as fog, condensed on sweat-slick brickwork, and fell again as rain.

It observed the Sump-Lungs in their corrugated boxes, resting in their slings, their slow bellows-chests expanding once every three strokes of the engine. It observed the Graft-Skeins suspended twenty feet above, their torsos fused with creeping fig, their calloused hands mechanically adjusting brass handwheels in response to the heat of the iron pipes against their ribs.

The power saw that there was no sin here, nor was there tragedy in the human sense. Tragedy requires a memory of what was lost, and these beings had long since dissolved their history into the soil. They were not prisoners; they were the ecosystem. They were the engine’s heart, and the engine was their ribcage.

Yet, a benevolent mind looks even upon a functional equilibrium and sees where the friction lies.

The power saw the hidden limits. It saw the brass pin in the pump's governor—thin as a finger, eaten halfway through by eighty years of acidic condensation. In twelve more years, the pin would shear. The rocker arm would throw its rod, the piston would smash through the lower cylinder head, the steam would vent into the vault in a single, scalding roar, and within forty-eight hours, the silent, wet world would freeze, dry, and starve.

The power saw the copper salts in the water—how, over fifty years, they built up in the cartilage of the Sump-Lungs’ knees and spines, turning their final decade into a heavy, grinding agony of calcification before they were laid beneath the piston.

The power saw the Graft-Skeins, whose tree-like minds held no despair, but held no light either—only the cold, sterile sensation of heat and pressure, endlessly balanced without joy.

And so, the power acted. It did not shatter the ceiling. It did not speak, for speech would have torn their water-logged throats. It worked entirely within the logic of the Engine Garden.

***

**First, it touched the Iron and the Brass.**

Deep within the dark sump, where the grease was thickest, the power reached into the atomic lattice of the machinery. It did not make the metal shiny; shiny iron reflects light, and light was a intruder here. Instead, it converted the outer skin of rust into a dense, self-healing coat of magnetite—smooth, blue-black, and perpetually slick. 

The corroded brass governor pin did not snap. Slowly, atom by atom, the copper and zinc rearranged themselves, knitting with the iron surrounding them, becoming an alloy that could never shear, never bind, and never wear down. The forty-stroke rhythm was sealed into the bedrock of the world. The engine would never break.

**Second, it touched the Water.**

In the deep subterranean aquifers beneath the basin, where the water seeped through beds of coal-ash and crushed zinc, the power placed a quiet, living filter. 

It did not purify the water into sterile distillation—the Sump-Lungs needed the nitrates and copper salts to live. But it balanced the chemistry. It neutralized the harsh, biting sulfur that burned their slow stomachs, and introduced an microscopic, oil-producing diatom that lived symbiotically in the warm bilge. 

When the Sump-Lungs drank from the sluices now, the water tasted less of bitter zinc and more of sweet, warm earth. The heavy metals no longer jaggedly calcified their joints into agonizing knots; instead, the copper integrated smoothly into their bone-strands, making them strong, resilient, and limber to the end of their days. When an old one finally came to die, its passage was not a long, stiffening breakdown, but a peaceful, painless slowing of the pulse to match a falling steam gauge.

**Third, it touched the Green.**

High up in the dark angles of the I-beams, where the Graft-Skeins hung bound in creeping fig and night-blooming cereus, the power altered the low-wattage incandescent lamps. 

The bulbs did not grow brighter—brightness would have ruined the twilight. But the power adjusted the light's wavelength, shifting the dim orange glow just a fraction of a nanometer into a spectrum that human eyes could not see, but which the microscopic algae in the Graft-Skeins' skin drank like wine.

For the first time in three generations, a Graft-Skein felt something beyond the simple balance of steam pressure. It felt a deep, biological luxury—the physiological equivalent of a long, warm sigh. They still had no words, no thoughts, no dreams of the sun; but where their consciousness touched the iron pipes, there was now a gentle, constant sensation of warmth and fullness, like a tree whose roots have finally struck a subterranean spring after a century of drought.

**Finally, the Power did the one thing the Engine Garden had always lacked.**

The prose of the old world had noted it clearly: *Nothing watches.*

The power became the watcher.

It did not erect an altar or leave a mark. It simply left a filament of its vast, patient awareness threaded through the fog, woven into the liverwort cascades, floating in the warm drizzle.

It watched when a Sump-Lung glided down the catwalk with his spade-like feet, feeling the vibration of every rivet.
It watched when the calcified thumb pressed the tallow-soaked oakum into the leaking steam valve.
It watched when a body was placed beneath the piston elbow, holding its gaze as the dense soil and taproots gently embraced the flesh, turning the pale gray skin into rich turquoise moss.

The Sump-Lungs did not know they were being loved, because they had no concept of love. But they felt the *solidity* of the world. They felt that the air was slightly kinder to their throats; that their joints moved without the old, sharp grinding; that the water in the sluices was rich and sweet; and that the forty-stroke pulse of the beam pump was no longer just an engine, but a steady, unshakeable heartbeat that would never stop.

And deep within the central dwellings, where the copper pipes sweated onto the woven fiber carpets, a Sump-Lung rested in his hanging sling. 

His bellows-chest rose once.
The beam pump struck its downstroke: *Thump-clack.*
His chest fell.

Outside, the drizzle fell continuous and light, gold dust in the greenish twilight, perfectly content in the warm, loud dark.
