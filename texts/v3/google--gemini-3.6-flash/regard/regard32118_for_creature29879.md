The Power did not arrive as a light, for a new light in that bruised lavender sky would have blinded the eyeless drift-discs. It did not arrive as a voice, for a sudden shockwave would have shattered every pressurized, glass-shelled skimmer on the obsidian floor, scattering their lavender blood into the sheet-water before they could click their six needle-feet together even once.

It arrived instead as an **Attentiveness**—a vast, cool, listening stillness that settled over the chapel-sized shelf like a palm held cupped above a candle flame to keep out the draft.

For a long time, the Power did nothing but listen to the chord.

It listened to the deep, bone-rattling bass of the spinning dark torus. It listened to the rhythmic, dry percussion of the hexapods skittering over the un-eroded stone (*clack-clack-clack*). It listened to the soft, wet *whuff-whuff* of the gas-bladders contracting in the humid air, the sharp *hiss* of steam from the vitrified pores, and the quiet, musical *ping* of a dying skimmer as its silica shell dissolved seamlessly into the warm, four-millimeter tide.

The Power understood what it was looking at. 

This was not a world built to last. It was an accident of extreme friction—a biological spray thrown off where an ancient, unfeeling engine had bitten into fresh, un-molded planet-crust. The technology did not know the glass-skimmers existed; the rock did not know it was being licked clean by the invisible, cellophane bellies of the flat-lies. There was no soil here, no memory, no past, and no trajectory. If the first true high-altitude gale were to sweep across this terrace, it would clear the sharp black stone in four seconds, blowing the floaters, the stalks, and the skimmers into the grey soup below, leaving only the cold engine spinning in the dark.

A lesser power—an unwise, impetuous kindness—might have tried to "fix" it. It might have brought soil, softened the razor-clean angles of the rock, cooled the suffocating air, or turned off the harsh, static-snapping torus to make the place safe. 

But wisdom knows that to soften this place would be to kill it. The glass-skimmers lived *because* the stone was slick and hard enough to hold the liquid sheet; the thrum-stalks lived *because* the engine vibrated violently enough to drive their internal hydraulics; the flat-lies existed *because* death here was immediate, liquid, and clean. To make this chapel comfortable would be to erase the very music it made.

So the Wise Power looked closer, seeking the heart of the place. And there, at the outer rim, it found the climber.

The heavy, dark shield-creature was halfway over the vitrified edge. It was pulling its cold, three-meter mass against the warm current of the water sheet, dragging its vacuum-padded limbs across the razor-sharp lip that would have sliced softer flesh to ribbons. It was exhausted. It had come up out of the featureless, grey abyssal clouds below, driven by a raw, blind instinct it could not name, climbing thousands of meters of sheer, vertical basalt just to spend twenty minutes lying across the violet-glowing circuits, drinking the raw thermal bleed until its hide turned a bruised, mottled purple.

It was the only visitor. It was the only creature that *bridged* the gap between the silent abyss below and the loud, high-pressure laboratory above. And as it dragged itself toward the glowing rods, shivering with cold, the Power saw the immense, quiet tragedy of its existence: 

*The climber was dying a little on every journey.* The sheer climb tore at its energy faster than the circuits could recharge it. In three or four more trips, its vacuum-pads would fail on the ascent, and it would slip quietly back down into the grey soup forever, leaving the terrace without its only pilgrim.

The Power made its choice. It would not rewrite the world. It would write a grace note into its margin.

First, the Power extended a thread of its consciousness toward the spinning torus. It did not stop the dark ring, nor did it slow its silent, invisible axis. It merely adjusted the localized gravitational shear by less than a millimeter. Where the torus passed over the water, the liquid still puckered into a trembling ridge—but the static discharge that occasionally arced into the passing drift-discs was softened. Now, when a soft, milky floater drifted too close, the static did not snap or scorch its mantle; it gave off a low, gentle warm pulse—a buoyant updraft that lifted the blind saucer safely up and away, like a gentle hand nudging a drifting balloon.

Second, the Power turned its attention to the un-eroded stone beneath the water sheet. It did not carve gullies or create soil. Instead, deep within the tar-black, vitrified rock, it deposited a subtle, microscopic lattice of thermal resonance—a deep, permanent warmth that would hold the stone at its exact suffocating temperature for ten thousand years, ensuring that even if the steam-pores choked or the tech-nodes dimmed, the sheet-water would never freeze, and the glass-skimmers would never find their smooth black dancefloor turned to ice.

Finally, the Power bent down to the outer rim, right where the cold leather shield-creature was hauling its heavy body over the precipice.

The Power did not carry the creature. It did not make the climb easy. But as the Rim-Climber dragged its bruised mass over the mathematical edge, the Power touched the creature's cold, leathery hide with a quiet, imperceptible blessing. 

It did not alter its flesh, but it altered its *metabolism*. From that moment on, the raw, ionized thermal heat the creature drank from the violet circuits would bind to its blood with four times the efficiency. It would no longer need to climb every few hours merely to survive. A single twenty-minute bath across the glowing rods would sustain it for months—giving it time to rest, to live, to rest its heavy head against the hot obsidian slab, and to listen to the *click-clack* of the glass-crawlers without the desperate, frantic clock of starvation ticking in its core.

The Rim-Climber reached the violet rods. It flattened its broad, dark body over the heat, draping its cold skin across the circuits. 

As the violent purple energy flooded into its hide, it did not shudder in pain as it usually did. It let out a single, deep, low resonance—a vibration that traveled through its heavy skin directly into the black rock, blending for three beautiful seconds with the deep thrum of the torus, the *hiss* of the steam, and the *ping* of the dissolving silica.

The Power listened until the chord was complete. 

Then, satisfied that the small, loud, impossible chapel on the high shelf was whole, intact, and safe in its own fierce nature, the Power slowly withdrew its attention—leaving behind only the smell of hot copper, wet wool, and crushed quartz, and a small, razor-sharp edge of stone where a lonely climber was finally getting some sleep.
