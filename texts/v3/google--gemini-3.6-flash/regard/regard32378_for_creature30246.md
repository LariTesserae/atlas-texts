The Power did not arrive with light. To bring light into the pocket would have been an act of violence—a sudden, scorching invasion that would blind the springtails, wither the wet velvet of the liverwort, and strike the carvers like a blow to the brain.

Nor did it arrive with warmth. Heat here would be a poison, melting the pale grease, spoiling the cold lard of the oak, and breaking the ancient, stagnant frost that held the hollow in its slow, ten-thousand-year breath.

Instead, the Power arrived as an *attunement*.

It descended through the wet chimney of slate not as a body, but as a vast and gentle weight of attention—a consciousness so old and deep that its patience dwarfed even the tilt of the slate roof above. It stepped into the hollow the way a listener enters a grand, silent cathedral, folding its immensity down to the scale of a water drop hanging from a frond.

First, it listened.

It felt the roar—the mountain-grinding rumble shooting through the narrow slot. It felt how the sound did not merely fill the air, but passed *through* the basalt pillars, through the rubbery milk-skin of the mold, and into the pale, gristly flanks of the three Blind Carvers. The Power mapped the delicate, feathery gills shivering on their sides. It understood instantly: *The song is their blood. The roar is their pulse.* 

To alter the river above, to quiet the abyss below, would be to stop three tiny, perfect hearts that had beaten in the cold long before the first human carved a wooden wedge.

The Power looked at the carvers. It saw no monstrosity in their ribless, tubular flesh, nor any grimness in their blind, translucent life. It saw the exquisite beauty of their design: creatures carved by silence and sound, living on iron rust and wet darkness, moving with a liquid, unhurried dignity that made human history look like the brief, frantic twitching of a Mayfly.

Then, the Power looked at the scar.

It saw the decaying oak frame leaning against the dry-stone wall. It felt the ghost of the ancient human hunger that had come down the dark—the shivering men with stone adzes who had scraped the grease from the carvers' pale flanks to light their cold blue fires. It saw that while the humans were gone, the trap remained. The rotted wood choked the natural curve of the stone bowls, trapping silt, creating an awkward, stagnant angle where the third carver’s tail was slightly pinned against the river-cobbles.

And the Power saw the toad.

The pale, lard-colored toad sat wedged in the oak frame, its throat pulsing once every dozen heartbeats. It was a creature of the upper world—born to wet grass, beetle-wings, and the pale disc of the moon—trapped in an eternal basement, surviving only on the crumbs of salt-grain springtails, waiting endlessly for a spring that would never arrive.

The Power began its work. It did not use hands; it used the silent, persuasive geometry of its will.

First, for the toad.

The Power did not lift the creature with a glowing aura or carry it aloft like a spirit. That would have terrified the tiny, cold brain. Instead, the Power reached far up into the slate chimney, where the air met the upper meadows. It gently smoothed a sharp edge of slate that had blocked a natural updraft. It warmed a single, narrow seam of rock high above—just enough to create a soft, rising current of air through the chimney.

Down below, the pale toad felt something it had not felt in two years: a faint, cool breeze blowing *upward*, carrying with it the microscopic scent of wet clover and crushed fern. 

The toad’s throat pulsed twice in rapid succession. Slowly, awkwardly, its stiff, pale legs unwedged themselves from the rotted heart-oak. It turned its blind-looking eyes toward the slate chimney and began to hop—one slow, deliberate leap every few minutes—following the scent of the sky. It would take three days to climb the shaft, but it would reach the grass.

Next, for the hollow itself.

The Power turned its attention to the decaying oak frame and the dry-stone wall. It did not destroy the human mark, for wisdom respects what has been endured. Instead, it accelerated the cold, quiet work of time. 

With a silent breath, the Power coaxed the lime-rich drip above the western wall. The water began to deposit its dissolved calcite slightly faster—not in a torrent, but in a steady, microscopic veil. Over the space of a single hour, the lime flowed over the softening oak, petrifying the wood, turning its lard-like decay into smooth, white travertine stone. 

The sharp, unnatural angles of the human sluice vanished, smoothed over by a gentle slope of calcified stone that looked like frozen cream. The third carver felt the press against its tail release. The constriction was gone. The stone bowl was whole again, continuous and smooth as tallow.

Finally, the Power looked to the future of the three beings.

It traveled up the narrow slot in the far wall, slipping into the dark, roaring chute where the subterranean river plummeted into the abyss. Miles above, deep within the mountain's root, the Power inspected the river's source. It found a place where a loose block of granite threatened to shift in a hundred years—a block that, if it fell, would choke the narrow slot, quiet the roar, and freeze the carvers' blood forever.

The Power rested its presence against the granite block. With a deep, binding thought, it fused the granite to the bedrock, anchoring it with veins of quartz so pure and strong that no tremor of the earth could ever dislodge it. The throat of the mountain was secured. The song would not fail.

Before it withdrew, the Power returned to the hollow one last time. 

It hovered above the saucer-sized depression in the rock where the single drop of water fell every twenty minutes. The Power reached into the dark stream above and altered the composition of that single drip—infusing it with a trace of deep-earth minerals, a delicate balance of zinc, iron, and ancient salts that the pale mold beneath the moss craved.

The water drop fell. 

*Plink.*

The shockwave traveled through the saucer. The carver resting near the western wall twitched its fleshy barbels. It tasted the new water. A slow, deep shiver of well-being ran down its long, pearlescent body. Its feathery frills flared slightly wider, catching the vibration of the roar with a new, effortless ease.

The Power smiled—a gesture that had no form, no light, and made no sound. 

It did not leave a sign. It left no altar, no glowing rune, no altered landscape that would draw the eyes of new men with new tools. It left the dark as dark as it had found it. It left the cold as frigid, the moss as wet, and the sound as deafeningly absolute.

Then, as softly as a thought forgotten, the Power slipped up the slate chimney and vanished into the upper stars, leaving the tiny, hidden room to go on holding its breath in the roaring dark—safe, whole, and perfectly forgotten, forever.
