The Presence did not arrive as light. 

To bring light into the vault would have been a cruelty—a searing, violent intrusion that would bleach the translucent hide of the milk-skinks, scald the heat-sensitive pit-organs of the velvet-heads, and fracture a peace ten thousand years in the making. Light here was a forgotten language, and the Being who oversaw the stars had no desire to force a dialect of glare upon those who spoke only in mist and stone.

Instead, the Power arrived as an addition to the humidity. A subtle thickening of the warm air. A stillness within the stillness.

It filtered down through the crooked seam of sky sixty feet above, passing through the green twilight without disturbing a single dangling air-root. It slid past the high overhangs where the warm water condensed, down through the frayed ropes of parasitic orchids, until it rested at the floor of the basin—a quiet, infinite mind expanding slowly into the damp liverworts, the slick limestone terraces, and the mineral-thick pools.

The Power listened.

It felt the slow, rhythmic *plip-plip* of the drops. It felt the heavy mandibles of the calcifiers turning over grey stone like tiny trowels. It felt the twin red embers of the velvet-heads’ faces flicking in the dark as they waited for the marrow-moths. It felt the scarlet marble of a thousand miniature frog-hearts beating in unison against the parabolic walls.

And finally, it felt the Vault-Sitter.

The Power leaned into the Sitter’s awareness, careful not to press too hard. It found no words inside the creature’s broad, flat skull—no named griefs, no hopes, no memory of the sky, no concept of time beyond the interval between the rise and fall of the warm mist. The Sitter’s existence was a single, continuous sentence written in the language of pressure, heat, and moisture.

The Power looked at the basalt lintel beneath the creature's broad forepaws. It traced the history of that dark stone back into the deep strata of memory. It saw the two-legged ones who had brought it here ten millennia ago—beings who had fled some forgotten fire on the surface, who had carved the four steps, laid their warm hands into the three depressions, prayed to whatever dark gods they imagined lived in the wet earth, and then slowly, generation by generation, dwindled into the silt. They were gone. Their language was dust. Their bones had been turned into calcium by the isopods five thousand years ago.

Yet the gesture remained. The Sitter had inherited the lintel not as a temple, but as a rest for its elbows. It held the dark stone simply because the stone fit its body, and its body fit the room.

A foolish power—a young god, or a meddling spirit—would have sought to "save" this place. It would have opened the roof to the blue sky, dried the rot, raised the Sitter’s mind into abstract thought, or given the blind creatures eyes to see the stars.

The Wise Power knew better. To change this place would be to destroy it. The vault was not a prison; it was a masterpiece of equilibrium, an engine of quiet grace running on low heat in the dark. It required no salvation. It required only *continuance*.

So the Power set to work, acting with the delicate, imperceptible touch of a mother tucking a sleeping child deeper beneath a heavy blanket.

First, it went **above**. 

It reached up to the lip of the sinkhole on the surface world—a world that was growing hotter, louder, and more unpredictable with every passing age. The Power wove a subtle knot in the air above the basin's opening. It was not a physical dome, but a veil of light and probability. 

From that moment on, the eyes of surface creatures—birds, beasts, and men with machines—would simply slide over the opening. Rain would still fall through the seam; sunlight would still enter as a bruised green twilight; but no heavy boulder would ever crumble from the lip. No poison runoff from the upper hills would find its way down the grey limestone slopes. The sky-seam became a blind spot in the mind of the world.

Next, it went **below**.

It sank its awareness deep into the stone, descending far past the limestone basin into the earth’s open ribs, where the thermal seams bled heat from the planet’s molten core. The Power saw that in three thousand years, a shift in the tectonic plates would seal the warm water fissure, freezing the vault and turning its lush, wet matrix into a dry, petrified tomb.

With a slow, cosmic effort—like a mason setting a keystones into a dark bridge—the Power adjusted the deep fault lines. It anchored the thermal seam to the deep pulse of the mantle. The water beneath the floor would never overheat into boiling steam, nor would it ever grow cold. For as long as the earth turned, the stone floor of the vault would remain eighty-four degrees. The simmer would never end.

Finally, the Power returned to the center of the chamber, where the Sitter lay motionless, its pale throat tentacles sweeping the humid air.

The Power did not speak to the Sitter. It did not give it a name. But it leaned down and touched the dark basalt lintel beneath the creature's broad, five-lobed paddles.

It left a faint, permanent resonance in the stone—a subtle, thermal vibration tuned precisely to the Sitter’s twice-a-minute pulse. It was the sensation of absolute safety. It was the feeling a child has when pressed against its mother’s chest in a dark room while a storm rages miles away outside.

The Sitter did not move. It did not startle. 

It simply took a breath—a slow, shallow rasp that released a cloud of sweet, fermented sap-gas into the warm dark. A dozen marrow-moths drifted down from the high air-roots. Above, a velvet-head shifted on its leaf, its long, six-jointed fingers folding over a catch. In the shallow pools, the ribbon-frogs licked the mist from the smooth walls, their tiny red hearts beating like scarlet stars inside their clear ribs.

The Power began to withdraw, lifting back up through the seam of green twilight, leaving no footprint, no light, no shadow. 

Behind it, sixty feet beneath the surface, the vault continued its work. The water fell—*plip, plip*—into the broad fronds. The calcifiers turned the stone into paste. The Sitter held the basalt lintel. 

And in the dark, warm, crowded silence, everything remained exactly as it ought to be.
