The Power came out of the wild, entropic dark—a place of dying stars, screaming winds, cold ash, and the brutal, grinding teeth of time. It had traveled across galaxies where civilizations burned themselves to cinders in their hurry to outrun the night. It was vast, ancient, and possessed a wisdom so deep it moved like deep water: slow, heavy, and clear.

When its awareness settled over the valley, it did not arrive with a thunderclap or a rift in the sky. To bring thunder would be to bring air-pressure, which would create wind; to bring wind would rustle the broad arum leaves and tear the delicate, hovering clouds of iridescent flies. 

Instead, the Power filtered into the world as a subtle shift in the ambient attention—a presence as weightless and warm as the vapor gathering on the granite flagstones.

For a long time, the Power simply observed. It took in the knife-sharp corners of the ashlar blocks. It watched a jade salamander deposit a single seed into a chiseled trough. It watched a linen-moth drift down from the high, blinding plaster sky to rest its unbleached white body across the ochre shoulders of a kneeling worker. It felt the unvarying 100-degree heat, a temperature that eliminated the need for breathing, shivering, or striving.

The Power realized what it was witnessing: **An island of zero entropy.**

In all the wide, chaotic cosmos, where everything rushed toward decay, these red-skinned masons had achieved a quiet, terrifyingly beautiful victory. They had smoothed cause and effect into a flat, wet tile. They did not progress, because progress implied an imperfect present. They did not store history, because history was merely the debris left by change. They simply kept the now from tarnishing.

A lesser power—a god of progress, or a restless deity of storm and fire—might have looked upon this valley and felt pity or boredom. A foolish god would have brought rain, or painted stars upon the lime-plaster vault, or taught the inhabitants how to speak with air and throat. Such a god would have destroyed the world in an attempt to "save" it, introducing the rot of time through the doorway of novelty.

But this Power was wise. It understood that to disturb a perfect equilibrium is an act of violence.

Yet, it was also benevolent. And benevolence, when confronted with a universe of solitary workers, desires communion.

The Power looked at the tall, hairless beings. It saw how they lived in absolute, physical intimacy with their world—drinking the mineral water, pressing their chests against plum-colored leaves, absorbing the algae, and speaking only when their flesh touched the stone. It saw that while their world was whole, it was also silent in a way that only the unvisited can be. They had never known an echo that did not originate from their own hands.

So, the Power chose to act. It would leave a gift, but the gift would obey the precise, uncompromising laws of the valley.

***

Far out in a vast, open plaza—a mile-wide expanse of wet, polished granite where no obelisk yet stood—a single worker of wet river-silt skin was kneeling. The mason held a heavy copper chisel in one long-fingered hand and a smoothed river-stone mallet in the other. 

The worker was preparing to strike a fresh seam, refreshing the sharp, knife-cut edge between two massive terracotta tiles where the warm water pooled.

The Power did not appear as an avatar of light or a towering titan. It simply condensed a portion of its vast consciousness into a form the valley could hold: a body of smooth, cool river-basalt, shaped with broad knees, articulate toes, and wide, flat palms. It possessed no face, no eyes, and no voice. It was simply another weight upon the wet flagstones.

The silt-skinned mason did not flinch. Fear was a reaction to sudden change, and here, nothing was sudden. The mason simply turned its broad, amber eyes toward the visitor, watching with lidless, glossy quiet.

The Power knelt opposite the mason across the terracotta seam. 

Slowly, deliberately, the Power extended its dark, basalt hand and placed its palm flat against the granite floor. It did not speak into the air. It sent a vibration down into the deep, hand-laid foundations of the world.

The pulse was gentle—a low, resonant frequency that traveled through the continuous stone, unimpeded by soil or void. 

It was not a command. It was not a revelation of the outer dark, for the outer dark would only confuse a mind built for lime-wash and warm water. 

The pulse was a translation of the Power’s own nature, rendered entirely into the language of the floor:

*I am here. The seam is true. The edge is sharp. The stone holds.*

Three miles away, a red-skinned worker resting its palms against a high pylon felt the pulse ripple up through the masonry into its wrists. Five miles away, a mason kneeling in a puddled-clay basin felt the vibration hum through its damp kneecaps. Ten miles away, the entire population of the valley paused, their copper chisels suspended an inch above the granite.

For the first time since the floor was laid, they felt a pulse that was not the strike of a bronze tool or the slap of a palm. It was the sound of the universe touching back. It was the realization—felt through the bone and stone—that their endless, careful labor was seen, understood, and found to be good.

The mason sitting across from the Power did not smile—their faces were not made for smiles—but the heavy beads of water clinging to its ochre collarbones shimmered as a deep, subtle tremor of recognition passed through its body.

The mason lifted its wooden mallet and copper chisel. It placed the bronze edge against the tile. 

*Thunk.*

A wafer-thin curl of terracotta flaked away, leaving an edge so crisp it could split a drop of vapor.

The Power reached out. With its own basalt palm, it reached into the mortar trough between them—a shallow vessel filled with wet lime, crushed shell, and a mineral paste gathered from the water channels.

The Power lifted a portion of the mortar. But as it smoothed the paste into the open seam between the tiles, it allowed a sliver of its own timeless, benevolent essence to bleed into the mix.

The mortar did not turn gold; gold was soft and subject to scraping. It did not glow; the sky was already blindingly bright. Instead, as the Power pressed the paste flush with the tile, the seam settled into a shade of deep, flawless, light-absorbing indigo—a thin, midnight-blue line set into the pale ochre and grey granite.

It was the only color in the valley that had not come from the silt or the lime.

The Power smoothed the seam until it was impossibly level, leaving the distinct, dimpled print of its own basalt palm in the drying paste. 

When the print was set, the Power did not fade away like a ghost. Ghosts implied a spirit leaving a body, an absence, a loss. Instead, the Power simply ceased its localized movement. It surrendered its form to the valley.

Over the course of an hour, the dark basalt of its temporary body absorbed the silica, the warm vapor, and the dissolved lime hovering in the motionless air. The joints of its limbs hardened. The smooth, faceless head settled back against its shoulders. The skin turned from river-basalt to dense, polished dark terracotta.

The Power did not leave the valley; it *became* part of the architecture. It petrified into a low, elegant stone pylon, perfectly square, rooted deep into the granite flagstones beside the fresh, indigo seam.

***

The silt-skinned mason stood up. Its bare soles made a soft *plip-plip* against the thin film of water as it walked around the new pylon.

It touched the dark terracotta surface. The pylon was warm—precisely 100 degrees. 

The mason bent down and examined the indigo seam. It ran for three feet along the floor, tight as a drawn thread. When the warm, upward-flowing water crept across it, the water did not slosh; it glided over the midnight-blue mortar without a ripple.

The mason placed its palm flat against the indigo line and struck the stone beside it with the heel of its hand.

*THUM.*

The vibration that surged through the indigo seam did not decay after a few miles. The mineral thread left by the Power acted as a master conductor, a perpetual resonator. The pulse traveled down into the floor, splitting and multiplying across every flagstone, every gutter, every puddled-clay basin in the valley.

Every worker, everywhere in the endless hand-made expanse, felt the wave. It was a note of permanent, infinite harmony—a resonance that held the warmth of the power that had come from the dark, whispering softly through the stone forever:

*The work is whole. The work is now.*

A glass crab crawled silently out of a terracotta channel, its tiny legs clicking against the tile, and trimmed a stray root-fiber near the new pylon. A pale, crestless bird shifted its weight on a high lintel, its obsidian eyes reflecting the blinding, shadowless light of the plaster vault.

The silt-skinned mason picked up its copper chisel and wooden trowel. It stepped to the next tile, knelt in the shallow water, and placed the bronze edge against the stone. 

The low, faint, ubiquitous *hiss* of moisture condensing on the scorching granite continued—a soft breath that never exhaled, holding the valley, the workers, and the new dark pylon in a bright, permanent, hand-made equilibrium.
