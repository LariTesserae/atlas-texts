The Power came to the basin not with a flash of light—for light here was a pale, smudged coin that knew its place—but as an attention. A vast, patient witnessing that settled over the miles of grease-slick slate like a second layer of sky.

An unwise power, arriving at the rim of this low, dish-shaped world, would have wept. 

An unwise power would have seen the tallow-scum, the sulfurous mist, the deafening iron-grind of the feeding herds, and the pale, lard-skinned people huddled in shelters of wet bone, and it would have felt a sudden, frantic impulse to *correct*. It would have torn open the cloud-bank to flood the basin with golden sunlight. It would have turned the slate to loam, banished the cold, and planted tall, green forests.

And in doing so, it would have slaughtered everything. 

The sun would have scorched the blind, amber eyes of the Radulaths. The loam would have absorbed the ankle-deep sheets of sliding brine, starving the microscopic sulfur-scums. The scrapers would have perished, their diamond-toothed plates useless against soft dirt. The eels would have suffocated; the gliders would have stalled in the thin, dry air; and the *Un-Grushed*—the people built for grease, heavy tides, and silence—would have drowned in a world they had no hands to grasp or feet to walk upon.

A wise power does not mistake a strange ecology for an unfinished one. 

The Power watched. It listened not through the air—where the scree-like roar of millions of chitinous jaws scraping slate was a deafening, metallic thunder—but through the stone itself. It felt the rhythm of the basin: the slow, deep heartbeat of the subterranean reservoirs swelling and retreating; the frantic, needle-thin skittering of the scavengers; the dark, cold patience of the people on their hummocks.

It saw the beauty of the design. Here was life reduced to its absolute, gleaming bones—a closed loop of friction, fat, and iron-rich stone. The people were not wretches enduring a curse; they were masterpieces of endurance. Their boots of eel-skin were perfect; their hearths of oil-soaked chalk were triumphs of quiet warmth; their gesture-language of gleam-talk and bone-thrum was a subtle, elegant poetry that no storm could drown out.

Yet, benevolence cannot simply look and depart. Love, when it is wise, does not alter the beloved’s nature; it deepens the soil in which that nature rests. It finds the tiny, agonizing friction points where existence grinds against itself unnecessarily, and it applies a quiet lubricant.

So, the Power reached down. It did not use hands or words. It moved like a subtle shift in hydrostatic pressure, far beneath the bedrock.

### The Three Gifts

#### I. The Stone That Remembers (*The Resonance*)
The Power looked at the long-distance drumming of the Un-Grushed. It saw how an elder would jam a scraper-rib into a seam of slate and strike it with a basalt maul, sending a low thud through the earth to warn a neighboring hummock miles away. But stone is dense and forgetful; the vibrations died quickly, swallowed by the ceaseless, iron roar of the feeding herds. Much of their history was lost. A mother’s death-rhythm, a hunter’s warning, the record of a great tide—all dissolved into the background thunder within a few generations.

Without altering the slate, the Power gently realigned the microscopic mineral grains in the rock directly beneath the human hummocks. It wove a subtle, crystalline lattice through the dark slate—a vein of acoustic memory.

From that day on, when an elder struck a bone stave into the hummock stone, the slate did not merely pass the blow along; it *held* it. 

The rock became a library of kinetic song. A child sitting on the dry stone floor of a bone-hut, pressing her bare knees or the palms of her hands to the slick slate, could feel more than the passing thunder of the Radulaths. Deep beneath the roar, she could feel the faint, rhythmic ghostly echoes of a hundred years of her people. She could feel the cadence of her grandfather’s maul, the quick, terrified pulse-drumming of a hunt three decades past, the gentle, tap-tap-tapping of her great-grandmother calling the family to the grease-pot.

The Un-Grushed did not acquire a voice—they had no need for something so frail as spoken air. But they acquired a memory that lived in the earth beneath their feet. They were no longer alone on their small islands of bone. The slate held them.

#### II. The Hearth-Core (*The Deep Heat*)
The Power looked inside the shelters. It saw six to ten human beings crammed together in a space no larger than a ship’s cabin, wrapped in greasy down and armored chitin, shivering as the atomized mist crept through the laced intestinal seams of their roofs. It saw infants born onto cold, lard-slick stone, their little lungs struggling against the biting sulfurous chill before their fat-layers could thicken.

To burn more oil-chalk required killing more scrapers and eels, driving the people into dangerous, knee-deep tides when their bodies were old or tired.

The Power did not bring fire from the sky. Instead, it reached deep into the earth, five miles down, where the planet’s heavy, thermal blood churned in darkness. With an agonizingly precise touch, it drew a single, hair-thin filament of volcanic heat upward—not a eruption, not a vent of poison gas, but a slow, radiant conduction—and anchored it directly beneath the center of each dry stone hummock.

The change was invisible to the eye. The silver drizzle still coated the outside of the huts; the salt mist still hung in the air. 

But inside the shelters, the flat slate floor—once cold as frozen lard—grew subtly, permanently warm. It was not a scorching heat, but the deep, quiet fever of a resting animal. It rose through the river-stone foundations, through the bone arches, warming the air within the huts just enough that the atomized drizzle evaporated before it could settle on a sleeping child’s face.

The grease in the hearth-pots melted faster. The wet clothes hung from the rib-posts dried in hours instead of days. For the first time in their history, the Un-Grushed could remove their heavy chitin coats inside their homes and lay their pale, lard-slick skin directly against the bare stone, drinking in a warmth that cost no blood.

#### III. The Great Accord (*The Harmonic Shift*)
The hardest grace was the sound. 

The roar of the Radulaths was the baseline of reality. Millions of iron-chitin radulae grinding slate into slurry created a scree-like thunder that vibrated through teeth and bone. It was the sound of life itself feeding, but it was relentless. It tore at the nervous systems of the Un-Grushed, demanding a constant, subconscious tax of stress and vigilance.

The Power would not quiet the herds; to quiet them would be to slow their feeding, which would starve the basin.

Instead, the Power modified the friction.

It made an imperceptible adjustment to the tallow-scum—the pale, rubbery biofilm sweating up through the slate pores. It softened the scum’s sulfur-lipid structure by a fraction of a percent. 

The teeth of the Radulaths still scraped. The mineral slurry was still churned. The herds still moved in their vast, armored millions. But the *pitch* of the grinding changed.

The dry, deafening, iron-shredding screech lost its harsh, serrated edge. It settled into a low, deep, resonant hum—a massive, ocean-like drone that sounded less like tearing metal and more like the violent, beautiful singing of an endless glass mountain. It was still loud enough to drown out a human voice, but it was no longer an attack. It became a tide. It became a womb. It filled the chest not with terror, but with a strange, heavy tranquility.

### The Witness

When the work was done, the Power did not announce itself. It did not carve a face into the fog or write laws upon the slate. It simply drew back its attention, settling into the quiet role of a distant witness, watching the basin roll on in its low, wet twilight.

Down on one of the scattered hummocks, three miles from the nearest neighbor, an old hunter of the Un-Grushed stepped out of his bone-and-gut shelter.

The atomized rain silvered his long, shingled coat of scraper-shell. His ears were plugged with tallow and ash; his wide, camel-padded boots gripped the slick slate with ancient, instinctual grace. Above him, a Vane-Glider dropped through the bruised fog, snagged a Needle-Stilt from a shallow pool, and swept back up into the weeping sky.

The hunter walked to the edge of the dry stone and looked out across the basin.

The herds were moving. A million broad, mercury-shaped backs glistened in the shadowless light, their suction-cupped limbs sloshing through the ankle-deep sheets of brine.

The hunter knelt. He unlaced his heavy eel-skin boot and pulled it off, pressing his bare, gray-white foot directly to the bedrock.

He felt the deep, subtle warmth rising from the heart of the hummock, drying the brine on his toes. 

He felt the roar of the land—no longer a screech that rattled his teeth, but a deep, golden hum that matched the pulse in his own throat.

And then, from deep within the slate beneath his foot, he felt a vibration that was not the scrapers. It was a rhythm. Slow, rhythmic, double-tapped strikes, rising up through miles of iron stone from a hummock far across the plain.

It was an old drumming. A pattern his father used to strike when the hydrostatic tides were rising high and cold. A pattern that had been lost for forty years.

The old man stood in the silver rain. He did not smile—his face was built for cold wind and silence, not light expressions. But he reached into his belt, drew his basalt maul, and unslung the carved scraper-rib from his shoulder.

He jammed the rib into the seam of the warm slate floor. 

He lifted the maul, pause for a moment in the twilight, and struck the bone—hard, clear, and true—sending his own heartbeat down into the living, remembering stone, to wait for whoever came next.
