A foolish god would have brought the sun.

A lesser power, arriving at the edge of the dark canopy, would have looked down through the interwoven broadleaves and felt the sudden, prickling urge of pity. It would have seen the blind wallowers wedged flank to flank in the black grease; it would have seen the cable-snakes closing like hydraulic presses in the stifling dark; it would have seen the pale, tallow-skinned salamanders scraping muscle from rusted bones in the sulfurous silt, and it would have called it a hell. 

It would have drawn a blade of starlight across the roof of lianas. It would have cleaved the ceiling open to flood the valley floor with bright, cold sky.

And in doing so, it would have laid waste to an empire of dark grace.

The sun’s glare would have scorched the unblinking, heat-sensing pits of the wallowers, driving them into frantic, crushing panics against the buttress roots. The dry breezes of the upper air would have cracked their vulcanized hides, evaporated the greasy condensate that fed the tube-dwelling barnacles, and cooled the iron-sap until the great trees choked on their own thickened blood. The sky, to this basin, would not be a gift. It would be an execution.

But the power that came was wise. And because it was wise, it did not look upon the basin with the arrogant eyes of the light.

It paused above the canopy, floating in the thin, cool air of the high sky, and it listened. 

It heard the wet, rhythmic *plip* of condensation striking the mud. It felt the low, tectonic rumble of the methane vents—*chug-hiss, chug-hiss*—throbbing through ten miles of saturated silt. It sensed the vast, collective mind of the floor: a mind without ego, without ambition, without the sharp, painful isolation of the self. Here was a world that had perfected the art of belonging. Nothing was lost; nothing was wasted. Every breath was shared; every death was a quiet reweaving of the carpet; every body was both a home and a path for another.

The power felt a deep, slow reverence rise within itself—a awe for this dark, heavy engine that had run in the heat for ten thousand years without a single cry for help.

So, the power did not alter. It did not redeem. It did not lift.

Instead, it transformed itself.

To touch this world, the power knew it must shed its brightness. It cast off its radiance like a heavy coat, laying aside its stars, its winds, its blinding white clarity, until it became something thick, warm, and dark—an unseen, gentle density that possessed no form and cast no shadow. 

Then, it descended.

It passed through the broadleaves without tearing a single leaf. It slipped along the dripping lianas, sliding down the iron-crusted trunks like warm, invisible oil, until it reached the floor of the basin and dissolved into the mud.

It did not stay above. It sank. It seeped into the black bitumen, filled the hollows between the sleeping wallowers, and coiled softly around the cold, slag-grey plates of the cable-snakes. It became the soil itself.

And then, with infinite care, the power began its work—not of change, but of *hallowing*.

First, it bound the sky. It wove an invisible, impenetrable veil over the upper canopy, sealing the roof forever against the outside world. No sun-god would ever discover this trough; no wandering sky-creature would ever drop a spark into the methane-rich dark; no storm would ever break the quiet, simmering heat. The basin was made eternal, locked away in a sacred, unassailable pocket of the earth.

Second, it touched the methane vents. Deep beneath the roots, where the hot gas built in pressurized chambers, the power smoothed the rough edges of the earth’s crust. It did not stop the vents, but it tuned them. The erratic, jarring bursts became a steady, rhythmic lullaby—a deep, soothing pulse of *chug-hiss* that vibrated through the mud at the exact frequency of a sleeping beast’s heart. In the hollows, the piled wallowers sighed in unison, their rubbery flanks rising and falling in a deeper, more restful slumber than they had ever known.

Third, it took upon itself the burden of the turn. 

In a world where life presses so tightly against life, the passage from breath to grease could sometimes carry a lingering, silent friction. When a cable-snake closed its coils around a broad-headed lizard, or when an old wallower grew too weak to surge forward and began to sink into the silt, the power moved into the space between them. 

It did not stay the snake’s muscles; it did not pull the wallower from the muck. To do so would break the engine. Instead, it became a quiet, numbing warmth within the dying creature’s chest. It erased the last dim flare of distress, replacing it with the heavy, sweet sensation of stepping into a warm bath. Under the power’s touch, death ceased to be a struggle and became merely a long, comfortable exhalation—a peaceful dissolving into the iron, the fungi, and the roots of the trees that held the dark together.

Finally, the power placed a single, subtle blessing upon the flora. Where the dark, viscous sap oozed from the splits in the bark, the power sweetened the mineral taste just slightly—enriching the iron oxide with a deep, nourishing warmth that made the carapaces of the sheet-beetles shine like burnished copper before they died and turned to rust.

Days passed, though there were no days to count. Centuries turned, though the dark kept no track of them.

If you were to crawl today through the deep troughs of the basin, you would notice no divine temple. You would find no gilded altars, no holy scriptures carved into the slick, iron-filmed stone. 

You would feel only the perpetual, sweltering heat trapped beneath the canopy. You would hear only the wet, muffled rustle of a million bodies shifting in the silt, and the slow, mechanical *chug-hiss* of the gas vents idling in the dark. You would smell the heavy grease, the wet earth, and the faint, sweet trace of sulfur.

Everything would seem as it always was: mindless, heavy, locked in a patient, unending crawl.

Except that now, beneath the leaf litter, deep within the black mud where the broad-headed salamanders wedge their tallow-white flanks against the stone, the ground is never cold. The pressure that holds the beasts together no longer feels like a weight, but like an embrace. 

And in the absolute blackness, where nothing has ever had eyes to see, the entire valley rests in the lap of a god that was wise enough to simply lie down in the muck, close its eyes, and sleep alongside them.
