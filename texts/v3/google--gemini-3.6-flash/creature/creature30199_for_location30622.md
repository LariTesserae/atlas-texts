To live here is not merely to occupy the basin; it is to be tuned to it. Nothing in this space possesses a voice, for a voice would be swallowed whole by the roar of the beam engine. Nothing here possesses ears as a mammal knows them; hearing has long since been traded for a deep, skeletal resonance. Every creature is a component in the same closed circuit, as essential and unchanging as the cast-iron beam or the copper valves.

***

At the lowest level, down in the microscopic rivers worn into the slate-like root-wood, are the **Glass Salamanders**. They are pale as boiled lard and entirely blind, their eyes smoothed over by generations of uselessness in the flat, shadowless gray light. Their bellies are not soft skin, but tough, calcified plates stained a deep rust-red from the iron-saturated water they drink. They do not swim so much as anchor themselves against the wood. Their ribs are wide and flat, pressed firm against the floor so that the engine's rhythmic *thump-clack* vibrates directly through their internal organs. This vibration is their clock, their compass, and their heart. They lay their translucent eggs in the warm condensation troughs directly beneath the main steam cylinder, where the heat keeps the gel from stiffening. If the engine were ever to pause, the sudden silence would rupture their delicate, vibration-stretched lungs; they would die of the stillness before they could starve.

***

Climbing the towering iron flues and the massive, verdigris-coated pipes are the **Grease-Beetles**. Broad as a man’s palm and heavy as river pebbles, their carapaces are a dark, iridescent black-green, like oil on stagnant water. They are the machine’s primary caretakers, though they know nothing of machinery. They feed on the thick, velvety mats of iron-moss that grow near the piston housings, but their true function is in what they leave behind. The beetles digest the moss's plant fats and excrete a smooth, aromatic wax—a mixture of fir resin, animal lipid, and iron dust—directly onto the moving joints of the engine. Where the great iron rods slide in and out of their brass sleeves, thousands of these beetles cluster, their hard, clicking legs moving in chaotic counterpoint to the piston's steady thrust, constantly sweeping their wax into the friction points. The machine stays oiled because the beetles are hungry; the beetles are fed because the machine sweats steam.

***

High above, in the wide, empty air between the polished floor and the leaf-woven ceiling, live the **Solder-Swifts**. Their gray wings are long, thin, and stiff, built for perpetual gliding through the cool, damp draft created by the rising steam and falling rain. Their bones are unusually dense, reinforced with metallic trace minerals absorbed from the midges they eat—midges that breed in the iron-rich water pooling on the machine's casing. The swifts never touch the wooden floor, nor do they land on the engine’s hot iron. Instead, they nest exclusively in the deep, vertical fissures of the ironwood walls, plastering their small cup-nests to the living timber using a slurry of pine needle, mud, and iron-water. They fly in mesmerising, synchronized loops through the mist, their flight paths carved by the invisible air currents pushed aside by the rising and falling of the thirty-foot walking beam. They do not sing; when they open their beaks, they emit only a dry, clicking snap of bone that is lost instantly in the *hissed-chug* of the exhaust.

***

But the true center of the basin’s life—the thing that gives this hollow its strange, quiet intent—is the **Keeper of the Sleeve**.

It is not human, nor is it an animal in any ordinary sense. It is a single, ancient organism that resides at the junction where the engine’s largest drive-pipe plunges into the living wall of silver-firs. 

It appears at first like a dense knot of root-wood and gray hair, tucked tightly into the crevice between the iron flange and the tree trunk. But as the engine completes a cycle—as the walking beam reaches the apex of its stroke and pauses for a fraction of a second before falling—the knot moves. 

It has an elongated, slouched form, four paces long, made of tightly braided bark-fibers, moss, and dense, sinewy muscle that lacks any red blood, pale and gray throughout. Its limbs are long and triple-jointed, ending in broad, padded palms covered in fine, hair-like rootlets that can grip cold iron or wet wood with equal tenacity. It has no face—only a smooth, sloping brow of polished heartwood, scarred by copper oxide and sap, with two deep, heat-sensitive pits where eyes might once have been.

The Keeper moves only during the stroke. It has matched its motion so perfectly to the machine that it seems like a shadow thrown by the ironwork. 

When a copper release valve gathers too much mineral scale, the Keeper reaches out with a heavy, thumb-like appendage tipped with a chisel-like piece of petrified wood. *Thump-clack*—it taps the valve, clearing the white crust. *Hissed-chug*—it retreats into the shadow of the timber wall. 

It breathes the steam directly, pulling the hot, metallic moisture into deep, internal lung-cavities lined with lichen. In return, it bleeds a thick, clear Amber-resin from the glands along its spine, smearing it over the places where the iron pipes rub against the living bark of the trees, preventing the metal from galling the wood, and the wood from strangling the metal.

The Keeper does not sleep. It has no memory of a time before the engine, nor any concept of an outside world beyond the three hundred paces of polished root-wood. It is as old as the iron beam, its heartbeats long since locked to the rise and fall of the geothermal piston. 

It is the nervous system of the basin. When a pipe grows too cold, the Keeper presses its flat heartwood head against the iron, feeling the flow of water deep within, and clears the silted intake with a slow, deliberate flex of its wooden limbs. When a tree trunk presses too hard against a supply line, the Keeper prunes the root with its heavy, calcified jaws, feeding on the bitter, sap-heavy shavings.

Around it, the drizzle falls continuously. The salamanders crawl through their dark grooves; the beetles scrape and wax the iron seams; the swifts cut through the gray mist overhead. 

Everything here is content. Everything is locked in place. The great iron beam rises, pauses, and falls, and the living things of the basin move with it, endlessly repeating the only world that has ever existed.
