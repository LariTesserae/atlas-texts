To move among them is to understand a world where fluid is an ancient, unnecessary memory. 

At the surface of the amber towers, where the sunlight turns the resin into glowing, honey-colored stained glass, live the **Masons**. They are the visible pulse of the shelf, creatures the size of a human hand, their carapaces banded like polished sardonyx and dark carnelian. No two carry quite the same pattern; some are striped with veins of milky chalcedony, while older individuals have shells so darkened by decades of solar warmth that they resemble scorched horn. 

Their bodies are masterpieces of waterless engineering. They have no blood as softer creatures know it, but rather a thick, paste-like lymphatic oil that never evaporates. Their joints are sealed with self-lubricating dry waxes, preventing the sharp quartz dust from grinding them down. Beneath their broad, armored thorax are six jointed legs, each ending not in a harsh claw, but in a thick pad of dense, silver-gray felt—a fibrous growth harvested from their own bellies and the fronds of the bronze plants. This felt muffles their weight entirely. As they walk across the razor-edges of the fractured quartz, the rock does not cut them; the felt grips the glass-sharp lips with static friction, allowing them to scale vertical mirrors of stone without a sound.

Their work is divided not by strict, rigid castes, but by age and the evolving chemistry of their bodies.

The **Young**, pale-shelled and light-footed, are the *Miners*. Their mouthparts are tipped with tiny, dense mineral shears—mandibles hardened with iron oxides absorbed from the rock. They spend their first years at the perimeter of the basin, chewing at the unweathered quartz face. They do not eat the stone for hunger; they micro-fracture it, grinding the sharpest ridges into a fine flour which they store in dry, internal crop-pouches. They are cheerful, hyperactive laborers, constantly tapping the rock with their antennae to feel for structural flaws in the mountain’s shoulder.

The **Adults**, heavier and darker, are the *Synthesizers*. Their abdomens are swollen, segmented bellows filled with dry structural resins and aromatic, starch-based waxes. When a miner delivers a crop full of quartz flour, the synthesizer ingests it, kneading the powder internally with its dry secretions. Then, working with slow, hypnotic precision, the adult extrudes a warm, pliable ribbon of mortar through its mouthparts. Using their fan-shaped lower jaws like plasterers’ trowels, they lay down the arches, the delicate spiral staircases, and the thin-walled nursery pods. The mortar sets within seconds in the dry, thin air, locking the quartz flour into a translucent matrix that is stronger than granite and lighter than pine.

Deep within the layered interior of the resin city, where the light dims to a warm mahogany glow, reside the **Elders**. These are individuals whose carapaces have grown too heavy with mineral deposits for rapid movement. They do not crawl the thoroughfares. Instead, they climb into designated niches along the city’s load-bearing pillars and allow their lower legs to be sealed into the wall mortar by the younger workers. 

They become living buttresses. Their hearts still beat—a slow, once-a-minute contraction—and their abdominal vents continue to exhale the warm, lavender-scented hydrocarbon vapor that heats the city and feeds the mica-forest. They are the city’s memory and its thermal engine. Younger masons linger near these living pillars, pressing their antennae against the Elders' carapaces to receive subtle, vibrational instructions: where a wall is leaning, where a flue must be opened, or where the solar heat is building too fiercely.

Floating above this terrestrial density are the **Velvet Moths**, the *Dust-Keepers*. Broad-winged and heavy-bodied, they do not possess eyes in the traditional sense, but large, hemispherical mirrors of polished black chitin that detect thermal currents rising from the resin chimneys. Their wings, spanning nearly ten inches, are covered in a thick, soot-like powder that absorbs static electricity. 

The moths are the pollinators of this dry metropolis. They spend their lives drifting between the high resin spires and the thickets of hollow, bamboo-like reeds. They feed on nothing; their adult lives are powered entirely by rich lipid reserves built up during their larval stage deep inside the pollen-reeds. As they hover silently above the thoroughfares, the beating of their velvet wings creates gentle, warm updrafts that pull stale air out of the lower galleries. The yellow pollen that coats their undersides sloughs off in invisible clouds, settling onto the slate-blue powder-moss that coats the ledges, causing the moss to glow with a faint, static phosphorescence in the twilight.

And then there are the **Plants**, which here cannot be separated from the animals. They are not passive scenery; they are slow-motion inhabitants with their own distinct behaviors. 

The **Mica-Fronds** act as the city’s solar collectors. Their leaves—sheets of clear, flexible silicate no thicker than a fingernail—are wired with fine, bronze-colored conductive veins. As the sun moves across the sky, these leaves don't bend; they expand and contract with thermal heat, shifting with a faint, dry *click* to track the light. They absorb the extreme ultraviolet radiation of the high altitude and convert it into heavy, complex oils, which they pump through deep taproots directly into the storage tubers buried in the quartz cracks. 

In times of construction, a mason will approach a swollen **Oil-Tuber**, tap its thick bark three times with its felted legs, and wait. The tuber, responding to the specific frequency of the tap, exudes a single, clear drop of lipid from a specialized pore—a trade of fuel for the carbon-dioxide-rich exhaust the mason exhales from its flank spiracles.

At the very bottom of the basin, in the narrowest, pitch-black crevices where the quartz shelf meets the mountain’s heart, lies the true foundation of the ecosystem: the **Anchor-Grubs**. 

These are the mothers of the Masons, though they look nothing like their gleaming, agate-armored children. They are vast, soft, pale creatures, the length of an arm, their bodies covered in a dense, felted fur that has grown directly into the stone. They never see the honeyed sunlight of the upper tiers. Blind and motionless, they sit anchored in the deep cold of the rock, their mouthparts fused permanently to veins of mineral sulfur and iron deep within the mountain. 

They are the primordial transformers. They crush the deep rock, digesting raw minerals in an anaerobic, waterless chemical furnace, producing the base lipids that their mobile children carry upward to build the light-filled spires above. 

The low, collective breathing that echoes through the quiet basin comes from them. It is the rhythmic intake and exhaust of thousands of tiny, valved bellows along the bodies of the Anchor-Grubs, pushing warm, scrubbed, aromatic air up through the vertical chimneys of the resin towers. 

Nothing here dies in the sense of decay. When a Mason’s time ends, its body does not wither or rot in the dry, sterile air. It simply stops moving. Its felted legs remain locked to the spot where it stood. Within hours, its neighbors assemble around it. They do not mourn; they build. With swift, respectful passes of their fan-shaped mouths, they coat the fallen mason in a fine layer of liquid amber and pulverized quartz, integrating its polished carapace directly into the wall, the arch, or the walkway it was helping to mend. 

The city is built of their work, and the city is built of *them*. Every spire is a genealogy; every archway is an ancestor; every breath of warm, lavender-scented air has passed through a hundred living hearts before it reaches the mountain sky.
