To look closely at the plain is to realize that the stone has acquired a second, softer skin—a tissue of millions of quiet, warm bodies that have adapted so precisely to the contours of the siltstone they seem less like inhabitants and more like a liquid mineral phase of the mountain itself.

They do not compete for space so much as merge into it, occupying different strata of the terrace's micro-topography.

***

### The Upper Canopy: Gliders and Sun-Keepers

On the rounded, tallow-smooth crests of the sandstone swells—where the heat gathers thickest and stays late into the twilight—lie the legless gliders. 

They are creatures of dry leather and old oil, three to five feet in length, as wide as a man’s thigh, but flattened like heavy leather belting. Their dorsal skin is dark—a deep, bruised indigo or scorched umber—lined with faint, longitudinal ridges that absorb the high-altitude light. They have no limbs, only broad, vestigial folds of parchment-like skin running the length of their flanks: the pale, dry remnants of ancestral membranes from an era when this tableland was still a jagged ring of peaks cut by howling, thermalling gorges. The wind that once bore them aloft is long gone, smoothed out into a lazy, warm breath, and the gliders have surrendered flight for an endless, sun-drenched slumber.

Their eyes are shielded beneath thick, cloudy caps of clear horn, permanently closed against the glare. They move by a slow, almost imperceptible peristalsis, a ripple passing down their underside once every few minutes. A glider does not seek food daily, nor weekly. Its metabolism is an ember tucked deep within a dense, fatty core. It feeds perhaps twice a year, during the brief late-summer hatch of the subterranean silt-maggots, and spends the rest of its decades converting sunlit heat into a slow, internal hum.

Where two gliders overlap, a pale, narrow shadow is created on the rock beneath them. In these tiny strips of cooler shade, smaller things gather: minuscule, ivory-colored geckos with skin so thin their ribcages show like delicate fan-bones, and wingless velvet-moths the size of thumbprints. The moths are dense with dark, moisture-trapping fur; they pack themselves tightly into the crevices between the gliders’ flanks, living off the faint film of salt left behind as the larger beasts slowly transpire.

***

### The Basins: The Shingle-Carpet

Below the crests, in the shallow, saucer-shaped depressions worn into the grey siltstone, the carpet-bugs hold the floor.

They are broad, flat arthropods, six inches across, shaped like rounded slate tiles. Their carapaces are dull, unpolished ochre, pitted with microscopic pores that give them the exact texture of the surrounding dust. They do not live side by side; they interlock. The trailing edge of one bug’s carapace fits into a polished groove on the leading edge of the next, forming a continuous, flexible pavement of living chitin that seals the bottom of the basin.

Beneath this armored roof, in the pitch-black, cool micro-climate between the bugs’ bellies and the stone, lies a secret, damp world. Hundreds of thousands of tiny, thread-like legs brush endlessly against the rock, scraping at the microscopic filaments of black wire-moss and the soft, dusty spores of the sulphur-lichen. 

The carpet-bugs are the sculptors of the plain. As they move—a collective, shuffling migration that turns the entire floor of a basin by a few inches a day—their hard undersides lap and polish the siltstone, grinding down the minor roughnesses of the rock until it becomes as smooth as turned clay. The fine dust produced by this endless grinding is pushed outward by their breath-spiracles, forming tiny, neat ramparts around the edges of their basins.

When a carpet-bug dies, it does not decompose in the ordinary sense. The dry air cures its innards into a odorless, amber-like resin. Its neighbors simply crawl over the empty shell, grinding it down over months with their shuffling legs until the carapace is reduced to pale powder, leaving no hollow space in the living carpet.

***

### The Furrows: The Glass-Lizards

In the long, fluted gutters and structural cracks—the deepest channels carved by ancient, long-vanished rains—the glass-lizards lie stacked like firewood.

They are supple, legless reptiles, eighteen inches long, with scales so clear and lacking in pigment that they resemble hand-blown glass or melted ice. Through their translucent flanks, one can see the faint, rhythmic pulse of their double hearts, the pale amber string of their digestive tracts, and the dark, slender thread of their spine.

They are creatures of dew and shade. During the day, they stack three or four deep in the rock furrows to protect the soft, unarmored skin of their bellies from the drying heat. The bottom-most lizards lie directly against the cool stone, while those on top shield them with their reflective backs, which scatter the high, unblinking light like crushed quartz.

Every morning, when the dawn mist settles into the ancient, man-ground gutters, the stack unwinds. The glass-lizards move with a smooth, pouring motion toward the carved stone basins. They do not lap at the water with tongues; instead, they press their dry, porous snouts into the thin film of condensation. Through a network of microscopic capillary grooves running along their jawlines, the water is drawn directly into their mouths by suction, without a single drop lost to the thirsty air.

Once hydrated, they return to the furrows, weaving themselves back into their dense, glistening cords, locking head-to-tail to wait out the long heat of the day.

***

### The Deep Fissures: The Anchorites

Deepest of all, wedged into the vertical frost-cracks that split the tableland down to its cooler, unexposed roots, live the rock-toads. 

If the glass-lizards are fluid and the carpet-bugs are slate, the rock-toads are the mountain itself made conscious. They are huge, squarish, and completely immobile for years at a time. Their skin is not damp or warty, but thick, heavily calcified, and studded with mineral granules that match the surrounding siltstone so perfectly that even the rust-colored lichen grows across their backs, bridging the gap between living flesh and dead stone without a break.

A rock-toad’s eyes have long since fused shut beneath thick folds of bone. It has no need for vision in the black subterranean slits. It senses the world through the subtle vibrations of the tableland—the faint, collective hum of the carpet-bugs shuffling three feet above, the passage of a glider’s shadow, the distant, sub-audible creak of the mountain expanding under the afternoon heat.

Its life is measured in centuries. Once every few hours, its chest expands with a single, long, whistling intake of air through its nostrils—a sound like wind passing over the lip of a dry bottle. If a wandering carpet-bug or an elderly glass-lizard slips into the fissure and touches the toad’s hardened jaw, a wide, pale mouth opens without warning. A thick, mucous-laden tongue, heavy as wet felt, rolls out, wraps around the victim, and draws it inside. The jaw snaps shut with a dry, stony *clack*, and the toad returns to its stasis, taking weeks to dissolve its meal in the dark cavity of its belly.

***

### The Ghost in the Stone

And what of the ones who carved the gutters? Who ground the shallow drinking cups and dry-stacked the slate windbreaks that now shelter the velvet-moths?

They are gone, but they were not strangers to this landscape; they were merely an earlier iteration of its patience.

They were likely low-slung, broad-chested creatures—perhaps distant kin to the ancestors of the gliders or the toads—possessing heavy, blunt claws that could chip siltstone without breaking. They did not build cities or smelters; they built water-traps. They lived here when the air was slightly cooler, when the mountain was still dropping its final tears of snow, and they understood that the only wealth on this high tableland was the morning damp.

They shaped the rock to catch that damp, and in doing so, they created the infrastructure that outlived them. The animals that crowd the plain today do not remember the builders, but their bodies have been shaped by the builders' handiwork. The glass-lizard’s snout fits precisely into the curve of the carved gutters; the carpet-bugs’ collective migration turns at the exact angle of the slate windbreaks, guided by the slight alteration in draft caused by walls laid down fifty thousand years ago.

Nothing here is wasted. No body rots; no energy is spent on anger, speed, or song. The inhabitants of the mountain shoulder have achieved a perfect, crowded equilibrium with the stone beneath them. They are waiting for nothing. They are simply filling the warm, quiet interval between the erosion of the mountains and the cooling of the sun, breathing together in the high, pale light, as slow and durable as the world itself.
