The life here does not merely inhabit the iron; it is tempered by it. Over centuries of dampness and blinding light, the ecosystem has aligned itself to the geometry of the girders, the eight-minute pulse of the deep counterweights, and the chemical wealth of the rusting decks.

### The Lowly and the Cleaners

At the smallest scale, the world belongs to the **Verdigris-Gazer** and the **Chisel-Beetle**. 

The beetles, broad as a man’s hand and armored in carapaces of iridescent bronze and zinc, are the sculptors of the substrate. They do not eat the leaf-litter; they strip the crusts of sulfurous oxides and mineral waxes that weep from the seams between plates. Their mandibles move with the steady, dry click of watchwork, chiseling away at the copper-green fringes to clear pathways for their grubs. Where they feed, they leave the bare metal polished to a mirror shine, reflecting the blue sky until the moisture returns and the rust begins anew.

Following them are the **Gasket-Slugs**—thick, translucent mollusks the length of a forearm, their flesh stained a deep indigo by the cobalt trace-minerals in the condensation. They crawl along the undersides of the giant overhangs, trailing a thick, magnetic mucus that binds the loose iron-dust into a dense, protective sheath around their bodies. When the sun hits the metal and raises the surface temperature to a searing heat, the slugs retreat into the shadow of the rivet-heads, sealing themselves in with a layer of hardened, iron-rich dried slime that glints like hematite.

### The Canopy Dwellers

In the mid-deck foliage, where the wild orchids wrap around three-inch steel cables, the **Pale Monkeys**—known to those who live here as *Loom-Maki*—move in silent, fluid leaps. They are light-bodied and long-limbed, with thick, ash-white fur that deflects the brutal midday glare. Their hands are their most striking feature: the palms and pads of their fingers are not soft skin, but tough, black, rubbery leather, grooved like the tread of an industrial tire. They can drop twenty feet onto the lip of an exposed I-beam, catch the wet, rust-slick edge with a single hand, and swing into the ferns without losing their grip.

The Loom-Maki are omnivorous scavengers of the structural joints. They harvest the swollen, water-heavy bulbs of the bromeliads growing in the exhaust stacks, but their favorite prey are the nestlings of the **Riveters**—the thick-billed, oil-black birds that occupy the hollow box-girders.

The Riveters themselves are the primary voice of the upper decks. Their plumage is so dense and oily that water runs off them in distinct, mercury-like beads. They build their nests deep inside the structural framework, lining the iron chambers with shredded fern-fronds and fine ribbons of shed copper wire. Their calls are not melodic; they are crisp, metallic *clink-clinks* and dry, ratcheting scrapes that bounce off the sheer three-thousand-foot bronze walls, making it impossible to tell if a bird is ten feet away or three decks down.

### The Deep-Structure Fauna

Far below the sunlit canopy, down in the dripping, shadow-choked gulfs where light only strikes for twenty minutes at noon, lives the **Piston-Crawler**. 

These are massive, slow-moving quadrupeds, built like a cross between a coatimundi and a tortoise, with slate-gray, hide-covered plates that mimic the texture of wet, cast-iron machinery. They are functionally blind, navigating by the vibrations that travel through the structural steel. 

A Piston-Crawler spends days clinging to the warm, vibrating sleeves of the mountain’s internal hydraulics. They feed on the thick carpets of iron-eating mosses and the dense networks of roots that penetrate the lower decks. Their heartbeats are unnaturally slow, synchronized almost perfectly with the deep, eight-minute groan of the shifting counterweights. When the three-story pistons settle into their sleeves with that low, wet sigh, the Crawlers raise their broad heads, open their throat-pouches, and emit a resonant, low-frequency hum that vibrates through the iron deck beneath them, signaling their territory across the abyss to others of their kind.

### The Deck-Folk

And high above the gloom, strung between the gantries like spiders in an iron orchard, live the **People of the Flange**.

They are small-strained hominids, light-boned and dark-skinned, their eyes shaded by thick, prominent brow-ridges and double eyelids that shield them from the violent flashes of sunlight off the sheet steel. They do not walk as flat-land dwellers walk; their feet are wide, with elongated, flexible toes that can curl over a two-inch cable or lock into the rim of a bolt-hole with equal ease.

They do not build houses on the decks—the foliage is too dense, the snakes and beetles too persistent, and the floor too prone to standing water. Instead, they dwell *underneath*. 

Their villages are suspended hammock-settlements slung in the dry, shaded rain-shadows beneath the giant five-mile refinery decks. They anchor their homes to the primary trusses using braided ropes made of split bamboo-vine and salvaged copper core wire. Their huts are light structures of woven palm and hammered bark, swaying gently whenever the mountain winds sweep up from the lower gulfs.

The Deck-Folk are farmers of the vertical space:
* **The Silt-Tenders** maintain long, narrow hanging troughs made of halved bronze conduit, filled with the dark, iron-rich rust-humus scraped from the gear-valleys. In these, they cultivate a variety of heavy, red-leaved tubers and a bitter, sky-blue berry that thrives on metallic runoff.
* **The Catchers** tend the condensation lines. They place carved wooden troughs beneath the weeping seams of the giant overhangs, directing the sweet, mineral-pure water into hollowed-out structural columns that serve as vertical cisterns for the whole clan.
* **The Splicers** are the elders who know the anatomy of the mountain. They can read the health of a deck by the sound it makes. They press their ears to the structural trusses; if a seam is under too much tension from the growing weight of the jungle, or if a fern-root has wedged too deeply into a load-bearing rivet-line, the Splicer hears the high-pitched *ping* of the metal protesting. With small copper wedges and mallet strokes, they gently relieve the stress or clear the root, preserving the ancient, quiet balance between the machine and the green.

To the Deck-Folk, time is not measured by hours, but by **The Stroke**—the seven-to-eight-minute interval between the shifting of the deep counterweights. A mother tells her child to wait *three strokes* before climbing down to the water-trough. A hunter stalks a Loom-Maki by moving only during the low, pneumatic sigh of the piston, when his footsteps on the corrugated bronze are swallowed by the voice of the world.

They have no memory of who built the iron range, nor do they imagine a world made of stone or dirt. To them, the iron is the earth: ancient, warm, weeping sweet water from its seams, growing its skin of green velvet, and breathing its heavy, steady breath into the bright, cloudless sky.
