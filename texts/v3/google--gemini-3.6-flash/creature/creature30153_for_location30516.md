To survive on a floor that never stops pounding, you must first give up your soft joints.

The people who inhabit the plateau—the **Ring-bound**, as they call themselves, or simply *the Tenders*—do not walk with the loose, swinging gait of those who tread on soil. Their bodies have been reshaped over generations by the perpetual *THUD-CLACK* of the Sap-Stamps. Their ankle bones are thick and fused like draft-horse pasterns; their hips are low and set wide. They move in a perpetual, shock-absorbing crouch, their knees never fully unbending, absorbing the percussive shudder that hits the soles of their heavy boots twice every three seconds.

Their teeth tell the story of their age. The constant vibration, travelling directly up through the skeleton, rattles natural enamel to powder by a person’s thirtieth year. The older Tenders have jaws lined with smoothed rivets of soft lead, copper, or tin—metal plugs driven into the gums to cushion the bite and keep the skull from ringing like a struck kettle. When they smile, it is a flash of dull, dark industrial metal.

They are a sparse population, numbered only in the few thousands across thirty leagues, and they do not live in families so much as in *Engine-Crews* or *Gall-Gilds*. Because thin, high-altitude air carries voice poorly against the deafening rhythm of the pistons, they rarely speak aloud when out on the open wood. Instead, they are masters of *pulse-talk*. 

Every Tender carries a *tapping-rod*—a two-foot stave of cold-drawn bronze tipped with a heavy iron ferrule. To speak to a companion fifty yards away, a Tender strikes their rod directly into the growth ring beneath their feet. The signal travels along the dense, petrified grain at incredible speed, rising through the boots of the recipient as a sharp, rhythmic telegraph. A veteran Tender can sit on the edge of a carved gutter, press the butt of his rod against the flesh behind his ear, and read the distant movements of three different maintenance crews by the distinct ring of their heels on the heartwood.

***

They dress in heavy, oiled smocks made of thick sail-cloth, blackened with petroleum and seal-grease to turn the icy cloud-mist that constantly settles from the sky. Over their eyes, they wear narrow-slit goggles of dark amber glass; the high-altitude sun, reflecting off the oily, dark walnut floor, can scorch the retinas in an hour.

Their relationship with space is profoundly non-Euclidean, mastered only through long, childhood trial. A young Tender learns the map of the city not by landmarks, but by **Fiber-Tension**. 

*   To travel **Ring-wise**—along the sweeping, curved path of a single growth ring—is to ride a slide. A runner can lean into the curve, take twenty long, loping strides, and find themselves two miles west, the landscape blurring beside them as if pulled through a funnel.
*   To travel **Cross-grain**—stepping perpendicularly across the rings toward a building that sits fifty paces off—is an agonizing march. Each step feels as though one is dragging an anchor through deep sludge. The air thickens; the legs grow heavy; the destination looms large and tauntingly detailed, yet refuses to draw closer.

Because of this, the city’s streets are not straight lines. They are vast, swirling spirals. To cross a space of thirty yards, a Tender will willingly run a three-mile loop along a dominant growth ring, letting the compression of space carry them around the obstacle before hopping across the grain at a "Knot"—one of the rare, swirling spatial eddies where the wood’s ancient limbs once branched. At these Knots, distance normalizes for a few dozen feet, allowing people to congregate, trade, and build.

***

In the hollowed burl-nodules and gall-spires, life is dark, warm, and intensely aromatic. The interiors of the buildings are carved directly into the petrified tissue, polished smooth, and heated by **Resin-Flues**—copper pipes that carry hot, static-charged sap directly from the engine houses. 

The air inside smells overpowering: a thick mixture of turpentine, bitter ozone, fried tallow, and the dry, sweet scent of warm cedar. 

The Tenders do not farm—no soil could ever take root on the petrified floor. Their sustenance comes from the engine-system itself and the thin ecosystem it supports:

*   **Black-Moss Paste:** The velvet-like mats lining the copper gutters are harvested daily. Rich in copper salts and dark lipids, the moss is scraped into iron pots, boiled with cloud-condensate, and mashed into a heavy, ink-black porridge that tastes intensely of iron and iodine.
*   **Gall-Grubs:** Deep within the softer, unpetrified core of the growing gall-structures live fat, blind grubs the size of a man’s forearm. They feed on the static-laden sap that leaks through the internal walls. The Tenders harvest them with long iron hooks, roasting them over resin-braziers until their skins turn crisp as charred parchment.
*   **Static-Spirits:** The icy resin drawn up by the Sap-Stamps is distilled in bronze stills. The resulting liquor is clear, heavy as liquid mercury, and leaves a sharp, electric tingle on the tongue that numbs the jaw for hours—a favored relief against the perpetual shaking of the world.

***

Humanity, however, is not the only thing moving across the dark walnut sea. 

Beside the **Rust-Wings**—who roost in the warm, steam-venting crowns of the Sap-Stamps and dive into the copper gutters to catch grit-lice—there are the **Grain-Walkers** or *Lignin-Skimmers*.

These are non-human, or perhaps things that were once human before they spent too long sleeping with their ears pressed to the wood. They are tall, shockingly thin creatures whose limbs have lengthened to match the spatial distortions of the plateau. Their skin is dry, dark, and cracked in fine, concentric circles that mirror the growth rings beneath them. They wear no iron; metal, to them, is too loud. Instead, they wear soft shoes made of woven black-moss fiber.

The Lignin-Skimmers do not use the cableways or the steel viaducts. They move exclusively along the "Dead Rings"—lines of heartwood where the petrification is so absolute that time and distance collapse entirely. A Skimmer can step onto a Dead Ring at the eastern edge of the plateau, vanish into a slight shimmering distortion of the air, and step off ten leagues away a second later, their movements marked only by a faint, dry *shhh-shhh* sound, like dry leaves sliding across ice. They do not interact with the Tenders, except to leave strange, carved wooden polyhedrons on the steps of the engine houses—offering-shapes that the Tenders leave untouched, knowing they mark rings that are about to "pinch" or shift.

Deep down, beneath the five-thousand-foot timber crust, in the dark where no piston reach can touch, something else exists. The Tenders call it **The Deep Strain**. 

On certain nights, when the high fog freezes into clear, starry air and the cold drops low enough to shrink the iron pistons in their housings, the Sap-Stamps are halted for maintenance. For a few precious hours, the deafening *THUD-CLACK* ceases.

It is during these silences that the true nature of the plateau reveals itself. The Tenders lie flat on their stomachs in the dark, pressing both ears against the smooth, cold walnut floor. 

Through thirty leagues of solid, ancient heartwood, coming from miles beneath the cold air, they can hear the slow, agonizingly massive shift of fluid—a deep, creaking groan that takes twenty minutes to complete a single breath. The plateau is not dead stone. It is a stump. And somewhere far below, in the dark depths of the world, the roots are still trying to turn.
