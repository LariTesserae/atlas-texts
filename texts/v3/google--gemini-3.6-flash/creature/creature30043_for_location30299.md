To live here, an organism must not merely endure the heat and the metal; it must be made of them. The life that inhabits the High Silt Terraces is an ecosystem without leaves, without eyes, and without blood as red as iron, though iron runs through almost every living tissue in the basin. 

At the base of everything is the gray slime-mould that carpets the slag-brick troughs—not a single creature, but a vast, low-voltage metabolic sheet miles wide. It is the terrace’s primary engine. The mould feeds directly on the dissolved sulfur, iron oxides, and silica carried by the thermal runoff. Where it creeps over the cast-iron walkways, it leaves behind a microscopically fine, glassy glaze that protects the metal from flaking into dust, binding the city’s bones to the stone. When the twenty-minute steam surges rise, the mould darkens from slate to an ink-black sheen, its network of capillary veins pulsing with accelerated fluid transport as it drinks the sudden deluge of hot, mineralized rain. 

Out of this living carpet grow the calcified shelf-structures. They are non-moving, semi-living mineral corals that use the iron-salts dripping from the thirty-story arcades to build their own skeletons. Over centuries, these formations have grown to resemble monumental, hollow ribs and weeping brackets that fan out from the iron pylons. They act as humidifying lungs: their porous, orange-stained surfaces draw up the warm water through capillary action, evaporating it steadily into the twilight air, maintaining the suffocating fog even during the quiet lulls between volcanic venting.

Moving across these crusts are the hand-sized scraper-plate creatures—the *litho-chitons*. They possess no limbs in the traditional sense, but move via three broad, muscular pads beneath a segmented carapace of translucent, silica-sheathed chitin. They look like living quartz tiles, thick and dull-eyed, completely blind in a world where light has no purpose. Their mouthparts are double-hinged rasps made of biogenic magnetite, hard enough to carve fresh metal. They graze on the slime-mould and the soft lime-deposits, leaving clean, dark tracks on the orange-crusted iron. 

When the steam vents hiss and the water in the flumes begins to boil, the chitons do not flee. They cannot move fast enough. Instead, they lock their carapaces flat against the wet brick, sealing their soft underbellies with a vacuum ring of thick mucus. The boiling water washes over their mineralized shells without harming them; to them, the thermal spike is a bath that melts away parasites and clears their rasping jaws of clogged silica.

Further down, in the train-marshalling yard where the steel rails have rotted into bright turquoise ribbons of dissolved copper, a different metabolic branch holds sway. Here, the mist carries a sharp, metallic tang that burns human lungs, but to the *copper-weevils*, it is air and food combined. These are longer, segmented creatures, the size of a forearm, with jointed legs like bent copper wires. Their bodies are coated in a pale, flaky powder of malachite and verdigris. They do not walk so much as hitch themselves along the crumbling rail-beds with slow, rhythmic twitches, chewing through the turquoise dust and excreting long, rigid threads of pure, spun copper. Over generations, they have woven dense, metallic bushes in the lee of collapsed boxcars—glimmering, wire-like thickets that hum faintly when the low thrum of the deep iron counterweights vibrates through the floor.

In the high air, suspended between the hollow sky-bridges, live the aerial floaters—the *vapors*. They are the only creatures here that seem delicate, though their delicate skin is woven from a dense, high-temperature polymer derived from organic silicates. A mature vapor is a bulbous, gas-filled canopy six feet across, milky white and translucent as boiled glass. They maintain their buoyancy through internal chambers that hold pockets of superheated steam and biologically produced methane, staying aloft in the thick, dense atmosphere like hot-air balloons in a cathedral.

The vapors have no heads or limbs. From their undersides hang long, gossamer fringes—filaments thirty feet long that trail down into the empty arcade bays like weeping willow branches made of damp silk. These threads are coated in a sticky, neutral-ph mucus that catches suspended fungal spores, mineral dust, and tiny, wind-drifted larvae. As the vapor floats on the slow thermal currents generated by the flumes, it continuously reaps its own fringe, pulling the filaments up into an underside oral pore with a motion as slow and rhythmic as breathing. 

Once in a long while, two vapors collide in the dark upper vaults. They do not fight; they coalesce, pressing their soft, steam-filled bells together for hours, exchanging genetic material through glowing, opalescent contact patches before drifting apart back into the purple mist. When a vapor dies, its internal gas cools and condenses into water. It drops out of the sky without a sound, falling like a collapsed silk parachute onto the wet flagstones below, where the scraper-plates and the slime-mould dissolve its mineral skin within a single thermodynamic cycle.

And deep within the dark, flooded vaults beneath the boilers—where the light never reaches even as a bronze smudge—there are larger things that never rise to the surface. 

In the massive, drowned cisterns that feed the drainage gutters, giant sessile organisms known as *furnace-reefs* have anchored themselves to the ancient hydraulic machinery. They are vast, pulsing mounds of fused chitin and iron-lime, some the size of small houses, built directly over the primary steam-relief vents. They are the apex of this quiet ecology: they do not hunt, for there is nothing to hunt. Instead, they sit directly upon the thermal pulses of the earth, their cavernous, valve-like bodies opening and closing in sync with the twenty-minute boil. They draw the boiling, mineral-choked water through internal chambers lined with metabolic plates, extracting heat and raw elements, then jetting the cooled water out through vertical chimneys that whistle softly in the dark.

If you were to stand on a wet iron balcony and listen closely between the steam-surges, past the gentle hiss of the aerosol drizzle and the distant, foot-shaking thrum of the falling counterweights, you would hear the sound of the ecosystem at work. It is not the sound of hunting or fleeing, of mating calls or territorial distress. 

It is a low, continuous scraping—a million tiny, iron-shod jaws rasping the rust from the girders; the soft, wet slap of drifting filaments against iron trusses; the slow, rhythmic sighing of the furnace-reefs deep in the flooded dark. 

Nothing here remembers the people who built the arcades, nor do these creatures know that the world outside the High Silt Terraces is cold or green. To them, the universe is thirty stories of wet iron, an endless rain of hot copper and silica, and a deep, volcanic heart that beats once every twenty minutes, keeping the mist warm and the iron alive.
