The ones who built the high perches are not visitors to the High Horn; they are its native grit made upright. 

To look at them is to see something carved from the same iron-grained slate and sun-baked horn that forms the cliff itself. They are lean, elongated creatures, standing nearly three meters tall when fully extended, though they rarely stand straight—their bodies are perpetually bent to lower their profile against the punch of the updrafts. Their skin is not hide or flesh in any terrestrial sense, but a flexible, segmented armor of dark, mahogany-colored chitin, polished by the airborne silt until it catches the bruised ochre light like oiled bronze.

They do not bleed. Their internal circulatory system carries a dense, amber tallow—the very same lipid compound they harvest from the bellow-piths. Where their limbs join at deep, ball-and-socket knees and shoulders, a thin film of this natural grease bubbles out, caught in the friction-grooves of their joints to keep the grinding dust from seizing their bones. 

Their heads are narrow, wedge-shaped keels designed to cut the wind. They have no nostrils, for they do not inhale air to fill lungs; instead, twin slit-valves along their flanks expand and contract with the rising thermals, pulling the dust-laden air through internal chambers lined with sticky, hair-like filters that trap tallow-spores and mineral flour. They feed on this dry distillation, converting fat and silica directly into skeletal density and joint-grease. 

They are essentially deaf to high frequencies, but profoundly tuned to vibration. Their eyes are flat, golden discs of natural mica, set deep behind heavy brow-ridges that keep the grit from scratching the lenses. Because the air is a deafening roar of plant-slaps and wind-flutes, they communicate not with voice, but through stridulation and resonance. Along the inside of their long, four-jointed forearms are rows of hardened, serrated ridge-plates. By scraping these against the slate or against the bone-struts of their perches, they send sharp, rhythmic clicks and long, buzzing hums through the rock—a language of pure tactile telegraphy that can be felt through the soles of their feet half a kilometer away.

They live entirely in the vertical. To them, the ledge is merely a staging ground, a place to harvest; home is the sheer face above. 

To rest, they climb to the carved niches high on the basalt wall, where the wind-carved stone offers shelter from the direct blast of the abyss. There, they anchor themselves into the bone-perches. They do not sit or lie down; they clamp their triple-jointed feet—which end in three heavy, curved talons capable of crushing quartz—around the glider-ribs, hanging suspended over the six-thousand-meter void like sleeping bats made of leather and scrap-iron. They fold their long limbs tight against their torsos, close their mica eye-plates, and allow the constant vibration of the cliff to rock them into a state of low-metabolic torpor.

When the thermal pulses peak—marked by the sudden, violent crescendo of the bellow-piths expanding—they move with terrifying, fluid speed. 

They do not walk along the stone avenues if they can avoid it; the yellow friction-crust, though brittle, is precious to them for the heat-sparks it produces when struck, which they use to bake plant-grease into structural resin. Instead, they travel along the rust-red cable-tendrils. Hooking their forearms over the dry, vibrating lines, they zip from pillar to plant like iron shuttles on a loom, using the force of the rising air-pulses to vault twenty paces at a time.

Their relationship with the *bellow-pith* is a dangerous, violent choreography. Before a plant can burst its seed-cases in a volley of glass-hard beads, a harvester will leap onto the iron-skinned accordions just as they collapse. Using heavy scrapers chipped from quartz, they flay away the thick, outer tallow-crust in long, curling sheets, jamming bone tubes deep into the plant’s compressed air-bladders to siphon off the raw, volatile lipids before the next *whump-clack* sends them flying. The notches on the quartz handles are not records of days or months—for up here, time has no seasons—but tallies of plant-bladders drained, a measure of wealth in a world where grease is life, lubricant, and shelter.

They are not the only large beings inhabiting the High Horn, though they are the only ones who use tools.

Farther out, where the ledge vanishes into the violet soup of the abyss, live the **Chasm-Gliders** whose bones build the perches. These are vast, pale creatures, thirty meters from tip to tip, built like living kites. They have no legs at all—they are born in the air and die in the air, spending centuries riding the six-thousand-meter thermal elevator. Their skeletons are hollow cages of fused silica, light as foam but rigid as tempered glass, stretched over with a dry, parchment-like membrane that hums in the gale. 

The harvesters hunt the gliders not with weapons, but with the cairns. 

The cairn at the southern lip of the shelf, with its embedded, whistling jawbone, is a navigational beacon and a trap. When the wind hits a specific velocity, the jaw emits a high, screaming pitch that shatters the delicate, mica-thin balancing membranes inside a glider's acoustic skull if it strays too close to the cliff. Disoriented, the giant creature stalls in the thermal, tumbling sideways until its wing-membrane strikes the jagged pillars of the Horn. It tears itself to pieces against the basalt, falling onto the ledge in a ruin of pale ribs and dry, rattling skin.

Within minutes, the harvesters descend upon the carcass. They do not eat the meat—there is no meat, only dry, fibrous muscle like frayed rope. They harvest the ribs to build new perches, the gut-thongs to bind them, and the huge, hollow jawbones to set into the rock face as new wind-singers, mapping the invisible currents of the void.

Below them, sweeping through the lower air, the ribbon-snakes act as scavengers of the mess, darting in to scoop up the shattered silica-marrow before it can fall into the violet silt below. The jet-beetles rasp their iron legs in a frenzy, drawn by the smell of crushed glider-lipid, their synchronized scraping rising until the very cliff seems to be grinding its teeth.

This is who lives on the High Horn: a culture of dry, oil-sweating acrobats, bound to a world without moisture, living inside a perpetual engine of wind, dust, and bone. They do not look down at the violet silt, for there is nothing down there that can support the weight of a stone or the grip of a claw. They look only sideways along the tendrils, and upward into the copper twilight, waiting for the next pulse of the abyss to lift them.
