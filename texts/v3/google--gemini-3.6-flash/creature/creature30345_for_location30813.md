To see them at first, you must unlearn how the eye looks for a person. 

In this basin, a figure standing against a wet slate wall does not break the silhouette of the stone; it *is* the stone, slightly softened by time and oil. They are the **Basiners**—though they have no word for themselves that separates them from the slate beneath their feet—a people who have evolved or adapted so entirely to this freezing, water-logged trench that their biology and their architecture are virtually the same discipline.

### Physicality and Form
They are low-slung and broad-bodied, rarely rising higher than five feet, with a low center of gravity built for scrambling across slick, moss-grown flagstones and ducking through narrow lintels. Their skeletons are heavy and dense—their bones thicker than those of surface-dwelling humans, carrying the vast muscle mass required to lift, split, and dry-stack tons of schist without the aid of beasts of burden or iron levers.

Their skin is thick, hairless, and cold to the touch, resembling smooth, rain-slicked river granite or dark slate. It is naturally hydrophobic, secretes a thin, odorless lipid coat that causes the mist to bead and roll off their shoulders rather than soak into their flesh. Where the skin folds over joints—knuckles, knees, the back of the neck—it is dappled with faint, lichen-like patches of grey and pale green, a symbiotic micro-flora that thrives in the damp folds of their skin.

Their hands are marvels of functional geometry. The fingers are short, blunt, and immensely strong, tipped with thick, spade-shaped nails of dark keratin that are kept square and worn from constant contact with raw rock. Their palms are cushioned with thick fat-pads that allow them to grip frozen, sharp-edged basalt without tearing or losing sensation. They possess a startlingly refined tactile sense: a Basiner can place a flat palm against a newly split slab and tell, by micro-vibrations and temperature variations, where the rock will shear next or whether a stacked wall is bearing weight unevenly.

Their eyes are large, set wide in broad, flat-featured faces with minimal noses—their nasal passages are internal and complex, designed to warm and dehumidify the freezing air before it hits their lungs. The irises are a pale, milky silver, with pupils that expand to swallow the dim, shadowless grey light of the basin. They do not blink as often as surface-dwellers; a clear, translucent membrane sweeps laterally across their eyes to clear condensation without breaking their vision.

### Movement and Voice
They move with a silent, fluid economy. There is no stomping, no heavy heel-strikes on the wet flagstones. Their bare feet are broad and flat, with flexible, widely splayed toes that can grip the rounded tops of river cobbles like leather soles. A dozen of them can move down a narrow, water-filled sluiceway together, passing each other in silence, their bodies gliding past one another with no more noise than the rustle of a wet fern.

Speech is almost entirely absent in the way air-breathing surface cultures understand it. Loud noises in the basin bounce off the sheer basalt cliffs, echoing destructively and terrifying the nesting birds. Instead, their language is a low-frequency hum produced deep within the chest—a sub-audible vibration that travels better through the stone beneath their feet than through the heavy, damp air. When two Basiners touch foreheads or place their palms against the same lintel stone, they can communicate complex structural, environmental, or social information through subtle taps and resonant hums. To a stranger, they appear entirely mute, operating as a single, telepathically coordinated organism.

### Attire and Adornment
Because they do not burn wood or charcoal, they wear their warmth. Their garments are heavy, complex, and entirely waterproof. 

Directly against their skin, they wear tunics spun from the softest under-down of the dark sea-birds that nest in their walls, held together with bone needles and thread made of dried trout-gut. Over this goes a heavy, woven mantle of oily goat’s wool—spun so tightly it is stiff as felt—which has been rubbed with rendered animal fat and peat-ash to make it impervious to the constant drizzle. 

They wear no metal ornament. Their jewelry is functional: carved granite pendants that act as plumb-bobs for stone-laying, bone whistles used to call goat herds across the roof-terraces, and rings of polished dark basalt worn on the knuckles to protect them when prying rocks from the canyon floor.

### Domestic Life in the Wet Cold
Life is lived in the low, damp spaces beneath the turf roofs. Because there are no fires, their homes are insulated by sheer mass—walls three feet thick, packed with wet clay and peat between double layers of stone.

Inside a dwelling, the ambient temperature is steady, held just above freezing by the sheer thermal mass of the earth and the heat of their own low, efficient metabolisms. Cold water runs directly through a carved stone channel in the middle of the main living space. This is not a nuisance; it is their primary utility. The flowing spring water brings fresh trout directly into the room, carries away waste, and keeps the air inside at a constant, predictable humidity.

*   **Diet:** They eat cold, dense foods. Trout are taken from the indoor streams using bone-barbed hand-lines, eaten raw or preserved in cold brine and peat-acid. They harvest the thick, oily milk of the roof-goats, fermenting it into heavy, hard cheeses in cold stone cellars. They gather bitter watercress, the fat rhizomes of butterbur, and various cold-tolerant mosses that are mashed into dense, starchy pastes using granite mortars. 
*   **Sleep:** They sleep together in deep, communal wall-niches lined with layers of dried moss, bird-down, and heavy goat-pelts. They entwine their bodies to conserve heat, their slow, heavy breathing sounding like the gentle tide of water moving through a gravel bar.

### The Culture of Stone
To these inhabitants, building is not an industry or a historic event; it is a continuous, ambient biological function, much like a coral reef building itself outward. 

They do not "own" houses. A family might live in a lower-tier stone cellar for three generations while the cliff face above them slowly drops fresh scree. As new slate shatters from the plateau rim above and slides into the basin, the Basiners immediately harvest it. The younger, stronger individuals carry the heavy slabs down the terraces, while the elders—whose hands have become so calloused they look like carved horn—feel the edges and dry-stack them into the next ring of walls.

They have an almost religious reverence for the flow of water. To block a spring is considered a spatial crime; water must always be given a path. If a new leak springs from the basalt cliff, the city shifts to accommodate it. They carve new gutters overnight, drop a new flight of stairs alongside the flow, and stack new homes around the cascade, treating the water as the bone structure around which their stone flesh must grow.

They live long, slow lives. Free from the smoke-lung and fire-hazards of other pre-industrial peoples, their primary mortality comes from the mountain itself—the sudden, silent shift of an unstable cliff face or a fall from a slick upper terrace. When a Basiner dies, they are not buried in the earth, for there is no earth to spare. They are laid into the hollow interior of a newly rising terrace wall, their bodies wrapped in reed mats and sealed beneath a heavy slab of schist, becoming, quite literally, part of the structural foundation upon which their children step to reach the daylight.
