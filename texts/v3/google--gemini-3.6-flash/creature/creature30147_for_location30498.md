They are not creatures of bark, though from a distance you might mistake them for peeled birch or winter-blanched tallow. Their skin is animal flesh—smooth, thick, and rubbery, lacking all hair, slicked with a thin, unctuous grease that smells faintly of cold lard and crushed juniper berries. It is a protective lipid they secrete from glands behind their ears and beneath their nails, an oil that repels the constant, freezing mist so that water never rests on their skin long enough to draw away their precious core heat.

They are narrow-shouldered and long-limbed, built like the gaps between the peat-walls they inhabit. A mature adult stands shorter than a man, but their torsos are deep-ribbed and barrel-shaped, housing oversized lungs that breathe the thin, fog-heavy air without gasping. They move with an unsettling, fluid silence. Their feet are broad, flat, and boneless in appearance, soles padded with a dense, fibrous callus that grips the slippery, iron-hard mats of creeping root-pavement without a sound. They do not walk so much as flow through the city’s anatomy, leaning forward, their forearms—unusually long and double-jointed at the wrist—hanging low to brush the bark-walls as they go.

Their eyes are the largest features on their pale, wedge-shaped faces: wide, lidless-looking orbs with pale, cloud-gray irises that reflect the faint, greenish bioluminescence of the arch-lichen. A clear, cold-resistant membrane sweeps across the eye horizontally to clear ice-dust without the need to blink. They have no visible outer ears, only angled slits behind the jawbone, but their sensitivity to acoustic vibration is extraordinary. They do not look up when a stranger approaches; they feel the strain of the juniper-root mats three tiers away, reading the weight and gait of the tread through the soles of their feet.

In this city, tools are an extension of anatomy rather than forged steel. Their hands are their primary instruments—elongated, six-digit hands tipped not with flat nails, but with thick, chisel-shaped horn-spurs. With these, they scratch, scrape, and direct the growth of living wood. They do not cut branches; they *steer* them. 

A citizen—if such a word applies to creatures who are less inhabitants and more mobile organs of the burg—can be found crouched for hours beside a expanding larch-buttress. Their warm, grease-coated palms are pressed firmly against a swelling cambium layer, applying steady, gentle pressure while singing a low, single-pitched hum from deep within their sternum. This hum is not music; it is a mechanical signal, a micro-vibration that alters the flow of sap within the branch, encouraging the wood to grain left instead of right, to curve into an archway rather than crack under the winter load.

Their attire is entirely organic and unspun. They wear close-fitting tunics made of beaten, oil-cured bark-cloth, lined with the shed grease-down scraped from the giant pale beetles that share their alleys. These garments are never washed, only re-greased and stitched with fine, thread-like hair-roots using bone needles. Over their shoulders, many wear heavy mantles of dried, compressed liverwort that trap stagnant air, creating a micro-climate of modest warmth around their throats and spines.

Because the damp air kills spoken sound—muffled instantly by the porous moss walls—their language is largely tactile and acoustic-structural. They communicate across the city by tapping. A series of sharp, rhythmic strikes with a horn-nail against the hollow wall of a cedar water-pipe will travel hundreds of yards through the damp timber network. A watcher in the lower cell-chambers, miles of root away, lays a hand against the wood and reads the message in his palm: *Cistern four is thick with needle-ice; divert the sap-line.* 

When they speak face-to-face, it is in a harsh, dry whisper that rarely rises above the sound of falling frost. They have no names for individual persons in the way lowlanders do. Instead, an individual is designated by their functional coordinate within the city’s growth—*Third-Tier-South-Cambium*, or *Duct-Keeper-Above-the-Shale*. If a person moves from the upper canopy down to the deep peat-fermenters, their designation changes to reflect their new physiological duty.

Their diet is simple, cold, and dark. They do not cook; fire is a catastrophe they cannot afford in a city made of resinous timber and dried peat. They feed upon the dark, sweet, gelatinous fungus that they cultivate in the deep, lightless peat-bins—a rubbery bloom that feeds on the slow decomposition of ancient needles and wood-dust. They supplement this with the thick, anti-freeze sap tapped sparingly from the vine-bridges, a bitter, amber syrup rich in sugars that burns hot in their bellies. Occasionally, when a giant grease-beetle reaches the end of its life, its fat is rendered cold by compression between heavy larch-presses, providing the tallow that coats their skin and fuels their slow, subterranean metabolism.

They do not sleep as mammals do. Instead, they enter a state of local torpor. A worker will wedge themselves into a tight recess between two overlapping cedar roots, draw their liverwort mantle tight, and drop their heart rate to three beats a minute. For eight hours, their body temperature drops until frost forms on their cheekbones, matching the temperature of the surrounding wood. To an outsider, they appear dead, a pale statue half-swallowed by moss. But as the morning mist begins to trickle through the root-aqueducts, a chemical pulse triggers their core; the frost melts from their eyelashes, their pale eyes roll open, and they slide out of the crevice to resume their endless, silent sculpting.

Their relationship with death is entirely structural. They do not bury their dead in stone, nor do they burn them. When an inhabitant’s heart finally fails—usually when their joints stiffen so far that they can no longer climb the vine-footways—they are carried down to the lowest tiers, where the city rests against the sheer, dark shale. 

There, in the damp, fermenting warmth of the deep peat-bins, the body is laid flat against a weakening wall or a sagging floor-joist. The living wrap the deceased in fresh liverwort, pin their long fingers into the surrounding grain with bone-splints, and pour a thick paste of crushed lichen spores and wet peat over the face and torso. Within months, the cold, fibrous mycelium weaves through the flesh. The calcium in their bones hardens and reinforces the wood; their tissues decompose into the rich, nutrient-dense humus that feeds the upper canopy. 

A master builder walking the third tier three generations later might lay his grease-slicked palm against a heavy, load-bearing archway, feel the dense, calcified knot within the heartwood, and know precisely whose grandfather holds the ceiling above his head. Nothing is wasted. The city is built of its ancestors, inhabited by its children, and steered by those who are slowly becoming the very floor they walk upon.
