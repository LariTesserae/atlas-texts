To understand what lives here, one must first abandon the idea of soft tissue. Nothing in the basin breathes air to fill a lung, nor does anything possess a pulse that could be stopped by a wound. The life that occupies this granite dish is entirely mineral, cold-jointed, and frictionless, existing not *in spite* of the iron and brick, but as an extension of their static weight.

They are called nothing—there is no voice here to name them—but in their structure, they are the **Porcelain-Backs**, the **Bronze-Spurs**, and the **Sump-Lurchers**.

***

### The White Carapaces (The Swarm)

The porcelain-shelled creatures that cover the flagstones are the baseline population. Close up, their bone-white enamel carapaces are not biological bone, but a dense, low-expansion ceramic, smooth as a teacup and cool to the touch. They have no faces, no mouths in any organic sense, and no eyes.

Where an head might be, there is only a rounded, seamless dome of white glaze, polished by endless contact with the damp iron girders. Beneath the overhang of this dome sits a transverse slot fitted with three heavy, dark-grey lamellae made of folded lead. These plates do not taste or smell; they read vibration. As the steam-driven beams thump inside the brick halls, these lead leaves expand and contract in micro-fractional increments, steering the creature along lines of structural stress in the granite floor.

Their underbellies are open: a shallow, dished plate of dull bell-metal, held off the stone by six thick legs. The legs do not end in claws or pads, but in solid, hemispherical studs of yellow bronze. When they walk, they do not lift their feet high; they drive them down with a sharp, hydraulic *tink*, six identical impacts per step, millions of times an hour. The hollowed-out stone steps of the valve-house were not worn down by water, but by these bronze toes repeating the same landing pattern for four hundred years.

Inside the shell, there are no organs, no intestines, no nerves. There is only a slow, sealed loop of cold, heavy fluid—a liquid compound of mercury, petroleum, and dissolved tin—that circulates through micro-bored brass tubes by the action of the basin’s ambient vibration. The noise of the great pumps is their heart; if the three steam beams were ever to stop, the liquid inside the carapaces would settle, cool by half a degree, and thicken into a inert, grey paste, locking every joint in place forever.

They feed on the wear of the place. When two carapaces crush together in the bottleneck between a brick wall and a drive-shaft housing, they do not fight or recoil. They slide past each other under enormous lateral pressure, their porcelain flanks grinding with a dry, ceramic squeal. The microscopic dust produced by this friction—fine white enamel powder—is drawn into the leg-joints of the creatures below them by the film of wet oil, where it acts as a polishing compound, keeping their internal ball-and-socket joints from seizing.

***

### The Cable-Clingers

Higher up, suspended above the deafening churn of the flagstones, a second, specialized variant inhabits the elevated iron walkways and the web of telegraph lines that spans the sky between the mill halls.

These are smaller—the size of a heavy iron mortar-shell—and much flatter. Their shells are not white, but a dull, unglazed stoneware grey that absorbs the flat white light without a reflection. Where the ground-dwellers have six bronze legs, the Cable-Clingers have four long, semi-flexible ribs of laminated spring-steel that curve completely around a pipe or wire like the fingers of a dead hand.

They do not travel in loops. They move only when the pitch of the steam-exhaust changes. When a jet of white condensation spatters against the brick, a wave of motion passes through the wire-strung colony: thousands of grey disks shift three inches to the left in absolute unison, their steel ribs clicking against the copper wire with the sound of a deck of cards being shuffled by an iron hand.

Their function is balance. They nest in dense, overlapping shingle-patterns over the riveted seams of the elevated walkways. Wherever a iron plate threatens to work loose from its rivets under the constant vibration of the pumps, three hundred Cable-Clingers pack themselves into the seam, wedging their tapered bodies into the gap until their carapaces form a continuous, load-bearing shim of solid glaze. They become the metal they rest upon.

***

### The Keepers of the Sump

Deep below the flagstones, down where the heavy cast-iron flywheels plunge into the black, oily water of the drainage basin, live the largest entities in the yard.

They never come up into the daylight entirely, though their presence is felt every time a flywheel completes its stroke. These are the **Sump-Lurchers**—massive, multi-jointed masses of lead plate, oak wood preserved in lard, and thick iron hoops that have rusted into absolute stability. They are the size of boiler tanks, lying half-submerged in the cold, black slush beneath the granite floor.

They have no legs at all. Instead, they are built around central, hollow axles that locked onto the submerged pins of the pump machinery centuries ago. As the flywheels turn, the Sump-Lurchers are dragged through the water in slow, fifty-second revolutions, silently scooping the heavy mineral sludge from the bottom of the basin with large, brass-lipped troughs built into their flanks.

They lift this sludge—a thick, silver-grey paste composed of pulverized iron, oil, and mineral salts—and deposit it directly into the open drive-gears that emerge from the granite floor. They are the lubricators, the self-appointed grease-injectors of the system. Without them, the dry iron gears would weld themselves together in a cloud of sparks within three hours.

When a Sump-Lurcher surfaces from the dark pool, riding the rim of a main gear, it brings with it a sound like a heavy canvas sail being dragged through wet gravel. It pauses for six seconds at the apex of the stroke, drops three gallons of black, odorless grease onto the teeth of the drive-shaft, and slides backward down into the drown-hole with a soft, heavy *gulp*.

***

### The Interior Life of the Halls

Inside the four red-brick mill halls, where the light does not reach and the sound of the steam beams is loud enough to crack human tooth-enamel, resides the apex of this cold ecosystem.

There are no small creatures inside the halls. The air is too dense with oil-mist and high-pressure steam. Instead, the interiors are occupied by the **Valve-Gaskets**—flat, leather-skinned organisms five yards across that cling directly to the faces of the massive iron cylinders.

These creatures are made of thick, vegetable-tanned hide and cured tallow, reinforced with copper wire stitches that look like surgeon’s sutures. They do not move from their spots for decades. They live by absorbing the high-pressure steam leaks that escape from the cylinder packing-boxes. As the live steam passes through their dense, fibrous bodies, it drops its load of dissolved lime and silica, which the Gaskets deposit into their own skins, gradually turning themselves into solid stone rings that seal the very leaks they feed upon.

When a Valve-Gasket becomes fully mineralized—when every pore of its tallow-soaked hide has been replaced by grey limestone—it loses its flexibility. The next downward stroke of the iron beam strikes it with thirty tons of force, shattering the hardened organism into ten thousand identical, cube-shaped gravel chips.

These chips drop through the floor grates directly into the paths of the Porcelain-Backs waiting outside. The ground-dwellers sweep up the fragments with their bronze legs, crushing them into the granite flagstones, filling every hair-line crack in the pavement until the floor is smooth, flat, and unbroken once more.

***

### The Equilibrium

There is no hunger here, because there is no expenditure of waste. Nothing rots. A Porcelain-Back that is crushed under a descending counter-weight does not die; its pieces simply become part of the pavement, smoothed down by the bronze feet of its companions until the surface is level again.

They do not know the white sky exists. They do not know the mill halls are made of brick or that the water comes from three canals that go nowhere. They are simply the moving parts of an engine that forgot its original product, operating at peak efficiency, cold, deafening, and absolute, long after the last reason for their creation has turned to dust.
