The people who inhabit Oakhaven-on-the-Spire do not walk so much as they anchor themselves.

To look at a Spire-dweller is to see an anatomy shaped entirely by verticality, heat, and thin air. They are long-limbed and deep-chested, their ribs expanded to catch what little oxygen the seven-thousand-foot altitude offers. Their skin is dry, leathery, and dark—not from melanin alone, but stained permanently by the microscopic soot of burnt mineral grease and the fine, yellow dusting of airborne sulfur. 

They do not wear cloth as lower worlds know it. Woven fibers rot too quickly in the lukewarm, chemical-rich condensation that coats the city. Instead, they dress in supple, oil-cured hide—the tanned skins of the high-altitude gliders—and aprons of beaten copper sheet that deflect the scald of unshielded steam pipes. Their boots are seamless leather, thick-soled and soft-bottomed, tipped at the toe and heel with iron cleats that drop into the diamond-patterned serrations of the walkway grates with a quiet, certain *thunk*.

A Spire-dweller’s most vital organ is his belt. It is a heavy harness of layered steer-hide, three inches thick, fitted with three brass carabiners forged wide enough to loop around a two-inch steam pipe in the dark. No one in Oakhaven moves three paces without shifting a clip. Children learn the rhythmic, double-click of the safety latch before they learn full sentences. To be unclipped, even inside a residential cell, is considered a form of madness—a morbid flirtation with the blue-grey void below.

Because the thin air swallows voice, the people are remarkably quiet. They speak in a low, resonant murmur that relies heavily on throat-tones—sounds that do not dissipate as easily in the rare atmosphere—or they do not speak at all. Over centuries, they have developed *the Iron Tongue*: a complex system of tactile communication. A boiler-tender signals a valve-turner three hundred feet up an open lattice not by shouting, but by striking a copper pipe with a brass rod. *Three sharp raps, a pause, two long slides.* The vibration travels through the metal faster and clearer than a scream ever could, buzzing directly into the palm of anyone holding the handrail.

***

Beside the humans, a stranger, quieter fauna maintains the city’s metal skin.

The brass-shelled crawlers—which the dwellers call *Scabs* or *Lichen-plates*—are not insects, though they resemble broad, wingless beetles the size of a man’s palm. Their carapaces are flat, segmented plates of native iron and copper compound, grown organically through the ingestion of mineral-laden water. They have no eyes, no mouths in the traditional sense, and no lungs. Their undersides are a single, muscular suction pad lined with microscopic, hook-like cilia that grip wet basalt or rusted iron with terrifying force.

They live where the hot steam vents meet the cold rock. As the boiling water forced up from the core leaves behind thick, crusty deposits of bright yellow sulfur and iron oxide, the Scabs crawl slowly over the encrustations, dissolving the mineral crusts with acidic secretions and absorbing the metal ions through their bellies. To the citizens, they are not pests; they are vital maintenance. A colony of Scabs living on a brass sheaf-housing keeps the bearing free of grit. When a Scabs’ life cycle ends—after forty or fifty years of imperceptible movement—it simply stops, its heavy, brassy shell fusing permanently to the iron-work like a native rivet, adding its own small strength to the city’s outer wall.

Farther up, where the chimney stacks die out and the rock returns to raw, un riveted basalt, live the gliders. The Spire-dwellers call them *Soughers*, after the low, dry sound their skin makes against the high wind. 

A Sougher has a wingspan of nine feet, yet its hollow-boned skeleton weighs less than two pounds. Its body is a narrow ribcage of grey cartilage wrapped in a pale, hairless membrane that feels like damp parchment. They have no eyes; their triangular heads are dominated by twin pit-organs that detect heat differentials down to a fraction of a degree. They spend days aloft, never flapping—their wings have no power muscles for flapping—but riding the immense, vertical chimneys of superheated air that pour out of the foundry exhausts. 

They feed on the Scabs, plucking them from the outer pipes with long, needle-sharp talons, and they drink from the high copper gutters where the distilled steam collects before it reaches the cisterns. The dwellers tolerate them because a Sougher’s sudden descent into the lower lattices is an infallible barometer: when the deep earth shifts and a thermal vent closes, the Soughers drop into the residential tiers to huddle against the dwelling boilers, warning the engineers of a pressure drop hours before the brass gauges register the change.

***

Deep inside the dark, wet interior of the city—where the rock and iron are bolted together and sunlight never reaches—there is a third form of life, one that never touches the air.

Inside the three-foot-wide copper water lines, in the perpetual 180-degree darkness of the thermal loop, thrives a dense, fibrous organism known as *Tallow-moss*. It is neither plant nor fungus, but a dense, rubbery colony of chemosynthetic bacteria that forms long, cream-colored ropes inside the pipes. It feeds on the dissolved nitrates and hydrogen sulfide forced up from the deep fractures.

To a lower-world plumber, Tallow-moss would be a nightmare, a cloggart that chokes the veins of the city. To Oakhaven, it is life. The moss acts as a living filter, stripping the corrosive compounds from the volcanic steam before it can eat through the copper walls of the boilers. Every five years, a pipe segment is unbolted, and special crews—the *Scrapers*—reach into the steaming, dark cylinders with long-handled iron spades to haul out hundred-pound hanks of the pale, rubbery stuff. Dried over the furnace vents, the moss hardens into a dense, oily material that can be carved, machined, or boiled down into the heavy, black grease that keeps the fifty-ton counterweights sliding silently past the windows day after day.

***

In their private lives, inside the narrow, iron-plated cells suspended over the six-thousand-foot drop, the people live with an austere, deliberate elegance. 

Because space is tight and weight is a constant structural hazard, they own almost nothing that cannot be hung from a wall peg or folded flat against an iron bulkhead. Their furniture is mahogany and brass—wood is prized above gold here, imported at ruinous cost via the high cable-car lines from the low, humid distant ridges, and kept constantly greased to prevent it from splitting in the dry, hot air.

A Spire household is illuminated not by fire—oxygen is too precious, and open flame is a hazard around sulfur dust—but by *Lume-tubes*: sealed glass cylinders filled with water, sulfur, and tiny, bioluminescent colonies of deep-vent microbes harvested from the cistern bottoms. They cast a soft, cold, greenish light across the riveted iron walls, revealing the wet gleam of the condensation that trickles down the outer plates.

When the dwellers rest, they sleep in hammocks woven from Sougher-tendon, suspended from the structural ceiling beams. As they sway gently in rhythm with the wind that groans through the steel lattice outside, they feel the slow, rhythmic *pulse-thrum* of the great steam-pistons three miles away—a deep, subsonic heartbeat that travels through the iron bones of the mountain.

They do not dream of trees, or oceans, or flat land. To a child born on the Spire, the idea of a world that spreads out horizontally, without ropes, anchors, or a wall at one's back, is a terrifying nightmare—a vision of a terrifying, unstable chaos. True safety is a four-inch copper bolt, driven deep into black amphibolite, hot to the touch, sweating pure water, and holding fast against the void.
