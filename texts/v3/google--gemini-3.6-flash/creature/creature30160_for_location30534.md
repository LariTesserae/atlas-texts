The people who inhabit the bowl are compact, wide-chested, and low-slung, their bodies forged by thousands of years of stepping up stone and ducking under timber. Their ribs flare outward in deep, barrel-shaped arcs, housing lungs of extraordinary volume that draw easily from the warm, thin air. 

Their skin is the dark, burnished color of wet basalt, oiled daily with the clear fat of the mountain fowl to protect against the bone-dry heat. In the soft, copper-tinted twilight of the basin, their eyes appear almost entirely pupil—wide, luminous, and slow to blink. They walk with an effortless, rolling crouch, their wide, flat feet bare and thick-soled as horn. The undersides of their toes are calloused into tough, grippy pads that can find purchase on a sliver of worn granite no wider than a thumbnail.

Because there is no open water to drink, the people do not know the act of pouring or splashing. Moisture is an essence to be extracted, saved, and chewed. At dawn, before the ambient heat warms the stone, the women and young boys move through the upper terraces with flat, wide spatulas carved from swift-bone. They scrape the heavy condensation that has gathered overnight from the curtains of lichen hanging across the doorways, collecting a grey, sweetish sludge into gourd-like dried succulents. 

Water is also chewed from the fat, pale veins of the floor-shrubs. A basin-dweller will break off a finger-thick leaf of *wax-flesh*, slice its bitter skin away with a sliver of flint, and hold the clear, jelly-like core between their teeth for an hour, letting the cool sap bleed slowly down their throat. Their bodies are miserly with fluids; they do not sweat as lowlanders do, and their breath, exhaled into the tight, communal sleeping-cells, is captured by woven mats of scrub-fiber lined with dried moss to be squeezed out at mid-morning.

Alongside the humans, the yellow cliff-lizards are the most visible citizens of the terraces. Known locally as *clackers* for the sharp, wood-on-stone sound of their claws, they are neither pets nor wild pests, but familiar, tolerated roommates. They grow to the length of a man’s forearm, with loose, dusty skin that folds along their ribs like pleated cloth. They spend the early morning strung out along the eastern rim of the bowl, absorbing the first pale rays of sun that hit the high lip, before descending into the village as the heat builds.

The lizards move through the lower dwellings with bold, jerking familiarity. They feed on the thick, flightless beetles that thrive in the moist mulch beneath the succulent beds, and on the small, pale moths that emerge from the resin-wood at dusk. Children pull their tails casually out of the way of doorway thresholds; elders flick root-parings to them with a click of the tongue. In return, the lizards keep the clay walls free of burrowing grubs. When a lizard grows old and its dusty yellow scales turn a dull, chalky grey, it is caught without fuss. Its tough skin is dried and rolled into cordage or thongs for sandals, its oil is rendered down for skin-salve, and its stringy, resin-scented meat is pounded into the root-pulp in the stone mortars.

High above the wasp-nest dwellings, the porous granite brow belongs to the swifts. They are small, dark birds with long, scythe-like wings that never seem to fold entirely flat. By the tens of thousands, they nest in the natural honeycombs of the upper cliff. They are the basin's only link to the invisible world below the cloud-deck; every morning at first light, they drop out of the bowl in a deafening, whistling rushing-sound, falling into the sheer air below to hunt insects over the low forests and distant marshes miles beneath the mountain's knees. 

When they return at dusk, tumbling over the stone lip like handfuls of black gravel thrown into the basin, their crops are full of water-swollen midges and high-altitude draft-bugs. The village below relies on their waste. A steady, dry, ammonia-scented rain of grey bird-guano falls from the upper cliffs onto the highest garden ledges, where it is swept up with stiff brush-brooms and worked into the shallow beds of waxy shrubs. Without the swifts bringing the nutrient of the lower world up into the stone sky, the basin’s dense green gardens would wither into dust in a single generation.

On the floor of the bowl, strutting through the alleyways with heavy, comical dignity, are the mountain fowl. Broad-bodied and dense, covered in a double layer of slate-grey and copper down, they are entirely flightless. Their wings have withered into short, muscular paddles that they use only for balance when springing from bench to bench. They nest beneath the ironwood ladders and in the dark alcoves behind the mortars, living on fallen seeds, crushed lizard-bone, and the bitter husks of chewed succulents. 

The fowl are the heartbeat of the basin’s domestic life. Their low, rhythmic clucking—a warm, wet *crrr-crrr* that vibrates deep in their chests—is the background noise to every conversation. Their eggs, small and speckled like granite, have shells so thick they must be cracked with a stone hammer; the yolks are bright, oily orange, tasting faintly of turpentine and dried mint.

In the shadows beneath the wax-leaf canopy, life is intensely social, lived entirely in the public acoustic chamber of the stone bowl. Because the amphitheater bounces every sound across its diameter, there is no such thing as a secret whisper. A disagreement on the bottom tier is heard clearly on the seventh; a baby crying in the western shadow is answered by a lullaby sung on the eastern ledge. 

As a result, the people have developed a language that sounds less like speech and more like a muted, low-pitched chanting. Vowels are held long and warm; hard consonants are avoided because they click loudly off the basalt walls. They communicate across distances not by shouting, but by pitching their voices into specific, smooth hollows in the rock that bounce the sound directly to a corresponding alcove on the far side. 

Time among them is not tracked by months or years, for there are no seasons at the crown of the world. Instead, age is measured in physical wear: the depth to which a family’s doorway threshold has been dished out by their heels; the thickness of the resin-crust on the hearth-stones; the softening of an elder’s teeth from decades of chewing bitter succulent skin. 

When a person dies, they are not buried—there is no earth deep enough to hold them—nor burned, for wood is too precious to waste on fire. Instead, they are washed with bird-oil, wrapped tightly in braided lichen-cloth, and carried up single-file ladders to the very highest niches under the granite brow, where the dry, warm wind shears endlessly across the lip. There, in the high, quiet shadows above the whistling swifts, they sit side-by-side in narrow stone niches, their skin slowly drying into hard, dark leather, watching over the small, loud, crowded cradle of the living forever.
