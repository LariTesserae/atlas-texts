To understand what lives here, you must first look beneath the green mattress, into the dark, silent space between the bottom of the moss roots and the flat face of the basalt. 

In that two-inch gap of black, roaring sheet-water live the **ribbon-glassers**. They are flat as pressed paper, three feet long, and entirely transparent except for their copper-colored gullets. They have no eyes—light never reaches the undersides of the turf—and no lungs; they absorb dissolved oxygen directly through their slick, cartilaginous skin as the freezing meltwater washes over them. They anchor themselves to the slick basalt with rows of iron-hard hooks along their flanks, pointing their triangular mouths upstream to catch the microscopic detritus scraped from the rock by the sheer force of the tide. When the pump-houses stroke—that heavy, distant *clack-thrum-hiss*—the pulse travels through the water and the stone simultaneously, and the glassers contract in unison, a subterranean rippling of thousands of pale, flexed ribbons, marking time by the engines.

Above them, on the surface of the green mantle where the light is a blinding, white pressure, life must protect itself from being burned or blown away.

The primary grazers of the turf are the **spade-vessels**. Low to the ground, no taller than a man’s knee but broad as a dining table, their bodies are shaped like overturned iron skiffs. Their hides are thick, pebbled leather the exact grey-black of the wet basalt, coated in a thick, secreted grease that sheds the cold water and reflects the fierce UV radiation of the thin sky. They do not walk so much as float across the moss; their four legs end in broad, dished plates of spongy cartilage—a foot across—that distribute their weight so perfectly they barely compress the liverwort beneath them. 

The spade-vessels are completely deaf. The air-roar of the precipices and the steam-screams long ago rendered delicate tympanic membranes useless. Instead, their lower jaws rest flush against the wet turf as they graze, receiving the world as a tactile map of vibrations. They can distinguish the gentle, rhythmic drag of a steel power-cable half a league away from the sharp, sudden vibration of a split seam in an iron flume. They feed on the silver-stemmed liverwort, stripping it with broad plates of orange enamel, their digestive systems so slow and heat-efficient that their flanks give off a faint, sweet mist of warm steam in the crisp air.

Higher up, in the aerial lakes held by the saucer-leaves fifty yards above the turf, resides an entirely separate, fragile ecosystem. 

Here, in the cold, clear reservoirs perched at the tops of the vascular pillars, live the **sky-skimmers**. They are lungless, bird-like quadrupeds no larger than a fox, with needle-thin bones made of hollow glass-like chitin and broad, translucent skin-sails stretched between their elongated ribs. They never touch the moss below; to fall to the ground is to drown in the dense, wet turf or be crushed by the wind. They live out their lives in the canopy of a single pillar, swimming through the elevated pools, feeding on airborne spores, and laying gelatinous eggs on the undersides of the giant leaves. When a saucer-leaf over-fills and spills its heavy, crystal cords of water to the plateau below, the sky-skimmers launch themselves into the glare, catching the rising thermal vents from the copper condenser towers, drifting on wide, clear wings until they can slide into the bowl of a neighboring pillar.

Where the organic world meets the riveted ironwork, life becomes strange and parasitic.

The long, steel power-cables—moving at their creeping, almost invisible pace across the plateau—have created their own nomadic fauna. The **cable-lice** are hard-shelled, segmented creatures the size of iron kettles, equipped with heavy, screw-threaded mandibles. They clamp onto the moving steel cables near the pump-houses and allow themselves to be hauled slowly across the leagues of moss, completely inert, bathed in the sun. As they travel, they scrape the tallow grease from the steel and filter the iron-dust ground off by the pulleys. A cable-louse may take three weeks to travel from a condenser tower to an outer precipice, doing nothing but soaking in the intense light and the oil of the machinery, before detaching itself to drop into the splash-pool of an engine housing to mate.

And inside the three-story, oxide-red pump-houses, where the air is thirty degrees warmer and smells of rich coal-smoke, wet cylinder tallow, and hot bronze, there are the **Keepers**.

They are human, or descended from the surveyors and engineers who bolted the first iron footings into the basalt a century ago, though they have changed to fit the scale of the place. There are rarely more than two or three to a node. They are thick-set, short-limbed, and walk with a heavy, rolling gait designed to keep their footing on iron grating slick with condensed steam and lard. 

The Keepers do not speak. The deafening *clack-thrum-hiss* of the four-foot pistons makes vocal language impossible, so they communicate in a swift, complex dialect of hand-signs and heavy iron rods, which they strike against the steam conduits to send ringing, metallic messages down the lines to one another through the pipe network. 

Their eyes are perpetually shadowed behind heavy brass goggles fitted with dark, smoked-quartz lenses to guard against the sun glare outside and the white flare of the furnace-doors within. Their skin, where exposed, is dark as cured oak, weathered by ozone and sun, and perpetually stained with red iron-oxide and black cylinder grease. 

They live in small, wood-paneled chambers built directly against the warm bellies of the iron boilers, where the tea in their tin cups shivers in rhythm with the engine's stroke. They do not farm; they eat preserved salt-pork, compressed oat-cake, and the pale, starchy tubers that grow in the dark scalds where the hot engine-runoff empties into the moss. 

The Keepers view the plateau not as a wilderness, nor as a workplace, but as a vast, living organ that must be kept oiled and cleared of rot. They move along the elevated flumes with long-handled tallow brushes and bronze scrapers, tending the iron skin of the engines, replacing worn leather gaskets, and painting over the weeping rust seams with fresh lead-gray. 

When a Keeper dies, they do not bury them—for there is no soil, only the five-foot mattress of wet moss and the hard, unyielding stone. Instead, they carry the body to the top of the highest condenser tower and slide it into the main intake pipe. The body passes into the vast, dark heart of the subterranean pumping system, washed away by millions of gallons of icy melt, carried through the darkness beneath the basalt, and finally cast out over the plateau’s outer lip, falling five thousand feet into the warm, forested valleys below, where the earth is soft enough to receive it.
