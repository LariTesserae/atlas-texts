To say the city is uninhabited is to make a mistake about what constitutes a inhabitant. 

There is no blood here, no warm grease in any joint, no lung to take in the thin, wet air. But the plateau is not empty. It is occupied by things that are continuous with the landscape itself—entities born where the weight of old mountains presses against a sky that has forgotten how to warp.

If you sit on the wet lip of a terraced plaza for a three-day frost, you will begin to notice that the slate is not merely resting.

***

First are the **Strata-Sieves**, the largest and slowest among them. 

They are the city’s masons, though they do not hold chisels; they *are* the chisels. A Stratum-Sieve appears, to an unpracticed eye, as a section of dry-stacked terrace wall—a broad, twenty-foot-thick incline of hand-cut slate slabs and granite blocks bound together by liver-red moss and frozen mud. But it is not a wall. It is an individual.

It has no face, no eyes, and no limbs in the biological sense. Its body is an aggregation of thousands of loose, flat stones held in a state of intense, silent gravity. It "breathes" through the capillary pull of the wet mosses carpeted across its flank, drinking tons of ice-melt every hour and expelling it through deep, stony pores as high-pressure mist. 

When a Stratum-Sieve shifts, it does not walk. It flows like a slow-motion landslide that never loses its internal geometry. A ten-ton granite megalith at its center moves three inches to the left; a thousand slate tiles slide over one another with a sound like a deck of heavy cards being shuffled underwater. By morning, the entity has moved four hundred yards across the basalt dome, perfectly intact, leaving behind a rectangular patch of scrubbed, naked bedrock where its weight previously crushed the frost.

The broad, irregular chisel marks covering the stone of the city are not signatures of a dead civilization. They are the friction-scarring left by these beings as they grind against one another in their slow, ceremonial realignments. They are the city, and they are its citizens, building and rebuilding the metropolis as an involuntary consequence of their own weight settling into the crust.

***

Near the deep chasms, where the waist-thick root-cables span the wet air, lives something altogether different—a life that is neither animal nor plant, but a dense, bone-like mineral growth given purpose by the cold.

These are the **Root-Threaders**. They resemble long, jointed armatures of pale grey calcium, braided tight with the fibrous yellow roots of the high mosses. They grow up from the basalt like pale, skeletal ivy, climbing the windowless halls and reaching out across the void. 

They have no nervous system, but they respond to tension and moisture. Where two basalt hummocks pull apart—or where a chasm widens by an eighth of an inch due to deep frost-heave—the Threaders react. Over the course of forty years, a single Threader will extrude thousands of fine, fibrous tendrils, weaving them into the tight, heavy cables that span the gorges. 

The "bone pegs" that fasten the petrified timber beams throughout the city are the shed segments of these creatures. When a Threader grows too large or dry, its older joints calcify, turn white as ivory, and snap off under their own tension, wedging themselves forever into the stone joints below. They are the stitchery holding the plateau’s megaliths against the wind.

***

And then there are the **Shadow-Beings**, which are not made of matter at all, but of the plateau's frayed physics.

Where the air is thinnest and the cobalt sky looks almost black, reality fails to fill its own corners. In the shadow of a broad stone arch, or in the wet angle where two slate walls meet, a presence gathers. It has the weight of an iron anchor, but no physical volume. 

If you step near one, the spatial geometry around you bends like a wet sheet. You might take a single step forward and find yourself forty paces away, standing at the edge of a different avenue, while the shadow itself remains behind, casting its dark, crisp outline at a ninety-degree angle to the sun.

These entities are the ancient "occupants" of the cathedrals. They do not move through space; space folds around them to accommodate their static, heavy contemplation. They are cold—colder even than the air—and they smell faintly of ozone, crushed frost, and very old dust. They collect in the windowless halls in vast, silent congregations, resting against the slate pillars like thick drops of night that refuse to disperse when the fog clears.

***

Nothing here eats. Nothing reproduces through seed or womb. 

Instead, the plateau operates on a cycle of **decay and crystallization**. When a Stratum-Sieve finally wears itself down—when eons of frost and sliding stone grind its slate slabs into fine, black gravel—the moss takes over. The liver-red and lichen-silver carpets consume the dust, digesting the minerals in the freezing moisture, and over centuries, compress the gravel back into dense, layered peat. 

In time, under the immense weight of the drifting fog and the settling stone, that peat petrifies, hardening into new slabs of jet-black slate. The Root-Threaders creep across it, binding it with fresh tendrils, and a new Sieve begins its agonizingly slow crawl across the green velvet tableland.

They exist without urgency. They do not know the continent exists below them, shrouded in its warm, muddy, blood-filled valleys. They know only the scrape of stone on stone, the weight of the mist, the tight grip of root-cables over empty air, and the long, cold centuries spent aligning the city with a sky that never moves.
