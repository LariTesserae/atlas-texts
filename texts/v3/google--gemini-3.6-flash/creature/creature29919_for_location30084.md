In the shallows—the three-millimeter sheets of condensation that pool between the raised diamonds of the steel floor—lives the **Platena**. 

They are creatures without thickness, no thicker than a leaf of beaten silver. To the casual eye, a Platena appears only as a subtle distortion in the water’s surface, a ripple shaped like a long, rounded lancet that moves against the natural flow of the condensation. They possess no eyes, no lungs, and no bones; their bodies are composed of a tough, oily membrane stretched over a delicate lattice of flexible lead wire. 

The Platena slide between the raised steel tread-patterns, never breaking the surface tension of the water. They feed on the microscopic flakes of zinc sloughed off by the damp air and the clear, nutrient-poor jellies secreted by the glass-mold. When two Platena meet in a puddle, they do not fight or mate in any familiar sense; they simply overlap, their silver membranes pressed tight together for hours, exchanging cold liquid humors through osmosis before sliding apart to resume their silent, two-dimensional trajectories across the deck.

***

Where the copper-wound conduits climb the vertical shafts in dense, trunk-like clusters, you will find the **Gaugers**. 

If the mica-shelled scuttlers are the mice of this metal forest, the Gaugers are its solitary, long-limbed hunters. A Gauger stands nearly seven feet tall, but its weight is negligible—less than twenty pounds. Its body is constructed from thin, hollow struts of dark, unpolished titanium, connected by intricate ball-and-socket joints that never require oil. It has no torso to speak of; its four absurdly long, triple-jointed legs converge directly upon a narrow, wedge-shaped head lined with dozens of small, needle-like glass prisms.

The Gauger does not run. It moves with a terrifying, deliberate grace, placing the hardened tips of its legs into the tiny, parallel cutting-striations left by the milling machines. Its movements are completely soundless. It stalks the thickets of copper conduit, searching for the heavy, tin-winged roosting organisms. 

When a Gauger locates a tin-wing asleep on a high beam, it does not strike with violence. It slowly spans the creature with two of its long legs, like a drafting compass measuring a distance on a blueprint. It presses its prism-lined head against the bird’s oiled tin wing and begins to vibrate—a high-frequency, ultrasonic tremor that shatters the delicate, mica-like joints of the bird’s wings without making a sound audible to the human ear. The paralyzed bird slips from the beam, and the Gauger catches it mid-fall with a silent, graceful sweep of a leg, draining the creature’s cold, tallow-like lipids before dropping the hollow foil husk into the dark chasms below.

***

Far stranger are the things that inhabit the spatial errors—the places where perspective widens incorrectly, or where the condensation runs sideways across the vertical pillars. These are the **Siphoners**, and they do not belong entirely to three dimensions.

A Siphoner has no permanent outer envelope. It appears as an oblong, shifting mass of dark, wet ribbon—resembling miles of magnetic tape or black silk drenched in heavy mineral oil—that twists and suspendedly folds upon itself in mid-air. It roosts precisely at the points where the geometry breaks: in the center of catwalks that never grow closer, or anchored to the sides of beams where gravity pulls laterally.

The Siphoners feed on the lag. 

When a living thing—or a shadow—moves through an area where time and space slip out of alignment, the Siphoner draws in the discarded fraction of that movement. If your shadow lags half a second behind your foot, the Siphoner is the reason: it is gently pulling on the dark cloth of the shadow, dragging it across the wet zinc deck, absorbing the friction of the delay. 

They are harmless, but their presence induces a profound, icy vertigo. If you stand near a Siphoner, your ears will not ring, but your inner ear will insist that you are falling upward, while your eyes report that the steel floor beneath your boots is simultaneously two inches and three miles away. The Siphoners acknowledge visitors only by slowly re-orienting their ribbons of dark wet silk, aligning their edges to match the impossible, non-Euclidean seams in the sky above.

***

Deep inside the three-story, cold-pressed steel housings—where the massive internal pistons slide with their muted *hiss-shuck*—dwell the **Sealers**. 

Of all the things that inhabit this wet industrial plateau, the Sealers are the closest to human in scale, though in nothing else. They are squat, fleshy entities, their skin the exact color and texture of wet lard. They have no faces, no limbs, and no hair. Instead, their bodies are shaped like thick, heavy rings or donuts, three to four feet in diameter, wrapped tightly around the major structural tie-rods that bind the machinery housings together.

The Sealers are living gaskets. 

They move only once every few decades, creeping an eighth of an inch along the polished steel rods by contracting their blubbery, oil-soaked bodies. They live entirely on the lukewarm heat and the faint, sub-audible hum emitted by the high-voltage conduits. Their skin secretes a thick, translucent grease that coats the critical joints of the machinery, preventing the damp mountain air from pitting the fresh steel beneath. 

A Sealer feels no pain, makes no sound, and possesses no mind as we understand it. It is an organ of the facility itself, evolved or manufactured to ensure that the unyielding right angles do not rust, that the clean bolt-corners remain knife-sharp, and that the massive, quiet weight of the plateau never grinds itself to a halt.

***

And finally, moving among them all, are the **Monitors**. 

There are perhaps only a dozen of them across the entire mile-wide expanse. They are tall, gaunt figures dressed in heavy, seamless aprons of thick, yellowed rubber that hang from their shoulders down to the diamond-tread floor. Their faces are hidden behind rectangular iron masks that have been riveted directly into the bone of their skulls; where their eyes should be, two small, circular brass sight-glasses reflect the flat, shadowless light of the sky.

The Monitors do not breathe the air; their rubber aprons rise and fall with a slow, agonizingly deep mechanical rhythm, powered by small, brass-fitted bellows strapped to their lower spines that exhaust a faint puff of white steam every three minutes.

They carry long, jointed measuring rods made of boxwood and inlaid bone. They walk the catwalks with slow, leaden steps, their heavy iron-shod boots making the only deliberate sound in this vast world: a rhythmic, booming *CLANG... CLANG... CLANG* that travels for miles through the wet iron lattice.

A Monitor will walk for three days without stopping, pause before a single six-inch steel beam, and extend its wooden rod to measure the gap between two bolt-heads. It will stand motionless for five hours, its brass sight-glasses fixed on the measurement, waiting for the glacial expansion or contraction of the metal. 

If the measurement is correct—if the beam has held its line to within the thousandth of an inch—the Monitor will mark the steel with a tiny, precise cross drawn in white chalk. Then it will retract its rod, turn with stiff, mechanical dignity, and continue its endless walk across the high, bright, dripping plateau.

They do not interact with one another. If two Monitors meet on a narrow catwalk suspended over a thousand-foot drop of interlocked pipes, they do not speak or yield. They simply press their rubber-clad chests together and push silently, with immense, patient pressure, until one glides smoothly over the other along a spatial glitch that only they can see, their iron masks nearly touching, their brass eyes reflecting only the endless, blinding, silver glare of a world made entirely of steel.
