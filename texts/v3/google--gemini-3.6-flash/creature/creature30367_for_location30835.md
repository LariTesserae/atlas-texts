They are creatures born without the concept of a shadow. 

To look upon the builders is to see things sculpted from the same cold, leached palette as the basin itself. They are short—hardly reaching the breastbone of a surface-man—with broad, square shoulders and low-slung hips that give them a heavy, tethered momentum. Their skin is smooth, utterly hairless, and the color of wet bone, slicked perpetually with the thin, saline sweat that condensation and labor draw from their pores. It is a skin that does not bruise red or purple, but bruises a deep, water-logged gray, as though their veins ran with the same clear, mineral-laden runoff that fills the street hollows.

Their faces are broad and flattened, dominated by large, unblinking eyes that have no dark pupils, only irises the color of tarnished silver. Raised under an overhead sky that never darkens and never shifts, their eyes do not adjust to light; they simply receive it, reflecting the unblinking glow of the overhead basalt like mirrors left underwater. They have no visible ears, only narrow, membrane-covered slits behind the jaw, tuned not to the vibration of voices in the air, but to the deep, subsonic hum of the earth and the wet click of stone sliding over stone.

It is their hands that reveal the nature of their existence. They possess four fingers—long, spatulate, and devoid of fingernails. Instead, the tips of their digits are capped with thick pads of hardened, keratinous callus, yellowed and tough as horn. With these, they grip wet chalk-blocks without slippage; with these, they send light, rhythmic clatterings through the masonry—a language of taps, double-taps, and long, dragging scrapes that can travel through fifty paces of connected wall to tell a crew on the far side of a plaza to alter the angle of a terrace.

They do not sleep as surface creatures do. They have no night to invite it, no fatigue that builds to a collapse. Instead, they enter periods of standing stillness. A worker hauling a bone sledge will simply drop the hemp harness, step into a shallow puddle, and lock its joints. For two hours, three hours, it will remain upright and motionless, its chest barely expanding, its silver eyes wide and unseeing, while the cyan gliders drift down to cluster upon its chest and arms.

***

These gliders are the only source of color in the basin, though it is a color born of cold chemistry rather than warmth. They are the size of a hand’s span, light as dry husks, with translucent, umbrella-like bellies that pulse with an ethereal, pale-cyan bioluminescence. They have no true limbs, only delicate, thread-like tendrils that trail beneath them like the roots of an airborne plant. 

They float upon the thick, humid drafts that rise from the floor of the plain, buoyed by the subtle heat generated by thousands of moving bodies and the friction of stone on stone. When a builder pauses, or when a wall is newly laid, the gliders descend in silent, fluttering clouds. They press their flat, damp bellies against the wet skin of the workers or the freshly cut chalk, their tendrils working with a soft, rhythmic twitching as they lick the salt-crusts and filter microscopic fungal spores that drift down from the ceiling veins. To the builders, the gliders are neither pets nor pests; they are simply part of the atmosphere, a cool, glowing moss that flies.

Down in the gutters, where the chalk-dust slurry collects into a thick, pale milk, lives a different order of life. Long, segmented crawlers—resembling pale, arm-length centipedes carved from soapstone—move in relentless lines along the base of every wall. Their legs are tipped with soft, flexible cartilage that clings to the slippery slate without a sound. They are the cleaners of the geometry. Equipped with heavy, grinding mandibles made of solid chitin, they devour the mineral waste, the sheared copper shavings from the rasps, and the tiny, shed flakes of the builders’ calloused skin. They leave behind them clean, scoured channels, ensuring that the shallow aqueducts never clog, that the wet flagstones remain smooth as oil.

Slithering beneath these crawlers, deep within the wet silt of the foundation-grooves, are the blind burrowers—smooth, eel-like things no thicker than a finger, possessing skin so clear their internal organs can be seen as faint, dark threads. They exist solely in the friction-zones. They follow the tracks of the bone sledges, feeding on the liquid mud churned up by the constant movement of heavy loads, their smooth bodies acting as living bearings that further lubricate the paths between the quarries and the plazas.

***

There is no hierarchy among the pale figures, nor any visible division of labor. A builder who spends three days waist-deep in the central pool, rasped copper held tight against a sphere of white quartz, will drop the tool without a gesture, step out of the water, and immediately join a rope-team hauling a ten-ton lintel up a chalk terrace. 

Their minds seem to operate like a fluid finding its level. There are no overseers, no plans drawn on slate, no spoken orders. When a hall is to be dismantled, three hundred workers simply converge upon it at once, as if responding to a sudden shift in the basin’s atmospheric pressure. Their four-fingered hands work with a terrifying, unhurried precision. Wedges of soft copper are hammered into the unmortared seams with muffled thuds; levers of dense, petrified wood are inserted; and the wall parts, block by block, falling back into the hands of those waiting below.

They do not build for shelter—there is no rain, no wind, no sun to keep at bay. 

They do not build for defense—there are no enemies in the basin, no predators lurking in the high shale cliffs.

They build because the architecture is their metabolism. The endless assembly, disassembly, and movement of stone is how they process the world, how they expend the cold, mineral energy derived from the salt-water they drink and the pale, sponge-like marrow they scrape from the bellies of the deep-trench crawlers. The city is not a place they inhabit; it is a slow, physical thought they are perpetually thinking. A spire raised on the eastern edge of the plain is the beginning of an idea; its demolition four hours later is the conclusion; the long, stepped aqueduct built from its remains is the logical response.

In the center of it all, in the shallow, ankle-deep sheet of water, the quartz-cutters work on the great eggs. These spheres, some as large as a beehive hut, are smoothed until they hold no angles, no corners, no leverage points. They are the only things in the basin that are never stacked, never built into walls. Once a quartz egg is perfectly rounded, the workers roll it slowly out of the pool, through the wide, shifting avenues, and up the long terraces to the base of the shale cliffs. 

There, in the soft, worn talus at the foot of the ancient stone walls, they leave them. Over millennia, thousands of these blindingly white, hand-smoothed eggs have been deposited along the perimeter of the basin, sitting in long, luminous rows like the clutch of some unimaginable, subterranean leviathan—unmoving, pristine, waiting under the shadowless glare of the roof, while around them, the city continues to tear itself down and raise itself up, forever.
