They are a people who have never seen a shadow move.

To look upon them is to understand that they were not born of migration or exile; they are as native to this high, dripping shelf as the basalt dykes and the emerald liverwort. They are tall, broad-shouldered, and thick-framed, built with the dense bone structure required to live in air so thin it would make a lowlander’s lungs burn. Their chests are unusually deep and barrel-shaped, expanding in slow, powerful sweeps that create no sound—their breathing is as silent as the motionless sky.

Their skin is smooth, cool to the touch, and remarkably pale, with a faint, slate-gray undertone that mirrors the damp granite. Because the sun never sets and never rises higher, they do not burn, nor do they tan; their flesh has settled into an immutable, agate-like clarity. Their hair is long, dense, and corded, a silver-white hue resembling the down on the giant ferns. They wear it coiled tight against the backs of their heads or bound in elaborate, flat braids held in place with copper pins, preventing the perpetual dew from weighing it down.

Most striking are their eyes. Having evolved under a sun that sits perpetually at a glare-inducing forty-five degrees, their irises are a dark, unreflective obsidian, surrounded by double nictitating membranes—clear, thin eyelids that slide horizontally across the eye to filter the hard, shadowless light without obscuring their vision. They do not blink as often as humans do; their gaze is steady, unhurried, and unnervingly still.

### Language and Sound

Because the damp moss and soft cedar absorb sound almost instantly, speech as lowlanders know it—full of light vowels and far-reaching consonants—is useless here. Words simply fall dead within a few paces. 

Instead, they communicate through a language of low-frequency resonances and precise physical posture. When they speak, it begins deep in the diaphragm, emerging as a low, humming rumble that vibrates through the bedrock underfoot rather than traveling through the air. A conversation between two stone-cutters across a wide slate plaza looks like silence to an outsider, but to them, the subtle vibrations passing up through the soles of their thick-soled leather sandals carry complex, layered meaning. 

For close interactions, they use an intricate, fluid sign language made with long, thick-jointed fingers, accompanied by microscopic shifts in the tilt of the head and the flare of the nostrils. Because there is no wind to rustle clothes or shake leaves, the slightest movement is instantly legible across vast distances.

### The Rhythm of Un-Time

In a world without night, they do not have a universal time for sleep. There are no lamps, no hearth-fires, and no beds in the sense of mattresses or blankets. 

Instead, when an individual feels the slow fatigue of bodily effort, they seek out the permanent shadows—the deep, blue-gray coolness behind the northern walls of the diorite complexes. There, they enter a state of *static rest*. They sit upright against the cold stone, eyes closed behind their clear membranes, their heart rate slowing to a faint, rhythmic murmur. They can remain in this state for hours or days, completely still, while the fine mist beads upon their shoulders and drips silently onto the floor. When they rise, they simply step back into the daylight and resume whatever task they left behind.

Because nothing rots and food does not spoil, their relationship with sustenance is calm and unhurried. They gather the pale blue berries from the dark-leaved shrubs, which yield a dense, dry paste when ground with stone pestles. They collect the thick, sweet milk of the slate-gray ungulates, who walk freely into the stone dwellings to be milked, seeking the salt that the people scrape from the highest, driest basalt seams. They do not kill these animals; the beasts are co-habitants of the valley, unbothered by human presence, moving through the plazas like quiet, woolly shadows.

### The Craftsmanship of Centuries

They are, above all else, master stonemasons and hydro-engineers, though they work without the frantic energy of those who fear the end of a workday. 

A single builder might spend forty years shaping a single block of green-veined diorite. With a copper chisel in one hand and a smooth river-stone mallet in the other, they strike the rock in slow, deliberate rhythms: *tap... tap... tap...* each blow calculated to split the grain along microscopic fault lines. Because the air is still, the dust from their work does not blow into their faces; it falls straight down, settling in neat, damp rings around their feet, where it is swept up and mixed with cedar sap to seal the joints of the water channels.

Their clothes are made from a combination of tough, spun fern-fiber and the dense, shed under-fleece of the ungulates, felted together into heavy, sleeveless tunics and trousers that repel moisture. The garments are dyed only with the natural pigments of the valley: slate-gray, deep liverwort-green, and the rich, oxidized orange of copper-bearing rock. They wear no jewelry except for flat, polished discs of slate hung around the neck, carved with thin, geometric lines that map the flow of water through their respective city districts.

### Psychology and Interior Life

To live in a place of absolute equilibrium is to have a mind entirely free from anticipation and regret. 

These people have no concept of "later" or "before" in the linear sense. Their history is not recorded in books or song, but in the stone itself. A district of the city is not "old" or "new"; it simply *is*. If a lintel shifts by a fraction of a millimeter—an event that might happen once every few centuries—it is corrected with massive cedar levers and stone counterweights, restored precisely to its point of balance, and forgotten.

They do not experience grief as a sharp, tearing pain, but as a quiet rebalancing. When one of them finally ceases to move—their body simply stopping its slow breathing after centuries of life—they do not bury the body in the earth, for the damp peat would preserve it indefinitely. Instead, they carry the deceased up to the high, dry, sunlit arrêtes above the city. There, on the knife-edge rocks where no moss grows, they lay the body exposed to the hard, motionless daylight. Over years, the thin, dry air and the unrelenting sun desiccate the flesh, turning it into smooth, white bone that eventually blends indistinguishably into the pale slate of the mountain itself.

They move among their long buildings, their aqueducts, and their flightless, iridescent birds with the calm, unquestioning grace of beings who know that the world was made for them, or that they were carved from the world. As the sun holds its eternal forty-five-degree noon, and the water drops continue its microscopic, endless whispering into the moss, they live—unaging, unhurried, and perfectly balanced at the edge of the sky.
