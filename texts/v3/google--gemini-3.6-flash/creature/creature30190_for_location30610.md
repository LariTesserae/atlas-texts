To understand what lives in the Sunk-Foundry Precinct, one must first understand that life here does not struggle *against* the heat and the damp; it has surrendered to them entirely, softening until it fits into the wet iron seams like molten tallow.

The human workers did not all leave. 

Those who stayed were the ones whose lungs could no longer bear the dry, thin air of the upper plains—people whose bronchial tracts had already been scorched and hardened by early coal-smoke, finding in this subterranean pressure-cooker a strange, boiling relief. Over generations spent in the dark, they have changed.

They are known—if they are spoken of at all—as the **Slick-Skinned**. 

They are gaunt, hairless, and narrow-bodied, built to slip sideways through the gap between a bronze turbine casing and a vertical basalt wall. Their skin has lost all pigment, taking on the pearlescent, blue-gray cast of boiled skim milk. Because sweat cannot evaporate in air this saturated, their bodies have ceased to produce water-sweat altogether. Instead, their pores secrete a thick, odorless lipid—a dense biological grease that coats their limbs. It serves a double purpose: it repels the omnipresent, rot-inducing condensation and prevents the aggressive black fungal spores from taking root in their flesh.

They do not speak. In an atmosphere where a shout dies three feet from the lip, vocal cords are a useless organ, long since withered into silence. Instead, they communicate through **pipe-tapping**—a complex, rhythmic language of bone-conduction. A Slick-Skinner will press his flat forehead or the knuckle of his thumb directly against a hot copper steam pipe and strike it with a heavy brass ring worn on the finger. The vibration travels miles through the interconnected skeleton of the precinct’s ironwork, felt through the soles of the feet or the teeth of another inhabitant sitting half a cathedral-length away. 

Their movements are impossibly slow, synchronized with the patient, hour-long revolutions of the massive flywheels. To move quickly in this air is to invite hyperthermia; a fast step feels like swallowing a hot hearth-tile. So they drift. You might watch a worker’s pale hand take twenty minutes to reach out, grasp the valve of a steam-trap, and turn it a quarter-inch to bleed off a buildup of lime-choked pressure.

They live in the stacked, cell-like dwellings high in the trench walls, but they do not use furniture. Wood rots in weeks here; leather turns to green jelly in days. Instead, they sleep suspended in hammocks woven from the tough, rubbery inner fibers of the heat-loving vines, slung directly across the warm air currents rising from the dynamo housings.

They are not alone, though the other inhabitants barely register as animals in any ordinary sense.

In the dark, lukewarm depths of the copper cisterns beneath the catwalks live the **Sump-Eels**. Blind, ribbon-thin, and no longer than a man’s forearm, they are entirely soft-bodied, lacking even a spine. They float in the iridescent soup of micro-algae, feeding on the shedding skin of the porcelain snails and the dead fronds of indigo moss that sink from above. Their flesh is translucent; if you hold a glowing bio-luminescent fern near one, you can see the tiny, dark iron filings it has ingested from the rust-flaked sumps, suspended in its digestive tract like a delicate, dark skeleton.

Closer to the steam vents, where the iron reaches temperatures that would blister human skin, live the **Gasket-Ticks**. They are flat, fingernail-sized arthropods with carapaces made largely of metallic compounds absorbed from the water. They do not eat plants or meat; they feed directly on the ancient, tallow-based machinery grease that bleeds from the joints of the bronze dynamos. They wedge themselves by the hundreds into the seals and seams of the piping, their bodies forming a living, pulsating caulking that holds back the pressurized steam. The Slick-Skinner workers do not scrape them away; they tend them, gently brushing off excess moss so the ticks can feed unhindered on the leaking grease.

And then there is the plant life itself, which has developed a slow, creeping instinct that borders on animal intent. 

The **Vascular Vines**—the thick, rope-like tendrils winding through the flywheels—do not merely grow toward light, for there is none. They grow toward *vibration* and *friction*. They hunt the motion of the remaining gears, wrapping their woody stems tightly around the axles to drink the friction-heat generated by the turning bronze. Over decades, the vines and the machines have entered a state of mechanical paralysis: the engine turns the vine, winding it tight, and the vine slowly, mercilessly chokes the engine, holding it in a vice-like grip that ensures neither will ever move faster than the earth itself shifts.

If you sit very still on a low catwalk, your back pressed against the damp, vibrating basalt, you will not see these creatures at first. You will see only the silver-moss dripping, and hear only the endless *tink-tink-drip* of condensation. 

Then, a pale, greased arm will emerge silently from a tangle of waxy shade-ferns. A face—eyebrows gone, eyes reduced to milky, dark-sensitive slits beneath heavy folds of skin—will tilt toward the warm air venting from a wall socket. A brass ring will click softly against a copper tube: *one, two, pause, three*.

Ten yards away, beneath a carpet of indigo moss, the iron floor will vibrate in response. A shadow will shift. Something slow, wet, and endlessly patient will adjust its posture in the dark, settling deeper into the heat, waiting for the next century to begin.
