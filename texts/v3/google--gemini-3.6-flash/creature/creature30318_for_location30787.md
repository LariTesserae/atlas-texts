To live here is to be anchored. Anything that drifts is swept over the lips of the world within an hour, ground to pulp against the scalloped granite of the lower terraces, or dropped a thousand feet into the choking mist below. 

The life of these drowned stairs has adapted to the crushing weight of the water, the thinness of the three-mile-high air, and the permanent, grease-slick presence of the iron machines.

### The Hunters of the Sluices

The bear-sized otters—known among the cable-tenders as *Whistle-Hounds* for the high, bird-like trills that carry over the roar of the falls—are not soft creatures. Their outer fur is a mat of coarse, tallow-slicked needle-guard, so dense that cold water never touches their grey skin. Beneath their heavy subcutaneous lard, their muscle is dark, dense, and scented with river-sulfur. 

They do not swim so much as they wedge themselves. Using flattened, iron-hard tail-blades and heavy forepaws equipped with blunt, spade-like claws, they climb the vertical flumes against the full weight of the current. They hunt by touch, threading their thick, fleshy whiskers into the drowned windows of flooded pump-houses and between the stacked stone blocks of the old quarries. 

When a Whistle-Hound brings up a heavy, black-shelled mussel from the depths, it does not haul out onto the land—there is barely any land to be had. Instead, it wedges its hindquarters into the crotch of a rusted iron girder or wraps its tail three times around a vibrating cable. There, suspended in the perpetual aerosol of the falls, it cracks the shell against a winch-housing with a rapid, metallic *clack-clack-clack*, devouring the cold, sweet meat within before slipping back into the emerald dark.

### The Cable-Tenders

The humans who tend the boilers are small, wide-chested, and barrel-ribbed, built low to the stone to keep the wind and spray from catching them. Generations at three miles above sea level, bathed in cold spray and breathing air thick with coal-smoke and pulverized water, have altered them. Their lungs are huge, their skin is permanently weathered to the color of wet walnut, and their fingers are broad and scarred from iron-wire splinters.

They do not speak with their voices. The sound of the drowned continent—the gut-shaking bass of millions of tons of water striking the lower shelves—makes human speech impossible beyond a distance of three feet. Instead, they are masters of the *Tap*. 

They communicate along the heavy iron cables that span the drowned valleys. A Tender sitting in an oilskin shelter on a whale-backed island can wrap a brass-gloved hand around a tethering cable and "listen" to the mountain. By the rhythm of the vibrations hummed through the wire, they know if a pump three miles away has thrown a valve, if a dredging bucket has caught on a submerged quarry ledge, or if a storm-surge is shifting the timber booms in the upper basin.

Their clothes are marvels of heavy, wet-weather survival: multi-layered tunics of thick, unwashed sheep’s wool soaked in boiled linseed oil and tallow, worn beneath aprons of cured sturgeon-skin. They plug their ears with beeswax softened with lard to keep the endless roar from rupturing their drums, relying entirely on the bone-conduction of the iron machinery to hear the world around them.

They live in the "Dry-Pockets"—shallow basalt caves carved by the ancient water, sitting directly behind or beneath the great waterfalls. Here, protected by the outward arc of the surge, the air is warm and heavy with the smell of wet soot, roasting char, and burning peat. They build no houses; they bolt heavy larch-wood bunks directly into the cave walls, sleeping in their oilskins to the steady, rhythmic *thud-clank* of the double-acting steam pumps that share their stone shelters.

### The Deep-Weed Grazers

Down in the submerged terraces, fifty feet beneath the emerald surface where the light turns to a cold, translucent moss-green, live the *Iron-Lice*. 

These are giant, dorso-ventrally flattened crustaceans, the size of carriage wheels, covered in thick plates of dark amber chitin that mimic the bladder-weed they inhabit. They possess no eyes, but their undersides are lined with thousands of delicate, feathery cilia that detect the micro-currents caused by the movement of fish or the slow rotation of the giant granite boulders spinning in their stone potholes.

The Iron-Lice are the janitors of the drowned machines. They gather in dense, overlapping clusters on the submerged cast-iron girders and copper sluices, scraping away the thick mats of river-slime, cold-kelp holdfasts, and the yellow grease that leaks from the winch-bearings. The Cable-Tenders leave them strictly alone; without the Lice, the submerged gears would seize within a season, choked by the relentless growth of the liverworts and water-mosses.

### The Quarry-Keepers

Deepest of all, resting in the quiet, current-dead pockets where the ancient, carved stone blocks remain stacked in their drowned rows, are the *Siphon-Ghouls*. 

They are not fish, though they share the translucent, ice-blue flesh of the char. They are long, legless amphibians, twelve to fifteen feet in length, with broad, shovel-shaped heads and no eyes. They have lived in these high drowned valleys since before the range was rounded off, surviving in the cold, oxygen-rich depths by breathing directly through their soft, ribless skin.

The Ghouls spend months motionless, wedged between the carved eyelets of the submerged stone blocks, their pale gills fanning the siltless water like fronds of white fern. They are long-lived and slow-moving, feeding only when a dead sturgeon or a drowned Whistle-Hound drifts down into the calm water of the flooded quarries. 

When the Cable-Tenders lower the heavy iron dredging buckets into the depths to clear silt from the channels, the Siphon-Ghouls do not flee. They nudge against the iron buckets with their soft, blunted snouts, curious about the vibration, before settling back into the dark crevices between the stone blocks—the last remnants of a forgotten purpose, waiting for the water to finally wear the iron away.
