In a world built of vertical razor blades, nothing walks. To possess legs or feet here would be to be shredded within three strides. 

What lives in the basin does not sit *upon* the slate; it is threaded *through* it.

The pale, coin-shaped creatures in the dark gaps are merely the lowest tier of a singular, cold-blooded ecology. They are the grazers of the deep seam, no larger than a palm, flat as parchment, sliding along the glass faces on a film of their own transparent mucus. But the greasy layer that coats every slate leaf across the four-mile basin does not come from them alone. It is the communal excretion of the basin’s true inhabitants—vast, limbless entities that inhabit the maze like oil worked into the narrow grain of dark wood.

To see one require patience, for they move at the speed of rising water, and in the perpetual dusk of the aerosol fog, their hides are indistinguishable from the wet, iron-stained shale.

They are long, wedge-shaped masses of dense, gristly cartilage and fibrous fat, six or eight yards from snout to tail, but rarely more than eight inches wide. They are built like massive, leathery shims. Their flesh is not soft; it has the density of vulcanized rubber, saturated with tallow and heavy lipids that render them immune to the glass-sharp edges of the stone. A knife pulled across their flank would find no grip, slipping off their thick, oily hide as if it were greased slate.

They live wedged vertically between the shale plates, suspended in the narrow alleys. A single individual might rest its weight across forty or fifty parallel blades at once, its long, segmented body conforming perfectly to the micro-rifts of the basin floor. Because their weight is distributed over hundreds of sharp stone points, they do not cut themselves; they rest upon the razor-edges as a fakir rests upon a bed of nails, held aloft by the very density of the blades.

They have no eyes—light has no meaning in the dark aerosol wall—nor do they possess ears. Instead, their entire skeleton is a single, continuous arch of dense, honeycombed bone that runs the length of their bellies. This bone keel rests directly against the shale plates, acting as a physical receiver for the subterranean roar. 

To them, the deafening noise of the subterranean air and water channels is not sound; it is vision.

They "see" the basin through the harmonic resonance of the slate leaves. As the cold air and iron-water burst through the deep fractures below, the stone plates vibrate at differing frequencies depending on their length, thickness, and how tightly they are packed. By feeling these vibrations through their chest-keels, the creatures map the maze in real time. They know where a plate has snapped three miles away; they feel the slow, century-long rise of the anhydrite beds beneath their bellies like a warm pulse; they track one another by the subtle dampening of the stone’s vibration where another heavy body is wedged.

Their movement is a process of hydraulic displacement. They do not crawl; they inflate and contract. By pumping cold, iron-rich body fluids through internal, muscular chambers, they widen their flattened bodies in one section to grip the stone leaves, then draw their rear forward, wedge by wedge, forcing the slate plates slightly apart with a wet, heavy squeal of friction. As they slide through the razor alleys, they slough off a thick, copper-smelling tallow that coats the rock behind them. It is this lard, mixed with the suspended lime in the fog, that forms the greasy film covering the basin—a self-manufactured lubricant that makes the glass-sharp world navigable.

It is these creatures that built the basalt dikes, though "built" implies an intent that belongs to a different kind of mind.

The long walls of uncemented boulders are acoustic dams. 

Where the subterranean vents are too loud, the acoustic vibration becomes violent enough to liquefy the creatures' internal organs or shatter their jaw-keels. To survive near the most active rifts, they use the only heavy, non-vibrating material available: the basalt boulders resting on the basin’s margins. 

Over decades, a creature will wedge its heavy, blunted snout beneath a detached basalt block and push. It does not lift; it scrapes. Resting its chin-plate—a thick pad of reinforced horn—against the stone, it heaves forward with the massive, slow contraction of its entire body, dragging the rock inch by inch through the shale field, shearing off the slate needles in its path. The smooth, wide gouges on the basalt boulders are the scars left by generations of these horned chins pressing against the stone.

They stack the boulders in straight lines across the basin's grain. These stone walls interrupt the physical transmission of sound through the shale. Behind a dike, the violent, bone-shaking hum of the deep vents drops to a low, rhythmic thrum—a quiet sanctuary where the heavy aerosol settles and the pale, coin-shaped sliders multiply in thick, jellied crusts. 

Here, in the acoustic lee of the basalt, the creatures gather to feed and rest. They press their broad, slit-like mouths into the deep gaps between the leaves, vacuuming up the pale, coin-shaped creatures and the thick mats of autotrophic bacteria that thrive on the wet iron. Their digestion is as slow as their gait; a single feeding will sustain them for months as they lie motionless, wedged between the cold stone pages, their hides absorbing dissolved minerals directly from the aerosol fog.

They have no language, but they sing. 

When the deep water channels shift and the ground vibration drops into a rare, harmonious register, a creature resting behind a basalt dike will press its jaw-keel firmly against the wall and force compressed air from its dorsal blowholes. The sound is not vocal; it is the mechanical friction of bone against basalt—a dry, grating rasp that travels through the stacked boulders and into the slate grid for miles. 

Another creature, miles away, feels the rasp through its ribs, adjusts its position within the stone leaves, and rasps back. It is a dialogue of pure mass and friction, measuring the density of the rock, the depth of the grease, and the slow, relentless growth of the valley floor.

They live for centuries, growing thicker, denser, and more heavily scarred by the glass blades, until eventually their tallow dries, their internal hydraulics fail, and they solidify. When one dies, it does not rot in the cold, mineral fog. The iron and lime in the water infiltrate its thick hide, petrifying the body where it rests. 

In time, the rising shale plates push up through the calcified hide, splitting the dead creature into vertical, dark leaves of its own—another layer of vitreous stone, standing shoulder-to-shoulder with the rest, singing the roar of the deep earth to the living that slide between them.
