At the foundation of this world’s life is a simple, relentless metabolism: the appetite for metal, water, and heat. Because there is no sunlight-driven foliage, the entire biological pyramid is rooted in lithotrophic and thermal life that feeds directly upon the city’s slow decay and the mineral-rich fluids forced up from the deep rock.

### The Shell-Scrapers and Channel-Dwellers

The smallest visible creatures—the fingernail-sized crawlers—are known informally as *iron-mites* or *rust-weevils*. Their carapaces are not made of chitin alone, but of a dense, metallic biomineralization resembling hematite and tarnished brass. They do not possess eyes; instead, their forelimbs are tipped with hardened, serrated rasps that vibrate at high frequencies, grinding the top layer of rust and wet slate into a fine paste. They absorb the iron oxide and excrete a thick, fast-setting orange cement that fills cracks in the brickwork. Millions of them live, die, and are crushed into the masonry, so that over centuries, the city's walls have become a composite material—half baked clay, half crushed shell.

Larger variants of these crawlers, the timber-length *vault-paddlers*, inhabit the underside of the elevated rail-girders and the dark spaces inside inactive boiler housings. They move with an excruciating, deliberate grace, taking hours to pull their heavy, barnacled bulks across a ceiling. Their underbellies are soft, translucent, and glowing with a faint, chemical heat, attracting clouds of tiny, glass-winged flies that feed on their sticky secretions.

In the runoff channels, the pale, lungless rope-creatures—the *cistern-eels* or *sloughs*—form the living muscular tissue of the water system. They have no bones, only thick bundles of fibrous cartilage encased in smooth, oil-secreting skin. They spend their lives wedged into the joints of aqueducts and the lips of spillways. Where a brass valve leaks or a stone conduit has cracked under its own weight, dozens of these creatures entwine themselves into the gap, their slick, hot bodies swelling to form a tight, living seal against the pressure. They feed on the micro-fauna that bloom in the warm, stagnant pools, filtering the water through throat-gills that hum with a faint, rhythmic purr.

### The Aerial Swarms

The clouds that drift between the six-story engine towers are not composed of birds or fragile insects, but of heavy, mica-winged *strata-fliers*. Their bodies are streamlined cylinders of dark, polished horn, built to withstand the fierce, warm updrafts blowing off the lowland plains and the violent bursts of wet steam from the exhaust chimneys.

They do not land on flat surfaces; their limbs are hooked steel-like talons designed exclusively to catch the lip of a brick cornice or the rim of a cast-iron pipe. They fly in coordinated, murmuring masses that obey the air currents generated by the slow strokes of the walking-beam engines. When an engine completes a twenty-minute stroke and exhales its towering plume of vapor, the fliers dive directly into the steam, opening their wing-cases to absorb the condensed grease and mineral salts carried up from the deep pistons.

### The Keepers of the Beam

Navigating the wet brick arcades, the stepped tiers, and the interior boiler-galleries are the plateau’s sapient inhabitants: a heavy, patient people who call themselves the *Weight-Bearers* or simply the *Tenders*.

Physically, they are built for a world without soft landings. They are broad-shouldered and low to the ground, with short, thick-boned limbs and massive, four-digit hands capable of gripping wet, algae-slick iron with absolute security. Their skin is hairless, thick, and colored like dark slate or wet leather, perpetually coated in a natural, odorless tallow that causes the warm rain to bead and roll off them instantly. They have no visible ears; their auditory organs are embedded behind heavy plates of bone at the base of the skull, tuned specifically to the deep, subsonic vibrations of the rock and the low *thump-hiss* of the engines.

Their eyes are large, dark, and protected by thick, transparent nictitating membranes that slide horizontally to keep out grease-spray and steam. They do not speak with high-pitched vocal cords—the endless roar of falling water and the clatter of millions of crawlers would swallow such sounds. Instead, they communicate through a combination of low, chest-resonating hums that travel through the stone floors and a complex language of rhythmic iron-striking. A Tender carrying a heavy brass rod will tap out short, reverberant codes against a handrail, sending messages that travel miles down the interconnected girders to another worker waiting at a valve station.

#### Life in the Tenements
The Tenders live within the deep brick tenements stacked four and five layers below the upper rail lines. Their homes are not dry; dryness in this world is a sign of stagnant air and death. Instead, their dwellings are hollowed-out brick vaults continuously washed by thin, warm sheets of condensation.

*   **Furniture:** There is no wood or cloth. Beds are shallow, smooth-carved stone basins filled with warm, circulating runoff, or hammocks woven from the tough, fibrous tendons of dead cistern-eels.
*   **Clothing:** They wear no woven textiles, which would rot within days in the perpetual humidity. Instead, they wear articulated harnesses of ground-shell plates, thick oil-cured leather skirts, and heavy tool-belts hung with tallow-wedges, stone-chisels, and brass measuring-weights.
*   **Diet:** Their sustenance comes entirely from the city’s fauna. They harvest the medium-sized crawlers, cracking their metallic shells with weighted mallets to extract the rich, mineral-heavy fat and grey marrow within, which tastes faintly of salt, iron, and hot cream. They scoop the dense, starch-like egg-masses of the cistern-eels from the walls of the aqueducts, boiling them in natural steam-vents until they form a dense, nutritious loaf. Drinking water is drawn directly from the highest aqueducts, where the warm rain first hits the pristine basalt peaks above the industrial basins.

### The Work of Generations

The Tenders do not view the city as a machine they built, nor one they are trying to fix. To them, the shelf is an eternal, living organism, and they are its symbiotic caretakers.

Their lives are measured not in years, but in the stroke-counts of the great walking-beam engines and the slow, millimeter-by-millimeter erosion of the stone staircases. A Tender’s daily work is characterized by an absolute lack of haste. A crew of four may spend three days using levers and counterweighted pulleys to shift a single granite block six inches to adjust the overflow of a spillway. Another worker might spend an entire lifetime overseeing a single brass valve the size of a house, polishing its stem with crushed-shell paste and leaning his body against its stone counterweights once every four hours to ensure its slow, perfect rotation.

There are no children running through the streets; the young are born small, sleek, and aquatic, spending their first years swimming in the deep, warm holding-cisterns beneath the city’s base tier before their limbs thicken and their skin cures into the heavy, slate-like hide of adulthood.

In the evenings—if "evening" can be measured in a place where the sun shines bright through thin ribbons of steam—the Tenders gather along the edges of the high tiers. They sit shoulder-to-shoulder on the rounded granite cornices, their heavy feet dangling over the miles-deep drop to the lowland plains below. They do not speak. They lean their backs against the vibrating brick walls of the boilerhouses, close their dark eyes, and feel the warm rain wash over them, perfectly attuned to the deep, resonant groan of the iron pistons cycling beneath the wet stone.
