In a place where movement is measured by decades and sound is choked out by four-foot carpets of damp moss, the things that live here have abandoned the fast, hot blood of lower altitudes. There is no fluttering, no scurrying, no territory fought for with shriek or display. Life at the Rift moves at the speed of cold grease creeping down a steel shaft.

Nearest the stone, in the perpetually dripping shadow beneath the waxy skiff-fronds, live the **rust-wickers**. They are gastropods the size of a man’s fist, but their shells are not lime or calcite; they are thick, overlapping plates of dark iron-pyrite, heavy as shot, rusted on the outside to a dull brick-red and lined inside with iridescent mother-of-copper. They crawl along the dormant drive-pistons and brass condenser fins, their soft, grey-white bodies secreting an acid so mild it takes months to dissolve a microscopic film of iron oxide. They do not eat the plants; they graze on the orange powder of oxidation and the pale pewter lichens, leaving behind them a thin, hydrophobic trail of silver slime that seals the metal against the mountain’s icy mist, unintentionally preserving the very iron they feed upon.

Higher up, where the copper cables span the chasms, live the **cable-walkers**. They are lungless, spindle-legged reptiles, long as a forearm, with skin like wet river-slate and digits that have flattened into semi-circular clamps. They spend years navigating a single three-hundred-foot span of copper hawser. Because the air is too thin to carry oxygen quickly through lungs, their blood is cold, dark green, and thick with copper-bound hemocyanin. They do not hunt with speed. They press their bellies flat against the braided wire, feeling for the faint, thrumming frequencies sent up from the subterranean pumps miles below. When a moth—a fat, pale creature with powdery, glass-like wings that feeds on the amber kerosene-resin— alights on the cable to rest, the walker inches toward it. A single stalk takes three hours. The strike itself is not a spring, but a slow, crushing closure of the jaw, silent and absolute.

Then there are the large things—the **bellows-beasts**, or **tallow-backs**, though there is no one here to name them. 

They are low-slung, broad-bodied quadrupeds that look, at a glance, like moss-covered slabs of basalt. Their backs are wide and flat, covered in a turf of rubbery green liverwort that takes root directly in the thick, lardaceous fat beneath their hides. Their legs are short, thick as tree stumps, ending in wide, padded soles made of a fibrous, self-healing horn that swallows the sharp edges of the slate-prisms without tearing. 

A tallow-back does not breathe like a mammal. Its ribcage is an articulated cage of dense, iron-hard bone that expands and contracts only two or three times an hour, taking in vast quantities of thin mountain air and holding it under immense internal pressure. When it exhales, it does so through spiracles along its flanks, producing a soft, barely audible *hiss* that smells of wet pine and cold oil. 

These beasts graze on the edges of the saw-toothed fronds. Their jaws are not set with teeth, but with broad, sliding plates of horn that act like hydraulic shears, cutting through the tough, resinous plant matter with a wet *crunch* that the moss instantly absorbs. The resin they consume does not kill them; it settles into a specialized chamber of their multi-chambered stomachs, where it is converted into a dense, oily tallow. As the tallow-backs move—agonizingly slowly, stepping from one deep cushion of moss to another—their flanks rub against the exposed drive-rods and sliding brass guides of the mountain’s buried machinery. They leave thick grease-marks along the metal, lubricating the joints that shift only once every few seasons, warming the cold steel with their slow body heat, and in return, drinking the sweet, mineral-laden sweat that trickles off the copper pipes.

And in the deepest clefts, where the brass condenser houses form artificial caves sheltered from the blinding UV glare, live the **Keepers**—or what remains of them.

They are human in shape, but they have become something more suited to the Rift’s quiet pressure. They are short, immensely broad-chested, with lungs that flare their ribs out like barrel-staves. Their skin is the color of wet pewter, hairless, and perpetually stained with graphite and dark oil. They do not wear woven cloth; their garments are made of heavy, vulcanized rubber skirts and thick leather aprons cut from the gaskets of ancient steam valves, bound around their limbs with strips of copper wire.

Their eyes are small, buried under heavy, shelf-like brow ridges, with pupils reduced to pinpricks by the violent azure light, covered by a permanent, semi-transparent nictitating membrane that looks like smoked quartz. They do not speak. The air is too thin for voice, and sound here is an intrusion. Instead, they communicate through a language of heavy, deliberate gestures and by tapping on the exposed ironwork with horn-tipped rods. The vibrations travel through the machinery, through the soles of their thick boots, and into their bones.

The Keepers do not build, nor do they repair in the human sense. They move through the choked green basins on a cycle that spans generations. One Keeper may spend forty years watching a single valve. They carry pots carved from horn, filled with the amber resin gathered from the skiff-fronds, and long-handled brushes made of stiff fern-fiber. With endless, unhurried patience, they sweep away the aggressive mats of moss from the intake grates, scrape the iron-pyrite snails off the sliding surfaces, and paint the friction-points with resin and animal tallow.

They do not know who built the grid, nor do they care. To them, the buried machinery is not a tool, but an organ of the mountain—a giant, cold liver that filters the underground water and breathes heat into the slate. When a Keeper dies, they are not buried in the thin soil. They are laid inside the dead, dry chamber of a decommissioned piston house, wrapped in heavy moss and sealed beneath a plate of cast iron. Within decades, the waxy ferns bloom out of their seams, their veins shining with that faint, metallic sheen, feeding on the rich minerals left behind.

Nothing here is an outsider. The machine, the plant, the beast, and the human have melted into a single, sluggish physiology. The sky glares down, cold and sharp enough to cut; the subterranean pumps heave once every ten minutes; and on a high slate terrace, a Keeper sits motionless for five hours, watching a glass-legged beetle crawl across a copper rivet, waiting for the mountain to take its next breath.
