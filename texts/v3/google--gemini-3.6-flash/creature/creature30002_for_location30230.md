To endure this place, life has surrendered the need for rhythm. Where there is no night, there is no pulse of sleeping and waking, no cycle of exertion and rest. Existence here is not a series of days, but an unbroken, static posture.

The smaller inhabitants understand this best. 

The silver slate-beetles do not possess eyes as lower creatures do; where their orbits should be, twin domes of polished, pearlescent horn reflect the white glare of the unmoving sun, protecting their tiny brains from being cooked inside their shells. Their bodies are light-harvesting mirrors, cold to the touch despite the radiation pouring over them. Beneath their chitinous bellies, hundreds of microscopic, spade-shaped cilia scrape at the rock, feeding not on organic decay—for nothing rots in this dry chill—but on the bitter, sulfurous salts left behind by the evaporating seep-water. Their endless metallic screech is not a song or a warning, but the physical friction of their breathing: every contraction of their tiny, silver spiracles forces thin air across serrated ribs on their thoraxes, converting the mountain’s cruel atmosphere into a continuous, deafening hiss.

Above them, the leathery flyers move with a silent, heavy efficiency. They have no feathers to freeze or rot in the damp, only skin like oiled parchment stretched over hollow bone. Their wing-spans are narrow and stiff, designed not for flapping—the air is too thin to catch with a stroke—but for falling. They drop sideways from the high, polished hollows, using the sheer weight of their heavy, slate-colored bodies to cut through the thin atmosphere. They hunt entirely by vibration. Their flat, eyeless heads are capped with thick pads of cartilaginous fat that press against the granite when they roost, feeling the distant, shivering hum of a beetle colony three hundred yards away. When they strike, it is a single, stone-hard plunge: a brief flash of wet grey wing, a sharp *snick* of an iron-tipped beak crushing a shell, and then a long, banking glide back down into the shadowless hollows to digest the chitin with stomachs full of swallowed granite gravel.

The long-necked grazers—the *bone-walkers*—are the great, silent clocks of the pass. They have evolved beyond the need for sound entirely; their vocal cords have withered into fibrous threads, for no voice could ever rise above the sub-bass thrum of the wind-pipes. Instead, they talk through the rock. Their broad, padded hooves are spongy and soft as wet tallow on the underside, laced with deep networks of nerve endings that register the micro-shocks of the mountain. A herd of five hundred moves as a single, distributed organism, spaced fifty yards apart across the shale amphitheatres. When a herd-leader senses a shift in the rock’s moisture—a basin drying or a new seep opening miles away—it stamps a single hind leg against the bone-smooth slate. The impact travels through the stone as a sharp, hydraulic pulse, rising up through the hollow leg-bones of every grazer in the valley, turning the entire herd in unison toward the new water.

They are milky-white, their hides so thick and dry that they feel like tanned parchment filled with wet sand. They do not drink; their long, flexible necks allow them to spend hours pressing their rasping, file-like tongues into the granite flutes, sucking the cold condensation directly from the stone as it forms. Their dung is dry, pale dust that the wind sweeps instantly into the cracks, leaving no trace that they were ever there.

And moving among them, almost invisible against the slate, are the people.

To call them human is correct, but it is a humanity pared down to its starkest architecture. They are short, barrel-chested, and wide-hipped, built low to the ground to keep their centers of gravity beneath the acoustic pressure of the high peaks. Their lungs are enormous, stretching their ribs into wide, armor-like cages that heave with a slow, rhythmic patience, drawing in the thin air and wringing every trace of iron from it. 

Their skin is not pale, but a deep, bruised charcoal, so heavily pigmented against the relentless vertical sun that it looks almost blue in the glare. It is hairless, smooth, and constantly smeared with a thick paste made from crushed slate-beetles and grazer-tallow. This grease dries into a dull, shimmering enamel that seals their pores, keeping the moisture in their blood from evaporating into the dry chill and turning the cold sweat at their necks into a silvery armor.

They do not speak with words. Speech requires breath, and breath is too precious to waste against a sky that swallows sound whole. Instead, their language is a swift, muscular semaphore of the fingers, hands, and shoulders, carried out at waist level where the eye can catch it against the pale granite. For deeper, more intimate communication, they press their foreheads together, letting the vibrations of their vocal chambers travel directly through skull and bone, bypassing the deafening roar of the air entirely. A mother does not sing to her child; she presses her throat to the infant’s ribs and thrums a low, rhythmic pulse that mirrors the deep bass of the granite pipes.

They have no concept of night, and so they have no concept of sleep as an interruption of life. They do not lie down to dream. Instead, when exhaustion touches them, a person simply sits in the curve of a granite flute, wraps their oiled hide tight around their knees, pulls a stiffened hood of grazer-skin down over their eyes to block the white sun, and slips into a half-conscious trance for two hours. Their heart rate drops, their thick blood slows, and they rest while the sun remains pinned overhead, unmoving, furious, and cold.

Their culture is one of total subtraction. They carry nothing that cannot be repaired with stone or hide. They have no art that lasts longer than a gesture, no history recorded in anything heavier than memory. The bone needles they use to stitch their hides are left behind in the rock-crevices when they grow dull, knowing that stone does not change, and another wanderer will find the bone, sharpen it on the slate, and use it again three hundred years later.

They follow the grazers, not as hunters who butcher at will, but as quiet, predatory parasites. They harvest only what the herd can lose without slowing: a single, aging beast bled carefully from the neck-vein into a horn bowl, its hide stripped while the meat is still warm and sliced into paper-thin ribbons that dry instantly under the sun into black, mineral-salty shards. 

Watch the woman squatting by the jasper stone. Her fingers are broad, short, and tipped with thick, dark nails worn flat by the slate. She does not hurry. Time has no measure here; there are no shadows creeping across the stone to tell her that the day is dying. She strikes the red jasper against the black volcanic glass—*clack, clack, snip*—and a razor-sharp flake falls onto her palm. She picks it up, lifts her grease-smeared face to the violet sky, and squints through narrow, yellowed eyes at the unblinking sun. 

She feels the sub-bass hum in her heels, the metallic screech of the beetles in her teeth, the cold salt on her neck, and the raw meat in her stomach. She is not surviving the mountain; she is simply what the mountain looks like when it learns to walk.
