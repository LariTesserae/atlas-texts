To look upon this drowned continent is to see a world that appears utterly forsaken, for there is no darting of silver scales, no skittering of claws across the slate, no soft bloom of red blood in the dark. Yet it is not empty. The life that abides here is simply forged from the same slow, crushing logic as the water and the stone.

They are not animals. They have no lungs to collapse under the miles of liquid, no fragile eyes to burst in the sunless gloom, no warm blood to be stolen by the glacial cold. If a traveler from the world of air were to sweep a beam of light over the terraced plazas, they would pass right over the inhabitants, mistaking them for fallen lintels, worn decorative corbels, or unusually dense outcroppings of the pale, fibrous moss.

They are beings of dense, calcified tissue and compressed peat-like fiber, encased in hides as tough and cold as waterlogged leather. Their forms are broad, squat, and slab-sided—built not for swift travel through water, but to resist the immense, relentless weight pressing down from above. They have no necks; their heads are mere sloping extensions of their heavy torsos, completely devoid of features save for broad, disc-like pressure-receptors aligned along their flanks, which feel the microscopic shivering of the stone beneath them.

Where an animal possesses muscles of red meat, these entities are laced with thick bands of cold-hardened lipid and tough, rubbery vascular bundles, similar to the stalks of the giant kelp with which they share the deep. They do not breathe air or water. Instead, they draw sustenance directly through the porous, moss-carpeted hide of their undersides, absorbing the trace minerals weeping from the eroded slate and the dense, oily enzymes produced by the roots of the black ivy.

Their movement operates on a temporal scale that renders it nearly invisible. To watch one is like watching the movement of a glacier or the growth of a stalagmite. A single step—lowering a spade-shaped, six-toed foot onto a forty-stride step—can take the better part of an afternoon. The joints of their limbs do not use cartilage or synovial fluid; they are lubricated by heavy, tallow-like fats that never freeze, grinding together with a low, sub-audible *crunch* that propagates through the stone bedrock for leagues.

They are the Carvers—or what the Carvers became when the world above fell into the sea thousands of years ago and the water never receded.

They do not swim; they are far too heavy, far too dense to float. They walk the paved avenues and climb the rounded stairways with a deliberate, earthbound gravity, anchored to the cyclopean floor. 

It is their hands that explain the primitive artifacts resting in the alcoves. Their hands are monumental, four-fingered spades tipped with blunt, horn-like nails that are continuously worn down and continuously regrown. They are the ones who turned the wooden levers of the sluice gates three centuries ago. They are the ones who hammered the copper rings into the stone piers.

Because the sea is patient and vast, constantly seeking to pry the masonry apart with the slow wedging of its currents, the inhabitants live in a state of endless, quiet maintenance. They do not build new cities—there is no need, for the continent-sized foundation is indestructible—but they mend. 

When a seam between two ten-ton slate blocks begins to widen under the tectonic shifting of the abyssal plain, two or three of these massive beings will gather at the site. The gathering may take a decade to assemble. Once there, they work with a terrifying, stoic focus. One will set its flat, slab-like shoulder against the rock to check its movement; another will lift a granodiorite mallet, its bone handle bound in kelp-cordage, and swing it with agonizing slowness. The blow, when it lands, makes no splash or sound in the water, but sends a sharp, ringing shockwave straight through the masonry. They drive wedges of petrified hardwood into the gaps, seal the cracks with thick pastes rendered from kelp-pith, and beat cold copper plates over the weak spots to bind the stone together.

When a tool is finished with, it is not discarded; it is simply set down on a slate workbench, where it may rest for eighty years until another hand, identical to the first, reaches out to pick it up again.

They have no language of sound, for sound dissipates into the endless black, but they communicate through the bedrock. By striking the stone with their calcified palms or dragging their heavy heels across the grooved slate, they send low, reverberating thrums through the continental shelf. A thrum can mean *a lintel has slipped in the southern amphitheater*, or simply *the current is bearing colder water from the trench*. The message takes hours to travel and days to be answered, a slow, monumental conversation carried on beneath the roots of the kelp forests.

In the deepest alcoves, beneath the sunken foundations where even the pale moss struggles to grow, the oldest among them lie dormant. They have allowed the aquatic ivy to grow entirely over their backs, binding them to the slate floor until they are indistinguishable from the architecture itself. Their hearts—simple, heavy pumps that pulse once every few hours—drive a cold, clear lymph through their bodies, keeping them in a state of suspended, everlasting awareness. They feel the continent settling. They feel the atom-by-atom erosion of the stone steps. They feel the slow, endless weight of the water.

They do not fear the light, for they do not know it exists. They do not fear the end of their world, for their world has already ended, drowned, and solidified into absolute permanence. They simply exist, dense and silent, tending to their submerged eternity, as much a part of the stone as the masonry itself.
