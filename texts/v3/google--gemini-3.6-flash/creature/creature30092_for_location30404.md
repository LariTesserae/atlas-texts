To understand what lives here, you must first understand that nothing in this water moves alone. Every living thing in the chapel-cistern is tethered to the brass-and-iron beat of the spire, tuned to the hum of the copper coils and the hot, rhythmic pulse of the lower piston. Life here does not fight the machinery; it reinterprets it.

***

In the tightest spaces—inside the empty, thread-bare bolt sockets and behind the smooth, curved backs of the bronze filter grates—dwell the **Grease-Spinners**. 

They are the size of a thumb, segmented and pale as tallow, with shells that look less like chitin and more like tarnished silver foil. They do not swim; they lack fins entirely. Instead, their eight jointed legs terminate in dense, brush-like pads of copper-colored bristles. They live in the places where old lubricants and heavy mineral waxes bleed out of the machine's unsealed joints. They scrape this warm, golden sludge into spinning, jelly-like spheres, rolling them between their forelegs until the spheres are impregnated with micro-algae. 

When the piston strikes below—*th-thump*—the shockwave releases the spinners from the iron plates. Hundreds of them drift out into the open water, suspended in the amber light, holding their golden grease-globes like tiny lanterns before the current catches them and sweeps them back toward the grates.

***

Where the current is fastest, near the roaring bypass valves that burst with underwater fire, live the **Ribbon-Jacks**. 

They are the slender, iron-scaled fish that flash through the shafts of light, but up close, they are stranger still. Their scales do not overlap like those of a river trout; they fit together like overlapping armor plates on a deep-sea cable, brushed with a dull, iridescent copper-green rust. They have no eyes. In their place are two soft, sensitive pits lined with glowing, hair-thin filaments that detect the electrical fields radiating from the copper coils along the walls.

The Ribbon-Jacks do not hunt by sight or chase their prey. They align their long, flexible bodies with the electrical field lines of the room, floating in perfect, motionless grids across the sunlit spans of water. When the high-pressure jets fire, blowing clouds of golden, oxidized iron flakes into the current, the Ribbon-Jacks open wide, hinged mouths lined with thousands of comb-like baleen plates. They drink the rust-cloud, filtering out the heavy mineral dust and the microscopic larvae that feed upon it, their scales vibrating in acoustic sympathy with the deafening roar of the jet.

***

Higher up, where the water touches the trapped cushion of pressurized air, the environment changes. Here, beneath the silver, mirror-smooth air-ceiling, hangs a colony of **Loom-Skins**.

They are amphibious, bat-shaped organisms with skin like oiled chamois leather, pale lilac on their bellies and dark slate along their spines. They cling upside-down to the iron ceiling plates using broad, vacuum-lobed digits that grip the wet metal with incredible strength. Their broad, wing-like membranes do not fly; they float on the water's surface from above, like living oil slicks.

Their heads remain entirely in the warm, pressurized air pocket, snorting the rich, ozone-heavy gas that bubbles up from the electrical coils below. Their long, translucent, ribbon-like tails hang down six, eight, ten feet into the water, swaying in the continuous current like living plumb lines. These tails are covered in sticky, sweet-scented mucus that attracts the clockwork-shrimp from the lower machinery. When a shrimp climbs up a tail to feed, the Loom-Skin twitches, hauling its tail upward into the air pocket with a quick, wet *slap* to harvest the climber.

***

But all of these are merely the parasites and grazers of a far larger presence—the true master of the warm cistern.

It sleeps wrapped around the central bronze turbine assembly, its body so immense and so seamlessly integrated into the machine that for long minutes it appears to be nothing more than a thick, leather-jacketed steam pipe or a section of smooth, fallen conduit.

It has no common name, for no human eye outside the spire has ever seen it. It is a **Spire-Siphon**, a leviathan born of a thousand generations of high-altitude isolation.

Its body is twenty feet long, thick as a wine cask, and entirely limbless. Its skin is not scaly, but resembles the wet, water-smoothed iron of the walls—a deep, mottled dark brown, slick with protective oils, and entirely hairless. Along its flanks run two thick, pale ridges of specialized muscle that flicker with bioluminescent copper light, beating in counter-rhythm to the low drone of the electrical coils.

It has no bones. Its interior structure is a dense lattice of tough, rubbery cartilage that can withstand the crushing hydraulic thrum of the chamber without bruising. Its head is broad and spade-shaped, wedge-engineered to fit perfectly into the curved concavities between the turbine's bronze blades.

The Siphon does not hunt in any traditional sense. It does not need to. It is an apex filter-feeder that has become an actual component of the water system. 

It anchors its tail into the dark, yawning floor grates using a cluster of muscular, ringed suckers. Its long, heavy body floats lazily up into the golden daylight, drifting through the fields of emerald silk-weed. Every few minutes, as the central wheel turns with a heavy, grinding groan, the Siphon shifts its position. It opens its vast, circular mouth—a soft, toothless vacuum lined with thick rings of pink, heat-sensing flesh—and presses it directly over one of the leaking wall-intakes.

The hot, mineral-rich water surges directly into the creature's chest. Its sides swell outward like an accordioned forge-bellows. Inside its body, specialized organ-chambers strip the water of grease, heavy copper salts, and micro-fauna, using the warmth of the fluid to fuel its impossibly slow, efficient metabolism. Moments later, it vents the purified water out through four large, slit-like gills on its upper back, sending shimmering, thermal plumes up toward the light shafts.

The Siphon is almost entirely blind, possessing only two small, milky lenses that can distinguish the golden beam of the skylight from the rust-red shadows of the lower grates. But it is acoustically radiant. It feels the *th-thump* of the lower piston deep within its cartilaginous core. When the piston strikes, the Siphon responds, emitting a low, resonant, organ-pipe hum from its gill-slits—a sound that vibrates through the warm water, through the iron walls, and out into the dizzying, wind-scoured abyss beyond the curved glass ports.

It has lived in this single chapel of warm water for centuries, growing slowly, rubbing its thick skin against the riveted iron until the metal was smooth, and the creature was rounded to fit the metal. It knows no soil, no sky, no cold. It knows only the continuous warmth, the taste of rust and ozone, and the eternal, thumping pulse of the iron heart below.
