To walk here without falling requires a body shaped by the bowl itself.

The people who inhabit this hanging hive—the *Root-bound*, or the *Graft-folk*—are short, squat, and shockingly heavy through the chest and shoulders. Their lungs are broad bellows, expanded to pump oxygen from the thin mountain vapor, making their rib cages look like turned oak barrels beneath their skin. Their legs are thick and slightly bowed, ending in feet that no longer look entirely human: wide as spades, with splayed, muscular toes that can curl around a wet bamboo plank like tree roots. The soles of their feet are covered in a dark, rubbery callous an inch thick, impervious to cold mud, jagged obsidian flakes, and the icy bite of the runoff water.

Their skin is the color of wet walnut wood, permanently stained by the bitter tannins of bark-dye and the dark peat-mud they knee-deeply navigate. To survive the constant dripping chill without freezing, they smear their limbs with a yellow-white pomade rendered from the fat of the lard-pale salamanders that haunt the lower staircases, whipped with crushed mint and pine-gum. It gives them a pale, glistening sheen under the diffuse mountain daylight—making them look like wet stones themselves—and causes the mist to bead and roll off their shoulders in endless tiny pearls. They smell of cold fat, sharp resin, crushed leaves, and the pungent, sour tang of their own heat.

They dress in layers of beaten bark-cloth, soft as felt but tough as leather, oiled repeatedly until it shines like beetle wing. Over this, workers wear thick, stiff aprons of boiled mountain-hide to protect their torsos from the flying chips of wood, snapping vine- lashings, and sharp obsidian blades that are their constant companions. Heavy fiber belts sag around their waists, weighted with stone mauls, bone scrapers, coils of green sinew, and horn flasks of tree-gum.

Because the city is a single, deafening resonator of rushing flumes and chiming frogs, the Graft-folk do not speak like lowland people. Their language is devoid of soft sibilants, which are swallowed instantly by the roar of the water. Instead, it is composed of sharp, resonant chest-clicks, deep glottal pops, and high, piercing whistles that cut through the metallic ringing of the glass-frog colonies. A worker three stories up on a swaying reed-piling does not shout instructions; he blows a staccato sequence through a notched bone whistle strapped to his wrist, telling the haulers below precisely when to heave on a vine-rope or drive a wedged peg.

There are no idle hands. In this place, idleness is a structural threat. 

The people are divided not by class, but by the material they master:

*   **The Grafters** are the architects and elders. They spend their days on slender, swinging ladders, carrying bone chisels and pots of warm grafting-wax. They know how to nick the bark of a living willow-wand so it fuses with its neighbor, how to starve a giant reed so its walls thicken into iron, and how to read the subtle leaning of the canopy overhead. They are quiet, observant folk whose fingers are scarred and knobby as the twigs they weave.
*   **The Cutters and Maul-men** are the muscle of the hive. Young, broad-backed men and women who move in rhythmic teams, chopping away the aggressive green shoots that threaten to choke the narrow stairways, and hammering heavy hardwood wedges into swollen, water-logged joints. Their bodies are built for short, violent bursts of labor, swinging stone mauls with a terrifying, practiced precision where a single missed strike could snap a suspended walkway into the abyss.
*   **The Flume-tenders** live in the misty lower levels, perpetually soaked. They manage the wooden plumbing, clearing moss-sludge from the split-reed pipes and tuning the small wooden water-wheels. They are the ones who harvest the water— directing it into cisterns, grain-grinders, and the deep, cold steeping-vats where bark-cloth is softened.

In the dark, dripping underworld beneath the woven huts—where the light fades to a dim green twilight and the air is thick with the scent of wet rot—live the children and the elderly. Here, where the giant roots form a dense, tangled floor over the three-hundred-foot drop, old women sit on slabs of wet river-slate, their gnarled hands moving with breathtaking speed as they split reeds into weaving-ribbons, strip the fibrous inner bark of mountain birch, and scrape the meat from gathered shrimp. 

The children are like slippery otters. Naked but for coats of salamander-grease, they scramble through the root-tangles with frightening agility, collecting the fist-sized, furry beetles that cling to the pilings. They do not kill the beetles; they scrape the thick, velvety fur from the insects' backs into birch-bark buckets to be spun into warm yarn, then set the armored creatures free to crawl back into the dark.

The fauna of the bench are not pets, nor are they mere wild animals; they are the clockwork and livestock of the settlement.
*   The **glass frogs**, whose metallic chiming fills the air, are the city's barometer. The Graft-folk know that if the chiming drops in pitch or suddenly ceases, a cold thermal shift is rising from the valley below, bringing violent wind gusts that could tear suspended rooms from their lashings. When the frogs go quiet, the entire human hive freezes, listens, and immediately begins double-knotting the safety lines.
*   The **moss-parrots** are messy, squabbling neighbors, but their habit of tearing at seed-cones is crucial. Teams of young gatherers follow the flocks from below, catching the shower of broken cones and crushed nuts in wide fabric hoppers; these seeds are pressed for a rich, golden oil that feeds the small stone lamps burning in the dark interior of the woven homes.
*   The **lard-salamanders** are farmed in a state of semi-domestication within the shadowed runoff troughs. Fed on kitchen scraps and shrimp-shells, they are harvested periodically for their fat and their smooth, tough skins, which are cured into waterproof gaskets for the flume-joints.

Nothing here is built to last forever, and so the people do not value permanence; they value *vigilance*. A house is not a static object passed down through generations—it is a living pet that must be fed, pruned, repaired, and eventually allowed to die and rot down into the spongy bedrock so a new hut can be woven over its bones. 

When a Graft-person dies, there are no graves, for there is no soil to dig. Instead, the body is wrapped tightly in layers of green vine and moss, weighted with river-stones, and lowered into the deep, dark heart of the root-bedrock beneath the city. There, in the cold, wet darkness where the giant trees draw their water, the dead are consumed by the same relentless, intertwining life that holds the houses three stories above the clouds. 

They live fast, move constantly, and creak like the wood beneath their feet—a human strain fully integrated into a city that is breathing, growing, and perpetually threatening to shake them off into the white fog below.
