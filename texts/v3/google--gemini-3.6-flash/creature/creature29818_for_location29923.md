They are beings formed for a world without velocity. 

To see one move through the drowned lanes is to watch something heavier than the water, yet entirely reconciled to it. They are tall—longer in the torso and arm than any terrestrial creature—with broad, slab-like chests and smooth, seamless hips that taper into long, double-jointed legs. Their skin has no scales, no hair, and no oil-shedding sheen; it is matte-surfaced and dense, like water-logged leather or unpolished basalt, shaded in muted hues of liver-brown, slate, and olive-bronze that make them vanish against the masonry if they hold still.

They are not fast swimmers. They do not possess the frantic, flexed tail-fin of open-ocean hunters. Instead, they move by drift and leverage. Their feet are broad and five-toed, with tough, calloused soles that grip the smooth pillow-lavas without slipping, and their long hands terminate in blunt, heavy fingers tipped with thick nails of horn—nails worn flat and smooth from generations of wedging granite and knapping glass.

They breathe the warm, sulfur-sweet brine through four long, vertical slits situated low on their rib cages, beneath the swell of their latissimus muscles. When they are at rest, tucked into the moss-lined alcoves of their towers, these vents expand and collapse with a motion so slow it can easily be mistaken for the pulse of a heat-plume passing through the room. They are warm-blooded, but their warmth is steady and ambient, banked deep within thick layers of dense, fibrous fat that smell faintly of bruised kelp and turned earth.

Their faces are flat, broad, and serene. They lack external ears; instead, two smooth, cartilage-rimmed depressions behind their wide jaws register the deep vibration of the earth-hum and the high, thin *ping* of knapped flint. Their eyes are large—the size of smooth river plums—with double pupils that collapse into narrow horizontal slits under the brassy glint of the noon sun, and dilate into vast, dark circles that fill the entire socket when they descend into the narrow, lightless foundations of the city. They have no eyelids; a clear, tough nictitating membrane sweeps slowly across the cornea once every few minutes, sweeping away microscopic algae and silt.

Language among them is almost entirely physical, a tactile and acoustic dialect borne through the dense water. They do not shout or sing. Speech is a combination of low, resonant clicks produced by snapping the hard plates at the back of their palates—a sound that travels hundreds of yards through stone and water—and a subtle, ripples-in-oil shifting of skin pigments across their foreheads and upper chests. A conversation between two stone-cutters hanging from a kelp-cable looks, to an outsider, like absolute silence punctuated by the occasional flash of pale yellow beneath their chin-hides and the rhythmic *clack-clack* of bone tools.

Their social structure is as immobile and enduring as the buildings they stack. They do not understand private territory in the way surface-dwellers do; a tower belongs to whoever is currently resting within its terraced cells. Yet their sense of obligation to the city’s structural integrity is absolute. A person will spend forty years repairing a single two-hundred-yard stretch of kelp-cable, hauling fresh vines up from the lower deeps, scraping away the encrusting barnacle-mollusks, and braiding the wet fibers with sinew until the span is taut and safe again. 

There are no children as terrestrial species know them—no swarms of small, darting fry. They reproduce rarely, perhaps twice in a lifetime that spans centuries. A single egg, the size of a stone mortar and wrapped in a tough, amber-colored parchment, is wedged deep into a thermal vent by both parents. For three years, the community tends the vent, clearing debris and ensuring the warm water continues to wash over the egg until it dissolves, releasing a miniature, fully formed youth who already possesses the heavy bone-density required to stand on the paved streets without drifting toward the mist above.

In the late afternoon—or what passes for afternoon in the invariant bronze twilight—the city’s inhabitants gather along the high, suspended reed-bridges. They do not play games or hold loud markets. They gather to groom and to weight one another. 

Because tools, heavy stone blocks, and kelp-tubers alter an individual’s personal buoyancy, long-term balance is a constant concern. A gatherer coming up from the deep vine-gardens with a heavy belly full of starched tubers becomes too heavy, dragging their feet along the basalt flags; a weaver who has spent days at the dry-reed frames becomes light and floats toward the ceiling of the sea. On the bridges, they assist each other in adjusting their weight belts—broad straps of cured sea-vine hung with lead weights and river-pebbles—adding or subtracting stones with practiced, gentle movements until each person hovers perfectly weightless in mid-water, neither rising nor sinking.

Once balanced, they drift together in quiet clusters, their long legs loosely folded, hands resting on each other’s shoulders or anchored to the cable-braids. They take their nourishment from thick, jelly-like cakes pressed from thermal tubers and pressed-vine oils, passing the dark slabs from hand to hand without speaking. 

They know very little of what lies above their ceiling of greenish mist. To them, the sky is simply "The Light-Without-Weight"—a cold, thin, lethal place where water ends and where a body, stripped of its buoyant comfort, would drop to the dry, hard stone and crush itself under the unmitigated pull of the earth. They look up at the brassy disc of the sun with a mild, indifferent pity, then turn back to the warm, thick, supporting dark of the city, where every stone fits neatly against the next, and nothing ever falls.
