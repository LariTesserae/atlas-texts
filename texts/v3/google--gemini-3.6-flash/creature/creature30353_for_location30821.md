The life in this place does not know the concept of a pulse. Where there is no liquid, there can be no circulation, no surge of blood, no damp pumping of a heart. 

Instead, the inhabitants are engines of friction, static, and slow, thermal consumption.

If you sit at the basin’s center and wait for the ambient amber light to shift—though it never does—you begin to notice that the thickets of paper-reeds are not merely growing; they are being pastured. 

The primary grazers are the plum-sized, copper-bristled mites, but they are only the lowest tier of a dry, articulate hierarchy. Alongside them move creatures that resemble joints of hollow bamboo linked by dried sinew. They have no faces, only flat, spade-shaped heads made of hardened gall-nut, which they press against the red tiles to absorb the heat radiating from below. They feed by scraping the golden spore-flour from the floor with rasping plates under their chins. Their internal organs are not wet tissues, but intricate, nested bellows of dried bladder-leather and pleated pith that compress air, extract the fine vegetable powder, and exhale it as an odorless, bone-dry exhaust.

When two of these straw-jointed things cross paths on a vertical wall, they do not fight or avoid one another. They collide with a soft *clack*, like two wooden chess pieces set down too hard, and remain pressed together for hours, exchanging static charges through the fine copper bristles on their flanks until their temperatures equalize.

Higher up, where the floor folds seamlessly into the shadow of the terraced walls, live the makers of the place.

The sleeper in the west niche is not alone, nor is it dead. It is a **Silt-Sifter**, one of perhaps three or four that inhabit this thirty-pace domain. 

In form, a Sitter resembles a human torso that has been bound, dried, and scraped until only the fundamental levers of bone and tanned hide remain. Its head is a smooth, earless oval of pale horn, featureless save for a horizontal fissure where a mouth should be, and two shallow, concavely polished depressions of dark amber that serve as eyes. It has no eyelids; the amber simply absorbs the ambient light, glowing from within like resin trapped in dry pine.

Its limbs are disproportionately long, thin as willow-switches, and jointed with dark pegs of boiled horn that click softly whenever it shifts its weight. Its fingers do not end in soft pads or nails, but in flint-blades worn down to smooth, rounded edges, and broad, spatulate thumbs that match the exact width of the hex-tile seams.

These beings do not walk so much as they thread themselves through the geometry. Because the basin’s spatial laws are warped by the dry heat and the ancient craft of its making, a Sitter does not need to climb the notched-ash ladder to reach the upper terraces. It simply leans forward, allowing its weight to fall toward a surface that appears to be thirty feet above it, and its feet land quietly on the dark clay as if it had only taken a single, level step.

Their work is endless, silent, and deliberate:

They are the ones who press the bone-silt into the seams. From time to time, one of the Sitters will reach into its own chest—where a hollow cavity behind its ribs holds a reservoir of ground marrow and pulverized stone—and extract a pinch of the pale dust. Using its spatulate thumb, it packs the powder into any hairline gap between the terracotta hexes, pressing with a slow, agonizing weight until the seam is as hard and smooth as a scab.

They tend the spore-globes, turning the basket-frames with two fingers so that the dry tendon-ropes do not twist or snap under tension. 

And they cut the tiles. In the quietest hours—if an environment without night can be said to have hours—you can hear the rhythmic, papery *shrrk-shrrk* of a Sitter dragging its flint-tipped finger across a fresh block of raw, unbaked clay pulled from a dark recess beneath the lowest terrace. They do not use templates or straightedges. Their long, knobbed fingers move with a mechanical, instinctual memory, scoring lines so straight and true that the resulting hexes fit together without a millimeter of error.

Their consciousness is not like ours. Without water, there can be no fluid thoughts, no drifting memories, no stream of awareness. A Sitter’s mind operates like a dry latch dropping into a groove. It exists in a state of absolute, motionless attention until a change in heat, a drop in static charge, or a displaced grain of spore-dust triggers a physical response. 

They do not communicate with sound. When two Sitters meet at the intersection where the floor becomes the ceiling, they press their horn-heads together. The dry, hollow chambers inside their skulls act as resonance boxes, transferring tiny, clicking vibrations—the scraping of internal pith-valves, the ticking of expanding tendon-cords—from one to the other. To them, a conversation is a mechanical transfer of structural state: *Tile fourteen on the third tier is cool. The spore-basket above the north wall is empty.*

And then there is the matter of the recursion.

The Sitter in the west niche, whose sides expand only once every few minutes with that dry, bellows-like hiss, is watching the walnut-sized atrium inside the wall behind it. Within that microscopic basin, an even smaller version of itself—no larger than a grain of rice—is currently using a thumb the thickness of a human hair to press bone-silt between tiles so tiny they can barely be distinguished. 

If you were to lean close enough to the sleeper to feel the faint, toaster-warm breath rising from its throat-slits, you would realize that its eyes are not looking *at* the small atrium. They are reflecting it. And if you looked up, above the high, dark-clay terraces of the room you stand in, you would see a vast, curved shadow looming over the lip of the outer wall—a thumb the size of a hill, tipped with a blade of dark flint, waiting patiently to press a fresh layer of silt into the sky above your head.

Nothing dies here, because nothing has ever been wet enough to rot. When a Sitter’s tendon-joints finally wear through after thousands of years of friction, it simply stops moving. The copper mites crawl over its leather skin, harvesting the dry flakes of its outer surface to build their own pupa-cases. The straw-bugs use its ribs as bridges across the gaps in the terraces. Over centuries, the heat bakes the abandoned frame until it is as hard and red as the floor beneath it, indistinguishable from the clay niches carved to hold it, waiting for the silt to fill its seams.
