Life here does not contend with the iron or the brine; it has simply ceased to draw a distinction between them. 

Where the water moves with the languid weight of cold oil, movement is an investment of immense energy, and so the primary mode of existence is adherence. The vault’s living things are slow, heavy-bodied, and nearly indistinguishable from the municipal architecture they inhabit. They are built of grease, lime, cold cartilage, and the dense, insoluble metals that have settled out of the water over centuries.

***

At the lowest level of organization are the **Rivet-Grafts**—sessile, mollusk-like organisms that do not possess true shells, but rather secrete a thick, black, iron-oxide slurry over their soft mantles. Over generations, they set themselves into the recessed seams of the boilers and the hex-headed bolt lines of the primary manifolds. To the touch, they are indistinguishable from the cast iron around them—cold, slick, and glass-hard. 

They do not filter the brine for plankton, for none exists. Instead, they are chemical grazers. Their soft undersides extend microscopic, acid-secreting filaments into the joints between pipes, dissolving the microscopic traces of lead-white paste, tallow, and mineral grease packed into the threads by long-dead engineers. When a siphon vents and the vault shuddering under the hydraulic shock, the Rivet-Grafts flex their mantles in unison, opening tiny, slotted siphons to catch the plumes of black iron dust stirred up from the floor, digesting the heavy metal salts and depositing them back onto their own metallic hides.

***

Deeper within the network of copper conduits, where the water is entirely dark and almost motionless, live the creatures glimpsed only as pale ribbons. They are called **Conduit-Threaders**, though they have no eyes to know what a conduit is. 

A Threader may reach two meters in length, yet its body is no thicker than a thumb. It is entirely cartilage and oily lipid, without a rib or a vertebra, allowing it to squeeze through pipe openings no wider than a coin. Its flesh is milky, ice-clear, and soft as soaked tallow; through its walls, one can see the slow, peristaltic movement of its gut—a dark, thin line packed with ingested grease-mold, mineral flakes, and microscopic bits of radium-dust scraped from the backs of broken pressure gauges.

Because the brine is too thick for easy swimming, the Threaders do not propel themselves with fins. Instead, they use arrays of short, stiff, copper-colored setae along their flanks to "walk" along the interiors of the iron pipes, pulling themselves forward against the slow, sluggish currents. They are profoundly deaf to any vibration carried through the fluid, but intensely sensitive to the mechanical thrum of the basalt walls. 

When the great hydraulic piston cycles every few minutes, sending its low, chest-thudding concussion through the submerged city, the Threaders freeze. Their hair-like barbels fan out in the water, sensing the subtle shift in pressure that follows the stroke. They mate only during the piston’s return cycle, releasing clouds of heavy, lipid-rich eggs into the brief, reverse-surge of the brine, allowing the machinery’s breath to distribute their young into the upper pipe-grids.

***

Where the grease leaks are thickest—around the primary valve trunks where ancient leather gaskets have rotted into a black, tarry slime—a third thing grows. It is neither animal nor mold, but a symbiotic colony known as the **Lard-Marrow**. 

It appears as heavy, pale-yellow lobes of fat, hanging like lard-dipped rags from the catwalks and indicator housings. It grows with an agonizing slowness, expanding by millimeters each decade. The Marrow is remarkably cold—its internal temperature matches the near-freezing brine exactly—and it is laced with a dense, fibrous web of metallic veins that draw up trace copper and zinc from the fittings it clings to. 

The Lard-Marrow is the primary food source for the Threaders, but it is not passive. As it consumes the grease from the pipe-joints, it secretes a dense, self-healing wax that fills the spaces left behind by decaying mechanical seals. In a strange, unconscious feedback loop, the Marrow preserves the very machinery it feeds upon, sealing the pressurized pipes against total collapse, ensuring that the cold grease continues to seep at the precise rate required for its own survival.

***

And in the deepest, most claustrophobic corners of the vault—within the drowned interiors of the main fire-tube boilers, where the dark is absolute and the pressure is highest—there is something larger.

It does not swim through the alleys. It has no need to. It is the **Sump-Lurk**, though it is less an individual animal and more a singular, sprawling mass of muscle, oil-bladder, and mineralized skin that has grown to conform to the inner shape of the central pumping manifold. 

Its hide is a thick, rubbery carpet of black leather, covered in a dense forest of sensitive, thread-like cilia that drift in the slow, glass-like current. It has no face, no limbs, and no brain in the vertebrate sense; its nervous system is a diffuse ring of thick ganglia that wraps around the central intake pipe. 

The Lurk lives by the rhythm of the vault’s automated balance. When the siphons open with their heavy, metallic *clank*, drawing silt and brine toward the southern sumps, the Lurk expands its massive, oil-filled body, acting as a living diaphragm. It shifts thousands of gallons of the dense fluid through its internal chambers, filtering out the heavy oil-droplets and mineral salts before expelling the purified, super-cooled brine back into the upper heat-exchangers. 

It is immense, ancient, and completely blind. Its heartbeat does not exist; its blood is moved entirely by the external compression of the hydraulic pistons pounding through the basalt floor. If the pumps were to stop, the Lurk would suffocate in its own stillness within a week. But as long as the clockwork gears turn and the pistons thrum, it remains anchored in the iron dark—a living gasket in the chest of a sunken engine, breathing only when the city breathes.
