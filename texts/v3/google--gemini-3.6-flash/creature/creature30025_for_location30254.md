To live here is to be boiled soft, then hardened into something that belongs to the basalt.

The people of the Sump—the *Steam-ribs*, as they are sometimes called by those who dwell in the cooler, upper galleries of the city—do not look like the inhabitants of the surface or even the high-vault districts. They are a population reshaped by humidity, iron, and total absence of sun, moving through the fog with the deliberate, low-energy economy of cold-blooded things.

### The Physicality of the Sump-Dwellers

Their skin is remarkably pale, not the translucent white of the cave eels, but a water-logged, doughy grey-pink that never fully dries. Veins show through their forearms like blue iron wires. Because the ambient air sits at body temperature, sweating does little to cool them; instead, their bodies are covered in a permanent sheen of condensate that smells faintly of sulfur, coal-soot, and boiled lard. 

Hair is worn either shaved to the scalp or braided tightly with tallow to keep the heavy moisture from plastering it across the face. Their eyes have adapted to the amber gloom of the filament bulbs and the sudden, violent orange flashes of the furnace doors: pupils are permanently dilated, surrounded by bloodshot irises yellowed by sulfur fumes. They do not blink often. The high humidity keeps their corneas slick, giving their gaze a wide, unreadable, glassy intensity.

Footwear is a matter of survival. Nobody walks barefoot on the jagged basalt or the scalding, grease-slick catwalks. They wear *sabots*—heavy wooden-soled clogs bound in oil-soaked leather, raised two inches off the ground on thick iron cleats. The sound of a shift change in the Sump is unmistakable: a heavy, rhythmic *clack-clack-clack* of hundreds of iron-shod soles striking the wet metal grates, echoing through the steam like the marching of a clumsy automaton.

### Roles and Industry

There are no idle hands in the vault; the environment requires constant maintenance merely to prevent the brick tenements from collapsing under wet-rot and the engines from seizing up with rust.

*   **The Pipe-Wrights and Steam-Caulkers:** These are the most heavily scarred among them. Their skin is a patchwork of shiny, white, hairless keloids—the legacy of live steam leaks. They carry long hardwood rods which they hold against the iron pipes, pressing the other end to their jawbones; by listening to the vibrations in their teeth, they can locate a thinning wall or a failing valve inside the canvas wrapping long before it ruptures.
*   **The Sluice-Skimmers:** Moving along the flooded trenches in shallow, flat-bottomed punt boats made of riveted iron, these workers carry long-handled copper nets. They scoop the thick rafts of thumb-sized cave beetles from the water pipes and sluice-edges, dumping them by the bushel into wicker hampers. They move slowly, accustomed to the heat, their shoulders broad and wet, shining like the rock walls around them.
*   **The Boiler-Keepers (Firemen):** Positioned at the far rock face, where the heat is almost hallucinatory, these men and women are permanently stained with soot that has bonded with their skin under the relentless steam. They work in short, brutal bursts, stripped to the waist, shoveling crushed coal into the roaring furnace bellies. They are largely deaf, their ear drums deadened by the concussive roar of the fireboxes, and they communicate entirely through a dialect of sharp hand-signals and heavy foot-stamping.

### The Symbiotic Ecosystem

The human inhabitants do not merely endure the vault’s vermin; they have incorporated them into a closed, claustrophobic economy. 

The thumb-sized beetles are the cornerstone of life in the Lower District. Once scooped from the walls, they are dumped into large iron hoppers and crushed under stone rollers driven by the beam engines. The resulting paste is boiled down to yield a dense, black oil used to lubricate the lower gear-trains and grease the catwalks. What remains—the dry, chitinous meal—is salted, packed into molds, and baked on top of the steam pipes into hard, bitter cakes known as *Chitin-bread*. It tastes of dry earth and copper, but it keeps the population alive.

The blind crabs, caught in crude wire trapsbaited with boiler grease, are boiled in iron buckets hung directly over the exhaust valves. Their pale meat is sweet and muddy, eaten straight from the shell with short bone picks. 

Even the swifts that nest in the cavern roof are integrated into the rhythm of the place. Children—agile, thin-ribbed creatures who run the overhead girders with terrifying casualness—harvest the dry guano from the high ledge-cracks to sell to the gunpowder mills further up, or string long nets between the upper steam pipes to catch the birds for the soup pots.

### Language and Culture

Speech in the Sump is rare and quiet. The air is so heavy with moisture and noise—the *thump-hiss* of the pumps, the rustle of millions of beetle legs, the shriek of the swifts—that long sentences are a waste of breath. 

Instead, the language of the vault is guttural, truncated, and heavily reliant on sound-mimicry. Words are clipped short at the throat to keep from inhaling steam. They tap out messages on the steam pipes with iron wedges—a primitive telegraph system that vibrates through the rock walls, letting families in distant brick blocks know when a pipe has burst or a boiler is being drawn down.

There is no concept of privacy. The brick housing blocks are built like honeycombs, their small, windowless rooms sharing walls that radiate the heat of the surrounding stone. Beds are suspended hammocks woven from rot-resistant canvas, swinging gently in time with the deep, rhythmic thrumming of the engines. When a Sump-dweller rests, they do not lie still; their bodies keep time with the machines, subtly swaying, breathing in sync with the wet *cough-and-drag* of the nearest water pump.

They possess a quiet, catatonic patience. They do not rush. To move too quickly in this air is to invite heat-stroke or a fatal slip into a trench of boiling boiler-bleed water. Everything is done with a slow, heavy precision. When they gather at the end of a shift, sitting on the lower rungs of the iron catwalks with their legs dangling over the warm, eel-filled channels, they rarely speak at all. They simply sit, slick-skinned and silent, watching the amber light flicker in the steam, listening to the vault breathe around them.
