Down on the floor plates, where the brass mesh meets the pitted ceramic, life begins with the groomers. 

They are small—some no larger than a pocket watch, others the size of a iron kettle—and they move with the dry, hitching precision of a clock whose pendulum is slightly too heavy. Their bodies are not carved or assembled so much as accreted: carapaces of dark, vulcanized resin fused around internal frameworks of bone-dry chitin and fine silver wire. They have no eyes. In their place are clusters of polished quartz prisms that register the shifting intensity of the cold glare above, turning their heads in stiff, ninety-degree increments as they read the grid.

These creatures—the *clearing-beetles*, though they resemble no insect known to warm soil—live entirely in the service of the circuit. Their legs are slender drafting-compasses tipped with pads of woven felt and dense graphite. They spend decades walking the seams of the steel floor, sweeping away the fallen, paper-thin mica leaves and dusting the white ceramic insulator rings with their brushes. If an insulator becomes fouled with quartz dust, the local current drops; the groomers feel this drop as an uncomfortable tightening in their spring-steel tendons, a cold stiffness that drives them toward the fault. 

When a groomer’s internal gearwork finally strips, or its resin shell splits from centuries of dry friction, it does not rot. It simply stops. Within hours, the trailing vines of silicon-cellulose briers find its motionless frame, send thin, copper-veined tendrils through its joint-seams, and weld it into the floor as a permanent structural bracket.

Higher up, threading the long avenues between the thirty-foot iron pillars, are the *strider-herds*. 

These are larger, tall as draft horses but narrow as iron gates, built from laminated slate, hollowed bronze tube, and accordion-folded leather that has long since petrified into something as tough as horn. They move on three or four jointed stilt-legs made of leaf-spring steel. Their movement is entirely soundless, save for the faint, rhythmic *clack-tap* of their hardened vulcanite feet striking the riveted floor plates. 

The striders have no mouths or digestive tracts. Instead, their long, rib-like torsos house coils of insulated copper wire wrapped around heavy cores of soft, laminated iron. They are galvanic grazers. When they reach a grove of piston-fronds, they do not eat the foliage; they slide their long, needle-like snouts into the open grease-ports of the cylinder heads. There, they absorb the raw electrical potential generated by the silent, sliding roots, recharging the heavy flywheels that spin deep within their chests.

The spatial anomalies of the landscape do not disorient them—they require them. When a strider walks down a corridor where perspective expands rather than narrows, its long, hinged body stretches in sympathy with the warped geometry. A observer watching from a distance would see the creature lengthen like a shadow at sunset, its bronze limbs extending ten, twenty, fifty paces without increasing in thickness, stepping effortlessly across gaps that should be impossible to span, only to snap back to its compact, rigid proportions the moment it steps out of the distorted fold.

And then there are the shadows themselves.

In the places where the light should fall clean, dark pools linger at the bases of the mechanical trunks. These are not the absence of light, but *dielectric fluid-life*—living, highly viscous masses of ultra-dense petroleum lipids saturated with billions of microscopic iron filings. They move at the speed of a cooling glacier, creeping over the bronze mesh like spilled pitch. 

These dark puddles are the recyclers of the plain. When a iron vine sheds a heavy, lead-weighted flower or a structural strut undergoes dry structural fatigue and snaps, the fluid creeps over the debris. Over months, through a process of cold, acidless reduction, the fluid strips the metal back into pure, crystalline powders. It leaves behind neat, geometric mounds of zinc bloom and graphite powder, perfectly sorted by atomic weight, ready to be drawn back up through the hollow roots of the nearest iron-oak. The fluid is sensitive to the subtle magnetic tides; during the static storms, when the silent blue sparks leap across the canopy, the dark pools rise into standing, needle-like cones, quivering in harmony with the sky before collapsing back into flat, shining mirrors.

Highest of all, drifting through the upper branches where the steel struts meet the silver fog, are the *drifters*. 

They are delicate, weightless structures resembling geometric kites or the skeletons of umbrella frames, woven from spun-glass filaments and gold leaf no thicker than a soap bubble. They have no limbs and no internal organs; they are pure surface area. They float on the thin, freezing air, buoyed by the intense static repulsion generated between their silver membranes and the mica canopy below. 

During the long, motionless days, the drifters hang motionless, their gold-leaf sails turned broadside to the sourceless radiance, storing charge in the thin ceramic capacitors that form their central nodes. When a static storm sweeps across the continent, turning the sky to brushed nickel, the drifters come alive. They tilt, falling into the silent blue electrical waves, riding the spark-crests across miles of shimmering forest without ever touching a leaf. They are the pollinators of this world—not carrying microspores, but transferring precise voltage signatures from one sector’s grid to another, triggering the growth of new iron pillars wherever the landscape's structural loads shift.

Everything here is bound into a single, dry, quiet pulse. There are no calls, no cries of pain or hunger, no territorial battles. When a strider, a groomer, or a drifter reaches the end of its functional endurance, it does not fight the cessation. It aligns itself precisely with the nearest structural seam, locks its joints into place, and waits for the cold zinc grease to solidify in its veins. The plants grow around it, the mica leaves drape over it, and within a century, it is indistinguishable from the factory floor—just another load-bearing ornament in a continent that continues to construct itself out of silence, light, and iron.
