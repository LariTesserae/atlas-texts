To live in the High Basin of Ostra is to be remade by vibration. The beings who inhabit this drowned, roaring shelf do not possess ears as air-breathing creatures elsewhere do; the passage into their heads is capped by a thick, concave plate of smooth cartilage, amber-colored and hairless, which seals out the acoustic fluid of the air. They do not hear the ten thousand vents; they *are* the vent sound. Their nervous systems have repurposed the sense of hearing into a total, systemic conductivity.

They are known—if they are named at all by those below the basalt curtains—as the **Ostran Sluice-Folk** or the **Thrum**, though among themselves, identity is a matter of frequency rather than a word spoken into the wind.

### The Morphology of the Thrum

Physically, they are broad and low-slung, built like the granite masonry they navigate. A grown individual stands rarely higher than five feet, but their shoulders are wider than a man’s, and their rib cages are massive, barrel-like vaults designed to withstand the constant, rhythmic concussion of the air. Their skin is smooth, hairless, and oily to the touch—a dense, pale-grey hide that secretes a natural tallow. This lipid coating allows the ceaseless, warm condensation to run off them in shimmering beads, protecting their flesh from the mineral-heavy, slightly acidic moisture that weeping aqueducts shed day and night.

Their feet are their primary organs of perception and language. Broad, flat, and completely devoid of arches, the soles are thick pads of dense, fibrous fat that conform perfectly to wet brick, pitted cast iron, or the glassy magnetite of the old rail beds. Through these soles, a Thrum reads the entire basin for miles around:
* They can feel the rising pressure in a subterranean boiler three terraces away as a subtle, high-pitched prickling in the heel.
* They recognize one another at a distance of two hundred yards by the distinct, low-frequency signature of a footprint pressing into the vibrating stone.
* They measure the twenty-minute cycle of the great iron walking beam as a cosmic pulse, timing their movements, their resting periods, and their slow migrations to its seismic plunge and groan.

Their eyes are large, set wide apart under heavy, prominent brow-ridges. They lack eyelids; instead, two sets of transparent, amber-tinted membranes sweep horizontally across the iris, wiping away moisture and filtering the intense, shadowless cream light that permeates the fog. They do not see sharp edges—there are few sharp edges left in Ostra—but they excel at perceiving the subtle thermal gradients in the mist and the faint, iridescent sheen of oil floating on the surface of condensation pools.

### Language Without Voice

In an environment where air-borne sound is a solid, impenetrable mass, speech is impossible. The Thrum communicate through **Contact Resonance** and **Thermal Coloration**.

When two Thrum meet on a wet plaza between the distant chimneys, they do not face each other and gesture into the air. They step onto the same continuous iron plate or fused rail bed. One will strike the metal with a heavy, brass-capped stave carried at the hip, or simply stomp a heel with calculated weight. The vibration travels through the magnetite, up through the soles of the second individual, and into their hollow, resonance-amplifying sternum. 

These "foot-strokes" are not crude signals; they are a complex, tonal language of harmonic variations. A master of the iron path can convey the structural integrity of an aqueduct, the location of a rich lichen-harvest, or a generational lineage through a series of subtle heel-taps that sound to an outsider like nothing more than the background thudding of the city itself.

For intimate conversation, they stand skin-to-skin. By pressing their forearms together, they pass micro-tremors directly through their muscles. Simultaneously, the specialized vascular network beneath their pale hide flushes with warm, oxygen-rich blood, creating shifting patterns of dark chestnut and pale mustard yellow across their chests and arms—a visual language that glows softly through the shadowless steam.

#### 1. The Brine-Skimmers (*Calx-carabus*)
In the shallow, warm pools that collect in the stone plazas, flat, saucer-shaped creatures the size of shield-bosses slide slowly over the submerged paving stones. Their shells are formed from layered lamellae of teal silica and iron oxide, giving them the appearance of polished turquoise scrap metal. They possess no eyes, feeding entirely by scraping the thick mats of chemotrophic bacteria and mineral crusts from the drowned iron tracks. The Thrum farm these creatures, harvesting the soft, lipid-rich flesh beneath the shell and using the discarded silica plates as scrapers, roofing tiles, and spades.

#### 2. The Great Siphon-Slugs
Thick as a man's thigh and long as a iron beam, these translucent, copper-tinted mollusks cling to the vertical faces of the wet brick tenement facades. They live where the hot runoff drips continuously from overhead gantries. They act as natural purifiers, siphoning the mineral-laden water into their bodies, filtering out the heavy metals, and excreting clear, drinkable condensate in slow, steady drops from their lower margin. A family of Thrum will often "adopt" a wall of Siphon-Slugs, building their living benches directly beneath the creatures' clean outflow.

#### 3. The Whistle-Spiders
Mentioned in passing as tiny, blind web-spinners, these creatures are actually the weavers of Ostra's social fabric. They do not build webs to catch flies—there are no flies in the steam. Instead, they spin dense, silvery funnels inside the mouths of silent whistle-pipes and pipe-joints to catch the rich, airborne protein-spores thrown up by the geothermal vents. 

The silk of the Whistle-Spider is immune to rot, acidic moisture, and heat. The Thrum collect this thread with long brass hooks, spinning it into heavy, rot-proof felt blankets and tough, water-repellent aprons that protect them when working near the boiling brine jets.

### Daily Life in Geological Time

The Thrum do not measure time in hours, days, or seasons. The sky above Ostra is a permanent white-gold cream; there is no night, only slight variations in the density of the fog when the distant plains far below enter shadow. 

Time is measured in **Beam-Strokes** (the 20-minute cycle of the great iron lever) and **Scale-Flushes** (the multi-year period it takes for a granite sluiceway to coat itself in a finger's-breadth of orange silica).

```
   0 min                  10 min                 20 min
---|----------------------|----------------------|---> [BEAM-STROKE]
   Apex Pause             Max Descent            Seismic Return
   (Rest / Inspection)    (Lubrication / Scrape) (Migration)
```

A Thrum's life moves at a pace that mirrors this mechanical patience:

* **The Tend-Work:** They are not inventors, nor do they consider themselves the owners of Ostra. They view themselves as vital components of the machinery—the living lubricant. A group of four Thrum may spend three entire Beam-Strokes (an hour) slowly pushing a single, massive tub of rendered brine-skimmer fat toward a dry axle-socket on a lazy flywheel, waiting for the exact moment of the stroke when the friction eases to apply the grease.
* **The Weep-Dwells:** They do not build houses with roofs; rain does not fall here—it rises and drifts as steam. Instead, they burrow into the hollow, double-walled brickwork of the ancient drum-houses. Inside these chambers, where the heat is steady and the deafening roar of the open plazas is muffled into a deep, womb-like rumble, they sleep hanging in silk hammocks woven from spider-thread, wrapped in damp felt, their feet resting against exposed iron structural ties so they remain connected to the pulse of the basin even in sleep.
* **Diet:** Their food is simple and constant. They consume the scraped teal-crusts from the sluices, boiled brine-skimmer tail, and a thick, sweet paste made from fermented velvet moss. They drink directly from the clean discharge of the Siphon-Slugs or from copper collection trays suspended beneath the dripping aqueducts.

### The Sense of the Whole

If an outsider could ask a Thrum what the High Basin of Ostra is, the Thrum would not understand the question. To them, there is no separation between the basalt shelf, the brick chimneys, the slow-pivoting iron levers, and the blood moving through their own wide veins.

They do not fear the breakdown of the machines, because in their long memory, the machines have never broken down; they merely change their pitch. If a giant flywheel slows over a century from one turn every three minutes to one turn every four, the Thrum simply adjust the rhythm of their steps, step onto a different magnetite rail, press their forearms together in the shadowless cream light, and let the new, deeper thrum carry them through another age.
