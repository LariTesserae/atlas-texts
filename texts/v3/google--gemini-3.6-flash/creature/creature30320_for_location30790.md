The life here does not merely occupy the stone; it is the friction that keeps the stone from grinding itself to dust.

At the lowest level of perception are the **Silt-Scrapers**—the armadillo-like isopods that crawl the lip of every water channel. They are the valley’s janitors and stonemasons' aids. Their undersides are fitted with dense rows of chitinous combs that sweep the basalt flagstones clean of slippery silt, directing the runoff precisely into the carved drainage lips. They feed on the micro-algae that draws life from the stone’s low thermal radiation. When a flagstone shifts even a fraction of a millimeter, the change in water flow alerts thousands of them; they gather in dense, armored clusters at the leak, their bodies acting as living dams until the sediment settles and seals the gap again.

Above them move the **Grip-Hounds**, though they are neither canine nor mammalian in any true sense. They are long-slung, eight-limbed hunters the size of broad-chested mastiffs, with skin like wet river slate and no eyes beneath their heavy, bone-shielded brows. They navigate the vertical terrace walls with terrifying, silent efficiency, their splayed feet ending in soft, disc-like pads that create vacuum-seals against the slick basalt. They do not hunt by sight or scent, but by acoustic mapping. The deafening wall of frog-croaks and cricket-rasps is their lantern; as the sound bounces off the wet, hard angles of the architecture, the Grip-Hounds read the echo-dampening shadows cast by a flying beetle or a roosting bird. They move like oil spilling down a staircase, slipping from one terrace lip to the next without dislodging a single drop of perched water.

And then there are the **Tenders**. 

They are the ones who walk the grand staircases, though "walking" implies a speed they do not possess. A Tender may take three minutes to lift a foot and place it on the step above. They are tall, lean bipeds, standing nearly nine feet high, but stooped forward at the spine so that their long, three-jointed arms almost brush the flagstones. Their skin is thick, rubbery, and a pale, bruised indigo, perpetually coated in the fine, beaded moisture of the valley air. They wear no clothes, for the stone provides all the heat they require, but their shoulders and flat, earless heads are draped in living mantles of star-moss that have taken root in the deep folds of their hide.

The Tenders are completely blind. Where eyes would be, their smooth, sloped skulls bear wide, lateral slits lined with thousands of microscopic, feather-light cilia. These cilia vibrate constantly, reading the humid air currents, the temperature gradients rising from the basalt, and the acoustic pressure of the valley's eternal chorus.

Their existence is one of endless, rhythmic maintenance:

*   **The Root-Pruners:** A Tender will stand before a centuries-old broad-leaf tree for three days, its long, spade-tipped fingers inserted deep into the narrow gap between two massive basalt blocks. It feels the expansion of the tree’s roots against the mortarless stone. With delicate, crushing strength, the Tender pinches back the root-tips just enough to halt their growth before they can displace the masonry, preserving the delicate balance where the plant anchors the stone and the stone confines the plant.
*   **The Cistern-Clearers:** They wade into the circular stone pools, their long fingers dredging the dark, leaf-heavy sediment from the bottom and laying it precisely into the stone planters along the terrace walls. Nothing is wasted; every scrap of organic decay is fed back into the Disciplined Green.
*   **The Singers:** Though the sound of the valley is a deafening roar to an outsider, to the Tenders it is a complex, living machine. Occasionally, a Tender will stop on a broad promenade, lift its flat head toward the vaulted canopy, and open its wide, toothless mouth. It releases a single, booming note—a flat, resonant baritone that strikes the basalt friezes and reverberates down through thousands of stone tiers. This single sound recalibrates the valley. Upon hearing it, millions of frogs momentarily shift the cadence of their croaking; the crickets alter their pitch; the roosting birds answer in sequence. It is how the Tenders measure the structural integrity of the valley—a sonar check across miles of dark masonry to detect a cracked lintel or a blocked aqueduct far above.

Deep within the mountain, in the windowless, vaulted chambers that tunnel beneath the terraces, lie the **Sump-Cradles**. Here, where the acoustic roar of the terraces is muffled into a deep, subterranean thrum, the oldest Tenders go to rest. They do not die in the manner of soft-bodied things; rather, as their hearts slow across centuries, their metabolic heat wanes, and they settle into carved basalt niches that match the contours of their bodies. Over decades, the mineral-rich water dripping from the ceiling encrusts them. They become indistinguishable from the wall—living statues of lime and basalt, breathing once every few hours, waiting for the mountain to shift or the warmth to fade before they finally turn entirely to stone.

In the lowest tiers of the valley—miles down, where the fog settles into a thick, warm soup and the architraves disappear into blackness—live the true ancients: the **Deep Basin Leviathans**. These are vast, legless amphibians, forty feet from blunt snout to flattened tail, that dwell entirely within the grandest, lowest cisterns. They are the original furnace of the valley. Their massive, slow-burning bodies generate immense body heat, warming the deep water that channels through the sub-floor flues and keeps the upper terraces radiating their perpetual, gentle warmth. 

The Tenders descend to these lowest tiers once every few generations. They walk down the endless steps in total darkness, carrying offerings of crushed isopods and sweet, fleshy ferns, laying them at the edge of the steaming cisterns. They pat the slick, armored heads of the Leviathans, feeling the slow, heavy thrum of their hearts through the soles of their feet—a rhythm that matches, precisely, the heartbeat of the mountain itself.

Nothing here is wild, yet nothing is tamed. There are no maps, no books, no written histories carved into the walls, because the history is written in the wear of the steps and the path of the water. The creatures of the valley do not inhabit the architecture; they are simply the soft, wet parts of a giant, stone clock that began ticking before memory, and has no reason to ever stop.
