The life here does not merely occupy the stone; it has taken the measure of every join, channel, and chisel-mark, reshaping its own biology to fit the brutal geometry of the basin.

At the lowest level of the food web is the dark, felt-like crust that clothes the mortar. It is not true moss or plant matter, but a dense, cold-tolerant consortium of iron-oxidizing bacteria and soot-colored micro-algae. It feeds on the trace minerals leached from the basalt and the faint, bitter sulfur carried in the mountain water. Where the ancient builders mixed crushed lime and mineral tar to seal the joints, this film grows thickest, binding with the degrading tar to form a slick, rubbery skin. 

Nested inside this black mortar-skin are thread-like, translucent worms no thicker than a horsehair. Millions of them inhabit the seams between the terracotta tiles. They never emerge fully into the open stream, but keep their heads anchored in the tar while their tail-ends trail out into the water like pale cilia, catching the minute flecks of organic debris blown down through the fog lid. By secreting a dense, sticky mucus that traps fine basalt dust, these tiny stitch-worms inadvertently reinforce the crumbling joints, binding the loose brick-dust back into the seams and preventing the fierce current from stripping the floor bare.

Above them are the fist-sized crustaceans—the primary drivers of the basin’s ceaseless noise. 

Close inspection reveals they are not true crabs or woodlice, but something closer to subterranean isopods, hypertrophied and hardened by generations in the cold flow. Their carapaces are not smooth, but rough and chalky, stained a mottled slate-grey from the basalt and orange-rust from the iron in the clay. They are completely blind; where eyes should be, they bear smooth, armored plates that protect their snouts when they wedge themselves into the drainage slots to escape the current.

Their paddle-legs are tipped with sharp, hooked dactyls that can grip the microscopic pitting of the glazed tiles even in the full force of a sluice-surge. Their two primary pincers are short, heavy, and offset—one broad and flat like a spatula for scraping the algae-film from the smooth faces of the bricks, the other narrow and notched like a pair of stone-pincer pliers, used exclusively for levering loose bits of tar or dead kin out of the seams. 

They are social only out of spatial necessity. When they crowd along the stepped tiers, their frantic feeding creates a constant, aggressive jostling. If one loses its grip and is rolled by the water down the stepped basalt, the others do not react; they simply step into the cleared space, their feet clicking against the terracotta in a continuous, rain-like patter. Their cast-off shells—shed every few months as they outgrow their armor—do not rot. They are tumbled through the runnels, ground against the basalt steps by the current until they are reduced to a fine, white sand that settles into the quiet corners of the floor, polishing the orange tiles to a glass-like sheen.

Preying upon these hard-shelled grazers are the broad-headed, lunged amphibia that hold the drainage wells. 

These creatures—called simply "boomers" if anyone were there to name them—are built for weight and anchoring. Their bodies are thick, cylindrical, and muscle-bound, clad in a loose, rubbery hide that ranges in color from milky white on their undersides to a bruised, slate-violet along their backs. They have no scales and no external gills; their lungs are immense, occupying nearly half their body cavity. When they fill them, their torsos swell until they wedge tightly between the oak timbers of the drain-shafts, allowing them to remain anchored in the throat of a roaring drain without being washed down into the dark sumps below.

Their heads are wide, flat, and wedge-shaped, acting as water-deflectors. Their mouths are lined not with sharp teeth, but with broad, serrated bone-ridges capable of snapping the thick carapaces of the crustaceans with a single, sudden contraction of their jaw muscles. They do not hunt by sight or scent, but by sensation; the delicate, thread-like filaments along their flanks detect the micro-vibrations of crustacean legs tapping against the nearby stone. 

When a boomer strikes, it does so with a mechanical, hydraulic speed, lunging forward just far enough to engulf a passing crustacean before drawing back into the safety of the oak frame. The low, booming call they produce is a function of their massive lungs and throat-sacs, used not only to advertise territory to rivals in neighboring drains, but as a acoustic weapon. A full-chested *thump* sent echoing through the water-filled channels is often enough to stun or dislodge the crustaceans clinging to the stone nearby, sending a shower of pale bodies tumbling straight into the predator’s waiting mouth.

Deep within the dark shafts below the floor, where the oak timbers are rotted to soft, black sponge, lies a older, slower generation of these creatures. 

These ancient matrons have grown too large to fit through the square floor grates. They live permanently in the drown-chambers below, blind, vast, and pale as lard, moving sluggishly through the subterranean silt. They subsist on the dead crustaceans, silt, and smaller amphibia swept down during the massive, gurgling drain-clearings. Attached to the submerged oak posts around them are their egg-masses—clusters of tough, leathery spheres the size of plums, swaying gently in the dark, cold draft that flows through the heart of the earth beneath the basin.

Up above, under the ceiling of grey fog, nothing flies. No birds dip into the hollow; no insects hover over the spray. The air is too cold, too heavy with moisture, and too violently shaken by the acoustic roar of the water. The ecosystem is closed, cold, and self-contained, running like a well-oiled stone machine—a clockwork of shell, flesh, and wet masonry that requires no sunlight, no earth, and no human hand to keep its gears turning.
