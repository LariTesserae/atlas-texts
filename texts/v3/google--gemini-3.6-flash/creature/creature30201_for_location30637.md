The life here does not struggle against the chamber’s impossible scale and deafening hydraulics; it is calibrated to it, shaped like the wood itself by immense internal pressure, dry heat, and spatial distortion.

To understand what lives in this hollow, one must look first at the creases—the narrow, sixty-degree seams where the glowing amber ribs meet at sharp geometric junctions. 

Here, wedged so tightly into the bark that they appear to be raised knots or relief carvings, are the **Vellum-Skins**. The parchment-like creature observed at the threshold is not an anomaly, but one of hundreds that inhabit the upper vaults. They are flat, legless for most of their lengths, ending in six segmented tongs of calcified chitin that bite deep into the heartwood to anchor them against the concussive thud of the water blasts. 

They do not hunt, nor do they possess true eyes. Instead, their entire upper surface is a stretched, semi-translucent membrane sensitive to heat and frequency. When the deep xylem tubes groan beneath the floor, the Vellum-Skins expand like dry bellows, soaking up the high-pressure mist through millions of microscopic pores along their undersides. They are living pressure-relief valves for the vault. When a wall-rib surges with fluid and threatens to split, the creatures gorge on the pressurized vapor, then exhale it in synchronized, violent bursts of cinnamon-scented steam. A traveler watching a far wall might see a dozen of these flat shapes suddenly bloom with white vapor, the sound arriving a second later as a soft, rhythmic *hiss-tuck, hiss-tuck* beneath the roaring engine of the wood.

In the open volume—the ninety percent of empty air crossed by mast-thick tendrils—the fauna must contend with gravity that does not pull toward a center, but toward the nearest surface. 

These mid-air zones belong to the **Skein-Crawlers**. 

They look like tangled knots of bleached cordage or spun glass, the size of a hounds-frame, with long, jointed legs like dried grass stalks. They do not fly. Instead, they drop off the high ribs into the open void, falling "downward" until the gravitational pull of a crossing tendril catches them mid-fall. They land with a dry, clattering scrape against the resinous bark, then sprint along the undersides of the wood with terrifying, jerky speed. 

The Skein-Crawlers are the caretakers of the sharp edges. Where cambium layers split and send out glass-hard, razor-thin shoots, the Crawlers gather. With mandibles shaped like heavy shears, they snip away the runaway growths before they can pierce the glowing amber light-veins. They eat the freshly sheared cellulose, digesting the raw starch and regurgitating it as a fine, yellow paste that they smear into the cracks around their nesting folds. This paste dries into a substance as hard as horn, insulating the chamber's sensitive inner conductive layers from the violent mist blasts.

And then there are the makers of the knot.

The bone wedge and the gourd are not the relics of a lost expedition, nor are they human in any ordinary sense. They belong to the **Tappers**—a tiny, fragmented tribe of hairless, long-limbed wanderers who squeezed down through the hemp seams centuries ago and whose bodies have slowly conformed to the kiln-like ecology of the deep roots.

A Tapper moves through the geometry without looking at their feet. Because space here folds back upon itself—where a straight forty-foot walk can turn three right angles—their eyes have lost the ability to focus on far horizons. Their pupils are vertical slits, veiled by clear, amber-tinted membranes that protect them from the constant, unblinking glare of the walls and the blinding spray of the water spheres. 

Their skin is dry, pale, and feels like pressed linen, continuously rubbed with the lemon-smelling resin to prevent the hot air from leeching the moisture out of their flesh. They wear no woven cloth; their clothes are single sheets of shed Vellum-Skin wrapped around their waists and limbs, bound with the same dried leaf-skin fiber tied at the gourd.

The Tappers do not build shelters—there is no stone to lay, no dirt to dig, and to cut into the wood too deeply would unleash a jet of hydraulic sap capable of stripping flesh from bone. Instead, they sleep wedged inside the empty, dried-out channels of dead xylem tubes, their bodies suspended in the warm, roaring vibration of the tree’s pulse.

They live on the sap they tap. The clear, viscous liquid caught in the gourd is rich in starches, lipids, and complex plant sugars refined by miles of biological filtration in the upper strata. A single cup of the resinous fluid sustains a Tapper for days, though it stains their teeth black and causes their breath to smell of crushed needles and wet ozone.

Communication among the Tappers is silent. In a chamber where the noise of expanding shoots and rushing fluid drowns out vocal sound, they communicate by striking the wood with heavy, river-smoothed stones—like the one trapped in the rootlets near the bone wedge. They send sharp, high-pitched vibrations through the heartwood, signals that travel for hundreds of yards through the continuous organism. A Tapper resting two ribs away feels the vibration in their ribs: a warning that a mist-pore overhead is about to burst, or that a new spatial fold has opened near the lower floor, altering the path to the upper hemp seams.

They view the chamber not as a cave, but as the interior of a vast, slow-moving beast that permits their presence so long as they remain small, silent, and useful. By driving their bone wedges into over-pressurized veins, they relieve localized fluid buildup, preventing the wood from tearing itself apart during its violent, hourly growth spurts. They are parasites that have learned the rhythm of the host's pulse.

When a Tapper dies, they are not buried—for there is no soil. Their companions place the body into one of the newly opening cambium splits high in the ceiling. Within hours, the pale, razor-sharp wood wraps around the corpse, its pale fibers reaching out like threads of silk to absorb the moisture and minerals, binding the bone and skin directly into the glowing scaffold of the vault. Within a week, nothing remains but a slight, smooth bulge in the amber rib, glowing as brightly as the rest.
