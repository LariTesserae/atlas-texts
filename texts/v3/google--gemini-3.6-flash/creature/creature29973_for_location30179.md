Where there is no breath, there is still displacement. Where there are no eyes, there is still the cold, tactile orientation toward heat.

The things that inhabit the Lower Sump do not walk upon the gantries so much as they are suspended from them, grown into them, or driven along their rails by the very pressures that keep the trench alive.

Closest to the raw basalt face move the **Solder-Glands**. They are not creatures of flesh, nor are they true machines in any sense that implies a blueprint or a builder. They are heavy, brass-bound hulls—some the size of a iron hearth, others stretched thin as rain-gutters—that cling to the vertical rock on webbed, iron-shod runners. They move without eyes or faces, steered entirely by the thermal gradients of the rock face. Where the basalt is ice-cold and sweating condensate, a Solder-Gland hangs motionless, its lower belly dipped into the lead-lined troughs. Where the rock fractures from heat, the hull shifts, gliding with a heavy, ratcheted *clack-clack-clack* along a fixed bronze rack. 

Their interiors do not hold blood or lungs; they are packed with dense, compressed blocks of mineral tallow, graphite powder, and soft, recycled lead. As they creep across the damp seams where rock meets iron, they leave behind a thick, smooth bead of dark grease that seals the basalt from the steam, keeping the raw stone from shattering under the dry heat. They feed on nothing; they are powered by the expansion and contraction of their own internal liquid-mercury bladders, which swell in the hot air of the lower gantries and sink in the cold drips near the rock face, driving a slow, endless mechanical pendulum within their bellies.

Higher up, within the dense lattice of zinc shanties, the space is occupied by the **Stills**.

The brick and corrugated-iron huts stacked four levels high are not homes; they are housings for heavy, slow-pulsing organs of copper and lead. Inside a typical shanty, behind a door bolted shut against the sweltering draft, hangs a **Tallow-Core**. It is a soft, cylindrical mass the size of a mature tree trunk, suspended from the roof beam by heavy iron chains. It is composed of layers of rendered industrial fat, sulfur, and coarse canvas, saturated with condensed steam and copper salts from the pipes above. 

The Core does not sleep, but it responds to the environment. It sways gently on its chain as the steam-relief valves blow overhead. As it absorbs the moisture and sulfur-yellow lichen spores floating in the heat shimmer, it slowly swells, dragging down a counterweighted lever arm. When the arm reaches the floor, it trips a heavy brass latch. A gallon of warm, mineral-thickened water cascades from a catch-basin into a lower boiler line with a sharp, echoing *whumpf*, and the Core, lightened, rises back toward the ceiling to begin the cycle again. 

The shanties are full of these blind, heavy balance-systems. They fill the dark interiors with a sound like slow, wet breathing—the stretch of wet canvas, the creak of grease-soaked rope, the heavy drip of oil into catch-pans.

In the narrowest spaces—inside the bronze conduits and along the undersides of the sweat-slicked floor-plates—live the **Scourers**. 

Where the pale, thread-like liverwort spreads its cream-colored mats across the damp iron, the Scourers creep in slow, rhythmic pulses. They appear as flat, overlapping scales of sheet-lead and tarnished copper wire, no thicker than a thumb, linked together like flexible shingles. They have no heads, no limbs, and no forward or backward; they are driven entirely by localized chemical reactions between their lead edges and the mineral-rich sweat dripping from the basalt. 

Where the liverwort grows thick, the moisture becomes acidic. This acid reacts with the lead plates of the Scourer, causing the metallic scales to curl, step forward, and flatten again. As they drag themselves across the wet ironwork, their ragged underbellies scrape away the excess moss and scale rust, keeping the bronze conduits clear and the drain-troughs open. Behind them, they leave a shiny, polished track of bare metal that immediately begins to sweat again under the ambient heat.

And deeper down, where the trench floor narrows to a slit barely wide enough for a heavy pipe, rest the **Hydropumps**. 

They are the largest inhabitants of Fracture-Nine, though they are entirely motionless save for their internal viscera. They are huge, pig-iron cylinders riveted directly into the dark basalt, coated in a thick, velvety fur of sulfur-yellow lichen. They do not have minds, but they have a distinct, heavy pulse. They draw the ice-cold condensation that trickles down the raw rock face into their deep, dark chambers. Inside, where the dry, sweltering air bakes their upper iron domes, the trapped water flashes into high-pressure steam. 

With a deep, resonant *thrum-thrum-thrum* that vibrates through the gantries, the Hydropumps force the steam back up into the multi-tiered city, feeding the upper headers and keeping the cycle alive.

Nothing here seeks to escape the heat; nothing remembers the open sky or the light of a sun. The inhabitants are made of the same iron, tallow, basalt, and steam as the trench itself. They are the Sump’s internal organs, blind and ceaseless, maintaining the pressure, scraping the rock, sealing the seams, and turning water back into heat in the hot, dark silence of the trench.
