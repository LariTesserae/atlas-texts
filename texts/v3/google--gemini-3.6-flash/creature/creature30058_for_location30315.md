In this valley, life is not an occupant of the stone; it is a dense, wet weather system generated by flesh.

The primary engine of the floor is the herd. These slate-grey ungulates—broad-backed and squat, built low to the center of gravity like wet granite boulders—do not possess the nervous skittishness of open-plain grazers. In a world without sudden shadows or sun-glare, their eyes have flattened into wide, milky horizontal slits that see only the shift of violet-grey light against wet basalt. They are blind to detail, but hyper-attuned to weight and vibration. 

Their hides are an inch thick, saturated with a dense, rank tallow that smells of turned earth and cold fat. The continuous drip of the ceiling does not soak them; the heavy beads of moisture gather on the guard-hairs and roll off in steady rivulets, leaving a pale ring of water around each beast as it stands motionless. Their horns do not point upward toward an open sky, but curve tightly downward and forward, flattened at the tips like heavy iron spades. With these, they spend hours in a state of slow, rhythmic labor, chipping the rubbery, pale lichen from the lips of basalt cracks. The sound of their feeding is a wet, mechanical scraping, accompanied by the deep, internal sloshing of their three-chambered stomachs. When they lie down to ruminate, they wedge their bellies into the gravel troughs, their collective body heat creating localized blooms of forty-eight-degree air that rise off their flanks like steam from a kettle.

Higher up, where the basalt faces are sheer and slick with condensate, the cave-fowl live in a state of perpetual, screaming density. 

They are birds reduced to muscle, grease, and bone. Their plumage is not soft; the feathers are flat, fused, and waxy, closer to the scales of a cold-water fish than the down of an eagle. Unwashed by rain or dried by wind, their bodies are caked in a greyish-white paste of their own dung and dried pool-algae. They do not fly so much as launch themselves into the thermal updrafts created by the thousands of cattle below, tumbling through the darkness on short, rigid wings to drop onto lower ledges or plunge into the cold pools for shrimp. 

Because light gives them almost nothing, their faces are dominated by massive, slit-like nostrils and broad, horn-hard beaks lined with backward-facing serrations. Their roar—that deafening, multi-throated rasp—is not a song or a territorial warning. It is an acoustic mapping. Each bird cries out continuously into the gloom, listening with delicate, featherless ear-pockets for the exact micro-second it takes for the rasp to bounce off the opposite basalt wall, measuring the distance to the ledge, the cliff face, or the rising back of a bull below.

Between these two great populations exists an entire ecosystem of dark, silent scavengers that live off the waste of the noise.

In the shadows under the cattle's legs, tiny, long-bodied quadrupeds move with the fluid, silent speed of wet snakes. Their fur is short, greasy, and jet-black, slicked down against their ribs so that they can squeeze through the narrowest basalt seams. They have no eyes at all—only patches of pale, skin-covered heat-receptors along their snouts. They feed on the afterbirth of the ungulates, the eggs that tumble from the high ledges, and the crushed chitin of the heavy beetles. They do not cry out; to make a sound in this valley is to invite the blind strike of a wader’s bill. Instead, they communicate through a complex language of musk, painting the wet stone with secretions that smell like wet copper and iron.

In the gravel pools, the long-legged waders stand like dead sticks. Their legs are translucent white, so thin that the dark fluid of their blood can be seen pulsing through the bone-seams under the ambient violet glow. They do not move until a pulse in the water tells them a shrimp has broken the surface tension. Then, their long, needle-thin beaks strike down without a splash, pinning the tiny, glass-like crustaceans against the gravel before swallowing them whole.

And then there are the transients—the knapper-hunters who descend from the dry seams miles above.

They are not creatures of the valley, but parasites upon its edges. They come down only when the grease-supplies above are depleted, moving in small, silent groups of three or four. Their ears are plugged with tallow and soft bird-down to protect their drums from the violent vibration of the cave-fowl’s roar, which can cause a man’s lungs to ache after a single hour. 

They walk with a strange, flat-footed gait, placing their weight carefully on the razored talus to avoid the metallic *clink* of stone on stone that would send a thousand slate-grey cattle stampeding through the narrow troughs. Their skin is smeared with ungulate fat to keep the forty-four-degree damp from drawing the life out of their limbs. 

They do not use fire down here; smoke has nowhere to go in the heavy mist, and wood is a memory from the surface world. Instead, they work in the violet dark by touch and muscle. They isolate an old bull or a fallen chick, drive heavy chert wedges into its throat or spine, and butcher it where it falls, using the fresh, glass-sharp flakes they strike from the valley’s own black floor. 

They work fast, their hands numb and red with cold blood. They take only the high-fat tongue, the marrow-dense limb bones, and the thickest sections of hide to twist into ropes or cold-weather capes. They do not speak. They communicate by slapping their thighs or pressing their fingers against one another’s forearms. When their packs are heavy and slick with fat, they climb back into the dark seams, leaving their broken tools and cracked bones behind to be washed over by the perpetual *plink-plink* of the sweating ceiling.

Behind them, the valley swallows the intrusion immediately. The dark-furred quadrupeds swarm the grease-stained stone where the kill was made; the beetles crawl over the discarded chert edges, licking the fat from the basalt; and the deafening, timeless roar of the roosting fowl rolls over the floor, smoothing out every sound until only the stone, the wet breath, and the cold remain.
