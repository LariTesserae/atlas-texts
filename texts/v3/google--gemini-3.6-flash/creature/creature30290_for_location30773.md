They do not stand. To stand here would be to shatter the delicate, paper-thin rims of the stone dishes, or to slip on the slick, algae-free calcite and break a thigh against the basalt below. 

Instead, they fit themselves to the curves of the basin, resting their weight entirely within the water. 

They are small, long-bodied beings, rarely longer than an arm’s length, with skin the color of wet lard or the pale undersides of river stones. In the dim, grey gloom, they look at first glance like larger, heavier cousins of the legless salamanders that slip between the pools—smooth, earless, and hairless, their contours blurred by the thin sheet of warm water that constantly washes over their backs. 

When they are at rest, which is most of the time, they are almost indistinguishable from the travertine bosses themselves. A creature will settle into a shallow dish, curled along its inner rim, its chin resting on the lip of stone where the warm water spills over. Its pulse is so slow that its chest rises only twice or thrice in a hundred heartbeats, a soft, barely visible expansion that creates no more ripple than a single falling drop. Over months of stillness, a faint, dusty rim of white carbonate begins to form along the curve of its flanks, anchoring it gently to the stone, until a sudden, deliberate shift of its body snaps the chalky bond with the sound of a dry twig breaking.

Their faces are flat and featureless but for a pair of wide, pale nostrils and a mouth that is little more than a horizontal seam. They have no eyes; where eyes might once have been, the skin is thick, smooth, and crisscrossed by a dense network of pale, raised veins that are intensely sensitive to heat and vibration. They do not look at the grey gloom above. They feel the basin instead. They know the depth of every dish to the thickness of a fingernail by the slight variation in warmth between the bottom silt and the spilling surface. They can feel the impact of a single drop of water falling three paces away, distinguishing the sharp *tink* of water landing on stone from the soft *plop* of water landing on water.

Their forelimbs are short and surprisingly dense, thick-boned and heavy, built not for swimming or running, but for leverage against wet, unyielding rock. Their hands are their pride and their only complex organ: broad palms ending in four long, double-jointed fingers. The undersides of these fingers are covered in thousands of tiny, soft papillae that grip slick stone like wet leather, and their fingernails are thick, blunt spades of overgrown keratin, stained reddish-tan by the iron in the siltstone.

It is these hands that wedged the slate into the wall. It is these nails, hardened by a lifetime of mineralized water, that scraped the soft limestone away to shape the double-clasped bowl near the back wall. 

They do not work with urgency. A creature might spend forty years leaning against a particular fissure, using the edge of a broken river stone or its own heavy nails to coax a tiny seep of water an inch to the left, ensuring that a lower basin receives its precise, quiet share of runoff. They work at the speed of the stone itself, carving, damming, and nudging the warm flow so that no pool ever runs dry, no rim ever collapses, and no quiet corner turns cold.

They live on the minuscule, blind life that shares their water. With infinite, endless patience, a creature will lie submerged in a catch-pit, its arms resting limp over the edges, its pale palms turned upward and open. Its skin secretes a faint, sweetish mucus that attracts the rice-grained crustaceans drifting through the dark. When enough of the tiny bodies are crawling across its skin, the creature’s fingers close—not with a jerk, but with a slow, inescapable fold, sweeping the grains into its mouth before settling back into the lukewarm stillness.

Communication among them is rare, stripped of all sound, for voices would be lost in the continuous, low-frequency sigh of the weeping wall. Instead, they speak through the water. A creature will rest its heavy chin against a rimstone lip and strike the rock with a single, blunt fingernail. *Tink.* The vibration travels through the dense, water-saturated stone, passing through thirty feet of bedrock to be felt in the ribs of another creature resting three terraces below. A single tap might mean a block in the upper seep; two slow taps might mean that an old one has ceased to breathe, its body remaining in its dish until the mineral water slowly coats it, smoothing its edges, turning it gradually into just another rounded boss of pale travertine.

The basalt hammerstone lying anchored in the shallow pool is not lost. It belongs to an old one who rests in the deeper dish just behind it. The old one’s skin has turned greyish-tan, matching the siltstone wall, and its joints have grown thick and stiff with mineral crust. It has not moved its legs in a decade, but every few days, as the twilight gloom remains unchanged above, its long, pale fingers creep out from the water, feeling the cool, rounded nose of the hammerstone, checking the thickness of the calcite lip that binds the tool to the earth. 

It is waiting for the stone to finish holding the tool, or perhaps waiting for the water to dissolve the bond away. It does not matter which happens first. The water keeps weeping. The air remains as warm as blood. And in the low, crowded basin, nothing is ever forgotten, because nothing ever leaves.
