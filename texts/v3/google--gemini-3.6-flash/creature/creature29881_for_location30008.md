They move through the wide, carved avenues like splinters of dried bone caught in a draft—slow, deliberate, and entirely unhurried by the thunderous roar that fills the dome.

To look upon one of the basin’s dwellers is to understand what happens to life when it is stripped of all fluid and left to cure under amber light for centuries. 

They are slender, tall beings, their forms built around skeletons of lightweight, porous calcium—less like dense mammalian bone and more like the hard, pitted coral of an ancient sea floor. Their flesh is not soft; it is a tough, desiccated leather, pale as the marl they walk upon, dusted constantly with the fine, sulfurous powder of the mosses. They do not bleed. If pierced, their wounds shed only a pale, dry pith that quickly calcifies in the heat, sealing the gap like dried mortar.

Their heads are elongated and hairless, crowned with thick, ridged crests of hardened keratin that curve backward over their napes. These crests are not ornamental; they act as acoustic dampeners, breaking the violent sound waves that bounce perpetually off the selenite roof. Where human eyes would wither under the incandescent glare, their eyes are protected by dual-layered eyelids: a outer shade of smoky, translucent horn that slides down like a welder’s glass, and beneath it, an iris of polished, obsidian-black mineral that reflects the amber sun as two tiny, pin-point fires.

They do not have ears—at least not openings that could be torn apart by the deafening friction of the glass-cacti. Instead, their hearing is entirely osteopathic. Heavy, fused plates of bone on either side of their jaws rest directly against their collarbones. They "hear" the world through the soles of their feet and the framework of their ribs, feeling the metallic scream of the vegetation as a violent, rhythmic shivering in their marrow.

Because vocalizing into the acoustic storm is a useless effort, their language is one of touch and impact. When two dwellers meet in the shadow of a three-story tenement, they do not shout. They press the flat surfaces of their horn-crests together, letting their voices—low, rasping vibrations generated in dry thoracic chambers—pass directly from skull to skull. To an outsider, they would appear to be gently knocking their heads together in a silent, solemn dance. For longer distances, they carry thin rods of bronze, striking the carved chalk walls of the city in complex, telegraphic sequences; the sound travels through the soft marl substrate faster and clearer than it ever could through the screaming air.

Their clothing is heavy, structural, and beautiful in its austerity. They wear layered coats stitched from the silvered parchment leaves of the glass-cacti, bound with cords spun from reed-pith. These garments do not drape; they stand out from the body in rigid, folded pleats designed, like the plants themselves, to radiate heat away from the skin. Around their shoulders they drape thick, felted cowls of sulfur-moss—dyed a deep, mustard-yellow—which muffle the tooth-rattling vibration of the air around their necks and chests.

There are perhaps no more than three hundred of them left in a city carved to shelter fifty thousand. 

They are not a dying race in the traditional sense, for they are almost absurdly long-lived. In this sterile chamber, where no bacterium can survive and no rot exists, aging is merely a process of gradual mineralization. A young dweller is nimble, their parchment-skin pale and slightly flexible. As the centuries pass, their tissues absorb the heavy nitrogen dust and ambient calcium, turning increasingly rigid. 

When a dweller finally reaches the end of their life, they do not fall ill. They simply stop moving. They sit down upon a carved step in one of the vast, empty plazas, lower their horn eyelids, and let the light bake them through. Over decades, the heat and dust turn them into solid chalk statues—identical in texture to the city itself. The living do not bury these ancestors; they simply carve their names into the pedestal of the stoop where they sat, leaving them to stand as permanent, white sentinels along the quiet boulevards.

Their daily existence is bound to the clockwork geometry of the selenite dome. 

As the burning focal point of light sweeps over a terrace, a handful of dwellers emerge from the cool, deep cellars beneath the tenements. They work with an astonishing, quiet efficiency. Armed with curved bronze sickles worn thin as butter knives, they leap into the frenzied, clicking growth of the glass-cacti, harvesting the silver vanes before they can stiffen into brittle glass. They sweep the heavy drifts of nitrogen dust out of the stone aqueducts with stiff-bristled brooms, collecting it in deep wicker hampers.

In the public squares, where the pavement stones are hot enough to scorch wood, women—or the dry, genderless equivalents of their kind—knead the dust with the sweet, sticky sap squeezed from pith-reeds. They flatten the gray dough into thin, round wafers directly onto the chalk flags. Within forty seconds, the dough bubbles, hardens, and snaps—baked into a crisp, nutrient-dense biscuit that tastes of salt, sulfur, and parched earth.

They have no need for furniture. A bed in this city is merely a shallow, polished trough carved into the marl floor of a high apartment, where the air is driest. They sleep standing up or reclining slightly, resting their heavy bone crests against carved niches, completely indifferent to the roaring scream of the forest outside their windows.

Why they built a city so much larger than their numbers remains written only in the soft, worn reliefs that line the aqueducts. The carvings show a time when the salt-crust above was thin, when rain still trickled through cracks in the roof to be caught in deep cisterns, and when these people were softer, damp things who sang with open mouths. But the water dried, the salt sealed the vault, the selenite fused into a solid lens, and the world outside became a distant, forgotten memory.

They did not flee. They simply adapted, shrinking their numbers as the water vanished, hardening their skin, shedding their ears, and letting the light remold them into something as permanent and dry as the basin itself. 

Now, they are the quiet caretakers of a monument built for ancestors who were vastly different from themselves. They sweep streets that a thousand feet will never tread. They weave tapestries for halls that host only the golden spore-storms. And as the great lens above turns the day into a blinding, shadowless eternity, they move through the grandeur of their bone-white city, deaf to the screaming world, perfectly content in the radiant, baking silence of their own bones.
