To understand what lives here, one must first discard the idea of an interior and an exterior. In a precinct where a three-inch gap can accommodate a walking body and six steps down lead forty feet up, life does not occupy space; it is woven into the folds of it.

The most numerous dwellers are not immediately recognizable as organisms. They appear first as dark, hairline fractures running along the razor-sharp lintels and narrow door-jambs. These are the *threads*—slender, segmented creatures no thicker than a violin string and several feet in length, made of a dark, semi-flexible chitin that matches the cold flint-iron of the buildings. They do not possess eyes or limbs. Instead, they move by magnetic or spatial peristalsis, slipping along the zero-tolerance seams where two walls meet. 

They feed on the localized thermal leakage—the minuscule heat generated when the facades slide past one another. When a building shifts its orientation by a fraction of a degree, the threads gather at the point of friction, drinking the silent, cold energy of the stone. To see one is rare; usually, you see only their shed skins—hollow, glassy ribbons that do not rot, hanging suspended in mid-air at the spatial dislocations where two mismatched air-currents cancel each other out.

***

Higher up, within the impossible volumes of the high-rise tenements, live the primary occupants. They have no name for themselves, for sound carries poorly here and echo does not exist, making speech an inefficient, muted effort. 

They are tall, dry-bodied beings, standing nearly seven feet high, though their mass is deceptively small. Viewed head-on, an occupant appears to possess a normal, if elongated, physical volume: narrow shoulders, long, spindle-like limbs terminating in three un-jointed fingers, and a torso as smooth and hairless as carved slate. However, if an occupant turns sideways, they do not merely present a profile—they nearly vanish. Their bodies are lamellar, composed of hundreds of hair-thin, dense leaves of dark, organic matter compressed tightly together like the pages of a closed book.

Their skin is the same matte, unreflective gray as the slate-like lichen on the lower steps. It is entirely dry, completely devoid of pores or oils, and so cold to the touch that moisture from the thin air instantly freezes into microscopic, geometric frost-patterns upon contact with their limbs.

They have no faces in the conventional sense. The front of the cranium is a flat, vertical plane of dark, polished horn, smooth as a river stone. Across this plane run three horizontal, thread-like fissures that open and close in slow, rhythmic pulses. These fissures do not admit light or air; they are pressure-sensors, tuned entirely to the sub-audible hum emitted by the compressed architecture. Through these slits, the occupants "see" the precinct not as shapes or colors, but as a complex map of structural tension and spatial depth. They know where an alley opens into a vast interior room because they can feel the shift in the wall's vibrational pitch.

***

Because space within the precinct is non-linear, the occupants do not live in "families" or "neighborhoods" defined by distance. Two occupants sitting on opposite sides of the sunken basalt trough may actually be sharing the same living space, separated by only a single spatial fold that exists between them.

An occupant will enter a doorway three inches wide not by squeezing, but by stepping through it normally. As their foot crosses the threshold, their lamellar body fans out along a hidden spatial axis—unfolding like a paper lantern into an apartment that, from the outside, has no volume at all. Inside these rooms, there are no chairs, no tables, no lamps. The pale daylight filters through the dark, dense walls as if the stone itself were slightly translucent to certain spectrums, casting a dim, shadowless illumination across the interior surfaces.

The occupants spend days—sometimes weeks—in absolute stillness. They stand or lean against the interior walls, their three-fingered hands pressed flat against the cold material, "listening" to the slow, tectonic adjustments of the city. 

They do not sleep, nor do they eat solid matter. Their nourishment comes from the lateral condensation that creeps across the dark stone. Once or twice every few days, an occupant will step out onto a high balcony—one that exists only when viewed from below—and hold out a small, shallow dish made of the same dark flint-iron. They gather the flat, perfectly round water droplets as they wander sideways across the walls, sweeping them up before they can vaporize. The liquid is mineral-heavy, dense, and unnaturally cold; drinking it is a slow, solemn act that takes hours, as the liquid is absorbed directly into the lamellar layers of their throats.

***

Reproduction among them is as slow and silent as the sliding of the buildings. It is not an act of union, but of geometric necessity. 

When a facade completes a long, glacial shift—when a corridor that has been three feet wide for a century suddenly compresses into a single, razor-sharp seam—any occupant caught within that space is not crushed. The stone is too perfect, the spatial physics too absolute. Instead, the occupant’s lamellar body is pressed flat between the converging planes of reality. 

Over the course of several months, while the wall sits in its new position, the compressed occupant undergoes a quiet division. Their mass splits along its thin, paper-like layers. When the wall eventually shifts back, sliding open with the quiet rasp of grinding ice, two occupants step out from the seam where there was previously only one. Both are fully grown, identical in their dry, slate-gray stillness, their facial fissures pulsing in unison as they step onto the un-weathered stone.

***

There is no violence here, no decay, and no garbage. Nothing breaks. A fallen utensil or a shed strip of chitin does not tumble into a gutter; it slides along the localized gravity gradients until it slips into one of the two-dimensional cracks between the paving stones, swallowed back into the precinct’s dense, un-namable interior mechanics.

If you were to stand in the center of the sunken square and watch very closely, you might see an occupant emerge from a third-story window that has no floor beneath it. They will step out onto the empty air, their foot finding an invisible, folded ledge that connects the window to a roof garden forty feet away. They will walk across the void with a slow, measured cadence, their shadow failing to fall on the ground below, their long, pale-gray fingers trailing along the sharp, un-chipped edge of a wall that was built before geography began, and will remain un-softened when it ends.
