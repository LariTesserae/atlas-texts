In the frost-fractured deeps of the rift, life does not fight the mechanical violence of the engines; it feeds upon it. 

Where the heavy iron bolts bite into the heartwood, a parasitic, iron-eating fungi called *gasket-crust* spreads in pale, thick shelf-layers. It looks like dirty tallow or cold lard, rubbery to the touch and inches thick. These fungi do not seek sunlight or air; they grow precisely at the points of greatest physical stress—where the roots snap under the weight of the cliffs, or where the iron conduits grind against the timber as the subterranean vault shifts. The continuous, bone-deep thrum of the pumps acts as a catalyst for them, shaking mineral-rich sap and microscopic iron rust out of the grain and into their porous undersides. If you pull a shelf of gasket-crust from the bark, it does not bleed water, but a thick, amber fluid that smells intensely of resin, grease, and crushed needles, freezing into a glass-like shell within seconds of exposure.

Near the base of these fungal shelves, where the sodium lamps cast their dim, mustard-colored halos, move the low-runners. 

They are the heavy, pale-furred animals glimpsed along the root-bridges—creatures the size of hogs, but built low and broad like iron wedges. Their coats are not soft; they are woven of dense, hollow guard-hairs that have become matted with frozen sap and coal-dust, forming an icy, flexible armor over their shoulders. They possess no visible external ears. Generations in the acoustic storm of the basin have stripped them of delicate hearing; their auditory canals are sealed with thick plugs of grey cartilage. 

Instead, the low-runners navigate entirely through bone-conduction. Their forepaws are massive, spade-like, tipped with blunt, spade-shaped claws designed to notch into the hard, freezing timber. Their heavy lower jaws, flat and hairless on the underside, rest almost continuously against the wood as they walk. Through their mandibles and ribs, they "read" the engines. They know the geography of the rift not by sight—their eyes are milky, tiny beads rimmed with pink skin—but by the acoustic signature of the floor. They can feel the difference between the high, thin screech of a binding flywheel three miles west and the slow, heavy *chunk-thud* of a main piston sinking into its cylinder below the basin floor.

The low-runners move with a slow, hypnotic rhythm, picking through the frozen needle-mats. They use their hardened snouts to pry open stress-cracks in the pale trunks, digging out the rubbery gasket-crust and the dormant, wax-coated grubs that burrow into the dead heartwood near the boiler houses. They do not hurry. Their hearts beat only three or four times a minute, their breath venting from their nostrils in small, dry puffs of grey steam that drop straight to the wooden deck like lead shot.

In the black, slushy pools trapped within the coiled root-bowls, an entirely different, nearly motionless life exists. 

These basins of stagnant condensation are thick with soot, iron filings, and dropped pine needles, covered in a slush so cold it slush-crystallizes around anything dipped into it. Living beneath this dark slurry are *tallow-eels*—thick, arm-length amphibians with smooth, pale-grey skin that feels like wet slate. They have no limbs, only broad, muscular tails and broad, underslung mouths lined with rasps of chitin. They lie frozen in the slush for months at a time, their metabolisms paused, until the heat of a distant geothermal vent or a rare flare of hot water ejected by a steam-trap raises the pool’s temperature by a single degree. Then, they stir sluggishly, grazing on the oily film that gathers on the surface of the water and stripping the mineral scale from the submerged iron footings.

Up above, in the pitch-black canopy where the boughs interlock like the ribs of a sunken fleet, there are long, hanging ribbons of *gantry-moss*. It is not true moss, but a fibrous, pale-blue lichen that clings to the rusted steel lattice of the abandoned cranes and electrical cables. It absorbs the misty condensation that rises from the lower depths and traps the fine, soot-laden dust blown out of the engine vents. 

Nesting within these hanging mats are small, solitary creatures known to the few who work the rift as *clinker-flies*. They are not flies at all, but dense, hard-shelled arachnids the size of a man’s palm, covered in a coat of silver-grey down that keeps them from freezing solid. They are entirely blind and completely indifferent to the deafening acoustic roar. They spend their years clinging to the warm undersides of the sodium lamp fixtures and the insulated steam pipes, moving only when the lamps flicker or die. When a sodium bulb finally burns out, the clinker-flies on that gantry cluster around the darkening glass, drinking the last traces of radiated heat before falling into a deep, frozen torpor that can last for decades.

And then, very rarely, there are the human tenders.

They do not live in towns or camps, but in small, egg-shaped shanties made of riveted boiler-plate, bolted directly into the sheltered crotches of the giant trunks, usually beneath the overhang of a high gantry. These shanties are insulated with layers of salted moss and thick wool felt, silent inside only because their double walls are packed with packed pine shavings that deaden the low-frequency thrum to a dull, stomach-churning pulse.

The tenders are few—perhaps a dozen across the entire multi-mile span of the rift. They are men and women who came down decades ago on the supply hoists and simply never went back up. They are stone-deaf, their ears long since ruined by the acoustics of the basin, and they communicate with one another—when they meet at all—through a precise language of heavy hand-gestures and chalk markings scrawled across the iron boiler plates.

They wear heavy suits of grease-soaked canvas, lined with tallow-rubbed deer hide and topped with high, felt-lined hoods. Their boots are studded with iron spikes that bite into the frozen timber floor with a sharp, double-click. Their work is singular and mindless: they are the oilers and grease-monkeys of a forgotten system. Armed with long-spouted bronze oilcans heated over charcoal braziers, they walk the lonely, miles-long gantries, climbing frozen ladders to pour lard and heavy mineral oil into the grease-cups of flywheels that have been turning since their grandfathers’ time.

They do not know who built the engines, nor do they care what the pumps are drawing up from the deeper dark below the wood. They live on dried moss-tubers harvested from the root-cracks, salted low-runner tail-meat, and tea brewed from cold, bitter pine needle shavings. They move with the same glacial patience as the environment around them. To see an oiler step out from behind a hundred-foot-wide trunk into the amber light of a sodium lamp is like watching a piece of the ironwork itself come detached. They do not look up at the dark canopy; they do not look out across the shadowy, endless expanse of pale timber. They keep their eyes down, watching for black ice on the root-edges, their hands tucked deep into their grease-stained sleeves, listening to the world through the soles of their thick leather boots.
