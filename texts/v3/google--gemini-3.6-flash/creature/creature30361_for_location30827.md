To live here is to be thick-limbed, hairless, and oily. 

The inhabitants of the basin—the builders of the basalt hive—are short, dense beings whose bodies seem cast from the same heavy sludge as the city floor. They have no body hair; hair would only trap the sulfurous condensate and rot in the perpetual steam. Instead, their skin is a dark, rubbery grey, slicked with a natural sebum so rich that warm mineral water rolls off their shoulders in clear, beaded streams. 

They walk with a wide, low-slung gate, their toes long, broad, and prehensile. The soles of their feet are not merely calloused; they are capped in thick, keratinous pads—almost like the dark horn of a hooved beast—allowing them to run across knife-sharp obsidian terraces without flinching or bleeding. Their hands are oversized, the knuckles bulbous from generations of knapping flint and hauling slate, the finger-pads worn flat and scarred by raw mineral crusts.

Because the air is dim and choked with steam, their eyes have adapted not for distance, but for contrast and proximity. They are small, pale-yellow, and set deep beneath heavy, bony brows that divert trickling sweat and condensate away from the sockets. A translucent third eyelid—a nictitating membrane—flashes horizontally across the eye every few moments, wiping away iron dust and acrid sulfur mist. They do not stare into the distance; there is no distance to see. They look at what is within reach of their hands.

In an environment that never ceases its deafening roar—where steam vents hiss and a hundred thousand stone hammers strike in uncoordinated fury—verbal speech as soft-fleshed beings know it is impossible. Shouting would only shred the larynx against the acoustic wall of the valley. 

Instead, they communicate through a complex, tactile, and vibrational language. 

When two inhabitants speak, they lean close, pressing their foreheads together or placing their wide, calloused palms against each other’s chests, sending deep, sub-audible rumbles through their thick ribcages. For longer distances across a workshop floor or down the narrow stone tenement steps, they use the city itself as a sounding board. They wear heavy bone rings on their thumbs, with which they strike the basalt walls in sharp, rhythmic sequences. The solid rock carries the crackle of these percussion-words far clearer than the steam-heavy air ever could. A stone-cutter three stories up feels the summons in the soles of his feet and knows to lower his basket of split slate.

Their clothing is minimal, designed solely to protect the vital organs from flying stone splinters and the scalding spray of the water pans. They wear heavy, overlapping aprons made from the thick, salt-cured hides of the deep-water bay-beasts, fastened around the waist and shoulders with polished bone spikes instead of thread. These aprons are blackened with render-tallow and stiff with dried iron silt. 

Nothing is wasted, because nothing arrives from outside the basin. Their relationship with the local fauna is intimate, bloody, and seamless:

*   **The Slime-Mappers:** Young adults, nimble and light, wade waist-deep into the scalding runoff channels, scraping the pale slime-mats from the rocks with broad scoops made of bay-beast shoulder blades. This slime, dense with chemosynthetic microbes, is their primary staple—boiled down into a thick, grey paste that tastes heavily of zinc and salt.
*   **The Shell-Crackers:** Elders, their backs bent like vaulted stone, sit in the open courtyards surrounded by heaps of the shield-boss mollusks. With heavy river-cobbles, they rhythmically crush the armored shells, separating the tough, purple flesh for salting while grinding the crushed shells into a white powder. This powder is mixed with volcanic ash and silt to create the binding mortar that cements their tenement stacks against the tremors of the earth.
*   **The Mud-Herders:** In the deep, sunken pans where the giant mud-beasts wallow, men and women stand on narrow basalt catwalks, wielding long bone pikes. They poke and guide the sightless, bellowing leviathans, feeding them offal and harvesting their thick, oily blubber. This fat is boiled in massive slate cauldrons over natural steam fissures, producing the tallow that lubricates every moving shuttle, coats every hide door, and fuels the small, smoky lamps that glow in the windowless cellars.

Because there are no seasons, no stars, and no shadows cast by a moving sun, the inhabitants have no concept of years, days, or hours. Time is not a line or a circle; it is a weight. It is measured in the depth of the grooves worn into the basalt stairways by generations of bare horn-soled feet. It is measured in the thickness of the tallow-crust on a workshop wall. 

They do not sleep all at once. There is no quiet hour. Instead, individuals sleep when their muscles fail, retreating into deep, honeycombed alcoves carved into the interior walls of the stone stacks, far from the outer drizzle. They sleep jammed together in groups of six or eight, body against body, sharing heat, their skin slick with the same protective oil. A hide curtain falls over the alcove entrance, muffling the external hammer-blows to a low, rhythmic thrum that mirrors the beating of a heart. They sleep for two hours, or ten, and then they rise, step out into the damp, roaring grey, and pick up the mallet again.

Their dead are not buried—for there is no earth to receive them, only solid, unyielding basalt. They are not burned, for there is no wood for a pyre, and tallow is too precious to waste on the dead. 

When an inhabitant dies, the body is brought down to the lowest, hottest cellars near the weeping fissures. There, specialists with knapped obsidian scalpels strip the corpse with practiced, unfeeling reverence. The skin is tanned into soft, durable leather for door-flaps and tool-grips. The long bones of the thighs and arms are scraped clean and carved into shuttles, needles, and hammer-handles. The skull is crushed to reinforce the mortar of the family’s living alcove. The flesh and organs are fed to the mud-beasts in the pens. 

Within a single cycle of steam, the deceased is fully absorbed back into the city. A child may work slate with a mallet whose handle is his grandfather’s femur, sitting in an alcove whose lintel is mortared with his great-grandmother’s bone-ash, shielded from the sulfur drizzle by a curtain made of his kinfolk’s hides.

They are a people without nostalgia, without art that does not serve a structural purpose, and without a word for the sky. They do not imagine a world beyond the basin, for a world without steam would dry their skin, a world without stone would offer no foundation, and a world without noise would feel, to them, like the end of life itself. They are as hard, warm, and permanent as the basalt beneath their toes—alive in the endless, roaring evening of a world that refuses to age.
