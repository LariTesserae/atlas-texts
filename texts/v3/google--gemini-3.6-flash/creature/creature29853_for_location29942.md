At the lowest tier of the ecology, where the iron grates overlap above the abyss, the life is flat, broad, and armored. 

Here, in the dark seams between cog-teeth and around the wet bases of hydraulic housings, live the *plate-beetles*. They are as wide as a man’s hand, their carapaces not shiny, but matte and pitted like weathered cast iron. They do not walk so much as slide on specialized oily pads beneath their torsos, pushing themselves along the grease-grooves left by the slow-moving shafts. They feed on the plum-colored oxide skin and the dense, sulfurous moss that grows over it, scraping the metal clean with heavy, crescent-shaped mandibles. They have no ears, nor do they possess eyes; where their heads should be, two thick, leathery palps press constantly against the metal, reading the micro-vibrations of the structure. When a heavy valve deep in the pavilion’s belly unseats itself, the beetles do not flee—they flatten their bodies against the rivets, magnetizing their underbellies slightly to the iron through iron-rich hemolymph, locking themselves in place until the shudder passes.

Above them, in the sunlit mid-levels where the copper pipes bleed warm steam, the turquoise-throated lizards move across the moss-sleeves like sparks of living mineral. Their skin is not scaled in the manner of desert reptiles, but soft, rubbery, and hydrophobic, instantly shedding the oily dew that condenses on every truss. Because the deafening roar renders all sound useless, their social world is entirely optical and kinetic. 

Two lizards meeting on a forty-foot copper conduit do not hiss or call; they engage in a intricate, blindingly fast ballet of movement. They snap their turquoise dewlaps open and closed like tiny, vivid lanterns, flashing patterns that correspond to the angle of the light beams slicing through the lattice. One will strike the copper pipe twice with its chin—a gesture that sends a tiny, localized *ping* through the metal—and the other will respond by flattening its crest and yielding the sunniest patch of moss. Their eggs are laid directly inside empty rivet-holes, sealed in with a plug of chewed orchid-fiber and hardened sulfur-grease, where the ambient heat incubates them over months of steady, unvarying warmth.

Higher still, in the vast, open volumes where the structural air is thickest with floating spores and mist, live the brass-feathered swifts. They are small, blunt-winged birds whose plumage contains actual microscopic filaments of metallic copper, giving them a dull, metallic sheen that glints green and bronze as they slice through the light-shafts. 

These swifts do not know the earth. To them, the world consists of two things: the warm, loud iron cage and the great, white floor of cloud three thousand feet below. Their nests are marvels of mechanical scavenging—woven from long, ropey orchid roots, shredded hemp-packing stripped from leaking pipe-joints, and shed beetle carapaces, glued together with a thick, amber-colored resin the birds harvest from the bases of the wild fig roots. They drop upward—or rather, they fall outward—through the floor-grates into the blinding blue sky, spreading their stubby wings to catch the powerful thermal updrafts generated by the pavilion’s heat. They soar for miles in the thin, ache-inducing upper air, hunting cloud-borne midges, before returning to the cage to land with a hard, metallic *clack* of claws on the high girders.

But the true masters of this iron jungle are larger, slower, and far more deliberate.

They are the *truss-dwellers*—beings whose very anatomy seems to have been drawn up by the same long-dead draftsman who designed the cathedral cage. They are quadrupedal, though their front limbs are long enough to give them a semi-erect posture when resting on a catwalk. Their skin is the color and texture of wet, vulcanized rubber, hairless except along their spines, where dense, fibrous mats of moss-like bristles grow, trapping warm condensation to keep their skin supple in the high-altitude drafts.

They have no language of the voice. Their throats possess no vocal cords; their mouths are small, soft-lipped, and designed for shredding the tough, fleshy leaves of the oar-ferns and scraping the sweet, sulfurous goo from the thermal vents. Instead, their faces are dominated by three features: vast, amber-colored eyes protected by thick, transparent nictitating membranes that shield them from oil-mist; broad, flat nostrils that filter the iron-scented air; and a wide, disc-like pad of cartilaginous bone set directly into the center of their foreheads.

This forehead-pad is their ear, their mouth, and their compass. 

When a truss-dweller wants to "speak" across the vast, roaring interior of the pavilion, it does not attempt to overcome the noise—it uses it. It leans forward and presses its forehead-pad flush against a structural girder. By striking the same girder with heavy, calloused knuckles in rhythmic patterns, it sends low-frequency vibrational pulses traveling miles through the continuous iron frame. Another dweller, perched two hundred feet away on a gear-housing, feels the pulse rattle through the metal, into its grip, and up into its own forehead. To them, the pavilion is not noisy; it is a complex, continuous map of touch. They know where the water is hottest, where the moss is ripe for harvesting, and where another of their kind is resting, simply by feeling the "flavor" of the iron’s hum beneath their hands.

Their movement is a study in exquisite patience. In an environment where a single missed grip means a three-thousand-foot drop into the white fog below, speed is an evolutionary error. A truss-dweller moving along a wet girder does not step; it *threads*. Its hands and feet have four double-jointed digits arranged in an opposition square, shaped precisely to clamp around standard three-inch angle-iron or drop through the square mesh of the catwalks. They lock each limb in place before releasing the next, moving with the same deliberate, rhythmic pace as the forty-foot flywheel in the central arch. It can take a dweller twenty minutes to cross a single fifty-foot span, its amber eyes tracking the shafts of sunlight, its leathery body moving in seamless harmony with the groaning, three-hour strokes of the hydraulic rams.

They build no shelters, for the pavilion itself is a shelter. When they sleep, they do not lie down on the catwalks where the air is moving; they climb into the dark, warm hollows behind the big bronze gear-cases, where the air smells of old lard and sulfur and the temperature never varies by a single degree. They sleep curled tightly together in clusters of three or four, their rubbery limbs intertwined, their foreheads pressed against the warm, vibrating brass. The deafening, bone-rattling roar of the vents is their lullaby; if the sound were ever to stop, if the great iron heart were ever to go cold and quiet, the sheer terror of the silence would likely kill them before the cold did.

They do not know that there is a ground beneath the clouds. They do not know that the metal they grip was once dug from dirt, or that it was shaped by fires other than the ones that rumble far down in the structural pylons. To the dwellers, the iron is not a structure built within the world; the iron *is* the world. The plum-colored rust is their soil, the sulfur-mist is their rain, the roaring air is their sky, and the slow, endless turning of the great flywheel is the steady, patient passing of time itself.
