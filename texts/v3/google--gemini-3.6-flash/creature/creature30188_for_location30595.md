At first, the terrace seems to harbor no life beyond the sculptured greens of the succulents and the velvet-ribbed ferns. But life here does not announce itself through flutter or scurry; it matches the cadence of the weeping stone.

In the shallowest, glassiest basins, where the mineral water rests before spilling over the wax-smooth lips, the surface is not entirely bare. Stretched across the bottom of these pools are vast, paper-thin sheets of a translucent, amber-colored gel. They look like forgotten shed skins or thin layers of boiled horn, but they are alive. They are colonial organisms—neither plant nor animal in any familiar sense—that feed directly on the silica and dissolved iron in the water. They breathe through rhythmic, imperceptible puckering along their edges, fanning the warm liquid over their membranes. A single sheet may take fifty years to carpet a three-foot bowl, cementing itself so firmly to the basalt that it becomes part of the stone’s smooth, glazed topography. When a glass-smooth seed-pod drops into the water, it does not rot; it rests upon these amber mats, which slowly, over decades, secrete a slick mucus that lubricates the pod's journey toward the spillway.

And then there is the tender of the notched stone.

It lives in the deep shadow behind the velvet fronds, where the rear cliff forms an arched recess that stays dark even at midday. It is a singular creature—perhaps the last of a relict lineage of mountain-dwellers, or perhaps something that was once human before the mineral air and the heat carved the urgency out of its blood.

Its skin is hairless and the color of wet slate, with a faint, iridescent sheen where micro-crystals of silica have bonded with the epidermis. It does not shiver; its core is warmed by the hot vapor it breathes and the radiant amber heat of the basalt it slumbers against. Its body is long-limbed and low-slung, built without sharp angles—its joints are padded with thick, fibrous cartilage, and its spine curves in a smooth, continuous arc, allowing it to rest against the rounded rock bowls as neatly as water filling a mold.

Its feet are wide and flat, the soles turned into tough, rubbery pads that grip the slick, wet glaze without tearing the delicate amber membranes beneath the water. It has no need for claws or sharp teeth. Its hands are long, with blunted, spatulate fingers that can slide into the narrowest water-channels to clear a stray pebble or guide a rolling seed-pod back into its groove.

Its eyes are large, pale, and lidless, covered by a permanent, glassy nictitating membrane that shields them from the brilliant, thin-air sunlight and the stinging mineral vapor. It does not see in sharp, predatory focus; instead, it registers the shift of light across the basalt, the slow swell of a seed-pod, and the gentle rise of steam. Its hearing is tuned entirely to the low, resonant frequencies of the terrace—the deep *thrum* of the heat radiating through the mountain's shoulder, and the rhythmic *clink... clink* of the water drops.

It feeds on the jade-colored succulents, but with an economy so refined it never kills a plant. Every few weeks, when the sun reaches its highest point, the creature crawls with agonizing slowness to the center of the terrace. It picks up one of the ancient obsidian blades from the shelf—handling the sharp glass with a light, practiced touch that does not disturb the thin layer of lime binding the tool to the stone—and scores a single, neat line into one of the thick, arm-like leaves.

It does not drink greedily. It presses its wide, soft mouth against the weeping cut and absorbs the bitter, water-dense sap as it oozes out. When it has taken what it needs, it reaches into a natural basin of grey, mineral-rich mud and smears a thick paste over the wound, sealing the leaf against rot and allowing the succulent to heal over the next three seasons.

It spends days at a time entirely motionless, submerged to its chin in the middle soaking bowl—the one fed by the ancient V-shaped spillway. Its breathing is so slow that its chest rises only twice in an hour, causing the glassy surface of the pool to send out single, smooth ripples that ring against the rock lip. While it rests, small particles of lime and silica settle upon its shoulders, forming tiny, pale crests along its collarbones that wash away only when it shifts its posture to follow the shadow of the cliff.

There is no mate here, nor has there been for centuries. Reproduction for this creature is not a matter of seasons, but of geological patience. Deep within its torso, a single, dense egg slumbers, encased in a thick, mineralized shell. When its own long life finally draws to a close—when its joints fully calcify and its slow heart ceases to pulse against the warm basalt—it will settle into the deepest, warmest basin. There, as its body slowly dissolves into the silica-laden water over a hundred years, the shell will soften, releasing a new, slow-breathing life into the quiet, sunlit warmth of the shelf.

Until then, it remains: a living part of the hydrology, as silent and permanent as the basalt wall, watching the water weep, the seed-pods roll, and the light turn the dripping steam to gold.
