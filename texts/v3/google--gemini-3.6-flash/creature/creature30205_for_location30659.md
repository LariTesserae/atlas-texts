Here, the line between what is terrain and what is inhabitant has melted away. Nothing that lives in the basin possesses the dryness of bone, the hardness of timber, or the sharp edge of claw. The creatures here are soft-bodied hydraulics—assemblages of wet hide, flexible cartilage, translucent membrane, and pulsing muscle, held together by the heavy pressure of the air and the warm fluid of their own blood.

They do not dwell *upon* the land; they are dissolved into it, a continuous, squelching layer of meat anchored to the mud.

***

Deep within the gray clay beds—buried three, five, ten feet beneath the surface—are the **Low-Bellowers**. 

These are the basin’s architects and its bass note. They are amphibians, but they have shed all need for limbs, eyes, or lungs that take in clear air. A single Low-Bellower can be twenty feet long, shaped like a fat, muscle-bound trunk of grey, slick skin, swollen with lymph and blood. They possess no true teeth; instead, their vast, circular mouths are lined with rings of fleshy, grinding pads that sweep the warm slurry through their digestive tracts, extracting minerals, bacteria, and the liquefying remains of whatever has sunk from above. 

They breathe through their skin, which is perforated by thousands of microscopic, weeping pores. As they contract their heavy, cylindrical bodies to push through the unresisting mud, they force trapped gas and steam up through the clay, creating the liquid, bubbling craters that pock the basin floor. The deep resonance that vibrates through the basalt mounds is the beating of their four-chambered hearts and the slow, rhythmic expansion of their throat-bladders, which swell with methane and drop back with a thick, wet *thrum* that never ceases.

***

Above them, where the clay meets the soft, six-foot-deep carpet of succulent vegetation, lives the **Blind Canopy-Herd**.

The dinner-plate isopods and forearm-sized orthopterans are not distinct, warring species, but different expressions of a single, endlessly churning order of soft-shelled detritivores. The isopods—pale, milky-white, and smooth as boiled lard—stay in the shallow pans. They move in massive, overlapping rafts, their dozens of short, feathery legs fanning the water to draw microscopic grease into their rasping mouthparts. They are so soft that if one is squashed under the weight of its neighbors, it does not bleed dry; it simply dissolves into a pinkish gel that the surrounding swarm laps up within minutes, leaving only the soft, translucent dish of its dorsal plate to soften and turn to silt.

Climbing through the purple skiff-leaves are the wingless orthopterans—the **Raspers**. Their dark, bruised-purple bodies are soft and segmented, like fat leather sacks filled with warm water. They have no eyes, navigating instead by the thick, velvet-like cilia that coat their flanks, sensitive to every vibration in the steam. 

The Raspers do not chew in the traditional sense. Their mouths are double-tubed siphons that secrete a warm, acidic bile onto the succulent vines, breaking down the leafy tissue into a green paste before sucking it back in. Because food is unlimited and requires no pursuit, their lives are a continuous loop of feeding and budding. They do not lay hard-shelled eggs; they exude jelly-like clusters directly onto the undersides of the broad leaves, where the warm mist hatches them in hours into tiny, translucent grubs that immediately begin to rasp.

***

Movement through the dense foliage is slow, but it is not entirely peaceful. The dominant predator of the mid-layer is not a fleet-footed hunter, but the **Velvet-Siphon**.

The Siphon looks like nothing more than a fallen, waterlogged leaf—a flat, leaf-shaped blanket of dark, wet muscle, four or five feet across, draped casually over a bend in a succulent vine or floating on the surface of a mud-pan. It has no head, no tail, and no eyes. It breathes the steam directly through its upper skin, which is indistinguishable from the bruised-green flora around it.

It waits by simply existing. When a heavy Rasper or a drifting flier stumbles onto its broad surface, the Velvet-Siphon does not strike. It simply *relaxes*. Its edges curl inward with a slow, hydraulic fold, wrapping the victim in a heavy, air-tight sleeve of warm, slimy flesh. Digestion begins instantly. The inner lining of the Siphon exudes a clear, powerful solvent that liquefies the prey's soft tissue while it is still writhing. Within twenty minutes, the Siphon unfurls again, sleek, wet, and slightly thicker, its surface clean and ready for the condensation to wash it clear.

***

In the dim, copper-tinted airspace between the high fronds, the **Drifters** tumble.

These are not birds, nor are they insects. They are tiny, gas-filled bladders of thin, vein-laced skin, suspended beneath short, leathery fins that beat with a slow, blind rhythm. The air is so heavy and dense with steam that these creatures hardly need to fly; they float like sea-jellies in a warm current, buoyed by the heat rising from the mud and the methane released by the Low-Bellowers.

The Drifters trailing long, thread-like tentacles covered in sticky, mucilaginous droplets. As they drift aimlessly through the haze, these threads catch the dense clouds of airborne micro-spores, silt-mites, and the tiny, flying grubs that hatch from the plant stems. When a Drifter grows too heavy with caught food, its bladder collapses, and it plummets like a wet stone into the lower mat, where it is instantly engulfed by the isopods or absorbed by the roots of the vine-carpet. They do not die so much as they are recycled back into the fog, their nutrients rising again as shoots within a single arc of the twilight.

***

And at the center of this boiling equilibrium, where the basalt mounds swell highest, sit the **Core-Maws**.

They are the largest living things in the basin, blurrings of plant and animal that have reached a total, motionless permanence. A Core-Maw appears as a slick, dark dome fifteen feet high, rooted deeply into the basalt weeping-rock. Its outer layer is woven from the thick, knee-deep vines that climb its flanks, but beneath the green skin is a solid mass of pulsating, pink liver-tissue and cartilage.

It does not hunt, nor does it grow leaves of its own. It is a vast, living sink. Water weeping down the stone runs directly into its cavernous, top-facing vent—a wide, wet blowhole lined with soft, pink papillae. The Core-Maw draws in hundreds of gallons of slurry, wet leaves, dead fliers, and live mud-skimmers every hour, filtering the organic paste through its internal labyrinth of warm, glandular chambers. 

It exhales the waste not as mud, but as the thick, warm, aerosolized silt that keeps the basin’s air heavy and opaque. The wet, rhythmic *gasping* of the Core-Maws, echoed from mound to mound across the miles, is the true engine of the mist. 

Around them, nothing is born to leave, and nothing dies to be remembered. The creatures of the basin are merely temporary shapes taken by the warm mud and the wet air, held briefly in a pulse of flesh before sliding back into the stew to rise again.
