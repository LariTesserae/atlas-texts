In this cold, bright, endless sea, nothing is wild. There are no wanderers, no accidental invaders, no species that adapt through the slow, chaotic drift of competitive evolution. Everything that moves, swims, crawls, or clings in these leagues of illuminated water was summoned by the system, grown from its nodes, and shaped to serve its hydraulic equilibrium. 

To live here is not to inhabit a landscape; it is to be a component in a five-mile-tall engine.

***

### The Scrapers and Fitters

On the smooth, basalt-dark surfaces of the iron-bark columns, where the water is pulled into intake grates with the pull of a falling tide, live the **Lathe-Ticks**. 

They are blind, broad-backed crustaceans the size of horses, though their bodies bear no resemblance to soft flesh. Their carapaces are thick plates of biological bronze—a dense composite of chitin, iron-precipitate, and polymerized resin that never pits or tarnishes in the pure, cold water. They do not have legs in any conventional sense; instead, their lower torsos split into clusters of jointed, diamond-tipped chisels and spring-loaded scrapers.

A Lathe-Tick is born inside a copper-veined seed-node along the column’s base. It emerges fully formed, its limbs pre-calibrated to the precise curvature of the wood trunk on which it hatches. It spends its five-hundred-year life moving along a single ten-yard strip of iron-bark. With its shear-shaped front appendages—blades that snap shut with the violent, hydraulic crack of a springing trap—it trims away the microscopic, thread-like algae that attempt to settle in the seams of the valve-housings. 

When a Lathe-Tick detects a micro-fracture in the polished wood beneath it, it does not heal the wound with its own body. Instead, it bores its drill-like snout into the crack, taps directly into a pressurized sap-duct, and metes out a fine, fast-curing latex amber, smoothing it down with the flat of its abdomen until the surface is once again flush to within a micron. 

They have no stomachs. They do not eat prey. They derive their sustenance directly from the high-voltage bio-electric cables they tend, clamping onto the copper-veined tendrils with specialized copper-toothed palps to draw off a steady, low-amperage current. When their joints finally seize after centuries of continuous scraping, they do not fall. They press themselves into the nearest high-pressure exhaust port, allowing the next *Thrum-clack* of the piston to crush their bronze shells into a fine, lubricating powder that flushes through the valves, reducing friction for the next generation.

### The Internal Gaskets

Deeper within the columns, inside the cathedral-sized chambers where sap and high-pressure water are mixed and forced skyward, live the **Marrow-Slugs**.

These are soft-bodied, translucent leviathans—some two hundred feet in length—that reside entirely within the dark, deafening interior of the wooden pumps. Their skin is thick, rubbery, and coated in a frictionless, bio-silicone mucus. 

The Marrow-Slugs are the living seals of the world-machine. As the giant timber pistons drop with crushing force, pushing thousands of tons of fluid through the vertical trunks, the slugs wedge their elongated, highly compressed bodies into the ring-shaped gaps between the piston head and the chamber wall. They are dynamic gaskets. Under a thousand pounds per square inch of hydraulic shock, their bodies flatten, expand, and flex, preventing the liquid from blowing out through the valve seats.

They feel no pain from this constant, rhythmic crushing; their nervous systems are simple, binary loops attuned entirely to pressure differentials. They feed on the high-sugar sap that leaks past the seals, absorbing it directly through their permeable hides. Between strokes, during the millisecond of vacuum before the next intake, they shift slightly, rotating a fraction of an inch so that their skin wears evenly down its entire length. A Marrow-Slug is little more than a living ring of dense, self-healing cartilage, breathing in pressure, breathing out frictionlessness, wedded forever to the dark interior of a single valve.

### The Conducted Schools

In the miles of open, illuminated space between the grid-columns, where the water is so clear that a shadow can be seen three leagues away, the mid-water is occupied by the **Brassy Spindles**.

They resemble ribbon-fish, twenty to thirty feet long, but their bodies are entirely flat and thin as a razor blade. Their scales are overlapping leaves of flexible brass-leaf that catch the steady, white daylight, turning the open water into a crisscross of blinding, reflected needles.

The Spindles do not possess hearts, nor do they swim by flexing their muscles. Their lateral lines are strung with fine, copper-wire nerves that pick up the deafening, subsonic *Thrum-clack* of the distant columns. Every time the million timber valves snap shut in unison, a massive kinetic shockwave passes through the clear brine. The Spindles’ brass scales are piezoelectric: the shockwave compresses the scales, generating a violent spurt of electric charge along their flanks that jet-propels them through the water without a single movement of their tails.

They ride the shockwaves. They are conducted through the ocean like copper needles caught in a magnetic pulse. 

A school of ten thousand Spindles moves as a single, rigid sheet of golden foil, shot across the vast, empty avenues from Column A-1 to Column A-2. They head directly for the high-pressure exhaust plumes. As a column vents its excess water through a whistling siphon, the Spindles dive straight into the jet, using the immense fluid friction to polish their brass scales clean of any oxidation. They feed on the microscopic, copper-rich mineral flakes thrown off by the grinding of the giant wooden gears, filtering the brine through gills made of fine, metallic gauze. 

They have no voices, no mating rituals, and no fear. When two schools, propelled from opposite directions by different column-grids, collide in the open water, there is no dodging or panic. The metallic ribbons slide past one another with a high-pitched, ringing chime—like ten thousand sword blades drawn simultaneously from velvet scabbards—before continuing their straight, violent trajectories across the bright void.

### The Acoustic Anchor-Nets

Suspended in the exact center of the vast, four-column voids—miles from any wooden structure—float the **Drift-Marrow**.

They are the largest living things in the ocean, though they are nearly invisible. A Drift-Marrow consists of a central, spherical node of solid, amber-like resin the size of a house, from which radiate millions of gossamer-thin, cellulose threads. These threads stretch out in all directions for leagues, anchoring themselves to the iron-bark of four adjacent columns, pulling tight like the rigging of a grand ship.

The Drift-Marrow is the system’s tension-gauge and neural relay. It has no mouth, no eyes, and no limbs. Its entire body is a fluid-filled resonator. 

As the four column-trees pump, their enormous trunks sway by a fraction of an inch under the recoil of the hydraulic stroke. The gossamer threads of the Drift-Marrow pick up this motion, transmitting the microscopic vibrations back to the central resin sphere. Inside the sphere, thousands of tiny, mineralized statoliths tumble through fluid, measuring the precise timing, force, and symmetry of the world-engine’s pulse.

If Column 112 falls out of phase by a single millisecond—if its piston drags, or its intake valves clog with sediment—the change in vibration alters the tension on the Drift-Marrow’s threads. The central sphere reacts instantly: it compresses its internal chambers, squirt-firing dense, bioluminescent chemical spores along the anchored threads directly toward the sluggish column. 

These spores travel down the lines like glowing beads of light, entering the intake grates of the column. Upon contact with the internal wood tissue, the chemicals act as a hormonal command, triggering the plant cells to dilate the hydraulic ducts, increase sap flow, or signal the local Lathe-Ticks to swarm the sticky valve that caused the delay.

The Drift-Marrow does not think. It does not dream. It is a living clock-balance, holding the rhythm of four square leagues of ocean in a state of absolute, mechanical perfection.

### The System’s Immune Response

On rare occasions—perhaps once every ten thousand years—a foreign element develops. A seed-node mutates; an iron-bark root grows an uneven, wild knot; a cluster of Lathe-Ticks fails to scrape a valve seat, allowing a thick, uncontrolled gall of resin to swell into the open water.

When this happens, the ocean produces its only mobile, predatory entity: the **Siphon-Rupturers**.

They are not born from eggs or nodes. They are formed when a hydraulic relief pod suffers a blowout. The tearing of the high-pressure latex membrane releases a bolus of concentrated, metallic sap mixed with reactive enzymes into the cold water. Within minutes, this slurry hardens around a core of bio-electric nerves, forming a sleek, segmented creature shaped like a torpedo, thirty yards long, made of raw, black heartwood and tipped with a whirling drill-head of iron-infused lignin.

The Rupturer has a lifespan measured in hours. Driven by the electrical imbalance of the faulty column, it tears through the clear water at terrifying speed, propelled by a continuous, chemical-jet pulse from its stern. 

It does not hunt fauna. It homes in on the structural flaw. 

Arriving at the mutated wood-knot or overgrown valve, the Rupturer rams itself directly into the anomaly. Its drill-head bites into the timber, grinding the excess growth into fine, inert sawdust. Once the obstacle is cleared and the hydraulic geometry of the column is restored to its exact blueprint, the Rupturer’s purpose is complete. 

It does not retreat. It secretes a high-concentration acid from its core that dissolves its own body in seconds, reducing its wood and metal back into basic mineral salts, which are instantly swept into the intake grates to nourish the column it just repaired.

### The Absence of the Individual

To look into the crystal-clear, sunlit depths of this high ocean is to see thousands of miles of open space, yet there is no loneliness here. 

There are no skeletons lying on the floor of petrified bark. There is no decay, no drifting detritus, no rotting flesh. The water is so pure because every molecule of waste is anticipated, caught, and re-absorbed by the living components before it can cloud the light.

The ribbon-fish chime across the leagues; the Lathe-Ticks scrape their assigned ten yards of bronze; the giant Marrow-Slugs compress beneath the weight of three thousand atmospheres of sap; the Drift-Marrow hums with the tension of the grid. 

None of them know what the water is. None of them know what lies beyond the plant-walls of the ocean, or what exists below the planetary basin. They are born without eyes to see the light, but with bodies that fit so precisely into the mechanical geometry of the plant-columns that to remove a single brass spindle or a single gasket-slug would be like removing a tooth from a gear.

They live in the thunder. They move in the pulse. They are the wet, metallic, self-repairing tissue of a machine that was running before the stars took their present shapes, and which will go on pumping its cold, clear sea through the silent air forever.
