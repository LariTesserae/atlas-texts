The life in the basin does not merely occupy the iron and slate; it has completed them. Where human engineers left off—abandoning their flues, their counterweights, and their threaded joints to the dark—these creatures have stepped in as living packing, gaskets, and check-valves, filling every tolerance until the boundary between the machine and the organism is entirely erased.

### The Siphon-Sacks (The Pipe-Bellows)

The lantern-globe creatures anchored to the copper conduits are the basin’s most numerous occupants, hanging in dense, dripping clusters like giant, translucent fruit. They possess no nervous system in the traditional sense, but rather a network of hydraulic channels that react directly to water pressure and mineral density. 

Their outer walls are composed of a tough, rubbery cartilage, layered like oiled pig-bladder and pale as lard. Through these thin walls, one can see the inner workings of their bodies: a central, dark-grey core—the *humor-knot*—surrounded by delicate, vein-like filaments that pulse with cold, clear fluid. 

They are shape-matched to the ruin around them. Over generations, their cartilaginous foot-stalks have grown into the threads of the iron piping, secreting a lime-rich cement that fuses flesh to rust. A siphon-sack does not select its pipe; it settles there as a free-swimming larva no larger than a bead of tallow, drifting on the cold runoff until it finds an open nipple or a rusted flange where water trickles. Once rooted, it expands, molding its mouth-parts into the exact contour of the orifice.

When the water flows freely, the siphons operate in a staggered, mechanical chorus. Each sack takes roughly forty seconds to fill, expanding until its skin is drum-tight and blue-grey under the faint phosphorescence. Then comes the sharp, wet *hiss-spurt* as the inner valve trips, ejecting a concentrated jet of mineral-depleted water into the pools below, retaining only the dissolved iron, copper salts, and sulfur compounds. They are the basin's primary pumps, keeping the water moving through channels that would otherwise choke on their own silt.

### The Quartz-Shelled Isopods (The Sand-Grazers)

In the shallow, shingle-lined pools beneath the siphons live the isopods, moving in dense, slow-drifting herds that cover the pool bottoms like living gravel. Each is roughly the size of a almond, covered in a jointed carapace of pure, crystalline silica—an organic glass secreted from the trace silicates dissolved in the dripping ceiling-water.

Because their shells are perfectly clear, the isopods are defined entirely by their insides. Their digestive tracts are thick, bright-orange ribbons of iron-paste, constantly churning as their mandibles scrape the deep orange bacterial velvet from the shale. They are heavy for their size, sinking quickly into the grit if dislodged, and their passage produces a constant, soft metallic rustle—a sound like handfuls of small brass nuts being stirred in a wooden bowl.

The isopods are the basin’s cleaners and stone-builders. They consume the rust-bacterial mat, pass the iron through their guts, and excrete a dense, cement-like pellet that settles into the cracks between the shingle. Over decades, these pellets fuse the loose gravel into solid, level floors, preventing the pools from eroding the slate bedrock. When an isopod dies, its orange insides dissolve into the cold current, but its glass carapace remains behind, hollow and immortal, to be ground into sand by the movement of its living kin.

### The Lung-Slugs (The Crawlers of the Gears)

Thick around as a man’s thigh and four feet long, the lung-slugs are the largest moving things in the basin. They are pale, slate-grey, and blind, their bodies covered in a thick, cool slime that smells strongly of cold mutton tallow and wet coal-dust. Their skin is not smooth; it is ridged with tough, calloused bands that allow them to drag their heavy bulk across rusted gear-teeth, sharp slag, and exposed bolt-heads without tearing.

The lung-slugs do not swim; they wallow and creep through the half-submerged structural debris at the center of the basin, their bodies contorting to fit the narrow gaps between the engine casing and the slate walls. They move with an excruciating, deliberate slowness, taking hours to traverse the length of the basin.

They are driven by two things: scent and vibration. They possess no eyes, but their forward ends are crowned with a cluster of soft, finger-like fleshy barbels that sweep the water ahead of them, detecting the faint chemical trace of the siphon-sacks’ egg-masses. These egg-masses—jelly-like ribbons anchored deep within the dark, flooded interiors of old valve-housings—are the lung-slugs' sole food source. 

To reach them, a lung-slug must force its soft body through the metal teeth of long-dead machinery, scraping its flanks until its gray grease coats the rust. Along their sides are a series of pulse-holes—primitive, fleshy lung-siphons—that open and close with a wet *schlub* sound as they draw in the cold mist trapped beneath the ceiling.

### The Stationary Masses (The Ancestor-Reefs)

Deepest in the basin, where the three water-channels converge into the lowest sump, the distinction between living creatures and calcified rock vanishes entirely. Here lie the *Ancestor-Reefs*—great, low mounds composed of generations of dead lung-slugs, siphons, and iron-crusts that have turned to stone.

Because the water is too cold and mineral-heavy for rapid decay, a dead creature in the basin does not rot. Instead, it acts as a crystal nucleus. When a lung-slug dies in a trough, its body is quickly sealed beneath a orange, velvet skin of iron-bacteria. The mineral-laden dripping water slowly replaces its soft tissues with lime and iron-carbonate, turning its flesh into a heavy, dark-grey monument. 

Over centuries, these petrified bodies have formed natural dams and dikes, redirecting the flow of water back into the iron pipes and troughs to feed the living siphons. The current basin floor is entirely supported by these bio-mineral foundations; the living organisms inhabit a landscape constructed almost entirely from the petrified remains of their ancestors.

### The Rhythm of the Chiming

Nothing in the basin exists independent of the hydraulic clockwork. When the upper cistern reaches capacity and tips—releasing its surge of cold water to turn the wooden paddlewheel—it alters the pressure of the entire system for a few brief moments.

The chorded chiming of the hanging brass bells—*clink, tonk, chime*—vibrates through the iron frame and echoes down into the slate bed. This vibration is not merely heard; it is physically felt by every organism in the basin. 

* The **isopods** freeze instantly, their brass-colored mandibles locking in place, their clear quartz carapaces catching the dull amber flicker of galvanic sparks from the nearby junction-boxes.
* The **siphon-sacks** suspend their pumping cycle, holding their breath as their inner cartilaginous valves stiffen under the acoustic frequency of the chime.
* The **lung-slugs** pause mid-stride, their finger-like barbels lifting clear of the mud, extending toward the ceiling as if tasting the air for changes in humidity.

For three seconds, the basin holds its breath. The sour green phosphorescence on the water seems to dim slightly as the ripples spread out from the paddlewheel. Then, as the wheel slows and comes to rest against the mud with a soft, final squelch, the counterweight swings back into place. The pressure shifts, the valves trip, the siphons spurt in unison, the isopods resume their sand-like rasping, and the quiet, industrial life of the basin continues its cold, petrifying crawl forward.
