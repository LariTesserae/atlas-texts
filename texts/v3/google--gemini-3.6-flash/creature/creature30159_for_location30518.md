Beneath the unblinking white glare of the ceiling, life does not evolve toward grace; it evolves toward density, speed, and armor.

At the lowest level, where the basalt meets the hot, welling saucers, the world begins with the film. It is a slick, pale-yellow skin of sulfur-eating bacteria that covers every inch of rock not actively being scraped clean. It smells of wet tin and warm rot. The film is the primary pasture, and everything in the basin is either grazing upon it, grazing upon the grazers, or falling out of the light to rot upon it.

### The Swarm-Fauna

The smallest free-moving things are the **glass-paddlers**—sightless, translucent crustaceans whose armor is not chitin, but a thin, vitreous shell formed from the dissolved silica in the thermal pools. They have no legs for walking, only six to twelve pairs of flattened, hair-fringed oars that beat in a feverish, asynchronous blur. They spend their short, furious lives—spanning perhaps three or four days—swimming against the boiling currents of the floor-saucers, straining bacterial curd through combs of stiff mouth-bristles. When the saucers boil over or dry into salt-flats, millions of paddlers die at once, their crystalline shells shattering under the feet of the scavengers into a coarse, glittering grit that fills the rock-crevices like crushed glass.

Above them are the **carpets**—the multi-jointed, leggy things that cover the dry ridges between pools. They are flattened like pressed leaves, wide as a plate, and move in liquid waves of thousands. Their undersides are a mass of rasping, file-like mouthparts that lick the gypsum bare. They are entirely deaf and blind, navigating by the thermal plumes rising off each other’s bodies. When one of the six-winged flyers plummets from the ceiling, the nearest carpet does not turn to look; it simply flows over the impact site like spilled oil, dissolving the fallen beast with regurgitated digestive acids before the carcass can even cool. Within ninety seconds, only the wet, blue-grey cartilage of the flyer’s sternum remains.

The flyers themselves—the **bladder-wings**—are creatures of pure displacement. They do not nest, nor do they ever voluntarily touch the ground, for their limbs have withered into tiny, recurved hooks used only to cling to one another in mid-air mating clusters. Their bodies are bulbous, sac-like, filled with superheated gasses produced by the sulfur-digesting flora in their double stomachs. Their skin is thick, rubbery, and dark olive, dripping with a cool, mucus-like sweat that keeps them from scorching under the radiant heat of the quartz seam above. They fly by beating three pairs of narrow, leathery wings in a high-pitched, droning rhythm, steering blindly through the muggy updrafts by sensing the pressure waves off the rock walls. Their death is constant and casual: they collide with one another, exhaust their gas reserves, or are clipped by the plumes of the mud-geysers, raining down onto the floor in an endless, heavy hail of flesh.

In the deepest, darkest fissures—where the basalt splits into cool, waterlogged guts six hundred feet beneath the basin floor—live the **slither-horns**. They are the apex of the basin's non-human life: legless, thick-bodied ambushers thirty feet long, covered in overlapping plates of bone-white calcite. They are almost entirely muscle and gullet. When the mud-geysers blow, the slither-horns are drawn up by the shockwaves, sliding out of the steaming cracks like greasy pistons to gorge on the carpets and the wicker-trap harvests of the people before retreating back into the crushing darkness of the underworld.

### The Ridge-Dwellers

The people who live here have no name for themselves, for they have no concept of another kind of person. They are simply the ones who stand upright.

To look at them is to see a creature sculpted entirely by heat, noise, and sharp edges.

#### Physicality
They are short—rarely over five feet—with broad, barrel-chested torsos built around oversized lungs that can pull oxygen from the thick, steam-laden air. Their skin is not brown or pale, but a dull, slate-grey, hairless and thick as cured hide, constantly slick with a cold, greasy secretion that repels the mineral-heavy water. Gypsum dust clings to this grease, giving them a ghostly, frosted appearance, as though they were carved from the same raw stone as their shelters.

Their heads are heavy-browed, with massive, bony ridges that overhang their eyes like built-in visors, shielding their pupils from the relentless overhead glare of the quartz seam. Their eyes are remarkable: small, narrow slits protected by a double nictitating membrane—one clear to keep out the stinging salt-spray, one cloudy white that acts as a filter against the burning light. They do not possess outer ears; the constant, deafening roar of the pit would render delicate ear-canals useless. Instead, their ear-holes are covered by thick flaps of skin, and they "hear" almost entirely through bone conduction. Their jawbones and heels are unusually dense, tuned to absorb and interpret the low-frequency thrum of tectonic shifts, geyser-pops, and the approach of large fissure-beasts through the basalt beneath their feet.

Their feet are their most radical adaptation. They do not have distinct toes; instead, the foot has fused into a thick, wedge-shaped pad of solid, horny keratin, like the hoof of a mountain goat, split only at the center to allow them to grip the razor-sharp teeth of the gypsum crags. They do not walk with a heel-toe stride, but with a low, springy, wide-stanced shuffle, keeping their hands free and their center of gravity close to the stone.

#### Language and Mind
Because speech is impossible over the wall of sound—the friction of chitin, the roar of vents, the hiss of boiling water—their communication is entirely visual and tactile.

It is a language of immense precision and speed. A single gesture is not merely a word, but a sentence composed of three simultaneous movements: a quick, rhythmic flex of the forearm muscles, a sharp tilt of the chin, and a flash of the inner eyelid or a widening of the nostrils. They can debate the placement of a basket trap or warn of a rising fissure-slitherer in the time it takes a flyer to strike the rock. When close together, in the shelter of their gypsum huts, they press their foreheads or the palms of their hands against one another’s collarbones, sending deep, vocal-cord hums directly through the skeleton—a dark, reverberating language of clicks and rumbles that can be felt but not heard.

Their minds operate in a state of absolute, hyper-vigilant present. They have no words for "yesterday" or "tomorrow," only terms for the current water-pulse, the state of the ceiling-burn, or the movement of the swarms. Because nothing they make lasts—their baskets rot in weeks, their hide aprons are consumed by salt-bugs if left unworn, and the rock itself ruptures and rewrites its topography every few days—they carry no possessions except what is strapped to their waists.

#### Life and Culture
They do not cook. The heat of the basin is warmth enough, and the flesh of the bladder-wings and glass-paddlers is eaten raw, warm, and dripping with lipid-grease, stripped from the cartilage with obsidian wedges or crushed between flat, powerful molars designed to break soft shell. Their kidneys are enormous; they do not urinate liquid, but excrete a thick, chalky white paste of concentrated calcium and uric acid, which they dry and use as an adhesive to mortar the slabs of their ridge-huts.

Marriage, lineage, and chieftainship do not exist. They live in small, fluid groups of twelve to twenty, bound together by the physical necessity of working the harvest baskets and maintaining the pike-guards. A child belongs to whichever ridge-dwelling it was born upon; it learns to walk on the horn-pads at three months and is harvesting flyers by six.

When a person dies—usually killed by a fall onto the sharp floor, a steam-scald, or a fissure-slitherer—there is no mourning. Mourning requires time, and time is a luxury the basin does not permit. The dead are not buried; the basalt will not open for them. Instead, the surviving ridge-dwellers immediately set upon the body with their glass knives. The skin is flayed to repair aprons and basket-straps; the long bones are shaved into pike-points; the teeth and jawbones are salvaged to make the rigid frames of the flyer-traps. What remains—the soft flesh and viscera—is tossed off the high ridge into the carpet-swarms below.

Within minutes, the loved one has been broken down into a thousand twitching, multi-legged movements, their nitrogen returned to the basin, their bones scraped clean to become tools for the living. By the time the next mud-geyser cannons into the blinding white air, nothing of them remains except the marks their jawbones make in the wicker, holding fast against the storm of meat.
