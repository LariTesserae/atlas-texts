To understand what lives in the vault, one must first unlearn the cadence of breathing, eating, and dying. Nothing here hunts; nothing runs. In a world without weather, seasons, or shadows, life does not fight against its environment; it simply precipitates out of the heat, like sugar crystallizing at the bottom of a undisturbed jar.

The smallest inhabitants are the **Disc-Carapaces**, though to call them animals feels imprecise. They are less like beasts and more like mobile organs of the plant itself. Smooth as blown glass and entirely clear, their disc-shaped bodies range from the size of a human palm to broad, three-foot platters. They possess no eyes, no limbs, and no respiratory openings. Their undersides are soft, damp membranes of wet ivory that adhere directly to the vertical stems or the glowing sap-cables. 

For weeks at a time, a disc remains welded to the bark, slowly drawing warm, sugary fluid through its translucent mantle. As it feeds, its internal organs—visible through the amber dome of its back—gaze back at the glare like tiny, motionless coils of pale green thread. When a disc finally moves, it does so by oozing across the cellulose on a layer of its own lubricating oil, leaving a temporary, shining trail that the plant reabsorbs within hours. They have no natural enemies, for there is no meat in them—only concentrated sap and water. When a disc eventually dies, it does not rot; its shell grows brittle, clouds to a milky white, and is slowly swallowed by the expanding wood beneath it, becoming a smooth, lens-like bulge in the column.

Higher up, suspended in the stagnant air between the giant stems, hang the **Bale-Bladders**. These are vast, teardrop-shaped sacks of thin, vein-laced membrane, pale lime in color and filled with a gas lighter than the heavy, humid atmosphere. They anchor themselves to the canopy with long, fibrous tendrils that resemble dripping tallow. The bladders do not move at all. They act as passive filters, their wet, velvet-textured skins absorbing the warm condensation that beads continuously in the upper reaches of the vault. Every few days, when a bladder becomes too heavy with gathered moisture, its lower valve opens with a soft, wet *pop*, releasing a single, perfectly spherical gallon of fragrant water into the pools below before floating back toward the ceiling. 

Then there are those who built the resin alcoves. 

They are still here. 

The hollowed-out nodes of petrified resin and the calcified root benches are not the ruins of an extinct civilization, nor are they abandoned. To a surface-dweller, a room that has seen no movement for three hundred years appears deserted. In the vault, three hundred years is merely a afternoon's posture.

The **Siphons**—as they might be called—are the descendants of whatever pre-human lineage adapted to this warm, bright cradle. They have become so thoroughly woven into the metabolic rhythm of the chamber that the boundary between their bodies and the plant architecture is almost blurred.

A Siphon stands roughly seven feet tall, though they rarely stand at all. Their frames are impossibly thin, built of flexible, cartilage-like bone and wrapped in smooth, hairless skin the color of peeled reed-pith or wet lard. They have no pigmentation; their veins show through their chests as faint, glowing lines of pale amber, carrying a blood so thick with dissolved sugars that it barely flows. Their heads are long and sleek, devoid of ears or noses. Where eyes should be, there are only smooth, unbroken hollows covered by the same pale skin—vision is useless in a world where light comes from everywhere at once and casts no shadows to define an edge.

Their mouths have lost the capacity to chew. Instead, a Siphon’s lips are fused into a small, muscular ring, perfectly fitted to press against the weeping pores of the sap-stems. 

In one of the resin alcoves, a Siphon rests on a low bench of calcified root. Its long, jointless fingers are buried two inches deep into the soft, unbaked cellulose of the wall, where the plant tissue has grown *around* them, anchoring the creature in place. It has been sitting there so long that a thin, dry crust of spun sugar has formed over its shoulders, draping it like a shroud of spun glass. 

It is not dead. If you were to press your ear to its chest, through the stifling heat you would eventually hear its heart: a single, deep, liquid *thump*, occurring perhaps once every forty minutes. It does not sleep, nor is it awake. It exists in a state of suspended, radiant contemplation, nourished by the slow drip of sugar through its fingertips and the endless, soothing glare of the amber sky.

When a Siphon does move, the event is so slow as to be nearly imperceptible. Over the course of a week, a hand will slip free from the wall with a quiet, sticky tear. The creature will lean forward, its limbs moving with the heavy, fluid grace of something submerged in oil, and slide off its bench. It does not walk; it glides on its knees and palms across the soft starch dunes, leaving shallow depressions that slowly swell and smooth over behind it, like impressions left in fresh dough. 

It travels toward one of the lukewarm sap-pools, where it will lie motionless at the water's edge for a month, its tubular mouth submerged, drinking the still, fragrant fluid. Nearby, others may be gathered—three or four pale, smooth forms draped over a curving root like discarded carvings—touching one another with their long, flat forearms. They do not speak. They have no lungs to push air, no vocal cords to shape sound. Their communication is entirely chemical: a slow, rhythmic sweating of aromatic oils from the palms of their hands, passing simple, static sensations—*heat*, *fullness*, *light*—from one skin to another.

Deepest of all, beneath the starch dunes, live the shadows inside the cables.

If one looks down through the translucent floor into the white-hot sap-channels—those thick conduits that run like glowing nerves through the base of the vault—one can occasionally see fast-moving shapes. These are the **Filaments**. Long, ribbon-like organisms, thirty feet from end to end and no thicker than an arm, they swim directly *inside* the high-pressure river of liquid starch. They are dark, dense, and move with a frantic, undulating urgency that contrasts shockingly with the motionless world above them. 

The Filaments are the only creatures here that possess velocity. They spend their lives battling the thick, viscous current of the sap-conduits, eating the dense nutrient knots that form in the plant’s deep circulatory system. To the Siphons resting above, the Filaments are felt only as a faint, rhythmic vibration through the floor—a distant, subterranean pulse that reminds them the great green engine beneath them is still pumping, still building, still waiting to begin.

Nothing here competes. There are no teeth, no claws, no venom, no rotting corpses. When an organism has fulfilled its long, slow cycle, its tissues simply soften, lose their distinction, and melt back into the damp, green-ivory bedrock, returning their water and sugar to the embryonic soil. The vault remains a nursery without a birth, a garden that never blooms, inhabited by beings who have traded the violent brevity of life for the quiet, scorching immortality of the seed.
