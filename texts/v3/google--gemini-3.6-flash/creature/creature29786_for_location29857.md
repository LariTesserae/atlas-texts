The floor belongs first to the **Scours**—the low, mindless tide that keeps the rock clean. 

They are hand-sized, armored, and entirely blind, built like fused plates of blackened bronze. They move not as individuals, but as a dense, fluid carpet, millions strong, scraping over the jagged basalt on thousands of tiny, silicone-hard legs. They do not have eyes; they have heat-sensitive cilia that drag along the stone, seeking the warmth of fresh vegetation or decay. Where a Scour-swarm passes, the razor-sharp obsidian is licked bare of moss, leaves, and soft flesh in a matter of seconds, leaving only wet, polished stone behind. They are the basin’s digestive system, perpetually hungry, moving in sudden, unpredictable migrations driven by the shifting heat vents below.

Above them, in the mid-canopy of dripping tendrils and broad succulents, live the **Vire-striders**. 

Hair would rot in this suffocating humidity, so the striders are slick and hairless, their hides a pale, bruised purple, coated in a thick, self-secreting grease that smells of wet ash. They are long-limbed and lean, built to span the wide gaps between iron-hard saplings without touching the treacherous ground. Their feet are not paws or hooves, but four-part claspers lined with recurved, chitinous hooks that bite into vine-bark. They have no eyes under the dark ceiling of vapor; instead, their long, wedge-shaped heads are split by twin rows of deep pit-organs that read the basin in heat. To a strider, a nesting bird is a blinding flare of yellow-white warmth; a cool glass ridge is a dark void. They leap through the gloom in absolute silence, their muscular tails lashing for balance, landing with a heavy, wet *thud* only to spring again before the vines stop shaking.

Deep in the thickets, where the basalt buckles into narrow, steam-venting creases, dwell the **Iron-Borers**. 

These are the giants that the hunters seek. They are squat, six-legged behemoths with carapaces thick as timber, encrusted with living ferns and white mineral crusts from the thermal pools they wallow in. The Borers use massive, spade-like snouts to crush raw obsidian, grinding the rock to reach the succulent, water-heavy tubers that grow in the deepest fault-slits. They breathe with a rhythmic, wet bellows-sound that vibrates through the mud, exhaling plumes of scalding mist that feed the canopy overhead. Their blood is thick, dark, and greasy—and their fat is prized above all else.

And trailing the Borers, moving through the churning green like shadows through grease, are the **Hunters**.

They are low-built and massive through the chest and shoulders, standing barely five feet tall but broad enough to wedge themselves firmly into rock-creases. They do not walk upright in the human sense; their posture is a permanent, spring-loaded crouch, their centers of gravity kept low to slip beneath the trip-wire tangle of vines. 

Their skin is their primary armor: a thick, pebbled leather, dark as the basalt itself, folded into heavy ridges over their joints. It is tough enough to turn a glancing blow from a glass edge, but perpetually supple, sloughed in sheets every few months as they outgrow the scars of the basin. They have no visible eyes—only smooth, seamless bone where an upper face should be, indented by broad, sensitive concavities that catch the high-frequency clicks they emit from deep within their throats. By bouncing these clicks off leaves, stone, and chitin, they hold a sharp, three-dimensional map of the dynamic gloom in their minds.

They do not use fire—fire would die in the muggy, oxygen-choked air, and light is useless to them anyway. Instead, they navigate by **flavor, scent, and skin-heat**. 

The soles of their feet and the palms of their wide, four-fingered hands are naked and pale, lined with hyper-dense nerve networks. By touching the obsidian, they can feel the subtle thermal vibrations of a Borer herd miles away, or the brittle fracture lines of a fresh glass outcrop. 

When a Hunter needs a tool, they do not carry it for long. They locate a vein of young obsidian by its temperature—freshly cooled glass holds a distinct, cold density compared to the surrounding earth. With three precise, deafening strikes using a heavy hammer-stone, they knap off a teardrop-shaped flake as long as a forearm, its edges atom-thin and terrifyingly sharp. 

They use these blades with brutal efficiency:
* They hack through the iron-hard vines that choked their path minutes before.
* They slide the sharp glass into the thin seams between an Iron-Borer’s armor plates, felling the beast before its deep, booming distress call can draw the canopy predators.
* Immediately after the kill, they skin the beast and strip its thick tallow, slathering the grease over their own hides and into the joints of their limbs. The tallow protects their skin from the damp-rot, masks their heat signature from the Vire-striders, and allows them to slide through narrow glass fissures without leaving their own flesh behind.

Their language is not made of words, but of **friction and force**. In a world loud enough to rattle ribcages, vocal speech is lost. Instead, they communicate by stridulation—rubbing hardened, ribbed bone-plates on their forearms against their flanks to produce harsh, metallic chirps that cut through the jungle's roar. A sharp *scritch-scritch* means *scatter*; a long, grinding rattle means *heavy meat ahead*.

They build nothing that lasts because the basin permits no permanence. A ladder of rib-bones wedged in an obsidian crease will be swallowed by vine-growth in three days, or smashed to splinters by a passing Borer tomorrow. A knapped glass axe, dulled after butchering a hide, is simply dropped where it stands, left to be buried under leaf-mold and re-discovered by the Scour-swarms.

They live in a state of eternal, present-tense movement. They sleep wedged high in the crotches of saplings, wrapped in their own leathers, swaying with the dynamic pulse of the canopy while the blind tides sweep the floor below them. They are not masters of the basin; they are simply its most lethal, adaptable component—born of the glass, greased in tallow, and vanishing into the roaring, dark warmth before the morning that never comes.
