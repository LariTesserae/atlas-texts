To find what lives here, you must unlearn what it means to be alive. 

If you look for breath, for moisture, for the soft give of warm flesh or the flash of a wet eye, you will see only stone and dry velvet. The creatures of this place do not possess blood; liquid of any kind in this high, thin vacuum would boil away or shatter like glass. 

They are there, resting in plain sight, but the eye mistakes them for the geometry of the ground. 

They look, at first, like discarded heaps of the landscape itself—a clump of dry sulfur-vine entangled around a splintered slab of feldspar, or a tall, narrow pyramid of obsidian wedged into a cliff seam. But watch long enough, suspended in the deafening, bone-shattering roar, and the rock moves. 

Not with the fluid ease of an animal, but with the sudden, precise micro-adjustments of a tectonic fault. 

Their bodies are dry assemblages of the massif’s own materials, bound together by a biology of pure friction and static tension. Their skeletons are hollow, geometric frames of hard fused silica—the same glass-like reeds that grow in the dry gorges. Wrapped around these bones are dense, braided cables of the sulfur-yellow vine, acting not as plant matter, but as muscle. When static electricity crackles through the atmosphere, these dry fibers contract with violent, elastic force, pulling the silica bones into motion with a sound like dry firewood snapping underfoot.

They have no faces. In place of a head, each creature bears a flat, upright fan of razor-edged slate, smooth and black as a mirror, tilted slightly to catch the ambient lateral light. They do not see through lenses or humors. Instead, they "see" by resonance. 

The monolithic roar that fills the sky is their primary sense. The sound wave, thick as earth, slams into their flat slate faces, bouncing off their internal silica chambers. They read the density of the air, the sharpness of a stone fifty yards away, and the subtle shifting of gravity’s angle by how the subterranean vibration rings through their hollow bones. To them, the deafening roar is not noise; it is a dense, high-definition map of the physical world. 

They move with absolute, grueling economy. A creature may sit tucked inside one of the low, stone wind-cheats for three decades without moving a single joint, its dry horsehair skin collecting fine pollen-dust, its obsidian face reflecting the unchanging violet-gray sky. It is not sleeping. It is tuning.

Inside the chest cavity of these beings—a ribcage woven from silver-gray lichen-fiber—hang small, meticulously carved wedges of basalt and shaved silica stalks. These are the tools found outside the structures. The creatures use their hard, glass-tipped fingers to scrape, chip, and slice the stalks into precise lengths, lashing them to their own skeletal interiors with simple, non-slipping knots of vine. 

They build the low stone shelters not to escape the cold—cold means nothing to dry mineral—but to create small pockets of acoustic shadow. Inside a shelter, the deafening roar is baffled just enough by the unworked slabs for the creature to work on its own internal geometry. They are constantly adjusting their own internal reeds, tuning their bodies to match the slow, shifting pitch of the continent grinding beneath them.

When two of them meet on a narrow obsidian ledge, there is no sound made between them—their dry vocalizations would be swallowed instantly by the sky-roar. Instead, they touch slate faces. The flat, polished stone of one strikes the flat stone of the other with a clear, sharp *tink*. In that instant of physical contact, the vibration of their internal silica reeds passes from one to the other. They exchange data on the localized shifts in gravity, the accumulation of static in the lower gorges, and the strength of the dry vines. 

They do not die, not in any sense that involves decay. When a creature’s silica frame finally fractures under the continuous stress of the acoustic pressure, its static charge grounds out. The vine-muscles dry into static brittle dust, and the skeleton collapses. The stones of its body simply fall into the talus. 

Another creature, emerging slowly from a nearby fissure decades later, will step over the fallen frame, pick up a suitable slab of slate or a wedge of basalt from the debris, and use it to build a wind-cheat or tune its own chest. 

They are the mountain’s self-awareness—dry, deafeningly loud, infinitely patient, and entirely complete without a single drop of water.
