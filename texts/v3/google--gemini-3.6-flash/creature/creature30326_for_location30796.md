Here, life has no soft margins. It does not ooze, rot, or yield; it strikes, meshes, vents, and welds. 

Beneath the brass canopy, where the air is a freezing haze of sprayed petroleum and ozone, the rock faces do not merely crawl—they *engage*. The gear-shelled organisms that carpet the basalt in three-deep layers are known as **clinkers**. Their bodies are asymmetrical disks of iron-infused chitin, edged with interlocking teeth that fit perfectly into the carapaces of their neighbors. They do not move as individuals; they move as living conveyer belts miles wide. A single clinker, separated from its line, is a helpless, twitching cog; joined to the swarm, it becomes part of a continuous, grinding sheet that climbs sheer cliffs by shear mechanical force. As they move, their undersides—tipped with diamond-hard radulae—strip the native copper and iron-shale down to bright, gleaming scarps, leaving a path so smoothly planed it reflects the harsh violet sky like a mirror.

Thriving in the friction between these grinding swarms are the **solder-ribbons**. These are flat, blind, lead-hued scavengers, thin as parchment and slippery with a dark, graphite-heavy mucus. They slide through the microscopic gaps between the moving clinkers, feeding on the metal dust and shed chitin flakes. Where the friction threatens to heat the rock to a dull red and bind the clinker-belts, the solder-ribbons squirt streams of dense, slick lipid synthesized from the trees’ pressurized sap. They are the living grease of the mountain, moving wherever the screech of metal-on-metal rises to a dry, tearing shriek.

Higher in the branches of the iron-bark trees, roosting in tangled masses so dense they resemble suspended, twitching tumors, are the **sibilants**. These are the white-furred, rib-winged fliers, each the size of a wolf, though far denser. Their skin is not hide, but a layered weave of silica-filled fibers that insulates their internal chemistry from the absolute cold. Inside their ribcages, behind plates of horn, lie double-chambered organ-furnaces where iron-rich sludge is endlessly oxidized with concentrated atmospheric oxygen. Their bellies glow through their translucent white undersides with a harsh, cherry-red light, throwing bloody illumination across the frozen zinc-gray leaves. 

When a sibilant takes flight, it does not flap in the traditional sense. Its wings are stiff, ribbed struts fitted with tough, membrane-like valves. By opening its internal furnaces to the freezing air, it ignites jets of superheated steam synthesized from its fluid reserves, launching itself upward with the deafening, sharp whistle of a blowing boiler. In the air, they do not hunt for prey through vision—they are stone-blind, their eyes covered by thick, quartz-like caps—but through galvanic senses. They track the turquoise lightning arcs that jump between the peaks, sweeping through the discharge clouds to catch the ionized dust, their copper-tipped beaks glowing with static discharge as they feed on the sky’s raw current.

Deep in the shadows of the cliff-faces, anchored directly over the deep-earth fissures where the basalt pistons drive their slow, weeks-long strokes, live the **calipers**. 

The calipers are the giant apex predators of this high-altitude engine, yet they move with the agonized slowness of the geology itself. A caliper is a low-slung, quadrupedal mass of dense magnetite bone and layered horn-plate, weighing many tons. It has no neck; its heavy, anvil-shaped head is fused directly to its shoulders, terminated in a pair of hydraulic pincers driven by internal liquid pressure so intense that its joints constantly drip a clear, boiling glycol. A caliper will set its heavy, spiked legs into the rock over a piston-vent and remain motionless for years, allowing the clinkers to grind over its back until it resembles a rough outcropping of the mountain itself.

When a flock of sibilants drops from the canopy to roost, or when a dense migration of clinkers reaches a choke point, the caliper strikes. It does not snap; it closes its pincers with the horrifying, unstoppable force of a hydraulic press. Bones of silica and shells of iron are crushed into a fine, dense powder, which the caliper draws into its ventral mouth-vent using powerful vacuum-siphon lungs. Inside the creature’s gut, the meal is reduced not by acid, but by a thermite-like chemical reaction that melts the copper and iron directly into its own skeletal framework, venting surplus gas from a row of dorsal blowholes with a long, low moan that sounds like a ship's horn echoing through fog.

Even the sky above the peaks is populated. Above the reach of the highest iron-barks, floating in the hard, glossy violet air, are the **aerostats**. These are vast, bulbous organisms shaped like segmented teardrops, fifty yards from tip to tail, clad in a shimmering skin of native foil-aluminum and silver-tinged scales. They carry no muscle for powered flight; instead, their hollow interiors are filled with volatile light-gases synthesized from the geothermal vents far below. 

The aerostats are the ultimate heat-harvesters of the heights. Their upper surfaces are flat and black, absorbing the flat, electric radiance of the unfiltered sun until their metallic skin is hot enough to melt rime on contact. Hanging from their undersides are long, trailing tendrils of pure biological copper, hundreds of feet long. These tendrils drift through the metallic canopy like the roots of a floating island, brushing against the high-voltage iron-barks and drawing off the galvanic currents generated by the forest. When a lightning arc snaps across a valley, an aerostat will often catch the edge of the flash; the entire organism glows with a blinding, incandescent turquoise light for hours, humming at a pitch that makes the teeth of every creature on the mountain ache.

Down in the deepest, dark valleys, where the boiled water surges out of the vent-pipes before freezing into razor-edged sheets of translucent glass, the **cylinder-worms** form solid walls of flesh and bronze. They are anchored to the basalt by root-systems that have grown directly into the atomic lattice of the iron-shale. Their bodies are thick as steamship boilers, ringed with heavy bands of dark, sclerotized chitin that prevent them from bursting under the immense internal pressure required to pump boiling water against the weight of the freezing air. 

Inside these cylinder-worms, vast colonies of chemosynthetic microbes—organisms that feed not on carbon, but on dissolved sulfur, iron, and liquid gas—transform the boiled mineral broth into rich, heavy fats. The worms do not die of old age; they grow thicker and more heavily armored until the rock around them shifts, or until a basalt piston beneath them rises, shearing them off at the base with a sound like a snapping cable. When a cylinder-worm is severed, its pressurized contents do not spill; they flash-freeze instantly into a towering, red-and-gold pillar of mineral ice, which the surrounding clinkers immediately descend upon, scraping it down to the stone within days.

Nothing is wasted here, but nothing is soft enough to be forgotten. When an animal dies, it does not decompose. It oxidizes. Its metallic plates tarnish, flake, and crumble into rust-dust under the fierce sun; its internal fluids evaporate into petroleum vapors that feed the cloud-engines above; its iron skeleton remains, a rigid, gleaming scaffold until the next swarm of clinkers grinds it down and feeds it back into the hungry, metallic pulse of the mountain.
