Underneath the thick velvet of the moss, where the freezing water coats the tallow-smooth basalt, life does not start with bone or blood. It starts with the rot.

The sour-sweet smell hanging in the stagnant air comes from a dense, fibrous mat of pale mold—a subterranean mycelium that does not grow in threads, but in tough, rubbery sheets the color of skimmed milk. It coats the undersides of the moss carpet, gluing it to the stone. It feeds on the microscopic dust of iron rust, lime, and organic detritus blown down the slate chimney during seasonal storms above. This mold is the floor of the hollow’s world; it holds the cold moisture like a sponge and vibrates continuously, its white skin rippling in tiny, perfect concentric rings beneath the force of the air-roar.

Navigating this pale skin are tiny, translucent flatworms, no longer than a pine needle, devoid of eyes or pigment. Their bodies are so clear that the dark grey silt they swallow is visible as a fine thread running through their centers. They do not swim; they slide through the film of icy water that coats every surface, moving in response to the deep hum in the rock. Beside them, in the dark joints where the basalt pillars meet the floor, cluster colonies of blind, wingless springtails—snow-white insects the size of salt grains that gather in thousands along the water's edge, rafts of living dust drifting across the black, glass-clear pools.

It is these insects that sustain the toad wedged in the oak sluice, but the toad is an outsider, an accidental prisoner that fell down the slate cleft seasons ago and adapted to the slowness. The true master of the hollow lies deeper, down where the floor curves into the shallow, interlocking bowls carved by the ancient water.

There, beneath the thickest blankets of liverwort and liver-colored silt, live the *Blind Carvers*—though there is no one left to name them.

They are long, ribless creatures, thick as a man’s forearm, with smooth, tubed bodies of pale, gristly cartilage and water-logged flesh. They look like a hybrid of lamprey and subterranean salamander, but they have no legs, no fins, and no eyes. Their skin is a dull, pearlescent white, so thin in places that the slow, cold pumping of their colorless lymph can be seen beneath it. 

There are three of them in the hollow. They rarely move more than a few inches in a day. 

Their entire existence is bound to the sound. Lacking lungs, they do not breathe air in gulps; instead, their flanks are ribbed with delicate, feathery frills of gill-like tissue that hang in the wet air and icy mist. The violent vibration of the air—the mountain-grinding roar from the slot in the far wall—physically shakes these frills, forcing fluid through their bodies without the need for a fast-hot heart. The sound is their pulse. If the stream above were ever to dry, if the roar were to drop to a whisper, the blood in their veins would stall, and they would slowly solidify into cold lard.

When they do move, it is with a sickening, liquid grace. A wedge-shaped head of dense, calcified bone—featureless except for two sensitive, fleshy barbels that feel the stone—pushes slowly under the moss. They scrape the tallow-smooth basalt with rows of tiny, horn-like plates lining their undersides, raspers that lift the thin film of iron-eating bacteria and wet mold from the rock. 

It was these creatures, and others like them before, that smoothed the basalt bowls over ten thousand years. The ancient water laid the course, but the slavering, mineral-rich mucus of the carvers finished the work, polishing the stone until it felt like fat.

And it was for these creatures that the human hands came.

The oak sluice and the charred trough were not built to keep the water out, nor to fetch it for drinking. Centuries ago, those who braved the chimney recognized what accumulated in the wake of the carvers. The creatures exude a thick, yellow-white lipid as they graze—a cold, heavy grease that coats their skin to protect them from the lime-rich water. Collected in the wooden trough, this tallow burned with a steady, smokeless, cold blue flame, and never went rancid. The oak drop-gate was designed to pool the water into the upper bowl, trapping the carvers in the shallow basins where they could be easily scraped with slate knives, their protective grease harvested before they were pushed back into the dark.

The humans are long gone, their oak gate rotted to the consistency of chilled marrow, but the carvers remain. They have outlived the tools, the fire-shapers, and the language of the people who hunted them.

One of them rests now near the western wall. Its tail is coiled around the submerged base of the dry-stone wall, its pale head rested in the saucer-sized depression where the single drop of water falls every twenty minutes. When the drop strikes the water, a tiny shockwave travels through the basin. The carver’s barbels twitch once—a slow, blind acknowledgment—before it settles back into the roar, its skin shivering in rhythm with the trembling air, cold, fat, and utterly indifferent to the light it will never see.
