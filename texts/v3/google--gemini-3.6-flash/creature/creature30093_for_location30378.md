To speak of the inhabitants of this place is to misunderstand where the building ends and the living begin. There are no eyes watching from the deep shadows of the cantilevered terraces; no lungs draw in the ozone-heavy steam; no hearts beat in the hollow chasms.

Yet the node is far from dead. What lives here is a single, distributed organism—a vast, phytometabolic entity that has woven itself so thoroughly into the obsidian geometry that the city and the garden are one and the same body.

If you look closely at the pale, translucent ferns that cling to the forty-story pylons, you realize they are not merely opportunistic weeds. Their fronds are structured like acoustic diaphragms, composed of living silica and flexible, glassy membranes. They do not seek sunlight, for there is none; instead, they feast on the acoustic violence of the chamber. When the subterranean pressure valves open and the pitch of the roaring water shifts, millions of these glass fronds flex in unison. They convert the kinetic thrum of the reverberating air and the deep, chest-shaking hum of the hidden conduits into chemical energy, pulsing with a faint, violet fluorescence whenever the sound reaches a crescendo. They are the ears and the lungs of the chamber, drinking sound and exhaling pure, cold mist.

Below them, wrapping the crisp, unmarred edges of the alloy walkways, is the moss. It presents as a simple, emerald cushion, but it operates as a hydraulic cortex. It is a single, contiguous nervous system spanning thousands of square meters of vertical wall. The moss does not dig root-holes—it cannot break the impossible alloy—but instead extends micro-tubular cilia into the microscopic, engineered pores of the metal. Through these connections, it trades ions with the architecture. It senses the approach of high-velocity water currents milliseconds before they hit, flattening its tiny blades to create a frictionless, hydro-phobic track that guides the sheeting liquid into the acoustic grates without a single erratic splash. Where the moss is thickest, the alloy glows brightest; it feeds directly on the cyan light-bands beneath the floor plates, drawing power upward to fuel its relentless, hyper-pure growth.

Higher up, hanging in heavy, dripping curtains from the razor-sharp structural ribs, are the tendrils. They are thick as cables, pale ivory near their roots and deepening to a dark, saturated turquoise at their tips. These are the long-distance vascular lines of the botanical collective. They creep along the underside of bridges and drop down into the central void, swinging gently in the warm draft created by the falling water. They act as sensory bridges between the city blocks, transferring water, light-borne chemical signals, and trace minerals from the drenched upper tiers down to the dark, lightless drainage shafts below. 

Every part of this botanical apparatus moves, though not at the speed of an animal. It moves at the speed of pressure and resonance. When a wave of warm vapor rolls through the atrium, the broad leaves of the tendrils uncurl like opening valves, exposing dark, absorbent undersides to catch the moisture. When the vertical cylinder of water at the center of the atrium pulses, a corresponding ripple of pale, cyan bioluminescence travels outward through the root networks, cascading down the faces of the towers in a slow, silent wave of light.

Nothing rots here because nothing is discarded. A dead leaf does not fall and decay; it is immediately reabsorbed by the living mat beneath it, dissolved by specialized, non-acidic enzymes that leave no residue on the pristine alloy. The air smells intensely of crushed leaves and ozone precisely because the metabolism of this place is so fiercely efficient, burning through organic matter the moment it ceases to serve the hydrologic balance.

The node has no master, no citizens, and no fauna. But it is not abandoned. The obsidian metropolis is the skeleton; the roaring, managed water is the blood; and the dense, crystalline flora is the flesh. Together, they form an unceasing, self-maintaining life form—an immortal, silent organism that sits in the hot, wet darkness beneath the world, endlessly drinking its own rain, listening to its own roar, and growing forever immaculate.
