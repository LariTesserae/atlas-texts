To live here is to be made of light materials and hard surfaces. In a world without dirt to burrow in or stone to lean against, life has surrendered all damp, heavy softness. What survives on the timber-plateau is either armored against the glare or light enough to ride the heat that burns off it.

### The Acoustic Swarms

The bronze-armored arthropods—the *stridulators*—are the true lords of the flues. They are not mindless pests; they are acoustic cartographers. Their bodies are built like heavy, low-slung shield-vaults, lined with internal chambers of dry, hollow air. Each species has evolved belly-ribs tuned to specific densities of timber: some prefer the dense, late-wood rings of the eastern plateaus; others seek out the soft, porous spring-wood troughs.

They gather on the high, vertical flutes of the hollow spires by the tens of thousands. When a drum-beetle strikes its underside against the dead ironwood, the vibration travels down the trunk and radiates through the floor for miles. They use the flues as mega-phones to declare territories, map out thermal updrafts, and signal the approach of moisture-bearing cloud caps. 

To walk near a flue is to feel their song in the soles of one's feet before hearing it in the ears. It is a dry, rhythmic thrumming—a mechanical machine-like roar that rattles the teeth. Beneath their heavy bronze carapaces, their blood is a thick, amber-colored resin that does not boil under the noon sun. When a beetle dies, its shell does not rot; it cures in the wind, turning into a hollow, iridescent brass bell that stays hooked to the bark for decades until a gale knocks it down to clatter across the plain.

### The Deep-Grained Herbivores

Far out on the featureless expanses, where the wind scours the floor down to dark, polished grain, move the *plow-palps*. They are massive, slow-moving beasts the size of oxen, though they possess no bones. Their skeletons are external—thick plates of compressed, horn-like cuticle, the color of weathered teak.

They have no teeth or eyes in the conventional sense. Instead, their wide, blunt heads are equipped with broad, chisel-shaped palps made of dense keratin. They spend their days inching forward along the grain-ridges, scraping off the thin, brittle crust of sulfur-yellow lichen that blooms in the sun. The sound of a herd grazing is like a hundred carpenters working with dull hand-planes: a steady, rasping *skritch-skritch-skritch* that never pauses.

Because water is scarce, the plow-palps do not sweat or urinate. Their digestive systems are furnaces of dry distillation; they extract moisture directly from the lichen’s cell walls and excrete dry, dense blocks of compressed fiber. These fecal blocks, baked hard by the heat, are the only loose "stones" on the continent. They lie scattered in long, straight trails across the timber, used by other creatures as landmarks or windbreaks.

### The Cloud-Catchers and the Flue-Dwellers

In the air above the spires, where the heat thermals shimmer like liquid glass, live the ribbon-tailed gliders. Their bodies are no thicker than a weaver's shuttle, wrapped in skin like oil-cured parchment. They have no feathers; their wings are broad, translucent membranes stretched between elongated finger-bones, so thin that the red veins within them glow when they pass before the sun.

They rarely land on the flat timber floor, where their long, trailing tail-ribbons would catch on the grain-ridges and leave them vulnerable. Instead, they live in the endless air, swooping down to pluck drill-mouthed borers from the walls of the spires or diving into the hollow interiors to drink from the condensate wells. Their call is a dry, snapping *whir-click*, produced by popping the joints of their wing-struts in flight.

Deep inside the flues, in the wet, perpetual dusk of the interior wells, a darker ecology exists. Here, away from the sun, live the blind, long-limbed *pitch-spinners*. They are pale, skeletal arachnids that feed on gliders that drop into the darkness. Instead of silk, they draw long, sticky threads of raw dammar resin from the weeping walls, weaving golden, viscous nets across the mouth of the water-bowls. Their eyes have vanished, replaced by sensitive, feathery antennae that read the acoustic hum of the beetles drumming on the outer hull.

### The Grain-Walkers

And then there are those who build shelters in the splits of the spires. They are called *people*, though their bodies have yielded to the demands of the wood until they seem less like soft flesh and more like root-wood given legs.

They are long-limbed and thin-chested, built to minimize surface area against the sun. Their skin is the deep, scorched brown of burnt walnut, coated in a fine, natural oil that smells faintly of turpentine and cedar. It does not bleed easily; their blood is viscous, dark, and quick to clot, sealing wounds like tree sap before the arid air can steal their moisture.

#### Biology and Adaptation
*   **The Feet:** They walk barefoot, but their soles are not soft skin. They are thick, dark pads of calloused horn, lined with microscopic ridge-patterns that lock into the fibrous wood-grain like grease-less gear teeth. They do not slip, even when running down a forty-five-degree incline of polished bark. Their toes are long and flexible, capable of gripping the narrow rim of a borer-hole or holding a carving-chisel while the hands operate a mallet.
*   **The Sight:** Their eyes are narrow slits, shielded by heavy, bony brows and protected from the blinding glare by thin, tinted visors cut from clear resin galls. They see the world not in terms of color, but in terms of *grain-flow*, *stress-lines*, and *checking*. Where a stranger sees a flat floor, a Grain-Walker sees the subtle, dangerous crowning of a wood-plate about to snap under thermal stress.
*   **Speech and Sight-Burn:** Because the roar of the drum-beetles makes speech impossible within leagues of a flue, they have no spoken language for daily use. Instead, they communicate through a rapid, tactile finger-drumming against each other’s arms, or through high-pitched, resonant whistles that slice clean through the insect noise. For long-distance messages, they use *sight-burn*: using convex resin lenses to scorch quick, glowing sequences of dark glyphs into the sun-bleached bark of distant spires—dots and dashes of carbonized wood that remain readable for decades.

#### Culture and Material Life
They own nothing that cannot be fashioned from the forest’s ancient bones. 

Their shelters are aerodynamic pods hung inside the heat-cracks of the flues, shaped like giant weaver-bird nests. The outer shells are shingles of peeled ironwood bark, overlapped like fish scales to shed the sudden micro-rains and pinned together with pegs carved from borer-beetle mandibles. Inside, the air is cool and smells of old sap and damp cork.

Water is their currency and their holy matter. Every scrap of bark-shingle on a shelter is angled toward a central spout that drains into a resin-sealed bladder made of glider-skin. They harvest the condensate from the deep flue-wells using long, jointed poles tipped with sponge-like masses of dried vine-pith. A family’s wealth is measured by the number of sweet wells they have mapped and sealed against the pitch-spinners.

They do not use fire for heat—the world is already too hot, and to set the floor ablaze would be a suicide beyond imagination. They use fire only in miniature, controlled bursts: tiny, resin-fueled torches used to melt dammar glue, or focused sun-lenses used to harden the tips of their hunting darts.

#### Death and the Rings
Time for the Grain-Walkers is not linear; it is radial. They do not measure their age in years, but in the number of *Grain-Shifts* they have survived—the great midday thermal snaps that echo across the continent. 

When a Grain-Walker dies, they are not buried; there is no earth to receive them. Instead, they are taken to the highest windward fissure of a Choked Flue, where the high-altitude draft is strongest. Their limbs are bound with vine-gut, their joints set in the posture of a runner, and their skin is rubbed with concentrated dammar resin. 

Over weeks in the hot, dry gales, the body does not decay. It mummifies, turning dark, hard, and translucent, until it is indistinguishable from the ironwood spire itself. Generations of ancestors stand tucked into the flutes of the great chimneys, their amber-beaded eyes staring out over the boundless timber plain, slowly being turned to stone by the sun, waiting for the continent to crack beneath them once again.
