It does not disturb the balance of the vault; it is another surface upon which the steam condenses.

To find it, you would have to feel along the basalt fins where they press closest together, where the stone leaves a gap no wider than a man’s ribcage. There, suspended off the wet floor, wedged horizontally between two rising blades of black rock, rests a body.

It is human in its architecture, but stripped of everything required by the sky. 

Its skin is the color of boiled bone—a pale, translucent slate completely lacking in melanin, so thin in places that the dark network of veins shows through like grey leaf-skeletons. Yet where it touches the rock, the hide has transformed. The palms of its hands, the undersides of its forearms, its knees, and the soles of its long, flexible feet are covered in thick, yellowish pads of dense callus, as tough and fibrous as shoe-leather. These calluses are grooved and scarred, mapped precisely to the sharp geometry of the chamber. It can slide a bare palm over an edge of freshly shattered obsidian without bleeding; its flesh has memorized where the glass yields and where it cuts.

It has no hair. The warm, damp air renders body hair redundant, and so its scalp, brows, and body are entirely smooth, polished by constant steam and the gentle rubbing of stone. 

Its eyes are still present, but they are milky and sunken behind thick, heavy lids that rarely open. The irises have faded to a pale, washed-out hazel, the pupils permanently blown wide, searching for a light that ceased to exist for its lineage generations ago. They are vestigial windows. It does not use them to see.

Instead, its face is broad and mobile, dominated by a wide, flat nose with flared nostrils that draw in the mineral steam in slow, rhythmic cycles. Its ears are unusually large, the cartilage thin and shell-like, turned slightly forward to catch the acoustic geography of the room. 

Watch it awaken—not from sleep, but from the long, cold-blooded torpor it enters to conserve energy between the drops.

It begins with a shift in breathing. A deep, wet inhalation that rattles softly in its chest, drawing the iron-hot vapor deep into lung-sacks that have adapted to process oxygen from air that is nearly water. 

Then, movement. It does not rise; it *unfolds*. It moves with a strange, oily slowness, sliding down the basalt channel with the silent friction of wet rubber. It does not reach out blindly or fumble. Its fingers—long, thin, with fingernails worn down to flat, hard nubs—flicker out to touch specific points on the rock with the lightness of an insect’s antennae. 

It knows every razor-blade fan of obsidian by name, if it had words for names. To this creature, the hall is not dark; it is a complex, three-dimensional web of textures, temperatures, and pitches. 

It navigates by sound and touch alone. The deep *thump* of the water drop striking the clay bowl is its fixed star, its cardinal north. By the pitch of that sound, it knows its exact distance from the ledge. The high *ping* off the obsidian ledge tells it where the roof-needles end.

It moves toward the clay bowl. 

Its posture is stooped, the spine permanently curved from a lifetime spent navigating low stone needles and tight basalt slots. When it reaches the flat shelf, it does not use the wooden ladder—the ladder was built by the one who came before, the one who still remembered the sky and needed rungs to climb. This creature climbs the rock face directly, using its toes to grip micro-cracks in the basalt that a surface-dweller could barely see with a lens.

At the shelf, it kneels. Its large, calloused hands reach out, framing the clay bowl without touching it, feeling the radiant cool of the collected water against its palms. 

It drinks. It does not scoop the water with its hands. It lowers its mouth directly to the surface tension skin, dipping its pale lips into the pool and sipping with a tiny, rhythmic lapping sound. It takes only what it needs—a few swallows of heavy, iron-flavored water—leaving the equilibrium of the bowl unbroken.

Its diet is a quiet, steady harvesting of the vault’s slow life. After drinking, it glides toward the rougher basalt walls where the pale, velvet microbial skin grows thickest. From a tiny pouch made of dried root-fiber tied around its waist, it produces one of the flat flint chisels—the edge long blunted by decades of scrape-work. With practiced, delicate motions, it scrapes the gelatinous film from the stone, gathering the grey slime onto the flat blade and lifting it to its mouth. The film is rich in sulfur-eating bacteria, metallic salts, and moisture; it tastes of warm blood and old brass, but to the creature, it is life.

Occasionally, its long fingers brush against the translucent crustacean gliding along the glass blades. It does not crush the small creature. It gently lifts it, feeling the tiny, dry tickle of its legs against its horned palm, checking its weight, its shell hardness, before setting it back upon the slime-mat. It eats them only when they are spent, when their tiny movements slow to a halt at the edge of the water-bowls.

It has no language, for there is no one to speak to. But it has a voice. 

Once every few hours, or perhaps once every few days—time has no meaning in the steam—it leans its chest against the main basalt dike, opens its mouth, and produces a low, humming drone from deep within its throat. 

The pitch is precisely tuned to the resonance of the stone chamber. The sound does not bounce; it vibrates through the rock itself, traveling up the basalt fins, through the hanging mineral needles, and down into the deep geothermal veins below. The creature rests its forehead against the cold stone and listens to the answer: the tiny, microscopic micro-fractures of the mountain settling in the heat, the deep, heavy throb of the earth miles beneath its feet. 

Satisfied that the world remains sealed, warm, and dark, it pulls itself back into its narrow vertical crevice. 

It wedges its pale, soft body between the sharp fins, letting the warm stone support its spine, its hips, its heavy head. It folds its long arms across its chest. The steam gathers immediately on its skin, forming beads that tremble, merge, and slide down its belly to fall onto the rock below.

*Plink.*

The creature closes its milky eyes. Its breath slows until it is indistinguishable from the soft *hiss* of the walls. It waits. It does not dream of sun, or trees, or sky, for it knows only the geometry of stone, the taste of iron, and the perpetual, warm mercy of the dark.
