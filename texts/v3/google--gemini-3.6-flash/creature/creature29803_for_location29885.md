To look at the ten stacked tiers of dry-stacked shale and lard-wood is to see no one at first, for those who inhabit the rift are built of its exact palette: slate, dark timber, and the pale, silvered grey of quartz. 

They are a small, wide-chested people, rarely standing higher than a man’s ribcage, but built with an unnatural, dense heaviness. Their bones are thick and solid as ironwood, giving them a low, unshakeable center of gravity ideal for living on ledge-edges. Their skin is smooth, hairless, and the color of wet river pebble—a soft, cool grey that sheds the relentless condensation without chilling them. They do not sweat; instead, their skin secretes a faint, scentless oil that keeps the damp from sinking into their muscles.

Because the rift’s air swallows sound like wet wool, they have long since abandoned the open throat. They speak, when they must, in low, resonant chest-clicks and nasal hums—frequencies that do not rely on air travel, but instead vibrate through stone and wood. A mother calls her child not by shouting across the five-foot gap between balconies, but by striking a flat palm against the lard-wood railing. The vibration travels cleanly down the damp timber tier, felt through the soles of the child’s feet three houses away. 

For longer, more complex exchanges, their language is entirely visual and tactile. It is composed of swift, precise finger-taps against the forearm, the dilation of their wide, dark-ringed pupils, and the slight, deliberate tilt of a shoulder. Across the narrow lanes, two neighbours can conduct a long, intricate debate about the repair of a roof-slate without producing a sound louder than the rustle of their sleeves.

They wear heavy, close-fitting garments made from the felted fibers of cold-ferns and the tough, water-repellent skins of deep-cleft eels. These clothes are dyed in soot, ochre, and wet-bark brown, rubbed with tallow to keep the ambient chill from penetrating. On their feet are thick, soft-soled slippers constructed from pressed moss and layered hide. They walk on the balls of their feet, their toes wide and splayed, gripping the smooth, basin-worn steps with the easy confidence of goats on a cliff. When they move along the narrow lard-wood walkways, they produce no sound at all—only the faint, rhythmic *shuck-shuck* of oiled leather sleeves brushing against oiled leather flanks.

Their physiology is attuned to the rift's stark, heatless zenith-light. Their eyes are large, set deep beneath prominent bone brows that shield them from the vertical glare. The irises are a pale, milky silver, capable of tracking movement in the blinding reflection off saturated quartz, yet instantly adjusting to the dim, damp interiors of the shale houses behind the skin-curtained windows.

Because the rift is a closed, motionless ecosystem, fire is strictly forbidden. A single wood fire would fill the narrow stone throat with lingering, un-blown smoke and disturb the cold equilibrium that preserves their timbers. Instead, their life is entirely cold-cured. They eat raw, salted river fish, blind crayfish plucked from the stone troughs, and a dense, sour bread made from dried fern-rhizome flour fermented in wet clay pots. For heat, they rely on their own slow, incredibly efficient metabolisms and thick, hand-woven blankets of downy bird-fur, plucked gently from the plum-sized nesting birds that share their eaves.

Their primary work is not building—for the city was finished centuries ago—but *preserving*. They are meticulous, silent maintainers. 

In every household, there is at least one person whose sole duty is the care of the mortar-roots. Armed with fine bone needles and small bone picks, they sit for hours on the damp ledges, weaving the tender white root-tips of ash and fern into the micro-fissures between the shale blocks. They guide the roots, trimming those that grow too fat and burst the stone, encouraging those that bind the tiers to the living rock wall. Others walk the wooden scaffolding with pots of rended crayfish fat, methodically rubbing every beam, post, and lard-wood balcony to ensure the cured wood never rots, never warps, and never dries out.

They do not measure time by the sun, which sits permanently above them like a burning coin, nor by seasons, which never reach the lowland shelf. They measure time by the slow, millimetric deepening of the basins in the stone steps, and by the lifespans of the jade moss carpets. 

They are a patient, unhurried populace. In a world without wind, sound, or thermal flux, their movements have taken on the rhythmic, eternal quality of the dripping water around them. A man may sit on a slate balcony for three hours, perfectly still, his slate-colored hands folded in his lap, his pale eyes tracking the slow, three-inch march of a yellow-spotted salamander across the opposite wall. To an outsider, he might appear frozen, a statue carved to match the town. But he is merely living at the speed of the rift—silent, bright, cold, and entirely at rest.
