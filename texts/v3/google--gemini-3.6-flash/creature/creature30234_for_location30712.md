To live here is to be soft-boned and velvet-footed. In a world without stone, hardness is an inefficiency; weight is a hazard. Everything that moves across the wet, petrified heartwood has been molded by the same slow humidity that turns fallen leaves into lacework and softens the pull of the abyss.

### The Splicers

They are the descendants of those who drove the first bone mauls, though they no longer remember what a hammer was, nor do they possess the thumbs to grip one. 

They are slender, six-limbed beings whose skeletons are made not of mineralized bone, but of dense, springy cartilage that bends without snapping when a beam flexes under a sudden shifting of weight. Their skin is smooth and grey-white, covered in a hydrophobic, velvet-like down that repels the constant seepage of the wood. They have no lungs as surface beasts do; instead, they draw the thick, wet air through wide, lace-curtained spiracles along their flanks, making their breathing as silent as the drifting vapor.

Their upper limbs have evolved into delicate, multi-jointed implements. The outer digits are tipped with flat, spade-like nails of thick keratin, used to scrape away patches of rot before it can reach the load-bearing grain. Their inner digits produce a thick, amber-colored latex from glands in the palms. When a joint groans—when an ancient dowel three miles long begins to shear under the weight of a water-logged burl—the Splicers gather in the dim twilight. In total silence, they press their palms to the stress-fractures, weeping their natural resin into the wound, then pressing living moss into the gap to bind it.

They do not speak with sound. The air is too dense, the distances too wide. Instead, they press their flat, sensitive chests against the grain of the great beams and strike the wood with the hard keratin of their knees. They communicate in sub-vocal thrums—pulses of vibration that travel ten miles through the solid heartwood, carrying news of rot, of water-spheres forming in the bays, or of the slow, majestic collapse of a forgotten archway.

They walk everywhere—on the tops of the beams, along the sheer sides of carved drainage troughs, and upside down along the undersides of the trusses, their splayed, suction-padded feet finding traction in the wet liverworts. To a Splicer, "up" and "down" are mere options; they orient themselves entirely by the direction of the grain.

### The Cable-Spinners

Where the ancient builders tied beams together with oak-thick braided cordage, the original rope has long since rotted into rich, dark compost. In its place live the *Cable-Spinners*.

These are immense, slow-moving organisms, vaguely resembling blind, soft-shelled lobsters, but grown to the size of riverboats. They spend centuries huddled in the massive elbow-joints where four or five trunk-spars meet. They do not hunt; they feed entirely on the bioluminescent gelatinous molds that digest the wood’s outer bark and the nutrient-dense sap that weeps from the spliced joins.

In exchange for this food, the Spinners perform the work of the long-lost rope. From spinnerets near their tails, they extrude thick, fibrous tendrils of liquid silk that harden upon contact with the misty air. As the great wooden frame sways and stretches, the Spinners continuously weave these tendrils around the joints, wrapping them in thousands of tight, pale bands that are stronger than forged iron. A joint inhabited by a family of Spinners becomes a swollen, white-wrapped burl—a living splice that grows thicker and tighter with every passing generation.

When a Spinner dies, its shell does not crumble. The surrounding mold quickly hollows out its soft interior, leaving behind a hard, chitinous chamber tied into the very ligaments of the world—a dry, safe cavern where the Splicers lay their rubbery, translucent eggs.

### The Drift-Bells and Sphere-Nymphs

In the mile-wide hollows between the structural bays, where the architecture gives way to warm mist and degraded gravity, an entirely untethered ecology exists.

The most magnificent of these are the *Drift-Bells*. They are translucent, umbrella-shaped creatures, twenty paces across, made almost entirely of trapped water and fibrous mucus. They float in the amber-tinted fog, completely detached from the wood, buoyed by the warm updrafts rising from the dark, unseen depths below. They move by rhythmic, slow contractions of their pale margins, trailing long, feathery tendrils that comb the air for seed-flyers, spore-clouds, and tiny, wingless gliders that have lost their grip on the higher beams.

Within the floating water-spheres—the house-sized globes of wetness suspended in mid-air by the world’s confused gravity—live the *Sphere-Nymphs*. 

These are pale, serpentine creatures with skin like polished glass and wide, shimmering fins made of thin membrane. They are born in the wet moss of the beams, but as larvae, they propel themselves off the lip of a beam into the void, aiming for the nearest suspended sphere. Once inside, they spend decades swimming in perpetual circles within their self-contained, mid-air ponds, feeding on microscopic larvae and the water-moss that coats the sphere’s outer skin. 

To the Nymphs, the universe is a circle of warm liquid floating in a sky of amber vapor. Occasionally, when two spheres drift too close and coalesce into a single, roaring, floating lake, the Nymphs engage in frantic, shimmering rituals of mating before the new, larger sphere settles back into its silent, buoyant equilibrium.

### The Rot-Gazers

Deep inside the dark mortises—in the vast, hollow sockets where pillar-trees three miles tall were forced into carved heartwood holes before history began—lives the oldest thing in the frame.

The *Rot-Gazers* are not mobile. They are vast, colonial networks of pale, optic filaments and fibrous ganglia that have grown into the very cellular structure of the petrified beams. They have no mouths, no limbs, and no consciousness in the human sense. Instead, they are the sensory nervous system of the wooden continent.

Where a beam is healthy, the Rot-Gazer’s embedded threads remain dormant, translucent, and cold. But where rot takes hold—where anaerobic bacteria begin to turn the ancient heartwood into soft, black slush—the Rot-Gazer’s filaments swell and glow with a deep, pulsing indigo light. 

This light shines through the wet moss and the bark, casting long, eerie purple gradients across the twilight paths. It is a beacon. Seeing the indigo glow, the Splicers gather from miles around, carrying mouthfuls of dry resin and spore-mats to scrape, seal, and cauterize the infection before it can weaken the truss. 

The Rot-Gazers do not care about the Splicers, nor do the Splicers worship the Rot-Gazers. It is simply a reflex of the world: the wood feels a pain, it glows, and the hands that are no longer hands come to soothe it.

### The Endemic Silence

Nothing here screams. Nothing calls for a mate across the abyss with a harsh voice. The atmosphere is too heavy, too damp; loud sound dies quickly, muffled by the wet liverworts and the dense, fibrous air.

The predators—for there are predators—are things of silent velocity. Pale, flat-bodied gliders with wide, skin-flap wings drop from the undersides of high trusses, falling through a mile of fog without a whisper, using their wide, lidless black eyes to pick out the movement of a grub or a young Splicer. They strike without a sound, clinging to their prey with hooked, cartilaginous ribs, then riding the warm updrafts back up to a dark mortise to feed in the dripping quiet.

It is a world that has forgotten the earth, forgotten the sun, and forgotten the stone tools that brought it into being. Everything that lives here is an extension of the carpentry—a soft, wet, slow-moving thought in the endless, living mind of the wood.
