Look closer at the fog. What at first appears to be suspended droplets of moisture churning in the glare is, in places, alive.

The air itself is populated. In the gravity-warped pockets around the three matte-black needles, where the steam spirals upward in wet threads, drift entities that have no need for legs or surfaces. They are *spindle-jellies*—translucent, bell-shaped masses of calcified membrane no larger than a human thumb, light as ash. They are held aloft entirely by the violent thermal updrafts and the local gravity distortions. They have no eyes or nervous systems in any familiar sense; instead, their glass-thin bodies are embedded with microscopic quartz grains that resonate with the high, bone-rattling whistle of the ceiling needles. This vibration keeps their internal fluids in a state of perpetual, low-frequency agitation, preventing their delicate tissues from cooking in the heat. 

They feed on the air. As the sourceless white light scatters through the steam, it illuminates thick clouds of aerosolized cyanobacteria, torn from the rock walls by the force of the geysers. The spindle-jellies drift through these blinding mists with open, funnel-like maws, filtering the light-fed bacteria through lattices of vitreous thread. When they die, their silicate bodies harden instantly in the scalding air, falling like tiny, translucent rain into the terraced basins below.

Down in the sloshing basins, that mineral rain becomes the primary material for something much larger.

The terraced rimstone pools are not merely geological formations; they are constructed reefs, built and maintained by *litho-crawlers*. These are broad, low-slung creatures the size of dinner plates, shaped like heavy, rounded anvils. They possess no soft, exposed flesh. Their backs are coated in thick, white travertine deposits—an armored carapace grown from the very minerals dissolved in the hot water. Beneath these living stone shells, a muscular, heat-resistant foot secretes an acidic mucus that etches deep grooves into the basalt floor, anchoring them against the violent, heavy *thwack* of the geysers.

When a jet of superheated water strikes a litho-crawler, it does not dislodge it. The creature leans into the blast, opening a row of hinged, calcified plates along its flanks. The scalding water rushes through its internal gut chambers, where specialized bio-membranes strip out dissolved copper, quartz, and heavy metals. What the crawler excretes is the smooth, pale rimstone that forms the lips of the terraces. The labyrinth of shallow pools exists because these creatures have spent thousands of years directing the water’s flow, building dams to trap the heat and moisture they need to survive.

They share the terraced pools with the glass-shelled isopods, though the relationship is not peaceful. Moving through the deepest, two-foot-deep gutters carved into the floor is the chamber’s apex inhabitant: the *piezocast*.

The piezocast is long, segmented, and pale as boiled bone, resembling a cross between a mantis shrimp and a hydraulic piston. It has evolved to hunt in an environment where vision is useless and sound is a deafening roar. It senses its world through piezoelectric plates along its spine that convert the chamber’s intense sonic hum into precise spatial awareness. 

It does not swim; it crawls along the gutters using three pairs of thick, horn-like legs tipped with diamond-hard mineral points that grip the slick, melted basalt. To hunt the isopods on the glass conduit or the litho-crawlers on the pool edges, the piezocast uses an internal steam-piston system. It draws the scalding basin water into a reinforced chest cavity, uses its own metabolic heat and muscle compression to flash-boil the liquid, and vents it through a narrow frontal siphon with explosive force. The resulting shockwave—a wet, localized cavitation pop—cracks the glass shells of the isopods instantly or blasts a litho-crawler free from its anchor grooves, sending it tumbling into the boiling gutter where the piezocast’s heavy mandibles wait.

Yet the most bizarre life in the vault is not crawling in the water or floating in the air. It has integrated itself directly into the ancient machinery.

Hanging beneath the eight-foot-thick glass conduit, completely submerged in the blinding glare, is a colony of *siphon-polyps*. They look like clusters of dark, flexible tubing, branching out like the exposed wiring of an engine. Over centuries, their rasping, metal-chewing radulas have bored micrometer-thin holes through the pitted, bone-like surface of the glass pipe. They do not eat the pipe itself; they tap the luminescent blue slurry pulsing inside it.

Rooted deep into the glass, the polyps drink the frantic, uneven energy of the forgotten reactor below. Their translucent bodies glow with a deep, liquid sapphire light that pulses in precise, eerie rhythm with the conduit’s internal heartbeat. They act as metabolic converters: they process the exotic, synthetic compounds in the blue fluid, exuding a warm, oily, copper-scented slime that coats the underside of the arch. 

This slime is the sole food source for the golden cyanobacteria and liverwort, which bloom in lush, velvet curtains wherever the polyps drip. In turn, the isopods graze on these curtains, the litho-crawlers feed on the dying microbes swept down into the pools, and the spindle-jellies catch the airborne spores thrown up by the steam.

Nothing leaves the chamber. Nothing enters it. The life here does not adapt to the environment; it is an extension of the engine’s function. If the black needles were to stop their whistle, if the glass pipe were to cease its frantic blue pulse, the air would cool, the gravity would flatten, the steam would drop, and every shell, jelly, and stone-backed crawler would collapse into silent, calcified sediment, waiting beneath thirty meters of desert hardpan for a power source that will never be turned off.
