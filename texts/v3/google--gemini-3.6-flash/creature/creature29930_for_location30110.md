In a place where the distinction between engine and ecosystem has dissolved, life does not merely inhabit the basin—it participates in the mechanical cycle. The creatures here are heavy-lunged, slow-pacing, and slicked with moisture, adapted to a world where the weather is measured in pounds per square inch and the soil is made of roots and engine grease.

***

At the base of the food web are the **grease-molds and tallow-fungi**. They do not grow on decaying wood, but along the hot, cast-iron undersides of the cylinder housings and around the brass packing glands. They appear as thick, rubbery mats of charcoal-grey and sulfur-yellow suede, thriving where cylinder oil and condensing steam mix. They feed on the heavy hydrocarbons, breaking down the lubricants into a sweet, rancid syrup that trickles into the moss beds below. 

Feeding on these mats are the **iron-shelled snails** noticed on the feed-water pipes. Their shells are not lime, but layered plates of dark, magnetite-infused horn, heavy enough to clink like slate when they bump against one another in the dark. They are thermophilic; they crowd where the metal is hot enough to scald human skin, scraping off the grease-mold with raspy, rasp-like tongues. Their trail is not clear slime, but a dense, black paste of pulverized scale and graphite that seals micro-fissures in the iron casing, inadvertently protecting the pipes from pitting under the constant moisture.

In the shallow, brick-lined sluices where the iridescent film of machine oil rides the current, **loaches** move like dark needles. They are blind, pale-bellied fish, no longer than a fingerspan, with thick, scaleless skins protected by a heavy coat of clear slime that repels both heat and oil. They navigate the swift, warm gutters, feeding on the larvae of **steam-flies**—tiny, silver-winged insects that hatch in the hot spray of the spillways and spend their brief adult lives tumbling through the illuminated plumes of exhaust steam like sparks above a fire.

***

Higher up, where the structural roots of the giant ficus interlock with the perforated steel catwalks, the acoustic environment dictates how larger animals live. The sound is too dense for hearing as air-borne vibration, so the basin’s larger denizens have traded sound for tremor.

**Iron-backed salamanders**, as broad as a boot and coloured like wet basalt, cling to the damp granite walls and the massive iron stanchions. They have no ears, but their throats and bellies are pressed flat against the damp metal and stone, reading the basin’s health through their flesh. They know the exact rhythm of the twelve-foot flywheels—eight thuds per minute. If a bearing runs dry or a valve sticks, the change in vibration brings them out of the moss-fur to lick the fresh, hot grease leaking from the journals. They breed in the sumps beneath the beam engines, where their jelly-like eggs adhere to the submerged rivets, warmed by the continuous waste-heat of the boilers.

In the high canopy of wild fig and overhanging oak, where the walls meet the sky, **oil-slick swifts** build nests out of moss, chewed canvas threads, and hemp packing picked from discarded gasket rings. Their dark feathers are perpetually wet, scented with ginger-root and cylinder oil. They rarely land on the basin floor, preferring to dive down through the rising plumes of steam to snatch steam-flies mid-air before shooting back up into the cooler, open sky above the rim.

***

And then there are the **Tenders**.

They are few—perhaps no more than three or four—and they are so thoroughly a part of the basin’s economy that they move with the same unhurried, eight-revolution rhythm as the beam engines. 

They are human, or descended from those who were, but their bodies have been reshaped by generations in the warm, wet sink. They are thick-set and short-limbed, with broad, flat feet that grip the slippery iron grates and soft root-carpet without stumbling. Their skin is smooth, hairless, and permanently tanned a deep, nut-brown by the humid heat and the constant soot-and-oil vapor that seals their pores. 

They do not speak. In the *chug-shuck* of the cylinders and the roar of the spillways, human voice is useless. Instead, they communicate through a complex language of tactile signs and visual cues—a slap against a resounding pipe, a double-tap on a wooden tie-rod, or a series of swift hand gestures held within the beam of light cast by a steam plume.

The Tenders are neither factory workers nor gardeners; they are something between arborists and millwrights. They carry heavy, brass-bound tallow pots, rolls of oiled canvas, and curved pruning knives made of hardened bronze that will not spark against the iron. 

Their life is a continuous round of gentle steering:
*   They groom the ficus roots, using leather straps to guide a tender young aerial root around the flange of a pump casing so that, as it thickens, it will clamp the joint tight against a steam-leak.
*   They harvest the iron-snails from cold pipes and transfer them to hot ones that need clearing.
*   They pack the stuffing boxes with fresh hemp woven from the fibrous stems of the wild ginger that grows in the wet corners.
*   They lay down new oak planks where the old decking has rotted into the root-mattress, stitching the timber into the living carpet with thick cords of green vine.

They sleep in the dry, warm hollows behind the boiler housings, on hammocks woven from fern-fiber, suspended between the warm brickwork and the granite wall. They eat the bitter, oily tubers of the ginger that grows in the sump-muck, boiled in the clean condensate caught from the drain-cock vents, along with the fat, crayfish-like crustaceans that inhabit the deep, dark sumps where the warm water leaves the basin.

The Tenders do not remember a time when the engines were installed, nor do they concept of a world beyond the granite walls where the steam does not rise. To them, the beam engines are not machines to be serviced, but massive, iron-boned hearts that pump heat and life through the garden. Should an engine falter, a Tender does not consult a manual; they lay their cheek against the vibrating cylinder, listen through the bone of their skull to the hiss of the internal steam, and prune a root or tighten a brass clamp until the rhythm recovers its steady, patient thud.

Together—the mold, the snails, the salamanders, the trees, and the silent people—they form a single, breathing organism. The iron gives the wood something to lean on; the wood keeps the iron from sinking into the mire; and the living things keep the whole vast, dripping engine turning through the centuries.
