To live here is to be constructed from the same slow, cold logic as the mountain itself. 

The people who inhabit these stacked terraces are deep-chested and low-slung, built with the broad, boxy ribcages of those whose lungs must pull twice as hard for every scrap of air. Their skin is not merely tanned, but wind-cured—dark, dense, and mapped with fine, dry cracks like old timber. Around their eyes, the skin is permanently puckered into deep fan-folds from decades of squinting against the absolute contrast of indigo skies and blinding white frost. Their irises are almost black, minimizing the burn of the high-altitude ultraviolet sun.

Movement among them is an economy of force. You will not see anyone run in this city. A sudden dash would tear at the lungs and waste precious calories; instead, they move with a low, rolling, pendulum-like stride that shifts weight smoothly from heel to broad forefoot. They step onto the ice-dusted flags with a quiet, practiced friction, their leather soles softened with fat and studded underneath with ironless wooden pegs or bone cleats. 

Because breath is a resource to be husbanded, they are a remarkably quiet people. In the open air, speech is rare. Across the narrow alleys or between upper and lower terraces, they communicate in a swift, sharp language of hand-signs and low, resonant whistles—pitches that cut through the mountain wind without demanding the air required for vocal speech. Full sentences are saved for the interiors, where the air is still, trapped, and warmed by the heat of huddling bodies and flickering tallow.

Their hands are their most remarkable feature: unusually wide across the palm, with thick, blunt-ended fingers calloused to the toughness of horn. These hands handle frozen slate without flinching, split timber along its grain with wooden wedges, and weave hemp rope until the fibers are slick with sweat and lanolin. To prevent the biting cold from cracking their skin down to the bone, they smear their exposed skin daily with a thick paste made from rendered animal fat, charcoal dust, and crushed juniper needles. It gives them a faint, resinous smell—bitter, greasy, and dark—that clings to every alleyway they pass through.

They dress in heavy, structural layers. There are no flowing garments here; loose cloth is a sail for the gale to catch and a draft for the frost to enter. Their clothing is made of thick, unbleached wool, felted until it is as stiff and windproof as hide, held tight against the body by broad leather cinches. Beneath these felt surcoats, they wear inner shirts of soft, scraped goat-skin, worn hair-side in against the body. Their boots are massive, leg-wrapping contraptions of triple-thick leather stuffed with dry alpine moss for insulation, bound tightly with braided gut.

***

Beside the humans live the *khor*—the heavy, low-slung ungulates that make human survival at this altitude possible. Neither goat nor ox, but possessing the sure-footedness of the former and the stoic endurance of the latter, these beasts are built like boulder-stones draped in shale-grey wool that sweeps the ground. Their horns are broad and flat, curving back over their skulls to protect their necks from falling debris, and their hooves are split, rubbery pads rimmed with hard, sharp keratin that can find traction on a wet ice-face no wider than a finger.

The khor do not live in separate stables; they occupy the lowest, coldest cellars of the terrace dwellings. Their massive body heat rises through the stone floorboards to warm the living quarters above. In return, the humans spend the brief, blinding hours of noon leading them along the outer cliff-paths to scrape at the yellow lichens and tough, wiry scrub-birch that cling to the sunny rock-faces. The milk of the khor is thick as tallow, yellow-tinted, and so rich in fat that it can be churned into a dry, salty cheese that never truly freezes and keeps for years in the mountain cisterns.

In the sun-lit nooks of the public plazas, curled on the warmest flagstones, lie the city's dogs. Broad-skulled and coated in dense, ivory-colored fur that mats into thick plates along their spines, they are passive, thermal anchors of the community. They are not working herders—there is nowhere for a beast to run here without falling—but sentinels and heaters. At night, children sleep buried beneath two or three of these massive, slow-breathing animals, their heavy pulses throbbing together under piles of felt. The dogs rarely bark; their warning is a low, chest-vibrating thrum that echoes through the stone walls long before a human ear can pick up the slide of shifting gravel on the peaks above.

***

The city itself is an aviary by design. When the dry-stone masons stack the dark slate to build a wall, they deliberately leave every fifth or sixth stone recessed, creating thousands of small, square tunnels that lead into the core of the masonry. 

These are homes for the mountain birds. Millions of yellow-beaked choughs and heavy-chested swallows roost within the very walls that shield the human population from the gale. The relationship is intimate and practical. The birds provide a constant, low murmur—a rustling, clicking warmth within the stone itself—and their droppings, scraped meticulously from the lower ledges by children with wooden hoes, provide the only rich fertilizer for the tiny, terraced plots of mountain-barley and bitter root-crops tucked into the warmer crevices of the lower gorge.

In the high, inaccessible crags far above the highest inhabited chimney live the snow-hawks—huge, broad-winged hunters the color of wet limestone. They are the apex of this thin vertical realm. The city dwellers view them not as pests or pets, but as indicators of the air. When the hawks drop low, flying below the level of the suspension bridges, the people know a downdraft-storm is coming from the north, and they drop the heavy felt curtains over every vent, bolt the juniper doors, and wait.

***

Inside the stone dwellings, life retreats into a dense, shared warmth. A single family unit might consist of three or four generations occupying a room no larger than a dry-goods vault, its ceiling so low that an adult must walk stooped. The air inside is thick—heavy with the smell of old wool, the sharp tang of dried meat, the oily smoke of tallow lamps, and the constant, rhythmic sound of human lungs at work.

Here, culture is tactile and slow. Without paper, their history is carved into the hard juniper beams that brace the stone ceilings—not in letters, but in deep, geometric notches smoothed by centuries of greasy fingers tracing them in the dark. A grandfather sits by the flickering grease-dish, a bone needle in his thick fingers, repair-stitching a leather water-bag with thread spun from sinew, telling stories in low, rumbling clicks that sound like the gravel shifting beneath the glaciers outside.

Nothing is discarded. A broken bone needle becomes a peg for a door frame; a worn-out boot is boiled down for its tallow grease; the carcass of a spent khor provides meat, wool, hide, horn for spoons, needles from its shins, and fuel from its dried dung.

There is no sense of struggle in their faces, for struggle implies an alternative. To these people, the sharp edge of the slate, the freezing dry air, and the absolute verticality of the world are simply the terms of existence. They live as the lichen lives on the granite face: rooted deep into the microscopic cracks, patient, cold-adapted, and entirely indifferent to the vast, silent void tumbling away beneath their feet.
