The life here does not visit. It does not migrate from the colder drop-offs beyond the slate pillars, nor does it look up toward the invisible surface. It was born of the warmth and the stone, and its entire existence is an unbroken, tactile conversation with the surfaces carved by those long-dead hands.

At the lowest level—the foundation of the ecology—is the **purple veil**. It is not a plant, nor simple slime, but an immense, sheet-like colonial animal, no thicker than a fingernail, that has grown over every square inch of the dark basalt and red terra-cotta. It is the source of the room’s dim, bruised light. Where the water is warmest, near the seams in the floor tiles, the veil glows with a faint, steady phosphorescence, a dull indigo that pulses only when stepped on or scraped. It feeds directly on the rich soup of dissolved minerals leaking from the earth beneath the tiles, turning iron, sulfur, and ancient volcanic heat into a thick, lipid-rich mucus. The veil is the room's skin; without it, the stone would be sterile, cold, and dead.

Feeding upon this veil are the **Slab-Backs**—the flat, broad isopods that crowd the niches and vaulting. They are the size of a man’s outstretched palm, clad in heavy, pale armor that has taken on the soft, salmon-pink tint of the terra-cotta beneath them. They have no eyes, no antennae, and no legs meant for running. Their limbs are short, hooked spades designed to clamp against the basalt with hydraulic force, anchoring them against even the gentlest eddy. They move in silent, agonizingly slow herds, scraping the purple veil with heavy, horny mandibles. The noise they make—that endless *clack-clack-clack*—is the sound of millions of microscopic jaws striking baked clay. They are so slow that young worms often sprout upon their carapaces, turning each wandering shell into a tiny, drifting garden of feathery turquoise gills.

Where the copper pins hold the arches, a different creature dominates. These are the **Patina-Worms**. They do not live on stone; they require the bitter, metallic bleed of oxidized copper. They are thread-thin, translucent, and long as a forearm, anchored deep within the cracks where the metal pins have swollen and split the surrounding rock. Their bodies are completely clear, revealing a central gut stained a brilliant, unnatural peacock-blue from the copper salts they digest. Their heads are nothing more than radial crowns of fine, hair-like plumes that fan out into the warm current. When a gas bubble thrums past, or when a ribbon-swimmer displaces the water with a heavy heave of its flank, the plumes snap shut in unison—a sudden, silent collapse of hundreds of blue threads—before slowly opening again like flowers in a dark, windless garden.

The largest and most fluid inhabitants are the **Ribbons**, the scaleless, blind swimmers that weave through the drowned colonnades. They are creatures of pure muscle and fat, three to five meters long, pale as milk, with skin as soft and smooth as wet chamois leather. They have no skeleton to speak of—only a flexible cord of dense cartilage that allows them to bend almost double within the narrow confines of an archway. 

The Ribbons do not hunt by sight or sound. Their long, pale barbels, hanging from their snouts like fraying ropes, are hyper-sensitive chemical and pressure receptors. As they glide along the basalt walls, they drag these barbels lightly across the polished stone, "reading" the masonry. They know every join, every copper pin, every egg-shaped, smoothed face carved into the brackets. They know which tiles leak the warmest fluid, and they know the exact geometry of the narrow drainage archways in the floor. 

They feed by vacuum: when a Ribbon locates a dense cluster of Slab-Backs or a patch of mature Patina-Worms, it expands its thick, muscular pharynx, creating a sudden, violent back-pressure that snaps the hard-shelled grazers off the rock with a dull *thump*. 

Yet, despite their size, the Ribbons are gentle in their movements. They move with an eerie, reverent restraint, never striking the pillars, never dislodging the stone. In a place where a single broken tile could disrupt the delicate rising draft of warm fluid, their buoyancy is perfect. They often gather around the smoothed, featureless stone faces, coiling their long bodies around the brackets, rubbing their flanks against the polished black ovals to slough off dead skin—a ritual that keeps both the fish clean and the stone faces perpetually polished.

Deep within the floor drains—the narrow, dark conduits carved beneath the terra-cotta—lives something older and far less motile. These are the **Cistern-Mouths**, or the **Keystones**. They are massive, sessile mollusks or brachiopods that settled into the drainage vents when the sanctuary was first flooded aeons ago. Over thousands of years, their shells have grown to fit the exact square contours of the ancient human masonry, effectively becoming part of the plumbing. They act as living valves. Their massive, muscular mantles pump the thermal fluid up through the floor tiles, maintaining the room’s constant, body-warm current. They do not think; they do not move. They simply draw the water in, filter out the shed skin of the Ribbons and the mineral dust of the tiles, and push the cleansed, heated fluid back up into the dark vault.

There is no death here that goes to waste. When a Ribbon grows old and its muscular cord finally relaxes, it does not sink to a floor of silt, for there is none. It floats weightlessly in the central vault, tumbling softly in the rising warmth, until the Slab-Backs slowly gather upon it, covering the white corpse in a thick, pink shell of armor. Within days, the body is stripped down to the soft cartilage, which the warm, mineral-rich liquid slowly dissolves, turning the fish back into the very fluid that feeds the purple veil on the walls.

It is a world without seasons, without light, and without end. The builders left behind a container, but time and water have filled it with a single, slow-breathing organism made of stone, shell, muscle, and heat—a place that exists purely to hold itself in the dark.
