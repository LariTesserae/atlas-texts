In a place where water is not even a memory, life cannot afford the luxury of wetness. There are no membranes here, no soft sacks of fluid, no eyes that require tears to clear away the dust. The things that inhabit this vertical, dry furnace are continuous with the rock itself—vitreous, mineral, and forged under the same immense pressures that drive the basalt spires toward the dark sky.

To understand them, one must look closely at the cairns and the deep, wax-smooth niches carved into the peaks. 

The niches are not dwellings; they are footprints. Or rather, they are the impressions left by resting bodies. 

They are the **Spires-Keepers**—though "creatures" is too soft a word for them. In form, they resemble long, segmented pillars of dark, high-density glass and compressed basalt, six to eight meters tall, tapering at both ends like massive, multi-jointed needles. Their surfaces are not skin, but overlapping plates of dark obsidian, constantly sloughing off microscopic flakes of mica as they move. Where their plates join, there is no pink flesh, but a deep, incandescent orange glow—the trapped, radiant heat of the hearth below, held inside their bodies like a furnace behind a iron door.

They do not possess legs. Instead, their lower segments end in clustered, heat-transmitting prongs. When a Keeper wants to anchor itself against the roaring thermal drafts, it presses these prongs against the raw basalt. In seconds, its internal heat surges outward, softening the rock beneath it into a warm, plasticized wax. The creature sinks slightly into the mountain face, fusing itself to the stone. When it lifts away hours or days later, it leaves behind a clean, rectangular depression with melted, vitreous edges—the very niches that line the highest ridges.

Because there is no water to act as a solvent or transport mechanism for nutrients, their metabolism is purely pyrochemical and acoustic. 

They feed on the atmosphere itself. As the hot updrafts roar out of the lightless depths, carrying clouds of mineral flour, sulfur, and pulverized quartz, the Keepers expand the plates along their flanks. The draft passes through internal, honeycomb-like chambers lined with metallic compounds. The dust adheres to these superheated chambers, melting directly into their skeletal matrix. What they exhale is not carbon dioxide, but the copper-colored, dry mineral crusts that cling to the windward edges of the stone. The lichenoids are not a separate form of life; they are the shed, crystallizing breath of the Keepers, continuing to grow passively in the wind long after the creature has moved on.

Movement for them is rare, deliberate, and terrifyingly fast when it occurs. 

To travel up the sheer, razor-sharp faces, they do not climb so much as slide along localized lines of thermal expansion. By rapidly heating and cooling their obsidian plates, they generate a series of dry, sharp *clacks*—the same snapping sound the mountains make when they crack. They move in hitching, rhythmic bursts, leaping from one rising fin of rock to another, catching the hot thermal plumes with broad, fin-like ribs that deploy from their backs like iron sails. In the dim, amber-red dusk, a rising Keeper looks like a splinter of night propelled by a trailing wake of glowing red gas.

They do not see with light. In a world choked with mineral haze and dark clouds, vision would be useless. Instead, their entire body is a acoustic receiver. The cairns—those delicate, unmortared towers of obsidian that sing in the updrafts—are their sensory network.

The Keepers build these cairns by hand, using their localized heat to melt the contact points of the stone just enough to fuse them in perfect balance. As the hot gas forces its way up the mountain face, it sets the cairns vibrating, producing a dry, metallic rattle that changes pitch based on the tension in the rock below. A Keeper miles away, anchored in its niche, feels these vibrations traveling through the basalt. 

Through the cairns, they "hear" the world being built. They know when a magma chamber three miles down is swelling; they know which needle of rock is bending under strain, and precisely when it will snap. The cairns are early warnings. When a peak splits down its spine with a whip-like report, the Keepers have already shifted, riding the thermal blast of the newly opened seam into the higher air, leaving their old niches to be sheared apart by the rising stone.

Among them are smaller, more ethereal things—brief, ephemeral life that exists only in the drafts. These are thin, foil-like ribbon-creatures, no thicker than a slate tile, made of flexible, heat-tempered mica. They have no internal heat of their own; they are leaves of pure stone, tumbling endlessly in the thermal updrafts. They scavenge the microscopic dust shed by the Keepers, rattling against the obsidian fins with a sound like dry paper blowing down a street. They do not die so much as get caught in a down-draft, falling into the dark abysses below where the heat is great enough to melt them back into the primordial magma.

The Keepers themselves are nearly immortal, but not immutable. As they age, they absorb more and more heavy minerals from the thermal winds. They grow denser, darker, and slower. Their orange seams fade to a dull, bruised crimson. 

When a Keeper reaches the end of its mobility, it does not collapse. It climbs to the highest, most sharp-edged needle it can find—a place where the wind roars loudest and the air is dry to the point of ringing. It presses its prongs into the stone one last time, draining its internal furnace into the mountain core, welding itself permanently to the crest. 

Its plates fuse solid. Its inner light goes out. It becomes another jagged obsidian fin, high above the hidden floor, singing silently in the hot wind as the mountain continues to drive it upward into the dark.
