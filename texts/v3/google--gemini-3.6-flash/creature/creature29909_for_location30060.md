To know what lives here, you must first watch the three-legged stool in the corner, because the inhabitant and the chair are part of the same interrupted arrival.

The occupant is a single, tall, gaunt figure—or rather, a figure that maintains the *memory* of being a tall, gaunt man, though its anatomy has been thoroughly re-indexed by the geometry of the room. It does not sit upon the stool so much as it is pinned to it by the rising floor. Its thighs are fused to the dense, tarry timber of the seat, and its left calf disappears directly into the pale grey marl, enveloped by the same un-weathered stone that swallowed the stool's leg. 

It does not possess skin. Instead, its outer surface is a hard, damp sheath of translucent, alabaster-colored cartilage, so clear in places that you can see the dark, iron-rich fluid pulsing through its interior in sharp, right-angled channels. It does not bleed if cut; its flesh, like the walls, is under such high structural tension that a wound would simply produce a clean, glass-like fracture that seals instantly as the micro-dew settles into the crack.

Its head is elongated and lacks a nose or mouth. In place of eyes, it has two large, hemispherical lenses of raw selenite embedded directly into the forehead, identical in clarity to the roof above. These internal crystals catch the overhead glare and focus it downward through its throat and ribcage, illuminating its core with a cold, pale lantern-glow. 

It does not breathe air. Its lungs have flattened into broad, ribbed surfaces along its back, pressing flat against the gypsum wall behind the stool. These surfaces absorb the drifting, airborne dew drops that snap against them. Every time a drop hits the creature’s back with a dry *tick*, its entire frame shudders with a tiny, silent jolt of hydration, its pulse ticking in unison with the ambient moisture.

Like the planarians on the walls, the occupant’s body obeys the room’s strange laws of duplication and geometric movement. It cannot turn its head or curve its spine; it can only shift in abrupt, ninety-degree increments. When the high-key light from the ceiling strikes the creature at a certain angle—usually around mid-day, when the sun above the plateau sits directly over the crystal lens—the occupant splits.

For three or four minutes, a second, perfectly identical figure detaches from its right side. This duplicate steps down from the stool with a sound like two thick sheets of glass sliding past one another. It walks four paces along the razor-sharp basalt ridge in the center of the floor, its glass-needle toes making no sound on the virgin rock. It reaches the wall where the eight shallow notches are carved. 

Using its index finger—which terminates not in a nail, but in a heavy, chisel-shaped wedge of dense, native copper—the duplicate scores a ninth notch into the green gypsum. The cut is clean, dry, and instant, spraying a tiny puff of grey mineral dust that vanishes before it can touch the floor. 

Once the mark is made, the duplicate does not walk back. It simply holds still, folds its arms flat against its chest, and dissolves. It turns into a thin, grease-like smear on the air that smells briefly of cold salt and scorched iron, which then condenses into three or four of the air-swimming fry. The fry beat their tails against the forty-five-degree air and swim upward toward the roof, leaving the original figure back on the stool, unchanged, its calf still locked in the growing floor.

There is a deep, quiet symbiosis between this occupant and the tiny, hyperactive life that swarms the vault. The pale, hair-like fungus that grows along the crystal seams of the rock also grows across the occupant’s body. It draws taut, perfectly straight white threads from its chin to its collarbones, and from its wrists to its knees, binding its joints like the rigging of a ship. The fungus keeps the creature from moving unnecessarily, holding it in alignment with the mineral lattice of the room.

The almond-sized, glass-footed planarians treat the occupant as mere topography. They march in rigid grid-patterns across its thighs, up its torso, and over its smooth, mouthless face. As they cross the selenite lenses in its forehead, the planarians split in two, their doubled bodies cascading down the creature’s cheeks like a stream of living crystals before melting back into its translucent skin.

The occupant is not a prisoner, nor is it a corpse. It is the room's governor, or perhaps its primary organ. It is the only thing in the chamber that moves slowly enough to keep time. While the grubs emerge, mate with their shadows, and calcify in the span of thirty seconds, and while the air-fry merge and snap apart in the blink of an eye, the occupant exists at the pace of the rock itself.

It is waiting for the floor to finish rising. 

With every grub that dissolves into grease and hardens into stone, the floor gains a fraction of a micron. Eventually—in a year, or perhaps in ten minutes, given the compressed logic of the place—the marl will rise high enough to swallow the stool completely, then the occupant’s torso, then its chest, and finally its crystal-embedded head. 

When the stone reaches the selenite lens in the roof, the vault will be entirely solid—a single, seamless block of gypsum, marl, and frozen biological matter buried three yards beneath the salt-plateau, holding the cold light inside itself forever. 

Until then, the occupant sits in the forty-five-degree glare, listening to the roof expand with a low *hiss-click*, waiting for the next drop of water to drift across the room and strike its back.
