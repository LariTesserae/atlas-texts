To understand what lives in the basin, one must first abandon the idea of individuals. What exists here is a single, continuous engine of desiccation and heat—a biological sea that has long since forgotten the concept of fluid.

At the lowest depth of the drift, directly against the glowing basalt floor, dwell the **Solum-Plates**. These are the largest of the basin’s inhabitants, some as broad as shield-bosses, flat and disc-like, forged from a heavy, silicate chitin that is almost indistinguishable from the rock beneath them. They do not possess eyes, limbs, or traditional mouths. Instead, their undersides are carpeted in millions of microscopic, mineralized cilia that "root" directly into the micro-fractures of the basalt. They are the primary harvesters of the mountain’s deep heat, absorbing pure radiant energy through their ventral shells and converting it into a dense, dry chemical matrix stored within their chalky internal tissue.

The Solum-Plates rarely move more than an inch a year, but they are the bedrock upon which the rest of the basin feeds. As they process the thermal energy, they exude a fine, crystalline crust along their upper rims—a dry, sweet-smelling efflorescence rich in copper and sulfur. 

This crust is the primary pasture for the **Ribbon-Weeds**—the long, legless forms that thread through the dark. These creatures are pure muscle and rasp. They are built like flexible files, their hides constructed from thousands of overlapping, leathery rings that slide over one another with the sound of dry grass in a gale. A Ribbon-Weed spends its entire life twisting through the gaps between heavier bodies, its underside constantly scraping the shells of the Solum-Plates, licking away the mineral crust with a dry, radula-like tongue that never knows moisture. 

The Ribbons are the kinetic nervous system of the basin. By coiling and uncoiling in tight, rhythmic waves, they push and herd the lighter organisms toward the surface, keeping the three-foot-deep carpet in a state of perpetual, fluid churn. Without their endless flexing, the lower layers would bake into a solid, lifeless pavement of fused chitin.

Above the Ribbons, in the shifting middle stratum, are the **Pebble-Husks** and the **Tile-Skimmers**. 

The Pebble-Husks are smaller than a knuckle, spherical, and wrapped in thick, sunless clay shells that they secrete from consumed stone dust. They are the recyclers. They possess no stomachs in the traditional sense; their interiors are simple, high-temperature combustion chambers packed with symbiotic, extremophilic mineral-reactors. They roll continuously over the backs of the larger beasts, tumbling into every crevice to consume the dry chaff: shed eye-caps, crushed leg-segments, and the powdery, copper-tinted ash that is all that remains of a neighbor when its thermal engine finally fails. When a Pebble-Husk reaches the end of its life, its internal heat spikes briefly, vitrifying its shell into a glass-hard bead that is eventually ground back down into dust by the scraping limbs of those above it.

Skittering over this rolling gravel are the Tile-Skimmers—the apex opportunists of the upper crust. Broad, flat, and light as parchment, their six joints end in needle-sharp points of pure iron-deposit that allow them to walk across the squirming backs of the herd without sinking. They are the only creatures in the basin with predatory mandibles, though these are not designed to bite or sever. Instead, they are high-frequency friction saws. A Tile-Skimmer pinpoints a neighbor whose pit-organs show a drop in body temperature—a sign of failing metabolism. It drops onto the dying creature, buries its saws into the leg-joints, and grinds the limb into a dry, digestible meal before the body beneath can be swallowed by the dark, churning depths.

And then there are the keepers of the masonry.

The ancient basalt blocks—the walls and lintels worn smooth by centuries of rubbing—are not mere landmarks; they are occupied. Deep within the mortarless joints between the heavy stones live the **Silt-Archons**.

These are the ancient, static elders of the basin. A single Archon may be several centuries old, its body having expanded to fill the entire gap between two basalt blocks, effectively becoming the mortar itself. Its outer shell has calcified into a rough, porous cement that binds the ancient stones together. Only its face-plate remains exposed—a massive, sunken dish lined with dozens of glowing, dark-red pit-organs that monitor the thermal gradients of the entire basin.

The Archons do not hunt, nor do they wander. They are fed by the passive pressure of the swarm. As the millions of smaller creatures flow up and over the masonry, thousands of them are crushed against the Archons’ rigid, exposed faces. The Archons absorb the resulting dry debris directly through porous, sieve-like plates on their foreheads. 

In return, the Archons act as thermal conductors. Their massive, mineralized bodies draw heat directly from the mountain's core through the deep foundations of the masonry and vent it out through their face-plates in shimmering, dry pulses. They are the fires around which the rest of the basin gathers. The squirming carpet pools thickest around the archways because the Archons are there, holding the stones together with their own calcified flesh, radiating a steady, merciless warmth that keeps the endless, papery engine turning.

There is no youth here, and no decay in the earthly sense. Reproduction is a silent, heat-triggered splintering: when a Tile-Skimmer or a Ribbon-Weed absorbs too much energy, its shell simply cracks down the dorsal seam, and a smaller, fully formed duplicate slips out into the dark, leaving the empty molt behind to be immediately rasped into dust by the waiting mouths below.

Nothing leaves the basin. Nothing enters it. The haze above blocks the stars; the sheer basalt walls block the wind. Down in the saddle, beneath the heavy dark, the calcified ocean continues its endless, dry shuffle—a world made entirely of armor, friction, and heat, grinding itself down and building itself up again, forever.
