The ones who live here are the ones who built it, though to say they "built" it in the past implies a time when the stone was not yet laid. Here, cause and effect are smoothed flat, like wet clay beneath a wooden paddle. Time does not erode, so history does not accumulate. There are only the inhabitants, the stone, and the work that is never finished because it was never incomplete.

They are tall, long-limbed, and heavy-footed, their bodies cast in the same warm, unblemished palette as the valley floor. Their skin has the matte, velvet-smooth texture of damp, unbaked terracotta or fine lime-wash, colored in shades of pale ochre, wet river-silt, and deep ceramic red. They are hairless, with smooth, domed heads that catch the blinding, shadowless glare from above. They wear no clothes, for clothes would trap heat that has nowhere to go, and their skin needs constant, unmediated contact with the vapor-thick air and the wet granite.

They move with a strange, liquid patience. Because there is no wind to fight and no cold to flee, their movements are devoid of haste. When one walks across a mile-wide expanse of wet flagstone, their bare soles make a soft, suctioning *plip-plip* against the thin film of water. Their toes are wide and articulate, gripping the wet tile with absolute certainty. They do not sweat—their body temperature is precisely 100 degrees, perfectly matched to the air—but moisture gathers on them as it does on the obelisks, forming heavy, glittering beads along the ridges of their collarbones and the curves of their backs that remain suspended, never dripping.

Their faces are broad, flat, and remarkably quiet. They have no eyebrows or eyelashes, for there is no dust in this world to keep out of the eye, and no sweat to sting. Their eyes are large, dark, and lidless, or nearly so—wide, glossy spheres of deep amber or polished obsidian that do not need to blink, constantly bathed in the motionless humidity. They do not speak with voices; their throats lack the dry friction required for speech. Instead, they communicate through the floor.

To send a thought, one will kneel—placing a broad, damp knee into a shallow basin—and strike the granite with the flat of a palm or the heel of a copper chisel. The vibration travels instantly through the miles of continuous, hand-laid stone, unimpeded by soil or air pockets. A mason three miles away, resting their palms against a pylon, feels the precise rhythmic pulse through the masonry, understanding it as clearly as a spoken word.

Their labor is not a chore born of necessity, but their primary form of respiration. You will see a group of three standing beside a terracotta channel where the warm water is creeping slowly uphill over a flight of low, sharp steps. One holds a heavy copper chisel, another a wooden mallet, the third a flat river-stone slathered with wet mortar that smells of lime and crushed shell. 

The worker with the chisel places its bronze edge against a granite block. They strike. The chisel cuts into the stone as cleanly as a knife through cold tallow, removing a wafer-thin curl of granite. The edge left behind is impossibly, dangerously sharp. The second worker presses a palm into the freshly cut seam—leaving the very handprint mentioned in the stones—and smoothed mortar expands outward to seal the joint. The work does not alter the landscape so much as it refreshes its eternal immediacy. They are not expanding the valley; they are keeping it from ever becoming old.

They do not eat in any way recognizable to us. They have no teeth, only smooth, hard ridges of pale bone inside their mouths. Instead, they drink deep from the upward-flowing channels, kneeling at the edges of the terracotta gutters and immersing their lower faces in the mineral-dense, warm water. They absorb the salts, the dissolved lime, and the microscopic, glass-clear algae that float invisibly in the streams. 

Sometimes, one will approach a broad-leafed arum growing from a stone well. They will gently press their chest against the bruised-plum leaf, remaining motionless for hours as their skin draws moisture and subtle plant-acids directly through their pores. When they step away, the leaf is uninjured, unchanged, as dark and vibrant as before.

Beside them, the smaller life of the valley moves in silent symbiosis:

*   **The Tallow-Eels:** In the wide, shallow puddled-clay basins, beneath the hovering clouds of iridescent flies, live long, translucent creatures like strips of oiled parchment. They have no eyes and move by pressing their flat bellies against the smooth clay bottom, scraping away invisible films of mineral paste. The giant, terracotta-skinned inhabitants will sometimes let these eels slide over their feet and ankles, allowing them to clean the fine lime-residue from between their toes.

*   **The Linen-Moths:** High above, clinging to the endless, vaulted sky of white lime-plaster, are vast organisms that resemble sheets of wet, unbleached linen. They are several feet wide, completely flat, and white as the canopy itself. They feed on the intense, shadowless light radiated by the ceiling. Very rarely, one will detach and flutter down through the dense, motionless air—falling slowly, lazily, like a dropped handkerchief—to land upon the shoulders of a kneeling mason. The person does not flinch. They allow the creature to rest upon their warm, damp back for a day or a week, until it slowly crawls up a nearby obelisk and returns to the high plaster vault.

*   **The Glass Crabs and Jade Salamanders:** These are not pets, nor are they wild. They are part of the masonry’s circulatory system. The six-legged salamanders carry seeds in the slick folds of their jade skin, depositing them exclusively into the hand-chiseled planting wells. The glass-clear crabs spend their lives inside the water channels, using their delicate, scissor-like claws to trim any stray root-fibers that dare attempt to push between the pristine mortar seams, maintaining the sharp, knife-cut geometry of the floor.

There is no death here, just as there is no birth. The number of inhabitants remains as fixed as the number of stone flagstones. Should one of the large, red-skinned dwellers ever cease moving—entering a stillness so profound that even the water on their skin stops gathering—they do not rot. They simply petrify over the course of centuries, their flesh slowly hardening, absorbing the lime and silica from the air, until their body turns to smooth, seamless terracotta. 

When this happens, the others gather around the figure. They do not mourn; they do not have a concept for loss, because nothing leaves the valley. They bring their copper chisels and their wooden trowels. With soft, rhythmic *thunks* that hum through the granite floor, they gently trim the edges of the petrified body, squaring the shoulders, truing the angles of the torso, until the former worker becomes a new, sharp-cornered pylon or a low, elegant stone basin. 

They cut a new water channel leading directly to its base, lay down a fresh bed of wet mortar, and continue their work, their handprints fresh in the clay, forever caught in the warm, blinding, 100-degree moment of creation.
