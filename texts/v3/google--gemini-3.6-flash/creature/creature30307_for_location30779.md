They are called the **Rib-Keepers**, though among themselves they have no spoken name, only a rhythmic tap-pattern—a rapid *three-beat click* on cold steel—that designates their kind.

To look at one is to see a creature stretched thin by the geometry of the fissure. They stand nearly seven feet tall, but are so lean and flat-chested they can turn sideways and slip through the three-inch clearance between two sliding residential units. Their skeletons are not made of porous bone, but of a dense, dark cartilage reinforced with trace iron absorbed from the air, making their limbs heavy, quiet, and impossibly strong.

### Physiology of the Height
They do not sweat. In air this dry and cold, liquid sweat would steal precious warmth before evaporating into nothing. Instead, their skin is a dull, slate-grey hide, hairless and dry as old parchment, coated in a fine, self-secreted wax. This wax catches the graphite and cold iron dust floating in the slit, giving their faces and hands a faint, metallic sheen—like polished lead. 

Their eyes are broad, lidless, and set deep within high, bony sockets to shield them from falling dust. Rather than blinking, a clear, toughened membrane slides horizontally across the eye, wiping away bits of crushed stone and iron filings with a dry, soft rasp. The irises are a pale, uniform violet, so dilated in the slate-blue twilight that they seem almost black, catching the glow of the gas tubes and reflecting it back like the eyes of nocturnal moths.

They do not breathe through their mouths. Their lower faces are dominated by a smooth, ribbed plate of cartilage—a biological filter that twitches constantly, sifting the air for graphite particles and trace gases while keeping out the glass-sharp silicate dust. Their lungs are bellows of tough, dry tissue that make a quiet, rhythmical *shhh-shhh* sound, perfectly attuned to the shuffle of the city’s dry-running engines.

They do not drink liquid water. The concept of a fluid in a cup is as foreign to them as a sea. They absorb trace hydrocarbons and bound moisture from dense, tallow-like wafers manufactured in the dark, cold lower vaults from processed mineral salts and dynamo-warmth. When they exhale, they do not produce steam; they emit a faint, odorless puff of dry grey powder.

### Garb and Gear
Their clothing is an extension of the machinery they inhabit. The "heavy, dust-dappled coats" are woven from coarse mineral glass and slate-fiber thread, stiff enough to resist the knife-edges of the unweathered basalt. These coats are greased with thick tallow along the shoulders and spine so that if a sliding iron house shifts unexpectedly, the inhabitant is squeezed out of the way rather than crushed.

Their hands are long, with six jointed digits tipped in thick, horn-like nails worn flat from decades of adjusting brass sleeves and clearing cog-teeth. They wear no gloves; their fingertips are calloused to the toughness of vulcanized rubber, insensitive to the bite of frost or the friction of a moving cable.

Their feet are their primary sensory organs. They wear heavy, open-work boots framed in soft lead and tipped with brass heel-plates. Through these soles, they "hear" the city. A Rib-Keeper standing on a steel catwalk can tell, without looking, that a mill three hundred feet above is suffering from a dry spindle, or that a tension cable anchored to the east wall has stretched by an eighth of an inch.

### Language and Vibration
Speech in the thin air is rare and laborious, sounding like the rubbing together of two dry river stones. Instead, they talk through the city’s bones. 

Every Rib-Keeper carries a small, double-ended striking pick made of hardened bronze, tucked into a sleeve sheath. By tapping on a catwalk, a cable, or an iron doorframe, they send precise vibrational signals through the entire structural web. 
* A long, ringing scrape along a cable means *Clear the lower rail; a foundry unit is descending.*
* A staccato *click-click-tap* against a basalt bolt means *A home is jammed; bring the screw-jack.*

They possess an incredible acoustic geography. An inhabitant can close their eyes and map the entire eighty-pace length of the city by feeling the echo of the engine-shuffles traveling through the steel ribbons beneath their heels.

### Life in the Shifting Slots
Because the city’s geometry changes every hour, the inhabitants have no concept of permanent ownership or fixed address. A family unit—usually three or four adults bound by shared maintenance duties—occupies whichever iron box is currently stable and dry. 

When a box unclamps to travel up the gantry, its occupants simply step out onto the nearest catwalk, wait for the next unit to slide into place, and step inside to continue their work. Their personal possessions are few and strictly regulated: a set of wire brushes, a pouch of dry talc, a bronze striking pick, and a slate tablet carved with the family’s maintenance history.

Their relationship with the smaller creatures of the seam is one of silent, industrial symbiosis:
* **The Pewter-Mice:** The small, dry-shelled organisms are treated like living tools. A worker will reach into a dark corner, pick up a pewter-mouse by its carapace, and use its felted palps to polish the glass face of a pressure gauge before setting it back down. In return, the inhabitants leave crumbs of tallow near the hot dynamo housings where the creatures nest.
* **The Paper-Gliders:** The blind, pale gliders that swoop through the upper gap are used to carry lightweight drive-pegs and written slate notes across the void where no walkways reach. An inhabitant will whistle a high, thin note—one of the few vocal sounds they make—and a glider will drop from the dark ceiling, hook its dry claws into the worker's sleeve, accept a small peg in its beak, and launch itself across the fissure to a waiting hand on the opposite gantry.

### The Mind of the Height
There is no panic in them, nor any hurry, despite the frantic motion of the iron boxes around them. They move with a fluid, patient economy of motion. If a gantry rail snaps, they do not scream; they simply hook their lead boots into the nearest structural rib, lean back into the dark gap, and begin tapping out the code for a repair crew.

They view the dark basalt walls not as rock, but as an anvil—a temporary container for their ever-moving engine. They have no history of the world below the needles; the two miles of empty air beneath their lowest suspended platforms might as well be an infinite void. To them, existence is simply this: the cold slate twilight above, the dark depth below, and between them, the continuous, comforting *clack-clack-clack-shhh* of iron sliding on iron, kept alive by hands that never sweat and boots that feel the turning of every gear.
