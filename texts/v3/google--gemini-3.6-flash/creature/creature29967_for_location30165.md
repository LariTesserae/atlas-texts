To know who lives here, one must abandon the idea of a creature that stands apart from its room. Nothing on the Kael-Shelf exists *upon* the city; the population is the final, living coat of the architecture itself, layered over the metallic glass like grease on an engine piston.

At the apex of this food chain—if a world without sunlight can be said to have a chain—are the **Siphon-Slabs**, the builders and primary citizens of the spires. 

They are colossal, dorso-ventrally flattened organisms, ten to fifteen meters across, shaped like roughly hexagonal tiles of wet, grey vulcanized rubber. They have no faces, no necks, and no eyes. Their upper surfaces are plated in overlapping shingles of organic zinc and calcified bone, which scrape against the metallic glass spires with a sound like iron sleds being dragged over gravel. Beneath these heavy carapaces lies an immense, muscular mantle that clings to the slippery vertical walls through a combination of thousands of microscopic hydraulic suckers and a continuous secretion of the thick, tallowy lipid that coats the city.

The Siphon-Slabs do not walk; they *pulse*. They creep up and down the faces of the towers at a rate of a few yards per day, following the thermal veins where the city’s field-generators bleed heat. Their front margins are fringed with thick, pale palps that reach into the upward-flowing streams of heavy brine, siphoning gallons of mineral slurry into internal gizzards lined with grinding plates of vitrified slag. They eat the mountain as it is pumped upward, digesting the heavy metals and extruding a quick-setting, self-healing bio-glass from their rear vents—effectively repairing and expanding the very spires they cling to.

Communication among the Siphon-Slabs is entirely seismic and chemical. They speak by vibrating their heavy carapaces against the metallic glass at frequencies that travel for miles through the city’s structural skeleton. A citizen five spires away does not *hear* a warning or a summons; it feels the message as an ache in its own calcified joints, accompanied by the distinct smell of tallow modified by fear or reproductive readiness drifting down the wet, narrow fissures.

Clinging to the Siphon-Slabs themselves are the second layer of life: the **Chisel-Grubbers** and **Glass-Ticks**. 

The Grubbers—those long, segmented creatures squirming in the razor-sharp rock crevices—are the garbage-sweepers of the shelf. They grow up to six feet in length, their bodies wrapped in slick, rubbery rings of skin that resist the cutting edges of the un-weathered pyroxene gravel. They possess no bones, only a dense, pressurized hydraulic gut and four heavy, obsidian-tipped mandibles that operate like pneumatic chisels. They follow the Siphon-Slabs, gnawing away the crusts of hard mineral precipitates left behind in their wake, clearing the transit conduits and keeping the field-generator vents from calcifying shut. Where a Grubber dies, its body does not rot; the sub-zero, heavy-metal brine preserves it, turning its slick skin into a stiff, waxy leather that other creatures carve out to line their breeding niches.

Higher up, where the braided transit conduits weave through the black air, live the **Vane-Wings**—the thick-bodied gliders. 

They are the size of oxen, though mostly hollow, constructed from a framework of dense, cartilaginous ribs wrapped in wet, hairless hide that smells intensely of cold zinc and stagnant brine. Because they cannot see, their skin is an acoustic mirror, covered in tiny, stiff cilia that feel the bounce-back of their own endless clicking. They do not fly so much as throw themselves off the upper ledges, dropping like heavy sacks through the dark until their leathery membranes catch the dense, wet updrafts rising from the lowland basin. 

The Vane-Wings roost in immense, tangled colonies on the undersides of the transit pipes, hanging upside down by barbed tail-hooks. Thousands of them pack together so tightly that their leathery wings overlap into a single, undulating ceiling of wet flesh. They feed by dropping onto the larger Siphon-Slabs or the shield-boss grazers, using long, needle-like probosces to drill through calcified hulls and tap into the cold, mineral-rich lymphatic fluids beneath. The "wet *thwack-thwack-thwack*" of their wings is the soundtrack of the city’s sky—a continuous, blind migration moving through the gaps between towers, guided only by the echo of their own noise hitting the cold metal.

At the microscopic and ambient level, the city is saturated with the **Tallow-Motes**. 

These are not true animals, but free-swimming, single-celled colonial aggregations that thrive in the thin sheet-curtains of liquid running down the walls. They produce the tallowy oil that gives the air its heavy, oppressive smell. The oil is their defense against freezing in the sub-zero wind; it forms an unbroken lipid film over every drop of water, trapping heat against the structural glass and allowing the cold-blooded ecology to function in temperatures that would otherwise turn living tissue into brittle ice. 

Nothing in this city ever speeds up. A courtship between two Siphon-Slabs involves six months of slow, grinding proximity, their carapaces pressed together with enough force to crush steel, exchanging genetic material in the form of thick, cold pastes while the brine flows around them. A Vane-Wing may spend three days hanging motionless from a pipe, its spiracles opening and closing with a faint *rasp-hiss*, waiting for the barometric pressure to shift enough to lift its heavy body.

If an outsider were to remain on the Kael-Shelf long enough, they would realize that the city and its inhabitants are not separate entities engaged in a struggle for survival. The spires are the bones; the gravitational fields are the nerves; the heavy brine is the blood; and the millions of clicking, grinding, tallow-dripping bodies are the muscle that keeps the entire cold, sightless apparatus from slipping off the razor-edge of the mountain into the abyss below.
