To live here is not to fight the metal, but to be born of its long, cold exhaustion. 

The organisms of the high plateau do not possess soft, perishable margins. Their hides, carapaces, and membranes are imbued with heavy elements, their blood thickened with ethylene glycol and dissolved copper salts. They are the lingering aftermath of a world designed for conductivity, surviving in the narrow thermal space between the frozen sky and the dying, subterranean heat of the machine beneath them.

### The Grazers: *Hexapodal Rust-Hides*

The herds that move across the hexagonal iron plains are known only by the sound of their breath—a wet, rhythmic draw of cold air filtered through thick pads of grease-matted wool. 

Standing no higher than an ox but twice as long, these six-legged grazers have no faces in the traditional sense. Where eyes and nostrils should be, their blunt, wedge-shaped heads are covered in a dense network of black, rubbery ampullae—sensory pits that read the faint, dying magnetic currents pulsing through the bed-iron. They walk with an eerie, liquid smoothness. Their six legs end in broad, dished soles filled with a dense, fibrous cartilage that muffles every step against the iron floor and spreads their considerable weight over the hollow, brittle turbine housings buried inches beneath the surface.

Their mouths are tucked beneath their chins: broad plates of self-sharpening, chert-like bone that scrape the red sludge—a hyper-dense colony of iron-oxidizing Archaea—from the edges of the oily meltwater pools. They swallow the rust alongside fine iron filings. In their multi-chambered stomachs, acidic enzymes dissolve the metal, extracting minute electrical charges and heat from the chemical reaction before passing dense, metallic pellets back onto the plain. 

They do not mate on a seasonal cycle. Instead, when two herds cross paths after years of solitary wandering, they pause for days, standing flank-to-flank in the twilight silver light. They press their warm, grease-soaked bodies together to conserve heat, exchanging chemical signals through skin-glands that smell powerfully of crushed mutton fat and cold kerosene. A single calf is born three years later, its hide already calcified and stiff with metallic dust, standing within minutes on the cold iron without a sound.

### The Inhabitants of the Geothermal Seams: *The Slate-Ticks and Velvet Curtains*

In the deep grooves where giant subterranean cables once drew power, the air is three degrees warmer. Here, the ecology condenses into dense, claustrophobic colonies.

The slate-grey ticks are not parasites of flesh; they are parasites of *conduction*. Their flat, river-stone carapaces are composed of layered chitin and iron sulfide, rendering them almost indistinguishable from the surrounding basalt. They anchor themselves directly over the stress-cracks in the cast iron, driving hollow, needle-like proboscises down into the warm, mineral-rich grease that still bleeds from ancient hydraulic sleeves miles below. 

Over centuries of slow feeding, the ticks exude a thick, pale tallow through the margins of their shell-segments. This substance does not spoil in the freezing air; it hardens into a dense, grey wax that coats the walls of the cable-canyons.

It is upon this wax that the blind moths depend. 

Hanging from the oxidized steel arches above, the moths form curtains twenty feet deep and hundreds of yards long. They have no mouthparts and live only three days in their adult form, emerging from silk cocoons spun from fine threads of metallic copper and chitin. They do not fly. They have no need for flight in a windless sky that offers nothing to seek. Instead, they crawl across one another in a endless, writhing mass, their soft, wet-velvet wings fanning the air just enough to circulate the warm, tallow-scented vapor rising from the ticks below. 

When a curtain of moths finally dies, it drops all at once—a silent, heavy blizzard of pale wings falling onto the iron floor, where it is instantly absorbed by the iron-sludge microbes and the slow, scraping mouths of the young ticks.

### The Deep-Canyon Scavengers: *The Copper-Weepers*

Deep within the fluted canyon walls, where green-patinated bronze forms vertical ribbons against dark steel, live solitary creatures that resemble long, multi-jointed ribbons of tarnished metal and cartilage. 

They are the *Copper-Weepers*. Forty feet long and no thicker than a man’s thigh, they anchor themselves high up on the bronze striations using rows of tiny, recessed hooks. They move so slowly that a human watcher could stand beneath one for an hour and see no sign of life. 

The Weepers feed on the toxic condensation that runs down the green bronze—water heavy with copper sulfate, lead salts, and sulfuric acid. Their skin is semi-permeable, drinking in the poison and secreting it along their backs as a thick, gelatinous slime that glows with a faint, cold greenish bioluminescence in the dark depths of the canyons. 

This glow is the only light generated on the plateau. It does not attract prey, for there is no sight here; it is merely a waste product of their bizarre, mineral-heavy metabolism. When a Weeper dies, its grip fails, and it falls into the dark cable-trenches below, ringing like a struck pipe as it hits the iron floor, where its metal-rich body slowly oxidizes over a hundred years into a long, green smudge of dust.

### The Monument-Habitants: *The Stratus-Manta*

Near the center of the plateau, around the base of the two-mile-high tungsten shaft, the atmosphere is slightly different. The massive rod acts as a thermal cold-finger, drawing down the extreme freeze of the upper cloud deck and creating a perpetual downward draft of ice-needles.

Here, living in the shadow of the monumental shaft, exists the largest creature of the plateau: the *Stratus-Manta*.

It is a creature of immense, flat proportions—a single, disk-like pancake of dense, grey muscle and blubber fifty yards wide, covered in a overlapping shingle of dark, iron-plated scales. It lies entirely flat against the iron floor, looking like a natural bulge in the crust or a capped hatchway. 

The Manta does not wander. It stays grounded near the tungsten shaft for centuries, its flat underbelly pressed to the iron plain. It feeds by creating a micro-vacuum beneath its body. Using tiny, muscular cilia on its underside, it draws the oily meltwater and iron-sludge beneath itself, filtering the liquid through a vast internal labyrinth of gills before expelling pure, desalted water vapor through a single vent on its back.

Every few decades, when the subterranean pressure shifts and a vent of greased steam exhales from the deep bed-iron, the Stratus-Manta responds. It does not lift its body—to raise itself into the cold air would mean freezing solid in minutes. Instead, it ripples. A heavy, wave-like pulse travels through its fifty-yard width, a slow, muscular shudder that clears the thick crust of rime ice from its back with a faint, crystalline shatter.

The ice falls to the iron floor. The Manta settles back down. The frost begins to build again instantly. Around it, the eyeless grazers pass in single file, their wide pads treading carefully around the creature’s rim, navigating by the magnetic shadow its massive, iron-rich body casts upon the floor of the world.
