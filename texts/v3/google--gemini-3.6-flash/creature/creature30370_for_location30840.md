In the absence of animal life, the Lower Sluice-Court has not defaulted to sterility. Instead, it has yielded to a slower, denser, and far more specialized taxonomy—a suite of botanical, fungal, and mineral organisms that have adapted so precisely to the court’s unique metabolism that they can no longer be separated from the machinery itself.

Here, life is not measured by locomotion, but by accretion, pulse, and chemistry.

***

### The Tallow-Mosses (*Bryum adiposum*)
The most pervasive residents are the liver-colored mosses that coat the ironwork. Unlike common surface mosses that rely on light and soil-borne nitrogen, these heavy, rubbery carpets feed directly on the rendered animal fat, paraffin, and mineral lubricants that leak from the stuffing boxes of the ancient pumps. 

Their lower layers do not possess true roots; instead, they produce an acidic secretion that dissolves the topmost microscopic layer of iron rust, converting it into an insoluble iron-tannate crust. This crust acts as a permanent anchor and a protective skin, sealing the metal beneath against deep structural rot. 

Where the moss grows over the teeth of the slow-moving spur gears, it forms a thick, dense pad that is constantly compressed and released as the teeth mesh. The moss does not die under this pressure; it thrives on it. The twelve-second hydraulic thrum forces water and dissolved tallow through the plant’s cellular structure like a squishy, living gasket. In return, the moss secretes a slick, viscous sap that acts as a natural bio-lubricant, allowing the gears to turn with almost no friction, silent save for the wet, kissing sound of their engagement.

***

### The Plasmodial Web (The Golden Sump-Slime)
If anything in the court serves the role of an animal, it is a single, vast, branching slime mold (*Myxomycetes*) that lives in the warm, pitch-black water of the lower sump-troughs. 

It manifests as a network of thick, translucent, yellow-amber veins, some as wide as a wrist, that web across the blackened brick walls and creep along the undersides of the copper pipes. Though entirely brainless, it possesses a crude, physical sensitivity to vibration and temperature gradients. 

It moves—not at a rate visible to the eye, but over days—following the lines of highest heat toward the boiler flues behind the masonry. Its entire mass pulsates in rhythm with the hydraulic pump below the flagstones, expanding and contracting every twelve seconds. As it pulses, it sweeps the wet brick clean of microscopic iron-bacteria and mineral scum, acting as the court's cleaner. Where it passes, it leaves behind a smooth, glass-like track of dried glycoprotein that glitters like mica in the amber twilight.

***

### The Valve-Fungi (*Fumes boileria*)
Clustered tightly around the high brass relief valve overhead are colonies of pale, fleshy shelf-fungi. They do not grow on decaying wood, for there is none; their mycelial threads penetrate deep into the lime-scale stalactites and the lead-sealed joints of the steam flues.

These fungi are thermo-hygrophilic, thriving only in the blast zone of the valve’s continuous *shhh-HISS*. Their leathery, cream-colored caps are ribbed like turbine blades, angled precisely to catch the high-pressure spray. They absorb the dissolved sulfur, iron carbonate, and trace minerals carried in the boiler steam. 

When the valve vents its hardest, shaking the air, the fungi respond to the pressure shock by flexing their gills. They release dense, visible clouds of oily, rust-red spores directly into the steam plume. The mist carries these spores across the quadrangle, ensuring that every dripping pipe flange and hot mortar joint is perpetually seeded.

***

### The Malachite Ferns
The largest plants in the court are the giant ferns that erupt from the seams between the vitrified bricks. Their fronds are dark green, almost black, with a heavy, waxy cuticle that repels the constant oil-filmed condensation. 

These ferns are hyper-accumulators of heavy metals. As their roots draw moisture from the runoff of the copper and brass conduits, they absorb dissolved copper salts. Over decades, this copper is deposited along the margins of their fronds, turning the undersides an iridescent, pale blue-green—the exact color of oxidized bronze. 

When an old frond eventually dies, it does not rot into soft soil. The concentrated minerals within its tissue cause it to petrify where it hangs. Over years, the dead fronds harden into brittle, malachite-colored stone brackets that support the weight of the new fronds growing above them, building out living, stone-hard shelves that buttress the bulging brickwork of the walls.

***

### The Calcified Canopy and the Living System
High above, where the wire-reinforced glass skylights cast their gold light, the ivy does not climb as a wild vine does. It grows down, hanging from the cast-iron catwalks like heavy green drapery. Its tendrils are encrusted with a thick shell of calcium carbonate—boiler scale deposited by forty years of micro-leaks—turning the vines into rigid, hollow tubes of stone through which the plant pumps hot, mineral-laden water from the sumps to its upper leaves.

Nothing here lives in isolation, and nothing is wasted. 
* The moss lubricates the gears that keep the water moving.
* The water feeds the ferns that petrify into wall supports.
* The walls contain the heat that feeds the steam.
* The steam blows spores through the fungi that seal the pipe joints.

The true inhabitant of the Lower Sluice-Court is not any single plant, mold, or fungus, but the **Court itself**—a hybrid organism of brass, vitrified clay, hot water, and specialized green flesh. It is an industrial climax-community that has forgotten the sun, forgotten the season, and forgotten human hands, content to breathe its heavy steam and beat its twelve-second iron heart in the warm, dripping dark forever.
