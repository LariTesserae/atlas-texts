If no rat scurry across the copper conduits and no fly hums around the dim filament lamps, it is because animal life requires a cadence that this place broke long ago. Animals are too fast, too dry, too volatile; their blood burns too hot and demands too much oxygen to survive in a vault where the air is forty percent steam and the soil is a soaked, dense slurry of coal-ash and crushed zinc.

What lives in the corrugated boxes stacked against the masonry are not men, not anymore, though they were laid down here in the frame of men several generations past. 

They are called the **Greasers**, or the **Sump-Lungs**, though they have no name for themselves because they do not speak. Speech requires sudden, sharp exhalations that would tear the delicate, water-logged tissue of their throat-linings. Instead, they exist in absolute, deliberate silence, their movements synchronized to the forty-stroke rhythm of the central beam pump.

To see one is to first mistake it for a knot of wet timber or a rolled length of canvas left to rot beside a steam pipe. 

Their skin is smooth, hairless, and cold to the touch, having lost all pigment generations ago. It has the rubbery, water-repellent texture of a boiled turnip, pale gray with faint, branching veins of verdigris-green running beneath the surface. They do not sweat—the air is already wetter than their flesh—and their bodies do not produce grease; instead, their skin secretes a thin, odorless lipid that smells faintly of damp starch, preventing the perpetual drizzle from sloughing off their outer layers of skin.

Their eyes are large, lidless, and covered by a thick, milky membrane—much like the glass panes in their tiny dwelling windows—that filters the harsh orange glare of the incandescent bulbs and the greenish twilight from above. They do not look *at* things. They do not have the quick, searching dart of a creature that hunts or fears being hunted. They stare straight ahead, their vision tuned only to motion that strays from the steady, forty-cycle pulse of the vault: a leaking union joint, a sagging vine, a flutter in a waterwheel’s bucket.

In the small, square habitations stacked five high, there are no chairs, no tables, no stoves. Cooking is an impossibility where nothing dry can exist. Inside a box, there is only a hanging sling woven from the tough, fibrous core of weeping liverworts, suspended over a copper trough that drains into the floor-mattress. 

A Sump-Lung does not eat meat or bread. Their digestive systems have simplified into long, slow fermentation tubes. They drink directly from the iron sluices—taking in the warm, mineral-thick bilge water laden with nitrates, copper salts, and pulverized wood-pulp—and they chew on the soft, pale taproot-nodes that bore through their corrugated walls. They digest this sap over days, resting suspended in their wet slings while their chests expand and contract with the agonizing slowness of a bellows underwater.

Their relationship with the engines is not one of operator and machine, but of organ and body.

Watch one move down a catwalk: 
It does not step; it glides, its long, spade-like feet—flattened and un-arched to spread weight over the soft, springy root-floor—feeling the vibration of every rivet. In its right hand, which has grown thick and square-boned, it carries an iron scraper worn smooth by fifty years of grip. Its left hand is different: the fingers are long, thin, and tipped with hard, dark horn that resembles the shell of a beetle or the edge of a pruned root.

The Sump-Lung stops before a low-pressure valve that has begun to whistle—a high, thin sound that cuts through the deep *thump-clack* of the pump. The creature does not hurry. It waits three full pump-strokes, leaning its brow against the warm, sweating pipe, feeling the internal pressure through its skull. Then, with a slow, heavy lean of its shoulder, it presses a lump of tallow-soaked oakum into the leaking seam, using its calcified thumb to press the hemp deep into the threads. It stays there for twenty minutes, perfectly motionless, holding the seal until the condensation glazes over the repair and the plant-matter inside the packing expands in the heat, sealing the leak permanently.

They do not age as surface-dwellers do. They do not grow frail and thin; they grow *heavy*. 

Over decades, as they absorb the copper and iron dissolved in the bilge water, their bones thicken, calcify, and retain the heavy metals. Their joints lose their quick flexibility, turning stiff and thick as tree-knots. When a Sump-Lung’s heart finally slows to a halt—matching a pump that has been shut down for blow-down maintenance—it does not rot in the way an animal rots. 

Its flesh stays firm and pale in the humid heat. The others do not weep or hold ceremony; there is no concept of a soul in a place where light is measured in low-wattage filaments. They simply carry the heavy, iron-laden body to the central sump, where the primary beam pump’s rocker arm dips into the black sludge. They place the body directly under the descending elbow of the piston rod. 

Within three days, the wet, dense soil and the greedily reaching taproots swallow the flesh entirely. The calcium in the heavy bones feeds the liverwort cascades; the copper in the blood stains the surrounding moss a deeper, richer turquoise; and the small iron splints or brass rings the creature carried in its fingers are left behind on the sump floor, where the water churns them down until they are smooth as river gravel.

Above them, in the dark corners where iron girders meet brick vaulting, live the **Graft-Skeins**.

If the Sump-Lungs are the maintenance tools of the lower deck, the Graft-Skeins are the connective tissue of the upper canopy. They rarely descend to the root-mattress. Their legs have withered into short, hook-like anchors, while their arms and ribcages have elongated, lightened, and wrapped themselves in living tendrils of creeping fig and night-blooming cereus.

They spend their lives suspended twenty feet above the catwalks, wedged into the structural angles between the corroded iron I-beams and the steam manifolds. They are thinner, almost translucent, their skin stained dark green by microscopic algae that live within their skin pores, feeding on the stray light from the glass pavement-reflectors high above.

The Graft-Skeins do not use tools. They *are* the valves. 

Where an iron pipe has rusted through and cannot be replaced, a Graft-Skein has wrapped its long, flexible torso around the breach, its skin toughened by decades of contact with 140-degree iron. Over time, the plant life climbing the vault wall has grown *through* the creature’s outer tissue, weaving fibrous roots through its skin, anchoring it to the girder. The creature feels the steam flow through the pipe against its stomach; if the line grows too hot, its body unconsciously reacts, its thick, calloused hands slowly tightening a nearby brass handwheel to bypass the pressure into a secondary cooling coil.

They are conscious, but only in the way a tree is conscious of winter or a boiler is conscious of pressure. They have no dreams. When they close their thick, green-tinted eyes, they hear only the infinite, wet music of their world:
The *thump-clack* of the beam;
The steady, cold *drip-drip-drip* from the vault bricks;
The low, wet tearing sound of a taproot expanding one sixteen-hundredth of an inch into a bed of wet ash;
And the deep, heavy breath of their companions, three levels down, moving through the mist like iron weights swinging in oil.

Nothing watches in the Engine Garden because there is no need for an observer. The garden is its own observer, its own clock, and its own engine. It does not wait for the city above to fall, nor does it remember when the city was built. It simply keeps its forty strokes a minute, drinking its own sweat, growing its green skin over the iron bones of the earth, perfectly content in the warm, loud dark.
