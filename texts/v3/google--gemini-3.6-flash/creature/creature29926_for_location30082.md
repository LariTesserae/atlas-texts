To understand what lives here, you have to stop looking for eyes. In a world illuminated only by a seam of bruised green twilight, eyes are an expensive luxury that the vault stripped away thousands of generations ago. 

Everything that moves in this deep, warm bowl moves by texture, thermal drift, and the fine, electrical mapping of vibration through stone and sap.

Start at the bottom, where the heat rises from the earth's open ribs.

In the slick, liverwort-choked silt, the **calcifiers** work the stone. These are the isopods—not mere bugs, but heavy, slow-grinding crustaceans the size of walnuts, their segmented backs thick with a crust of grey calcium carbonate they harvest from the dripping ceiling. They are the vault’s masons. They feed on the fallen orchid petals and the wet, decaying fronds, their heavy mandibles making a sound like sand being turned over with a trowel. As they move, they rub against the limestone, smoothing the jagged bits, filling micro-cracks with their mineral-rich cast, leaving behind trails of wet, white paste that the mosses immediately drink.

Hunting them through the liverwort matrix are the **milk-skinks**. They are thin as fingers and nearly translucent, their ribs showing through skin that has the texture of boiled pork fat. They have no lung-sacs to swell or deflate; they breathe directly through their damp, vascular hide, absorbing oxygen from the water-logged air. A skink does not strike; it flows. It presses its chest flat against the warm stone, catches the heat-wake of an isopod, and glides forward until its wide, toothless mouth can scoop the crustacean up from behind, swallowing it whole with a soft, wet gulp that leaves the skink’s stomach bulging like a swallowed stone for days.

Higher up, where the air-roots hang like frayed ropes, live the **velvet-heads**. 

They are small mammals, no larger than a child's fist, but built like drops of warm oil. They have no external ears—their skulls are perfectly smooth, covered in a dense, water-repellent pile of charcoal-grey fur that never retains a single bead of moisture. Where eyes should be, there are only twin depressions lined with microscopic, heat-sensitive pit-organs that flicker like tiny red embers when warm air passes over them. 

The velvet-heads do not climb so much as they hang. Their long, six-jointed fingers end in soft, disc-like pads that secrete a faint, sticky musk, allowing them to cling upside down to the slick undersides of the leathery fern fronds for hours without moving. They are the gleaners of the canopy. They wait for the **marrow-moths**—fat, wingless insects whose soft bodies are filled with sweet, fermented orchid nectar—to crawl past. When a moth brushes a velvet-head’s sensory pits, a long, hairless hand shoots out with the silent, terrifying precision of a spring-loaded trap, folding the insect inward and tucking it into a wide, cheek-pouch in a single motion. They never squeak. They communicate with their kin through low, abdominal thumps delivered directly into the wooden vines—vibrations that travel up to the top of the vault like faint heartbeat taps.

In the smooth-bottomed basins and the shallow, water-filled bowls of the ancient steps, a different life takes shape.

Here, in the warm, mineral-thick pools that never drop below eighty degrees, the **ribbon-frogs** breed. They are tiny—barely the size of a fingernail—and so clear that when they sit on the grey limestone, you see only their miniature, copper-colored skeletons and the bright, scarlet marble of their beating hearts. Their life is measured by the weather of the cave. When the cloud of micro-mist forms every few hours, the frogs crawl out of the pools by the hundreds, scaling the parabolic limestone walls with wet *shluck-shluck* sounds to lick the tiny beads of warm moisture off the rock face. They lay their eggs not in free water, but in gelatinous ribbons that they wrap around the thumb-sized depressions of the dark basalt lintel, anchoring the next generation to the only stone in the chamber that does not dissolve.

And then there is the thing that sits by the lintel.

It is not a monster, nor is it a survivor of whatever kin carved those four wide steps ten thousand years ago. It is simply what happens when a air-breathing creature stays in a hot, dark room for long enough that its lineage forgets the sun.

It is the **Vault-Sitter**, though there is only ever one at a time, or perhaps a pair so still they seem like part of the same moss-draped shelf. 

It is thick-bodied and low to the ground, four or five feet from snout to blunt tail, with a torso like a water-logged log and skin the pale, bruised violet of a scraped turnip. It has no fur, no scales, no claws. Its limbs are short, ending in broad, five-lobed paddles that fit precisely into the rounded terraces of the stone. Its head is broad and flat, dominated by a wide, lip-less slit of a mouth and a crest of soft, fleshy tentacles around its throat that sweep the humid air like pale sea anemones, reading the chemical shifts of the mist.

The Sitter does not hunt the skinks or the velvet-heads; its mouth is built for sucking up the thick carpet of oil-slick moss and the gelatinous frog-ribbons that coat the stone. It moves perhaps three feet in a day, creeping along the ancient steps with a slow, heavy drag that leaves a wide, polished track in the algae. 

For hours at a time—sometimes for days—it remains entirely motionless at the basalt lintel. It rests its broad, soft forepaws directly in those three worn, thumb-sized depressions, its body bowing low over the dark stone. Its heart beats twice a minute. When the fine, warm mist drops from the high ceiling, the water trickles down the folds of its loose, grey back, feeding a garden of pale liverworts that have taken root directly in the skin folds between its shoulder blades.

When it breathes—a slow, shallow rasp that happens once every thirty seconds—it releases a faint, sweet cloud of fermented sap-gas that draws the marrow-moths down from the high vines. The velvet-heads follow the moths; the skinks follow the fallen larvae; the isopods clean the droppings from the stone beneath its belly. 

The Sitter is not the master of the vault; it is simply its pendulum. It marks the heat, feels the micro-vibrations of the earth shifting deep below the thermal seams, and holds its palms against the basalt, anchoring the wet, dark, furious little world to the cold, forgotten bones of the hill above.
