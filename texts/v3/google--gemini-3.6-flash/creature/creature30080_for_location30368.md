They are not creatures of the surface that fell, nor are they fish that rose. They are stone-heavy, lungless things—the *Grasp-born*, if they have a name at all—built not to swim through the dark, but to anchor against it.

They have no fins, no scales, no buoyancy. In a world that wants to throw everything over the edge of the drowned valleys, their anatomy is an architecture of resistance. 

To look upon one in the brief, thermal glow of a vent is to see a silhouette carved from wet slate and tallow. They are squat, broad-shouldered, and four-limbed, their torsos thick as forge-bellows to withstand the crushing weight of hundreds of fathoms. Their skin is smooth, hairless, and oily, a dense layer of dark, blubberous hide that retains heat and sloughs off the calcifying crust of the mineral water. Where a man’s ribs would flex, their ribcages are solid plates of fused bone, protecting hearts that beat only twice or thrice a minute, pumping thick, iron-black blood through bodies that move with agonizing, efficient slowness.

They are blind. Their eyes are mere smooth mounds of pale scar tissue beneath the hide, long useless in a basin where sunlight dies a hundred feet down. Instead, their heads are broad and wedge-shaped, dominated by a thick, horseshoe-shaped ridge of cartilage across the snout and jaw. This is their map. The cartilage is packed with dense nerve-nodes that read the water—not by sight, but by the subtle, violent shift in hydrostatic pressure, the salinity of the thermal plumes, and the precise, bone-rattling resonance of the basin’s song.

They do not speak with lungs. They speak through the basalt. 

When two of them meet along the cordage paths, they press their broad, flat foreheads together, or strike the stone between them with heavy, bone-knuckled wrists. The vibrations travel through their dense skeletons, bypassing ears entirely to vibrate directly in the fluid of their inner skulls. A sharp knicking against the basalt means *the Western Current comes*; a long, dragged scrape of stone means *the kelp-sap is rich*.

Their hands are their greatest tool and their only anchor. They are wide, four-digit paddles ending not in nails, but in blunt, hooked spurs of overgrown bone. These spurs fit perfectly into the flutes and chisel-marks they have carved into the rock over generations. When the roaring wall of the four-hour current cascades over the western ridge, they do not hide—they merely drop to all fours, slot their hand-spurs into the basalt grooves, and let the ice-cold avalanche scour over their backs. The water pulls at them with the force of a team of horses, but they are as rooted as the mountains.

They are harvesters, bound to the rhythm of the drown-valleys. 

They live in the streamlined pine-log shelters pinned beneath the boulders, sleeping wedged together in tight, slick knots of flesh to share what little body heat they generate. Their life is an endless, methodical pilgrimage along the guide-ropes. 

When the thermal vents belch their sulfurous plumes and the gland-kelp writhes and slaps against the cold, the Grasp-born move into the canopy. Using heavy scrapers fashioned from chipped flint and tied to their wrists with reed-cordage, they bleed the kelp. They notch the pressurized trunks, catching the thick, golden, oil-rich sap in pouches made from the dried, tanned bladders of the plant itself. This oil is their lifeblood: they smear it over their skin to stave off the freezing brine, use it to preserve their cordage against rot, and swallow it in thick, gelled lumps for sustenance.

Beside the Grasp-born, the black water harbors other life—none of it possessing a fin, a shell, or an eye.

In the dense, carpeted turf of the iron-moss live the *Slag-worms*. They are long, pale ribbons of pure muscle, thick as a man’s thigh and twenty feet long, possessing neither joints nor bones. They move through the moss like sluggish lightning, squeezing themselves through the crevices of the basalt. They have no jaws; instead, their undersides are lined with thousands of microscopic, chemical-secreting pores that dissolve the iron-crusted rock and the decaying kelp-fronds, turning stone into a sludgy, nutrient-dense paste. The Grasp-born hunt them with heavy flint-spits, driving the stone points through the worms' rubbery flesh to harvest their dense, fat-rich muscle bands.

Deeper in the hanging valleys, where the glass-reeds chime their metallic scream, float the *Drift-velvets*. They are translucent, skin-like sheets of living tissue, broad as barn doors, that have no organs save for a wet, pulsing digestive underside. They do not swim; they are carried entirely by the violent currents. They roll and fold through the dark like discarded silk, wrapping around the hollow glass-reeds to strip them of the mineral crust and fungi that cling to the calcified stalks. To the Grasp-born, the Drift-velvets are both a hazard and a resource: a loose velvet can wrap around a harvester's face and smother them against the rock, but if caught and dried upon the wooden hurdles in the basalt rings, their leathery hides form the pitch-coated shrouds used to roof their stone-anchored shelters.

Nothing here is light. Nothing here drifts without purpose or anchor. 

When a Grasp-born grows old—when their bone-spurs wear smooth against the basalt and their hands can no longer hold the cordage against the roar of the four-hour surge—they do not drown, for they have no lungs to fill. They simply let go of the rope. 

The current catches them, dragging their heavy, iron-dense bodies out of the glass-reed passes, over the lip of the submerged gorge, and down into the absolute, pathless abyss of the lower world, where even the sound of the kelp-drums dies, and the heavy water holds them forever.
