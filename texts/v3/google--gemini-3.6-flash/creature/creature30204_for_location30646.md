To live here, you must be built to survive the thunder.

Nothing in this basin possesses delicate ears. The tympanic membranes of any ordinary creature would be shredded within minutes by the organ-pipe bass and the hydraulic roar of the sap-conduits. Instead, the inhabitants of the living plaza hear entirely through their bones.

The dominant dwellers—the ones who occupy the hollow trunks and walk the high-wire sky-bridges—are tall, jointed entities whose skin is identical in texture to the grey, water-smoothed wood of the city itself. They look like limbs carved from petrified root, slender and five-jointed at the knee and elbow, moving with a fluid, terrifying patience. They have no faces in the human sense. Where eyes and mouths should be, their heads are smooth, oblong domes of polished amber-colored cuticle, pitted with tiny, dark heat-sensing pores that allow them to read the fractured, geometric bands of bent sunlight without being blinded.

They do not walk along the ground; the ground is a churning floor of mud, sloughed bark, and red-shelled scavengers. Instead, they move along the vertical planes and the undersides of the leaf-pulp terraces. The undersides of their broad, flat feet are covered in thousands of microscopic, wet cilia that create a powerful vacuum against the wet cellulose. A citizen of this place will step off a high balcony, turn upside down, and stride casually along the belly of an ancient vine, their body dangling over a hundred feet of empty air, unaffected by the warp and weave of the local gravity.

They do not speak through the air. To communicate, they press their broad, flat sternums against the bark of the nearest trunk. Deep within their ribcages lie dense, calcified organs—heavy as ironwood—that strike against their inner bone walls, sending sharp, rhythmic acoustic pulses through the plant’s vascular system. A message struck into a trunk on the western wall travels through the dense, sap-soaked wood in fractions of a second, emerging as a distinct, mechanical vibration felt in the feet of another dweller three hundred yards away on the opposite side of the plaza. To an outsider, they seem entirely silent, moving in a graceful, silent choreography through a landscape that is screaming at the threshold of physical endurance.

They are not builders; they are surgeons and cultivators. They carry no metal blades, for metal would rot or be swallowed by the quick-growing wood. Instead, they carry living, parasite-slugs wrapped around their forearms—creatures whose acidic saliva can dissolve hardened cambium in seconds. With these, the dwellers trim back the vines that grow too fast, carve fresh drainage channels for the condensation, and sculpt the expanding bark-sphincters to keep the tenement doorways from closing shut.

Shared alongside them are the creatures of the mid-air and the sap-conduits, each finely tuned to the city’s bizarre physics:

**The Air-Marrow:**
Floating in the sunlit void of the central plaza are hundreds of featherless, translucent organisms the size of hound-dogs. Their bodies are thin, vein-laced bladders filled with warm, biologically generated methane gas harvested from the fermenting root-bowls below. They have no wings, only long, feathery tendrils that trail behind them like kite strings. They ride the thermal updrafts that pour out of the central hollows, drifting upward until the distorted gravity catches them, turning them upside down to graze on the thick clouds of golden spores suspended in the humidity. When they wish to descend, they contract their skin-sacks, squirting a jet of warm liquid into the air and dropping like lead weights until they catch a passing sky-bridge with their prehensile tail-hooks.

**The Valve-Borers:**
Deep inside the walls, where the sap surges with the pressure of a steam boiler, live the thick-bodied, armor-plated creatures responsible for the city’s acoustics. These are the air-horn amphibians and giant beetles mentioned in the whispers of the place. They are heavy, squat things, shaped like river stones, with mouthparts like hydraulic drill bits. They bore directly into the phloem pipes, plugging the holes with their own hardened, chitinous backs. By flexing their abdomen valves, they divert the high-pressure sap into the hollow organ-trunk chambers, intentionally generating the deep sub-bass thrum that vibrates the entire basin. They feed on the sugar-rich resin that bypasses their bodies, growing so heavy and calcified over centuries that they eventually become part of the wood itself, their fossilized shells serving as step-nodes for the taller dwellers.

**The Pod-Born:**
The population here does not give birth, nor do they lay eggs in nests. They are fruit. 

Every few hours, when a pressurized seed-pod at the top of the canopy explodes with that wet, cannon-like *crack*, it does not merely scatter timber-seeds. Scattered among the heavy nuts are smooth, rubbery galls the size of a man’s torso. These galls crash onto the lower terraces, bouncing off the springy leaf-pulp before coming to rest in the warm, dripping condensation pools at the base of the pillars.

Within hours, the rubbery skin of the gall is dissolved by the acidic pool-water. From inside uncurls a young dweller—pale, tender, and soft as boiled pith. The older, hardened citizens wait at the edge of the pools, reaching down with long, five-jointed arms to haul the wet newborn out of the soup. They immediately press the young one’s chest against the mother-trunk, introducing it to the roar, the thrum, and the endless, structural conversation of the wood.

And when a dweller reaches the end of its long life—after centuries of scaling vertical bark and sculpting cambium—it does not perish and decay. Its movement simply slows. Its skin thickens, losing its flexibility and growing pale, rough, and fibrous. It selects a high, sunlit terrace where the growth is fast. It presses its feet deep into a moisture groove, wraps its long arms around a supporting pillar, and stops moving. Within weeks, the vine-tendrils sweep over it; the bark swells up from below to swallow its ankles; its amber head-dome dulls into dry cork. It becomes a new spur on a helical staircase, a new bracket for a sky-bridge, or a fresh rib in the wall of a tenement—leaving behind its bones to amplify the eternal, deafening song of the plant that birthed it.
