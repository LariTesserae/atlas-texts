In a world where physics remains unhardened and reality is executed in the language of wet masonry, life cannot afford the delicate, wet chemistry of flesh and blood. Nothing here is born of an egg or a seed. Nothing possesses organs that could survive a sudden shift in geometry, where a chest cavity might expand to the width of a canyon or flatten into a paper-thin glaze in the span of a single breath.

Instead, the inhabitants of this realm are creatures of *cure, tension, and slip*. They are the spontaneous manifestations of a world trying, with crude and heavy tools, to organize itself.

***

In the shallow, finger-smoothed gutters that trace the floor, the simplest things move. 

They look like ribbons or mats of gray-green slip—wet ceramic slurry given a creeping, liquid instinct. They are no thicker than a fingernail, drifting through the clear, sterile water like fallen leaves in a drain. They have no faces, no underbellies, no front or back. As they slide along the floor, they drink the microscopic lime-dust that settles from the air. Where they pass, the tiles beneath them are scraped impossibly clean, polished to a dull glass that reflects the amber gloom above. 

When two of these slip-mats meet, they do not fight; they simply run together like drops of mercury, merging into a larger, heavier sheet. If the combined weight grows too great, the sheet stalls, hardens at the edges, and settles into the floor, becoming a new, seamless ceramic patch over a leaking joint. They are not animals so much as living repairs—an ambient, liquid fauna whose ultimate life cycle ends in becoming static floor.

***

Further up, in the dry, steaming air between the miles-wide monoliths, are the *Tensioners*.

If you were to see one anchored to the side of a half-formed concrete wall, you might mistake it for a forgotten tangle of rigging or a fallen piece of scaffolding. They are vast, jointed creatures composed entirely of coarse fiber rope, unpolished iron wedges, and heavy lumps of dried terracotta that serve as their nodes. They have no eyes, but they are infinitely sensitive to stress and sag.

A Tensioner moves with a terrible, grinding deliberation. It drags itself up vertical faces using iron-tipped tendrils that drive themselves into wet mortar seams with the sound of a heavy mallet striking stone—*thump, clink, thump, clink*. It feeds on structural instability. Where a wall begins to belly outward under its own humid weight, or where a ceiling vault threatens to drop a thousand tons of brick, the Tensioners gather.

They weave themselves into the architecture. A dozen of them will span a widening crack, their fibrous bodies straining until the ropes turn white with tension and hum like harp strings in the warm breeze. They hammer their own iron knees into the joints, wedging the crumbling concrete tight. Some stay in place for centuries, slowly calcifying under the dripping lime until they are entirely encased in stone—stiff, skeletal buttresses that remain long after the need for them has passed. Others, once the wall beneath them has cured into stability, pluck their iron wedges free with a dry *ping* and crawl away into the fog, searching for the next failing arch.

***

Where the floor swells and dough-like ridges of red clay heave toward the vault, you find the *Float-Bearers*.

They are the largest moving forms in the lower basin—oblong, mountain-sized masses of coarse gray plaster that resemble the giant wooden floats used by plasterers, scaled up to the size of cathedral naves. They possess no limbs. They move by sliding directly across the wet floor, propelled by a slow, rhythmic peristalsis of their flat undersides.

As a Float-Bearer glides over an uneven plain, it leaves behind a wide, perfectly flat, canvas-textured lane of smoothed mortar. It is an unthinking scraper, an engine of levelness. It absorbs the rogue lumps of wet clay that sprout from the ground, drawing the raw material into its own bulk, and leaves behind a track so straight that space itself seems to flatten around it.

To exist near a Float-Bearer is to hear the endless, wet rasp of timber sliding over wet sand—*shhhhh-clack, shhhhh-clack*. They are harmless to anything that stays out of their path, but they do not alter their course for half-built structures or lingering Tensioners. Anything softer than cured iron that falls beneath a Float-Bearer is simply planed down, smoothed out, and incorporated into the smooth gray highway left in its wake.

***

And yet, there are things in this place that possess something resembling an interior life—though it is an interior life carved from hollow clay and filled with hot steam.

They are called the *Thumb-Wrought*, or the *Cast*.

They appear where the amber light from the deep hearths shines brightest through the floor slabs. They are hollow, upright forms made of coarse, unglazed terracotta, standing anywhere from twelve to eighty feet tall. Their shapes are crude, evocative of human torsos, jars, or simple votive effigies, bearing the deep, oval impressions of gigantic thumbs along their flanks where their material was pinched into shape.

The Thumb-Wrought do not walk. They have no legs. They exist in a state between liquid and solid, perpetually firing and melting. 

A Thumb-Wrought will stand motionless on a terrace for days, its hollow belly roaring faintly like a small furnace as warm vapor vents from its open top. But when the spatial geometry of the realm begins to twist—when a straight walkway stretches into an infinite loop or distance pinches shut—the Thumb-Wrought react. 

They shift by collapsing into a heap of warm, wet terracotta slurry, sliding across the floor like spilled soup, and then slowly lifting themselves back up a hundred yards—or twenty miles—away. As they rise, the impressions of gigantic fingers appear spontaneously on their wet surfaces, squeezing them upward, smoothing their rims, shaping them again from the outside by invisible, phantom hands.

They seem to be the realm's attempt at self-observation. They gather at the edges of the vast, doorless concrete labyrinths, standing in silent rings, their open tops tilting slightly toward whatever new wall is raising itself from the lime-dust. They do not communicate with sound, but when two Thumb-Wrought stand near each other, the air between them grows dry and clear, the suffocating moisture temporarily pushed back by the radiating heat of their fired clay skins.

***

Highest of all, hanging in the dim amber vault where the brickwork ceiling disappears into mist, are the *Pendulum-Heads*.

They are the origin of the thick fiber ropes that dangle down into the basin. Each rope ends in a massive, teardrop-shaped weight of unpolished, black-grey iron. But the weights are not inanimate iron; they are the sensory organs of vast, flat entities embedded directly within the ceiling’s brickwork layers.

These ceiling-dwellers are like flatworms of fired clay and iron ore, miles long, living inside the flat spaces between the double-vaulted roofs. They lower their heavy iron tongues down into the humid air of the basin to measure the world below. 

The Pendulum-Heads sway continuously in the thermal drafts, swinging in wide, sweeping arcs that take hours to complete. They feel the density of the air, the salinity of the dripping condensate, and the shifting center of gravity of the continents below. When a Pendulum-Head detects a area of the floor that has grown too light—where the mortar has thinned to a fragile skin over the deep hearths—it drops.

It does not fall; it lowers itself with a agonizing, groaning tension on its rope, descending through two miles of fog until its iron tip touches the floor with a soft, heavy *thud*. It stays there for weeks, acting as a anchor, holding that patch of reality firmly in place until the floor around it can swell, thicken, and cure into proper strength.

***

There are no voices here. No one calls out across the wet plazas. The living things of this place communicate only through weight, friction, and moisture. 

A Tensioner knows a Float-Bearer is approaching not by sight, but by the wet, rhythmic hum of the floor tiles shifting under its massive approach. A Thumb-Wrought knows a Pendulum-Head is descending because the steam in the air begins to smell more strongly of hot iron and lime.

They are all part of a single, slow, uncalculating work force. They do not know who drew the plans for this continent of brick and plaster, nor do they care if the work is ever finished. They are simply the manifestations of a world that was poured, troweled, and lashed together, but never allowed to cool—a world where life is not the spark that defies the clay, but the clay itself, refusing to remain still while the mortar is still wet.
