The thing that lives here is the reason the clay never dries.

It is a singular creature, though in the folded, endless loop of the cell, it often appears as a procession of three or four identical backs stooping along the spiraling ramp. It is heavy—weighing as much as a draft horse—yet its body is built entirely of soft, scalded cartilage and dense, waterlogged muscle, leaving no hard skeleton to clatter against the wet walls. 

Its skin has the color and texture of boiled suet, thick and hairless, constantly sweating a milky, alkaline grease that smells sharply of turnip and old tallow. It has no eyes; where a face might be expected, there is only a broad, convex plate of wet, grey horn, smooth as a river pebble, which it presses directly against the terracotta walls to "see." By feeling the vibrations of the wind outside shearing past the fat-plugged apertures, it knows the exact thickness of the mud shell at any given inch.

Its breath is the primary furnace of the place. Two massive, valve-like blowholes beneath its shoulder blades expand and contract with a wet, sucking sound, drawing in the humid air and exhaling it back out as a sour, ninety-degree vapor. This is the herbivore breath that fills the dome. It does not sleep; instead, it enters brief, standing trances, wedging its thick rump into the curve where the floor becomes the ceiling, its blowholes rhythmically spouting thin jets of boiling mist that keep the celery-vines plump and popping.

Its hands are its most extraordinary feature. There are four of them, set on short, muscular forearms that emerge from its thick chest. They are wide, spatulate, and utterly flat on the underside—less like claws and more like living trowels. The fingers are fused into solid pads of toughened leather, perfectly matching the broad, overlapping thumb-grooves that pattern the wet mud of the walls. It is *its* palms that made those marks. It is making them still. 

In its belly, within a deep pouch of folded skin, it carries the materials of its eternal maintenance. It does not find the ash-wood or the bone pegs; it secretes them. When an old beam begins to rot from the constant moisture, the creature reaches into its ventral fold, pulls forth a length of pale, dense cartilage that hardens upon contact with the steam, and drives it through the wall with a single, heavy strike of its horny face-plate. The marrow still clinging to the pegs is its own, scraped fresh from its interior ribs.

It feeds on two things: the celery-vines and its own tail.

Every few days, as the pale vines knot too tightly around the bone pegs and threaten to strangle the cell’s skeleton, the creature grazes them back to the mud with broad, flat plates of yellow tooth. It crunches the watery stems with a sound like snapping fresh bones, swallowing the scalding sap to replenish its own internal reservoir. And when the vines grow sparse, it reaches behind itself, takes the blunted tip of its own regenerating tail in its trowel-hands, and shears off a thumb-length joint with a quick bite. This fat, pale meat is its fuel for the harder labor—the continuous re-plastering of the ceiling.

It moves with a rhythmic, heavy swaying, navigating the impossible geometry of the cell with complete indifference. To watch it walk the spiraling ramp is to watch a lesson in topological grace: its front feet tread upon the lowermost clay pots of blue liverwort, while its high, pale back brushes against the ceiling floor five feet above, leaving a smear of grease that immediately seeds a new tangle of green roots.

It is not lonely, for it lacks the void inside itself that loneliness requires. It is an engine of dampness and weight. It knows, through the horn-plate on its head, that outside the thin tallow plugs there is only cold, blue emptiness—an abyss that would freeze its soft, scalded meat to stone in a second. So it continues its endless lap around the central pillar. It slaps fresh, warm mud over the micro-fissures. It drives a new bone peg where the gut-string sings too high in the wind. It breathes its hot, sour breath into the wet dark, keeping the bell boiling, keeping the world small enough to hold.
