To understand what lives here, you must first unlearn the mechanics of flesh that requires water. Here, life does not leak. It does not sweat, bleed, or tear easily. It is an architecture of dry friction, porcelain-bone, and thermal exchange.

If you sit long enough on the brass grate near the opened ceramic furnaces, the air begins to tell you who walks these sheet-iron streets.

First come the **Solderers**—the most common upright inhabitants of the upper city notch. They emerge not from doors, but from the deep, dry shadows between the copper-domed power stations, moving with a heavy, deliberate triple-gait. They stand roughly eight feet tall, though their spines are curved in a long, load-bearing arch like the corbels supporting the streets. Their hides are not leather or skin, but a tight, overlapping armor of boiled fibrous horn and dark, nickel-veined slate, completely matte and perpetually warm to the touch.

They have no mouths in the human sense. Where a lower jaw might be, there is a broad, triangular plate of self-sharpening magnetite, used to scrape pure mineral scale from the sides of the dry aqueducts. They feed on the static potential of the environment; to them, a hot iron plate under the amber sun is a banquet, and an unshielded copper cable hanging over the chasm is a roaring hearth.

Watch how two Solderers meet on a cast-iron sky-bridge. They do not gesture with their arms, which are long, five-jointed, and tipped with four heavy, pincer-like talons capable of crushing raw basalt into gravel. Instead, they step together until their broad, plate-armored chests press firmly against one another with a dull, metallic *thunk*. 

They stand locked in this embrace for twenty minutes, perfectly still in the high amber light. They are talking. Communication here is conductive; it moves as a sequence of microscopic, high-frequency voltage ripples passing from chest-plate to chest-plate, felt directly through the rib cage. To stand near them during a conversation is to feel a faint, prickling itch in your teeth and the hair on your arms rising toward their dark shoulders. When they part, both bear a slight glow of residual static where their hides rubbed together, a soft bronze luminescence that takes an hour to fade.

***

Farther up the cliff-face, where the millions of rectangular recesses honeycomb the sheer magnetite walls, live the **Stills**.

For a long time, you might mistake the niches for empty tombs or discarded storage lockers. But if you watch through a high-magnification glass as the sun hits a particular vector of the cliff, you will see a pale, jointed plate extend from the darkness of a recess, grip the outer lip of the rock, and pull a heavy, segmented body an inch into the light.

The Stills are the ancient core of this world’s population. They are much longer than the Solderers—fifteen feet from head-plate to tail-spur—with flattened, ribbon-like bodies made of interlocking rings of pale, porcelain-like bone. They are designed to fit perfectly into the dry carved slots of the Massif. 

They do not walk the streets. They spend decades—sometimes centuries—slid into their magnetite niches, acting as living thermal batteries for the mountain itself. Their bodies absorb the 85-degree heat radiated by the deep geothermal hearths, store it within dense, lead-heavy internal sacs of metallic paraffin, and slowly release it back into the rock face during the rare orbital eclipses when the pale sun goes dim.

The Stills wake only when the planet’s magnetic poles shift by a fraction of a degree, or when the deep pneumatic pulse from the mountain’s root changes its rhythm. When a Still decides to move, the sound is like a thousand heavy slate tiles sliding over one another. They crawl out along the high narrow ledges, their amber mica-lenses blinking against the sun, to groom one another with stiff brushes of spun brass wire, sweeping away the flour-fine plum dust that gathers in their joint-seams.

***

Down beneath your feet, far below the brass grates and the silent flywheels, are the **Weavers of the Shafts**.

If the Solderers maintain the surface and the Stills stabilize the rock, the Weavers belong to the dark, vertical shafts that drop seven miles to the dust-ocean floor. You rarely see them in full sunlight, but you can track them by their works: the sweeping parabolas of tree-thick electric cable that bridge the mountain needles were not cast in foundries; they were *spun*.

A Weaver is a creature of long, needle-like proportion. Its limbs are made of hollow, light-weight titanium-boned spars, and its main body is a compact, spherical shell of compressed graphite. They move along the undersides of the bridges and the vertical faces of the iron cliffs with terrifying, weightless grace, held in place by powerful electromagnetic pads in their feet.

From the rear of their graphite carapaces, they extrude thin filaments of flexible copper-sheathed carbon fiber. They weave these threads into the massive power lines that hum through the city. A team of four Weavers, working in absolute, rhythmic silence over a span of thirty years, can construct a sky-bridge cable capable of carrying three million volts of geothermal static across a ten-league chasm. 

They do not know hurry. A Weaver will sit suspended over a five-mile drop for three months, holding a single structural strand in place with its jaw-vices until the metal cold-welds under the sun’s dry heat, its dark eyes turned toward the crimson dust-tides below, completely indifferent to the wind.

***

And then, deep in the heart of the silent foundries—where the massive riveted stacks still radiate their low-mantle warmth—there are the **Pulse-Keepers**.

These are not individual beasts, but collective colonies of microscopic, iron-ingesting iron-spores that inhabit the city's internal pneumatic pipes and geothermal channels. They are the ones who trigger the three-hour exhalation. 

Over three hours, as the geothermal pressure builds in the deep basalt chambers beneath the Massif, these microscopic colonies cluster along the lip of the giant brass release valves. Their weight keeps the heavy seals shut, allowing the pressure to mount until it reaches a critical density. Then, at the precise moment, the heat causes the colony to momentarily alter its magnetic polarity; they scatter, the valve drops, and the mountain releases its long, sighing *houghhhh*. 

The nitrogen rushes through the shafts, clearing the iron dust from the internal coils, cooling the copper housings, and feeding the low, dry metabolism of everything that clings to the rock.

***

To live here is to be part of a single, slow, solid-state machine.

There is no concept of youth or old age as wetter worlds understand it. Nothing rots. A Solderer who ceases to move does not decay; its joints simply lock as the internal mineral fats solidify. Its body remains standing on the iron street where it stopped, serving as a windbreak for the shard-beetles, or as a fresh grounding post for the next generation to touch when they need to share their static.

After a few decades, the dust gathers in the folds of the fallen creature's slate hide. A Riveter will wander over, weld its heavy legs securely to the street plate so the wind cannot tip it, and peel a few loose flakes of nickel from its crest to mend a comb in the lower galleries. 

Eventually, the fallen Solderer becomes indistinguishable from the street, the corbels, or the mountain walls. It becomes part of the architecture, waiting patiently for the centuries to turn, as smooth, dry, and warm as the rock from which it was born.
