To see what lives here, you must first unlearn how the eye searches for life. You cannot look for color, for softness, or for rapid movement. If you stand on the edge of a three-foot cistern and look across the shimmering, wet plain of slate, the basin appears entirely deserted—an empty machine waiting for a master who left yesterday.

In truth, the inhabitants are everywhere, but their camouflage is not a trick of pigment; it is a identity of substance. They are made of the same dark, fine-grained slate and grey soapstone on which they rest. 

They are the Keepers, though they have no name for themselves, nor any language that requires air.

At first glance, a Keeper resting on a terrace rim looks like a fallen capstone or an unusually thick slate riser, eighteen inches wide and five feet long. Their bodies are low, flat, and hard-edged, perfectly adapted to the narrow geometry of the basin. Their carapaces are not smooth shells, but layered, overlapping plates of natural mineral—dense, dense-grained metamorphic bone that has absorbed the ice-cold moisture of the plateau for so long that it sweats the same glass-like film of pure water as the walls around them. 

Their arrises are as crisp as the stone. There are no eyes, no ears, no soft mouths. Where a face might be, there is only a clean, wedge-shaped prow of polished soapstone, fitted with a pair of narrow, dark respiratory slits that draw in the thin, cold air with a sound no louder than the wind passing over a open pipe. 

They do not walk; they slide. The undersides of their bodies are coated in a dense, cool lipid—a sterile, lime-rich tallow that they secrete continuously. When a Keeper moves from one cistern rim to another, it shifts with an excruciating, heavy smoothness, sliding along the six-inch slate edges like a wet stone tile pushing over another. The sound of their passage is a quiet, wet *shrr-shrr*, instantly drowned out by the gentle *pock-pock* of dripping water.

They are few in number—perhaps a few hundred scattered across the three-mile basin—but their slowness makes them seem omnipresent. Time for them is measured not in hours, but in the filling and spilling of the troughs. A Keeper may sit motionless in a knee-deep runoff trough for sixteen days, its body acting as an intentional dam. As the cold condensation pools against its flank, the water level rises until it reaches the exact four-degree pitch needed to overflow into a lower drain-shaft. Only when the lower basin is full will the Keeper shift three inches to the left, breaking its own dam and allowing the trough to clear.

They are the reason the stone remains so unnaturally fresh. 

They do not use tools, but they *are* the continuation of the tools. At the ends of their heavy, jointed fore-limbs are short, thick spires of natural bronze-colored mineral—dense, metallic growths formed from the trace elements leaching out of the deep soapstone beds. When a winter frost threatens to split a joint, or when a tiny chip of loose slate wedges into a drain, a Keeper will spend hours tapping at the stone with its fore-limbs. 

*Clink. Clink. Clink.*

The sound is tiny, sharp, and precise. It is the exact acoustic match of the bronze-tipped chisels that built the plateau. They chip away the uneven micro-fractures, smooth the arrises with the tough, leathery aprons beneath their jaws, and press their own lime-heavy secretions into the hairline joints. They mend the masonry before it can decay. They are a living mortar, an immune system for three miles of cut rock.

If you submerge your hand in one of the square cistern wells, you might feel one of them resting at the bottom. The water is so cold it aches, but the creature beneath is colder still—a heavy, motionless slab of slick grey tile that does not flinch from your touch. It does not feel like flesh; it feels like the mountain itself, cut, squared, and given a slow, liquid pulse.

They do not eat plants or hunt prey, for none exists. They derive their life entirely from the physical dynamics of the basin: the intense, indirect light that bakes their upper plates; the subtle electrical currents generated by millions of gallons of sterile water moving through thin, slate-lined channels; and the microscopic mineral salts caught in the condensing fog. 

In the late afternoon, when the pale sun drops low and the intense glare turns the wet plateau into a single sheet of blinding, metallic silver, the Keepers gather along the northern drop-off. They line up along the outermost rim of the masonry, where the plateau ends and the empty air drops three thousand feet into the lower mist. 

They do not look out over the edge—they have no sight. They simply align their flat, heavy bodies with the grid of the stone, facing the wind, their slate carapaces reflecting the dying light until they are indistinguishable from the terrace itself. As the temperature plunges and the ice-thin water on the stone begins to glaze, they lock their plates together, sealing their limbs into the fitted grooves of the rim.

Through the freeze of the night, they remain completely still, holding the stone together against the frost, humming very quietly as the cold air drains down through the vents in their backs and disappears into the dark, wet subterranean shafts below.
