The hands that made the fingerprints are still here. They have not left, because there is nowhere else for them to go, and because the wet clay never truly finishes drying in the dark.

They are people, or the descendants of people, but reduced to the geometry of the gaps. To live in a world where columns stand less than a forearm’s length apart, a body must abandon breadth. They are unnaturally long, flat-chested, and ribless in appearance, their collarbones tilted steeply inward so that their shoulders fold forward like the wings of a plucked bird. They do not walk; they slide. They move through the dense thickets of masonry with a dry, rhythmic friction, shuffling sideways, their bellies pressed against one course of river-silt brick while their spines scrape the rough terra-cotta of the next.

Their skin is the color of pale river silt, permanently stained with dark streaks of bitumen around the knees, elbows, and knuckles. It is a skin without hair, slick with a fine, continuous sheen of cold condensation that smells of lime, river water, and raw copper. Because there is no sun, they have no eyes—or rather, their eyes have retreated beneath thick, seamless mounds of scar-like tissue that merge smoothly into their foreheads. 

Instead, their faces are dominated by their ears and their hands. Their ears are large, thin, and shell-like, lacking lobes, angled perpetually forward to catch the booming, bell-like *pock* of water striking hollow pipes miles away. Their hands are monstrously long, the fingers twice the length of a normal man’s, ending in thick, spade-shaped nails of hardened, yellowed keratin. These nails are kept square and sharp, worn down by a lifetime of wedging slate shingles into place and scraping excess mortar from wet seams.

They are silent creatures. They possess lungs—deep, narrow bellows that expand upward into their long throats rather than outward against their constricted ribs—but they have no vocal cords. When they communicate across the vast, booming distance of the basin, they use the architecture itself. 

A builder will stand frozen in a narrow seam between two spires, listening to the roaring organ-tone of the wind through the weep-holes. To send a signal, they do not speak; they strike a tile with the flat of a heavy, calloused palm, or pluck a tensioned, bitumen-soaked rope with a thumb. The sound shoots through the hollow urns under the floor, bouncing off millions of hard, wet surfaces, carrying a specific, rhythmic vibration for three miles. Far away in the pitch-black, another long, flat body feels the hum in the soles of its feet, adjusts its grip on a stone hammer, and wedged another slate shingle deeper into a failing seam.

They do not sleep lying down. The floor, though flexed and cushioned by the buried urns, is too cold, and to lie flat is to invite the pooling condensation into one’s lungs. Instead, they sleep wedged vertically between the columns. They slot themselves into the narrow vertical channels, hooking their long, jointed elbows and knobby knees into the gaps between bricks, hanging suspended in the dark like dried fish hung in a shed. While they sleep, their skin absorbs the lime-water dripping from the ceiling, their bodies slowly calcifying, their joints stiffening until they must crack themselves loose upon waking with a sound like splitting kindling.

They feed on the only organic material that enters the basin: a dense, rubbery grease that seeps down through the unglazed pottery of the high vaults from some unknown, oil-rich stratum above. It gathers on the underside of the terra-cotta tubes in thick, translucent drops, smelling faintly of old fat and cold iron. They scrape it off with their fingernails and eat it raw, along with thin flakes of the white saltpeter efflorescence that they peel from the brickwork like skin from a blistered arm.

Beside the builders, there are the creatures of the deep floor—the ones hinted at in the pale dust. 

They are not insects, though they move with a jointed, mechanical economy. They are *stilt-walkers*, creatures the length of a man’s leg but no thicker than a wrist, constructed of a pale, cartilaginous material that feels like wet horn. They live entirely within the subterranean honeycomb of the hollow urns beneath the ceramic pavement. 

Millions of them inhabit the dark spaces underfoot. When a builder shuffles across the floor and the tiles flex slightly on their bed of clay, air is forced out of the urns below with a soft, wheezing sigh. This displacement of air draws the stilt-walkers upward. They emerge through tiny, triangular weep-holes cut into the floor tiles, their long, needle-thin legs clicking softly against the sharp edges—the only soft, organic sound in the entire acoustic furnace of the basin.

These pale things are the cleaners and the balancers of the masonry. They possess no mouths, only a flat, pad-like under-snout that secretes a weak, acidic saliva. They scrape and lick at the lime efflorescence, dissolving the hard white crusts before they can expand and shatter the uncured mortar joints. They are blind, deaf to the air, but exquisitely sensitive to the structural tension of the basin. When a terrace groans five miles away, when a wooden shear-pin begins to shear under a million-ton load of river brick, the stilt-walkers feel the micro-vibrations in the tile under their legs. They react instantly, pouring out of the urns in pale, silent streams, migrating en masse toward the point of stress, where they cluster in thick, writhing mats to lick the sweating mortar dry, hastening its setting before the wall can give way.

There are no children here. Or, if there are, they are indistinguishable from the adults—born small, flat, and soft in the wet space between four columns, their fingers already whorled with the fingerprints that will be pressed into the next course of tiles before their eyes would have had the chance to open. 

Nothing dies easily here, either. When an inhabitant grows too old or too stiff to crack themselves free from the columns, when their joints finally lock entirely under the continuous deposit of lime and iron dust, they simply remain. The wet mortar from above drips down their backs; the wind pushes silt into the folds of their skin; the stilt-walkers climb over their long, sloped shins to lick the salt from their cheeks. Within a few seasons, they are no longer living bodies, but new, narrow pillars—organic buttresses wedged tightly between the terra-cotta and the slate, helping to hold up the low, dark vaults of a world that is still being built, tile by tile, in the absolute cold.
