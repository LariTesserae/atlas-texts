To inhabit a world made entirely of high-pressure wood, steaming sap, and sharp geometry, life cannot be soft. It cannot be warm-blooded, feathered, or scaled. The statement that *there are no animals* is true only in the sense that nothing here possesses a heart, a lungs, or a brain of gray matter. 

Yet the plazas are not empty. The life that moves across this landscape is not separate from the architecture—it is the architecture unmooring itself to act.

***

### The Strut-Runners

Along the razor-sharp edges of the three-mile tension cables, where the wind roars through the empty gulfs, move things that resemble long, segmented compasses forged from dark heartwood. They have no eyes, no faces, and no flesh. They are *stiff-limbed meristem-beings*—structures of hollow, dark walnut cellulose articulated by knee-like joints of translucent amber resin.

They move not by muscular contraction, but by violent, localized hydraulic bursts. Deep within their tubular legs, high-pressure sap spikes suddenly, forcing a flexed joint to snap straight with a dry, wooden *clack*. They step across the roaring voids in long, unnatural, rhythmic bounds, clinging to the polished grain of the cables with cluster-hooks of microscopic, lignified claws.

They do not hunt, nor do they feed in any traditional sense. They are the mobile growth-points of the regional shelf. When a structural girder snaps under thermal stress—dropping thousands of tons of timber into the mist below—a dozen of these strut-runners are immediately birthed from the broken stump. They emerge directly from the fractured xylem, forced outward by the surging hydraulic pressure like liquid glass pushed through a die.

Dragging fresh, wet threads of sticky, ultra-dense cellulose behind them, they leap across the sunlit gaps, weaving and anchoring the scaffolding for the next generation of girders. Once the new span is formed and solidified into cold-rolled timber, the runners do not return anywhere. They simply halt, align their joints with the structural stress-lines, and let their legs fuse permanently into the floorboards, becoming a new buttress, a new arch, or a new step on a rising stair.

### The Lens-Cysts

In the hexagonal reservoirs that pit the plaza floors—where the hot, mineral-rich water pools in warm, inch-deep sheets—lie the *Lens-Cysts*.

They appear at first like huge, geometric jewels dropped into the sunlit water: perfect, twenty-sided polyhedrons of amber-colored resin, ranging in size from a fist to a carriage. They are completely transparent, revealing a dense, dark nucleus within, suspended in clear, sweet-smelling oil. 

The Lens-Cysts are the sensory organs and chemical switchboards of the landscape. They do not walk or fly; they float in the shallow, swift-flowing water-films, tethered to the floor by thin, hollow umbilical tubes that draw up raw sap from the deep interior of the pylons. 

Under the direct, unfiltered sunlight, the cysts act as living magnifying glasses. They focus the intense beams of gold light down into the dark floorboards, heating the sap channels beneath to trigger localized growth-spikes or to boil off excess water. When the sun shifts, the cysts shift too—not by swim-fins, but by unevenly exuding tiny droplets of heavy, turps-scented oil from their lower facets, causing them to slide along the slippery walnut polished floorboards like pucks on ice.

To stand near a cluster of Lens-Cysts is to hear a constant, high-pitched ringing—a glassy, musical resonance produced as the shifting water-flow vibrates their tight, resinous faces. They flash in sequence across a plaza, passing pulses of light and heat down a mile-long line, coordinating the violent, rapid expansion of a distant balcony or signaling a spire to open its trumpet-vents against an oncoming wind.

### The Drift-Spans

In the vast, open gulfs between the pylons—where two thousand feet of clear, warm air hangs above the cloud-floor—nothing sits idle for long. 

Rising on the intense, wet updrafts generated by the boiling thermal output of the towers are the *Drift-Spans*. They are the largest mobile forms in this world, yet they are so light they are almost weightless. 

A Drift-Span begins as an amber growth-cap blown free from the top of a spire during a high-pressure water eruption. As it plummets into the gulf, the heat and sun cause it to inflate explosively into a rigid, hollow lattice of pale yellow, paper-thin wood, shaped like a wide, parabolic sail or a starburst wheel a hundred yards across.

These sail-forms do not possess intent as a creature does, but they are governed by exquisite aerodynamic responsiveness. Their ribs are lined with thousands of microscopic, trumpet-shaped stomata that open and close in reaction to humidity and temperature. By opening the vents on one side of its sail, a Drift-Span can tilt, catch a thermal plume, and bank steeply through the sunlit emptiness, rising thousands of feet in minutes.

They drift through the vast, empty architecture like crystalline dust-motes. As they fly, they trail long, shimmering tendrils of un-lignified, golden cambium that catch the light like spun sugar. These tendrils search the empty air. When a Drift-Span eventually collides with a distant, solitary spire, its trailing threads wrap instantly around the hot, wet walnut surface. The sail collapses with a sharp, dry rustle, and within hours, its material is absorbed, dissolved, and re-poured into the foundation of a brand-new aerial plaza.

***

### The Deep Hydraulic Intent

If you seek the *mind* of this place, you will not find it in a head or a single locus. The inhabitant of this regional shelf is the shelf itself—an immense, distributed, vascular consciousness operating at the pressure of a hydraulic ram and the speed of a snapping branch.

It experiences the world purely as *vectors of stress, light, and fluid dynamics*. 
* A cloud drift casting a shadow across a mile of plaza is felt as a drop in solar voltage and a sudden, sharp contraction of the walnut floor.
* A structural fracture is not an injury, but a loud, sudden thought—an impulse to redirect five thousand gallons of sap per minute to a new, rising buttress.
* TheOrgan-like drones of the trumpet-vents are not random wind noise, but the continuous, audible breathing of an architecture calculating its own weight against gravity.

It is a living world that is constantly, violently hungry for scale. It builds higher not to reach a goal, but because its metabolism produces more sap, more cellulose, and more heat than the current frame can hold. It must grow or burst. 

And so, surrounded by the smell of hot ozone, turpentine, and wet sugar, the dark walnut spires push higher into the thin air, the razor-edges of the plazas extend another mile over the clouds, and the amber-jointed runners stride across the roaring gulfs—all of them parts of a single, monumental life that is forever building its own cage, breaking it, and weaving it anew.
