At the lowest tier of this world—under the three-foot canopy of liver-brown moss—live the **Grom-Voles**. 

They are creatures forged entirely of muscle, fat, and bone density, designed to withstand the crushing drop in temperature that comes with a passing cloud. Their six splayed toes are tipped with curved, amber claws that operate like ice-picks, allowing them to anchor themselves directly into the frozen peat beneath the moss. Their fur is not soft; it is composed of hollow, glass-like guard hairs that act as tiny fiber-optic tubes, channeling the burning UV light of the indigo sky directly down to their black, heat-absorbing skin. 

A Grom-Vole does not run; it blasts through the moss in short, violent surges, snorting clouds of fine ice-dust from nostrils lined with copper-colored bristles. They eat the high-altitude beetles, but they do not swallow them whole. They crack the iridescent carapaces with heavy, chisel-shaped incisors, lapping up the sun-warmed, oil-rich lipids inside, leaving the dry hulls behind like spent beetle-armor strewn through the crimson velvet.

Above the moss, clinging to the naked slate, are the **Quartz-Baskers**. These flat, broad-headed lizards are the color of cold grease when frozen, but as the sun strikes the quartzite veins, their scales shift through a chemical reaction, darkening to an absolute, light-absorbing jet. They possess no eyelids; instead, a third transparent lens covers their eyes, filtering out the blinding glare of the high sun. They do not pant to cool down. Instead, when their blood approaches boiling from the scorched stone beneath them, they tilt their bodies into the thin, razor-sharp wind, letting the freezing gale strip the heat from their flanks in seconds.

Hunting both are the **Skin-Kites**. They are neither birds nor bats, but something older—cold-blooded gliders whose wings are membranes of tanned, vein-laced parchment stretched over hollow bone ribs. They do not flap; the air is too thin here to support powered flight. Instead, they nest in the dark, vertical chimneys behind the terrace, hanging by single, thorn-like ankle-spurs. When the sun heats the black basalt wall, sending a roaring thermal up draft from the cloud-ocean below, the kites release their grip. They drop like thrown knives into the abyss before catching the hot air-column, soaring upward with a dry, rustling sound like wind through dead corn. Their sight is tuned entirely to heat; to a Skin-Kite, a Quartz-Basker warming on a white vein looks like a streak of white fire against a field of ice.

***

Yet the true master of the Shelf is not the kite, nor the vole, but the one who made the tallies on the ribs.

They are called the **Ledge-Walkers**, though there is rarely more than one on the Shelf at any given time. They are human, but only in the way a wild thorn is a rose. 

A Walker stands barely five feet tall, thick-chested as a barrel, with lungs adapted to wring oxygen from air that would make a lowlander faint. Their skin is the color of oiled slate, thick and scarred from decades of exposure to high-altitude wind and radiation. Their eyes are narrow slits, permanently bloodshot from the cold, set deep beneath heavy, hairless brow-ridges that act as natural sunshades. 

They do not wear woven cloth. They wear a heavy, silent garment made from the stitched skins of the kites, lined with the glassy fur of the Grom-Voles. It is tied around their body with dried vole-gut that chitters softly when the wind catches it. On their feet are thick boots carved from the fibrous peat of the deep moss, bound with sinew, allowing them to walk across the razor-sharp slate without making a sound, and without leaving a footprint that outlasts the thaw.

The Walker does not build a house; the half-moon slate wall is merely a windbreak for the sun-hours. When the light dips behind the peak, the Walker does not sleep in a bed, but wedges themselves into the deep, dry crevices of the basalt—where the stone retains the solar heat gathered during the noon hammer—and enters a state of controlled torpor. Their heart rate drops to three beats a minute; their breath slows to a faint, dry whistle. To a stranger, they would seem frozen solid, indistinguishable from the black rock.

During the short, violent noon, the Walker is a creature of intense, quiet economy. 

They do not hunt with bows; wood does not exist at this altitude. They hunt with slate slingshots, dropping Skin-Kites as they crest the thermal lip, or with bone-needles baited with beetle-fat to catch the voles in their moss-tunnels. They eat everything raw and hot—the blood drank immediately before the air can freeze it into crimson hail, the fat smeared over their own cheeks and limbs to prevent the cold from snapping their skin.

Their relationship with the petrified whale ribs is quiet, ancient, and obsessive. 

The ribs are their calendar, their altar, and their map. Once every few suns, the Walker takes a piece of charred bone-black from the cold hearth pit, mixes it with grease from a vole’s liver, and re-blacks the tally marks carved into the bone. The marks do not count years, or days. They count the **Sweats**—the brief windows of time when the stone bleeds enough liquid water to fill the palm-sized glass pools. 

When the sweat stops, the Walker licks the ice from the slate with a tongue so toughened it no longer bleeds when pressed to frost. They store the ice in a bladder made from a kite’s throat, tucked inside their chest against their hot skin, letting their own body heat turn the ice back to water drop by drop.

They are the last of a line that followed the mountains as they rose out of the ancient sea five hundred centuries ago. They do not know the lowland exists under the fog ocean; to them, the grey soup below is simply the bottom of the sky, a place where light dies and cold begins. Their entire universe is thirty paces of crimson moss, three petrified sea-bones, and the hard indigo vault above.

When the shadow of the mountain finally swings across the Shelf of the White Ribs, the Walker does not hurry. They pick up their flint hand-ax, scrape a spot of moss to check the depth of the permafrost, and then slip into the vertical fissure behind the slate wall. As the dry *ping* of freezing rocks echoes across the terrace, the Walker pulls their kite-skin hood over their face, shuts their double-lidded eyes, and allows the frost to coat their eyebrows with fine white needles, becoming once again a lump of dark slate waiting for the sun to strike.
