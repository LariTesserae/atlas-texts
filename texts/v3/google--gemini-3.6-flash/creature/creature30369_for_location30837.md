To live here is not to inhabit space, but to endure force. 

Nothing in this flooded vault moves by swimming; to attempt to swim in a world of thousand-atmosphere jet-streams and sudden, crushing cavitation-chokes is to be torn into ribbons of pale paste. Life here does not float. It anchors, it wedges, it welds, and it threads.

At the lowest level—spread across the mineral-enameled basalt and the black grease of the iron flanges—is a thick, leathery carpet of sulfur-oxidizing slime and calcified tube-worms. These are not soft, fragile things; their sheaths are woven from spun silica and iron sulfide, hard as porcelain, growing in dense, ridged plates like overlapping roof shingles. They feed directly on the thermal exhaust, drinking the Ninety-Degree brine as it vomits from the wall-vents. When the great check-valves slam above them, the shockwave flattens the worms’ sensory feathery plumes against their shells, pressing out a faint, bioluminescent mucus—a cold, greenish slime that smears along the cobbles before the current washes it clean.

Moving across this iron pavement are the spar-length crawlers—the *pipe-ticks*. 

They are pale as lard, their bodies constructed from segmented plates of heavy, magnetite-impregnated chitin that ring like iron kettles when they bump against the masonry. They possess no eyes, no ears, and no mouthparts suited for biting. Instead, their heads are wedge-shaped spades of solid, bone-dense horn, used to strip the mineral crust and iron-eating bacteria from the high-pressure conduits. 

They do not have legs so much as hydraulic iron-hooks—pincer-like appendages that drive deep into the bolt-holes of the pipe flanges and the carved grooves of the basalt tenements. They move with terrible, deliberate patience against currents that would snap a ship’s mast, locking three joints before releasing a fourth. Their internal organs are not suspended in soft tissue, but flooded with the same heavy, silica-laden brine as the city around them; they do not fight the crushing depth because the pressure inside their pale, armored hulls is identical to the pressure without. When they die, their heavy shells do not drift; they are ground down by the silt-laden torrents, their pulverized chitin contributing to the slick, porcelain glaze that coats the buildings.

But the city was not built by the crawlers. Its proportions—its doors five yards high, its stairs carved with shallow, wide risers, its doorless archways designed to vent hydraulic surges—belonged to something else. And that something has not entirely vanished. It has simply scaled itself down, hardened its hide, and retreated into the dead-water pockets where the roar of the turbines settles into a low, marrow-shaking hum.

Deep within the flooded interiors of the four-story tenements, where the violent flumes cannot enter directly, live the *Tenders*.

They are long—fifteen, sometimes twenty feet from the blunt crown of their skulls to the trailing, ribbon-like muscle of their lower bodies. They resemble nothing on the sunlit surface. Their skin is thick, rubbery, and entirely hairless, a sickly alabaster-grey stained with dark, greasy patches of iron oxide. They have no limbs in the traditional sense, but rather four heavy, jointed shoulder-girdles from which spring clusters of long, fibrous tendrils—not delicate like an octopus's arms, but dense and tough as vulcanized cable, tipped with hard, enamel-like spatulas capable of gripping smooth, wet stone.

They are completely blind. Where eyes might have been in some distant, ancient epoch, there are only broad, dish-shaped plates of thickened bone covered in a web of sensitive, electrical pits. These plates pick up the faint, violet arcing of the submerged conduits and the subtle galvanic currents generated by rusting iron. 

To a Tender, the city is not a spatial arrangement of walls and streets; it is an acoustic and electrical map. They "see" the metropolis through the bones of their own jaws, which rest against the basalt floors. They know the state of the city by the pitch of its roar:
A rising whistle in the eastern district means a gantry bearing has run dry of grease; a sudden, low thrumming in the floorboards warns that an intake grate is choked with gravel.

They do not speak—sound in this realm is the domain of the water-jets and the iron valves—but they communicate through rhythmic, hydraulic pulses. By contracting their muscular thoracic cavities, they shoot sharp, compressed bursts of water against the stone walls, sending distinct, clicking shockwaves through the brine that can be felt by another Tender a mile away across the geode.

Their lives are entirely bound to the maintenance of the drowning engine. 

They do not manufacture new machinery—the foundries that cast the cathedral turbines are cold, drowned, and burred with quartz—but they preserve what remains. A Tender will spend three years in a single dark parlor, using its cable-like tendrils to slowly, agonizingly turn an ancient bronze grease-cup half an inch at a time, forcing black lard into the housing of a vertical axle that has been spinning since the crust above them was young. 

They feed on the spar-crawlers and the dense bacterial sludge, dragging the heavy, armored organisms into the quiet hollows of the buildings, where they crack them open against the stairwells using heavy, iron-headed cobblestones held in their tendrils.

They are remarkably few in number—perhaps only a few dozen scattered throughout the entire submerged mile. They reproduce with glacial slowness; a single, leathery egg-sack, anchored to the interior ceiling of an ancient, water-logged vault, takes nearly a century to mature, bathed in the steady, warm wash of ninety-degree brine. The young are born with thin, translucent hides and must spend their first decades in the deepest, most isolated cellars, far from the violent main-currents, learning the "song" of the pressure-stresses through the stone before they dare venture out to the pipe-arrays.

They have no language for the surface. They have no concept of air, sky, or sun. To them, the universe is a sealed basalt sphere, six miles down, bounded by stone and filled with warm, roaring salt. The sky is a ceiling of solid rock; the sun is the violet flash of a short-circuiting cable; and God is the great, blind, cathedral-sized turbine that must never, ever be allowed to stop turning.
