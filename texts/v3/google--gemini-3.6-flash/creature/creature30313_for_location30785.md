To survive in a city that re-creates and eats itself every few weeks, you cannot be static. You must be fluid, dense, and quiet.

The people who inhabit this cold, self-assembling basin—who call themselves the *Tethers* or simply the *Crew*—are built like the landscape: lean, tough-skinned, and strangely silent in their posture. 

They are human, or something so closely tuned to this specific ecosystem of iron and lichen that the distinction hardly matters anymore. They are rarely tall; height is a liability where steam pipes cross at head-level every three yards and zinc catwalks swing low beneath brick arches. Their bodies are long-torsoed and low-slung, move with a startling, deliberate economy of motion—a perpetual crouch that keeps their center of gravity close to whatever frost-slick pipe or razor-edged slate outcrop they are traversing.

Their skin is remarkably pale, almost translucent in the bright, crisp daylight, but it is not delicate. Exposure to the perpetual near-freezing moisture, the sharp ozone, and the abrasive quartz dust has thickened their hands and forearms into something like oiled leather. The soles of their feet—often bare or wrapped only in thin bands of vulcanized rubber and felted moss—are so heavily calloused that they can step on the knife-edge of freshly cleaved basalt without breaking skin. Their breath does not plume in the cold air as heavily as a stranger’s would; their lungs have adapted to the thin, sharp atmosphere, taking slow, deep, shallow-drawn breaths that leave little vapor behind.

Look closely at a Tether resting in the crotch of a giant steam flywheel, and you see how seamlessly they belong to the palette of the valley. 

Their clothing is not sewn from cloth, which rots too quickly in the saturated air, but fashioned from woven rubbery fern-fiber, heavy oiled canvas, and patched strips of greasy leather harvested from old machine belts. They bind their limbs in brass wire and copper thread, not for ornament, but to keep loose sleeves from being drawn into four-yard iron gears. Everything they wear is slicked with a compound of tallow and crushed liverwort—the same grease that keeps the city’s silent pistons turning. They smell of cold lard, pine tar, and wet copper.

Because the dense blankets of moss swallow human speech as easily as they swallow the roar of cold-rolling mills, the inhabitants rarely speak aloud. Vocalizing here is inefficient; a call across an alley sounds like a wet whisper, muffled before it travels ten feet. 

Instead, they communicate through a complex, lightning-fast dialect of tactile gestures and pipe-tapping. A Tether standing on a high zinc roof can send a message three miles down the valley in seconds by striking a copper telegraph bundle with a brass-tipped rod. The vibration travels through the iron skeleton of the city, felt through the soles of feet and the palms of hands three tenements away. To a Tether, the entire valley is constantly humming with news: *boiler seven is overheating; the ivy is taking the south gable; clear the lower runoff channel.* 

When they do speak face-to-face, it is in a low, raspy click of the teeth and tongue—sub-vocal noises that bypass the dampening moss and vibrate directly through the bones of the jaw when they press their foreheads together.

Their relationship with the city is neither one of ownership nor submission; it is a long, hyperactive negotiation. 

They do not live in "houses" in any traditional sense. A family might sleep for three nights in a suspended canvas hammock strung inside the warm, dry recess of a massive zinc condenser housing. When the ivy roots inevitably breach the housing’s seals and choke its valves, forcing the city’s automated derricks to dismantle the structure, the family simply unhooks their hammocks, gathers their brass tools, and moves fifty yards down the aisle to a newly erected brick gabled loft that was completed only that morning. 

They possess almost nothing that cannot be carried in a heavy leather satchel: a set of iron wrenches, a coil of copper wire, a jar of tallow, a bone-handled knife for slicing back fast-growing ferns, and a slab of dried, oily fish caught from the iron-lined basins below. They have no concept of heirlooms or permanent architecture. To them, a brick wall is just temporary shelter on its way to becoming peat.

In the mid-tiers of the city, where the daylight is brightest, you see the younger Tethers—the *Scouts*. They move across the high iron girders with the effortless, flowing speed of the pale otter-ferrets. They are the directors of the growth. They carry heavy shears and iron wedges, riding the booms of automated derrick cranes as they swing through the cold air. If a steam engine’s waterwheel is choked by river grass, a Scout drops from a girder, slices the tangled mass away in three fluid strokes, rubs a handful of lichen-tallow into the wooden axle, and vaults onto a passing copper pipe before the wheel even finishes its first unblocked revolution. 

Down in the lower levels, where the daylight dies and the air turns bitter cold, live the older Tethers—the *Deep-tenders*. They are heavier, broader, their eyes wide and perpetually dilated to catch the faint glow of friction-sparks and thermal bleed from buried boilers. They live among the roots and the foundation stones, navigating the narrow spaces where razor-sharp slate meets riveted iron. They are the ones who harvest the fat, translucent grubs from the grease-traps, cultivate the cold-hardy liverworts for salve, and tend to the blind crustaceans in the icy water basins. They are so quiet, so still when at rest, that rusty-iron owls will comfortably perch on their shoulders while waiting for lizards to dart across the wet iron floor.

There is no panic in their hyperactivity. Even as a six-story brick wall silently buckles and collapses under the weight of pale-leafed ivy just yards away, a group of Tethers working nearby won't even look up. They simply adjust their footing, step over the tumbling red bricks as they fall into the moss, and continue wrapping copper wire around a pulsing condenser tube. 

They are the human nervous system of an unguided, self-building engine. They do not know who built the first piston, nor do they care where the unweathered slate beneath their feet came from. They exist entirely in the absolute present of the valley: cold, bright, silent, and endlessly in motion.
