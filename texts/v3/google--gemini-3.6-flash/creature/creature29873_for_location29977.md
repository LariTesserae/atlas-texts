To live here is to be blind to shadow and deaf to voice.

The people of the shelf-valley—if they can still be called merely people—have eyes like smoked glass. Under the relentless, shadowless glare, their pupils do not dilate into round black pools; they contract into horizontal slits no wider than a blade-edge, shielded by heavy, double-folded lids that weep a clear, protective mucus against the salt-glare and the wind. Around their sockets, they smear a paste of charred grease and ground basalt, painting thick, black rings from eyebrow to cheekbone to absorb the terrible reflection bouncing off the wet stone. 

They do not speak with breath. In a world where the air is thin and wet, and where the constant, heavy *hush-slosh* of water cancels out the human voice, spoken words are an extravagance that freezes on the lip. Instead, they converse in a complex, percussion-driven sign language—a dialect of bone and stone. They tap the palms of their skin-wrapped hands against their slate aprons; they click their teeth; they strike their elbows against stone lintels to mark a direction or signal a shift in the rock. A master builder does not shout orders when a upper seam gargles its warning; he strikes the flat of his slate-axe against his thigh, three fast *thwacks*, and thirty men move in unison, their bodies operating with the quiet, effortless synchronization of a single hand closing into a fist.

Physically, they are low-built and immensely thick through the chest, their lungs expanded to pull life from the high, wet air. Their hands are their primary tools—the skin of their palms calloused to the hardness of cured leather, scarred and cross-hatched by fifty years of handling sharp, freezing slate. Their fingerprints are long gone, worn smooth by silt and stone. Their feet are broad and short, bound in three-tiered booties of oiled hound-hide stuffed with dried liverwort, designed not for walking, but for gripping slick, angled slate without slipping.

They are a people who do not perceive their homes as static places, but as slow-moving liquids. A child born in the basin does not grow up in a "house"; they grow up in a *stream-line*. They remember their lives not by years, but by the migration of their cells across the valley floor. *"I was small when we were at the Far North Seep,"* an elder might tap against his knee. *"I lost my teeth when the center wall crept south to meet the warm spouts."*

***

Alongside them, sharing the dark warmth of the lower cells, are the six-legged hounds—the *iron-slips*, as they are known in the bone-click tongue. 

They are not domestic dogs, nor are they wild predators; they are cold-water climbers that have co-evolved with the town’s shifting masonry. Their bodies are long, low, and armored in a seamless hide of gray-black skin that feels like wet slate to the touch—completely hairless, heavily blubbered, and perpetually coated in a clear, slick oil that repels the frost. Their six limbs are triple-jointed, ending in wide, webbed pads studded with tiny, keratinous hooks that can anchor into the microscopic grain of wet basalt.

The iron-slips do not bark. Their vocal cords are vestigial, but their ribcages vibrate with a deep, subsonic hum—a low, rhythmic thrumming that matches the boiling pulse of the underground water seams. They are the basin’s living barometer. Minutes before a dry seam bursts into a warm, mineral-heavy torrent, or before an over-burdened terrace wall gives way to the pressure of wet gravel behind it, the iron-slips stop their restless scrambling. They flatten their wet hides against the rock, their six legs sprawling wide, and begin to hum. The humans feel this tremor through the slate under their soles, drop their tools, and begin the silent, frantic work of diversion before the first drop of water breaks the surface.

In the dark of the sleeping cells, the iron-slips are the furnace. They pile together in the corners, three or four deep, their internal temperatures running high on a diet of boiled larvae and mineral-sludge. The humans sleep pressed directly against them, wedging their frost-numbed feet beneath the warm, heavy folds of the beasts' bellies, breathing in their strange, sweet breath—a smell like fresh turned earth, wet peat, and crushed iron.

***

High above, in the crannies where the dry-stacked slate meets the soaring cliff-face, live the tallow-birds. 

They are fat, comical, and essential. Lacking a single feather—which would instantly trap water, freeze, and drag them into the abyss—their skin is a quilted, pinkish-gray leather, thick as a thumb and endlessly secreting a dense, yellowish grease. They cannot fly in the traditional sense; instead, they have short, paddle-like wings tipped with bone spurs that allow them to vault from ledge to ledge, tumbling through the mist like heavy, rubbery melons.

The tallow-birds exist in a state of perpetual, lazy mutualism with the human population. They feed on the swarms of blind, tiny midges that rise from the warm liverwort mats, as well as the stringy moss that grows inside the wooden water-flumes. In return, the humans farm their grease. Once every few days, a worker climbs the bone ladders to the bird-crannies, scoops up a fat, blinking bird, and gently scrapes its thick skin with a curved spatula made of ivory. The bird sits placidly, grunting with a strange, wet pleasure as the stale, amber grease is peeled from its back to fill the village tallow-pots. 

This grease is the blood of the town. Without it, the lichen caulking would rot, the skin-coats would saturate and freeze into iron-hard sheets, and the bone ladders would splinter in the cold. Everything is smeared with it: the tools, the roofs, the boots, the faces of the children, and the very slate slabs themselves, which are coated in a thin sheen of grease before being laid down, allowing them to slide into place without shattering under the sledge.

***

And deep below it all, down in the black, subterranean channels where the light of the high basin cannot reach, is the origin of the wealth: the pale larvae.

They are the young of something vast and unseen that lives deep within the mountain’s hydrothermal veins—creatures that never emerge into the sunlit world. The larvae are as long as a man’s forearm, white as peeled birch, and filled with a thick, cold, protein-dense curd. They are driven upward through the rock seams by the irregular warm surges, spilling out into the shallow, stone-rimmed pools at the bottom of the basin like wriggling rice.

To harvest them is a ritual of quiet, violent precision. Young boys and girls, their legs tied with grease-soaked skin ropes, lean out over the rushing, churning seep-holes with long bone-toothed scoops. They haul the thrashing white bodies from the foam, flinging them up to the lower terraces where old women—their eyes almost entirely closed against the blinding white sky—methodically snap the head-caps off the grubs with a twist of their thumbs, squeezing the rich, sweet pulp into slate troughs to be mixed with pounded fish and left to set into cold, jelly-like bricks.

***

In this place, nothing is distinct from the mountain. The humans are not visitors building upon the rock; they are a mobile, biological crust that has formed over the mountain’s weeping pores. 

When a man dies, he is not buried—there is no earth to hold him, and the slate is too hard to dig. Instead, his body is stripped of its oiled skins, his long bones are carefully harvested to replace worn rungs on the terrace ladders, and his flesh is weighted with gravel and dropped into the deepest, warmest spring-throat at the rear of the basin. Within three days, the mineral-rich water and the subterranean life have claimed him entirely, returning his salt to the wet, black stone.

And above it all, the red-bearded man on his high ledge fits another slab of slate into place. He does not think of the valley as harsh, nor the light as cruel. To him, the world is simply a wet, white mirror, a constant pulse of water, and the cold, satisfying *thud* of stone seating perfectly against stone.
