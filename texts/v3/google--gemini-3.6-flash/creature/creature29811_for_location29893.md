To live here is to be flattened, broadened, and tuned to the hum of the stone. 

Nothing that moves in this place stands tall against the sky. The wind has nowhere to catch, and gravity has already won every argument it will ever have with the landscape; consequently, the life that occupies the expanse has abandoned the ambition of height. Everything spreads sideways, clinging to the warm, wet plane like a stain or a skin.

The most numerous inhabitants are the ones that give the silence its roar: the dark-green frogs. They are not the delicate, long-limbed jumpers of shadowed streams, but broad, dish-shaped creatures, their bellies as flat and pale as damp quartz, their backs mottled with the dull purples and oranges of oxidized iron. Their legs are short and thickly muscled, designed not for vaulting into the air, but for skittering across wet, slippery granite. They do not burrow—there is nowhere to dig—nor do they seek shade, for the sun is their furnace. Instead, they lie half-submerged in the skin-deep sheets of water, their throats ballooning into translucent, mustard-yellow bladders that reflect the yellow sky. When they call, their sound does not travel through the air so much as it strikes the bedrock, vibrating through the thin film of water so that a creature miles away feels the chorus against its underside before it hears it with its ears.

Shadowing the frogs are the waders—the pale birds whose legs are like peeled reeds. They have no song, only a dry, whistling click made by snapping their long, black-tipped mandibles. Their feathers are the color of dry granite dust, tinted faintly pink along the underwing from a diet rich in the tiny, brine-tolerant shrimp that swarm the shallows. These birds do not build nests. There are no twigs to gather, no soil to shape. They lay their eggs—heavy, oval, and speckled so precisely like ironstone grit that they become invisible the moment they touch the rock—directly onto the dry crests of the rock swells. The parent birds sit upon them through the humid days, their bodies turned always into the faint breeze, looking from a distance like smooth, weather-worn cobbles.

Below the surface film, in the two inches of warm liquid that covers the world, exists an entirely horizontal kingdom. 

Among the floating threads of golden algae live the flat-worms—gelatinous ribbons four inches long and wide as a thumb, but no thicker than a wet leaf. They are black as obsidian, moving across the ironstone in smooth, undulating waves that leave no ripples. They feed on the chalk-shelled snails, sliding over their round houses and dissolving the soft flesh within with acidic digestive slimes. Beside them, the pale, translucent shrimp kick through the silt, their thousands of tiny legs creating micro-currents that draw invisible organic dust into their mouths. They are the scrubbers of the shield; without them, the iron-scum would choke the pools in a single season.

Where the rock swells rise just above the water line, gripping the black lichen mats, live the slate-sliders. They are neither lizards nor salamanders, but something older, holding the middle ground between the two. A foot long, with skin the texture of wet basalt and eyes like tiny gold pinheads set flush into the top of their skulls, they spend hours motionless in the midday glare. Their bellies are wide and soft, pressed tight against the ironstone to absorb the heat stored in the planet’s basement. When they move, they do not lift their bodies; they slide, lubricated by their own thick mucus, gliding through the shallows like buttered ribbons, hunting the glass-winged dragonflies that hover too low over the rushes.

The dragonflies themselves are the only creatures that truly claim the air, though they rarely rise more than a foot above the surface. Their wings are transparent, stiff, and veined with metallic bronze. They do not drop their eggs into deep ponds; instead, their nymphs—armored, flat-headed grubs that look like rusted washers—spend two years wedged inside the hair-thin fissures of the granite, feeding on microscopic worms that live in the dark capillary networks where the water never dries.

And occasionally, in the widest, deepest basins—where the water reaches the depth of a man’s knee and the silt has gathered into a rich, red mud—something larger slumbers. These are the shield-turtles, creatures four feet across, whose carapaces are not domed, but flat and smooth as flagstones. Lichen grows upon their backs in thick, rubbery rings, matching the stone around them so perfectly that when they are still, they *are* the floor of the basin. They breathe only once every hour, thrusting tiny, tube-like nostrils through the water surface with barely a ripple, before settling back down into the warm, iron-tasting dark. They eat whatever drifts into their path: floating mats of dead algae, dying frogs, the dropped eggs of the waders. They move with an agonizing, ancient slowness, taking a hundred years to walk a mile across the flooded plateaus.

There are no predators here that hunt with speed, no prey that flees with panic. There is no cover to hide in, no height to dive from. Life here is a long, sun-drenched meditation on surface area. Everything is exposed, everything is wet, and everything is content to exist on the absolute bottom of the world, bathed in warm light and the sound of falling water.
