To live here is to abandon the idea of the individual. 

In the heat-soaked dark of the basin, life does not exist in distinct, bounded bodies so much as it exists in strata—layers of muscle, tallow, scale, and shell that press against one another until the boundaries between one creature and the next are blurred by sweat, mud, and continuous contact.

The largest of the basin’s air-breathers are the broad-ribbed wallowers—low-slung, hairless beasts the size of oxen, though built far closer to the earth. Their bodies are shaped like smooth, water-worn boulders, entirely devoid of sharp angles or protruding ears. Beneath skin as thick and slick as vulcanized rubber lies a heavy casing of yellow fat, a dense insulation that does not keep cold out, but rather holds their internal chemistry at the exact, sloshing equilibrium required to survive the perpetual heat. They have no functional eyes; where sockets should be, smooth plates of cartilaginous hide have grown over, protecting them from the sharp root-snags and iron-grit of the muck. They navigate by the deep, low-frequency vibrations of the methane vents, feeling the *chug-hiss* of the earth through the thick pads of their brisket and chin.

These wallowers do not roam. They shift. A herd of fifty will occupy a single hollow among the buttress roots for months at a time, piled three-deep in a soft, steaming mound. They breathe in unison—a long, wet siphon-wheeze that rises and falls like a tide in a closed cavern. When the lowermost beast feels the urge to feed, it does not stand; it simply surges forward through the mud, sliding beneath the bodies of its companions, who roll over its back with a heavy, suctioning sound like wet clay being lifted from a mold. They feed on the iron-crusted root-bark, using broad, shovel-like lower jaws to scrape the mineral skin away, chewing with a dry, grinding crunch that is swallowed instantly by the humid air.

Woven through the spaces between these sleeping giants are the snakes—though to call them snakes gives a false impression of quick, whipping grace. These are heavy, slow-pulsing cables of smooth muscle, some as thick around as a barrel, draped over the lower boughs and coiled through the troughs like discarded iron chains. Their scales are not overlapping tiles, but flat, interlocking plates of dull, slag-grey horn, cold to the touch despite the ambient heat. 

They do not hunt by sight or chase. A cable-snake will lay itself directly across a trail or inside a wallower-hollow, becoming a passive piece of the landscape. It can remain immobile for a season, its heart beating only once every few minutes, until a mammal or a heavy burrowing lizard shifts its weight directly onto the snake’s midsection. The trap does not spring with a strike; it closes like a hydraulic press. The coils tighten by fractions of an inch with every breath the prey exhales, a silent, crushing squeeze that takes hours to finish. The rest of the sleeping herd, numb to the darkness and accustomed to the constant pressure of their peers, simply shifts into the space left behind, indifferent to the slow, quiet consumption happening beside them.

Lower down, where the mud meets the iron-rich groundwater, the life is armored and metallic. 

The sheet-beetles that carpet the tree trunks do not scurry. They move in vast, collective drifts, sliding over one another like coins pouring from a sack. Their carapaces have absorbed so much iron oxide from the sap they feed on that their discarded shells do not rot; they rust. Where generations of these beetles have died at the base of a trunk, the ground is covered in a deep, crunchy gravel of rusted chitin that clinks softly under the weight of passing wallowers. 

Underneath this rust-gravel live the broad-headed salamanders. Pale as tallow, with skin so translucent that the dark, iron-laden blood can be seen pumping through their gills, they wedge themselves into the tightest crevices of the slick stone outcrops. They are the basin's scavengers, possessed of short, thick legs ending in spade-like claws that allow them to wedge into narrow mud-seams. They do not have teeth; instead, their mouths are lined with hard, serrated ridges of bone capable of shearing through the thickest hides of dead wallowers that have collapsed into the black water-courses. They eat in silence, submerged entirely in the silt, leaving behind only polished bones that are slowly dissolved by the acidic groundwater.

Near the methane vents, where the dark soil is hottest and smells most strongly of sulfur and cooked oil, exists an ecosystem entirely dependent on the earth's pressure. 

Here, tube-dwelling barnacles—soft-bodied, as long as a man's arm, and encrusted in thick tubes of iron and sulfur-crystals—grow in dense clusters around the gas vents. Every time a vent releases its rhythmic *chug-hiss*, these creatures expand, opening wide, rubbery frills to catch the plume of hot, nutrient-dense gas and iron condensate. They snap shut with a wet *thwip* as the pressure ebbs, digesting the chemicals in their dark, muscular interiors. Small, blind crabs—their claws adapted into wide, flat scrapers—clamber over these tube-colonies, shaving off the bacterial mats that grow on the warm mineral crusts.

Nothing in the basin ever looks up. There is no sky to look for, no light to track, no air current clean enough to carry the scent of something distant. The eyes of every creature here have either withered away into smooth skin or reduced to simple heat-sensing pits that see the world as a dull, shifting map of warm mud and hotter breath. 

When a creature dies here, it does not leave a void. The pressure of the surrounding life is too great; the gap is filled before the body has even cooled. A wallower that ceases to breathe is simply absorbed into the floor—pressed down by the weight of its kin, scraped clean by the salamanders, bored through by the iron-beetles, and slowly dissolved into the black, oily grease that feeds the roots of the metal-sheathed trees. 

It is a closed system of unbelievable mass, where every living thing is both a parasite and a foundation, crawling over the dead and being crawled over in turn, content to simmer forever in the dark.
