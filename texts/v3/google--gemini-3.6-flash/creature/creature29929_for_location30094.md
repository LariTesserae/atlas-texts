They are creatures of dense bone and cold blood, built for a world where weight is an illusion and every movement is a conversation with resistance. 

To see one for the first time is to mistake them for a piece of the city itself. Their skin has the smooth, oily sheen of dark river-pebbles—a slate-grey or deep moss-green hide, hairless and thick with subcutaneous fat, rubbed clean of silt and glistening with the same rendered char-oils they use to preserve the timber-reed. They do not have the quick, nervous twitches of warm-blooded air-breathers. Their hearts beat with a slow, heavy rhythm—no more than eight or nine times a minute—matching the deliberate, glacial pace of the lake.

They are long-limbed and heavy-chested, their torsos broad enough to hold the vast, internal respiratory chambers that draw oxygen directly from the frigid, mineral-dense water. Behind the angle of their jawlines, beneath fine folds of dark flesh, lie thin, leaf-like gill-frills that bloom outward when they breathe, fanning the liquid like delicate, translucent ferns before collapsing flush against their necks. 

Their eyes are large, unblinking, and set slightly wide, equipped with dual nictitating membranes that filter the sharp green sunlight by day and gather the faint, cold luminescence of the thermal vents by night. Their irises are the color of tarnished brass. They do not possess outer ears; instead, their hearing is entirely skeletal. The bones of their lower jaws and wrists are unusually dense, honed to press against the timber-reed to catch the vibrations traveling through the lattice.

They call themselves the *Oul-Vat*, though they rarely use voice to say it. 

Language here is not spoken into air; it is tapped into wood and hummed through bone. A mother on a high balcony does not call to her child with a shout. She presses her obsidian ring against the heartwood of the pillar, striking a brief, three-note cadence. Two hundred feet away, the child feels the pulse travel up through the soles of their bare, webbed feet, the timber acting as a hollow telegraph wire. For longer thoughts, they use low, throat-driven resonances—pitched so deep they are felt in the solar plexus rather than heard—that roll through the cold liquid like the thumping of the giant mussels.

Because the water is so dense and cold, the Oul-Vat move with an effortless, low-energy grace. They do not swim with frantic kicks; they glide. Their feet are broad and flexible, the toes long and webbed to the first joint, allowing them to push off carved lintels with subtle, precise pressure. They carry small, teardrop-shaped weights of carved basalt strapped to their thighs or woven into their hyacinth-fiber aprons. By shifting these stones or relaxing their lungs, an Oul-Vat can adjust their buoyancy instantly—sinking like a dropped pebble to the silt-floors to tend the mussel beds, or drifting upward to the canopy like a rising bubble to prune the broad leaf-roof.

Their social structure is written into the wood. They do not buy or sell homes; they inherit stalks. A family lives within a single vertical column of the timber-reed for centuries. As the stalk grows slowly upward, the youngest generation carves new chambers into the fresh, upper wood, while the lower, older rooms—darkened by generations of oil-rubbing and polished smooth by the brush of shoulders—are handed down to the elders or set aside as dry-less granaries for stored char-meat and dried kelp-cake.

Their tools are extensions of their physical patience. An arbor-carver does not hack at the living heartwood; she works with the grain, using a broad chisel of ground obsidian attached to a heavy elk-antler mallet. She taps once every few seconds, letting the cold water dampen the recoil, paring away curls of timber that float off like dark ribbons. A doorway is not made in a day, nor a season; it is coaxed into being over a decade, the edges bound with wet sinew to force the living reed to heal into a reinforced, rounded arch.

In the mid-day light, the street-shafts are alive with their quiet labor. 
Weavers, suspended mid-water between two parallel stalks, maintain looms made of bone frames, their long, supple fingers moving with rhythmic precision as they interlace grey water-hyacinth with the tough, fibrous inner-skin of the timber-reed. Hunters descend toward the shadowy floor in pairs, carrying long, barbed spears of fire-hardened wood. They move in silent synchronization, stepping lightly along the root-arches, searching for the armored char that nest in the warm, sulphur-rich gravel near the vents. 

Childhood among the Oul-Vat is long and buoyant. Infants are born pale and soft, with oversized respiratory frills that drift around their heads like halos of smoke. Before they learn to glide, they are tied to their parents’ backs with bands of soft moss-cloth, or tethered to the interior walls of the heartwood chambers with long cords of braided vine. By five or six years, their bones have densified, and they are turned loose into the sunlit upper shafts. They play games of endless, silent tag among the lattice, dropping forty feet through open liquid only to catch themselves with a single, lazy flick of a webbed foot against a balcony rail.

They have no fire, and no concept of it. Heat is something that comes from the deep vents as a low, smelling drift of mineral warmth, or from the sun as a pale green skin on the surface of the canopy. Their food is eaten raw or cured: char sliced into paper-thin ribbons and steeped in bitter, high-altitude salt-brine; the sweet, dense adductor muscles of the giant river-mussels; and the tender, inner pith of the timber-reed’s fresh shoots, which tastes of cold water and crushed stem.

When an Oul-Vat reaches the end of their long, slow life, there is no decay to hurry them away. The community carries the body down to the base of the central stalk—the oldest part of the organism, where the wood is black, iron-hard, and thick as a fortress wall. There, in a niche carved precisely to their measure, the dead are placed with their hands folded over a teardrop stone. The arborists bind the opening with living shoots. 

Over the next fifty years, the timber-reed slowly grows over the cavity, swallowing the body in a seal of living wood. The dead remain there, suspended in the dark heart of the city, perfectly preserved by the cold, oil-cured grain, becoming part of the very pillars that support the homes of their great-grandchildren.

As twilight falls and the lake turns to cobalt, the Oul-Vat retreat toward the inner chambers of the stalks. They do not close doors—there are no doors, only open oval ports that let the cool, oxygen-rich water circulate continuously through their sleeping quarters. They sleep suspended, often holding onto a woven wall-strap with a single hand, or resting three-deep on padded benches of braided kelp.

Outside, the city hums. The metallic rasping of the barnacles, the distant, rhythmic *thunk* of the filtering mussels, and the low, wooden shudders of the lattice under the mountain wind above all blend into a single, continuous symphony. The Oul-Vat sleep inside the sound, their gill-frills pulsing slowly in the dark, held safe in the cold, liquid heart of the mountain.
