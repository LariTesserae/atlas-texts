To look upon the basin from above is to see an environment that appears to have squeezed out the possibility of flesh. The violence of the iron, the coldness of the cloud, and the absolute weight of the water suggest a realm where only mineral and metal can endure. 

And yet, life has not been expelled; it has been distilled, toughened, and woven directly into the works.

At the lowest, most pervasive level, there are the **Lard-Molds** and **Tallow-Crusts**—living, rubbery blankets of black-grey fungi that do not grow on soil, but feed exclusively on cold steam, grease, and iron-dust. They form natural gaskets between the vibrating faces of masonry blocks, sealing the mortar joints with dense, fibrous mats that stink faintly of wet copper and old lard. Where the water runs hottest near the boiler exhausts, these molds thicken into translucent, quivering ribbons several yards long, trailing down the sluice-walls like the manes of drowned horses, filtering heavy metals and particulate soot from the rushing current.

Nesting within the dense, water-logged fern-canopies are the **Gasket-Crabs**—broad, flat arthropods with carapaces like smooth chunks of wet slate. They are stone-blind and entirely deaf, possessing no eyes or ears, but their flattened legs are tipped with sensitive, keratinized pads that read the structural vibrations of the I-beams. They do not scuttle; they move with slow, deliberate, hydraulic patience, scraping lime-scale, algae, and sulfur deposits from the rims of the pressure-relief valves. When a valve blows, unleashing a roar of scalding steam, the crabs do not flee; their shells are insulated with a thick layer of fibrous moss, and they simply hunker down, letting the hot mist wash over them, opening their feathery feeding appendages to catch the nutrient-dense condensate.

Deeper in the works, where the warm oil drops continuously into the dark galleries, live the **Soft-Joints**. 

They are the primary inhabitants of the basin, though a casual observer might mistake them for long-abandoned bundles of wet leather or discarded grease-rags wedged between the gears. They are humanoid only in the vaguest, most functional sense—elongated, stooped, and completely hairless, with skin the color and texture of salted pork lard. They possess no bones in the traditional sense; their skeletons are composed of dense, flexible cartilage that can withstand the intense, bone-rattling thrum of the hydraulic rams without fracturing.

The Soft-Joints have no voices. In a world where all sound is swallowed by mist and wet brick, they communicate entirely through the iron itself. They carry short, heavy rods of cold-rolled bronze, which they press against the girders to send sharp, rhythmic metallic clicks traveling through the structural frame of the valley. A Tender resting three terraces down can feel the click through the soles of its feet, reading the pulse of the metal: *Piston Four is binding; Sluice Gate Nine needs tallow.*

They wear no clothes, for cloth would rot within days in this perpetual drizzle. Instead, their bodies are permanently coated in a thick, self-sealing layer of dark engine-grease, soot, and crushed fern-sap. This black paste fills the deep folds of their skin, making them entirely waterproof and immune to the biting mountain cold. Their eyes are tiny, amber, and covered by clear, thick nictitating membranes that allow them to see through the heavy mist and the flying spray of grease-laden water.

Their lives are strictly synchronized with the hyper-dynamic rhythm of the basin:

*   **They move without haste, yet never stop.** A Soft-Joint can step into the path of a six-story flywheel or between the jaws of a slamming counterweight gate with terrifying precision. They do not look at the machinery; they *feel* the air displacement and the shift in bedrock vibration. They glide through the crushing mechanisms, reaching in with long, double-jointed fingers to scoop leaves out of a gear-race or slap a fresh wad of tallow onto a steaming journal-bearing, slipping back out a fraction of a second before the iron teeth clash shut.
*   **They sleep in the warm, deafening darkness of the pipe-vaults.** They wedge themselves into the tight spaces between high-pressure steam lines and wet masonry, curling into tight, glistening balls. The intense heat of the pipes keeps them warm, while the endless *thump-thump-thump* of the pumps acts as their cradle.
*   **They feed on the basin's abundance.** They do not hunt or farm. They scoop up hand-sized quantities of the fat-rich tallow-crusts from the warm water channels, and gather the pale, blind water-beetles that cluster around the copper strainers. Their bellies are broad and barrel-shaped, housing long, inefficient digestive tracts built to extract calories from tough, oily, mineral-heavy organic sludge.

Above them all, in the vast, lightless reservoirs deep beneath the lowest slate terraces, live the **Siphon-Beasts**. 

These are the oldest things in the valley. They are massive, slow-moving organisms that resemble a cross between a whale’s heart and a river-sponge, rooted permanently to the flooded foundations of the lower retaining walls. Their skin is thick, black, and embedded with thousands of tiny, iron-colored grit particles. They draw thousands of gallons of dark mountain water through their ribbed, accordion-like bodies every hour, straining out the silt, rust, and vegetable rot before spewing the water back into the deep copper sluices. They are the living filters that keep the delicate pump-valves from seizing up with grit. 

The machine needs the Siphon-Beasts to remain unchoked; the beasts need the machine’s relentless, violent pumping to force the water through their vast, spongy lungs. Neither can exist without the other.

As the amber lamps flicker to life at dusk, casting soft, wet halos through the drizzle, you might finally spot a Soft-Joint. It sits high up on a rust-pitted gantry, fifty feet above a churning hopper, perfectly still. The rain cascades off its shoulders in steady, shimmering streams, running down its grease-slicked back to drip onto the lush ferns below. It does not look at the cold sky, nor at the slick crows perched on the stacks. It rests its pale, flat palm against the wet iron girder, listening to the vast, muffled heart of the valley beating beneath its skin, waiting for the subtle shift in vibration that tells it another valve is about to open.
