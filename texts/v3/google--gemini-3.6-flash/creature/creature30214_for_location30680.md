Above the camps, where the white glare is so absolute it blurs the joints of the ceiling, live the **Gale-Kites**. 

They are not birds, nor insects, though they share the dry, hollow light-weightedness of both. A Kite’s body is a six-foot spindle of bleached, cork-like pith, barely thicker than a man’s wrist, encased in a hard, translucent shell of fused cellulose. They have no eyes; in a realm of shadowless light, vision is useless and blinding. Instead, their heads are blunt, crowned with delicate, fan-like arrays of silver-white hair that register the finest shifts in atmospheric pressure. 

When a spire twenty miles away groans and fractures, the Kite feels the shockwave seconds before the sound arrives. It releases its grip on the high thorns—where it hangs suspended by four long, needle-sharp tarsal spurs—and unfurls its vanes. These are not soft wings, but dry, ribbed sheets of tissue thin as onion skin, stretched over frame-poles of flexible bark. They do not flap. They ride the vertical cold-squalls created by the expanding floor, soaring for hours across the vast, empty glassiness of the void. 

They feed on the "sweat." When the house-sized droplets of cold water bleed from the ceiling arches and shatter on the lower trusses, the Kites descend in silent, descending spirals. They land on the wet, mist-slick surfaces, extending long, thread-like probosces into the fresh, uncalcified cracks where the rich, mineralized sap bursts out before the frost seals it over. They are completely mute, save for the dry, paper-like rustle of their wings when they clip themselves back onto the ivory columns.

***

Far below, clinging to the crotches of the structural giants, are the **People of the Lattice**—the *Knot-Walkers*. 

To look upon a human here is to look upon a body hollowed out and rebuilt by cold, glare, and verticality. They are long-limbed and remarkably lean, their ribcages deep and flared to draw warmth from the thin, nose-burning air. Their skin has no pigment; it is the color of skimmed milk or frosted kelp, so tough and heavily calloused at the joints that it resists the razor-edges of the floor like oiled leather.

Their eyes are their most striking adaptation. Generations in the shadowless, surgical light have made raw human vision impossible. The Knot-Walkers wear no cloth over their faces, but their eye-sockets are deep and shadowed beneath exaggerated, bony brow-ridges. Over their eyes, they wear thin plates carved from the bone-pith of dead spires, perforated only by horizontal slits no wider than a fingernail’s edge. Through these slits, their irises appear not brown or blue, but a pale, reflective silver-grey. They do not blink often; their tear ducts produce a thick, viscous oil that protects the eye from freezing instantly in the dry ice-dust.

Movement among them is an art of absolute friction. A Knot-Walker does not walk with a rolling stride. They move with a low, wide-based crouch, planting the heel and the ball of the foot simultaneously. The soles of their footwear are made from the textured, abrasive under-bark of young shoots, cross-hatched with deep grooves that bite into the slick, frozen cellulose. 

Their hands are tools of severe specialization. The thumbs are unusually long and powerful, the palms covered in a pad of yellowed callus so thick they can slide a hand along a flint-sharp leaf-truss without drawing blood. Around their wrists and ankles, they bind thick wraps of braided tendril-hair, which serve both as insulation and as emergency shock-absorbers should a sudden squall tear them from a walkway.

***

In the camps, life is governed not by the sun or seasons, but by the **Vascular Pulse**. 

Because stone is a sacred, ancient relic—rare pieces of dark basalt brought down from the roof-rifts by ancestors forgotten to memory—it is reserved exclusively for the hearth-rings. A camp’s hearth is its anchor. Around it, the huts are built not on the ground, but suspended *above* it. They are spherical pods woven from the pliable, inner bast-fiber of newly sprouted tendrils, sealed against the draft with a thick plaster made of crushed leaf-pulp and ice-slush that dries as hard as glass. 

The pods are hung from long, braided ropes pinned deep into the column walls with four-foot obsidian-tipped spikes. They hang suspended because the floor beneath them is alive and untrustworthy; a hut built directly on the bark might be split in two by a sudden growth-spire during the night, or crushed beneath a buckling truss. Suspended, the pods merely swing and rattle when the floor shudders, swaying like heavy fruit in a gale.

The Knot-Walkers are almost entirely silent. Sound in the void carries for miles, echoing off the bone-white pillars with disorienting clarity. Loud speech is considered an indulgence of fools or a sign of panic. Instead, they communicate through a complex language of tactile signals and low, resonant whistles that mimic the natural stress-cracks of the forest. A mother calls her child not by name, but by a double-click of the tongue that slides cleanly through the high-frequency hum of the freezing air.

Their diet is narrow, cruel, and precise:
*   They harvest the **Pith-Marrow**—the soft, wet, sugar-rich core of spires that have just ruptured and have not yet calcified in the cold. Men called *Vein-Cutters* rope themselves together and leap onto newly risen columns, driving obsidian chisels into the steaming cracks to pry out dripping, fibrous chunks before the frost turns them to iron-hard wood.
*   They cultivate **Frost-Moss**, a dark, velvet-like fungus that grows only in the pitch-black concavities *underneath* the broadest structural leaves, where the ceiling light cannot reach and the liquid tin-mirrors of water pool.
*   They hunt the Gale-Kites using long, weighted throwing-cords made of twisted fiber. A hit does not break the creature's bones; it entangles the delicate vane-frames, sending the beast tumbling into the lower depths where hunters retrieve the carcass for its light, hollow bone-bark, its water-resistant skin, and its dense, oily muscle-string.

***

The highest authority among them belongs to the **Listen-Keepers**.

These are the oldest among the camps, often individuals whose eyes have finally failed entirely from the glare, but whose sensitivity to vibration has become superhuman. A Listen-Keeper does not sit in a hut. They spend their days strapped to the vertical face of a primary column, their bare chest and forehead pressed directly against the ivory bark.

They feel the deep hydraulics of the world. They can distinguish between the low, wet thrum of sap rising five miles below—a sign of safety—and the high, dry, shrieking vibration that precedes a structural shear. Hours before a section of the plain snaps and shoots ten thousand feet into the air, the Listen-Keeper feels the cellulose fibers tearing within the wood. They give the signal: a sharp, repeated strike of a stone mallet against a hollowed pith-cylinder.

Within minutes, the camp unpins its huts, hauls up its reed walkways, and retreats up the suspension ropes into the higher, older, more stable crotches of the giant struts. They watch in silence as the ledge they occupied for three generations buckles with a sound like thunder, shatters into a billion flying quartz-like splinters, and is consumed by a violent, surging pillar of fresh, pale growth.

When the squall clears and the new spire hardens into whalebone rigidity beneath the blinding overhead light, the Knot-Walkers climb down their ropes, obsidian chisels in hand, to carve new foot-notches into the warm, unsoftened bark. They do not mourn the lost ledge. They measure the new one, notch their poles, and light the small blue fire in the stone hearth once again.
