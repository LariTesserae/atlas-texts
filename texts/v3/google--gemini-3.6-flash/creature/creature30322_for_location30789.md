To live here is to be made of water, oil, and silence.

At the lowest level of existence—where the iron gantries meet the saturated stone—life begins as a rust-eating crust. It is a world dominated by the **copper-mosses and iron-marrow lichen**, organisms that do not draw their strength from sunlight, which never truly arrives, but from the slow decay of the city’s bones. They bloom in wet, bruised-purple carpets along the underside of every beam, their roots secreting a mild acid that slowly dissolves the iron trusses, binding the metal to the basalt in a soft, crumbling calcification. Where the moss grows thickest, it produces tiny, bulbous spore-capsules that glow with a faint, cold bioluminescence—a pale violet foxfire that outlines the structure of the bottom tiers in the dark, like the skeleton of a submerged ship.

Feeding on this fungal velvet are the **iron-lice**, broad, flat crustaceans the size of a man’s palm, with shells the color of wet slate. They crawl sluggishly along the wet rivets, their many-jointed legs making a tiny *click-click-click* that is swallowed almost instantly by the mist. They are the city’s scavengers and polishers; as they graze on the lichen, their underbellies scrape away the loose flake-rust, leaving the underlying metal strangely smooth, dark, and gleaming with a coat of protective, glandular grease.

Higher up, where the carbon-filament lamps cast their amber halos, the air belongs to the **velvet-wings**. These are moths with wingspans as wide as a robin’s flight, their bodies coated in a thick, soot-colored fur that repels the constant condensation. Unlike common moths, they are entirely mute and move without the papery rustle of lower-altitude insects; their wings are soft as wet felt. They do not flutter frantically against the warm glass of the gas cages. Instead, they land, press their damp abdomens against the brass fittings to drink the oily condensate that settles there, and remain motionless for hours, their black-ringed eyes reflecting the faint yellow glow like drops of tar.

Nesting in the mortar-gaps and the rusted hollows of unused pipework are the **glass-salamanders**. Translucent, pale as peeled willow, and no longer than a finger, their hearts are visible through their chests as tiny, beating specks of ruby. They have no eyes—eyes are useless in the perpetual fog and the dripping dark beneath the floorboards—but their skin is covered in microscopic, sensory cilia that read the vibrations of the mountain’s steam pumps. When the pumps cycle and the stone shudders, the salamanders emerge by the hundreds from the cliffside fissures, clinging to the vertical iron with broad, suction-padded toes. They feed on the velvet-wings and the blind midges that breed in the suspended water flumes, their long, ribbon-like tongues striking with a wet, silent precision.

And then there are the people. 

They do not look like the inhabitants of the sunlit plains five miles below. Generations in the thin, cold air have changed them. They are broad-shouldered and short-statured, with deep, barrel-shaped chests that expand and contract with a slow, deliberate cadence—their lungs long since adapted to extracting oxygen from the wet, rarefied mist. Their skin is a pale, milky slate, almost veinless in appearance, but their hands are thick, calloused, and stained permanently with the dark grease of coal oil and the orange dust of iron oxide.

They move through the vertical town with a quiet, sure-footed grace that resembles climbing more than walking. They wear heavy coats of tallow-soaked canvas and boiled leather, stiff enough to turn the icy spray of the cliff-water, bound tight at the wrists and ankles with copper wire. Over their faces, many wear low-slung, brass-rimmed respirators—not to filter the air, which is pure if metallic, but to capture the warmth of their own breath and keep their lips from cracking in the damp chill.

Their language has shed its sharp consonants. Shouting is useless here; the fog dampens high frequencies, and the acoustic echo off the vertical brick walls twists loud words into unrecognisable murmurs. Instead, the inhabitants speak in a soft, sibilant dialect of low hums, clicks, and hand-signs. A conversation between two flume-tenders standing thirty feet apart on a swaying cable-bridge looks like a dance of lanterns and a exchange of quiet, throat-deep resonance, like the purring of large, wet animals.

Human life here revolves entirely around the management of the wet and the heat. They are not miners, for the mountain is too old and worn to yield fresh ore; they are **maintainers**. 

*   **The Valve-Hearders** live in the warmest tiers, nearest the steam vents. They wear heavy felt aprons and carry long, copper-tipped bone rods. They spend their days pressing these rods against the shuddering iron pipes, listening to the pitch of the steam inside, adjusting relief valves with massive wooden wrenches to ensure the mountain’s mechanical heart does not over-pressurize and tear the tenements from the stone.
*   **The Flume-Gardeners** work high up on the open gantries, tending the suspended wooden troughs that channel the condensation. They farm a dense, edible water-cress with dark blue leaves and fat, turnip-like tubers that grow in the dark silt swept down from the upper ridges. Their harvest is bitter, tasting of cold iron and crushed fern, but it is rich and crisp, providing the city with its main source of nourishment alongside salted salamander-tail and boiled moth-larvae.
*   **The Cable-Greasers** are the youngest and leanest among them. Suspended in small leather slings beneath the groaning ore baskets, they travel out into the empty, grey void between the mountain’s spur-ridges. Out there, suspended five miles above the hidden earth, wrapped in blinding white mist, they slather the moving steel cables with buckets of rendered animal fat, keeping the city’s transportation lines from seizing up in the frost.

Above even the cable-greasers, inhabiting the highest, most desolate spires where the architecture gives way to the smooth, tooth-like peaks, live the **Gauze-Skimmers**. They are not human, though they share the air with them. They are massive, solitary creatures—half-bird, half-ray—with light, cartilage frames and wide, membrane-thin wings spanning twenty feet across. They never land on the ground, for their legs have withered into tiny, hooked claws used only for hanging from the iron girders during the fiercest gales. They glide silently on the thermals of warm steam that rise from the town, their vast, translucent wings catching the dim pearl light of the overcast sky. They are the true natives of the altitude; they drink the cloud itself, skimming the damp air with open, baleen-lined mouths to filter out airborne spores and high-altitude midges. To the people in the oilcloth coats below, the sight of a Skimmer’s grey shadow passing slowly through the mist overhead is not a wonder, but merely a mark of the weather—a sign that the upper winds are cold, and that the fog will hold for another thirty days.

Everything here is bound into a singular, damp clockwork. The human tends the pipe; the steam from the pipe warms the moss; the moss feeds the louse; the louse cleans the iron; the iron holds the stone; and the mountain, old beyond memory, continues to exhale its quiet, wet breath into the grey sky, indifferent to the small, cold, persistent life clinging to its ribs.
