To understand what lives here, one must first unlearn the necessity of wetness. Nothing in the basin possesses blood, sap, or membrane. There are no lungs to expand, no eyes that require tears to blink, no soft bellies to be pierced by the razor-edges of the fresh selenite. Life in this cold, vitreous pocket is strictly dry, constructed from the same elemental ledger as the buildings: sulfur, bismuth, horn, silica, and graphite.

The pale, fox-sized grazers—those multi-jointed wanderers of the razor lintels—are the most mobile expressions of this ecology. Up close, their bodies reveal a construction as precise as the architecture they inhabit. Their outer shells are not skin or chitin, but articulated plates of cold, translucent horn, tinted a faint lavender by traces of embedded manganese. They have no eyes; in a world without sun, illuminated only by the uniform, sour grey of the overhead haze, light is an inefficient medium. Instead, their long, triangular heads terminate in broad, flat plates of piezoresistive cartilage. Pressed against a wall, these plates feel the microscopic strain-vectors of the stone—they "see" the city by reading where the walls are growing, where the stress is building, and where a lintel is about to snap.

Their movement is entirely silent because their limbs end in dense, woven tufts of spun basalt-wool—the "felt" pads. These pads do not slip, nor do they cut. They absorb the lethal sharpness of the floor like snow absorbing a footstep. 

As these creatures move, their mandibles—two overlapping chisels of sintered tungsten-carbide—work with monotonous, mechanical speed. They do not hunt each other, nor do they seek organic decay. They feed on the "growth-milk": the soft, newly extruded calcite needles that bloom along the advancing fronts of the towers. By scraping away these delicate, fresh extrusions, the grazers act as unintentional sculptors. Where they feed heavily, a corridor remains open; where they avoid a passage, the white needles grow unchecked until the street bridges over and closes into a dark, hollow vault. 

Their digestion is an abiotic catalytic reduction. Inside their hollow horn-carapaces, mineral dust is ground, stripped of its sulfur and trace volatile metals, and compacted. They do not excrete moisture or soft waste. Every few hours, a grazer halts over one of the porous floor-grates, opens a rear ventral valve, and drops a small, perfectly square pellet of compressed graphite and pure antimony. These pellets crumble immediately upon hitting the dry grates, rejoining the velvet rivers of black dust that flow through the alleys.

Below the streets, inside the dark, hollow shafts of the obsidian pylons where no grazer can fit, lives a second, far more passive form of life: the *Sieves*.

The Sieves resemble immense, folded sheets of hammered mica, no thicker than a fingernail, suspended vertically across the interior shafts like the bellows of forgotten cameras. They are anchored to the obsidian walls by dark, amber-colored resin. They do not move from place to place. Instead, they respond to the dry thermal suction that draws the graphite dust through the city’s lower veins. 

As the fine soot of graphite and pulverized bismuth is sucked down through the shafts, the Sieves flutter—a movement so slow and dry it produces only an ultrasonic rustle beneath the threshold of hearing. Their surfaces are covered in microscopic, electrostatic cilia made of flexible glass. These cilia catch the powder-like spores dropped by the hair-like glass vegetation of the alleys. The Sieve absorbs the spore’s heavy-metal core through direct solid-state diffusion, using the sulfur and antimony to thicken its own mica-like sheets. When a Sieve reaches the end of its life, it does not rot; it simply grows too heavy for its resin anchors, breaks cleanly along its crystal axes, and falls down the hollow shaft to shatter into a thousand mirror-bright flakes on the floor below—flakes that the subterranean gas-jets eventually grind back into dust.

Higher up, in the tiered balconies and light-wells twenty stories above the basin floor, hangs the most subtle inhabitant of all: the *Lattice-weavers*.

If you were to shine a light into the narrow gaps between the double-skinned selenite walls, you might see what looks like a thick, web-like deposit of silver wire. These are not webs, but the static nervous system of the city’s upper layers. The Weavers are long, thread-like organisms, rarely thicker than a guitar string, but extending for hundreds of meters through the hollow core of the architecture. Their bodies are made of pure, ductile silver and carbon filaments, wrapped in a protective sheath of dry, flexible resin.

The Weavers do not possess a central brain. Instead, they are living strain-gauges. As the cold basalt scarps shift or the gas-jets beneath the basin floor change pressure, the towers of the city flex. The silver threads of the Weavers are stretched or compressed within the walls. This physical distortion generates tiny, silent electric currents that flow through the silver networks. 

It is these currents that dictate the growth of the city itself. Where a Weaver thread is stretched tight by an unstable wall, the micro-current it generates alters the chemical deposition at the wall's base, causing the stone to stop extruding calcite and begin depositing dense, non-yielding obsidian. The city grows where its living silver threads tell it to reinforce itself; it sheds wall-sections where the threads go slack and silent.

There is no conflict here. No creature hungers for the flesh of another, for there is no flesh to be had. There are no rivalries, no matings, no territorial displays. The grazers prune the growth; the glass filaments absorb the volcanic breath; the Sieves filter the dry rivers; the Weavers direct the mineral extrusion. 

They are not a civilization, nor are they a feral ecosystem in the biological sense. They are the moving parts of a cold, self-maintaining machine that happens to be made of mineral and horn instead of brass and oil. To linger among them is to realize that the city was not built *for* them, nor did they build the city. They are simply the physical mechanism by which the basin breathes—a dry, silent, immortal clockwork, forever adjusting its own geometry in the shadow of the basalt scarps.
