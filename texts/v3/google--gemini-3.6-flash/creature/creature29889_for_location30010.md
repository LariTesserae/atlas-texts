They are a long-bodied, deliberate people, moving across the wet basalt with the quiet grace of creatures who have never known a predator or a winter. 

Their skin is smooth and luminous, flushed with a subtle, radiant warmth from the humid air, varying in tone from polished copper to dark, earth-rich bronze. Because the sun sits in an eternal noon, casting no long shadows to mark the passage of hours, their eyes have adapted to the constant, shadowless brightness: large, dark irises framed by dense, pale lashes that shed the fine, floating mist of the cascades. 

They walk barefoot. Their feet are wide and remarkably flexible, the soles calloused not into rough hide, but into a tough, supple, rubbery texture that grips the wet, slick quartzite like moss. They move slowly, with a low center of gravity, placing each step with conscious precision. To watch them traverse a three-inch-deep sheet of rushing water across a ninety-degree ledge is to watch water moving over water.

### Attire and Craft

In a world devoid of animals, there is no leather, no wool, no bone, no feathers. Their material culture is entirely botanical, mineral, and metallic.

They dress in garments of beaten bark-cloth—harvested from the fibrous interior of the giant reed-beds—which are soaked in aromatic plant resins to make them water-repellent. These garments hang in long, straight folds, echoing the vertical step-faults of the plateau: sharp, ungathered skirts, heavy shoulder wraps, and broad loincloths dyed in soft tones of ochre, sage, and pale slate.

Their hair is long, dense, and straight, kept strictly bound to prevent it from soaking up the ambient moisture. They weave it into complex, geometric grids along the scalp, secured with flat copper pins and small, hollow beads forged from virgin copper mined from the rift’s deep veins. 

Ornaments are simple and heavy. They wear wide, unadorned copper cuffs on their forearms and ankles, and heavy slabs of translucent, polished quartzite hung around their necks on braided bark-fiber cords. These stones catch the bright, diffuse light, glowing softly against their chests.

### Sustenance

Their diet is drawn entirely from the lush, quiet flora that carpet the wet stone. 

They are master gardeners of stone-beds. Along the edges of the shallow water channels, they cultivate rows of the jointed reeds, harvesting the soft, starch-heavy marrow inside the stalks. They process this marrow into a smooth, pale paste that is baked on sunlight-heated quartzite slabs into dense, flat bread. 

From the vertical basalt faces, they harvest specific varieties of broad-leafed liverwort and emerald moss, which they press into dark, mineral-rich cakes or ferment in covered clay jars with seep-water to create a thick, tangy broth. The translucent succulents that cling to the rock fissures yield a sweet, gelatinous pulp—their primary source of hydration and sugar—which tastes faintly of crushed rain and crushed stone.

Because there is no rot born of decay or insects, food does not spoil quickly; it simply stays wet, preserved by the clean, mineralized waters that wash continuously through their open-air storage chambers.

### Work and Rhythms

Without night, seasons, or sudden weather changes, their lives are not measured by urgency, but by *maintenance*. Time is kept by the subtle, centuries-long wearing of water against copper gates, or the slow, predictable growth of the tall reed-joints.

Their primary work is the stewardship of the water and the stone. They are stonemasons, weavers, and hydraulic engineers of quiet brilliance. 

Groups of three or four will spend days sitting together on a dry terrace platform, utterly silent, shaping massive basalt blocks. One holds a heavy copper chisel; another strikes it with a rounded flint hammer. The rhythm of their work is unhurried—*clack, clack, shhh*—the sound of tool on stone blending seamlessly with the sliding water. They do not force the stone into decorative curves; they follow its natural crystalline cleavage, honoring the ninety-degree angles given by the earth’s uplift.

Others walk the miles of elevated aqueducts and stone channels. Carrying long, leaf-shaped copper scrapers, they gently clear excess moss from the water troughs to ensure the sheets flow at an even, silent depth. Where a channel has worn a fraction of an inch too deep, they bed it with fresh, hand-smoothed tiles of pink quartzite, set in a mortar of crushed volcanic ash and tree resin.

### Language and Social Life

Because the open air is vast and the sound of sliding water is constant, their spoken language has evolved to blend into the acoustic landscape. 

They speak in a quiet, sibilant register—a soft language composed of breathy vowels, gentle clicks of the tongue, and low, resonant humming that does not echo harshly off the cliff faces. They rarely raise their voices. A shout is considered a severe violence against the peace of the plateau.

For long-distance communication across the wide terraces, they have developed a complex, highly expressive sign language. Stand on a high platform, and you will see two figures a quarter-mile apart, standing motionless on their respective stone piers, communicating through sharp, geometric hand movements that slice crisply through the warm mist.

Their living arrangements reflect the vast, uncrowded nature of their city. A family unit—often spanning three generations—occupies a long, low compound of stone slabs. These compounds have no doors, only heavy, resin-soaked wooden shutters that are lowered only when a family seeks deep rest. 

They sleep on raised platforms woven from thick, dried reeds, cushioned with layers of sun-cured ferns that give off a dry, sweet, hay-like fragrance in the damp air. Because there is no darkness, they sleep when their bodies require it, not when the sky dictates. At any given hour, half the city is awake and working, while the other half rests in the cool, quiet shade of their thick-walled stone halls.

### Life, Death, and Philosophy

They possess no concept of an underworld or a distant sky-heaven. To them, existence is an eternal, stationary present—a balanced realm of warm stone, rising water, and green growth. They view themselves not as masters of the plateau, but as its final, logical layer: the intellect that keeps the water flowing cleanly over the rock.

Children are raised collectively in the wide, shallow plazas. They learn to walk by balancing on the narrow stone ribs of the water channels, and their earliest games involve building tiny, temporary aqueducts out of basalt chips to redirect thin threads of seep-water into palm-sized basins.

When an inhabitant dies—usually at a great age, their joints gradually stiffening until they simply choose to lie down upon their fern beds—there is no mourning marked by noise or weeping. 

The deceased is wrapped in layers of dry bark-cloth and bound with copper wire. They are carried up the wide stone staircases to the highest step-faults of the plateau, where the water first emerges from the deep fissures in the rock. There, in high, icy-clear cisterns cut directly into the pristine basalt, the body is laid to rest beneath the flowing surface.

Over decades, the mineral-rich waters slowly calcify the wrapped form. They do not rot; they petrify, turning gradually to stone, becoming a permanent part of the plateau’s foundation—an quiet, unseen block in the vast, eternal garden of water and basalt.
