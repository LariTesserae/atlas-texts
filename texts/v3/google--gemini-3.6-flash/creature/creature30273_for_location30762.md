To understand what lives here, one must abandon the idea of an inhabitant as a visitor or a tenant. Nothing arrives at this altitude, and nothing leaves. The life that moves across the turned heartwood is not separate from the plateau; it is the plateau’s mobile organ tissue, dispatched to trim, seal, tune, and await.

If you were to stand still on the wet parquet, your eyes would slowly adjust to the subtle, colorless motions that attend the great, roaring structures.

High in the fluted vaults, four hundred feet above the floor, are the **Strung Nerves**. They appear at first like thick, pale ropes of unspun silk stretched taut between the spiral columns, but they are alive—striated ribbons of translucent cartilage, entirely devoid of hair, pigment, or eyes. They have no heads, only wide, vascular disks at either end that grip the pale wood like suctioning roots. These creatures do not feed on organic prey, for there is none; instead, they exist entirely on the kinetic energy of the architecture’s hydraulics. 

As the water roars and whistles through the high arches, the Strung Nerves vibrate in exact, harmonic resonance with the wood. They are acoustic balancers. When an internal pressure spike threatens to crack a fluted spire, a dozen of these pale ribbons draw themselves bone-tight across the arch, their flesh hummed into a blur, absorbing the shockwave and dampening the acoustic roar into a smooth, deep chest-tone. Where they touch the pale timber, the bark secretes a sweet, thick weeping that nourishes them directly through their skin.

Lower down, floating upon the still surfaces of the lip-vases, are the **Meniscus Keepers**. They are flat, disc-shaped organisms no thicker than wet parchment, spanning three to eight feet across. Their skin has the pearlescent, milky clarity of peeled grapes. They float motionless on the water’s surface, held in place by microscopic, fibrous cilia that anchor to the inner lip of the wooden basins. 

The Keepers are the reason the water never splashes. As the heavy, fat drops run down the columns and fall into the pools, they hit the backs of these floating sheets. The Keepers absorb the impact instantly through a system of fluid-filled dorsal capillaries, dispersing the force laterally and venting the excess water through tiny underside siphons. They are living tension-regulators, ensuring that not a single drop of the clear, viscous liquid strays onto the dry, unweathered wood outside the basin walls.

Deep within the floor itself, embedded inside the six-foot-thick hexagonal tiles, lives the plateau’s subterranean nervous system: the **Marrow-Threads**. They are never seen in their entirety, but their presence is felt as a rhythmic, localized pulse beneath the soft terraces of the grand stairways. They are long, segmented strands of white, spongy pith, thick as a wrist, that creep perpetually through pre-grown conduit channels beneath the tiles. 

Where two hexagonal floor-tiles join, the Marrow-Threads push tiny, blind, fleshy papillae up through the seams. These papillae taste the air, sampling its sweetness, humidity, and temperature. If a seam begins to dry—if the clear water exuded by the floor stops flowing for even a fraction of a second—the papillae release a hormonal chemical that summons the log-sized, blind resin-slugs from their dark hollows beneath the bio-arches.

And then there is what rests inside the columns.

The cathedral-spires are not solid wood throughout. At their thickest bases, where the spiraled stems meet in broad, fluted buttresses, the wood is translucent—a thick, pale amber bark through which the sunlit mist casts a soft, shadowless glow. 

If you press your ear against the pale alabaster wood and look deep into its semi-translucent depths, you can see them: the **Gestants**.

They are colossal, smooth-limbed forms suspended vertically inside the sap-filled vascular chambers of the pillars. They do not resemble beasts or insects; their shapes are idealizations of weightless geometry, like statues carved from ivory and wet heartwood, with long, ribless torsos, tapering, jointless limbs, and featureless, elongated skulls that curve backward into the spiral of the column itself. 

They are not trapped; they are being grown. Their vascular systems are spliced directly into the main trunk-lines of the plateau’s plumbing. The deep, wet cannon-shots that echo across the plain every few seconds are the pulses of massive, vegetative hearts forcing nutrient-dense resin through the chambers in which the Gestants float. They do not breathe air; their lungs are filled with the same clear, sweet water that hums behind the bark. Their fingers—eight on each hand, long as reeds and fine as needles—are fused to the inner walls of the pillars by fine, white rootlets.

They are completely motionless, locked in the same perpetual noon as the landscape around them. They are not waiting to wake up today, nor tomorrow. They are waiting for the architecture to finish them—for the endless, un-eroded, rot-free cycle of the plateau to reach a final, total saturation. 

Until then, they hang in the pale, warm light of the columns, their translucent eyelids closed, listening to the roaring organ pipes of their cradle, their hearts beating in perfect, silent unison with the wet valves beneath the floor.
