To live in a place where air never moves and light never shifts requires a different relationship with time. Nothing here breathes quickly; nothing here needs to run, hide, or stalk. The life that occupies the basin does not break the stillness—it is made of it.

If you sit by one of the saucer-sized pools and wait for the dark glass of the water to reset after a drop falls, you begin to notice that the bottom of the pocket is not mere silt. Lying perfectly flat against the dark earth are small, oval discs, no larger than a fingernail. They are pale, almost translucent, with the wet-paper texture of peeled seeds. They have no eyes, no visible legs, and no shells. They are *cleaving-plates*—simple, soft-bodied creatures that spend their entire lives pressed against the floor of these tiny, quiet pools. They feed on the invisible film of minerals washed down the sandstone wall by the seepage. When a drop falls from the stone above, the vibration passes through the water and the plate contracts by a fraction of a millimeter, drawing itself tighter against the mud before going completely still again for hours.

Higher up, along the seams where the black-green moss meets the rock, live the *gravel-weavers*. They are small, slow mollusks whose shells are not grown from smooth mother-of-pearl, but built out of the fine, dark silt and micro-pebbles of the basin itself. Their bodies secrete a thick, amber-colored mucus that binds the grains together into lumpy, heavy domes that look exactly like the debris at the foot of the wall. A weaver might spend forty years traveling three paces along a single crack in the sandstone, scraping the microscopic algae from the damp stone with a tongue like a fine rasp. When two weavers meet in a crevice, they do not fight or mate in a hurry; they simply rest shell-to-shell for months, their mucus fusing slightly, sharing warmth in the humid shadow before drifting apart as the moisture shifts.

Beneath the silt, where the mud stays permanently cool and packed, runs a network of pale, fibrous threads. They are not roots, though they resemble them—thin as twine, tough as tanned leather, and smelling faintly of turned earth and old water. They belong to something buried deeper down. 

Near the center of the hollow, in the angle formed by the four square-cut stones, the silt is slightly raised, forming a low, smooth mound that never quite dries out. 

Living in that mud, half-wrapped around the base of the ancient stones, are the *mound-marrow*.

They are broad, low, legless shapes, each about the size of a heavy iron kettle, though soft and yielding to the touch like waterlogged leather. Their backs are dark, grey-brown and hairless, pitted with tiny pores that absorb the ambient moisture directly from the warm air. Moss grows on their upper surfaces in thin, patchy velvety smears, so that when they are resting—which is almost always—they are indistinguishable from the silt-covered blocks of quarried stone beside them.

They have no faces in any familiar sense. At the front of each mound-marrow is a wide, soft fold of skin that stays pressed directly into the damp silt. Through this fold, the creature draws in the fine mud, filtering it through a simple internal gut to extract the trace minerals and decaying leaf-dust washed down from the higher country centuries ago, before venting the clean, smooth clay out behind it. A single mound-marrow moves perhaps a foot every decade, plowing a shallow, almost imperceptible groove through the pebbles that is erased by the seepage long before the creature ever moves again.

They do not make sounds, but they are aware of sound. Inside their thick, rubbery bodies are tiny pockets of fluid suspended over delicate bone-granules. When a drop hits a pool six paces away—*plink*—the shockwave travels through the air, down through the silt, and into the marrow's hide. In response, a faint, rhythmic pulse passes down the length of its back, a low ripple in its wet leather skin, as if acknowledging the water.

Whether these creatures were here when the square stones were carved, or whether they crawled into the hollow long after the masons left, cannot be told. They fit the space too well. They share the temperature of the stone, the moisture of the air, and the deep, heavy patience of the silt. When the sky above slowly dims toward night—though the basin itself barely darkens, only softening from amber-grey to a deeper dusk—the mound-marrow swell slightly, drawing in the thick warm air, exhaling a faint, humid warmth of their own that smells of wet rock and old iron, keeping the hollow just as it has been for a thousand years.
