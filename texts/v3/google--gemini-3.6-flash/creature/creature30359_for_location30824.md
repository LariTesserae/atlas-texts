They are beings built for an economy of dust and wax, no taller than a songbird’s rib cage, slender as dry twigs, and corded with tight, waterless muscle. 

To look at one closely is to see a creature that has traded all fluidity for durability. Their skin is not moist or smooth, but resembles fine, sand-dusted chamois or the tough, velvety surface of a dried apricot. It bears no sweat glands, no pores for weeping; its only secretion is a thick, fragrant tallow—an oily balm produced along the spine and palms that they rub obsessively over their bodies and tools to seal against what little moisture their internal fires generate. 

Their heads are wide and flat-profiled, designed to slice through the warm convection drafts without catching the wind. Their eyes—large, lidless spheres of polished amber set deep within orbital hollows—are shielded by dual nictitating membranes, thin and translucent as snake-skin, which sweep away windborne silt with a dry, soft *click*. They do not blink as wet creatures do; they wipe their eyes with the soft fur of their forearms, which is dense and golden, like the dust-catching hairs on a bee’s leg.

They have four hands. The primary pair, long and six-fingered, ends in flat, spatulate nails of clear horn—perfect scoops for mixing sandstone flour with plant-glue. The secondary pair, smaller and set lower on the sternum, is reserved for high-precision labor: knotting hair-thin gut-lines, carving flint chisels, and harvesting the microscopic spore-capsules of the hanging lichen. 

When they move, they do not walk so much as flow across the vertical city like rust-colored shadows. Their long, double-jointed ankles and prehensile, splayed toes grip the porous resin-walls with absolute security. A worker can carry a mud-brick three times its own weight up a seventy-degree incline, its chest pressed flat against the baked plaster, its four hands operating in a continuous, blurred relay of placement and balance.

They are known—if they are known to anything outside the shadow of the overhang—as the *Pith-bound*, though among themselves their identity is expressed not in a spoken name, but in a shared, rhythmic tapping of the heels against the hollow stone floor.

***

Water, to these people, is not a beverage or an element; it is an ancestral myth, a terrifying solvent that dissolves homes and rots food. They do not drink liquid. To ingest raw water would be to poison their condensed, salt-dense biology. 

Instead, their entire metabolism runs on lipids, aromatic resins, and metabolic synthesis. Their gardens are the succulent carpets; their farms are the thickets of resin-thorn. In the deep, cool niches beneath the plant roots, specialized guilds of extractors—elderly workers whose skin has hardened into something like carved walnut—drive stone wedges into the swollen, silver discs of the fleshy plants. They press out a heavy, pale milk—not water, but a dense emulsion of plant fat, mineral salts, and turpentine-like oils. 

This lipid-milk is the sap of their civilization. They lap it from small, fired-clay cups using short, rasped tongues like those of small cats. It satisfies both hunger and thirst, combusting slowly inside their hot, fast-beating hearts to produce the metabolic energy needed to fuel their relentless activity. Their breath, expelled through slit-like nostrils lined with fine filter-hairs, is hot, bone-dry, and smells intensely of warm spice, iron, and dried amber.

***

Inside the stacked domes, life is dense, communal, and loud. There are no private chambers in the twenty-tier cliffs; a dwelling is a cluster of smooth, egg-shaped cells shared by three or four intergenerational work-lineages. 

Because they do not sleep in long, unconscious stretches—an impossibility in a world where wind and heat demand constant structural maintenance—they rest in short, fifteen-minute trances, wedged together in soft pockets filled with harvested lichen-fluff. They sleep standing, or hanging by their lower limbs from the ceiling beams, their bodies curled into tight, felted balls to conserve warmth during the rare cold spells when the canyon air drops its temperature.

Their culture is governed by a single, absolute taboo: *The Law of the Cold Spark*. 

In an enclave composed entirely of dry resins, parchment-thin blossoms, dried sinew, and tinder-dry lichen, fire is the ultimate demon—a hungry god that would turn their entire universe to ash in the space of fifty heartbeats. They have no lamps, no hearths, no cooked food. They work entirely by the soft, ambient amber haze reflected from the canyon floor and by the faint, cold bioluminescence of certain dust-beetle carapaces crushed into their wall-plaster. 

Their stone tools are never struck together with violent force; flint is flaked by steady, immense thumb-pressure, and all wooden mallets are tipped with compressed succulent-pith to ensure that no friction-heat is ever generated against the sandstone. To create a spark is the only crime punishable by exile—thrown outward into the abyss of the open wind, where the sun bakes a body to parchment in a single afternoon.

***

Their relationship with the alcove’s animal life is not one of mastery, but of tight, mechanical integration. They do not view the beetles or gliders as lower beings, but as specialized citizens of the shelf.

The large walnut-sized scarabs are the city’s draft animals and heavy armor. Fitted with tiny harnesses of braided vine-gut, the beetles drag the heavy stone-slabs up the terraced ramps. In return, the Pith-bound groom their shell-joints with oily leaf-pulp, clearing away abrasive sand, and build dedicated subterranean granaries where the beetles can lay their parchment-textured eggs in safety. 

The thyme-furred gliders are their hunters and aerial scouts. Young Pith-bound, whose bodies are at their lightest and most agile, live high in the golden beard-lichen alongside the glider colonies. They communicate with the small mammals through a language of shrill, bird-like whistles and dry fingernail-snaps, guiding the gliders to leap through the thermal drafts to catch the heavy dust-moths. When a glider returns with its catch, the prize is shared: the glider eats the fat, oily thorax of the moth, while the Pith-bound harvest the dry, fibrous wings to be ground into mortar-binder, and the silk-threads of the cocoon to be spun into resilient rope.

***

Communication across the city is acoustic and structural. The sandstone bluff acts as a massive sounding board. 

A master builder on the lowest tier does not yell to a crew on the nineteenth level; she takes a small, hollow bone rod and strikes a precise, rhythmic sequence into the stone face. The vibration travels instantly through the butter-colored sandstone, emerging as a distinct, musical humming in the floors of the upper tiers. 

Through this rock-talk, the city maintains its furious, hive-like synchronization. A message about a crumbling wall, an incoming dust-draft, or a newly tapped vein of plant-resin ripples through the entire population in seconds. The air is never silent because the rock is never silent; it is a living organ, humming with the continuous *tap-tap-tap* of bone on stone, the dry scraping of flint on mortar, and the high, insectoid chatter of thousands of tiny voices discussing the geometry of their vertical world.

***

In their quietest moments—during the stillness of mid-day when the outer sky is a blinding, white furnace and the alcove rests deep in its amber shadow—the elders gather on the high gut-bridges. 

They do not look down toward the canyon floor, which they consider a dead, bottomless void of dust, nor do they look up at the bare cliff face above. They look inward, at the city they have built out of sand, spit, resin, and time. 

They are small, hot, dry things, living in a space no larger than a chest, yet to them, this pocket of shade is an infinite fortress. They have cut water out of the equation of life, and in doing so, they have made themselves immune to drought. As long as the sun warms the sandstone, as long as the resin-thorn pushes its bitter oils into the dry air, and as long as their tiny mallets continue their relentless, rhythmic tapping against the rock, their crowded, roaring, dust-dusted paradise will never end.
