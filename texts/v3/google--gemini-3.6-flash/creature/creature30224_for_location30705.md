To understand what lives in the basin, one must first understand that here, life is not built on green light, but on gray friction and underground heat. There is no wood to carve, no fiber to spin, and no soil to turn. Every organism—from the microscopic greases to the men huddled on the hummocks—is a parasite or a harvester of the deep sulfur rising through the slate.

### The People of the Hummocks

The human inhabitants call themselves nothing that requires a voice, but in the drumming language of the stone, they are designated by a quick, double-tapped strike that mimics a heartbeat against bone: *The Un-Grushed*. They are the ones who do not grind.

Visually, they seem almost continuous with the landscape. Their bodies are short, wide, and heavily muscled, built with a low center of gravity that keeps them from slipping on the oil-filmed bedrock. Their skin is a pale, water-logged gray-white, perpetually slick with a coating of rendered scraper-fat that protects them from the biting, atomized mist. To touch their skin is to touch cold lard.

Because the ambient noise of the basin—that endless, iron-grinding roar of millions of feeding carapaces—would shred delicate inner-ear membranes over time, their ears have evolved or been culturally modified. Their ear canals are narrow, lined with dense, wiry hair, and plugged with balls of boiled tallow and ash. They do not hear sound through the air as land-dwellers do; instead, they *feel* it through their bones. 

Their feet are their primary sensory organs. They go shod in heavy, flat-soled boots crafted from the flexible throat-hide of predatory eels, lined with the downy, oily feathers of sky-gliders. The soles are wide and soft, spreading under pressure like the pads of a camel, sensitive enough to detect the subtle seismic shifts in the slate that herald a hydrostatic surge or the sudden, erratic charge of a territorial bull-scraper.

#### The Domestic Sphere and Social Fabric
Inside the bone-and-gut shelters, life is quiet, warm, and intensely communal. A family unit—usually six to ten individuals across three generations—lives crammed together in a space no larger than a ship’s cabin. 

Their clothes are masterpieces of scavenged engineering. The outer layer consists of overlapping shingles cut from the glossy black carapace of young scrapers, stitched together with dried, twisted intestine. Beneath this armored shell lies a thick, yellowed layer of inner-skin, lined with the greasy down of gliders. The garments do not absorb water; the perpetual mist simply collects on them in silver globules and rolls off into the tide.

Without fire-wood, their hearths are marvels of chemical ingenuity. They collect porous, bone-white chalk that oozes from mineral vents, soak it in hot, rendered lard, and light it with flint struck against glassy basalt. The hearth does not crackle or smoke; it burns with a quiet, heavy, sulfur-scented yellow flame that casts long, greasy shadows against the white rib-arches of the hut. Over this flame hangs an earthenware pot—knapped and shaped from the thick, iron-rich mud that settles in the slate saucer-pools—wherein chunks of scraper-tongue and eel-tail simmer in their own rendered fat.

Communication is a visual and vibrational art. At close range, they use *Gleam-talk*: a rapid, intricate sign language utilizing the broad palms and dark, mineral-stained fingers, amplified by wrist-bands made of polished scraper-shell that catch the yellow hearth-glimmer. For long-distance communication between hummocks miles apart, they use heavy staves carved from scraper-ribs. By jamming the stave into a specific seam in the slate hummock and striking it with a basalt maul, they send low-frequency thumps traveling through the solid bedrock, through the shallow sheets of water, to be picked up by the bare knees or feet of a neighboring clan.

### The Mega-Fauna: The Scraping Herds (*Radulaths*)

The undisputed masters of the basin are the broad, beetle-like leviathans that create the thunder. A fully grown adult—a *Radulath*—weighs several tons, yet stands no higher than an ox's shoulder. Its body is shaped like a flattened drop of mercury, allowing the shallow sheet-water to slide smoothly over its back without offering any resistance to the howling, cold winds.

Their carapaces are not solid bone or simple chitin, but a composite material: layers of organic protein reinforced with iron and sulfur extracted directly from the slate. The shell is a dark, iridescent charcoal-gray, marked with swirling, concentric growth rings that resemble the grain of polished ironwood.

On their undersides, where a mammal’s chest would be, lies the engine of their existence: the *Grand Radula*. This is a conveyor-belt of thousands of microscopic, diamond-hard chitinous teeth, driven by colossal bands of black muscle. As the beast crawls forward on eight stubby, suction-cupped limbs, the radula rotates, scraping the tallow-scum and the top millimeter of slate into a fine, mineral-rich slurry that is sucked directly into a multi-chambered stomach.

The Radulaths are functionally blind. Their small, amber-colored eyes are buried beneath thick folds of protective horn, useless in the endless twilight. Instead, their entire shell is covered in microscopic pits lined with sensory cilia that read the chemical composition of the water and the depth of the brine sheets.

They do not give live birth, nor do they lay eggs on the slick stone where the tide would sweep them away. During the great hydrostatic swells, when the water rises to knee-depth, the herds aggregate in the center of the basin. There, in a thrashing, roaring mass of grinding armor, females release cloud-like masses of sticky, egg-laden mucus that anchor firmly to the freshly scraped slate seams.

### The Mid-Tier Hunters and Parasites

Where there are millions of heavy, slow-moving herbivores, there is an entire web of violence operating in the shadows between their massive carapaces.

#### The Needle-Stilts
Darting through the gaps in the moving herds are the *Needle-Stilts*—pale, spindly scavengers the size of hounds, built like a cross between a spider and a heron. Their carapaces are thin, almost translucent, revealing the dark, pulsing organs within. Their legs are long, hair-thin tubes of hardened chitin that end in sharp, needle-like tips. These tips pierce the tallow-scum and find purchase in the tiny pits of the slate, allowing them to skitter across the grease-slick stone at incredible speeds without falling.

They possess long, telescopic mouthparts that they plunge into the churned, frothy pulp left behind in the wake of a scraping herd. They feed on the crushed micro-fauna, the loosened sulfur-scum, and the feces of the giant scrapers. They are hyper-alert, their large, faceted silver eyes constantly tracking the sky for gliders and the water for eels.

#### The Slate-Eels (*Grief-Worms*)
In the ankle-deep sheets of water, invisible beneath the ripples, swim the *Slate-Eels*. They are muscle and mucus, three to six feet of slate-gray, legless violence. Because the water is so shallow, they do not swim by waving their bodies vertically, but laterally, flattening themselves until they are no thicker than a man’s wrist.

They are completely blind, relying on lateral lines that run the length of their bodies to feel the turbulence caused by moving limbs. They do not attack the armored backs of the scrapers; instead, they lie in wait in the shallow saucer-depressions until a scraper steps down. With lightning speed, the eel wedges its narrow, wedge-shaped head into the soft, unarmored skin of the beast’s leg-joints or respiratory siphons. Their jaws are lined with backward-curving teeth made of hollow bone that pump a digestive, numbing venom into the host.

To the hummock dwellers, the eels are both a lethal hazard—a single bite can numb a man's leg until he falls and drowns in two inches of water—and a prized resource. Their oil burns cleanest, and their jaws make the sharpest knives.

#### The Sky-Rays (*Vane-Gliders*)
Above the thunder, hanging in the low, weeping cloud-bank, are the *Vane-Gliders*. They have no feathers; their wings are vast, leathery membranes stretched across a framework of hollow, cartilage-like ribs, spanning up to twelve feet from tip to tip. Their bellies are lined with a thick, waterproof mat of silver down, which the hummock dwellers harvest with great difficulty.

Gliders do not flap; the dense, heavy air of the mist-laden basin allows them to soar indefinitely on the thermal updrafts created by the sulfurous heat vents. They hunt by sight, using huge, coal-black eyes that can cut through the silver haze. When a Needle-Stilt strays too far from the safety of a scraper's shadow, or when a Slate-Eel exposes its dark back in the shallow water, the glider folds its wings and drops like a lead plumb. A second before impact, it extends a heavy, anchor-shaped claw at the end of its tail, snags its prey from the slick stone, and snaps its wings open to catch the air barely an inch above the streaming slate.

### The Cycle of the Basin

Nothing is wasted here, because the basin is a closed system bounded by mist and stone. When a scraper grows old and its chitinous teeth wear down to smooth, useless nubs, it can no longer scrape the tallow-scum. Weakened by starvation, it falls behind the herd.

Within hours, the Slate-Eels detect its slowing pulse. They swarm the beast, entering through its mouth and siphons, liquefying it from the inside out. The sky-gliders drop from the fog to tear at the softening eye-sockets and shell-seams. The Needle-Stilts circle the carcass, drinking the dark blood that spreads across the water sheets.

Finally, the hummock people arrive. They come carrying basalt mauls, levers made of glider-thigh, and heavy baskets woven from eel-skin. They strip the thick, valuable plates of iron-chitin for their clothes and roofs. They drain the yellow fat from the body cavity for their lamps. They take the long, arched ribs to repair their homes or carve into long-distance drumming staves.

Within a few hydrostatic tides, the rain washes the stone clean. The bones that are left behind—too heavy or weathered to be used—are slowly dissolved by the sulfurous water, returning their minerals to the tallow-scum. The slate glimmers black and grease-slick once more, waiting for the next herd to come grinding through the silver rain.
