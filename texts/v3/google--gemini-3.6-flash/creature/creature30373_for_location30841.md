At the crest of the highest gallery-towers, where the baking heat meets the amber sky, live the **Sun-Glazers**. 

They are broad, disk-shaped arthropods, their carapaces forged from a dense, natural enamel that shines like fired porcelain under the relentless day. They do not possess eyes in the traditional sense; instead, the upper surfaces of their shells are embedded with thousands of microscopic, quartz-like lenses that focus light into thermal energy. The Sun-Glazers spend their long lives crawling across the outer seams of the towers, scraping the sharp, elevated ridges of obsidian with scissor-like mandibles. They do not eat the glass; they pulverize it within a secondary muscular gizzard, mixing the dry powder with a thick, vulcanizing lipid secreted from glands beneath their flanks. 

When they extrude this mixture over the sun-baked clay walls, it sets within minutes into a seamless, translucent varnish. It is this vitreous coating that protects the continent-spanning towers from the scouring abrasion of the continental silt-fronts. A Sun-Glazer moves with a slow, heavy rhythm, its twelve short, padded feet clicking against the hardened resin like small wooden pegs on tile. When a dust-front approaches, a Sun-Glazer simply flattens its broad rim against the tower face, sealing itself to the wall like a barnacle on a hull, letting the roaring wall of lime and silica wash over its polished back without a sound.

***

Farther down, in the crisscrossing mid-tiers where the leathery canopies of burgundy and jade foliage interlock with the gallery walls, the **Lacquer-Clippers** operate in endless, silent processions. 

These are the six-legged harvesters, each the size of a hounds-skull, encased in husks of deep, iridescent bronze. They are the gardeners and fuel-gatherers of the city. Their forelimbs are asymmetrical: one side ends in a heavy, notched blade that functions like a pruner, while the other is a soft, segmented paddle covered in velvet-like cilia. 

The Clippers march in single file along the suspended tube-bridges, their paths marked by a sticky, fragrant trail of dried amber resin that acts as a highway guide. Upon reaching the massive, succulent columns, they do not harvest the living tissue of the plant. Instead, they seek out the heavy, shell-like blossoms filled with dense, petroleum-rich nectar. With surgical precision, a Clipper uses its blade-limb to crack open the blossom's outer casing, then uses its velvet paddle to soak up the viscous, sugary oil, transferring it into an internal crop-sac. 

As they work, a faint, rhythmic sound fills the foliage—not a voice, but the dry, paper-like friction of their wing-cases rubbing against their abdomens as they vent internal heat. They are entirely unbothered by the towering height or the sharp mineral drop below; their feet are tipped with micro-hooks that can anchor into raw obsidian or waxy leaf-skin with equal ease.

***

In the shaded, vaulted arcades beneath the canopy—where the mineral heat of the ground is buffered by the thick walls—move the **Chalk-Paws**. 

These are the pale, leathery quadrupeds, the grazers of the dry city. They stand low to the earth, their bodies long and flexible, draped in thick, loose hides that resemble oiled chamois or tough, unpolished leather. Their skin is entirely sweatless, dusty with the fine lime-powder of the streets. They have no fur; instead, their spines are lined with short, overlapping plates of smooth bone that shield their kidneys from the ambient warmth of the stone walls.

A Chalk-Paw’s head is blunted and spade-shaped, with micro-eyed sockets covered by protective nictitating membranes that keep out mineral grit. They feed on the tough, wire-like roots and fibrous mat-grasses that bind the dry dust of the plain together. Their teeth are broad, flat slabs of dense dentine that grind stone and root alike into a fine paste. 

They travel in tight, silent herds of forty or fifty, moving through the lower avenues with a soft, shuffling gait. Their hooves are split and tipped with chisel-like edges that allow them to climb near-vertical chalk faces to reach high-hanging root-mats. They do not vocalize. When a herd moves, the only sign of their passage is the hushed *shhh-shhh* of their leathery bellies brushing against the low archways and the rhythmic *thunk* of their hooves striking the hollow stone floors.

***

Stalking the margins of these herds are the **Vellum-Runners**, the quietest lifeforms on the plain. 

A Vellum-Runner is a predator of striking, unsettling grace. Long-bodied and lungless, its torso is a narrow, segmented lattice of lightweight cartilage and dark, hardened chitin, covered in a skin that feels like old parchment. Because it does not breathe air through lungs, it has no ribcage and no chest rise; oxygen is drawn passively through long, dust-filtered spiracles running the length of its flanks. 

It moves without a sound, its four long, jointed legs ending in wide, fibrous pads that absorb all vibration. Where a mammal’s head would be, the Runner has a sleek, tapered wedge dominated by two broad, fan-like antennae made of delicate, golden feathers. These antennae do not catch scent alone; they are hyper-sensitive to the faintest thermal ripples and the micro-vibrations of a Chalk-Paw’s hoof on the gypsum floor.

When a Vellum-Runner hunts, it does not sprint or leap with explosive force. It simply flows through the shaded galleries like a shadow cast by a passing cloud. It can remain motionless for days in an archway notch, its parchment skin blending imperceptibly with the dry terracotta, waiting for a grazer to pass beneath. When it strikes, it drops down with absolute silence, pinning its prey with long, barbed raptorial limbs. It does not spill liquid blood—its prey’s circulatory fluid is a thick, lipid latex that quickly thickens into an amber paste upon exposure to the air, sealing the wound even as the Runner feeds.

***

Deep beneath the city, in the dark, cool galleries carved into the raw chalk and volcanic roots of the plain, live the **Marrow-Masons**. 

These are massive, sessile creatures—the ancient, long-lived heart of each gallery-tower. A single Mason can fill a chamber the size of a cathedral. They resemble vast, segmented grubs or soft-bodied crustaceans, their hides encrusted with layers of calcified silk, dried resin, and mineral dust. 

The Masons never see the light of the amber sky. They sit in the deep dark, receiving the endless streams of raw materials brought down to them by millions of smaller worker-arthropods: powdered bone, crushed obsidian, harvested petroleum nectar, and dry plant fiber. 

Within the warm, churning chambers of their multi-chambered bellies, the Masons process these raw ingredients into the master-mortars of the continent. They extrude thick, rope-like conduits of calcified silk that serve as the structural rebar for new gallery-towers. They spit out pastes that heal cracks in the city’s foundations. 

Surrounding each Mason are thousands of small, blind **Tenders**—tiny, crab-like creatures that scrub the Mason’s hide, collect its extruded mortars in their mandibles, and carry them upward through the dark shafts to the expanding frontier of the city. 

In these deep nurseries, where the air is warm and smells heavily of bitter cedar oil and sulfur, the next generations lay resting. Eggs—not soft or wet, but encased in hard, amber-like shells that rattle like dry beans—are stacked in vast, organized catacombs, dusted with fine mineral spores. They require no liquid to hatch, only the steady, gentle heat generated by the metabolism of the Mason and the low, rhythmic friction of a million bodies moving in unison above.

***

Nothing here knows what water is. Nothing here has ever seen a cloud drop rain, nor felt the soft yielding of mud. 

Life on this fractured plain does not fight the dry, mineral reality of its world; it *is* the mineral reality, elevated into motion. The creatures are made of the glass they walk upon, the chalk they bore through, and the rich, oily resins they cultivate. They are both the citizens and the brickwork, an endless, self-replenishing engine of horn, silk, and stone, quietly expanding its borders across the gold-hazed floor of the world.
