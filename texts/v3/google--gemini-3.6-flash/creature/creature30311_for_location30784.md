To live here, a body must give up the warm, wet physics of the surface. There is no blood in the Lower Trench—at least, not blood as the sunlit world understands it. Liquid is an extravagance the rift forgotten; fluid would evaporate into the dry, dense air, leaving behind a crust of salt and a shriveled carcass in hours.

Instead, the creatures of the trench are built of chalk, keratin, horn, and pressurized gas. Their interiors are lubricated not by water, but by microscopic platelets of graphite, talc, and dry sulfur powder that coat their jointed plates, keeping bone from grinding to dust against bone.

At the base of this food chain are the **Cliff-Striders**. Up close, their hound-sized bodies resemble six-legged chess pieces carved from bleached boxwood and slate. They have no soft eyes; their forward faces are smooth shields of translucent quartz through which they perceive light only as vague shifts in the indigo twilight. To navigate, they rely entirely on the deafening ambient sound of the cavern. The wall of sound is their lantern. Their long, jointed legs end in corundum-hard spurs that vibrate in sympathy with the air; by reading how the sound waves bounce off carved lintels and eroded causeways, they move across the vertical facades with terrifying, fluid speed. They are the city’s gleaners. Their mouths are simple, pneumatic shears that snip the iron-stemmed shrubs and scrape the chalk-moss from the ancient stone, dropping the dry material into internal gizzards packed with basalt grit. When a cliff-strider dies, it does not rot; it simply stops, dries further, and is slowly dismantled by its kin, its hollow shell used as a shelter by smaller, unseen things.

Sinking deeper into the plazas, one encounters the **Cragsmen**—the heavy, shell-backed grazers. They are the size of oxen, though far lower to the ground, looking less like animals and more like boulders draped in overlapping shingles of grey mica and bone. A Cragsman moves at the pace of a shadow stretching across an afternoon. Beneath their heavy carapaces, they possess no feet, but rather a wide, muscular underside covered in thousands of tiny, hardened bristles. They crawl across the moss-flats like living sandpaper, leaving broad, polished tracks on the flagstones. 

Their respiration is the source of the deep, rasping calls that shake the trench air. Lacking wet lungs, they draw the dense, mineral-rich nitrogen through long, baffled side-vents. As they exhale, they expel fine clouds of white chalk dust and dry mineral dross, making them look from a distance like slow-burning engines. A Cragsman’s heartbeat occurs once every forty seconds—a heavy, mechanical *thump* that can be felt through the floorboards of the ancient stone plazas before it is heard. They live for centuries, their shells slowly accumulating layers of blown yellow dust and wind-borne spores until young tube-trees take root directly on their backs, traveling with them across the forgotten city.

High above, in the gloom of the carved arches and vaulted ceilings, roost the **Parchment-Swifts**. These are the dry-winged flyers, creatures with wingspans six feet across, made of stiff, sunless leather that crackles like old parchment when flexed. They have no feathers; their flight is a series of stiff, mechanical glides and sudden, explosive snaps of their wing-joint tendons. They nest in the millions inside the upper floors of the ancient buildings, using a paste of ground chalk and excreted mineral resins to cement their hives to the friezes and cornices. 

When the swifts take flight, it is not in search of water or meat, but to feed on the "yellow storm"—the dense clouds of aromatic, spore-rich powder thrown off when the tube-tree forests mature. They ride the slow air currents that shift with the planet’s breathing, their wide, scoop-like maws filtering the dust mid-air. The thunder of their wings is not a sound of panic, but of routine; it is the heartbeat of the upper canopy, vibrating through the stone pillars and keeping the dust from ever fully settling.

And yet, deep within the widest, most eroded avenues—where the causeways have smoothed into long, grey dunes of crushed slate—there lives something else. Something that does not merely inhabit the architecture, but seems to remember it.

They are called the **Silt-Walkers**, though there is no one left down here to name them. 

Standing twelve feet tall, they are spindly, upright beings constructed from jointed rings of hollow, petrified bone, wrapped in tight bands of dried, fibrous muscle that look like cured hemp. They move with an agonizing, majestic slowness, taking minutes to cross a single flagstone. Their heads are tall, narrow crests of horn, devoid of mouths or eyes, perforated like flute-pipes. 

The Silt-Walkers do not hunt, nor do they eat the moss. They are energy-harvesting organisms of a stranger sort: their long, needle-thin limbs are tipped with piezoceramic nodes that convert the relentless acoustic vibrations of the trench into minute electrical currents, powering their slow, dry metabolisms. The deafening roar of the cavern is their food. In a quiet cave, a Silt-Walker would starve; here, bathed in perpetual, booming thunder, they endure indefinitely.

They spend their centuries tending the ruins. With long, delicate fingers that click like dry castanets, a Silt-Walker will spend forty years clearing dust from a single carved inscription, or adjusting the angle of a fallen stone so that the wind whistles through it at a specific pitch. 

Their language is the only thing down here that competes with the roar. They speak by rubbing hard, pyrite-coated ridges along their flanks—a sound like a heavy iron door dragging across granite. A single word can take three hours to articulate; a thought can last a season. When two Silt-Walkers meet in the center of a terraced plaza, they may stand face-to-face for a decade, their horn-crests leaning together, exchanging long, rasping harmonics that blend so perfectly into the acoustics of the Lower Trench that an outsider would hear only the wind, the scraping of claws, and the endless, ancient roaring of the earth.
