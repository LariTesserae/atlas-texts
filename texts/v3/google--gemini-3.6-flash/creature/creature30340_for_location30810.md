To live here is to be a creature of lock and ledge, adapted not to the soft yielding of dirt, but to the unforgiving, polished grain of iron-hard heartwood. In a world without soil, life does not dig; it anchors, wedges, slides, or floats.

### The Shell-Grinders (*Iron-sheaths*)
On the vast, exposed domes and timber-cliffs, where the warm winds sweep unobstructed, the surface belongs entirely to the clingers. These are broad, low-slung organisms the size of shield-bosses, covered in overlapping plates of dark, iridescent chiton that mimic the oiled-horn sheen of the wood beneath them. They have no soft skin exposed to the air. 

Their underside is a single, muscular foot lined with thousands of microscopic, horn-tipped hooks that ratchet directly into the sub-millimeter striations of the wood grain. To dislodge one requires tearing the wood itself. They move without rising, crawling in rhythmic, shuddering pulses. 

They do not eat the wood—which is too dense for any digestive enzyme—but live on the thin, grey film of micro-mosses that drink the tea-colored pool-water, and the dried, salty wax left behind by evaporating sap-mist. Their mouthparts are heavy, mineralized scrapers that work like twin chisels, grinding side-to-side in perfect, mechanical unison with millions of their kind. It is their collective feeding—a endless, miles-wide rasping—that produces the deafening, metallic roar that hums through the mountain's core.

### The Bellows-Beasts of the Hollow Spires
Deep inside the three-thousand-foot vertical flutes—where ancient trunks rotted out internally long before the wood turned to stone—lives a pale, soft-bodied fauna that never sees the sun. 

The largest of these are the *Flute-dwellers*: vast, blind creatures shaped like ribbed bladders, forty feet long, hanging suspended in the vertical shafts by dense webs of spun silk that have calcified into rigid cables. Their skin is smooth, moist, and semi-translucent, stained amber by the bitter air. They possess no eyes, only wide, heat-sensitive pits along their flanks that read the rising thermals pulsing from the depths.

As the hot, sap-scented air surges up through the hollow spires like chimney smoke, these creatures expand, drawing the thick gas deep into cavernous internal chambers. When they contract, forcing the air out through muscular, cartilage-ringed siphons, the sound hits the wooden walls of the spire and amplifies into the low, foghorn-like boom that rolls across the twenty-mile gaps. They do not hunt; they are stationary filter-feeders, catching the clouds of heavy spores and drifting wax-pith that the updrafts carry up from the unseen bottom of the world.

### The Mid-Air Predators (*Leather-snappers*)
The open voids between the root-arches are the domain of the hound-sized flyers. They are built entirely of wire-like muscle and dark, oil-slick leather. Their wings are not feathered, but are thick, flexible sheets of skin stretched over light, hollow-boned frames derived from dense wood-fibers. 

Because the air is warm and dense, they do not flap constantly; they leap from the high ledges, drop a thousand feet into the sunlit gulfs, and ride the rising warm currents, their broad, blunt-nosed heads searching for movement on the wooden slopes. They target the shell-grinders when the beetles lift their armor plates to mate, or swoop down to scoop drinkable dew from the saucer-pools. 

When a flock turns in unison within a narrow air corridor, the crack of their wings catching the thermal sounds like a volley of whips snapping against wet leather. They roost upside down on the underside of the giant arches, hanging in clusters of thousands, bathed in the glass-like beads of condensation that drip endlessly into the abyss below.

### The Resin-Crawlers (*Amber-gills*)
In the dark, six-foot-wide fissures that split the plateau, where the thick, golden resin trickles at a rate of an inch a day, exists an ecosystem operating on an entirely different scale of time. 

Here live the *Amber-gills*—elongated, blind, worm-like organisms with skin as tough and slick as wet horn. They spend centuries pushing through the slow-moving rivers of sticky sap. They feed on the ancient botanical matter, preserved insects, and trapped air bubbles locked within the golden resin. Their metabolisms are so slow that a single breath can last three days. 

They secrete an oily, non-stick mucus that prevents the viscous sap from trapping them, allowing them to swim, in slow motion, through the dense, honey-thick depths. When a resin slide finally breaks free and falls into the gulfs, it carries generations of amber-gills with it, entombed in glass-bright amber that will crack open on the valley floor miles below.

### The Human Clinging
And among these specialized giants live the humans—few in number, sparse, and entirely shaped by the wood.

Physically, they have adapted to a world without flat, soft ground. Their feet are wide and flat, with unusually long, opposable toes and leathery soles cracked like dried bark, giving them an almost claw-like grip on the slick grain. They walk with a low, bent-kneed gait, their bodies perpetually leaning into the wind, always maintaining three points of contact when moving outside their sheltered knots.

They do not wear clothes of woven thread, for there is no flax or wool; instead, they wear supple, oil-cured layers of inner-bark fibers, beaten until soft and stained dark with bitter tannin juices that make the fabric waterproof against the sudden, drifting mist-veils. Their skin is deeply tanned, weathered by the unimpeded high-altitude sun, and permanently scented with the warm, rich odors of wax, smoke, and resin.

Their culture is one of extreme stillness and reverence for weight. To lose a tool off a ledge is to lose it forever—it will fall two miles before hitting anything. Thus, every horn scraper, bone mallet, and gourd cup is attached to a lanyard of braided vine, pinned to the user’s wrist or anchored to the wood grain. 

They live in symbiosis with the landscape's strange fauna:
* They harvest the outer wax from the tendrils, using it to coat their bark-dwellings, render their ropes water-resistant, and light tiny, smokeless wax-lamps in the windless hollows.
* They harvest the shed armor-plates of dead shell-grinders, binding them together with tendon-thongs to form shields, scrapers, and wind-breaks.
* They listen constantly. To these people, the deafening wall of sound is not noise; it is a complex meteorological map. If the grinding roar of the beetles turns sharp and metallic, it means the wood is drying and a cold wind is coming. If the foghorn booms from the hollow spires drop an octave, it signals that the deep thermals are cooling, and the fog will soon settle heavy into the rifts.

They build no permanent cities, alter no landscapes, and leave no scars on the iron heartwood. They are simply another species of lichen-gatherer, nesting in the lee of the world's ancient root-knots, suspended between the pale golden sky and the endless, dark depth below.
