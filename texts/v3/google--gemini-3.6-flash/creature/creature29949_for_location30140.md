To endure this place, life must forgo all softness. In a world made of razor-edged obsidian and water moving with the force of a hydraulic ram, soft tissue is merely chum for the current. Whatever survives within this stone throat must be constructed like a spring, an anvil, or a lens.

The pale, six-foot ribbons that shoot through the blue column are the masters of this violent volume. They are not fish; they possess no scales, no gills, no eyes, and no swim bladders—a bladder would implode under the sheer mechanical weight of the water column or burst during the rapid updrafts. Instead, their bodies are composed of dense, high-density cartilage and a thick, self-healing gel that feels, to the touch, like vulcanized rubber slicked with cold lard. 

They are creatures of pure hydraulics. Their internal organs are not suspended in soft cavities, but are embedded directly within a solid, muscular lattice that uses the mountain’s own absurd pressure to drive their circulation. They do not swim by waving fins; they possess series of jet-like siphon valves along their flanks, through which they compress and expel the high-pressure water around them. They move with an terrifying, erratic speed—snapping sideways in an instant, using the violent shear forces of the whirlpools to sling themselves from one side of the chimney to the other.

Because they are blind, they navigate entirely through acoustic resonance. The metallic ringing of the water tearing against the quartz edges acts as a sonar map for them. Their heads are smooth, bone-hard spades of solid cartilage, capped with a dense array of electro-receptive pores that "read" the crystalline charge of the basalt and the iron pulses from the vent below. When they strike the sharp rock face—as they often do, using the edges to scrape away skin-parasites or shed old layers of tough, milky cuticle—their bodies do not tear; they bounce with a dull, heavy thud that vibrates through the rock like a wooden mallet hitting a cask.

They feed on two things: the golden glass-weeds and the sun itself.

The needle-weeds clinging to the quietest basalt seams are not true plants, but cold-water coral-hybrids. Their golden, spun-glass needles are hollow light-pipes. Piercing mountain sunlight hits these needles, and the glass fibers act as natural fiber-optics, channeling the raw photons straight down into the calcified bulbs anchored to the basalt. Inside these bulbs, colonies of chemosynthetic bacteria turn the sunlight and the minerals dissolved from the raw volcanic glass into a rich, sugary sap.

The pale ribbon-creatures glide along the razor-sharp rock faces, pressing their hard, ringed mouths against the base of the glass-weeds. They do not graze smoothly; they strike the rock, crushing the calcified bulbs with powerful, muscular pharyngeal plates, vacuuming up the bacterial sap before the current sweeps it away.

Higher up in the water column, where the human rigging hangs, a secondary, opportunistic ecosystem has taken root. 

The crude oak wedges and braided sinew ropes have been submerged long enough to absorb the harsh mineral tea of the rift. The sinew lines—taut as bowstrings and humming at a terrifying, bone-rattling pitch—are covered in tiny, wedge-shaped barnacles with translucent shells made of pure fluorite. These organisms do not feed on organic matter swept in from the outside; they feed on kinetic energy and acoustic vibration. Their shells are piezoresistive—they convert the violent hum of the rope into metabolic chemical energy. When the pulse of warm, mineral water surges from the vent and sets the heavy stone pendulum swinging, these tiny shells flare with a faint, internal bioluminescence, glowing like frost-covered embers beneath the churning froth.

The basalt sphere of the pendulum itself has become the central, unnatural altar for the ribbon-creatures. It is the only smooth, rounded object in a world of knife-edges. 

They use it. When the warm, iron-heavy surge hits and the dark basalt sphere swings in its wide, underwater arc, two or three of the pale ribbons will shot out of the lower depths, tracking its motion. They loop their smooth, white bodies around the sweeping sphere, using its mass to drag them against the main current. They ride the pendulum like a carousel, twisting around the cord, using the smooth stone to massage their muscular flanks and loosen the heavy, calcified crusts that form around their siphon valves. For a few seconds, as the sphere reaches the peak of its swing and scatters the sunlight into shifting caustics, the pale creatures are bound to the human artifact—spinning together in the blue, violently bright water, before they let go and are hurled back down into the roaring blackness of the bottom vents.

Down at that bottom, where the sun cannot reach and the fissure narrows into the pressurized throats of the earth, sits the origin of the warm pulses: a massive, dense mass of colonial sea-sponges, hard as iron ore and shaped like the pipes of an organ. They grow directly over the subterranean thermal vents, acting as biological pressure valves. They grow until their stony bodies seal the vent; pressure builds beneath them until the mountain violently blows the seal, driving a geyser of superheated, iron-rich water upward through the pipe, washing the chamber in heat and minerals, feeding the weeds, driving the pendulum, and starting the kinetic engine all over again.
