To understand what lives here, you must first unlearn the notion that life is a thing of soft membranes, wet channels, and pulsing sacks of water. 

Here, life is not liquid; it is *kinetic architecture*.

If you stand in one of the wide, paved plazas and watch the long, red shadow cast by a tufa terrace, you will eventually notice that the shadow does not move with the sun—it moves with the heat. It shrinks and expands in rigid, rhythmic hitches. 

What appears to be a buttress supporting the lower wall of a granary is not masonry. It is a creature at rest.

Its body is a segmented lattice of compressed iron-dust, dark amber resin, and thin, interlocking plates of slate. It has no head, no eyes, and no lungs. In place of muscles, it relies on bimetallic expansion: two distinct minerals fused along its limbs expand at different rates under the deep, subterranean heat, forcing its long, jointed legs to flex with immense, silent force. When the basalt below groans and releases a wave of radiant heat, the creature’s joints creak like dry floorboards, and it steps forward. 

Its movement is slow, jerky, and deliberate—the motion of a heavy clockwork mechanism driven by the heat of the earth.

These are the Builders, though it is more accurate to call them the *Maintainers*. They do not inhabit the houses; they *are* the houses when they choose to be, locking their slate-plated limbs into the dry-stone walls during the hottest hours to brace the shale against the tectonic creeping of the basalt rift. Their "hands" are heavy, chisellike scoops made of dense hematite, worn down by millennia of scraping the valley floor. 

It was not a forgotten race of men who hand-chipped the tufa blocks or grooved the steep staircases; it was these creatures, working through the endless dusk. The concave hollows in the steps were not worn down by human feet, but by the endless, grinding descent of these mineral weights, sliding down from the upper terraces to harvest dry materials from the resin-forest.

They move with purpose, though it is not a purpose driven by hunger or thought. They are governed by thermal gradients and acoustic frequency.

When a giant, hollow stalk splits open with its violent, rifle-shot *crack*, showering the terrace in mustard-yellow sulfur, the sound sets off a chain reaction among the smaller life-forms lurking in the wax-leaf thickets. 

These smaller entities—no larger than a mason's hammer—are the *Clickers*. They look like flattened, multi-faceted burs of dark slag and polished quartz. They nest in the geometric breathing-holes of the tall cane-structures, held in place by static charge. When the air vibrates with the snap of an exploding pod, the sudden release of pressure sends them skittering across the dry pumice in short, frantic bursts. They do not walk; they leap by snapping their curved, leaf-spring legs of tempered resin, making a sound like hail hitting a tin roof.

The Clickers are the gatherers. They swarm the fresh sulfur-fall, rolling the hot, dry powder into dense, compact pellets using sticky, wax-coated palps. They carry these pellets into the empty, windowless rooms of the city.

This explains the strange emptiness of the buildings. The rooms are not dwellings; they are dry-chemistry hearths. 

Inside the small, square chambers, the sulfur pellets are stacked in precise, geometric pyramids near the corner niches. There, exposed to the blistering heat radiating through the stone floors, the sulfur slowly reacts with powdered iron-hematite brought in by the Maintainers. The chemical reaction releases no flame, no steam, and no liquid—only a low, creeping volume of dense, hot gas that fills the room, building pressure until it vents through the narrow, lipless doorways.

This escaping gas is the breath of the city. It feeds the whistling stalks outside; it drives the warm drafts that rattle the gray wax fronds; it keeps the entire engine of the rift turning.

And high above, clinging to the dark, slag roof where the sky is just a violet thread, live the *Chime-Weavers*. 

They are vast, flat sheets of silken, vitreous lace—woven from thin threads of pulled glass and resinous sap that the wax-plants secrete when scorched by gas vents. Suspended across the narrowest parts of the canyon like dust-choked spiderwebs, the Weavers have no independent locomotion. They are played like instruments by the rising column of superheated air. As the draft roars upward toward the roof-slit, it passes through the fine perforations in their glass bodies, creating a deep, resonant hum that vibrates through the rock walls. 

The Maintainers adjust their stone-shaping, the Clickers leap, and the resin-canes burst all in response to the harmonic frequencies produced by the Weavers above.

There is no death here in any sense we would recognize, only wear. 

Eventually, after three or four centuries of thermal flexing, a Maintainer’s resin-joints become brittle. Its iron-dust plates lose their spring. During one of its long, creaking ascents up the canyon staircases, a joint locks, a slate plate fractures, and the creature simply stops. It remains standing on the staircase, locked in place, swept clean by the dry drafts. 

Over the next hundred years, the heat and the drifting grit reduce it to a smooth, dark boulder resting on a rounded step. Other Maintainers will come, scrape away its flaking iron shell with their hematite chisels, and use the dense core of its body as a lintel or a floor slab for a new terrace.

The city is never empty. It is perpetually full of its own slow, dry, mechanical self-creation. The inhabitants do not live *in* the valley; they are the valley's upper layer, a self-repairing skin of stone, resin, and friction, quietly clattering in the dark heat, completely indifferent to the absence of water, sky, or soul.
