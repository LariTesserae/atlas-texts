They are known, if they could be said to be named at all, by the touch of their skin against the slate: the Silt-Fleshed.

There are rarely more than five or six of them present in the vault at any given time, though to look at the thousands of stacked, polished cells, one might imagine an ancient army had once retreated here. In truth, the cells are not built for a crowd; they are built for time. A single steward may occupy a dozen different niches over the course of a century, sleeping in whichever hollow sits closest to the channel currently requiring its care.

They are small, standing no higher than a child’s waist, but heavy as packed wet clay. Their bodies are low, broad, and hairless, shaped like river-smoothed boulders with thick, neckless shoulders and short, spade-like limbs. Their skin is the exact shade of the black mudstone—cool, perpetually wet, and smooth to the touch, lacking pores or sweat glands, covered instead in a micro-textured mantle that sheds water in fine, running beads. They have no visible eyes; where eyes might have been, there are only seamless, thickened ridges of slate-gray hide, dense with nerve endings sensitive to the slightest shift in air pressure, temperature, and hydraulic thrum.

In a realm where the roar of falling water shatters speech before it can form, the stewards do not use sound to communicate. They live in a world of pure vibration and mineral resonance. 

When two stewards meet on a narrow ledge of quartz, they do not speak or gesture with their hands. They lean forward until their broad, flat foreheads press together, or they press a palm against the same slab of wet basalt. Through the continuous, deafening thunder of the subterranean vault, they feel each other’s pulse and the subtle, rhythmic tensing of muscle. A tap of a hardened horn-nail against a quartz vein carries a message through the stone faster than a scream could travel through the fog: *a channel in the third tier is choking on sand; a tallow-pot in the lower basin is growing cold.*

Their fingers are five-jointed and tipped with blunt, spade-like nails made of a hard, horn-like mineral that never grows brittle in the damp. These fingers are their primary organs of sight and thought. With them, a steward can feel a micro-fracture in a stone gutter long before it opens into a leak, tracing the path of the water with delicate, questing strokes, reading the grain of the mudstone as if it were a raised map.

Their work is quiet, endless, and entirely restorative. A steward will spend three days wedged into a single, water-drenched nook no larger than a bucket, using a narrow probe fashioned from the rib-bone of an extinct, cavern-dwelling beast to lift individual grains of heavy quartz sand out of a drainage channel. They mix their mortar in small, flat stone trays held against their bellies, blending fine river-silt with a thick, gelatinous secretion scraped from their own sides—a natural binder that cures underwater into a dark, rock-hard seal.

When they sleep, they fold themselves so completely into the carved niches that they become indistinguishable from the wall itself. They pull their limbs tight against their bellies, drop their heavy heads into the hollows of their chests, and let the weeping water sheet over their backs. In this state, their heartbeats slow to a single, dull thud every few minutes, matching the slow, geological pulse of the surrounding slate.

Alongside the stewards, a tiny, specialized ecosystem persists in the shadow of the wet walls.

Deep within the quartz-veined cracks, where the water squeezes through under high pressure, live the *grubs of the stone-seam*—the pale, finger-length amphibians. They are entirely cartilage and translucent skin, so thin that the dark line of digested calcium grit is visible through their bellies. They possess no eyes, no lungs, and no limbs; they move by undulating through the film of water that coats the walls, clinging to the polished rock with tiny, suction-like mouthparts. They do not eat plants or fungi, for none exist here. Instead, they feed directly upon the mineral crusts—scraping microscopic layers of soft calcium and iron salts from the stone, converting the dead lithic world into pale, living jelly.

More rarely, the ceiling fissures yield larger treasure. Every few months, the steady weeping of the basalt ceiling will falter for a moment, accompanied by a wet, slapping sound that stands out even against the roaring acoustic trap of the room. A drift-beast has fallen through from the deeper, flooded subterranean channels above.

These drift-creatures are soft, blind, eel-like things, as long as a man’s forearm, white as milk and swollen with thick layers of dense, cold-water blubber. They arrive dazed or dying, crushed by the immense hydraulic pressure of the deep seams they were swept from.

The arrival of a drift-beast is the only event that alters the slow tempo of the vault. The nearest steward moves toward the catch-basin with immediate, silent purpose. Nothing is wasted. The thick, pale fat of the creature is painstakingly rendered down in small, natural sulfur-heated hollows near the vault’s floor, turned into the cloudy, slow-burning tallow that feeds the tiny lamps set in the wall-niches. The soft, cartilage-rich bones are saved, scraped clean, and carved into new gutter-probes, fine scrapers, or structural bridges to span newly eroded steps. The skin is dried over sulfur vents until it turns into a tough, water-impermeable leather, used to patch deep wall-leakages where clay mortar alone would wash away.

Life here does not progress toward a future; it circles within a tight, perfected loop. 

The stewards do not build new tiers. They do not expand the vault. They are not conquerors of the stone, but its caretakers, as much a part of the vault’s hydrology as the basalt gutters and the weeping fissures. 

When a steward finally reaches the end of its immense lifespan—after centuries of wet, rhythmic labor—it does not decay in the manner of surface creatures. Its flesh slowly hardens, absorbing the heavy iron and calcium from the water that washes over it day and night. It climbs into one of the deepest, lowest niches, curls its limbs tight, and ceases to move. Over decades, the continuous mineral weeping coats its silt-skin in a fine, glittering rind of calcite. The body turns to stone, merging seamlessly with the wall, becoming another polished lintel, another step, another permanent, silent curve in the roaring, dark citadel.
