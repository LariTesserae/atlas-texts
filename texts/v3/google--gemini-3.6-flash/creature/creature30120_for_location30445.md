To live here is not to be made of flesh. Flesh would be tenderized by the bone-shaking hum in an hour, dissolved by the chemical drizzle in a week, and ground into red silt by the shifting floorplates long before it could find anything to eat.

Instead, the inhabitants of this iron basin are the descendants of function—things that began as maintenance, or monitoring, or simple structural containment, and have spent three thousand years forgetting what they were built to fix. They have adapted to the rust the way sea creatures adapted to salt.

***

In the shallow pools that fill the worn tread-patterns, where the standing waves create endless shivering grids, there are **the Skimmers**. 

To a casual glance, they appear to be nothing more than discarded leaves of heavy gauge steel or fallen boiler plating, three or four feet across, lying submerged in the dark condensate. But when the regional hydraulic shift tilts the deck and the water begins its slow, sheet-like migration, the Skimmers turn into the current. 

They have no limbs, no eyes, no engines that burn fuel. Their undersides are coated in a thick, fibrous pelt of galvanized wire—not true fur, but dense brushwork of copper and lead filaments that have grown through galvanic accretion. As the tea-colored water flows beneath them, these copper fibers strip out the suspended iron dust, the crushed quartz grains, and the dissolved zinc washed down from the ceiling lattice. 

They move by magnetostriction: as the high-voltage insulators arc overhead, sending invisible ripples of electromagnetic force through the damp air, the Skimmers’ bodies expand and contract in tiny, rapid spasms. They slide smoothly over the rounded rivets like flat, metallic soles, leaving long, polished tracks in the wet rust-mud. Where two Skimmers cross paths, they do not fight or mate; they simply grindingly overlap, shedding their frayed edges against one another like river stones polishing their sides, dropping small flakes of oxidized iron that feed the pale, wire-like lichen in the seams.

***

Farther out across the flats, where the distance between landmarks is measured in hours of deafening travel, live **the Sump-Gaiters**.

These are the largest mobile shapes in the low basin. They are the size of locomotive chassis, built of heavy, cast-iron ribs and thick, tallow-greased ball-joints that have seized and loosened ten thousand times until they move with a heavy, rhythmic *thunk-drag, thunk-drag*. They are entirely blind, but they are not deaf. They do not navigate by sight or scent, but by the acoustic geometry of the basin. 

The sound here—the compound, three-minute pulse of the pneumatic valves and the deep, regional thrum of the subterranean heat-exchangers—is their landscape. A Sump-Gaiter senses the world through massive, saucer-shaped plates of cold-rolled steel mounted where a head might be on a living thing. By pressing these plates directly against the wet deck, they "see" the basin as a map of vibrations. They know where a floorplate has cracked because the tone changes from a deep bell-resonance to a hollow rattle. They know where a cooling tower stands because its massive weight deadens the bass frequencies for half a mile around it.

The Gaiters wander between the dying transformer housings. They are drawn to the dull cherry-red embers that bleed faint warmth into the damp air. When a Gaiter reaches a housing, it folds its heavy, jointed limbs inward and presses its broad, flat thorax against the hot iron casing. It will remain there for days, perfectly motionless, soaking up the heat, while the ceiling-drizzle rains down over it, streaking its dark flanks with bright orange rills of fresh rust. 

Inside their hollow, box-girder bellies, old mechanical logic still operates: tiny, gear-driven pumps, long since stripped of their original housing, endlessly siphon condensation from the deck, strain the grit through internal copper mesh, and blast it out through rear-facing bypass tubes in sudden, whistling jets of vapor. They are the stewards of a system that no longer has a superintendent.

***

In the high, dark ceiling lattice—hundreds of feet above the iron marsh, shrouded in the persistent drizzle—there is a different ecology entirely.

Here, suspended among the structural beams where the air is warmer and thick with the smell of ionized air and sour oil, cling **the Arc-Drinkers**.

They resemble tangled nests of heavy insulated cable, long-decayed copper wire, and multi-jointed hydraulic clamps that have grown together into dense, dark brambles the size of haystacks. They do not touch the ground. They spend their lives crawling along the underside of the ceiling grid, moving so slowly that a human eye could not track their progress over the course of a day.

The Arc-Drinkers live off the green, snapping bursts of the high-voltage insulators. When an insulator arcs, throwing a blinding splash of emerald light across the dripping girders, the nearest Arc-Drinker extends long, articulate rods of laminated mica and copper mesh toward the flash. They catch the stray voltage, storing it in heavy, cylindrical capacitors that form the core of their bodies. 

When their capacitors are full, they sing. It is not a voice, but a sharp, high-frequency hum that cuts through the low, bone-marrow roar of the basin floor below—a thin, musical whining that sounds like a wet finger dragged across the rim of a crystal glass. The sound travels along the steel girders for miles, answered here and there by other nests clinging in the dark, a sparse, invisible network of electrical conversation passing over the silent, flooded world.

***

And beneath them all, down in the subterranean galleries where the tea-colored water flows toward the unseen sumps, live **the Blind Valves**.

They are not creatures that move at all. They are the oldest things in the basin—massive, bronze-ringed apertures six feet wide, set directly into the deck-seams. Over the centuries, the accumulation of concrete silt, rust sludge, and mineral scale has given them a thick, stony shell that resembles fossilized brain-coral.

Yet, deep within their calcified bodies, internal balance-weights still float in the rising condensate. When the water level in a lower gallery reaches a precise, ancient threshold, the valve’s counterweights shift with a deep, echoing *clack*. The heavy bronze disk tilts on its trunnions, and thousands of gallons of dark water go swirling down into the lower darkness with a loud, throaty gulp that shakes the surrounding floorplates.

The other inhabitants know this sound. The Skimmers alter their glide; the Sump-Gaiters turn their broad, steel ears toward the drain; the wire-lichen in the seams sways in the sudden draft created by the rushing water.

***

There is no death here in the biological sense, only **Seizure**. 

Eventually, an inhabitant—a Gaiter whose joints have lost their tallow, or a Skimmer whose plates have worn down to tissue-thin foil—simply stops moving. The chemical rain continues to fall on it. The standing waves continue to ripple against its flanks. The rust creeps over its rivets, softening its hard edges, until the deck-marsh slowly reclaims the iron. 

Within a century, the form is gone, smoothed out by the water and the silt into another river-rounded rise on the endless, low-lying expanse—just another floorplate for the next generation of iron-feeders to glide across, while the engines roar on in the dark forever.
