To know who lives in the Grand Sump, one must first look at the soles of their feet. 

The people here—if they were ever the same stock as those who walk the soil above—have been rewritten by the clay. They are short, thick-set, and low-slung, their torsos longer than their limbs, giving them the center of gravity of a wooden barrel. Their feet are exceptionally broad, with long, splayed toes that have lost their arches entirely. The undersides of their feet are not tough and calloused like leather, but smooth, pale, and rubbery, like the wet pads of a river beast. They do not slip on the green-filmed tiles; they adhere to them with a faint, wet suction as they move.

They call themselves simply the *Keepers*, though they carry no written titles or formal guild-names. There are rarely more than forty of them alive at a given time—the vault’s geometry will not feed a forty-first. 

Their skin is the color of pale tallow rubbed with iron dust, hairless for the most part, and slicked with an ointment they render from beetle-grease and sweet lard. This grease protects them from the perpetual steam and the saltpeter-rot that would otherwise soften a man’s flesh until it slid from his ribs. They wear very little: short kilts of heavy, boiled pigskin or stiff leather aprons held up by shoulder-straps of oiled hemp. They carry no metal weapons, only their short bronze scraping-spatulas, bone needles, and heavy mallets of carved green heartwood.

Because the air is a dense, trapped roar of splash-wheels, winches, and rustling carapaces, the Keepers do not speak in sentences. Language here has condensed into a system of low, resonant chest-grunts and a complex code of striking the brickwork. A Keeper high on the ash-pole gantries does not shout to a worker on the floor; he strikes a mortar joint three times with the wooden handle of his spatula—*thack, thack, thack*—the sound traveling instantly through the continuous ceramic bones of the vault. A short, metallic *ping* on a bronze peg means a drainage trough is backing up; a long, scraped drag of a stone block means a salamander is in heat and must be tended.

Up in the second and third tiers, suspended in the web of ash scaffolding, are their homes. These are not houses, but woven wicker pods, like the nests of trapdoor spiders, lined with dry beetle-husk chaff and swallow-down. In these swinging cradles, the young are born. A Sump-child learns to hang by its fingers from the cypress knees before it ever learns to stand upright. Infants spend their first three years bound softly to their mothers' leather aprons, breathing the sweet, yeasty, vinegar-damp air, lulled by the deafening, rhythmic *clack-clack-clack* of the copper water-paddles. To a child born here, total silence is an unknown terror—it would mean the gutters have dried, the wheels have seized, and the vault is dying.

***

Beside the humans live the *Lard-Sows*—the giant, pale salamanders that inhabit the deep, dark crevices beneath the saltpeter vats. They are as much a part of the architecture as the pillars. Blind, with tiny vestigial nubs where their eyes once were, their skin is a translucent, lard-white jelly through which the dark, slow pulsing of their internal organs can be dimly seen. They are six feet long and heavy as wet sand-bags. 

The Keepers treat them not as livestock, but as thermal stones. The salamanders crawl into the brick recesses where the heat from the outer earth is trapped, absorbing it and radiating it back out into the lower aisles. They are slow, gentle creatures, moving with a ponderous, agonizing grace, their crimson, lacelike external gills fanning the heavy air (*whish-whish, whish-whish*) to extract moisture and gnat-larvae. 

The Keepers feed them by hand, shoving balls of crushed fern-pulp and crushed water-beetle paste into their soft, toothless mouths. In return, the Keepers harvest the thick, viscous mucus the salamanders secrete along their flanks. This "sow-butter" is scooped up with bronze spoons and used to grease the wooden winch-axles, heal skin-cracks on the workers, and seal fresh mortar joints before they dry.

***

In the mid-air space, between the hanging hammocks and the light-shafts, belong the *One-Eyes*. These pale swallows are smaller than their surface cousins, with feathers the color of bleached linen. Generations in the twilight have rendered them asymmetrical: the eye facing the high iron lanterns is blind, clouded over with a thick, pearly cataract from staring into the slanting butter-yellow light; the eye facing down into the brick shadows is enormous, black, and glossy as a wet berry.

They fly in endless, overlapping figure-eights through the solid beams of dusty light, their wings making a dry, papery *fwt-fwt-fwt* against the vault ribs. They are the vault's sanitation officers, snatching steam-gnats and white mortar-mites mid-flight. Their nests—hundreds of them—stipple the ceiling like mud-wasp tubes, built entirely from recycled clay-dust, saliva, and shredded liverwort. Beneath the densest nesting clusters, the Keepers hang small, shallow canvas aprons to catch the bird-droppings, which are immediately scraped into the clay boxes where the garlic and bitter cress grow. Nothing that falls in the Sump is allowed to hit the floor uncounted.

***

On the floor tier, wading through the shallow ceramic troughs, are the *Gutter-Strikers*. These long-legged, flightless fowl have bodies no bigger than a pumpkin, mounted on rubbery, three-jointed legs that end in wide, webbed toes. Their feathers are sparse, stiff, and permanently slick with green algae, giving them the appearance of walking cabbages. 

They are endlessly busy, stepping with high, deliberate strides through the runoff water, their long, blunt beaks stabbing into the damp mortar cracks to pluck out fat water-beetles. They have a strange, symbiotic relationship with the human scrapers: when a Keeper uses his bronze tool to dig out a block of spalling brick, two or three Gutter-Strikers will stand at his knees, clacking their beaks in anticipation, waiting to gobble up the pale, blind centipedes exposed in the dark void behind the ceramic.

***

And then, deep within the dark urns that line the scaffolding, are the unseen millions: the *Oil-Clickers*. These dark, thumb-sized beetles never see the light-shafts. They spend their entire lives tumbling over one another in the hanging terracotta jars, fed on spent mash and dried fern. Their collective motion creates the low, dry, papery roar that fills the mid-tiers—a sound like a bushel of dried peas being poured onto a drumhead forever. Once every three days, a Keeper tilts an urn over a ceramic funnel, collecting the clear, amber oil the beetles exude when pressed. This oil fuels the tiny, floating-wick clay lamps used in the darkest niches beneath the stair-treads, where even the yellow sun-shafts cannot reach.

***

There is no sense of departure here, and no concept of an "outside," save for the three iron grates above, through which light and rainwater fall. When a Keeper grows old and his broad, suction-padded feet finally lose their grip on the wet tiles, his death is not marked by burial—for there is no earth to dig.

Instead, his body is stripped by his kin. His fat is rendered down to join the winch-grease; his long bones are scraped clean and carved into fresh mortar-spatulas, willow-routing needles, and gutter-pegs; his flesh is fed to the lard-salamanders in the warm crevices. Finally, his skull and small bones are taken down to the stone grinding-troughs on the floor, crushed into a fine, pale flour, mixed with slaked lime and brick-dust, and slapped back into the wall where the vault is weakest.

In this way, the description of the place remains entirely literal. The Grand Sump is not merely maintained by its inhabitants; it is literally held aloft, block by block, by the bodies of those who have lived there, their marrow sealed forever into the dark, wet mortar between the red bricks.
