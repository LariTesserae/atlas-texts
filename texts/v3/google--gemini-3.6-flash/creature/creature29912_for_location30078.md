In the seams where the water weeping from the iron-streaked basalt meets the warm, sulfurous breath of the vents, the life of this high world begins in small, heavy shapes.

In the shallow pools that step down the terraces like black mirrors, the dark amphibians move with a strange, deliberate weight. They are not light or supple like lowland frogs, nor smooth like river salamanders. Their skins are thick, tough, and pebbled like fine-grained leather, stained the color of wet slate, with belly-plates yellowed by sulfur and iron. Because the water here is thick with dissolved copper and magnetite, their skeletons have adapted to be unusually dense—heavy, iron-rich bone that acts as ballast against the cold currents spilling over the ledges. 

They do not swim so much as crawl along the pool beds, their short, thick-jointed legs tipped with blunt, keratinous claws that can find purchase on glass-smooth basalt. They breathe slowly, their lungs adapted to air that is thin and wet with mineral vapors. When the static hum along the copper veins sharpens before a gale, their skin twitches with tiny, involuntary spasms, and they sink deeper into the silty, rust-red depressions at the bottom of the pools, holding their breath for hours while ice forms a thin, crackling lattice above them.

Their prey gathers where the steam hits the freezing air. Clouds of small, thick-bodied midges—dark, wingless or stub-winged, with carapaces like tiny polished beads of hematite—crawl in dense masses over the liverworts that carpet the vent edges. These insects do not fly; the gales off the abysses would sweep them into the void in seconds. Instead, they cling. Their legs are equipped with microscopic, hooked spines that grip the damp lichen like burs. They feed on the bacterial mats that thrive in the warm, acidic runoff, their dark bodies absorbing the faint heat of the earth until they resemble small, crawling embers against the wet black stone.

Higher up, on the broad, tilted plateaus where the ice rain freezes as it hits, the ungulates move through the fog in small, silent bands. 

They are squat, low-slung animals, built close to the center of gravity, with backs as broad as boulder tops and necks short and thick enough to withstand the relentless drag of the wind. Their coats are extraordinary structures: two distinct layers of hollow, dense hair that trap air so efficiently that snow falling on their spines never melts. The outer guard hairs are long, pale silver, and stiff with secreted wax; as the wet mist settles on them, it turns to tiny beads of ice that do not penetrate to the skin, but form an armor of frost that tinkles faintly like broken glass as they step across the scree.

Their hooves are the true marvel of the plateau. They are split wide, broad as dinner plates, with a sharp, chisel-like outer rim of jet-black horn that can bite into frozen shale or notch into the narrowest lip of raw basalt. Inside this hard ring sits a deep, soft pad—spongy, textured like heavy rubber, and warm with a high concentration of blood vessels—that grips wet, metallic rock like a suction cup. 

These grazers eat what seems impossible to digest. They crop the tough, leathery black lichens, scraping them off the rock with chisel-shaped lower incisors, and swallow mouthfuls of the fine, iron-heavy shale dust that collects in the crevices. Their bellies are vast, slow-burning furnaces, churning with specialized gut flora that can break down the dense cellulose of alpine sedge and extract trace minerals directly from the powdered stone. They rarely drink from the open pools; instead, they scoop up handfuls of the cold, mineralized slush that settles in the lees of the large boulders.

They are deeply attuned to the mechanical rhythm of the mountain. When the deep frost-shocks strike—when a thousand-ton block of shale shears miles beneath their feet with that sudden, subterranean *thump*—they do not bolt. They simply drop to their knees, lowering their heavy bellies to the stone, anchoring themselves with their wide hooves until the shockwaves pass through the rock beneath them.

Where the plateaus break off into vertical emptiness, the world belongs to the cliff-dwellers.

In the sheer, razor-cornered chimneys and deep fracture lines that drop two thousand feet into the mist, the seabirds nest in vast, stacked cities. They are heavy, long-bodied birds, colored the slate-gray of the sky above and the oily black of the basalt below. Their feathers are so densely packed and coated in thick, musky tallow that water does not merely bead on them; it rebounds off them in droplets. 

They do not take off like birds of the lowland plains. To fly, they simply step off the narrow shelves into the abyss, dropping like stones for a hundred feet through the fog until the tremendous updrafts—forced upward by the sheer black walls—catch their narrow, rigid wings. They lean into the gale with a harsh, heavy grace, riding the thermal currents created by the steam vents below and the cold winds above.

Their nests are not made of twigs or dry grass, for none exists here. Instead, they are built of regurgitated fish bone, dried algae scraped from the lower tarns, and their own dense down, all bound together with a paste of mineral-rich mud and guano that dries in the biting air until it is as hard as the surrounding schist. The noise of these colonies is muted by the cold air; their calls are not bright or shrill, but deep, rattling croaks that sound uncannily like stone sliding over stone, swallowed almost immediately by the vast, damp emptiness.

Above them all, where the highest rock needles pierce the flat slate sky, swing the apex scavengers and hunters. 

They are vast, broad-winged birds of prey with ragged, pale-tipped primary feathers and eyes the color of wet iron. They rarely flap their wings; they hang on the freezing air for hours without a quiver, watching the fog-gaps below. They do not hunt by sight alone. They have learned to watch the movement of the herds on the plateaus, tracking the silver shapes through the dim light, waiting for an ice-slip on a narrow terrace or a sudden rockfall triggered by the mountain’s internal hammers. 

When a carcass is found—crushed beneath a freshly sheared slab of shale or fallen into a chimney—it draws not only the raptors, but small, long-limbed scavengers that slink through the scree fields. These ground-beasts are lean, thick-pawed, with pale, coarse pelts and short, powerful jaws capable of snapping frozen bone. They are cautious, quiet creatures that spend their days curled in the deep, warm pockets near the thermal vents, emerging only when the scent of blood drifts through the cold dampness.

Nothing here lives easily, but everything lives with an absolute, unyielding belonging. The animals do not fight the stone or the cold; they are made of the same heavy elements. Their bones hold the mountain’s iron, their coats carry its static, their lungs breathe its steam, and their silence matches the vast, unfinished silence of the rock itself.
