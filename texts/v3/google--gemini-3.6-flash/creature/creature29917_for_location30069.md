To understand what lives in the High Sieve, one must first discard the distinction between the creature and the ground it moves upon. Here, life does not merely inhabit the continent of heartwood; it is an extension of its circulation, an organ of its slow digestion.

At the lowest tier of movement are the **Marrow-Ploughs**—the huge, legless grazers whose hides match the wet, fibrous grain of cedar. They possess no eyes, for light here is only an ambient, bronzed haze, but their snouts are broad, rubbery shovels of cartilage lined with rows of dull, chitinous rasping plates. They do not eat plants in the conventional sense; they eat time. They scrape away centuries of compacted liverwort, dead shelf-fungi, and softened cork, chewing through the upper skin of the plateau and leaving behind smooth, mile-long troughs. 

These furrows are vital. Within hours of a Marrow-Plough’s passing, the surrounding peat-slurry slumps into the groove, and warm, tea-colored water fills the trench. What was a flat expanse of yellow moss becomes a sluggish, slow-weeping river. The Plough itself is an ecosystem: along its wet flank grow colonies of tiny, pale-green barnacles that filter resin from the air, and in the deep folds of its tail-joint, blind glass-shrimp breed in small, portable pools of warm sweat.

Above them, in the suspended gloom between the giant vine-sacs, drift the **Lung-Moths**. With wingspans as wide as river-barges, they do not flap; they expand and contract their rib-ribbed thoraxes like sea-jellies, pumping the heavy, mist-laden air through vast, internal chambers. Their wings are thin as oiled parchment and veined like decaying leaves, capturing the constant condensation until they droop with weight. When a Lung-Moth grows too heavy with water, it alights upon a petrified stump, folds its vast lace sails, and simply *weeps*—draining the trapped moisture from its wing-veins through tiny, valved pores into the saucer-pools below. A single moth may remain anchored to the same ridge for thirty years, its amber eyes slowly dulling as lichens anchor to its legs, indistinguishable from an outgrowth of bark until it exhales.

Deep within the fifty-mile depth of the substrate—where light never penetrates and the wood has compressed into a dark, warm, resinous stone—lives a phantom fauna that knows nothing of the air. In the hollow vascular channels of ancient, buried trunks, **Pith-Eels** swim. They are translucent, rubbery ribbons up to twenty paces long, completely devoid of pigment or bone. They feed on the thick, sweet sap that still seeps from fossilized heart-valves, propelled through the black, fluid-filled pipes by cilia that line their skin. Their movements are what generate the low, pulsing *thrum* felt in the soles of anyone walking the surface above—a continuous, biological hydraulic rhythm that keeps the plateau’s deep moisture from stagnating.

And then there are those who carved the basins and wove the root-discs. 

They are called the **Knot-Keepers**, though they have no name for themselves, having forgotten the need for speech long before the sharp edges of the wood-rock rounded off.

They are long, supple creatures, standing twice the height of a man when upright, though they rarely stand. Their bodies are built for sprawling and sliding across yielding ground: four long, jointed limbs terminating not in claws, but in clusters of fine, muscular tendrils that can feel the subterranean pulse of the sap. Their skin is the color of peeled birch, smooth and rubbery, kept perpetually slick by a thin sheen of clear, resinous oil that smells of crushed pine needles and damp earth.

The Keepers do not build homes, for the warmth is constant and the mist is gentle. Instead, they recline. They spend decades settled in the shallow depressions of the high terraces, their lower limbs intertwined with the fibrous substrate, slowly sinking into the liverwort carpet until only their broad, lidless eyes and nostrils protrude above the pale yellow green. While they recline, their bodies exchange fluids with the heartwood—drawing up water rich in tannins through their foot-tendrils, and exuding a mild, acidic oil from their bellies that gradually dissolves the wood-rock beneath them.

This is how the long, shallow steps and basin-caves were made. They were not cut with chisels; they were *dissolved* by generations of Keepers sitting in solemn, motionless communion with the same ledge of petrified oak. A step five paces wide represents the resting place of a single matriarch across four centuries.

The calcified reed-spires that dot the landscape are their only deliberate constructions, built not for shelter, but as respiratory organ-pipes for the world below. Working over lifetimes, the Keepers gather the lime-rich secretions of deep-dwelling wood-sponges and paste them around vertical air-shafts that lead down into the suffocating, peat-stifled lower layers. As the upper canopy swells and contracts with the day-to-night temperature drift, air is drawn through these spires, producing a low, resonant whistle that carries across miles of mist. 

It is a mournful, beautiful sound—a single, unending chord in a minor key that vibrates through the liverwort plains. When a Keeper hears a spire whistle, it presses its broad forehead against the nearest shelf-fungus to receive the tone as a physical sensation in its skull, knowing by the pitch and warmth of the vibration which region of the High Sieve is breathing, which is rotting, and which is growing anew.

They do not gather food. They do not hunt the Lung-Moths or butcher the Marrow-Ploughs. When a Keeper is hungry, it crawls slowly to the lip of a tannin pool, submerged to its throat, and laps at the amber fluid with a long, feathery tongue that filters microscopic fungal spores and dissolved sugar from the water. 

Birth among them is so rare and quiet it leaves no trace. A young Keeper is born no larger than an acorn, soft and clear as jelly, and is placed gently into a water-filled vine-sac high in the canopy. There it floats for a hundred years, nourished by the sweet water of the sac, watching the pale smudge of the sun drift through the bronze fog, until its skin darkens to birch-white and it becomes heavy enough to drop softly into the moss below.

Death, too, is a reabsorption. When an old Keeper’s heart begins to slow—after a thousand years of watching the water weep across the cork-cliffs—it does not lie down in a grave. It simply stops moving where it sits. Its body softens, its skin breaks down into a pale, milky sap that feeds the liverworts around its knees, and within three seasons, the moss has closed over its head, leaving only a gentle, rounded swell on the terrace floor—another hummock in a world made entirely of what came before.
