To live here, a body must match the stone: unrounded, dense, and indifferent to the cold.

The people who walk these terraced flights are few, but they are not remnants of a larger race, nor are they survivors of a decline. They are a first population. Their skin has the matte, pale-grey cast of basalt under mist, thick-soled and uncalloused in the human sense, but tough like cured hide. They walk barefoot across the razor-edged scree and wet slate without bleeding; the bottoms of their feet are wide, flat, and slightly damp, gripping the slick incline with the effortless traction of a climbing snail.

They are tall and long-limbed, moving with an economy of motion that makes them seem slow until you try to follow them up a four-story flight of stone steps. They do not shiver. Their core temperature runs low, and their breath, though it fogs in the sharp night air, smells faint and clean, like wet iron and broken cedar.

Because the acoustics of the basin are overwhelming—the ceaseless drone of dripping water, the whistling wind-pipes of the upper tiers, the thunder below the drainage grates—their speech has evolved away from sharp consonants and high vowels. They do not shout. When they speak to one another across a wide terrace or down the shaft of an aqueduct, they produce a low, sub-vocal rumble in the throat, a sound that pitches beneath the roar of the water and carries directly through the stone itself. Two workers thirty yards apart, pinning a green-wood beam to the cliffside, communicate through vibrations felt in the timber and the soles of their feet.

Their clothing is functional and sparse, made from the fibrous inner bark of high-altitude conifers, beaten flat and oiled with tallow harvested from the fat of deep-canyon eels. It does not hold water; the continuous spray of the basin beads on their shoulders and runs off in fine, silver threads. Around their wrists and ankles they wear cold-hammered copper bands—not for decoration, but to bleed off the intense static charge that builds in the dry, sharp air above the cliff-edge when the winds turn.

In the vast, empty halls, where a human would feel crushed by the silence and the height of the ceilings, these people find their rest. They do not use soft beds. They sleep leaning against the wet interior walls, where the thermal mass of the mountain stays constant, or curled on heavy cedar benches that have been rubbed smooth by skin oils over decades. A chamber built to hold three hundred houses only five or six. They move between these grand vaults with a quiet, casual ownership, like solitude is not a lack of people, but a physical material to be preserved.

Beside them, the basin supports a sparse, highly specialized web of life that has adapted to the sharp-edged, un-eroded world:

*   **The Slate-Hawks:** Larger than a man’s chest, these birds have no down beneath their feathers; their plumage is composed of flat, interlocking scales of dark keratine that shed the spray completely. They nest in the deep window apertures of the highest, unoccupied tiers. They are almost entirely silent, dropping from balconies into the wind-currents without a wing-beat, hunting the subterranean slots for blind eels or snatching the glass-crayfish from the log flumes with broad, pale talons.

*   **The Stream-Lizards:** In the moss carpets that pad the joins of the monoliths, tiny, lungless salamanders—no longer than a finger—live in vast numbers. Their bodies are completely clear, showing the faint pink trace of their organs beneath glass-like skin. They breathe entirely through their damp hides, feeding on the microscopic midges that hatch in the copper-tinted pools of the aqueducts. When the sun hits the terraced walls at noon, the moss-seams glisten as thousands of these small, translucent bodies shift together toward the light.

*   **The Lichen-Grazers:** Beneath the lower terraces, where the stone gives way to the dark, roaring slot canyon, live small, heavy-bodied creatures the size of badgers, with flattened skulls and wide, shovel-like claws. Their fur is stiff and oily, colored like wet granite. They climb the lower courses of the buildings at night, using their stout claws to scrape away the black-green liverwort and silver moss, keeping the drainage channels clear and the mortar-less joints of the stone blocks bare. The inhabitants do not hunt them; they treat them with a distant, functional tolerance, occasionally tossing them the scrapings from the timber-mills.

*   **The Timber-Moths:** In the damp, dark spaces where green-wood timber meets the rock face, large, velvety moths with pale grey wings lay their eggs. Their larvae do not eat the wood—the cedar is too saturated with resin—but feed exclusively on the fine, iron-rich dust generated by the workers when they hammer stone. The moths’ eyes glow with a faint, cold phosphorescence in the dark vaults, flickering like tiny candles along the upper beams.

There is no childhood as we understand it here, nor rapid aging. Reproductive cycles are tied to long, subtle changes in the mountain’s water output; children are born rarely, perhaps once every few years across the entire settlement, and they are born broad-shouldered and quiet, stepping into the wet air without crying. They learn to work the stone before they learn to speak, handling tiny copper chisels and learning the grain of the basalt by touch.

Nothing in this place suggests an empire that fell, nor one that is rising to conquer. The people are engaged in a perpetual, quiet maintenance of the *present*. They build another tier not because they need the room, but because the stone is there, the water flows, and the act of setting a true, straight block into the moss-lined notch of the mountain is the natural outcome of having hands, time, and cold, clean water. They are the living edge of the unweathered world, as sharp, quiet, and durable as the granite beneath their feet.
