To live here is to abandon the third dimension. 

In a world crushed into verticality, breadth is impossible. Everything that exists between the slate blades is flattened to a fraction of a millimeter, evolved to inhabit a realm made entirely of length, height, and shear surface area. There are no rounded bellies here, no spherical eggs, no limbs that extend outward. Existence is a sheet of paper sliding between two grinding millstones.

Deep within the narrowest slits, where the blinding glare of the high sun is refracted into a cool, silver luminescence, the rock is not merely coated in water—it is coated in *tissue*.

The coin-thin organisms mentioned in passing are not isolated individuals; they are the nodes of a vast, plate-wide web known only to the geometry of the basin. Call them *Laminates*. They are composed of flexible, translucent silica-chitin, so sheer that the grain of the quartz beneath them is perfectly visible through their bodies. They do not possess eyes, lungs, or digestive tracts. Instead, their entire undersides are carpets of microscopic, tubular cilia that lock directly into the microscopic pore-space of the slate. 

The Laminates do not eat in any traditional sense. They are biological piezoelectric converters. The ceaseless, roaring vibration of the plateau—the high-frequency scream and the deep sub-bass hum—passes through the quartz plates and into their mineralized bodies. This kinetic agitation, combined with the chemical bath of iron and sulfur weeping up from the deep crust, generates a faint, continuous electrical current across their membranes. They feed on the roar. The louder the wind, the faster the subterranean water creeps, the more intensely their translucent organs pulse with a pale, cold bioluminescence. 

Where several Laminates overlap, they fuse. Across miles of the plateau, beneath the surface skin of the water, a single, brokenly continuous network of living film stretches through millions of parallel rock slits. It is an animal that is five miles wide, three hundred feet high, and less than a hundredth of an inch thick. 

Moving across this living skin are the scavengers of the grid: the *Chladni mites*. 

Blind, colorless, and hard as carved topaz, these needle-legged creatures are no larger than a grain of coarse salt. They do not crawl at random. Because the acoustic force of the plateau is so intense, it creates stationary wave patterns—Chladni figures—in the thin film of water sliding over the rock. The fluid collects in geometric grids of perfect stillness, surrounded by zones of violent, microscopic turbulence. The mites walk exclusively along these invisible lines of silence. To step off a node of stillness is to be instantly shaken to pieces by the ultrasonic frequency of the slate. 

Their needle-like legs do not end in claws, but in microscopic tuning forks made of dense iron-protein. As they scuttle along the quiet lines, they feel for subtle dampening in the stone's vibration—the dead zone left by a Laminate that has exhausted its mineral node and crystallized into unmoving slag. When a mite finds a dead spot, it uses its spade-shaped mandibles to scrape the petrified chitin from the quartz, clearing the stone face so the weeping water can flush the surface clean for the next layer of film to anchor.

Higher up, where the light hits the rock with laser-like intensity, a different kind of life claims the stone. 

Here, in the bright upper inches of the blades, live the *Prism-Ribbons*. They are long, flexible strands of clear, segmented glass-chitin, no thicker than a human hair, but stretching for yards along the lip of a single slate plate. They are anchored at one end by a deep mineral taproot driven into a fissure. The rest of their bodies float freely in the upward-oozing water skin.

The Prism-Ribbons do not avoid the blinding sunlight; they harvest its geometry. Their segmented bodies are grown in perfect, triangular cross-sections that act as living prisms. As the harsh, white rays bounce back and forth between the wet rocks, the Ribbons catch the light and split it, bending the white glare into tight, concentrated bands of ultraviolet and infrared. They direct these focused beams downward, into the dark crevices below, beaming energy to the Laminates in exchange for the heavy sulfur compounds the film-dwellers pump upward through their cellular network. 

The air above the plates seems empty, but it is not. It is populated by the only things that can survive the acoustic wall: the *Sliver-Siphons*.

They are the apex of this vertical world, though they look less like animals and more like discarded ribbons of silver foil. A Sliver-Siphon may be twelve feet long and six feet tall, but it is so impossibly thin that when turned edge-on to an observer, it vanishes entirely into the air. They do not fly by flapping; they possess no muscles for such movement. Instead, they are lifted and suspended by the acoustic pressure itself. The high-frequency scream rising from the millions of mineral slots creates a dense cushion of vibrating air—an ultrasonic updraft—upon which the Siphons float like autumn leaves resting on a jet of water.

Their bodies are perforated by thousands of microscopic, valved pores. As the wind is forced through their thin, sail-like forms, the Siphons extract airborne mineral dust, salt crystals, and the microscopic reproductive spores shed by the Laminates below. They drift horizontally across the bristling hide of the plateau, passing through the stone forest like ghost-ships, their trailing, thread-like feelers dipping into the wet slots between the blades to trade chemicals with the stone-dwellers.

Death in this place carries no decay. There are no bacteria to break tissue down, no moisture variations to encourage rot. When a creature dies on the mountain plain, the weeping water immediately begins to replace its organic silica with dissolved iron and quartz. 

Over centuries, the dead are not buried; they become the stone. The razor-thin slate blades that crowd the high basin are built, layer upon microscopic layer, from the petrified skins of eons of Laminates, crushed together by tectonic force and cemented by the mineralized bodies of the mites that walked them. The mountain plain grows upward, millimeter by millimeter, propelled by the very life that clings to its sides.

Whoever or whatever lives here does not merely inhabit the terrain. They are its outer skin, its organ of hearing, its optical focus, and ultimately, its future bedrock. The creature is the mountain; the mountain is the creature; and between them, the scream never stops.
