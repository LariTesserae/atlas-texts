To an outside observer standing on a high rampart, the city appears utterly vacant, its vastness abandoned to the sun and the cold weep of the stone. But life here has not disappeared; it has simply compressed, flattening itself against the rock until its margins match the seams of the slate.

The primary inhabitants are beings that have grown out of the city’s logic, so perfectly aligned with its geometry and climate that they seem less like visitors and more like a mobile phase of the architecture itself. 

They are long-limbed and thin-chested, built for a world where air is scarce and spaces are narrow. Their skin is smooth, hairless, and oily, ranging in tone from the bluish-gray of wet slate to the pale, bruised violet of deep ice. Because they have no timber for fire and no metal for tools, they wear very little—only tight, wrapped aprons and sleeves fashioned from the fibrous, felted mosses harvested from the lower catchments, cured in mineral salts until they are waterproof and tough as hide.

They move through the arm-width fissures between the buildings not by turning sideways, but with a fluid, chimneying motion, their shoulders angled, their bare feet gripping the micro-textures of the unmortared stone. They are nearly soundless. The soles of their feet and the palms of their broad, spatulate hands are thick and soft, damp enough to stick slightly to the rock, dampening the scrape of a step before it can start.

In a realm where the thin air swallows speech and wet slate deadens echoes, they rarely use their mouths to talk. Their vocal cords are vestigial, capable only of a low, rhythmic chest-hum that sounds remarkably like the murmur of water passing through a narrow pipe. Instead, their language is physical and acoustic in a different way: they communicate by tapping and pressing against the stone. A sharp striking of a knuckle on a key-slate can carry a mile through the dense, continuous bone of the mountain, telling those three terraces down that a upper conduit is choked with silt, or that the morning frost is lingering late in the northern shadow.

Their biology is bound completely to the water cycle. They do not drink in long gulps; rather, their skin is highly absorbent, drawing in the thin, trembling sheets of moisture that coat the walls. During the heat of midday, when the sun is sharpest and the slate burns with dry, brilliant light, they retreat into the deep, multi-tiered cellars carved five levels into the plateau’s mantle. There, in the profound dark where the water pools in absolute still, their body temperatures drop, their breathing slows to three or four deep, chest-expanding inhalations a minute, and they wait out the glare.

At dawn and dusk, when the vapor rises in pale ribbons from the cisterns, they emerge. They are gardeners of a austere sort. Armed with scrapers made from the dense leg bones of high-altitude birds or hard granite flakes, they tend the black and copper lichens that paint the north facades. They scrape the growth into slate bowls, mash it with cold water and tiny, preserved amphipods harvested from the deep wells, and consume it raw. It is a bitter, mineral diet, heavy with iron and sulfur, but it sustains their dense, slow-twitch muscle and thick, antifreeze-rich blood.

They do not build new structures—the city was completed before their oldest memories—but they are obsessive maintainers. A single family might spend three generations caring for a hundred-yard stretch of aqueduct, clearing tiny pebbles carried down by the snowmelt, re-seating slates that have shifted under the frost-heave, and polishing the stone gutters with sand to keep the water moving without splash or turbulence. To them, a dry terrace is a disaster; a blocked channel is a break in the breathing of the world.

Beside these people, the city holds a few other quiet lives:

In the deepest, lightless cisterns—where the water is so cold it feels thick—lives the *silt-salamander*. Pale, nearly translucent, and growing up to four feet long, these creatures have no eyes, only sensitive, frilled gills that fan the dark water. They anchor themselves to the dry-stacked joints with short, clawed limbs, waiting for the microscopic detritus and dead amphipods to drift down from the upper city. They move so rarely that lichens sometimes take root on their backs, turning them into living, undulating stones.

High in the corbeled arches, where the stone stays dry and smells of wet ash, small colonies of *frost-moths* cling to the ceilings. Their wings are thin, stiff plates of gray chitin that look like flakes of shale. They remain torpid for weeks, waking only when the wind drops completely and the air fills with the faint, warm mist of the midday thaw. Then, they flutter heavily down to the water channels, sip the mineral condensate, mate in absolute silence, and lay their eggs in the mossy seams between the pavement stones before freezing solid again when night falls.

And above it all, keeping a wide, wary distance from the slate-dwellers, are the ash-winged gulls—not birds of the sea, but travelers of the continental sky. They do not nest in the city itself, for the stone is too slick and cold for their eggs, but they use the plateau as a landmark and a rest-stop. They land on the highest stone ramparts, their gray feathers matching the granite so precisely they seem to dissolve into the horizon, resting their wings while they watch the thin, gray figures far below moving through the water-bright streets, shifting stones so quietly that even the wind barely notices they are there.
