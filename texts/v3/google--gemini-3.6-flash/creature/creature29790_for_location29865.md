They are called the Riveters, or the Plumb-Liners, though among themselves they have no name that needs to be spoken above the sound of the steam vents.

To see one out on the high gantries is rare, and when you do, the eye at first mistakes them for part of the machinery—a heavy crane-block wrapped in tarpaulin, or a bundle of wet grease-rags left hanging from an iron stay. But then the bundle shifts, moving with a silent, double-jointed momentum that belongs only to living bone.

They are tall, though they rarely stand at full height, preferring a low, triple-bent crouch that keeps their center of mass near the brass handrails. Their limbs are unnaturally long, the forearms and shins elongated as if stretched by generations of reaching across abysses. Their flesh is the color of wet slate, hairless and thick, with a texture like cured pigskin that has been rubbed with tallow. It does not bleed easily when cut by the razor-edges of the young granite; instead, the skin forms hard, dark calluses that smell faintly of machine oil and wet zinc.

Because gravity here leans like a wind, their inner ears have evolved a strange, fluid complexity. A Riveter does not walk *up* or *down*; they walk *against* the local pull. You can watch a woman carry a copper bucket along a sheer vertical cliff face, her boots—shod in iron plates that snap into the grid-iron catwalks—clacking rhythmic patterns as she leans forty-five degrees into empty air, completely untethered from the horizontal.

Their clothing is heavy, functional, and permanently sodden. They wear high-collared coats made of dense, greased wool, stiff with mineral salt and coal-soot, fastened with thick brass toggles that do not rust. Over these, they strap harness-systems of dark, oiled leather, heavy with lead weights, plumb-bobs, turnbuckle keys, and short, broad-headed hammers.

They live inside the iron wasp-nests—the suspended tenements.

Step through one of the heavy, spring-loaded airlocks into a tenement interior, and the deafening clatter of the abyss drops away into a rich, warm, liquid quiet. The air inside smells of boiled cabbage, damp leather, fern-head tea, and the sweet, dense grease used to pack the hydraulic valves. The walls are corrugated zinc, lined with thick pads of dried emerald moss that absorb both the cold and the constant, bone-deep vibration of the cliffside pumps.

Here, in the dim glow of tallow lamps and low-voltage filament bulbs, their true domesticity emerges. They are not harsh creatures, despite their jagged world. They live in dense, quiet family groups, sharing long, curved benches built directly into the iron ribs of the buildings.

Their diet is drawn entirely from the vertical ecosystem. They cultivate the rubbery ferns in dark, humid basements warmed by steam-bypass pipes, harvesting the bitter, starch-heavy tubers. They catch the sky-rays using long, barbed brass harpoons fired from the lower balconies, rendering down the creatures' oily skin for fuel and salting the dark, stringy meat in oak barrels. The blue glass-spiders are gathered for their silk, which the women weave into fine, waterproof undergarments that never rot, even in the perpetual dampness.

Communication among them is peculiar, dictated entirely by the world’s clock.

Because the massive hydraulic pumps strike their thunderous *THUMP* every twelve seconds, the Riveters have built their language around the rhythm. They speak in short, dense bursts of low-frequency clicks and chest-resonated vowels during the eleven seconds of silence, then fall entirely still during the beat itself. To an outsider, a conversation sounds like a series of rapid, clicking poems punctuated by synchronized pauses, as if they are all listening for something in the heart of the stone.

Across long distances—from one hanging tower to a gasometer half a mile away—they do not shout. Instead, they use long, thin rods of tempered brass. By pressing the end of a rod against an iron cable and striking it with a small hammer, they send high-pitched vibrations ringing through the suspension lines. A child sitting in a bay window three chasms away can lay his forehead against the iron frame and read the vibrations like speech: *Trolley incoming. Heavy mist at the seventh needle. Bring the tallow.*

Perhaps most strange is their relationship with the world’s untethered reality.

Where the rock grows in real-time, the Riveters are there to manage it. They are not afraid of the cold crystals pushing up through their floors; they treat the emerging basalt as a butcher treats a carcass. They use heavy, hand-cranked diamond saws and steam-heated wedges to "trim" the mountains as they burst from the mist, carving the fresh stone into anchor-points before it has even fully set.

They are deeply aware of their shadows. Because a Riveter’s shadow often lags three or four seconds behind their physical body—or points toward an unseen, future light source—they use their shadows as navigational tools. A veteran cable-walker will not step onto a high catwalk if his shadow refuses to follow him; it means the space ahead is currently "stretching," and a ten-foot leap might suddenly become a sixty-foot void mid-stride. They wait patiently on the iron platforms, smoking short clay pipes filled with dried moss, until their shadows catch up and snap back into alignment with their heels.

They do not seem to have a religion, at least not one involving gods or heavens. Instead, they have a profound, technical reverence for *Tension*.

Once a year, during the period when the pale sun-ring reaches its highest point in the gray atmosphere, whole tenement blocks gather on the suspension bridges. In complete silence, save for the 12-second pulse of the pumps, they lay their palms against the massive, tree-thick iron cables that hold their homes above the cloud-sea. They feel the hum of the wire, the pull of the growing mountains, the weight of the rain, and the resistance of the iron.

If the cable rings true, they nod to one another, pull their grease-soot coats tight against the biting cold, and go back inside to turn the spit over the tallow fire, content in a world that is still being hammered into shape around them.
