At the lowest level, where the grey zinc silt shifts like dry ash, the ground is alive with things no larger than a grain of coarse grit or a carriage bolt. 

These are the spark-tickers and the relay-fleas. They have no organs, no fluid, no soft tissue to dry out under the silver sky. Their bodies are stamped from leaves of brittle brass and cold-rolled steel, pinched at the joints by tiny, coiled tension springs. They move not by muscle, but by the accumulation and violent release of static electricity. As they drag their sharp, needle-like tarsi through the iron dust, they build up immense electrostatic charges against the dry stone. When the potential becomes too great, a tiny blue arc jumps across a gap in their bellies, snapping a leaf-spring shut and hurling them six feet through the shimmering heat. Millions of them hit the rocks at once, a sound like hail falling on a sheet-metal roof. They feed on the abrasion itself—their bellies are simple, hollow grinding chambers that crush zinc dust into finer and finer particles, stripping the static charge from the friction and discarding the dead, exhausted powder behind them in long, sparkling wakes.

Among these swarms crawl the mid-tier grazers: the boiler-backed quadrupeds and the cast-iron millipedes. 

Consider the *bellows-beast*, a construct the size of a furnace tender, supported on four stubby, ratchet-jointed legs of pitted pig iron. It has no head, no eyes, and no mouth in the organic sense. Instead, its forward bulkhead is a wide, open scoop lined with interlocking teeth of hardened manganese steel. As it plows through the shattered shale, its heavy legs drive a massive internal crankshaft that works a pair of heavy, leather-textured steel bellows in its chest. The bellows do not breathe air; they force dry silt through a series of internal cyclone separators, sifting the iron-rich dust from the barren silica. The iron is crushed between internal flywheels that spin endlessly on dry, diamond-hard carbite bearings, generating intense friction heat that keeps the beast’s metallic core at a low, dull red glow. 

The bellows-beast is a creature of pure thermodynamics. It lives entirely off the temperature difference between its glowing, heat-soaked undercarriage—pressed close to the baking basalt—and the bitter, vacuum-thin chill that sweeps over its corrugated iron spine. Heat rises through its ribbed cooling jackets, driving tiny, high-pressure thermal pumps that flex its leg-linkages in a slow, rhythmic, pile-driver stride. It has no nervous system, only a web of bi-metallic strips that bend as they heat and cool, steering the creature toward the hottest thermal plumes rising from the canyon cracks.

Higher up, where the air shears against the parabolic stone spires, live the aerials. 

They do not flap. Nothing here has the energy to spare for flapping. The *mica-gliders* possess wingspans twenty feet across, made of translucent sheets of naturally occurring sheet-mica pinned to a frame of thin, lattice-worked steel tubing. They are absurdly light, weighing no more than a few pounds despite their metallic bones. They spend days caught in the permanent, soaring updrafts generated by the plateau’s blistering surface, circling in vast, silent rings beneath the furnace-door sun. 

Their eyes are multi-faceted lenses carved by wind-driven dust from clear quartz crystals embedded in their snouts. These crystal eyes do not process images; they refract the intense sunlight into precise, needle-sharp beams that strike internal banks of light-sensitive crystals. When a glider passes over a dead or seized boiler-beast below, the sudden shift in thermal radiation alters the refraction pattern inside its head. The bi-metallic steering wires snap taut with a loud *clack*, its mica wings tilt, and it drops out of the sky like a thrown chisel.

Death on the plateau is not a matter of decay, but of *seizure*. 

When a creature’s bearings score, when its dry graphite lubricant burns away, or when its internal flywheels expand too far in the heat and lock against their housings, it stops. The sudden halt is violent; the immense kinetic energy stored in its moving parts has nowhere to go. A locked gear tears through a carapace plate; a pressurized piston snaps its housing and vents a cloud of red-hot iron dust into the air. 

Within minutes, the scavengers arrive.

They do not come for meat, but for momentum and geometry. The *chisel-worms*—long, flexible chains of hardened steel links that slither through the silt like saw blades—wrap themselves around the seized limbs of the dying behemoth. Using the kinetic energy of their own sliding segments, they grind into the structural pins, shearing the dead monster apart into manageable plates and rods. 

Behind them come the *weld-wasps*, tiny, spindle-legged constructs that move with a terrifying, jerky speed. They carry no venom, but their abdomens are tipped with heavy carbon rods that draw power directly from the massive static potential of the dust clouds. They leap onto the exposed innards of the dead beast, striking their carbon tips against the raw metal to draw bright, blinding electric arcs. In a shower of white-hot sparks, they fuse loose scrap onto their own carapaces, expanding their armored shells or welding new, scavenged gear-segments into their own primitive drive-trains. 

Look closely at a weld-wasp, and you will see that it is not a single, factory-made unit, but an absurd, centuries-old collage of stolen parts: a clockwork escapement welded to a pneumatic valve, driving four needle-sharp legs taken from three different generations of dead arachnids. They do not give birth; they grow by accretion, snapping off over-heavy limbs when they become too encumbered, leaving those severed, twitching limbs to wander off on their own simple, static-driven instincts as new entities.

And then there are the things that inhabit the warped physics of the plateau’s interior.

In the deep, sun-bleached basins where light shears into parallel ribbons, live the *shadow-shapers*. They are perhaps the most unsettling of all the plateau’s inhabitants, because they have no solid volume. They are thin, flat lattices of razor-sharp spring steel, no thicker than a coin, that press themselves flat against the ironstone. They do not walk; they flex, rippling across the surface like oil on water. 

The shadow-shapers do not cast shadows of their own. Instead, they inhabit the misaligned shadows cast by the larger tripod-walkers. As a massive, pile-driving walker moves across the plain, its long, distorted shadow slides over the rock. The shadow-shaper clings to the interior of that darkness, feeding on the sharp temperature drop between the sunlit rock and the shaded stone. If the shadow moves too fast, the shaper is exposed to the full, unmediated glare of the silver sky; the instant sunlight hits its flat chassis, the thermal shock causes its spring-steel body to curl up with a loud, metallic *ping*, snapping itself into a useless, twisted ring of dead metal.

Farther out, near the horizon where distance refuses to hold its shape, dwell the *resonators*. 

They are the mega-fauna of the deep plateau—entities so large they are often mistaken for natural formations of wind-carved basalt or ancient turbine housings. A resonator may be sixty feet high, a hollow, ribbed cone of cast iron half-buried in the zinc silt, facing the prevailing thermal winds. It has no limbs, no engine, no moving parts at all save for a series of massive, suspended brass reeds hanging inside its hollow interior. 

As the howling, dust-laden thermal drafts sweep through the hollow spires of the plateau, they pass through the resonator’s central core. The massive brass reeds begin to vibrate, producing a deep, sub-audible tone that travels not through the thin air, but directly through the granite bedrock for fifty miles in every direction. 

This sound is the pulse of the plateau. It is a seismic heartbeat that orders the chaos of the swarms below. The smaller quadrupeds and relay-fleas align their strides to the vibration; the friction-welders use the rhythmic shaking of the stone to settle their welded joints into place. Around the base of a resonator, the fauna density reaches a frenzy: millions of metallic bodies piled three-deep, grinding against one another in a glowing, arc-flashing sea of brass and iron, all turning, shifting, and wearing themselves down to dust beneath the motionless, furnace-door sky.

Here, life is not a fragile green film clinging to stone. It is the stone itself, refined, hardened, set in motion by thermal rage and wind, constantly rebuilding its own teeth as it grinds itself away to nothing.
