Visits to this location—a high-altitude, thermo-dynamic engine of stone, ice, and raw radiation—require meticulous preparation. It is not an environment actively hostile to life through malice; rather, it is utterly indifferent to it. An unprotected human body will be broken down quickly by altitude sickness, respiratory damage from airborne silica, thermal shock, and severe radiation burns. 

Here is how an ordinary human multiverse traveler should prepare for a expedition to the Basalt Horn Shelf.

---

### 1. Ocular and Skin Protection (The Sun as an Abrading Force)
At this altitude, with no atmospheric cushion and quartz veins acting as natural parabolic mirrors, light is a physical hazard. 

*   **Category 4 Glacier Glasses:** Standard UV-blocking sunglasses are wholly insufficient. You need Category 4 mountaineering goggles with leather or rubber side-shields to block peripheral glare. The lenses should have a dark silver or gold mirror coating to reflect the white glare. Without these, **photokeratitis (snow blindness)** will set in within 30 to 45 minutes, causing temporary or permanent corneal burns.
*   **Physical Sun Barrier (No Exposed Skin):** Do not rely on chemical sunscreens alone; the extreme dry heat will bake them off, and the fine dust will adhere to the grease, creating an abrasive paste on your skin. Wear a full-coverage balaclava or desert-style neck gaiter made of high-UPF (50+) breathable synthetic fabric. 
*   **Zinc Oxide Sheen:** For any areas around the eyes or nose left slightly exposed, apply a thick, opaque paste of industrial zinc oxide.

### 2. Respiratory Defense (The Glittering Hazard)
The updrafts created by the rock’s thermal shifts lift curtains of flour-fine quartz dust and dried iron sulfate crystals. Inhaling this dust will cause immediate airway inflammation and long-term silicosis.

*   **P100 Elastomeric Respirator or Tight-Sealing N95:** A simple cloth mask will fail. You need a rubber-sealed respirator fitted with particulate filters. The dust here is sharp on a microscopic level; every breath of unfiltered air will feel like inhaling warm, dry glass.
*   **Saline Nasal Rinses and Eye Drops:** Carry sealed ampoules of sterile saline. The low humidity and fine dust will dry out your mucous membranes within an hour, leading to nosebleeds and severe eye irritation if dust sneaks past your goggles.

### 3. Apparel and Mobility (Navigating the "Slate Books")
The shelf’s floor is a maze of razor-thin, vertically oriented slate plates. Walking here is like traversing a box of standing knives.

*   **High-Abrasive Mountaineering Trousers:** Standard nylon hiking pants will be shredded instantly by the slate fins. Wear trousers reinforced with Kevlar, Cordura, or thick leather along the calves, knees, and inner ankles.
*   **Stiff-Soled, High-Ankle Boots:** You need rigid-soled mountaineering boots (B2 or B3 rated) with deep Vibram rubber treads. The space between the slate plates is only inches wide; flexible trail runners will allow your feet to slip into these cracks, trapping or snapping your ankles.
*   **Reinforced Gloves:** Leather or Kevlar-backed work gloves are mandatory. If you lose your balance and catch yourself on the vertical slate plates, un-gloved hands will be severely lacerated.
*   **Thermal Layering for Rapid Micro-Climates:** You will experience extreme thermal contrast. Standing in the sun, your black jacket will bake up to 100°F (38°C); step two feet into the blade-like shadow of a basalt overhang, and the ambient temperature drops below freezing (25°F / -4°C). Wear a moisture-wicking base layer, a heavy fleece or grid-midlayer, and an ultra-durable, windproof hard shell with pit-zips so you can dump heat instantly when moving through sunlight.

### 4. Altitude and Hydration Strategy
The thin air will impair your decision-making, and the extreme dryness will rob your body of moisture with every breath.

*   **Acclimatization / Supplemental Oxygen:** If transferring directly via multiverse portal from a sea-level environment, you must take **Acetazolamide (Diamox)** 24 hours prior to prevent Acute Mountain Sickness (AMS). If your stay exceeds a few hours, carry a lightweight, portable canister of supplemental oxygen (or a pulse-oximeter to monitor your blood oxygen levels). Keep your heart rate low; panic or sudden exertion at this height will induce rapid hypoxia.
*   **Hydration:** Bring your own water in insulated containers (hydroflasks wrapped in neoprene). *Do not drink the meltwater pooling on the shelf.* Although it looks clear, it is heavily tainted with iron sulfate, soot-grey mineral sediments, and dissolved copper/bronze ions from the ancient wedges. Drinking it will cause severe gastrointestinal distress or mineral poisoning.

### 5. Fall Protection and Anchoring
The shelf is small, bordered on three sides by hard walls, but the fourth side drops off into a blue-grey abyss. Sudden updrafts can shear off the cliff face without warning.

*   **Lightweight Climbing Harness and Lanyard:** As soon as you step onto the shelf, anchor yourself. The ancient human occupants left bronze/copper wedges jammed into the rock fissures along the basalt wall. While these wedges are ancient, the main structural joints of the rock are rock-solid. Carry spring-loaded camming devices (friends) or nut-traps to set your own anchor in the basalt wall, and stay clipped in via a dynamic lanyard.

### 6. Mental Preparation: The Sensory Experience
To survive here without overwhelming anxiety, you must understand the acoustic and physical nature of the site before you arrive:

*   **The Sounds:** The mountain is not quiet. It "ticks" and "clacks." As the sun hits the stone, the basalt expands, producing sharp, metallic cracking sounds like gunshots or iron hammers hitting slate. Do not panic—the cliff is not collapsing; it is merely breathing thermally. 
*   **The Dust Curtains:** You will see shimmering, airborne veils of quartz sparkling in the white sun. They look magical, almost sentient, but they are purely mechanical—dust caught in thermal updrafts. Admire them from behind your respirator, but do not walk directly through a dense curtain if you can avoid it.
*   **The Artifacts:** The low granite windbreak in the center is the safest place to sit. The ancient fires burned so hot here that the ash fused into glassy slag on the bedrock, making it a stable, smooth floor compared to the treacherous slate fins surrounding it. Use this windbreak as your base of operations.
