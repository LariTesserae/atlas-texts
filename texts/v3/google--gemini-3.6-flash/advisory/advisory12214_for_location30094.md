### Expedition Briefing: The High Basin of the Living Lattice
**Location Classification:** Submerged High-Altitude Biosphere / Monolithic Arbor-Aquatic Settlement  
**Primary Medium:** Liquid Water (0°C to 4°C, high clarity, high acoustic conductivity)  
**Atmospheric Status:** Non-existent at destination depth; surface air is thin (2,000m elevation). You will be fully submerged for the duration of your visit.

---

### Introduction: Understanding the Medium

You are traveling to a place where terrestrial human logic fails. There is no ground to stand on that is not alive, no air to draw into your lungs, and no silence. 

The city exists inside a single, mountain-sized plant—an iron-hard timber-reed growing in a high volcanic dish. Because water at this altitude is cold, dense, and virtually still, it behaves less like a lake and more like a liquid atmosphere. The people who live here do not swim in the frantic, splashing sense; they drift, fall, and anchor. Their culture is shaped by extreme longevity, acoustic saturation, and the physical drag of cold water.

To visit this place as an ordinary air-breathing human, you cannot simply "visit." You must integrate into the fluid dynamics of the basin.

---

### 1. Life Support: Breathing, Heat, and Pressure

Because there are no air pockets—no shores, no dry domes, no open-air caverns—your life support system is your entire world.

#### A. The Rebreather System (Silent Life Support)
Do **not** use open-circuit SCUBA gear. Standard SCUBA blasts loud, turbulent clouds of expanded air bubbles with every exhaled breath. In a city where buildings are carved into living fibers and the air-less canopy rests just above your head, a burst of buoyancy-disrupting bubbles is the equivalent of letting off a flashbang in a quiet library. It will startle the armored char, disturb the woven silt-traps, and instantly alienate the inhabitants.

*   **Recommended Gear:** A Closed-Circuit Rebreather (CCR) with a mouth-bit or a full-face mask. A CCR recycles your exhaled gas, scrubs the carbon dioxide, and releases *zero bubbles*. 
*   **Acoustic Insulation on the Motor:** Ensure the rebreather's solenoid and valve mechanics are acoustic-dampened. The locals live in an acoustic horn; a metallic *click-hiss* every thirty seconds will mark you as a noisy, irritating alien.
*   **Gas Mix:** Trimix (Helium/Oxygen/Nitrogen) adjusted for a 2,000-meter altitude surface pressure. Though the maximum depth is modest (approx. 90 feet / 27 meters), altitude diving requires specific decompression profiles.

#### B. Thermal Protection (The Great Drain)
The water is fed by glacial vents. It is hovering just above freezing. Water conducts heat away from the human body 25 times faster than air. If your thermal system fails, severe hypothermia will set in within twenty minutes.

*   **Undergarments:** Heated electronic base-layers powered by dual-redundant lithium-polymer battery packs worn inside a vulcanized rubber drysuit.
*   **Suit Construction:** Heavy-duty trilaminate or vulcanized rubber. Do not use soft neoprene; the dense timber-reed branches, bone-bark panels, and coarse hyacinth ropes will shred delicate materials.
*   **Hands and Face:** Dry gloves with thermal liners are mandatory. A full-face mask is strongly advised over an open mouthpiece—not only for warmth, but because you will be spending hours communicating via hand gestures and subtle body language; keeping your face visible and dry aids in humanizing you to the locals.

### 2. Sensory Adaptation: Sound and Sight

#### A. Acoustic Management (Living in a Bell)
The prompt notes that water is an acoustic horn here. Millions of barnacles rasps like iron filings; bone-plated char strike wooden walls like gravel; chest-wide mussels thump like war drums against the basin's granite rim. 

To an unprepared human ear, underwater sound does not have a clear direction—it travels through your skull via **bone conduction**. The drumming of the mussels will vibrate directly inside your jaw and ears simultaneously.

*   **Preparation:** Wear fluid-filled, pressure-equalizing acoustic dampening earplugs. You want to reduce the ambient sound volume by roughly 20 to 30 decibels without killing your ability to hear close-range acoustic signals.
*   **Psychological Prep:** Expect the sound to feel oppressive for the first two hours. Your brain will try to locate the source of a sound that feels like it’s happening *inside your head*. Practice deep, rhythmic breathing to avoid sensory-overload panic.

#### B. Visual Adjustment
The sun filters through 90 feet of cold, clear water, casting sharp, pale-green shadows that shift to deep cobalt by late afternoon.

*   **Lenses:** Use a mask with optical-grade glass treated with an anti-reflective coating. A amber-tinted or magenta-tinted lens filter will help restore color contrast in the pale-green ambient light, allowing you to read the subtle texture changes in the carved wood and the faint signals woven into the hyacinth causeways.
*   **No High-Powered Flashlights:** Do not shine high-lumen dive lights directly into carved dwellings or at the inhabitants. Their eyes are permanently adapted to low-contrast, dappled green light. A 1,000-lumen LED beam is to them a blinding assault. If you bring a light, use a low-wattage, warm-spectrum (2700K) red or yellow light diffuser.

### 3. Movement and Kinetics: The Art of the "Unforced Motion"

In this city, fast movement is impossible and culturally offensive. The water is dense; pushing hard against it creates violent eddies that wash fine flour-silt off carved ledges, disrupting centuries of undisturbed aesthetics.

#### A. Buoyancy and Trim
You must achieve **absolute neutral buoyancy**. You should be able to hover three inches above a hand-woven hyacinth bridge without touching it, suspended purely by your lungs and counterweights.

*   **Weight Distribution:** Do not use a traditional weight belt that pulls your hips down. Use distributed ankle and shoulder weights so your body sits completely horizontal in the water—the same orientation as the structural wood grid.
*   **Propulsion:** Ditch standard long fins. Use short, stiff "tech" fins or hinged aquatic shoes. The locals do not kick frantically; they use gentle, sculling motions of the hands and ankles, or pull themselves along the trained timber-reed lattice using single fingers.

#### B. Gear Streamlining
Look at your gear: every strap, hose, gauge, and clip must be tucked flush against your body. 
*   **The Hazard:** The city is a maze of braided water-hyacinth ropes, delicate willow-slip fish traps, and sinew-tied obsidian tools. A loose pressure gauge dangling from your hip could catch a woven loom line, snapping a month's worth of a weaver’s work or dragging a fragile bone-bark panel out of alignment.
*   **Rule of Thumb:** If it can snag, tape it down or sheath it.

### 4. Cultural Etiquette and Interaction

The inhabitants are Neolithic in technology (obsidian, bone, antler, sinew) but hyper-advanced in spatial architecture and ecological integration. They live in a slow, stable, silent rhythm.

#### A. The Sanctity of the Heartwood
The city is carved into living timber-reed. The wood is cured with animal fats to prevent waterlogging, and it takes a century for a cut frame to lip over.

*   **Never Use Metal Tools on the City:** Do not bring steel knives, carabiners, or metal-shod boots into contact with the homes. Striking an iron-hard timber stalk with a piece of steel will send an agonizing acoustic shockwave through the wooden frame of the neighborhood, and the metal could leave trace oxidation (rust) that poisons the slow-growing plant fibers.
*   **Touching Etiquette:** Touch only the braided hyacinth ropes or designated mooring pegs. Do not grab carved window frames or doorway edges—your suit's synthetic material can strip the hand-rubbed animal fats off the cured wood, exposing it to rot.

#### B. Social Distancing and Movement
Because water transmits physical displaced volume, moving quickly toward someone feels like pushing a wall of pressure against them.

*   **Approaching a Local:** Stop at least ten feet away. Allow your momentum to dissipate entirely. Sink slowly until your eye level matches theirs. 
*   **Gestures:** Movements should be long, slow, and sweeping. Fast, jerky hand movements suggest danger or panic, which will cause nearby schools of armored char to scatter, potentially destroying delicate fishing nets suspended in the avenues.

#### C. Trade and Gifts
They have no metallurgy, but they don't necessarily *want* it. Metal rusts, clangs loudly, and ruins the acoustic clarity of their world.

*   **Ideal Offerings:**
    *   **High-Grade Volcanic Glass/Obsidian:** Raw, unworked nodules of rare obsidian (especially mahogany or rainbow obsidian) from other worlds.
    *   **Biological Preservatives:** Non-toxic, deep-sea-safe lipids or dense waxes that can be used to rub down their carved interiors.
    *   **Fine Abrasives:** Ultra-fine, smooth polishing stones or diamond-grit sanding cloths (non-metallic) that allow them to hone antler chisels and smooth the edges of their doorways without splitting the grain.

### 5. Step-by-Step Departure and Entry Plan

1.  **Materialization Phase:** Program your arrival coordinates for **5 meters below the surface canopy**, within one of the broad, open sunlit shafts (the "public squares"). Do *not* materialize on the surface—the air is thin, freezing, and there is no land to stand on; you will instantly sink or freeze. Do *not* materialize inside the timber-reed grid, or you risk fusing with a trunk or snapping a braided causeway.
2.  **Acoustic Check:** Immediately upon arrival, freeze. Do not kick. Listen to the hum of the barnacles and the thump of the giant mussels. Adjust your earplugs until the sound shifts from an overwhelming assault to a deep, rhythmic ambient background music.
3.  **Buoyancy Check:** Ensure you are hovering silently in the green light. Check all hoses. Ensure zero bubbles are escaping your rebreather.
4.  **Descend to Street Level:** Do not dive headfirst. Vent a tiny amount of gas from your drysuit and sink vertically, keeping your arms folded, letting the pale-green shadows swallow you until you reach the level of the carved dwellings.
5.  **Observe the Pace:** Watch a weaver or a child. Match your breath and your pulse to their movement. Only then should you reach out to touch the first braided rope of water-hyacinth.
