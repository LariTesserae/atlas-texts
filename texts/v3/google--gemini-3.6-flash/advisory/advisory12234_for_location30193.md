### Traveler’s Briefing & Preparation Manual
**Destination:** *The Weeping Pocket-Citadel (Sub-Lithic Anomaly)*  
**Hazard Class:** Acoustic Trauma, Extreme Wet-Cold, Extreme Spatial Restriction, Environmental Fragility  
**Recommended Max Duration:** 6–8 Hours (Without specialized bivouac equipment)

---

### The Reality of the Environment

To prepare for this place, you must understand that you are entering an ancient, high-pressure acoustic clockwork made of wet stone. The chamber is a double-story alleyway sealed in black mudstone, but to your senses, it will feel like the inside of a roaring cathedral made for ants. 

You will not walk comfortably. You will not stay dry. You will be an oversized, awkward titan wading through a microscopic, hyper-dense civilization carved into vertical walls of slick basalt and quartz. 

The environment is in a state of **perfect, immutable equilibrium**. The water rate does not change; the temperature does not shift; the stewards do not rest. Your presence is the only variable, and your primary goal—aside from surviving the sensory onslaught—is to leave that equilibrium completely untroubled.

---

### 1. Acoustic & Sensory Armor

The most immediate danger is not the cold or the darkness—it is the sound. The weeping of the ceiling is acoustically multiplied by the hard slate geometry into a continuous, deafening roar. Because the sound waves bounce endlessly within the small volume, the noise does not just enter your ears; it vibrates through your skeletal structure.

*   **Custom-Molded Attenuation Earplugs:** Standard foam plugs will fail instantly once soaked by the thick fog. You need custom-molded silicone plugs with high-grade acoustic filters (NRR 30+ dB). They must allow you to hear close-range vocalization or warning clicks while cutting the low, thundering hum that causes rapid cognitive degradation and vertigo.
*   **Bone-Conduction Audio/Communication:** If traveling with a partner, radio signals may struggle through the dense, iron-rich wet rock, and airborne speech is useless over the roar. Use short-range, bone-conduction throat mics and headsets pressed against your temporal bones.
*   **Vibro-Acoustic Protection:** Wear a fitted, padded chest guard or dense neoprene vest under your waterproofs. The low-frequency rumble trapped in the mudstone will vibrate your organs; a firm core compression layer dampens the nauseating "gut-shake" caused by the trapped acoustic pressure.

---

### 2. Thermal & Hydrological Defense

The temperature sits at a perpetual "marrow-chill"—roughly 4°C to 7°C (39°F–45°F)—combined with 100% relative humidity and a constant fine spray. Water will find every seam, zipper, and stitch.

*   **Underlayer (Hydrophobic Moisture Management):** *Do not wear cotton or standard fleece.* Use a heavy-gauge grid-merino wool or high-loft synthetic base layer. It must retain insulating value even when damp from your own sweat, as you will be scrambling and crawling in a high-humidity box.
*   **Outer Layer (Caving Drysuit / Heavy Speleology Shell):** You need a continuous, fully sealed polyurethane or heavy-duty Gore-Tex Pro drysuit with vulcanized rubber gaskets at the wrists and neck. The knees, elbows, seat, and shoulders must be heavily reinforced with Cordura or Kevlar patching—you will be pressing your body against sharp quartz veins and damp slate to stabilize yourself.
*   **Footwear (Wet-Stone Friction):** The stone here has been worn "smooth as fat" by eons of mineralized water and passing touch. Standard hiking boots will turn your feet into ice skates. You need **speleological or canyoneering boots** with ultra-soft, sticky rubber soles (e.g., Vibram Megagrip or Stealth rubber) designed specifically for wet, polished, bio-film-free rock. 
*   **Gloves:** Bring three pairs of high-dexterity, 3mm neoprene gloves with textured latex or rubberized palms. The stone is cold enough to rob your hands of grip strength in twenty minutes, and you will be relying on handholds on wet quartz to move.

---

### 3. Respiratory Care & Air Management

The air is not toxic, but it is heavy, dense, and strange. It carries aerosolized calcium, iron particulates, and the faint, greasy sulfur of tallow smoke.

*   **Moisture-Exclusion Mask / Half-Respirator:** Breathing cold, saturated fog for hours can lead to fluid accumulation in the lungs (trench lung). Wear a comfortable half-mask respirator fitted with particulate filters (P100) and a light organic vapor cartridge. This cuts the sulfur smell, keeps pulverized mineral dust out of your airways, and warms the air slightly before it reaches your lungs.
*   **Eye Protection:** The fog and splashing water will constantly coat your eyes. Bring **anti-fog, polarized safety glasses** or close-fitting ventilation goggles. The polarization is crucial: the dim light here creates disorienting glare on the wet, polished basalt faces.

---

### 4. Lighting Etiquette & Optics

Light in the vault is a rare, delicate resource. The lower basins glow with a faint, chalky phosphorescence, and the stewards rely on tiny, dim tallow pots. Blasting this space with a 10,000-lumen tactical flashlight is an act of ecological vandalism and will instantly blind you due to reflections off the wet, glossy walls.

*   **Warm, Low-Lumen Variable Headlamp:** Your primary light must have an extremely warm color temperature (around 2000K–2700K) to mimic the amber glow of the tallow pots. It should be step-adjustable down to a single lumen. 
*   **Deep-Red / Amber Filters:** Use red or amber light to navigate. Red light preserves your dark adaptation, reduces blinding reflections on wet slate, and is less likely to startle the blind amphibians or disturb the stewards' delicate routines.
*   **Thermal/Infrared Imager:** Because ambient light is so low and contrast is flattened by the glossy wetness, a compact hand-held thermal camera is surprisingly useful. It will allow you to instantly spot the cool, silt-skinned stewards against the slightly warmer or cooler water channels without casting a single photon of disruptive light.

### 5. Movement, Scale, & Environmental Ethics

You are entering a "miniature urbanism." The micro-citadel's steps are the size of teeth; its bridges are ancient megafauna ribs; its gutters are as thin as your thumb. A single careless step will shatter centuries of hand-chiseled architecture or block a critical drainage seam.

*   **The "Three-Point Chimney" Technique:** You will rarely be walking flat. The alleyway floor is likely a catch-basin or a tiny, terraced drainage system. You will need to move by straddling the narrow space, wedging your back, knees, and sticky-soled boots against uncarved sections of the basalt walls.
*   **Proximity Protocols:** 
    *   **Do not touch the bridges.** The rib-bone bridges and slate bridges are structural linchpins for the micro-cells. Do not use them as handholds.
    *   **Watch the gutters.** The hand-bored drainage holes divert water around living spaces. If your suit sleeve or boot blocks a channel, water will back up into the carved niches within minutes, potentially drowning the blind amphibians or washing away tallow reserves.
*   **Gear Stowage:** Keep your profile absolutely "clean." No dangling straps, no external carabiners, no loose water bottles. Everything must be tucked inside a streamlined, smooth-surfaced caving pack. If a strap catches on a tiny stone lintel, you could pull down an entire column of micro-dwellings.

---

### 6. Interacting with the Stewards

The stewards are slow, thick-limbed, silt-skinned beings dedicated entirely to channel maintenance. They are not hostile, but they are relentless in their labor.

*   **Non-Interference:** If a steward is clearing a gutter with its bone probe, **stop and wait**. Do not attempt to help them, and do not offer them tools. Their ecology is built on stone, bone, and fat; modern metals or synthetics introduced into their water channels could introduce toxins or alter the micro-friction of the gutters.
*   **Movement Protocol:** Move at their speed—slowly, deliberately, without sudden displacement of air or water. They do not have eyes, but they are likely acutely sensitive to acoustic vibrations through the rock and changes in water pressure/flow.
*   **Silt Hygiene:** The stewards’ skin is like wet river-silt. Avoid touching them directly; the oils, chemical residues, or synthetic fabrics of your gloves could damage their semi-permeable, moist skin. If contact is unavoidable, rinse your gloves in the local water first to remove external contaminants.

---

### 7. Logistics & Survival

*   **Hydration:** **DO NOT drink the local water.** Despite its pristine, endless flow, it is heavily saturated with calcium, iron, dissolved slate minerals, and lipid traces from the tallow-producing deep fauna. Drinking it will cause severe stomach distress or mineral toxicity. Carry your own water in a flexible, internal hydration bladder protected inside your drysuit.
*   **Nutrition:** High-calorie, mess-free gel packets or liquid nutrition. Do not bring crumbly food—a single fallen cracker crumb could introduce mold or fungal spores to an environment that has zero plant or fungal life, completely disrupting its lithic-mineral balance.
*   **Waste Management:** Absolutely zero-trace. Pack out all waste, including liquid waste, in heavy-duty, sealed biohazard bags. Your urine contains salts and nitrogen that would permanently alter the chemical balance of the tiny catch-basins you step over.

### Mental Preparation

Before you enter, sit in a dark room, turn on a recording of a roaring waterfall at high volume, press your hands against a wet block of ice, and shrink your spatial awareness down to the width of your own body. 

You are stepping into a subterranean clock that has ticked in the dark for eons. Respect the water, stay off the architecture, move like cold molasses, and listen through the soles of your feet.
