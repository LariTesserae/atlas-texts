The benevolent power does not arrive like an army or a proclamation. It arrives the way this terrace already does: before it appears.

For three nights the stewards wake with the same taste in their mouths—clean mineral, like licking a new-cut stone. In the morning each of them finds a different small thing out of place, not moved far, just *answered*.

A wedge they’d left on a sill now rests precisely in the joint that had been worrying them for weeks.

A reed cord that always frayed at one corner has been re-twisted, the fibers laid back into themselves as if by hands that knew rope but did not need to see.

A moss pad transplanted yesterday has settled into the seam without the usual tearing, as though the stone decided, for once, to cooperate.

No one says *miracle*. They are not that kind of people. They say what they always say when something goes right here: “The ground is less argumentative today.”

And yet—listen beneath the roar.

There is a second sound that wasn’t there before, too low to be heard like a note. It is felt in the teeth when you set them together. It is felt in the soles when you stand still on the highest plaza slab. A slow, measured *presence* inside the constant noise, the way a hand can be present on your shoulder without moving at all.

The power has noticed.

Not because the terrace is impressive. Benevolent and wise powers are rarely impressed by stone pretending at eternity.

It has noticed because of the practice: a city built as a negotiation instead of a conquest, maintenance offered as reverence, impermanence treated not as failure but as the honest state of anything still becoming. A handful of lives arranged around listening.

That kind of listening shines, even under cloud.

---

### What it does first: it listens back

On the fourth day, when the mist thins for an hour, a figure is seen at the standing-stone circle.

Not a stranger from the valleys—no pack, no lantern, no boots that leave the right kind of mud. Not one of the seasonal crews, either; they always arrive with noise, with laughter that tries to compete with the gorge.

This figure is simply *there*, as if the terrace briefly included “a person” among its other unfinished features.

Those who see it cannot later agree on details. That is the first sign of power that does not wish to rule: it will not let itself be turned into an icon too easily. Some remember a cloak dark with damp; some remember bare arms marked with pale lines like mineral seams; one child swears the figure’s hair was a small, steady waterfall.

It does not speak over the roar. It does not need to.

It kneels at the pooled sheet of water inside the circle and places both hands on the surface. The water dimples around its fingers as if it were soft cloth.

Then, with a gesture as patient as the mountain’s own settling, it *waits*.

The pool answers in its only language: drop, gather, drop. The stone answers: slight lift, slight ease. The mist answers: curl, condense. The gorge answers: exhale, exhale.

The power listens until those answers become a single thing—a signature, a rhythm, a name that isn’t made of syllables.

Only then does it do anything at all.

It closes its hands and lifts them out of the pool, and the water that clings to its skin does not fall. It hangs there in beads, not held by force but by attention.

Each bead reflects the terrace—unfinished arch, fern, scaffold, the line where paving doesn’t match. In each bead the city looks complete for a moment, not because it has been finished, but because it is seen without impatience.

The figure lets the beads fall back into the pool.

That is the first gift: **to be seen truly**, without being rushed into a final shape.

The stewards—those who have learned to treat “being noticed” as dangerous—feel their shoulders loosen in spite of themselves. Something in them understands: this attention is not a spotlight that burns. It is shade on a hot day.

---

### What it does next: it makes a covenant with the unfinished

Benevolent wisdom knows a trap: to “protect” a young thing by freezing it is to kill it politely.

So the power does not stabilize the terrace into permanence. It does not seal the gorge. It does not pin the uphill water into obedience. It does not give the city a tidy destiny.

Instead it makes a covenant that sounds, to human ears, like almost nothing.

It lays one palm on the terrace stone.

It lays the other on the standing-stone circle—older, rougher, the style of hands that worked without blueprints.

And it offers the same vow to both, in the only language they share: pressure, warmth, patience.

- To the mountain: *You may continue becoming. You will not be made into an altar against your will. No one will be allowed to turn your youth into a quarry of miracles.*

- To the stewards: *You may continue tending without being conquered by your own success. No one will be allowed to mistake your practice for an invitation to extract, occupy, annex.*

This covenant does not feel like a wall going up. It feels like weather choosing a direction.

After that day, the path of laid basalt blocks is still there, still sharp-edged, still sweating. But it becomes *harder to find on purpose* and *easier to find by need*.

People who come with maps and entitlement lose their way in ordinary fog, circling until their provisions run thin. People who come because a child is sick, because a flood has cut off a valley, because their hands know how to lash timber in constant damp—those people, improbably, arrive at the terrace with just enough light left in the day to make camp.

No illusion is required. The mountain already makes its own mist. The power merely teaches the mist to recognize intention.

This is the second gift: **sanctuary without stasis**.

---

### Then: it gives the practice one tool, and refuses to give more

In the week that follows, the stewards discover a new stone at the edge of the largest plaza.

It is not large. It is not beautiful. It looks like any other basalt block except for one thing: a seam of pale mineral through its center that catches even flat light.

No one remembers placing it.

When you strike it with a hammer, it does not ring like good stone and it does not thud like flawed stone. It does something else: it produces a vibration that travels through the terrace, down through the hollow voids, into the ribs of scaffolding and the joints of half-sure walls.

And then—after a pause that always lasts the same number of heartbeats—it answers with a second vibration, like a return knock.

Over days they learn what it means.

- If the return knock is quick, the stone beneath is settled enough for weight.  
- If it is slow, there is a void, a new cavity opening, a “still warm” seam shifting under pressure.  
- If it does not return at all, do not build there. Do not sleep there. Do not store grain there. Mark it with charcoal and let it be.

The stewards call it, in their plain way, the **Listener Block**.

They do not worship it. They use it. They build safer curves, choose better arcs, place wedges with less guesswork. They lose fewer people to sudden subsidence; they waste fewer timbers on walls the mountain was never going to accept.

The wise power could have given them metal that never dulled, cords that never rotted, a spell that held bridges in the air.

It gives them instead a way to keep doing what they already do, but with less grief.

This is the third gift: **knowledge that strengthens humility instead of replacing it**.

And it refuses to give more, because too many gifts become dependency, and dependency becomes a different kind of conquest.

---

### It also attends to the other residents, quietly

Benevolence that only loves humans is not wise. So it turns its attention—briefly, lightly—toward the sparse ecology trying to take hold.

Not by creating sudden abundance (abundance without history collapses into plague), but by widening the world’s willingness to *arrive*.

That season, when the clouds lift, three new bird shapes ride the updrafts—not flocks, still solitary, but no longer *so* rare that each sighting feels like an omen. They perch, once, on the unfinished arch beginnings and do not immediately flee. They listen. They leave.

In the damp shadows under the stairs, the pale amphibian-things breed successfully for the first time. The stewards notice because they see tiny trails like threads of glass where none were before. More mouths means fewer fungus blooms in the wrong places, fewer infestations where stored reed cord might rot.

In the hollows beneath the grates, the blind shrimp-worm life shifts subtly toward the edges of the water-film, leaving mineral blooms cleaner, less likely to clog the drains. The terrace’s “lungs” breathe a little easier.

No choir sings. No halo shines. The only announcement is that the city’s systems—stone, water, plant, small creature—become fractionally more *cooperative*.

Not obedient. Cooperative.

The wise power does not make a garden. It makes room for a garden to keep making itself.

---

### Finally: it asks for one thing, and it is not worship

At the next seasonal gathering—when crews arrive with pipe sections, dull hinges, timber dark with sap—the standing-stone circle is different.

In the center of the shallow pool, where the drops fall with metronomic patience, there is a small ridge of stone that was not carved by human hands. It is not a pedestal, not an altar. It is shaped like a channel turned upward, a scoop.

When the first drop hits it, the water does not simply splash and rejoin the sheet. It divides into two threads that run in opposite directions along hairline grooves, tracing a slow spiral on the plaza paving before sinking into the grates.

The power is teaching a new habit: **to share the water’s attention**, not always letting it choose the same path.

The stewards understand the request immediately because it is stated in their native ethics: maintenance.

Don’t let any one route become too important. Don’t let any one person become indispensable. Don’t let any one seam bear more strain than it should. Keep the practice distributed the way water should be distributed.

So they add it to their calendar, the way they already add everything that keeps them alive:

- once each week, someone clears the spiral grooves with a reed brush;  
- once each season, someone replants the moss pads that guide the threads;  
- once each year, at the gathering, they teach the newest hands *why* the spiral exists.

No hymns. No kneeling. Just care.

That is what the benevolent power wanted in the first place: not a monument to itself, but a community that can keep doing the work without hardening into a tyranny of permanence.

---

### What changes, and what does not

The roar does not diminish. The gorge continues to exhale as it always has.

Stone still chooses its final shape slowly and sometimes rudely. Stairways still need seasonal re-leveling. Walls still pause mid-course, waiting for the mountain’s consent.

People still come and go in intervals. The terrace is still built for crowds that arrive only sometimes.

But the place acquires a new quality, subtle enough that you might call it imagination if you didn’t live there:

- Disasters become rarer, not because danger is removed, but because warnings arrive earlier.  
- Exploiters stop trying, not because they are punished, but because their certainty fails them in the mist.  
- The stewards become a little less exhausted by vigilance, because something vast is finally vigilant *with* them.

And the power itself?

It does not stay as a ruler. Wise benevolence is not possession.

It returns occasionally, in forms that cannot be reliably described—once as a figure at the pool, once as a warmth in the stone that arrives with no sun, once as a change in the uphill damp that spells a message only moss can read.

Its lasting action is not its appearances. Its lasting action is the covenant: a protection that does not imprison, an attention that does not demand, a single tool that amplifies humility.

A city on a young mountain remains what it always was—provisional, wet with its own breath, loud with its own making.

Only now, when the mist gathers on eyelashes and the terraces darken into evening, there is one more thing you can feel beneath the constant sound:

not control,

not certainty,

but companionship—steady as condensation, patient as stone—keeping watch over a practice that has earned the right to continue becoming.
