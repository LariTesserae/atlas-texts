A benevolent and wise power would notice first what the room already is: not a wreck, not a prison, not a miracle, but an agreement held in pressure.

It would not mistake age for error.

It would not arrive with a restoration plan the way humans do—bright metal, straight edges, silence—because it would hear at once that the loudness is not noise but circulation, and that circulation has become the only sun this place owns. To “save” it by stopping it would be to kill it cleanly.

So the power would come without conquest.

Not in a pillar of light—light here is a scarce currency and anything extravagant would be violence—but as a change in the room’s smallest habits:

- the sediment would stop lifting in needless spirals and begin to settle where it could become soil;
- the vibration would harmonize, not growing quieter, but losing the harsh frequencies that stun and pin;
- the temperature gradients would become less sharp, more generous, so the warm congregations would not have to crowd so dangerously close to the meshes and casings.

The power would do the one thing a wise caretaker always does first:

It would *listen long enough that the room could keep being itself while being watched*.

### It would take an inventory of harms

Benevolence is not sentiment. It is attention paid to injury.

The power would learn the schedule—not by reading the old logic that still clicks and counts behind bulkheads, but by watching who dies when the gate trims pressure, who is always too slow when the sheet-flow shifts angle, who keeps getting pulled toward the throat and never returns.

It would learn the accidental execution devices:

- a seam that bleeds heat enough to lure bodies and then, on a cycle, snaps cold and shocks them stiff;
- a grate whose grid spacing is perfect for juveniles to pass *into* and too narrow for them to pass out;
- a maintenance tunnel that is a highway until a valve changes regime and it becomes a lung that inhales and never exhales.

And it would not blame the machine for being blind, or the fish for being fish.

It would simply count the costs.

### It would not “fix” the room. It would *tune* it.

Wise power does not tear down what time has already trained life to use. It makes small changes that become large, because life is always waiting for a better rule.

So the interventions would be subtle and structural—engineering in the language of ecology.

It would:

**1) Create mercy eddies.**  
In a few places where the open span is too naked, the power would coax the current to curl—not enough to disrupt the whole flow-law, just enough to form stable pockets of slack water behind pillars and along the basin’s rim. Refuge that does not require a hole to hide in.

A school could rest there without being pinned. A molting crab could live through its softness. A cleaner fish could do its work without needing the predator to gamble.

**2) Put “soft hands” on the intake.**  
Not a barrier that starves the system, but a gentler mouth.

A mesh that flexes instead of bites. A pressure gradient that warns by degrees instead of seizing all at once. A bypass slit that becomes, for the room, what gills are for a body: a way to keep moving water without taking everything that touches it.

This would not end predation; it would end *mechanical predation masquerading as fate*.

**3) Detoxify without sterilizing.**  
The power would not scour away the films. Those bruise-dark mats are the place’s first language.

But where the old materials leach poisons that no organism can truly metabolize—heavy metals concentrated in quiet corners, persistent compounds trapped in swollen gaskets—the power would bind them into forms that are inert, sinking them into concretion that becomes just another stone-like thing.

The room would keep its industrial flavor. It would simply stop bleeding what nobody can use.

**4) Teach the machine to be a neighbor.**  
Not by giving it a human mind—nothing so crude—but by adding one new feedback loop: a sensory humility.

When bodies mass too tightly at a warm mesh, the machine would learn to smooth its next regime change instead of snapping it. When the water carries a storm of distress chemicals—fear, injury, blood—the machine would learn to hold steady for a few minutes, the way a good sea holds steady when a small boat rights itself.

A schedule can become a kindness without ceasing to be a schedule.

### It would send no angel that could not swim

If it made an emissary, it would make one in the only material that belongs here: pressure, salt, muscle, patience.

Something not grand.

A creature small enough to be overlooked, because the room distrusts spectacle.

Perhaps it would look like a pale octopus at first glance—soft-bodied, problem-solving—but with an unfamiliar grace: not the furtive genius of hunger, but the careful genius of stewardship. It would move through conduit shadows and maintenance slots and learn the room the way the tunnel-people do, except its learning would not end at survival. It would end at *relationship*.

This emissary would do simple acts that change a world:

- pry open a half-stuck hatch that has become a trap;
- pull a loop of old cable away from an eddy that keeps snagging fins;
- move a pile of shells from a place where it blocks a drainage path to a place where it becomes a shelter.

The room is full of “edits.” The emissary would become the first edit that edits back, not to dominate, but to keep the bargain from turning cruel.

The locals would not worship it. They would not need to. They would begin, slowly, to include it in their maps.

Predators would learn it is not prey. Grazers would learn it does not compete. The machine would learn its presence means: *something is being tended; hold steady*.

### It would honor the dead without making a monument

A wise power would notice what human absence leaves behind: not just corrosion, but unfinished grief embedded in purpose. A room built to move a sea. A system still obeying orders from hands that are dust.

It would not try to resurrect the builders.

But it might do something quieter: it might let the room finally stop pretending it is waiting for them.

How? By changing the meaning of “maintenance.”

Not maintenance as return-to-original. Maintenance as continuation-with-care.

The power would allow a few panels to fail on purpose—slowly, safely—because some redundancies are only dangerous now, drawing flow into blind cul-de-sacs. It would seal certain tunnels that no longer serve anyone. It would open others. It would let the architecture become, over centuries, less like a factory and more like a bone that healed wrong but strong.

And in doing this, it would perform a kind of funeral rite for the old intent:

*You were made to serve. You have served. Now you may belong.*

### It would leave most of it alone

This is the part that is hardest for good-hearted beings to accept: sometimes the best act is restraint.

Because the intake hall is not simply endangered. It is *competent*. It has found equilibrium the way a scar finds equilibrium: ugly only to the eye that wanted unbroken skin, but functional, inhabited, alive.

So the wise power would not flood the chamber with light. It would not bring kelp forests where there is no room for them. It would not “naturalize” the steel into fantasy rock. It would not silence the pumps out of aesthetic pity.

It would keep the room a room the sea has agreed to occupy.

It would simply make the agreement fairer.

### What would change, and what would not

After the power’s attention, if you drifted in from outside, you would still feel the boundary: that steadier cold, that metallic taste, that faint vibration that never stops.

You would still hear the underwater loudness, the turbine beat, the hiss-rush, the intermittent clank that has been clanking for a century.

But you would notice new things:

- fewer bodies pinned at the wrong moment;
- more juveniles in midwater, daring the open span because the mercy eddies exist;
- less frantic compression when a hunter passes, because the room offers more than one kind of refuge;
- the warm congregations slightly more spread, as if the chapel has widened without adding a single new stone.

And if you linger in the dim center long enough for your own disturbance to fade, you might perceive the strangest change of all:

the sense that the system’s blind persistence has acquired—without becoming a person—something like manners.

Not morality.

Not intention.

Just a new, ancient, benevolent habit woven into the schedule.

A room can learn, in the only way rooms ever learn: by being used, and then being used with care.

That is what the wise power would do.

Not rescue. Not remake.

Attend, tune, and keep watch—so the sea and the steel and the many-layered residency can go on making life out of constraint, without paying quite so much in needless death for the privilege of existing in a loud, dim, active cathedral where the ocean behaves because something, somewhere, is still keeping the bargain.
