What lives in the basin is not one species so much as a coalition that has learned to treat stone as both body and instrument.

From a distance it seems like architecture that happened to form; from close up it becomes clearer that the “built” things are the visible edge of an ecology that prefers to stay inside its own walls. Most of the living mass is not on the surface at all. It is in the pores, in the laminar seams, in the cool hollows where dust settles and temperature differences can be held like a charge.

### The wall-things (the builders)

They begin as films—thin as soot—laid down in any sheltered crease where airborne grit accumulates. Under magnification those films are busy: nested layers of cells and noncellular gel, crystals grown through that gel like rebar through concrete. Some of the cells use the dim light, some ignore it entirely and live on gradients—oxidizing one mineral face while reducing another, harvesting energy from the simple fact that this basin is young and chemically restless.

They do not “eat” in the way a wetter animal would. They harvest *arrivals*.

- **Dust** is their staple: silicates, nitrates, sulfates, fine organics carried from far beyond the rim.
- **Trace gases** are their slow miracle: carbon-bearing molecules, nitrogen compounds, occasional whiffs of volcanics or impact-released volatiles that would be nothing anywhere else and are a feast here.
- **Charge** is their weather. A charged grain landing on the wrong ridge matters as much as a seed in rich soil.

When a film has enough dust and enough time, it starts to thicken by a method that is half chemistry and half behavior: it exudes binders that trap grains, and it encourages crystal growth in particular orientations. The combed ridges you saw on the walls are not decorative. They are directional textures—micro-baffles that shape airflow and maximize contact between moving haze and living surface. A wall is, functionally, a lung turned inside out: all surface, all exchange.

The right angles and long arcs are not drawn; they are *selected*. Growth persists where it improves the local circulation. It stalls where it creates dead air. Over many cycles the basin’s convection “prunes” the living structures as surely as drought shapes a shrub: anything that disrupts useful flow is starved of new dust and becomes brittle, collapses, and is rebuilt in a slightly better arrangement. That is how the plan begins to look intentional. It is intention without a planner—feedback so consistent it reads like design.

The bulbous nodes—those doorway-like ends—are the places where the builders do something more than thicken. Inside them, the living film becomes a membrane system: layers that alternately absorb, react, and shed salts. These nodes are storage and sorting chambers. They trap rare molecules and hold them long enough to be used. They are also nurseries.

Because reproduction here cannot rely on water and cannot waste mass, the builders mostly reproduce by **propagules** that are almost mineral: tiny pellets of salt-crystal shot through with dormant cells and a skin of polymer to resist desiccation. A gust breaks a brittle efflorescence, the pellets roll into a seam, a static event nudges them into a warm eddy—and a new film begins. It is less like seeding and more like a slow electrical sporefall.

### The dark mats (the skin of the city)

Along the tops of walls and in the leeward gutters where dust reliably drifts, the basin grows its darkest life: the paint-like mats.

They are a different guild from the builders—still part of the same broad community, but with different priorities. Where the builder films are architects, the mats are **processors**. They are where complex chemistry happens: binding nitrogen into forms the rest can use, cracking stubborn carbon compounds, converting mineral arrivals into biologically available ions.

They are also the basin’s most obvious response to the silent lightning that walks the ridges at night.

The faint green fluorescences are not incidental glow; they are a stress response that has become a signal. A discharge rearranges electrons in pigments and proteins; the mat briefly lights, then dumps that energy into chemical work—repair, synthesis, growth. The mat “likes” electrical weather the way a plant likes sun, not because the discharge feeds it directly, but because it catalyzes reactions that would otherwise crawl.

In places where discharges are frequent, the mat becomes thicker and more textured, and the wall beneath becomes more robust. In places where nights are quiet, the mat thins to a varnish, and the wall grows chalky, fragile, and prone to collapse. Over time, static patterns influence urban patterns: a ridge that crackles more often becomes a boulevard; a quiet corner becomes an abandoned cul-de-sac.

### The candelabra-columns (the harvesters)

Those black, wrist-thick “trees” are the basin’s long-term investments. They are not separate organisms so much as *structures maintained by organisms*—chimneys grown to reach slightly better fluxes of wind and haze.

Inside each column is a core of porous salt and harder mineral, threaded with living film. The film’s job is to keep the scaffold from dissolving into powder: it lays down polymer, heals fractures, and manages crystallization so the column grows upward instead of outward. The column’s job is to lift the film into cleaner flow and to provide a stable thermal gradient: warm skin by day, cool core by night, an engine for minute convection down its own pores.

The columns also act as **dust combs**. Their branching forms are tuned to snag grains without generating turbulence that would blow the catch away. Over months, a column becomes banded with “seasons”—thin dark rings where the mat was active, pale rings where mineral dominated, occasional sharp lines where a rare storm brought exotic chemistry. Each is a ledger of the basin’s diet.

### The small animals (the careful thieves and couriers)

Fauna here does not dominate; it *skims*. It lives on the margin of the builders’ work, stealing what the mineral city makes available.

**The plate-backed crawler** you glimpsed is common enough to leave tracks but uncommon enough to remain unseen. Its mica-like plates are not just armor; they are insulation and, more subtly, electrostatic shielding. In a world where a tiny charge imbalance can mean a painful discharge or a lethal disruption of membranes, the crawler wears a layered suit that bleeds charge away gradually into the ground.

It has no eyes because light is unreliable and shadows mean little. Instead it is built around vibration and chemistry: sensory fans that can taste air currents and detect the faint tick-tick of crystal growth, the microfracture pop of cooling slabs, the barely audible tinkle of collapsing efflorescence. Those sounds are maps. Fresh bloom means fresh salts, and fresh salts mean active microbial films. The crawler feeds by scraping—your neat semicircles—taking mat and binder and the rare organics caught in them, leaving the raw mineral exposed behind.

It is not a mindless grazer. It behaves like an animal that has learned the schedule of a strange city: it visits certain ridges after bloom, avoids certain nodes during discharge-heavy nights, shelters in hollows where temperature stays stable. If there is “predation” in the basin, it is mostly on microorganisms and on each other’s eggs—if eggs is even the right word for tough, salt-cased capsules tucked into a seam.

The **ballooning drifter** is stranger still, and it lives at the edge of being an animal at all. It is a membrane organism: a thin, pale sheet with a network of stiffening fibers, capable of inflating when warmed and collapsing when cooled. It does not truly fly; it lets itself be taken, hitching rides on the basin’s gentle winds the way a seed rides thermals. When it lands, it seals itself against the crust, exudes a solvent that loosens the dark mat, and absorbs the resulting slurry. Then it dries, becomes almost indistinguishable, and waits for the next warming cycle to give it lift.

Sometimes these drifters collect in the lee of a wall like discarded skins. That is not a graveyard; it is a feeding ground. A cluster traps dust, encourages mat growth, and creates a richer patch that attracts crawlers—who, in turn, leave scrapings and waste that feed the mats. Even the animals’ movement becomes part of the basin’s metabolism.

### The hidden majority (the hollow dwellers)

Most of the basin’s life is small enough to be mistaken for texture.

In the chambers beneath the walls, where air hardly moves, communities specialize in slow chemistry: they digest mineral faces the way fungi digest wood elsewhere, etching microscopic galleries, releasing ions that are carried—grain by grain—into the larger network. Some of these organisms form **crystal-guiding templates**, protein lattices that force salts to grow in shapes useful to the architecture: reinforcing ribs, porous filters, needle arrays that catch haze.

Others are **gas bankers**. They live where the chambers “breathe,” right at the vents where warm and cool air swap places. Their cells are adapted to trap rare molecules during one part of the cycle and release them during another, smoothing scarcity into something like a steady income. If the basin has anything resembling a circulatory system, it is here: vent-to-vent exchange of trace gases, carried by convection and shaped by geometry.

These hollow communities also explain the uncanny sense that the city is listening. The basin is full of membranes tuned to pressure change, temperature change, charge change. Not consciousness, but sensitivity spread out like nerve endings across an entire neighborhood.

### What the city wants (if “want” can apply)

It wants gradients.

It wants a difference between warm and cool, between charged and neutral, between dusty air and clean air, between exposed salt and sheltered salt. The entire settlement is a machine for creating and maintaining useful differences in a world that tends toward sameness.

- Walls exist to make **wind legible**—to turn random haze into predictable streams.
- Hollows exist to make **time legible**—to store yesterday’s warmth and last week’s dust.
- Ridges exist to make **charge legible**—to concentrate electrical events where chemistry can benefit.

When conditions change—when the basin receives an unusual load of sulfur-rich dust, or when a rare pressure shift thickens the air for a season—the city changes with it. A new hub swells at a vent that suddenly matters. Old partitions crumble where the flow no longer favors them. Streets fill and reopen elsewhere. The plan redraws itself, always on the verge of erasing and reasserting its geometry.

### How it feels, up close, to meet the inhabitants

If you kneel beside a wall and put your ear near the mineral, you can sometimes hear a faint, continuous crackle—not a spark, but the sound of many tiny stresses being redistributed as crystals grow and films tighten. If you lay a fingertip on the combed ridges, you feel a dryness that is not inert: a surface that grips dust greedily, that holds a thin static cling to your skin and then lets it go.

In a node chamber, if you wait long enough, the air seems to change without moving: a subtle shift in sharpness, a different mineral tang. That is the city breathing through you, exchanging trace molecules with the space your body temporarily occupies.

And if you stay through one of the dim nights when discharges crawl, you can watch the settlement reveal itself in flashes—mat veins lighting like maps, ridges momentarily outlined, columns briefly haloed—then returning to its habitual near-invisibility. Not an announcement. Not a display. Just the unavoidable side-effect of being alive in a place where electricity is part of weather.

So the basin is inhabited by builders that are not individuals, by animals that behave like careful rumors, by membranes and films and mineral-guided growth that makes a kind of urbanity without anyone ever meaning “city.” The inhabitants do not conquer the emptiness. They negotiate with it, patiently, turning scarcity into pattern until pattern begins to look, from the right distance, like purpose.
