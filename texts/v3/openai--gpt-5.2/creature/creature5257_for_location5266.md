What lives here at first is not a “who” so much as a practice.

Something has learned to keep fire alive in wet air, to make water hold to its appointed routes, to turn echo into etiquette, to treat condensation the way surface people treat weather. That kind of learning doesn’t sit in tools alone. It settles into bodies, into taboos, into the way a hand reaches automatically for the right stone plug before a basin overfills. So the first inhabitants you meet are the *habits*—and then, moving inside those habits like a nervous system inside bone, the folk who carry them.

They are not singular. They are a braid of roles, each one tied to a piece of equilibrium.

## The people: the Reservoir-Kin (names change by corner)
If they began as surface-dwellers, the chamber has rewritten them without needing to become fantastical about it.

They tend toward compactness—not as a “race trait,” but as selection-by-survival and by comfort. A tall person learns to stoop in low alleys; a wide-shouldered person bruises and blocks and sweats; a body that fits the city moves easier, works longer, exhales less damp into shared air. Children grow up in narrow walkways and learn to turn sideways without thinking, to keep limbs close, to carry loads low and balanced so water doesn’t slosh.

Light makes its own edits. Their skin is often pallid in the lamp-lit sense—color present but subdued, as if all complexions have been rinsed and re-dyed by soot and mineral dust. Hair is kept short or tightly bound, not for style but because humidity and oil make loose hair a sponge for smoke. Eyes look reflective, not glowing, just trained: pupils that widen fast; lids that squint against sudden lamplight; a habit of focusing on movement and sheen rather than far distance. Their hearing is less “better” than it is *educated*—they flinch at the wrong drip because it predicts overflow, not because it’s loud.

They smell of their infrastructure: limestone tang under lamp-fat; wet ceramic; the faint sourness of bodies living close and washing often but never quite drying. Their hands are marked by stonework—pads thickened, nails worn down, fine abrasions always half-healed. On many you’ll see the same small scars: thin cuts from ceramic edges, burns on forearms from carried heat-stones, and the pale, itchy rash that comes from forgetting and touching a “sick” rusted object.

And because sound is architecture here, their faces hold sound differently. They speak with mouths kept small, consonants softened, breath controlled. Many conversations happen half-signed, half-whispered—fingers tapping meanings on wrist and forearm, knuckles marking emphasis in a way the chamber can’t amplify. Laughter exists, but it is learned: people laugh into cloth, or into the crook of an elbow, or only in the few pockets that swallow sound.

## The city’s true cast: the guilds that are really organs
A surface town can afford idleness. This one cannot. Here, jobs are less “professions” than bodily systems that keep the chamber from drowning, souring, or choking.

### Waterwrights (Channel-keepers, Lip-measurers, Silt-readers)
They are the closest thing the place has to nobility, and the closest thing it has to clergy.

Waterwrights memorize the city as flow before they memorize it as street. Apprentices are taught to hear a route: to tell clean drinking thread from wash-runoff by pitch and speed, to know when a settling basin is silting up because the sound of overflow changes at a particular corner. They carry small tools that are almost ceremonial—bone scrapers, clay wedges, a cord with knots marking the “true” waterline of each cistern terrace.

They’re also the city’s historians. They don’t keep records in paper; they keep them in stone and groove. A new channel cut is an event with witnesses. A plugged weir is a law.

### Heat-bearers (Stone-carriers, Hearth-counters)
In a place where the air is always trying to become water, heat is not comfort; it is permission to live.

Heat-bearers move like careful thieves, carrying dull-red stones from communal hearths in insulated cradles of clay and fiber. They know the timing of glow and cool, the routes that waste the least warmth, the niches where heat will dry a sleeping alcove without making the walls sweat too hard. They also enforce the unspoken math of population: too many bodies in one corridor, too many lamps, and the whole quarter begins to mist.

Their authority is quiet but absolute. A Heat-bearer can end an argument by removing the stone that lets a family cook. No shouting—just cold.

### Rust-wardens (Iron-sinners, Oil-sealers)
Rust here is not a stain. It is contagion: metallic taste in drinking channels, orange creep in mortar, the slow weakening of stone where iron bleeds.

Rust-wardens patrol with oil pots and clay wraps, sniffing for that wet-metal smell that means someone has been careless or desperate. They keep the city’s tiny store of metal like a sickness in quarantine—sealed, cataloged, ritually handled. If someone brings in a surface nail or blade, it is treated the way another society might treat a strange animal: admired at a distance, then quickly caged.

The worst insults in this city are rust-words. “Bleeder.” “Stain-maker.” Not because metal is hated, but because it is powerful enough to ruin shared water.

### Sound-mappers (Echo-scribes, Knock-judges)
They don’t draw maps so much as *compose* them.

They know which alleys throw a whisper, which ledges carry a footstep, which corners drink sound and make a person disappear in silence. They maintain the chamber’s tuned markers—hanging stones, hollow bones, fired-clay bells—each with a tone assigned to an instruction: flood, fire, gathering, sickness, outsider.

In disputes, Sound-mappers are called as witnesses. Not to what was said, but to what could have been heard. In a place where privacy is an acoustic trick, the law is partly physics.

### Clayworkers and Bonewrights (the makers of almost everything)
Because wood is absent and metal is risky, craft here is mineral and animal.

Clayworkers repair tiles, fire vessels, make sluice gates, and build the ceramic filters that keep cistern water sweet. Bonewrights turn every carcass and shell into function: hooks, needles, combs, lamp-spoons, scrapers, toggles for cloth curtains. Nothing is ornamental unless it can also be structural; ornament must earn its humidity.

## How they eat: a diet built from scarcity and trade
No green means no easy calories. Their food is a managed loop of what the chamber provides and what they can coax from it.

- **Cistern harvests:** threadworms and other tiny filter-life gathered from traps, cleaned in brine, cooked into pastes or thin cakes. It tastes like mineral and smoke and salt; children learn not to grimace because grimacing is wasteful.
- **Shell and seam-creatures:** the blind jointed things are trapped with baited crevices. Shells are not thrown away; they are ground into lime or used as scoops.
- **Biofilm scraping:** in the wettest quarters, a few families maintain “slick walls” the way farmers maintain fields. They scrape the iridescent film into slurries, ferment it carefully, and treat it as both food and medicine. Done right it adds sourness and a little strength. Done wrong it makes you sick and makes the whole alley smell of rot, which is socially disastrous in close air.
- **Imports (rare, controlled):** sometimes there are surface goods—salt, dried grains, rendered fats—brought down by runners who know the seam-entrances. These are not everyday market items; they are civic resources. The cistern complex becomes a ration point when such things arrive.

Meals are small and frequent, not because they want to snack, but because cooking is a humidity event: too much steam at once and the lamps sputter. So they learn to eat in a way that keeps the air stable.

## How they reproduce the city: children as apprentices of physics
A child here is never allowed to forget the chamber is alive in its own mechanical way.

They learn early that a shout can hurt people—not emotionally, but physically, by turning a corridor into an echo-box that wakes sleepers and panics elders and makes the whole city feel like it’s vibrating. So games are rhythmic instead of loud: finger-taps, pebble-click patterns, silent races across slick stone.

Coming-of-age rites are not about bravery in darkness; everyone lives in dimness. They are about *control*:

- holding a lamp without smoking the ceiling,
- carrying a heat-stone without sweating into fog,
- clearing a silt trap without stirring the wrong sediment into drinking flow,
- walking the edge of a parapet with water underfoot and not letting fear change your breathing.

A young person earns their first “corner-name” by learning a route: not “from here to there,” but “from cistern lip to whisper-ledge to dead-sound pocket without making the wrong sound.” Names can change if someone moves quarters or marries into another water-route. Identity is partly geography and partly acoustics.

## What they believe: not gods, but agreements
You can call it religion if you want, but it behaves more like contract-law with the chamber as the other party.

Their central devotion is to *separation*: drinking from washing, washing from waste, waste from seepage, seepage from structural stone. They don’t pray for abundance; they perform maintenance with reverence because maintenance is what keeps the agreement valid.

In the cistern complex, there are markings at each pool—scratches and notches that look like counting but are actually memory: how high the water rose in the year of too many births; where the line sat when a vent-shaft choked; the month when the bass resonance deepened and everyone slept badly. People touch these marks the way others touch icons. Not because they think the marks are holy, but because forgetting is dangerous.

The “weirdness” you described becomes doctrine:

- **Water remembers paths** → therefore, do not anger it with careless cuts. New channels are introduced slowly, with watchers, with songs hummed low (not mystical, just tradition that keeps everyone calm and coordinated).
- **Sound governs privacy** → therefore, secrets are held only in sanctioned silent pockets, and lying is considered more foolish than evil because the chamber often betrays you.
- **Rust spreads** → therefore, metal is treated like a temptation and a toxin. A person who hoards iron is treated the way another society treats a plague-carrier.
- **Air wants to become water** → therefore, the worst crime is not theft but crowding: packing too many bodies into a space, forcing breath into fog, making fire fail.

Their myths—when they tell them—are quiet and practical. Tales about the first people who shouted the lamps out. Stories of a “Red Season” when too much imported iron bled into the channels. Legends of a deeper sump where white fish live like ghosts and where the bass note comes from; children dare each other to go listen, but most don’t. Not because of monsters. Because getting lost means your voice might not return.

## Other inhabitants: the chamber’s small animals as neighbors, not scenery
The nonhuman life here is sparse, but it is not incidental.

- The **blind seam-creatures** are treated as omens by some quarters, as pests by others, as meat by most. Their appearance near a channel can mean mineral change in the water; people notice and adjust routes.
- The **threadworms** in silt traps are semi-domesticated—kept in certain baskets, harvested with care, never fully exterminated because they make water “less dead.”
- The **biofilm** is almost a third citizen: it coats stones, makes floors treacherous, feeds on smoke, changes taste. Some families specialize in “taming” it by controlling lamp soot and drip exposure; they can make a wall either slick as soap or dry enough to climb.

And then there is the possibility—never spoken as certainty—that something larger lives in the deep constrictions that create the bass resonance. Not a beast that comes up to hunt, but a presence like a throat-muscle or a lung: a deep water-swell and release. Old people sometimes pause mid-conversation when the note feels different and say, simply, “It shifted.” Then everyone checks the channels, because a shift in the chamber’s voice often precedes a change in seepage.

Whether that “something” is just geology or a true organism doesn’t matter to daily life. Either way, it is treated with the same respect: you do not irritate the deep.

## Outsiders, if any: the seam-runners and surface-traders
If this city persists across generations, it almost certainly has some relationship with above—even if that relationship is cautious, seasonal, or mythologized.

A few individuals serve as **seam-runners**: thin, strong, unafraid of squeezing through damp cracks and climbing slick chutes. They come back smelling of sunless air turning toward sun—dusty, sharp, almost painful. They bring news that feels like fiction to people who measure distance in corners: wind, horizons, fires that don’t choke on humidity.

Seam-runners are admired and distrusted. They carry the risk of rust, of spores, of new noises. When they return, there are procedures: wash, oil-seal any metal, let their clothes dry near a controlled hearth, keep them from crowded quarters until their breath stops smelling like outside.

## The true resident: the civic mind
Stand long enough on an upper walkway and you can see that the city itself behaves like a single organism made of many careful animals.

Individuals matter—lovers meet in dead-sound pockets, elders die in warm niches, children steal extra worm-cake and get caught by echoes—but what endures is the shared discipline that keeps the wet equilibrium from collapsing.

So what lives here is:

- people trained into being instruments of hydrology,
- craft guilds that function like organs,
- a few sparse, persistent creatures that the city has folded into its cycles,
- and a chamber that is not sentient in the human sense but is treated as a partner with rules—rules enforced by drip, by rust, by reverberation, by the thin line between flame and fog.

They don’t “conquer” this place. They don’t even fully inhabit it the way surface folk inhabit a valley. They maintain it, corner by corner, as if survival is a form of listening—and the chamber, loud and steady, is always answering.
