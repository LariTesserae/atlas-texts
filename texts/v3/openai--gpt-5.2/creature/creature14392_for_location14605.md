What lives here is not a single tenant so much as a stacked residency—three kinds of life, one kind of quasi-life, and a fourth that comes and goes but has left so much trace it counts as indigenous.

At first it feels simplest to say: **plants live here**. Then you stay long enough to notice how much of the place is not merely planted but *inhabited*—occupied with routines, boundaries, tolerances, and a kind of slow agency. After that, it becomes hard to keep calling the machines “infrastructure” as if they’re inert, because their schedules are as regular as breathing. And finally you find the signs of the ones who designed the compromise: the people who treat this wet district as a workplace, a parish, a nervous system they’re paid to keep calm.

### 1) The primary residents: the structural plants (grown, trained, domesticated into architecture)

They are not garden plants, and they are not wild plants either. They are **utility organisms**, bred and pruned into roles the way dogs were bred into jobs—except here the “jobs” are *walls, seals, cable runs, flooring, insulation, damping, filtration*.

You can tell, even without seeing a single label, that different species have been selected for different mechanical virtues:

- **The beam-vines** (the ones that make ceilings): thick-stemmed climbers whose fibers run long and straight. Their stems don’t snap; they crease. When they’re young, they’re guided through iron hoops. When they’re older, they engulf the hoops and make them irrelevant. Their skin is slick with constant condensation; their interior wood stays tough and faintly warm, as if it’s always metabolizing just enough to resist the wet.

- **The panel-stems** (the ones that make walls): shrubs trained flat, interwoven until they behave like wattle that never dries. Their growth nodes are close together; they can be pruned hard and respond by thickening rather than dying back. They make good partitions because they accept grafts—patching a hole is literally splicing in a living repair.

- **The root-mats** (the ones that make floors): not a single organism but a managed braid of several, chosen for grip and for their willingness to live in half-oxygenated water. They hold sediment the way geotextile holds soil. They’re why the ground is a sponge instead of a slurry. Step on them and you feel the tug of a living weave, as if the place resents being compressed but tolerates it.

- **The seal-barks** (the ones that solve leaks): slow, thick-trunked plants positioned where masonry meets pipe, where cracks tend to open, where vibration worries joints loose. They exude resin—not romantic amber, but a practical, persistent ooze that cures in damp and stays flexible. In the district’s logic, a “caulk gun” is sometimes a pruning knife and patience.

- **The sacrificial greens** (the ones that take the hit): thin films of algae-like growth and simple mossy layers that are allowed to colonize certain surfaces because they *prefer* the mineral bloom and help manage it—holding salts in a controlled crust that can be scraped or rinsed on schedule rather than letting it creep everywhere.

They live without any animal partners. No bees. No beetles. No birds. If anything flowers here, it does so in a way that assumes nobody will come. You see blossoms that never open fully; pollen that gets caught in condensation and runs down in pale streaks; seeds that are sterile by design because someone decided long ago that self-seeding would be a kind of structural vandalism.

The plants’ “behavior” is mostly slow, but it is not passive. They **claim** any unclaimed void. Leave a maintenance gap and, within months, it’s a green narrowing; within years, it’s a new partition. Their residency expresses itself as a pressure toward occupation—the district’s default answer to emptiness is to fill it with living fiber.

### 2) The invisible majority: microbes and the disciplined fungi

If the plants are the architecture, then the microbes are the **atmosphere’s citizenship**—the thing you’re always breathing, the film you’re always touching without quite touching it.

There are biofilms on everything that stays wet: on the lower brick courses, inside the channels, along the lips of sumps, under the edges of metal where water beads and holds. Their job is not sentimentally “natural”; it’s functional:

- Some communities **buffer the chemistry** of seepage—turning sharp industrial traces into something less corrosive over time.
- Some **eat oils** that inevitably appear around pumps and bearings, preventing slicks from spreading.
- Some **fix nitrogen** in pockets where the plants’ root zones need it, because there are no animals above to supply the usual cycles.

Fungi exist, but in a **policed** way. You don’t get fruiting bodies because the district cannot afford surprise rot. What you do get are controlled mats in designated dark corners, the equivalent of compost piles in a place that can’t have compost piles. When they bloom too far, you see the signs of intervention: scraped patches, lime washes, localized heat treatments, the telltale smell of a sterilant that is chosen specifically to not kill the structural plants.

It’s a managed ecology—life allowed to do work, not allowed to become flamboyant.

### 3) The resident machines: pumps, valves, relays (a nonliving population with habits)

There is a sense in which the machinery “lives” here because it has **cycles, moods, and a body** distributed through the district. It is sensitive to pressure, temperature, flow; it responds; it corrects itself; it sometimes overcorrects. People can read its state the way sailors read weather.

- The pumps have a baseline thrum that becomes as familiar as a roommate’s breathing. A slight change in pitch is a warning. A different vibration pattern means a bearing is thinking about failing months from now.
- The valves are the district’s reflexes—sudden, loud, decisive. Their opening is the closest thing this place has to an animal’s startle response.
- The sensors (where they exist) are old and damp and reliable in the way old things become reliable once their failures have been corrected enough times.

Over time the machines and the plants have learned each other’s tolerances. The plants grow where vibration is low, unless they’ve been trained as dampers. The machines are raised where roots will otherwise swallow their housings. You see compromises everywhere: pipes wrapped in bark not for romance but because bark insulates and reduces condensation; cable trays run through living hollows because the plants keep the air there just stable enough.

This is not an ecosystem in the classic sense—no predation, no quick movement—but it *is* a balanced occupancy: a population of devices constantly negotiating with wetness and growth.

### 4) The intermittent but native residents: the keepers (people shaped by the district)

Whether they sleep here every night or not, there is a class of human for whom this place is not “belowground” but simply **where the work is**. The district bears their scale everywhere: hand-width clearances, ladder rung spacing, stenciled numbers at eye level, tool marks, the particular kind of neatness that only appears when someone is responsible for a system that never stops.

They are not many at once. This is not a bustling undercity. It’s more like an engine room with a small watch.

They come in with the right clothing—not dramatic hazmat, but practical layers that accept permanent damp: rubberized aprons over woven synthetics, gloves that can grip slick stems, boots designed to release from suction without tearing the root-mats. They carry tools that look wrong for a greenhouse and wrong for a factory but right for this hybrid: insulated cutters, grafting knives, corrosion scrapers, clamp meters in sealed housings, spools of fiber packing peeled from service bark.

Their work divides into rituals:

- **Pruning as clearance**: not gardening but maintaining human-scale corridors. They cut back the district’s tendency to fill itself. Sap runs; the air sharpens; green smell becomes almost metallic. The cuttings don’t go “to waste”; they go to the little line that presses and pulps, turning excess structure into fiber product, feedstock, insulation, fuel—whatever this wet economy values.

- **Listening as inspection**: because sight is limited by haze and leaf curtains, they listen. A keeper will stand very still with a hand on a pipe, feeling the difference between normal shudder and wrong shudder. In a place where nothing else moves quickly, *a new sound is an emergency light*.

- **Cleaning as containment**: mineral bloom gets scraped where it matters; drains get cleared; sump screens get rinsed; junction boxes get opened and dried with heat packs. They don’t try to make the place dry—only to keep wetness from becoming destructive.

- **Pollination as labor** (when it happens): a small, careful task in a designated chamber where certain structural plants are allowed to reproduce in controlled ways. It is sterile work, more like wiring than like farming: move pollen, label grafts, track lineages, prevent surprise traits.

The keepers develop a culture that fits the acoustics and the air. They don’t waste speech down here because speech is hard. They mark things instead: chalk on wet brick that looks impossible but holds because it’s the right chalk; tags punched from corrosion-proof foil; knots in fiber twine tied in a code only the crew reads. Their knowledge is tactile and rhythmic: when to expect the pump surge, which corridor fogs first, which stem-wall hides an access hatch if you don’t know where to press.

And because there are no animals—because nothing with eyes lives in the corners—the keepers become, in a strange way, the district’s only witnesses. The only ones who can look back at it.

### 5) What *doesn’t* live here—and why that absence becomes a resident of its own

The most haunting “inhabitant” is the rule you already felt: **no fauna**. That rule has machinery around it. Airlocks. Filters. Sterilizing basins at entrances. A kind of cleanliness that isn’t dry, but is absolute in its prevention.

The district does not permit the quick, hungry, multiplying chaos of animals. Not because it’s impossible—plenty of basements acquire rats—but because the district is too valuable, too balanced. Animals would chew fibers, introduce pathogens, nest in warm housings, die in channels, clog sumps. In a place where water is everywhere, decay would become a logistical disaster.

So the place enforces an anti-ecology: life without creatures. Growth without motion. Sound without footsteps.

### In the end, the truest resident is the compromise itself

If you ask, “Who lives here?” and you mean, “Who belongs here?” the honest answer is: **the living architecture**—the plants that have been shaped into civic structure, and the microbial skin that makes wetness tolerable—and also **the maintenance mind**, partly human and partly mechanical, that keeps the wet district from tipping into ruin or jungle.

It’s a settlement whose citizens do not meet in streets, because the streets are roots. Its builders do not leave when the building is done, because the building never stops growing. Its most faithful inhabitants are the ones who can endure constant damp without wanting to conquer it: the vines that accept training, the rust that accepts work, the keepers who accept that “stable” here means *always in motion, just slowly*.
