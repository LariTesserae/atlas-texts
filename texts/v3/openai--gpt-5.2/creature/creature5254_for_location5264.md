What lives here is not a single population so much as a stacked agreement between species—some negotiated deliberately, some merely tolerated until tolerance became custom.

At the broadest scale, the *town itself* is alive in the plainest sense: it is made of organisms that keep growing. The oldest residents are not people.

### The first inhabitants: the load-bearers
Before you meet anyone with a name, you meet the ones that have been named by use.

There are trunk-plants trained into arches and ribs—thick-stemmed, slow-to-split things that accept bending when they’re young and hold their shapes when they’re old. They are pruned like livestock and treated with the same wary respect: fed, cut, bound, never injured in ways that would weaken them later. Their bark bears scars where braces once sat. Their lower stems are smooth where a thousand hands have steadied themselves climbing past.

Below them, the root-mat is not “ground” but a long-lived, engineered colony of intertwining fibers: some chosen for tensile strength, some for rot resistance, some because their roots naturally braid rather than compete. In places you can see the maintenance—patches where new runners have been laid like sutures, or where older sections have been allowed to die back and be replaced before they collapse. It is infrastructure the way a dam is infrastructure, except it eats light and drinks runoff.

And then there are the water-plants that make the slope habitable at all: the ones trained to open porous channels when the wet season becomes a threat, and the ones trained to swell shut and hold when the dry days stretch longer than expected. They live at the town’s command only because the town’s people have spent generations learning the timing of cuts, the persuasion of weights, the patient cruelty of pruning. The settlement survives because these plants do.

If there’s a law older than any spoken rule here, it’s this: **the town is a garden that holds you up.** Break the garden and you break the city.

### The second inhabitants: the small, constant multitudes
Life fills the damp the way smoke fills a room.

Insects are not background; they are a weather system with its own seasons and politics. There are the biting ones that everyone curses and the non-biting ones everyone still swats at out of habit. There are the glittering swarms that rise when a corridor is disturbed, and the heavy-bodied pollinators that thrum around the fruiting edges like tiny engines.

Some insects are effectively municipal workers. Thin-bodied hunters patrol the moss curtains where larvae would otherwise explode into plague. Burrowers keep the silt loose enough to be carried uphill. Their presence is encouraged indirectly: certain roof leaves are chosen because they fold into dry pockets insects prefer; certain alcoves are never smoked out because they house predators that keep worse things in check.

Spiders live in the eaves in a kind of tolerated tenancy. Their webs make lacework between lashings. People tear down the thick old webs when they block vents, but they do it carefully, as if sweeping out an ancestor’s room: you remove, you don’t insult.

Mold and fungi are also residents—sometimes invited, sometimes fought. In the deep humid pockets under platforms, pale blooms creep across wood and fiber overnight. The town’s people recognize them the way coastal folk recognize tides. Some molds are simply part of the price of living here; others are treated like arson. There are alcoves where edible fungi are cultivated with almost religious attention—cool, shaded places where the air tastes of rich rot and the ground is velvet with feedstock. Those rooms are tended like granaries.

Even the algae in the pools has its place: skimmed when it thickens, left alone when it keeps the water sweet, cursed when it turns slick at the wrong time.

### The third inhabitants: the opportunists with teeth and hands
Small mammals make a second economy, and they live close to people because people produce edges—scraps, shelter, predictable rhythms. They’re sleek, damp-furred, quick as spilled water. They know where the drying racks are highest and where the dogs are laziest. Their comb-like teeth aren’t monstrous; they’re specialized, suited to scraping lichen and gnawing soft plant fiber without choking on it.

They’re not fully pests and not fully companions. Children sometimes trap them with woven funnels and keep them briefly in reed cages, feeding them peelings until an elder scolds and releases them—less out of sentiment than out of caution. A hungry opportunist learns too much too fast.

There are dogs (or doglike animals) as well—lean, loud, and tireless in the wet. Their work is practical: driving off the small thieves, warning of the heavy unseen thing in the hedge, keeping herd beasts from pushing through a living pen. They are always slightly muddy, always shaking water from their coats, always underfoot. People speak to them as co-workers, not pets.

And there are the herd animals: goatlike, sure-footed, with hooves that drum the root-mats at dawn and dusk. They live in enclosures made of thorn and binding, and they test those enclosures the way water tests a seam. Their keepers read their moods for weather and for trouble. When a herd presses against its living fence for no reason anyone can see, conversation pauses in half the town, because that pressure often means something larger moved nearby.

Birds are everywhere, but they belong to different factions.

- The insect-eaters are tolerated tenants. They nest in woven eaves and under layered leaves, leaving streaks of guano that are accepted as rent. Their sharp, quarrelsome calls are the sound of a functioning pest-control system.
- The thieves are at war with everyone. They watch drying racks with bright, calculating patience. People throw pods, wave smoke, rig fluttering strips of bark-fiber, and still lose food.
- The marsh-stalkers own the pools. Long-legged, slow, violent when they strike. They don’t fear people much, because people don’t bother them unless they start taking fish or raiding larvae pens.

Some birds have learned the town’s signals: a certain shout means scraps soon; a certain whistle means dogs released; a certain sudden quiet means the outer hedge has shivered in the wrong way.

### The human inhabitants: a wet-city people
When you finally focus on the people, you notice first that they move like those who live on a surface that can betray them.

They step with care—not timidly, but with constant calculation. Hands touch rail-vines automatically. Loads are carried close to the body, wrapped in leaf-fiber that wicks water and can be retied quickly. Everyone has cordage on them: a coil at the belt, a bundle slung over the shoulder, spare ties looped around a wrist. Losing your grip here isn’t just clumsiness; it’s hunger, injury, wasted work.

Clothing is built for moisture rather than warmth: layered plant-fiber that doesn’t collapse when wet, oiled cloth collars, hoods stiffened with resin so they keep a lip against drip. Bare skin is uncommon not because of modesty but because of bites, rashes, and the cold ache of damp that never quite leaves. People smell of smoke trapped under wet canopies, of crushed green, of animal pen, of sour-sweet fermentation from food stores. Their hands are cord-toughened; their nails often broken; their forearms mapped with minor scratches from thorns and lash ends.

You can tell trades by posture and by what their fingers look like.

- The **rootwrights** (whatever they’re called here) have stained hands from sap and resin, and their arms carry the fine tremor of people who tie knots all day and then inspect the tension by feel. They walk the town the way a shepherd walks a hillside—eyes on supports, on swelling moss, on the subtle sag that means a vine-cable is tired. They are listened to when they speak about safety the way other towns listen to masons.
- The **water-keepers** read the slope the way scribes read ink. They clear channels, open and close root-gates, and argue constantly about flow rights—who gets more seep-spring this week, whose terrace must accept overflow tonight. Their authority is practical and therefore endless.
- The **fiber-workers** sit in groups under the roofed corridors, twisting and braiding. Their conversation is as rhythmic as their hands, and their work is never finished because the town consumes cordage the way a fire consumes air.
- The **cultivators**—fern, tuber, vine, fungus—smell like soil and crushed stems. They carry blades that are sharp but not precious, because here a tool is a thing you must be able to drop into mud and recover without despair.

Children grow up inside the maintenance. They learn rules as bodily habit: don’t grab that vine (it’s a support), don’t step there (it’s a sponge-bed), don’t throw stones into that pool (it’s a silt trap), don’t cut unmarked donors. They play on platforms and in green tunnels, but even play has an undertone of apprenticeship: a game of balancing on flexing bridges is also training for crossing in fog; a game of finding hidden pools is also learning where the ground lies.

Elders are not separated from labor so much as shifted within it. The oldest people are often the ones who remember how the town behaved in the last bad slide, the last season of sickness, the last time the springs ran thin. They may no longer climb as fast, but they are consulted before anyone makes a cut that can’t be undone.

The town’s social life is loud because it has to be. Information must compete with rain and insects and the constant chorus of water. People call warnings—about slippery mats, about a herd pushing, about a channel running too high—with the same casual urgency as market banter. News travels as shouted fragments passed along corridors: not elegant, but effective.

Conflict here tends to be about the living infrastructure. A dispute is rarely abstract; it’s anchored to a particular trunk, a particular donor plant, a particular channel that flooded someone’s storage loft. Arguments are settled with demonstrations: someone kneels and shows the strain in a vine-cable by plucking it; someone points to the silt line on a pool’s rim; someone produces the marked binding that proves a donor was authorized. There may be councils, but the slope itself is the ultimate judge. If a decision makes water behave badly, it will be corrected by force of consequence.

### The outer residents: the ones that make the hedge shiver
Beyond the ring of planted density, larger animals pass along the mountain corridor. Most of the time they are only implied: a gap in birdsong, a sudden stirring in the outer vegetation, the dogs’ hackles lifting before any human sees why.

Sometimes the town glimpses them: a heavy-bodied browser that can push through thorn if desperate; a predator that tests the perimeter in lean weather; a boarish thing with a hide thick enough to ignore all but the sharpest stakes. The town does not romanticize these animals. They are not monsters, but they are forces—like runoff, like rot—things you prepare for and then respond to quickly.

The relationship is not dominion; it is boundary maintenance. People reinforce gates, keep certain paths clear, hang scent-deterrents, and post watchers during seasons when migration pressure is high. When something big comes through anyway, they don’t chase it into the wet forest-marsh where footing turns treacherous. They defend the gardens, the herd, the donors, and then they let the corridor do what corridors do.

### Who “lives” here, in the end
If you stand still long enough, it becomes clear that residency in this place is defined less by ownership than by participation in the saturated system.

- The plants live here because they have been shaped into an architecture that still lets them be alive.
- The insects and fungi live here because damp is law and abundance is inevitable.
- The birds live here because roofs and eaves and scraps create niches as surely as cliffs do.
- The small mammals live here because a town is a predictable feast if you’re clever.
- The herd beasts live here because people have made passage into permanence.
- The people live here because they learned to treat a hillside as something you can bargain with—through pruning, binding, drainage, and constant repair.

And under all of it is a quieter kind of inhabitant: the habit of maintenance itself, passed from hand to hand. In a stone city, a wall stands whether you remember it or not. In this green city, forgetting is collapse. So what truly lives here—what reproduces most reliably—is attention: practiced, communal, loud enough to be heard over rain.
