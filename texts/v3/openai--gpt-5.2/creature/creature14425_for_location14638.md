The first thing to understand is that the *district itself* lives there.

Not metaphorically. Not as a pretty way of saying it’s overgrown. The resident with the oldest claim is the knitted mass of root, vine, membrane-leaf, and fungal felt that has learned the shapes of pillars and machines and has kept those shapes the way a body keeps a posture: by constantly, quietly correcting.

It is present in the way warmth is present—distributed, responsive. A bridge-vine thickens over months where traffic is habitual, then thins again if a route falls out of use. A leaf-terrace sags under an unfamiliar load and, days later, has grown a rib exactly where the strain was. When metal sweats oil, there are plants that *lean in* and plants that recoil; the whole district has preferences, appetites, tolerances. Its boundaries are not walls but gradients: more ammonia here, more resin there, a pocket where the air is just a degree too dry and so nothing ambitious will sprout.

If there is any “governance” in the place, it begins as that slow, distributed refusal to be treated as inert. You can bolt a bracket into living wood, sure—but after a season the wood has put its own opinion around the bolt, and now the bracket belongs to the district as much as to you.

Then come the ones that move.

### The innumerable citizens (small enough to be a weather)
Most of the population is the kind you never count because it behaves like a single phenomenon.

The moth-things are not ornaments; they’re the district’s loose, drifting skin. They gather at vents because warmth means fungal bloom, and fungal bloom means a certain sweetness that coats their mouthparts. Their wing-dust is not just dust: it’s spores hitchhiking, oils from machine housings, mineral grit from old rails. When they settle, they seed things. When they panic, they *sandblast*.

The many-legged scavengers—jointed, patient, quick as poured water—are sanitation and logistics rolled into one. They aren’t “tamed,” exactly, but the district has shaped their habits: chew-plants planted near cable runs, salt-lick stones placed where you want them to gather, pheromone-smears on root-streets that make a spill become a road sign. When something drops—fruit, scrap, carcass—they arrive in disciplined lines not because they are disciplined, but because the smell is a command written in a language their bodies understand.

Bats (and the things that pass for bats down here) aren’t just roosting; they’re running the airspace. Their colonies are layered by niche: some are insect trawlers that skim the luminescent canopy, some are heat-lurkers that cling to motor housings and take what comes to them, some are bold enough to pick parasites from larger bodies. Their squeals and clicks aren’t background noise so much as a public announcement system: a change in their chorus tells you which vents are cycling, which clearings are safe to cross without waking something you’d rather stay asleep.

And in the basins, where water appears as a temporary permission, there are the thin amphibians: still as stone until vibration reaches them. They don’t flee from you so much as measure you. They have learned the difference between the tread of a scavenger line and the tread of something that hunts.

You begin to notice that even the “empty” clearings have residents. Lichen films that bruise color when stepped on. Fungal mats that tense, almost imperceptibly, under pressure. Invisible clouds of mites that rise and settle with every warm exhale from a pipe seam. The district’s smallest citizens are its sensors; they are how the place knows what has changed.

### The predators (the ones that make quiet)
There are animals here that don’t fill space with noise; they *remove* noise when they arrive.

They are not singular monsters so much as solutions to abundance. The district produces so much life that something must specialize in cutting cleanly through it.

Sometimes it is a shape along the underside of a leaf-membrane—flat-bodied, patient, moving with the slow certainty of something that doesn’t need to hurry. Sometimes it is a ripple in moss that turns out to be a long, flexible body threading through the root-mat the way a needle threads cloth. Sometimes it is a pause in the moth-weather, an absence that travels.

These predators have learned the industrial edges, too. They know where belts throw off heat and where the ground hum makes small prey careless. They don’t fear metal; they fear sudden light, because sudden light changes the behavior of everything else and makes the hunt unpredictable.

When one passes through, the district holds its breath in sections. Not silence everywhere—machines keep thumping—but a localized thinning of life, as if a crowd has leaned back from an unseen blade.

### The people (few, adapted, never central)
Only after you’ve absorbed the district as an organism do you start noticing the human (or human-adjacent) residents, because they’ve learned the same lesson the machines learned: to survive here, you don’t announce yourself.

They tend to be fewer than you expect for something that calls itself a town. Not because the place is inhospitable, but because the ecosystem is already *full*, and anyone who stays has to earn a niche without displacing too much. Their footprints are careful. Their light is hooded. Their voices—when they use them—are pitched and timed to avoid setting off swarms.

They dress for negotiation: oilcloth and woven fiber, boots with soft soles that don’t transmit a clean vibration through the root-streets. Many wear wraps or masks—not to hide from each other, but because breathing here means breathing spores, wing-dust, and the sweet bite of ammonia. Their gear is a hybrid the way everything else is: copper wire lashed with living tendril, ceramic insulators seated in bark, tools with handles grown to fit a hand and then hardened.

You can sort them by what they listen to.

- **Pump-tenders** live by the thud in their teeth. They know the district’s heartbeat. They keep flywheels oiled just enough—too much and the oil becomes food in the wrong place; too little and the machine heats and attracts the wrong swarms. Their hands are always stained, and their attention is always divided between gauge needles and leaf-pore waves.

- **Rootwrights** (or whatever name they give themselves in a place where names are more function than identity) are gardeners with structural authority. They don’t prune; they *argue*. They train a trunk into a corner by binding it gently over weeks, by applying heat where it wants to bend, by feeding it certain minerals so it thickens where needed. They carry small knives that are more for cutting cordage and fungus than for fighting, because fighting here is a last resort that usually teaches the district to push back harder.

- **Fermenters** keep the vats that blur the line between workshop and habitat. They tend cultures the way other towns tend fires. They know which microbial blooms will eat oil mist, which will make fuel, which will make poison. Their spaces smell sharp and alive, and they are careful with their waste because waste is currency and insult in equal measure.

- **Lampkeepers** don’t “light” the district so much as *shape attention*. They cultivate sap-lanterns, fit leaf-filters over caged bulbs, keep shutters oiled so they don’t squeal. They understand that brightness is a kind of shouting, and shouting summons the wrong neighbors.

- **Handlers** deal with the fauna not as pets but as partners and liabilities. They lay down chew-plants to spare cables. They spread salt where scavenger lines should form. They know the difference between “the bats are restless” and “the bats are warning you.” Their authority comes from familiarity, and familiarity here means scars.

If there’s a hierarchy, it’s less about who commands and more about who can make changes without provoking retaliation from the living architecture. The most respected residents aren’t the strongest; they’re the ones who can introduce a new pipe run, a new terrace, a new vent schedule—and have the district accept it as if it had always been there.

### How they live (in a place that watches by growing)
Their dwellings aren’t blocks or rows; they are pockets: hollows in thickened columns, chambers behind leaf-sheets that bead with condensation, platforms woven into vine-arches where the air is drier and the bat colonies are less likely to shift overhead.

They eat what the district can spare without taking offense: fungus-cakes grown on carefully curated mats, pale tubers pulled from root-braids that are planted specifically to be harvested, insect meal ground fine and flavored with resin and crushed leaf. Meat happens, but it’s opportunistic and often ceremonial—a predator kill scavenged at risk, a carcass portioned quickly before the scavenger lines arrive to claim what they believe is theirs.

Their social rules are written in avoidance patterns:

- Don’t shine bright, not because it’s forbidden, but because it changes the district’s mood.
- Don’t build straight unless you’re willing to watch it become curved.
- Don’t waste heat; heat is a signal.
- Don’t leave a spill unattended; it will become someone else’s territory by morning.

They speak with their hands a lot. They knock on living columns and listen to the answering tremor. They use soft whistles and clicks that don’t cut through the canopy like a shout would. Some of them carry little vibration-makers—tuning-fork devices, ratchets, knuckle-bones on strings—to communicate through the root-mats the way you’d communicate through a wall.

And they *mark* themselves, not for decoration but as a kind of passport: resin rubbed on boots that tells chew-insects “not this one,” oil-smoke rubbed on sleeves that tells moth-things “already tasted,” a strip of specific fungus cloth tied near the throat that seems to make certain bat-colonies tolerate your proximity.

### The oldest mind in the room (whether anyone admits it or not)
Spend enough time there and you start to suspect there is another resident that doesn’t have a single body.

Not a god, not a monster—an accumulated intelligence made of feedback loops: plant response to vibration, animal migration to heat, human habits turning into paths, paths turning into root-thickening, root-thickening turning into where machines can be safely placed, machine placement turning into new heat gradients. A mind you could only build by letting industry and ecology rub against each other for years until the friction became a language.

The people don’t worship it. They can’t afford to. Worship is insistence in a fancy coat.

But they defer to it the way sailors defer to weather. They argue with it, they bargain with it, they learn its tells. They talk about “the district liking” a certain vent schedule, “the district rejecting” a new alloy, “the district being hungry” when scavenger lines get bold and the fungus mats surge.

So what lives there?

A living infrastructure that grew into the vacancy left by mines. A riotous, self-managing animal city that treats warmth and waste as compass directions. A small, stubborn human settlement that survives by becoming quiet enough to be tolerated—and clever enough to make itself useful.

And threaded through all of it, the sense that you are not visiting a ruin reclaimed by nature, but a functioning neighborhood whose true residents are not obligated to recognize you as anything more than another moving pressure in the root-streets, another heat source, another negotiation.
