The first thing living in that pocket is not an animal at all, but a skin.

It wears the machine the way lichen wears a boulder: not as decoration, but as a second interface between environment and structure. On the wettest ribs, where droplets never fully dry, there’s a continuous film—olive to nearly black—that looks like grime until it moves in slow gradients. It’s a consortium rather than a species: algae threaded with bacteria, bacteria threaded with fungi, all of them comfortable with trace metals and warm condensate, all of them feeding on what the place is always making available—vapor, mineral ions, heat. In the dim pulses of blue-green light, the film doesn’t “shine” so much as it organizes; its surface breaks into faint patterns like tide marks, changing after every pressure cycle. When the condensation blooms, the film swells slick and active. When it dries back, it toughens into a varnish that protects whatever is underneath from being eaten too quickly.

In seams where the light lines run warmer, the film gives way to cushions of moss—tight, low, almost architectural. They don’t spread like forest groundcover because there is no ground. They thicken where microtexture catches mist, where a hairline gap offers a grip, where warmth leaks from behind a panel. From a distance they read as soft, but up close they are armored: leaves scaled small and dense, surfaces waxed against constant dripping, roots replaced by a mesh of gripping filaments that pry into microscopic pits in alloy. Their “soil” is a paste of shed exoskeleton, rust flake, and biofilm—everything the pocket refuses to waste, compacted into nourishment.

And on older metal—places where condensate has done its patient alchemy long enough to bring oxides to the surface—there are pale constellations of lichen that are neither entirely natural nor entirely familiar. They look like chalky blooms until you notice their edges are busy, eating. They metabolize corrosion the way other lichens metabolize rock: slowly, chemically, persistently. Their byproducts aren’t just acids and sugars; they lay down a thin ceramic-like residue that can seal micro-pits, a kind of living spackle. They don’t care whether they evolved here or were introduced. They function like a repair layer in an ecosystem that treats decay as an input.

Once that living skin is established, everything else has somewhere to stand.

The smallest animals occupy the boundary conditions: the hair-thin layer where water becomes air, where droplets form and break. In the shallow trays and gutter channels, “crustacean” is almost too generous a word at first glance—animated flecks, translucent grains with jointed legs and constant motion. They filter runoff the way plankton filter a sea, combing the tea-colored water for film-shed, fungal spores, softened composite dust. Under the pulses of light they flash like mica, not because they glow, but because their shells are mineralized with whatever the place provides: iron, manganese, silica. You can tell where a population has been feeding by the faintly cleared lanes on a biofilm mat, as if something has vacuumed it in stripes.

Among them live things that are less like shrimp and more like living seals. Slugs with hard mouths. Larvae with suction pads. Flat-bodied grazers that cling so tightly to metal you’d mistake them for wet stains until they peel themselves free and you see the underside: rows of tiny hooks and a mouth that rasps, not at metal itself, but at the microbial glaze that metal is always wearing. They are the pocket’s lawnmowers, and they keep the film from smothering vents you can’t see.

The amphibian-like creatures are the first ones that feel like “residents” in the way mammals understand the word. They hold territory—not land, but temperature. You find them where warmth is most reliable: pressed belly-flat to plates above active conduits, toes spread wide into suction cups, bodies thin enough that heat moves through them quickly. Their skin is almost invisible except where it isn’t: a faint opalescence, like the inside of a shell, from mineral layers grown in their dermis. They have no eyelids because nothing here ever truly dries, and their eyes, reflective and lidless, don’t blink at you so much as measure you—movement against constant vibration. When the pressure cycle shifts and the rainfall thickens, they don’t flee the water. They reposition into it, mouths opening to catch the fresh runoff, because fresh runoff carries the richest particulate—newly loosened oxides, recently shed microbial cells, the fine nutrition that arrives on droplets.

Insects are everywhere, but they don’t behave like outdoor insects. They behave like maintenance crews.

They run the conduits in purposeful routes: thick-shelled walkers with feet made for slick surfaces, antennae constantly sampling chemical gradients. Some specialize in the warm damp—burrowers that wedge into gasket seams and emerge with mouthparts stained dark from eating the soft, reactive layers that form where composites meet. Others prefer the drier ledges behind ribs where vapor thins for a moment: beetle-analogues with wing covers sealed tight, opening only when humidity rises, when flight is possible without desiccation. Their wings are papery and strong, meant for short hops between structures, not long travel. They don’t need long travel; everything they need is within a chapel’s volume, endlessly recycled.

Predation starts small. There are threadlike hunters that look like cables until they move: pale ribbons laid across drip paths, inert as a piece of insulation, until vibration and scent align and they snap. You rarely see what they are because the pocket makes all movement ambiguous—steam, shadow, water. When they strike, it’s not a dramatic lunge; it’s a brief reconfiguration of the environment. An insect is there, then it isn’t. A droplet chain trembles. The ribbon is gone, retracting into a seam so narrow you can’t find it again.

Some predators hunt not with speed but with architecture. There are traps built out of secretions: thin, clear nets spanning corners where acoustic currents funnel insects into predictable flight lines. The silk is resistant to constant moisture, beading water rather than soaking it. It’s anchored to spots that are always warm enough to keep it flexible. The makers are rarely visible—small, patient arthropods that live inside hollows and extend only what they must into the open, letting the pocket’s constant airflow do the work of delivery.

Higher up, in the hollows of beams and the darker cavities behind translucent panels, the warm-blooded things congregate.

They are not elegant, and they are not solitary. They hang in colonies because the pocket encourages collective heat management: bodies pressed together into a single textured mass, fur or down matted slightly from humidity, faces tucked away to reduce condensation loss. They are small, because a small animal can live on small returns—on the constant, low-grade productivity of biofilm and insects, on the occasional larger catch, on the certainty that warmth will continue as long as the machine continues to breathe.

Some of them are bird-analogues with feet made for inverted perching, claws shaped to hook into perforations. Their wings are short, built for controlled fluttering in tight spaces. When they launch, they don’t cut through air so much as push through it; humidity makes flight feel thick, and the roar makes communication impossible except at close range, so they speak in touch: beak taps, wing brushes, the tremor of a foot against a shared perch.

Others are mammal-like, with sensitive whiskers that read airflow and vibration as much as space. Their ears are small or folded—adaptations against constant sound pressure. They don’t listen for quiet; they listen for changes in the roar: the subtle shift that means a pressure cycle is cresting, the brief thinning that means a vent pathway is open somewhere, the particular harmonic that signals rainfall will increase and insects will climb. They time their foraging to the machine’s breath as reliably as a shore bird times tides.

If you look for a single “top” predator you’ll miss the point, because the pocket doesn’t reward a king so much as it rewards specialists. Yet there is something that feels like an apex presence: not a creature that dominates by size, but by integration.

It’s the thing you keep mistaking for structure.

In certain drip corridors you notice lengths of pale material that could be conduit insulation—smooth, slightly ribbed, fixed in place along a beam. Then you see a faint peristaltic ripple, too slow to be mechanical, too deliberate to be settling. The “cable” is alive, or at least partly alive: a composite organism that lives half in the open and half inside the machine’s seams, feeding on what falls and on what flows. It positions itself where prey must pass, and it also positions itself where the pocket’s chemistry is richest: near the places where water runs uphill, where fields or heat gradients concentrate ions. It can afford to be patient because the pocket is never done producing.

Whether it is native, engineered, or an opportunist that found a perfect niche, it behaves like a line of living maintenance—clearing channels by eating the wrong accumulations, harvesting biomass when it threatens to clog, leaving behind secretions that either discourage further growth or encourage exactly the kind of growth that keeps surfaces wettable and functional. It is predatory, yes, but it is also curatorial in the way a reef predator is curatorial: its hunting shapes the traffic patterns that keep the whole system moving.

And then, beneath all of it, the largest inhabitant is the pocket itself.

The structure breathes. It cycles pressure. It moves heat with intention. It persuades water to crawl where gravity would not carry it. It heals cracks by exuding new material. Those are not just “features” in the background; they are behaviors—slow, tireless, repeating. The animals have learned them the way forest animals learn seasons. The films have adapted to them the way river algae adapt to flow.

Somewhere inside the engineered stone, beyond the chapel-sized cavity, there are organs you can’t see: chambers that exchange heat, surfaces that condense and then re-evaporate, pathways that route moisture like blood. The pocket is one exposed fold of a larger body, and everything living there lives *because* it is part of that body.

If there is an intelligence, it doesn’t announce itself as a mind in the room. It announces itself as constraint: nothing is allowed to be wasted, nothing is allowed to fail in a way that breaks the cycle. Life complies because compliance is profitable. Biofilm turns leached minerals into edible layers. Insects turn edible layers into motion. Amphibians and small fliers turn motion into heat and waste. Waste becomes nutrient again. Even rust becomes shell. Even shell becomes food.

So what lives there is a city of conversions—creatures that have stopped expecting “nature” and started expecting *infrastructure*. They don’t merely survive the machine; they inhabit its rules. They are the damp, warm chorus in the throat of something ancient that never quite stopped working, and that learned—whether by design or by accident—that a marsh is one way to keep a body alive.
