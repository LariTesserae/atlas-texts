What lives in the pocket is not a single community so much as a stacked set of occupations—tenants that take turns being architecture, traffic, weather, and law.

At the very bottom of it all—before there are “buildings,” before there are recognizable animals—there is a skin of life that behaves like mortar.

### The foundation: the films that make stone usable
On the youngest basalt and the pale, frostlike mineral crusts, bacterial and archaeal mats spread in thin, patient layers. They are not lush; they are efficient. Some live on what little organic snow drifts in, turning scraps into more scraps. Others take what the rock exhales—reduced compounds leaking from weak seeps, traces of methane or sulfide so faint they seem imaginary—and convert chemistry into biomass at a pace so slow it looks like stillness.

These films do three quiet, urban things:

- They *soften edges* without rounding them, coating sharpness with slipperiness so larvae can settle.
- They *mark territory* in scent and texture, making certain ledges “right” for certain settlers.
- They *hold grit*, catching mineral dust and shell powder until bare stone becomes something that can be built on.

If you could see the city in time-lapse, the first sign of a new neighborhood would not be a worm or a sponge; it would be a darkening, a slight velvet sheen on fresh rock, a barely-there greenish suggestion near a seep—then a slow thickening into a living primer.

### The builders: organisms that turn food into vertical space
The most obvious residents are the ones that do what coral does in warmer seas: they make three dimensions out of scarcity.

**Tube-worm clusters** rise like thin towers, each tube an exoskeleton of secreted material and trapped mineral grit. They are paler than their hydrothermal cousins, more bone-colored than red, because nothing here can afford flamboyance. The worms themselves are often hidden—withdrawn, conserving—yet their tubes remain as civic infrastructure even when individuals die. Empty tubes become affordable housing for smaller things: amphipods, juvenile snails, brittle stars that thread themselves into the lattices like living rebar.

Around the faint vents and seeps, **chemosynthetic partnerships** form the city’s industrial districts. Worms and bivalves host bacteria inside their bodies the way a building hosts a furnace: not decorative, not optional. Where a seep brightens by even a fraction, you’ll see a change in zoning within weeks—more settlement, denser stacking, a sudden willingness of animals to tolerate one another’s touch.

**Sponges** are the great block-makers. They swell into rounded, shared-wall masses, each one a slow filtration engine. Their surfaces are not smooth; they are crowded with pores, chimneys, folds—micro-alleys where small crustaceans graze and hide. Sponges also behave like time capsules: they trap silt, molts, larval shells, the fine fallout of the entire pocket. They thicken not just by growth but by accumulation, until a sponge isn’t merely an animal but a neighborhood’s archive.

**Bryozoans and other colonial lace-makers** are the fences, screens, and overpasses. They knit into meshes that catch detritus like windbreaks catch snow. When two colonies meet, they don’t “merge” so much as negotiate: one overgrows, one retreats, or both settle into a boundary that becomes, for everything smaller than a finger, a wall.

In places where the ceiling’s underside receives even slightly better light, thin **algal and microbial beards** cling to the ice. They are not a forest; they are graffiti—patches and smears that say *here, the sky is barely edible*. Tiny grazers browse these films the way city animals browse neglected planters.

### The street-life: the grazers, scavengers, and commuters
If the builders are the city’s masonry, the small mobile animals are its motion—its constant, economical traffic.

**Amphipods and copepods** skitter and hover in the boundary layers, using the currents like escalators. They graze microbial films, pick at sponge surfaces, and steal from the filtration streams of larger animals. Many are nearly transparent; pigment is expensive where the only light is tired, and camouflage here is often just “don’t cast a shadow.”

**Isopods and small snails** do sanitation work. They patrol the ledges where dead tissue collects, where a predator’s leftovers snag in lacework. Nothing rots for long in this pocket; if it pauses, it gets eaten into smaller pauses.

**Brittle stars** and **thick-bodied pale sea stars** are the slow public works crews. Brittle stars thread themselves through colonies, arms out like nets, catching whatever the current brings. The heavier sea stars take up positions on overhangs, flexing with deliberation. They are both predator and janitor: patient enough to wait, strong enough to pry, unhurried enough to outlast a neighborhood.

In the open central “plaza,” **small translucent fish** hold themselves aligned with the clearest lanes of current. They look idle, but they are constantly making tiny corrections, reading the water the way a pedestrian reads a crowd. They feed on passing zooplankton and on the sudden, sparkling confetti of bioluminescent disturbances—because in this city, even alarm can be edible.

Threading through all of it are **jelly-like drifters**: ctenophores and small medusae that pulse with restrained light when bumped, as if the city itself briefly inhales. They are not lanterns out of generosity; the glow is a reflex, a byproduct, a startle—yet it becomes part of how other animals navigate. Some predators learn to use it, herding prey into structures where a panicked flash gives away positions. Some prey learn the opposite: to freeze when touched, to deny the city its involuntary streetlights.

### The specialists: the ones adapted to the pocket’s odd physics
A place like this selects for particular talents.

- **Antifreeze blood and slow metabolisms** are common. Many inhabitants can pause—lowering activity to survive lean weeks when detritus arrives thinly or currents shift away from their feeding faces.
- **Contact-senses** matter more than sight. Feelers, lateral lines, pressure sensitivity: the pocket is a map you read with your skin.
- **Attachment strategies** are a form of wealth. Larvae that can stick to barely-coating biofilm, adults that can hold on through an ice-settling tremor—these succeed. The city is built by those who can remain when the ceiling sighs.

You find creatures that seem designed for the seams: wormlike predators that can flatten and pour through cracks; tiny shrimp-like things that can fold their bodies to fit the mesh; fish that can reverse out of alleys because turning around costs too much room.

### The large presences: quiet predators and rare visitors
The pocket has its higher-tier hunters, but even they behave as if noise is taxable.

A **seal-like predator**—or something filling that niche—moves through the “streets” one at a time, using the clear lanes the currents maintain. It does not ram; it threads. Its eyes are built for dimness, its whiskers or facial sensors for reading turbulence, the way a hand reads Braille. It hunts by subtraction: a school tightens, a flash pops, and then there is simply one less body in the water. The pocket closes around the event like it never happened.

There is also the occasional passage of a **ribbon-bodied night-thing**, more suggestion than anatomy: a long, bending strip that can slide through gaps too narrow for rigid bone. Whether it is fish, worm, or gelatinous colony is hard to tell in the filtered light. It moves like an idea of predation—quiet, opportunistic, capable of vanishing behind any screen. It may not live here permanently; it may use the city as a hunting ground the way an owl uses an alleyway: a place where architecture makes prey predictable.

### The city’s true “citizens”: generations
The most faithful inhabitants are not the dramatic ones. They are the lineages that turn catastrophe into urban renewal.

When the ice ceiling sheds a plate and crushes a district, the first to return are the films—then the small grazers that can live on films—then the settlers that can anchor in scraped cleanliness. The ruins become opportunity. Broken tubes become scaffolding. Sponge fragments become new foundations. Dead shells become aggregate. Even a cleared lot is only temporarily empty; vacancy is simply a stage.

Over time, neighborhoods acquire a recognizable social texture:

- **Seep-adjacent blocks**: denser, warmer in metabolic terms, more chemically powered, full of symbioses and fast colonization.
- **Current-facing terraces**: dominated by filter-feeders arranged like crowds leaning into wind.
- **Sheltered back-alleys**: nurseries, where larvae settle, where small animals hide, where the city’s future is stored in crevices.
- **The central open water**: comparatively sparse, a place for transit, courtship, patrol, and the slow drift of the edible.

So what lives there is, finally, a civic machine made of animals: builders that secrete structure, grazers that keep surfaces usable, scavengers that ensure nothing is wasted, predators that keep the traffic honest, microbes that make raw stone habitable, and generations that treat collapse not as an end but as a zoning change.

The pocket feels like a town because it is one: not planned, not spoken into being, but continuously inhabited—its streets made of current, its buildings made of bodies, its history written in layers of mineral grit and the pale, persistent decision to attach.
