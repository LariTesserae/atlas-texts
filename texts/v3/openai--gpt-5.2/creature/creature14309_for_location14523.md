What lives here is mostly not the kind of life that casts a shadow.

At first glance the basin looks sterile—no soil to cradle seeds, no soft green to admit that time has been passing. The surfaces are too intentional, the wetness too managed. If anything lives, it must do so without asking the place to change shape for it. It must take what the basin already offers: stone that sweats, ceramic that beads, plaster that stays cool, darkness that never fully dries, and a constant delivery of sky-water so thin it arrives as film and breath.

So the first resident is the system itself: a body made of thresholds.

It drinks without gulping. It inhales at night, when temperature drops and the air can’t keep what it’s carrying, and it exhales at noon in a faint glare of evaporation that never quite wins. Its “organs” are redundancy—collector into basin into overflow into channel into cistern—so that function persists even as individual parts crack, bloom with salt, or slump. If you want a creature-shaped analogy, it is a slow animal spread out into architecture: all mouth and throat and stomach, the whole basin a tongue laid flat to catch moisture.

And then—because water is never just water for long—come the smaller, stranger occupants, the ones that hide in pores and seams.

### The film-people: microbes that live on almost nothing  
In the hairline joints where moisture wicks up and weeps down again, there are biofilms so thin they don’t announce themselves as “growth.” They aren’t moss; they can’t build plushness without soil and organics. They live as a smear of chemistry: bacteria and archaea using trace minerals leached from stone and ceramic, feeding on minute deposits carried in windborne dust, on the faintest scraps of carbon that arrive in fog and leave as quickly.

You don’t see them the way you see algae. You see them indirectly, as reluctance: a surface that stays damp a little longer than its neighbors, a seam that darkens in a way mineral varnish alone doesn’t explain, a faint slickness inside a covered run where light never reaches but water keeps moving. Their tempo is dictated by condensation. They are active in the hours when the mist is low and the basin is quietly sweating; they go inert when the sun makes the upper surfaces too clean and bright.

They are not thriving. They are persisting—the difference matters here. Persistence is the dominant life strategy in a place designed to be wet but not fertile.

### The salt-flowerers: halophiles in the crust  
Where salts bloom under overhangs and inside drain lips, the crystals look like weather, not biology—frost that formed in the wrong season. But brine concentrates as it creeps, and in those brief phases when a seam is wet with mineral-rich water before it turns to grit, salt-tolerant microbes can ride the edge of saturation.

They live *inside* the bloom: in microcaves between crystals, in brine pockets that appear and vanish as humidity shifts. When rain undercuts the feathering crust and it falls away in sheets, it isn’t just mineral that collapses; it’s an entire temporary neighborhood erased. The next cycle starts again from whatever cells remained in the sheltered roughness of ceramic, or arrived fresh on the wind.

### The iron-writers: organisms that stain without greening  
Along the preferred routes of flow, where stone has developed a dark varnish “like writing,” most of the visible layer may indeed be deposition—calcium, iron, a record of repeated water. But water that runs the same path again and again also builds a stable microclimate: steady moisture, steady mineral feedstock, protection from full drying. In those sinuous tracks, iron-oxidizing and manganese-oxidizing microbes can take up residence, catalyzing stains that read like geology but behave like slow metabolism.

They don’t make the basin look alive; they make it look *used*. Their signature is subtle: a track that deepens in color faster than neighboring routes should, a boundary that sharpens where chemistry alone would blur, an enamel-like sheen in a shadowed channel where the water never quite stops.

### The cistern fauna: dark-water minimalism  
Down through the access ports—past the smell of cold wet stone into chambers lined with primitive plaster—another kind of living becomes possible, because time behaves differently underground. Temperature swings soften. Evaporation is a rumor. Water stands black and still long enough for it to become a habitat rather than a transit.

Here, if life exists, it is sparse and patient: microbes that feed on whatever the surface world leaches down—ash traces, lime, metallic ions, the occasional organic speck that managed to arrive at this altitude and survive the brightness. If anything larger lives, it is the sort of smallness that doesn’t require a food web to be visible: tiny grazing protozoa, perhaps, or dormant hardy micro-animals carried in as eggs and revived when conditions suit—organisms that can spend most of their existence as dry possibility and only briefly as moving body.

Nothing here breeds in abundance. Nothing here makes noise. The cisterns are not ponds in the usual sense; they are reservoirs of *potential*, where biological activity is measured in chemical shifts rather than swarms.

### The occasional visitors: things that don’t stay  
The basin’s openness means the wind can deliver almost anything—spores, insects lost to altitude, a bird blown off course. But there is nowhere for most visitors to convert arrival into residence. No soil to dig. No vegetation to eat. No sheltered warmth. A traveler can drink, can rest on a capstone, can die unnoticed in a channel and be dissolved into the system so completely that it becomes chemistry before it becomes carrion.

That, too, is a kind of occupancy: the basin accepts bodies the way it accepts fog—briefly, and then as components.

### The true dominant resident: process  
If you stand still long enough, what you begin to recognize as “inhabitant” is the set of repeating actions that the place performs:

- condensation netting itself onto porous fins;  
- droplets ticking down glazed basins;  
- salts assembling delicate architectures and then shedding them;  
- stone swelling microscopically with cold wet, shrinking with bright cold;  
- frost finding seams and practicing its wedge-work;  
- water choosing, again and again, the same routes and thereby teaching the basin its own future erosion.

In most landscapes, life makes the rules visible—growth, decay, reproduction. Here the rules are visible without those comforts. The basin is inhabited by the insistence of water and by the minimal, opportunistic life that can cling to insistence without demanding richness.

If the builders ever meant this place to support gardens, forests, herds—anything that would make “ecology” familiar—they left too soon, or designed too well. What remains is an engineered wetness that refuses to become lush, and a thin, nearly invisible biosphere that refuses to be completely absent.
