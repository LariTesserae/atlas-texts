What lives here is what can live without *taking bites*.

At first it looks like a botanical monopoly: frond-forests, rope-curtains, slicks, lattices—green mass converting heat and dissolved minerals and whatever bruised light survives into more of itself. But the longer you stay with it, the less “plant” feels like the right word. The living things here don’t merely tolerate the drowned machinery and the unstable physics; they use them as organs. And the machines—still spending power, still making weather—have stopped being background. They have become habitat in the old sense: a thing that houses, shapes, and is shaped by what inhabits it, until you can’t say which is host and which is tenant.

### The broad bodies: fronds, curtains, and floating roots
The largest life is architectural.

Kelp-like blades anchor wherever there is hardness to bite into—basalt, limestone, concrete, corroded steel—and they do it with holdfasts that look less like roots than like poured sealant. They spread in fans and counter-fans as if they’re trying to triangulate the currents. In a surge they bow together, not randomly, but in coordinated sweeps that feel rehearsed: the whole stand tilting, flattening, recovering, as though the water’s violence is a language they’ve had time to learn.

Between these stands hang the curtains: fibrous growths braided into ropes and sheets. Up close you see that each “rope” is a composite—dozens of hair-thin strands clinging to each other by mucus and static and sheer pressure, splitting and recombining whenever turbulence tears them. They don’t resist damage the way land plants do; they treat tearing as a normal state. A ripped edge is simply a new edge, quickly lacquered, quickly thickened.

And then there are the midwater lattices, the mangrove-analogues that shouldn’t be possible without air. Pale filaments, chalky and semi-translucent, knitting into floating grids that drift until they snag on a cable or a catwalk rail. Once caught, they tighten and elaborate, turning a simple line into a scaffold, a scaffold into a room, a room into a maze. They make “ground” where there is none, and they are the reason the place feels crowded even in open expanse: not because bodies swim through it, but because structure does.

### The thin skins: films, slicks, and mineral breath
On every surface—plant, rock, machine—there is a second life that behaves like a skin.

Microbial films lay down iridescent sheens that flash and vanish with the angle of light, as if the water briefly remembers oil. In mineral-leaking fractures these films thicken into velvets, drinking the milky plumes and precipitating them back out as crust. Where the water turns syrup-thick, the films become denser too, forming coherent membranes that don’t quite qualify as walls but can hold a straight edge for minutes at a time.

They are not scavengers in the animal sense. Nothing in this region eats by chasing or chewing. These skins live by contact: absorption, exchange, catalysis. They convert dissolved metals, warmth, voltage, and the steady violence of mixing into more film. If there is any “digestion” here, it happens at the scale of molecules and charge.

Sometimes, around a warm housing or an electrified conduit, you can see the film arrange itself in deliberate textures—ripples at right angles, concentric arcs, fine hatching like the grain in wood. It looks decorative until you notice it repeats across different structures with the same spacing, as if the living skin is reading fields you can’t see and rendering them into pattern.

### The amber flash: living fracture
The strangest moment is when damage reveals the inside.

A frond torn by a sudden pressure gradient exposes that warm amber tissue—briefly luminous not by shining, but by *holding* light the way a split crystal holds a sunbeam. In those seconds you can see internal channels: not veins, exactly, but runnels and capillaries threaded with darker fluid. The glow fades as the tissue darkens and seals, and the plant returns to being just another black-green slab in the dim.

That flash tells you something important: these organisms are built for interruption. Their bodies assume they will be cut, shredded, rethreaded. The “individual” is not the blade; it’s the capacity to keep reorganizing into blade-shapes.

### The machine-lichens: growth that treats electricity as weather
Closest to the industrial remnants, the living things change their manners.

Around transformer boxes, seized valves, and drowned cabinets, the growth becomes tight and intentional. It forms rosettes with clean radial symmetry, spirals that match bolt-hole spacing, bands that hug seams and edges as if seams are nutrient lines. It makes collars around cables. It thickens at junctions. It avoids some alloys and adores others.

Here the inhabitants behave like electro-trophs: organisms that harvest not just light and chemistry, but field and leak and stray current. A conduit that arcs blue-white inside its flooded jacket will, days later, be wrapped in denser growth; a pump housing that warms by a few degrees will sprout a more luxuriant coat. The plants treat heat leaks and electromagnetic hum the way a forest treats sunlight and rain-shadow.

If you watch long enough you start to suspect feedback. The more growth there is, the more it changes flow; the more it changes flow, the more the machines strain; the more the machines strain, the more heat and turbulence and cavitation they produce; the more turbulence there is, the more fragments the growth can cast off to colonize elsewhere. The machines are not caretakers, and the plants are not parasites. They are coupled systems, locked in a loop that neither needs to “intend” for it to continue.

### The distributed resident: a nervous system made of pressure and hum
There is still, despite all the abundance, a profound absence of the familiar: no eyes, no darts, no hungry trajectories through the water. Nothing here turns its head toward you because nothing has a head.

And yet the place does not feel unobserved.

The closest thing to an “inhabitant” in the animal sense is the *pattern* that repeats through everything: growth clustering around certain frequencies of sound, around certain intensities of vibration, around certain geometries of current and flow. The vegetation is thickest where the pumps cycle, where cables hum, where turbulence folds the water into standing waves. In those regions the fronds don’t merely survive the noise; they seem to use it. They align with it, flatten under it, spring back into it, as though pressure is a medium for coordination.

It’s possible to imagine the whole drowned network—machines, cables, films, fronds, lattices—as a kind of distributed body whose signals are not nerve impulses but gradients: temperature shifts, salinity bands, resonant clangs, braided currents. The “thought,” if that word can be stretched this far, would not be centralized. It would be something like weather is: local, continuous, responsive, and without a single seat of self.

### Reproduction without mouths: how life spreads here
Life in this region doesn’t need predation to make room. The water makes room.

A surge tears a stand of blades into confetti. The confetti doesn’t become food for fish; it becomes seed. Fragments lodge in grates, wrap around rails, snag on cables. Films colonize the fresh torn surfaces, sealing them and converting the damage into new attachment points. Slurries of rot don’t attract scavengers; they thicken into nutrient clouds that the next wave smears across everything like paint.

The closest thing to “migration” is what the storms do: rolling mats of vegetation like green smoke along the bottom; lifting lattices and draping them across new pylons; redistributing spores and grit until yesterday’s bare platform is today’s garden and tomorrow’s scoured slab again.

### What, finally, lives here
If you insist on a roster, it looks like this:

- Vast, storm-trained phototrophs and chemo/electro-trophs—fronds, ropes, sheets, and lattices—built to be torn and to keep going.
- Omnipresent microbial skins that act as both glue and metabolism, turning minerals, heat, and electricity into structure.
- A half-ruined industrial system that still cycles power and water, no longer as human utility but as ongoing climate.
- And the emergent behavior that arises when those three things are inseparable: a living infrastructure that grows toward hum and warmth, that uses turbulence as dispersal, that “responds” without sensing in any way we’d easily name.

Nothing with a mouth lives here. So nothing hunts, nothing flees, nothing ever arrives as a single body with a single agenda.

Instead the region is inhabited the way a reef is inhabited—except the reef is made of cables and basalt and pump-houses, and the coral is made of plants that learned to eat voltage and vibration, and the tide is a machine that never quite stops deciding what the world should look like next.
