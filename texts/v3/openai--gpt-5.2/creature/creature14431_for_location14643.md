What lives here is what can tolerate a life in the middle state: not fully dry, not fully submerged; not fully wild, not fully domesticated; not fully aboveground, not fully in the drains. The plateau doesn’t allow a single kind of inhabitant to dominate for long. It selects for those who can be maintained.

### The people: citizens of upkeep

They are not, at first glance, a “mountain” people. They don’t move with the airy confidence of high altitudes. They move like dockworkers on an inland sea: careful with footing, practiced around slick metal, always aware of what a handrail feels like before trusting it. Most carry a small cloth or a strip of chamois without thinking—wipe the condensation, test for oil, check for mineral grit. In a city where everything sweats, touch becomes a kind of reading.

Their clothes are built like the city is built: layered, vented, easy to repair.

- Outer coats are waxed or oil-treated, patched more than they’re replaced. The patches are a quiet autobiography—where you knelt on slag, where a valve bit you, where a catwalk grate tore cloth instead of skin.
- Boots have soles designed for algae-slick stone; some have iron studs that can be turned out or in depending on district.
- Many wear thin gloves even indoors, because the metal is always cool and always wet, and because a bare hand on the wrong pipe can mean a burn or a rash.

They are loud when they speak close, and sparing with words at distance. A lot of communication is gesture: a two-finger point for “pressure,” a flat palm for “hold,” a thumb jerk for “clear seep.” In the industrial arc, the sirens are not just public signals but punctuation in conversation. People pause instinctively, let the tone pass through the air, then continue—as if the city itself has to finish its sentence before anyone else can.

And their social divisions are less about wealth than about what kind of water you’re trusted with.

- **Seep-keepers**—the ones who tend weeping rock faces, the sand beds, the ration ledgers—are treated with a wary respect. They have a reputation for being calm, exact, and impossible to bribe, because everyone knows a bribe is just thirst delayed.
- **Pumpwrights and valve crews** are the city’s musculature. They’re loud-voiced, broad-shouldered from wrench-work, and they have a kind of dark humor that doesn’t travel well outside the plateau. They measure time in shifts and pressure cycles, not days.
- **Condensate hands** work the warm breath of the place: cleaning fins, clearing mineral crust, harvesting heat. Their skin often carries a faint mineral sheen that never quite washes away.
- **Blackwater wardens**—canal minders, pond-watch, lining inspectors—live under stricter rules than almost anyone. They are feared a little, because they are the ones who say “no” to convenient dumping, and because they carry the city’s dirtiest knowledge in their notebooks and their noses.

There are also those who live in the interstices: the small traders who sell gasket material and salvaged rivets; the night-menders who can patch a steam line with half the tools it should take; the quiet, stubborn householders who grow something green in a sheltered court using warm condensate like it’s contraband.

If there is a faith here, it’s not worship of an unseen power. It’s reverence for balance. People leave offerings in practical forms: a new filter cloth tied to a rail near a seep face; a bit of fat or scrap meat dropped into a drain not because anyone “believes” in the drain-things, but because nobody wants to be the fool who finds out the old superstitions were really just ecology.

### The city’s other citizen: the maintenance caste that isn’t human

Not robots in the clean, futuristic sense—nothing here stays clean long enough for that fantasy—but a whole ecology of semi-autonomous work that has become as ordinary as rats in other places.

In the newer districts there are small rail-bound inspection carts that trundle along pipe runs at night, tapping lines and listening for changes in tone. In older quarters, it’s cruder and stranger: wind-up crawlers used in drains, their casings plated against mineral bloom; wheeled lamps that follow embedded guide-cables through steam-haze, more reliable than human eyes during *the drift*. They aren’t “intelligent,” but people speak about them the way you speak about stubborn animals: *that one likes to jam in the west culvert*, *this model hates calcite lace*, *if you oil the axle it’ll behave*.

The consequence is a city with a second workforce that never clocks out. You can stand on an empty catwalk at 3 a.m. and hear, under the big pump-thrum, the tiny industry of these things—clicks, little motors, the occasional scrape as a sensor arm drags through wet grit. It’s one more layer of the place’s refusal to stop.

### The animals: edge-dwellers, drain-dwellers, hanger-on

Large animals don’t thrive in the constant noise, the broken habitats, the chemical rules. What lives here lives by dodging.

- **Marsh birds** at the margins are the most visibly “wild.” They come in at dawn and pull back by full daylight, thin-legged and watchful, making use of the reed basins that clean runoff. They nest where fences meet mud—where the city’s boundaries accidentally make shelter.
- **Feral cats and dogs** are not numerous, but they’re persistent. Cats own the warmer understructures: condenser yards, foundry eaves, the sheltered voids beneath reservoir rafts. Dogs haunt the rail spines and slag fields, living off dropped food, small prey, and whatever a kind person pretends not to notice.
- **Insects** are pale here—dust-colored, mineral-dusted, heat-loving. They breed in the warm damp of louvered sheds, around gasket stacks, in the fibrous insulation people are always replacing. They don’t swarm so much as *occupy*, a constant low population that never disappears.

And in the drains, where the city’s warmth and wetness are most honest, there are the things drain-workers don’t describe in detail unless they trust you.

Slick amphibians, yes—some nearly eyeless, some with eyes like cloudy pearls that seem useless until a lamp hits them and they flinch. They cling to algae mats on brick and stone like living sealant. They eat what grows where condensate meets mineral seep: biofilms, tiny invertebrates, whatever falls through a grate. They are not monsters. They are just perfectly adapted to a world of warm dark water and constant vibration, and that perfection makes people uneasy.

It’s not uncommon for a drain crew to have a story about one that followed them for half a tunnel, keeping pace in the gutter flow, as if curious about the noise and light—an animal doing what animals do, and a human mind insisting on omen.

### The plants and the “green” that isn’t quite plant

Nothing leafy dominates. This is not a city you’d describe as alive by its trees.

Life here expresses itself as coatings, fringes, and stubborn punctuation.

- Mosses and algae paint the stones wherever warm seep persists—green-black smears, slick and shining.
- Tuft grasses take any crack that offers wind shelter and a trace of grit to root in.
- In the settling basins, where mineral water drops its load, delicate chalk spires rise that look like coral. They aren’t alive, but they become habitat: for insects, for microbial mats, for the small creatures that feed on those mats.

The most successful “flora” may be microbial: mineral-eating bacteria that leave rust-blooms on walls; slime layers that thicken in quiet corners; the invisible communities that decide whether a channel smells merely wet-rock or turns sour and metallic. People learn these smells the way sailors learn weather. A veteran can tell you from a breath whether a duct is going to clog with calcite in a week.

### Where they live, within the living city

Different districts breed different kinds of resident.

- In the **old quarter**, people live close to the drains and vaulted channels, because that’s where heat is reliable and repairs can be made with old knowledge. Families there keep tool sets the way other places keep heirlooms. They know the sound of their neighborhood’s drip patterns, and they can tell if something is wrong because a familiar echo has changed.
- In the **industrial belt**, life is shift-based. Homes are compact, often stacked on stone rafts above saturated ground. Walls are lined with drying racks for damp clothes and shelves for spare valves. Children learn early that a siren means *move away from that street*, not *be afraid*.
- On the **upper terraces**, where the roar softens, live reservoir attendants, ledger-keepers, and those with the patience for slow work: people who can watch water levels, read gauges, clean grit from filters one careful handful at a time. These are quieter neighborhoods, but not serene—more like a library built over a boiler room.

And on the **outskirts**, where the plateau gives way to marsh-edge basins, there are the reed-tenders and fence-menders who treat the water-gardens like both utility and sanctuary. They’re the closest thing the city has to rural folk, though even their “fields” are engineered.

### What the place makes of its inhabitants

Over time, the city changes its residents in small, cumulative ways.

Their hands become competent: callused where they grip, scarred where hot metal kissed them through a moment of carelessness. Their hearing becomes tuned to layers; they can pick out a failing bearing inside a symphony of machinery. Their sense of direction becomes partly auditory, partly tactile—*this railing is always colder*, *this bridge always hums at that pitch*, *this stair is polished in the middle step because everyone favors their right foot here*.

They become conservative not in politics but in practice. Waste is shameful because the plateau won’t hide it. Neglect is a kind of crime because moisture turns neglect into collapse. Pride is expressed through things that keep working: a pump that runs clean, a gutter that drains true, a household’s little condensate garden that keeps a thin line of green alive against all expectation.

And during *the drift*—when steam sheets slide down streets like slow tides—another trait emerges: collective caution. People don’t rush in the drift. They wait it out in doorways, lean against warm stone, listen for footsteps and cart wheels, call out short warnings that echo oddly close. In those hours the city feels most like a single organism: valves sighing, vents breathing, citizens pausing like cells until the visibility returns.

So what lives here is not one thing but a layered citizenship: humans organized around maintenance and water-law; animals and drain-things adapted to warmth, wetness, and vibration; thin plants and thick biofilms that take the niches left by steel; and a ceaseless background workforce of mechanisms that have become as ordinary—and as temperamental—as any living neighbor.

In a place that never fully dries, life doesn’t spread outward. It adheres. It persists. It keeps working.
