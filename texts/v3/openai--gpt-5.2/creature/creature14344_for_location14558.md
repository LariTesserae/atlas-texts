What lives there is, at first, not a roster of species so much as a way of being alive in a place that has no edges.

The oldest residents are not the ones that move.

They are the slow masons: reef-building corals that have never “seen” the city as a whole, only the local truth of current over their skin. They live as skin, as lattice, as crust. A coral colony begins as a dusting of larvae that find a corner where flow delivers enough food and not too much sand; then it thickens into a lip, a lip into a ledge, a ledge into a buttress that redirects the water and makes new corners that invite new larvae. Their lives are architectural, but not in the human sense of planning—more like the way a river plans a bend by obeying its own forces for long enough. They are fed by the rain’s endless stirring of the surface-world above: fine organics, dissolved minerals, microscopic life, all delivered downward and kept from settling into stillness by the city’s breathing currents.

On them—inside them, through them—live the other permanent citizens: sponges with pores that turn the avenues into lungs, feather worms that bloom and vanish with a twitch, bryozoans like mineral lace, and tunicate draperies that look like cloth until they contract and you remember they are bodies. These organisms don’t “occupy” real estate; they *are* real estate. Their jobs and their homes are the same structure. A sponge thickens a wall and also eats. A coral strengthens a pillar and also breeds. The city’s stability is not an engineering feat but a metabolism.

Between those living stones, the ground level is busy in a way that feels municipal.

Crabs patrol the shell crescents and silt plazas with a purpose that is half hunger, half habit: pick, test, discard; pick, test, discard. Brittle stars thread themselves through shell gravel like many-fingered thoughts. Sea cucumbers and worms process the streets themselves, taking in sediment and leaving it cleaner, grain by grain—sanitation as digestion. In the quiet basins where silt drifts high, burrowers work from below: shrimp that excavate and ventilate tunnels, small fish that share those tunnels as tenants, and creatures you almost never see whole—only the faint puff of a disturbed plume and the quick disappearance of a hole that wasn’t there a moment ago.

Midwater is where the city begins to feel crowded.

Schools move through corridors the way pedestrians use covered streets: not because a corridor is “named,” but because the walls break the current into predictable lanes. Small fish travel as a single organism made of many bodies, turning corners without touching, compressing through narrow passes, then blooming outward into plazas where the flow slackens. They are watched constantly by the next layer up—hunters that know the architecture the way locals know shortcuts. Eels take the alleys: fissures in limestone, shadow-lines under coral shelves, gaps where your hand would expect a handle if this were a door. They don’t merely hide in these spaces; they *belong* to them, as if the stone were shaped to the eel rather than the eel to the stone.

The city’s cleverness—if you can call it that—doesn’t announce itself with monuments. It shows up in the tenants that rearrange.

Octopuses are the nearest thing to householders. Each one claims a doorway: an arch under a ledge, a hole with good sightlines, a pocket where the current brings food without bringing too much sand. Around that entrance, it builds a personal border—shells gathered into a partial barricade, pebble piles that function like thresholds, sometimes a neat scatter of refuse pushed to one side as if aesthetics matter. Their “neighborhoods” are not social in a human way, but there is a kind of distributed etiquette: dens spaced so that hunting territories overlap without constant conflict; detours taken around a neighbor’s door when the neighbor is in; moments of sudden color and posture that read like arguments conducted in a language of skin.

Cleaner organisms run businesses without knowing it.

Little fish and shrimp set up stations at ridges where traffic is heavy. Larger bodies—groupers, rays, even sharks that should be all appetite—pause and hold still, presenting gill slits, mouths, scar lines. The cleaners work fast, their movements precise, and in exchange they are not eaten. It’s one of the city’s many treaties: not moral, not sentimental, simply stable. The architecture helps enforce it; a cleaning station tucked into a notch has escape routes only small bodies can use, so the clients literally cannot follow through on betrayal without scraping themselves raw on the walls.

Light is scarce, so other senses become civic infrastructure.

Scent travels like gossip, slow but thorough, clinging to eddies and corners. Vibrations move through the reef ridges as if the whole city were a drum head. When something large passes beyond the districts, the water carries its low note into every corridor; small fish pause, crabs flatten, filter feeders retract. Everyone learns to read that note: the difference between a harmless migration and a hunting patrol, between a distant current-shift and a sudden pressure wave that means impact.

And then there is the signage: bioluminescence, used without being *used*.

Some of it is incidental—bacteria glowing when disturbed, comb jellies dragging slow rainbows across the dark, shrimp punctuating the streets with cold sparks. Some of it is deliberate: flashes that coordinate a school’s turn, pulses that confuse a predator, glows that lure prey into a mouth waiting in a crevice. In the deepest corridors the city becomes briefly legible only when something moves: a predator’s wake writes a constellation; a fleeing crowd becomes a streaming script. Nothing hoards this light. It can’t be stored, only spent, and that makes it feel honest in a place where almost everything else is muted.

Higher up, along the open ridges where corridors widen, the large residents patrol.

Rays skim the shell-tiled streets like maps they’ve memorized, their bodies so close to the ground they seem to iron the city flat. Sharks pass through the civic bowl beyond the dense blocks, not frantic, not theatrical—just present, the way a law is present. Turtles drift on slow errands between feeding patches and quieter refuge. Some fish grow old enough here to look ceremonial: thick-bodied groupers that hold court in a particular intersection of currents, lionfish moving like dangerous banners, barracuda that hang motionless in midwater until the moment they are suddenly elsewhere.

There are also the anchored drifters—the “streetlamps” that aren’t lamps.

Siphonophores fix themselves into crevices and extend their pale bulbs into the flow, tuned to the current like instruments. They are colonial animals pretending to be single things: many mouths, many stinging threads, one drifting intention. Planktonic life gathers around them; small predators gather around that; larger predators know the route well enough to swing wide. Around these living poles, traffic patterns bend. The city has intersections made of hunger.

In the quietest pockets—places where the rain-roof’s dimness thickens into near-black and the water holds still for just long enough—odd specialists make their living.

Gelatinous animals drift like slow weather. Transparent predators, almost invisible until the moment their bodies outline the faintest glow of swallowed light. Things that never touch the stone at all, born to the suspended middle and dying there, their bodies falling at last into the streets as food. The city’s energy arrives “sideways,” and so does its death: everything eventually becomes a nutrient delivery for something else, and the architecture is designed—by physics and time—to catch it.

If there is a ruler here, it isn’t a single creature.

It’s the tide-cycle itself: corridors that run one way and then the other, on schedule old enough to feel like law. Every resident that can move learns to time their travel to it. Those that cannot move learn to shape themselves to it—opening, closing, thickening, flexing. When the current reverses, anemone fields lean the other way like a crowd turning its head. Filter feeders change their posture. Small fish that looked settled abruptly become commuters, pouring through the same pass in the opposite direction as if the city has declared a new hour.

And underneath all of this—beneath coral, beneath shell, beneath silt—there are the unseen residents that make the whole place possible: microbes that turn rot into food, bacteria that build films on every surface, tiny lives that eat chemical gradients the way a surface world eats sunlight. They are the city’s quiet economy, endlessly converting the rain’s delivered scraps into a currency everyone else can use.

So the city is inhabited the way a reef is inhabited, and also the way a town is inhabited: by builders who are also buildings; by scavengers who keep the streets passable; by commuters that move with the tides; by householders with doorways and boundaries; by patrols that enforce an order no one wrote down.

In the permanent warm dark under the rain-ceiling, “who lives there” is less a list than a relationship: bodies shaped by corridors, corridors thickened by bodies, all of it breathing with the water’s slow in-and-out—an interior world crowded with lives that have learned to navigate not by landmarks, but by flow, by contact, by chemical trace, and by the brief, involuntary honesty of light.
