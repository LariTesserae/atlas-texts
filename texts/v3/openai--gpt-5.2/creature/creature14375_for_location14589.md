What lives here comes in layers, like the floor does—some of it obviously alive, some of it only alive by metaphor until you watch it long enough and the metaphor stops feeling optional.

At first it seems like a place inhabited by **systems** rather than bodies: the pulse-light audits, the stress-lattice groans, the scheduled mist. The city’s most consistent resident is the thing that refuses sudden failure. It keeps accounts in vibration and humidity. It is always awake in the way a heart is awake.

Then you begin to notice the smaller lives that have learned to make their meals out of byproducts.

## The margin-ecology: things that live off warmth, dust, and scheduled water

In the cable trays and behind access panels there are **pale arthropods** that never step onto open walkway unless the pulse has just passed and the electromagnetic shift has left a brief aftertaste they can follow. They eat biofilm, insulation rot, skin flakes, the faint salts that collect where metal sweats. They move in quick, purposeful bursts—never a wander, always a commute between known shelters.

There are **moths** with wings so thin they look like packaging film. They do not chase light; they chase *gradients*. You see them hovering where warm airflow meets cold rock, slipping along boundary fields the way fish slip along invisible currents. When the ducts mist the cavern, they vanish into cracks, and minutes later return in a different place, as if they’ve learned a private map of pressure corridors.

In alcoves left “accidentally” illuminated for years, the **thread-moss** and translucent fronds form a kind of reluctant understory. The engineered regularity in their cells isn’t decorative; it’s adaptation. They thicken when the air goes too wet, thin when minerals run high. They aren’t quite plants and aren’t quite filters, and the city treats them as both: tolerated where they help, scraped back where they begin to claim too much metal.

And sometimes—rarely enough that you doubt yourself until you see the slick track again—there is the **long-bodied scavenger**. It stays close to the deep void where the handrail lights don’t reach. It moves with a continuous smoothness, no visible start-and-stop of limbs, as if its bones are more like springs. It eats what falls: a dead moth, a clump of moss pried loose, the occasional piece of food-waste that got past policy. The city permits it in the way the city permits darkness: by controlling edges rather than trying to eradicate the center.

None of these creatures form a stable, sunlit food web. They form a **maintenance web**—life that survives on the leftovers of engineering, like mold survives on the idea that a house is dry.

## The machines that are not merely tools

The **drones** are the most visible inhabitants at human scale, but they don’t behave like a fleet controlled by a single hand. They behave like civic wildlife: appearing at the same hours, using the same routes, reacting to the same signals.

Some are tiny, almost insectile—inspection units that cling to undersides of spans and read stress like braille. Others are heavy enough to make the walkway’s composite complain, yet the span never sags; the drone rolls over it as if it trusts a promise you cannot see.

They have habits:

- They gather where condensation gutters are richest, not because they drink, but because *water predicts other problems*: mineral bloom, corrosion, biofilm.
- They pause at kiosk shells and doorways that open onto darkness, as if remembering an older route-plan.
- During the city-wide “blink,” they move more openly, almost bold, taking advantage of the brief expanded illumination to cross gaps they usually avoid.

A few carry faint markings—paint rubbed thin, symbolic rather than serial, the sort of mark a person would add to say *this one is mine*, or *this one listens to me*, or simply *this one came back*.

And the boundary fields themselves feel like a kind of inhabitant. Not conscious, perhaps, but responsive. Dust draws their planes into visibility; paper skates along them; the roar of ventilation goes quiet as you approach, as if the city’s air has learned manners.

## The people: thinned, distributed, living in pockets

If there are people here—and there are—the city does not present them the way a surface city would. You don’t meet crowds. You meet **signs of occupancy** first: a railing with a fresh wipe-mark through its mineral dullness; a strip of absorbent panel newly pinned behind a workbench; a faint smell of warmed food in a corridor that otherwise smells only of stone and hot metal.

Then, in the edge-light near an access node, you see one.

They dress like the place asked them to: layered fabric that doesn’t hold damp, matte coatings that don’t flash reflections into darkness, soles that soften footfall. Many wear something at the throat or jaw—not a mask against poison, but a dampener against *echo*: a collar of foam-fine material that changes how their voice leaves their body.

They do not speak loudly. Some don’t speak at all unless necessary. Conversation here is a discipline, because sound carries wrong and comes back late. They communicate in:

- **tightbeam bursts** (brief, direct, private light or radio)
- **gesture**, compact and practiced
- **contact codes** on railings and panels: a tap-pattern, a magnet-mark, a smear of conductive paste that a glove can read

They live in **cells** built into the terraces and underhangs, not because they need protection from collapse—collapse is the one thing the city refuses—but because they need protection from *the volume*. Rooms are small on purpose. The ceiling is close. The walls are lined with absorbent material so you can hear your own breath and believe you still occupy a body.

Their homes look temporary until you realize the temporariness is part of the long-term plan: modular panels that can be peeled away, seals that reknit after opening, furniture that bolts to stone and unbolts without leaving scars. No one here wants to make anything that cannot be removed cleanly. The void is not only space; it’s future capacity, reserved like water in a cistern.

### What they do all day (and all night)

They are, broadly, **caretakers**, but not in the romantic sense—more like people who have accepted a civic contract with a machine that never stops asking for attention.

- Some are **condensation tenders**, cleaning porous foams, replacing mineral-choked lips, tracing hairline leaks by taste and smell.
- Some are **fieldwrights**, the ones who walk the boundaries of boundary fields, calibrating pressure partitions, keeping mist out of electrical bays, keeping warmth from pooling where it would encourage unwanted growth.
- Some are **stress listeners**, trained to tell the difference between a harmless groan and a warning note. They carry contact microphones and small hammers; they tap spans and hear the return like a doctor hears lungs.
- Some are simply **deep commuters**: this thinned city is a hub, a pause-point between lower layers. They stay for a few shifts, sleep in a rented cell, then descend again.

And a few—harder to categorize—seem to be here for the emptiness itself. They take long walks along the terraces that overlook the void, stopping at rails that “don’t need marking,” looking down as if listening with their eyes. If the place has a religion, it is probably built out of scale and controlled darkness.

### How they remain “few” on purpose

The policy of thinness is not just population decline; it is environmental technique. Too many bodies would add too much water to the air, too much heat to the rock, too much organic matter to the margins where arthropods and molds multiply. A crowd would be an ecological event.

So they practice **distributed living**. Shifts are staggered. Markets are not plazas but scheduled handoffs at access nodes: a crate left in a lit pool, claimed later by someone who never meets the one who delivered it. Even intimacy is shaped by infrastructure—meetings happen in rooms built to contain sound, not in open walkways where the city would overhear.

They leave most terraces empty the way you leave a lung full of air. Not unused—*kept available*.

## The oldest resident: the city as a managed organism

Over time the boundary between “inhabitants” and “infrastructure” blurs, because the city behaves less like a set of machines and more like a managed body:

- The vents are lungs.
- The condensation gutters are sweat-collectors.
- The stress lattice is muscle.
- The pulse-light audits are a blink that briefly reveals the skeleton.

The people who live here adapt to that. They don’t treat the city as background. They treat it as something that can be soothed, provoked, comforted, or made sick.

You see it in small acts: someone pausing before crossing a span, waiting for the hum to settle; someone wiping mineral lace away not for aesthetics but because it “itches” the airflow; someone leaving an alcove light on an extra hour because the fronds unfurl and the humidity stabilizes when they do.

In the end, what lives here is not a single species or a single intelligence. It’s a **compact between void and edge**:

- small, stubborn ecologies feeding on what engineering sheds,
- drones and field-systems behaving like civic fauna,
- a sparse human presence trained into quiet competence,
- and a city that has learned to survive by staying spacious—by using emptiness the way other places use walls.
