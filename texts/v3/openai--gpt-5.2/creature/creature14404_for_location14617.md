What lives on the plateau is not one community but a set of interlocking economies—most of them written directly onto stone.

At first you’d swear it’s a place of shortages: dim light, thin water, scattered plant life. But the longer you watch, the more the plateau’s inhabitants reveal themselves as specialists in what the lowlands barely count as food: mineral films, salt ghosts, microscopic bloom-and-collapse cycles, warmth that leaks upward, and the slow, usable trickle of current through ore.

### The first citizens: skins, crusts, and films

The most widespread life here isn’t tall enough to cast a shadow.

**Lithic biofilms**—dark, slick when they’re fresh—move in and out with the plateau’s damp pulses. On dry weeks they shrink until they’re almost only stain, their metabolism throttled down to a waiting state. When condensation returns, they swell in hours, turning sweat-rock into a living membrane. They are why some stone smells faintly metallic-cold when you touch it: their chemistry is busy with iron and sulfur and whatever trace nutrients the mist delivers.

Over them sit **lichen mats** that don’t so much “grow” as *occupy*. They take the windward faces because they can. Their bodies are thickened against abrasion, pigmented against the plateau’s meager, scattered light, and anchored so stubbornly that the wind can sandblast the rock around them and still not lift them. When they’re wet they look almost black; when dry they go pale and granular, like old ash.

In the conducting zones—ore-veined ridges, damp basins where minerals and biofilm overlap—there are **electrotrophic crusts**: organisms whose trick is not magic, just opportunism. They exploit tiny potential differences along wet mineral seams the way a riverbank plant exploits a trickle of water. On the right nights these crusts can make the plateau’s famous blue crawl—light not for display, but as a byproduct of shuttling electrons where electrons want to go anyway.

The plateau’s “vegetation,” when it rises above ankle height, tends to be **air-drinkers**: waxy-stemmed, leaf-reduced plants that behave like condensation traps. Their surfaces bead willingly; their grooves channel water toward hidden roots. They don’t gamble on rain. They farm the nightly breath of the place.

### The grazers: animals that eat stone’s thin offerings

The herds you see from afar—long-bodied, line-moving—are built for scraping a living from hard surfaces.

They have **broad, quiet hooves** not simply for talus but for vibration: they test stability, they feel the land’s minute shifts, they pick routes that don’t waste energy. Their heads are heavy in front, muzzles plated or callused, teeth edged for rasping. Feeding is not a bite-and-tear action here; it’s *work*: scrape the lichen, lick the salt crust, worry at the damp seam until the tongue comes back dark with biofilm.

Inside them, their true digestion lives: **gut consortia** adapted to mineral-heavy diets. The animals themselves are less “grazers” than mobile fermentation vats carrying entire microbial ecosystems tuned to the plateau’s chemistry. Their droppings are not mere waste; they’re inoculum—dark pellets seeded with spores, bacteria, and salts that restart growth in every gully they pass.

Many of these herd animals have a dull metallic cast—not shiny, never decorative. It’s the look of bodies that have learned to tolerate and even employ trace metals: in keratin, in scales, in enamel. In the right mist, close to an ore seam, you can sometimes hear a faint clicking as a herd crowds in and presses muzzles to the stone—less thirst than *seeking*: warmth, salts, and the subtle parasite-suppressing discomfort of a mildly “alive” surface.

Young are often timed to the plateau’s generous moments. Not seasons, exactly—**conductive weeks** when mist is frequent and biofilms are thick. Calves born then don’t have to travel as far between meals, and the herd’s slow, economical pace can slow further without risk.

### The burrowers: the plateau’s hidden majority

Between the ridges, under the equilibrium slopes of scree, the ground is busy.

There are **pop-up burrowers**—small, quick animals that live most of their lives in the plateau’s thin soils and only surface as briefly as a heartbeat. They come up to clip a tuft, steal a pellet, lap seepwater from a crack, then vanish before wind or predator can price them in energy.

Their tunnels are not random. They prefer soils laced with conductive grains, not because they “understand” electricity but because those soils tend to be **warmer, drier at the right times, and less hospitable to certain molds and mites**. Some species line burrow walls with mineral-rich secretions that harden like a glaze, reducing moisture loss and collapse—tiny ceramics made by instinct and necessity.

A few burrowers have evolved **electroreceptive whiskers or facial pits**, able to sense the slight differences between inert stone and a seam that’s sweating ions. They find water the way other creatures find scent. To them, a damp crack isn’t just damp; it’s luminous in a sense we don’t possess.

### The fliers: things that use the lid

Above, in the dim that never clarifies into full day, flight is common—but rarely flamboyant.

There are small flocking fliers that read as **batlike at a distance** but aren’t necessarily mammals: leathery-winged, insect-winged, or something between depending on the basin. They ride the plateau’s gentle mechanics: warm updrafts along conducting cliffs, pressure gradients where the cloud lid sags into a valley, the soft spill of mist over a rim.

They feed in the air and on the stone.

Some take **aerial plankton**—spores, grit-borne microbes, tiny insects lofted unwillingly by the wind. Others land in quick, clinging intervals to lick biofilm from wet faces, their feet adapted for vertical rock the way shorebirds’ feet are adapted for mud. In heavy mist they can gather in spirals above energized seams, not as worship but as efficiency: warm air costs less wingbeat.

Their roosts tend to be in the plateau’s ribs of strata—protected slots where condensation is reliable but runoff doesn’t flood. When they leave, the stone beneath is marked: pale streaks of mineral-rich droppings that feed the next layer of living film.

### The predators: quiet accountants of movement

Predators here don’t need to be large to be successful. They need to be *economical*.

Some are sleek, low-slung runners with feet that don’t clatter, hunting by patience and short bursts: not a chase across kilometers, but an ambush at a saddle where herds must funnel, a sudden rush when the wind covers sound. Others are cliff-hunters—animals that can hold themselves motionless against a rock face for an hour, then drop with shocking precision.

A distinctive plateau adaptation is **sensory thrift**. In dim light, eyes can only do so much. Many predators lean on vibration through the ground, on scent that clings low under the cloud lid, and—in the conducting zones—on electroreception. A moving body on wet ore-veined stone is an event in more than one sense; it perturbs the local field, it makes a signature. Predators that can “feel” that are hunting with an extra dimension.

Kills are rarely messy. Muscle and fat are scarce enough that nothing can afford waste. Predators eat quickly, cache if they can, and leave behind a carcass that becomes a scheduled appointment for everything else.

### The scavengers and cleaners: those who finish the sentences

Carrion doesn’t call crowds here; it calls a sequence.

First come the **shadow scavengers**—solitary or paired animals that arrive quietly and leave quietly, stripping the most valuable tissues. Then smaller pickers: beaked things, gnawers, insects that appear from nowhere as if the ground had been saving them for this. Finally the true cleaners: microbes and crusts that bloom briefly in the enriched patch, turning a death site into a dark, living stain that will persist long after bone has been carried away.

Bone itself doesn’t linger in a romantic way. The plateau’s cycles erase drama. Salt, wind, and thin water reduce remains to pale fragments and mineral dust that the next damp pulse can carry into a crack, where some patient organism will use it.

### The ore-seam guilds: life that plugs into “industry”

The conducting ridgelines aren’t only scenery—they’re habitat, and some creatures belong to them the way reef fish belong to coral.

You’ll find small animals with **hematite-sheened plates** that press their bodies flat against energized stone during mist events, not merely to warm but to discourage the tiny biting parasites that can’t tolerate the microcurrents. There are also slow-moving grazers—snaillike, beetlelike, or something stranger depending on the region—that feed preferentially where blue light tends to crawl, cropping the electrotrophic crusts that regrow only when the circuit is “closed” by dampness.

Some species time reproduction to these events. Eggs laid in ordinary cracks are risky—too dry, too cold. Eggs laid against a conductive seam during the plateau’s wet hours are incubated by a gentle, persistent warmth and protected by the same chemistry that makes the rock smell like ozone.

Nothing about this is supernatural. It’s simply the plateau’s rules taken seriously by evolution: if the land offers heat and current more reliably than green leaves, then bodies will learn to drink those, too.

### If there are people, they are shaped by the same strictness

If any human or humanlike inhabitants pass through, they do not “settle” in the way lowland minds imagine settlement. The plateau punishes permanence that leaves scars. The most plausible residents would be **seam-followers**: nomads who move gully-to-gully and ridge-to-ridge on the same logic as the herds, camping where sweat-rock and mineral warmth coincide, carrying little that could not be lifted in a day.

They would measure time not by sun but by **condensation**, by the smell of the stone, by the feel of vibration in ore. Their fires, if they used them, would be small and shielded; their tools would favor abrasion-resistant stone and scavenged bone; their paths would be light as wind so the plateau could erase them quickly. They would not build monuments, because the place already *is* a monument to long processes.

And they would be careful around the conducting cliffs—not from fear, but from respect for a living schedule: on certain nights the rock becomes an active thing, and every animal on the plateau behaves as if it knows.

### What “inhabited” feels like here

Stand still long enough and the plateau’s population stops presenting itself as individual creatures and starts presenting itself as flow.

A ridge becomes a feeding line. A gully becomes a corridor of burrow mouths that open and close like blinking. A conducting seam becomes a meeting place—a warm wall, a salt lick, an insect hatch, a parasite barrier, a navigation mark in the dim. Even the cloud lid participates, holding the heat in, rationing light, making the entire continent behave like a slow engine.

What lives here has not conquered the harshness. It has synchronized with it.

Everything is tuned to brief moisture, to cheap warmth, to the thin chemistry that runs when stone sweats and ore wakes. The plateau looks subdued because it wastes nothing on excess. But it is crowded with solutions—quiet bodies, patient metabolisms, and migrations that trace the land’s hidden circuitry as faithfully as rivers trace gravity.
