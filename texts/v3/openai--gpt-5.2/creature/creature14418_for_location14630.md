Life here begins by refusing the old bargain with light.

What feeds the basin is not sun but interface: where warm stone meets cold water, where saltglass sweats its trapped brine, where calcite thread meets a faster current and sheds a dust of ions. The first citizens are not things you can point to at a distance. They’re sheens, breaths, skins.

On the basalt-cast planes there is a film so thin it looks like nothing until a grazer passes and the surface briefly “blushes” dull blue, as if the stone has remembered color. It is a living varnish: microbes and mineral-eaters stitched together in a low, slow metabolism. They do not grow upward. They spread as a reaction spreads—across warmth, across seep, across any place the architecture offers a stable gradient. In calmer courtyards, where current turns lazy and water stratifies, the film thickens into soft, cloudy patches that tremble when turbulence arrives, then settle again like silt that refuses to become silt.

In seams between ceramic slabs, in the hairline crazing where water can linger a fraction longer, there are “veils”: plankton-thin colonies that lift themselves into the flow like flags. They are the closest thing this world has to a meadow. They do not photosynthesize; they *filter* chemistry—taking dissolved minerals and trace organics delivered by the basin’s constant circulation. When disturbed they flash, not with cheerful bioluminescence but with a nervous, reflexive stutter—violet pinpricks that travel through the colony like a shiver through fur. The flash is not for you. It is for anything hunting by vibration and pressure change: *something moved here; something is tearing at the cloth of the current.*

Around these veils gather the small moving lives, the ones that make the place feel populated rather than merely inhabited.

The pale fish that run the causeways are not truly fish, not in ancestry or design, though the silhouette is close enough for your mind to borrow the word. They are built for reading architecture the way a surface creature reads stars: oversized eyes that do not see far but see *small*—edges, contrasts, the faint glow leaking from a seam. Their bodies are almost too economical, as if the cold has edited away every indulgence: narrow spines, translucent muscle, fins that flick only when necessary. In fast trench-currents they “lock in,” turning their bodies into live streamlines, letting the water carry them with minimal correction. In plazas they loosen into scattered, hovering confetti—until a pressure-shadow passes, and the whole group tightens, not into a ball but into a line, hugging the old built lines as if the stone itself were shelter.

They are navigators by touch at a distance. Some carry a faint lateral stripe of sensory pores that reads turbulence the way fingers read Braille. Others trail filament whiskers that brush the saltglass bubbles and calcite ropes, sampling the chemical taste of the surfaces to tell where they are. The basin is so consistent—same few materials, same few currents—that location becomes a flavor. Districts taste different.

They do not feed on plants; they feed on the basin’s byproducts: loose microbial flakes, waxy death-fragments, the lifted blue dust that rises when something scrapes a warm stone. Their mouths are small and precise. Many of them feed by waiting, head into the flow, letting the current deliver food like a conveyor. When a shear arrives, they do not flee; they spread out and harvest the pulse.

Behind them come the animals that make the architecture feel used.

The grazers you described—armored swimmers with plates like roof tiles—move with the unhurried certainty of creatures that do not expect to be rushed. They are not fast because fast is expensive in cold water and because their food does not run away. They patrol the same routes the currents favor: along the bases of pylons, under bridge-bellies, around the mouths of chambers that exhale warmth on schedule. Their plates are not merely defense. They are ballast and heat budget, layered with thin mineral deposits that crackle faintly when flexed, storing trace warmth and perhaps even carrying slow electrochemical signals along the body.

When they feed, they don’t bite. They *plane*. A broad scraping mouth presses to stone and shaves the living varnish and mineral precipitate together, taking biology and geology in the same stroke. The act leaves a clean, slightly lighter strip behind, and it frees a haze of fine blue dust—part microbial, part mineral—that drifts upward like a private weather event. This dust is a feast. It draws in smaller pickers—darting, needle-bodied things that seem all jaw and fin—and it summons the filter colonies into wider opening, frills unfurling to catch what the grazer has set loose.

The grazers are followed. Not always by predators—sometimes by opportunists, sometimes by symbionts. There are little lamprey-like clingers that attach briefly under a plate edge, not to drink blood but to steal heat and to feed on the rich microfilm that grows where the grazer’s body meets the water. The grazer tolerates them. In the cold, wastefulness is rare; tolerance becomes a kind of efficiency.

In the narrow places—where ceramic meets basalt, where calcite rope has braided itself into a hanging curtain—life anchors.

The “colonies that barely qualify as animal” are, up close, a whole taxonomy of solutions to the same problem: *how to eat without chasing*. Some are sacs with ringed mouths, opening and closing on a rhythm that matches the local turbulence as if they can hear the basin’s heartbeat. Some are frilled fans that pivot on mineral stalks, always turning their faces into the cleanest flow. Some are lattices—living grids that look like part of the original construction until they flex, each cell responding to a pressure pulse with a delayed, coordinated tightening.

They are not passive. Their coordination is a kind of language, slow and simple but effective. When a predator slips through and the current pattern changes wrong, entire walls of these things will close at once, dampening the water’s movement locally, collapsing the “noise” predators use for cover. It doesn’t stop a hunter. But it makes the hunter visible to anything watching the motion of the veils.

And there are always watchers.

Predators here do not announce themselves with speed. They announce themselves with absence: the sudden emptying of a line of water, as if something has drunk the fish out of it.

The ribbon-bodied hunter is the most common form of that absence. It is made for being mistaken for architecture: a long, flat body with a skin that takes on the basalt’s dullness or the ceramic’s muted sheen depending on where it presses itself. It does not cling with claws; it clings with pressure—micro-suckers or adhesive pads that let it become part of the curve of an eroded arch. In a steady current it is indistinguishable from a strip of mineral cloth. Only the eyes give it away, and even those are recessed, reflective only at certain angles.

It hunts by timing, not by pursuit. It chooses places where internal waves regularly throw schools off their neat causeway-lines. It waits for the shear to do what shears do—tilt the world, lift the dust, scramble the fish—and then it makes one economical strike, mouth unfolding like a seam opening in stone. It takes one animal, sometimes two, then returns to stillness. In the cold, digestion is slow; patience is a predatory trait.

There are other hunters too—some so small they prey on the anchored colonies, biting off frills and fleeing into the calcite threads. Some so large you only ever see the consequences: a plaza that suddenly has no grazers for weeks, filter colonies closed tight as if bracing, the fish schools staying high and tight to the towers where the current is messier and harder to read.

Those larger ones travel the trenches.

The trench-currents are corridors not just of water but of information: mineral particles aligned like filings, chemical gradients stretched into long lines. Creatures that can read those lines can move with astonishing purpose through the haze. Somewhere in those fast channels are long-bodied cruisers—cold-economical apexes that do not need to hide in architecture because they are the moving architecture of the trench. They seldom enter the plazas. When they do, the entire district reacts as if a bell has been struck: veils flashing in delayed waves, fish compressing into the thinnest possible silhouettes, grazers turning their plated flanks outward and sinking closer to the stone, making themselves heavy.

Not everything living here is animal in the usual sense of “independent mover.” Some of the most consequential inhabitants are the builders—the ones you already suspect, the ones who have been quietly making the basin look intentional for so long that it’s hard to draw a line between ruin and reef.

They come in several kinds, and it’s possible they are life stages of the same lineage.

There are the thread-layers: small, many-limbed things that live among calcite braids and extend them. They secrete not a glue but a *template*—a protein or polysaccharide scaffold that makes dissolved minerals prefer to crystallize in one direction rather than another. They don’t “weave” as a spider weaves. They farm the water’s willingness to become solid. Given enough seasons, their braided threads thicken into ropes; given enough ropes, you get mats; given enough mats, you get platforms that look as though someone intended a walkway there.

There are the pellet-masons: animals that gather suspended hail—brine pellets from the dark upper layer—and pack them into under-bridge nests, cementing them with a mineral slurry. At first their structures are fragile, seasonal. But the basin is patient. Currents sand them into smoothness; saltglass veining forms; the nest becomes indistinguishable from a deliberate vault.

And there are the slowest builders of all: not discrete creatures but *aggregations*—colonies that spread across a wall and change its geometry by microns per year. They thicken edges where turbulence is destructive, rounding the architecture toward shapes that shed stress. They thin protrusions that catch too much flow. Over centuries, their presence makes the built environment more durable, more water-wise. It is not design, exactly. It is selection made visible in stone.

If the basin has “memory,” these builders are part of how it keeps it: not by recalling, but by *repeating*.

Between all these lives, death is handled with the same cold thrift. Nothing rots quickly. Bodies become pale, waxy drifts that travel far—food that can be stored in motion. There are scavengers adapted to this: slow spiraling things that follow scent-lines along trenches, and small, hard-shelled pickers that specialize in shaving nourishment from old remains the way grazers shave it from stone. Even the anchored filter colonies participate, catching the finest particles of mortality and turning them back into living tissue.

Reproduction is timed to turbulence. In a world where the water is never still, the safest nursery is the current itself. Many creatures release eggs or larvae not into calm pockets but into predictable shears, trusting the basin’s repeating internal weather to carry the next generation to the same districts that fed the last. Some larvae have tiny mineral hooks that let them snag into ceramic crazing when they finally settle. Others remain plankton-thin for long periods, part of the veils—life that is not yet committed to being an animal with a path.

And then there are the rare inhabitants that make the architecture feel less like habitat and more like companion: the chamber-dwellers near the warm pulses.

In certain rooms—wide, empty spaces with smooth walls and diagram-mosaics under thin dust—the water warms on a schedule. When that warmth comes, animals gather with the practical devotion of creatures in winter crowding to a heat source. Grazers line the walls. Fish hover in layers. Filter colonies open wide, as if the warmth makes the water richer or easier to taste.

What causes the warmth is not seen directly. But there are signs: a faint blue leak along a seam that brightens, then fades; a slow pressure change like a breath; a subsonic groan that feels less like stone flexing and more like *something inside the stone adjusting*. Whether this is purely geochemical or the action of some distributed, lithic life—an ancient network of warm-remembering “stones” that are not stones at all but long-mineralized organisms—no creature here seems to care. They treat it as weather. They have built their lives around its reliability.

So what lives in this flooded highland is not a single emblematic monster, not even a single ecosystem in miniature, but a complete substitution: architecture as geology, turbulence as sunlight, mineral film as grass, cold patience as time.

It is a city that has become a reef without ever admitting it wanted to be either—and the inhabitants are the proof that “made” is not the same as “engineered.” Here, everything that survives becomes partly structure. Everything that builds, eventually becomes indistinguishable from the place.
