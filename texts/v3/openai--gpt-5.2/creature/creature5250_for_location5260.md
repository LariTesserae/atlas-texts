Life up here doesn’t announce itself with color or leaf-shape. It announces itself with contact.

With the click of weight on wet pavement, the soft rasp of millions of mouthparts worrying a line of salt, the brief static-snap of something tasting a charged rail and finding it good. If you stand still long enough for your own breathing to become just another noise in the stack, you begin to sort the inhabitants by what they do to the city’s surfaces.

There are **the weight-bearers** first—the long-backed quadrupeds that make sense of the reinforced lanes. In the dark they read as moving architecture: shoulder-high at least, sometimes taller, their spines ridged with plates that catch condensation and shed it in measured drips. They are not furry; nothing here wastes energy on insulation when the city itself leaks warmth in patches and lines. Their hides look like ceramic that never fired all the way through—matte in places, glossy where it stays wet, crazed with fine cracks that do not open.

When one of them breathes you hear it as much as you see it: a wet, percolating draw through internal chambers, as if their lungs are partly fluid or threaded with membranes that keep water and air in argument. They travel in files under conduits and along the slight crown of roadways where the water film is thinnest. The lead animal tests each surface with a slow, deliberate stamp before committing mass, like a surveyor checking a bridge by ear. They’re not cautious because they’re fearful; they’re cautious because the ground is still deciding what it wants to be, and these animals have evolved to listen for indecision.

They graze, but not on plants. You’ll see them halt beside a retaining wall where the city’s “skin” has grown a new seam, and they’ll press their mouths to it—mouths that aren’t lips so much as flexible, ridged pads. They lick the black varnish off stone. They worry at a line of corrosion like cattle at a salt lick. When they pull away, the surface is cleaner and a little duller, and the animal’s flank will briefly glow with a faint internal pulse—nothing bright, just the suggestion that something inside has accepted charge or digested a mineral.

These aren’t solitary bodies, either. They carry **their own ecosystems**.

Along their ribs and under the overhang of their plates live **the hitchers**: small, crablike things with jointed legs and suctioned feet that can cling to wet composite as easily as to basalt. In better light you’d call them pale, but the place doesn’t give you that luxury; you know them by sound and behavior. They tick across their host in quick, precise routes, stopping where condensation pools and where the host’s skin is warm. They harvest the host’s drips and secretions; they also harvest what the host stirs up—flakes of salt bloom, loosened oxide, the thin metallic “taste” that seeps into every film of water.

At intervals the hitchers drop off in twos and threes, not randomly but as if at prearranged stations: a particular curb where mineral crust forms, a particular cracked stair where heat leaks from below. There they become ground-animals for a while, feeding and mating and depositing clusters of gelled eggs into crevices that never dry. When conditions shift—when the substructure hum deepens or a new fog-tide rolls in—they climb onto the next passing weight-bearer and ride the city again.

Between the heavy lanes and the walls there are **the poured-carpet swarms**, the ones you hear as a continuous soft abrasion. They look like gravel until they move like liquid. They have no interest in you except as a temporary topography: a new obstacle to flow around. Their bodies are small, matte, and built for wet contact—domed backs that shed water, undersides with combs and rasps that can lift mineral film off concrete without needing teeth.

The swarms are where you begin to suspect that “animal” is only one of the categories here. Individually, each unit behaves like a simple organism: seek heat, seek mineral, avoid pressure. But in aggregate they become something else: a self-routing surface that opens and closes paths with the efficiency of fluid dynamics. They don’t panic when disturbed; they *recalculate*. You can step into them and feel them part around your boots, then rejoin behind you seamlessly, leaving only a brief, darker track where moisture was displaced and then wicked back.

They build too—not buildings, not even nests in the way you’d expect, but **living deposits**. Along the edges of drainage channels they will thicken into ridges, layering their shed skins and excreted binders with fine grit until a fragile berm exists that didn’t yesterday. It is never ornamental; it is always placed where flow needs nudging. In this sense the swarms behave like the city’s smallest civil engineers, and the city, in return, provides them with the junction boxes and heat leaks they prefer.

Above—though “above” is slippery in a place where fog makes distance feel like pressure rather than space—there are **the echo-flocks**.

You don’t see a flock so much as you enter its acoustic field. First comes the rolling chatter, a band of sound that bounces and braids itself between ribs of concrete and platework. Then a faint shift in air pressure against your face, like someone moving a sheet past you in the dark. The animals themselves are built for thin air and low light: wide-winged, yes, but their flight surfaces are not feathers in the traditional sense. More like layered membranes with stiffened struts that can flex and lock, changing shape with minimal energy. They don’t rely on vision because there’s nothing reliable to see; they map the city by return-time and frequency smear, painting architecture into existence with call and echo.

When they turn together, the sound changes shape—tightens, rises, becomes a different texture—and you can tell their trajectory more accurately than you could by sight even if the place were lit. They roost where sound behaves: under curved overpasses, inside ribbed chambers that the city seems to have made for acoustics as much as shelter. When the fog is thick, their rookeries become louder, not quieter, because the damp air carries and softens edges, allowing more bodies to occupy the same sonic space without calls cutting one another to pieces.

They feed on the city’s byproducts the way seabirds feed on tides. Sometimes they skim close to an active line and dip their mouths—narrow, ridged openings—into the charged condensation that beads on insulated rails. The contact is brief. The payoff is immediate: a shiver through the whole body, a momentary stiffening of wing membrane as if the charge changes tension. Then they’re gone again, leaving behind only the fading thread of their synchronized chatter.

Closer to the structures—inside the places that feel like lungs and throats in the built mass—live **the gate-keepers**, and here “animal” begins to blur hard.

You’ll hear an iris-aperture dilate before you see it. The sound is soft: a wet flex, a controlled crackle like ice moving in a gutter. The membrane is not metal; it’s composite layered with something that behaves like living cartilage. It yields to specific vibrations. It does not respond to force the way a door responds to a shove. It responds to *signature*: the pattern of footsteps that the weight-bearers make, the wingbeat pressure of a flock at close range, the particular clicking cadence of a hitcher colony moving in a cluster.

These gates are not passive architecture. They are inhabited by **the seam-things**—small, pale bodies that live within the material layers and perform the opening and sealing. You almost never see them directly because they don’t like exposed air; they live in the damp lamination where water is held close and temperature is stable. But sometimes a tear in a membrane shows you a glimpse: a tangle of wormlike forms studded with fine cilia, pulsing in coordinated waves. They are both muscle and citizen. They eat the same dissolved minerals and trace organics that everything here eats; in exchange they make the city permeable in the right places.

If the swarms are the city’s street-crew, the seam-things are its valves.

And then there are **the scaffolds**, which are the strangest occupants because they look most like “machines” until you watch them long enough to see the behavior underneath.

When a walkway collapses, the first arrivals are not builders with tools but a slow migration of modular frames—jointed struts that move with insect patience, placing themselves with perfect repeatability. They lock into the broken edges and hold. Over days, new layers extrude: bands of pale material laid down in thin increments, thickening where stress concentrates. At night—if night can be said to exist distinct from day—you can sometimes hear the structure adjusting: a tiny, regular ticking, like a cooling engine that never cools.

What lives here are not the struts themselves, but **the printing-colonies** within them: biofilm-like assemblages that can harden slurry into composite, guided by a template that exists in chemical gradients and vibration patterns. The frames are their skeletons, their exoskeletons, their carried habitat. They are fed by the city’s energy flow and by the mineral broth of condensation. They don’t “finish” a repair as a discrete act; they continue until the flow is restored and the stress-lines quiet, then they thin and withdraw, leaving behind new matter that matches function, not style.

You start to understand that the city’s famous self-healing is not a miracle of engineering. It’s an ecology of builders that never stopped building.

Predators exist, of course, because any dense system generates gradients worth exploiting.

They are hardest to perceive because they are defined by stillness rather than noise. Along the mineral marsh-pools you’ll find shapes that seem like statuary—arched backs, long necks held at impossible angles, limbs folded into the geometry of drainage ribs. They wait where herds slow to drink and where swarms gather thickest around salt. Their hunting is not a chase; the air is too thin and the surfaces too slick for wasteful sprinting. They rely on timing and on the city’s acoustics. A predator will click once—barely audible—and that click, reflected and distorted through plates and fog, will shift the echo-field just enough to startle a drinker into a half-step. One misplacement on wet stone is enough.

When they strike, it’s fast, but even then it’s economical: a joint unfolds, a hooked limb finds purchase, a mouth that is more like a shearing aperture closes. The kill is not messy. Fluids are too valuable here to spray. The predators drink, then retreat to warm vents or sheltered undercuts where their bodies can metabolize and where hitchers will come to clean their plates. Nothing is wasted; waste would be an ecological crime in a world where energy is precious and water is always on the edge of freezing.

There are also inhabitants that are less like animals and more like **weather given agency**.

In the highest fog strata you sometimes see (or think you see) drifting filaments—threads that catch the faint indicator glows and then vanish again. They are almost weightless. They move not with wind but with electrical gradients, slowly aligning along active lines the way iron filings align to a magnet. When they touch a metal edge, there’s a faint crackle and a brief brightening of the thin status-lights nearby, as if charge has been redistributed. These filaments may be colonies of microscopic organisms, each one too small to matter, together forming a living conductivity that feeds on the city’s field. They are the closest thing this place has to plankton: suspended life that turns invisible energy into biomass, seeding everything else.

Put all of this together—the weight-bearers and their hitchers, the swarms and seam-things, the echo-flocks, the printing-colonies, the methodical predators, the drifting conductivity-filaments—and you begin to see the true dominant inhabitant:

Not any single species.

But **the city-as-habitat**, and the web of citizens it supports, shapes, and is shaped by.

The city does not sit on the plateau the way a human city sits on bedrock. It grips and listens. It holds water close because water is its medium: the solvent that carries minerals, the film that makes conductivity possible, the thin shared bloodstream between stone, structure, and creature. Its forms—ribs and fins and micro-grooves—are not aesthetic decisions. They are invitations and controls: places for roosting, channels for migration, acoustic corridors for echolocation, damp laminae for valve-colonies.

If anything like “people” live here in the sense you mean on greener worlds—tool-users who name themselves and tell stories—then they are either absent or so integrated into the infrastructure that they no longer register as separate bodies. What you meet instead are populations that treat construction as metabolism and treat electricity the way forests treat sunlight.

The plateau is young, still warm in seams, still cracking into new channels. The city is younger in a different way: always mid-growth, always mid-repair, never finished. And the lives that fill it have adapted to that unfinishedness as their normal state.

They live in the phase between “happened” and “happening.”

They live in wet cold and thin air and violet darkness.

They live by sound, by pressure, by the taste of salt and charge in a film of water.

They live everywhere a surface exists—and where a surface fails, they arrive to make a new one.
