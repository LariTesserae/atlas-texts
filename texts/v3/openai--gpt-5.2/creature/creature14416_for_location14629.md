What lives there is not one thing, and it’s not even a clean stack of things (people on top of buildings on top of land). It’s a braid: dwellers, animals, trained plants, and the town’s own half-hidden “rules” all occupying the same wet thickness, sharing boundaries the way water shares a basin.

At first, you’d swear the town is mostly empty—because it refuses the usual proof of habitation. No hard roofs in rows, no stone chimneys, no rigid fences. Then you begin to notice the kinds of presence that don’t announce themselves with permanence.

### The town itself, as a resident
The first inhabitant is the infrastructure—because it behaves like an organism that has learned civics.

It doesn’t just *support* life; it participates. The root-grids tighten and slacken with schedules. The reed-conduits keep time in pulses. Moss decides where traction is needed. Leaf membranes turn their faces as if listening to heat, glare, crowds, moods. The weeping rocks “work” all day, condensing, cooling, bleeding a careful warmth into the right corners so something delicate can bloom at the wrong hour.

Whatever coordination drives this—computation, hormone-logic, microbial signaling, something you can’t point to without breaking it into metaphors—counts as a living presence in its own right. People in the town don’t speak of “systems” the way outsiders do. They speak of *temper*, *tides*, *hunger*, *patience*. They’ll tell you the south lanes are “thirsty,” or that the plaza is “holding its breath.” They don’t mean it poetically. They mean it as operational fact.

And like any resident with power, the town has preferences. It likes light loads. It likes repeated routes. It likes the kind of use that can be revised without violence. It “dislikes” deep digging, straight lines forced too long, heavy static weight. Not as morality—more as physiology.

### The people: marsh-civic, light-footed, pattern-minded
Humans live here, or something close enough to humans that you’d recognize the gestures even if you didn’t recognize the adaptations.

They dress like people who expect contact: fabrics that don’t mind wet, layers that breathe, wraps that can be rinsed in a canal and dry on the body while you walk. Soles are soft and wide, sometimes with gripping fibers grown into them, because the town rewards distributed weight. Hands are rarely clean; fingernails carry peat stains the way sailors carry salt.

They move like commuters in a place where every surface is negotiable. You’ll see them pause before stepping onto a root-bridge—not from fear, but to give a tiny allowance for the bridge to answer them. A palm to a railing-vine, a brief pressure, the way you might press a door to feel whether it’s latched. Many carry short tools that look like knives but behave like pruning hooks, grafting splices, reed-openers. Maintenance is not a profession separated from life. It’s closer to sweeping a floor: constant, casual, essential.

They don’t “own” land the way dry towns do. What they possess—what they argue about, inherit, trade—is **access to patterns**:
- whose footsteps the moss is trained to accept without slicking,
- whose scent-key opens a hedge-wall into an interior garden,
- who is allowed to ask the canals to pulse a boat-lane clear at dawn,
- who has priority at a weeping rock when the air turns unusually thin and everyone can taste mineral on their tongues.

Their governance is likewise light. More choreography than statute. The town’s agreements are embedded: in growth habits, in routings, in the timing of floods, in the way certain lanes become impassable for an hour because that’s when the surface mat “breathes.” If there are councils, they look like gardeners talking—standing in damp shade, listening to the pump-hum and watching which leaves swivel. Decisions are often phrased as questions to the living architecture: *Can this district carry a market today? Can the edge hold another dock?*

Children grow up bilingual in the obvious sense (spoken language) and in the local one: **flow-reading**. They learn to notice the tremble that means water will reverse in the canals. They learn the subtle darkening of a moss strip that means “too much traffic here, reroute.” They learn not to fight the town’s edits, because fighting costs more than compliance ever will.

### The specialists: those who speak “plant” and “physics” without separating them
Some residents live closer to the infrastructural organs than others.

There are reed-keepers—people who tend the evenly spaced conduits along the canals, trimming at exactly the right nodes, checking the faint banding for stress, listening for changes in resonance. Their work is half botany, half plumbing, half something that looks like tuning an instrument. They carry small resonant devices (sometimes nothing more than hollow stems with cut lengths) to “ask” the reeds what’s happening inside them.

There are rock-tenders who know the weeping stones the way a mechanic knows an engine: where the crackle means normal condensation and where it means the internal lattice is drifting. They keep basins clean, direct overflow, and collect the thin scums that form on the underside of the sweat-lines—useful in medicines, in adhesives, in whatever quiet chemistry the town runs on.

And there are designers—though they would not call themselves that—who manage the town’s biggest art: **controlled growth**. They plan not in blueprints but in growth-cycles, seasonless microclimates, root-logic. Their “buildings” are schedules plus seed plus constraint. They can ungrow a wall. They can coax a bridge to thicken where deer keep crossing. They can encourage a lane to become acoustically private by introducing the right hollow-stem species that swallows speech in one direction and throws it in another.

### The nonhuman citizens: commuters, custodians, opportunists
Animals here are not background. They’re part of circulation.

- **Marsh deer** move through the town with the ease of long familiarity. They know which root-bridges bear weight without flexing too much; they time their crossings to the town’s breathing cycles. People don’t shoo them the way you’d expect. The deer are traffic, like bicycles in a street grid—sometimes inconvenient, usually accommodated.

- **Otter-like canal folk** (sleek, pale-eyed, quick) treat intersections as social spaces. They’ll pause where two channels meet, bump shoulders, trade scents, then vanish down different routes. They are bold enough to steal fish from market basins, clever enough to do it when the flood pulse makes pursuit awkward. Some households leave offerings—not worship, exactly, but recognition that these animals act like fellow residents with their own claims.

- **Amphibians** own thresholds in a literal sense: gutters, shaded cracks, the cool lip of a weeping rock basin, the underside of a bench-root. They’re a living measure of town health. When frogs vanish from a lane, people notice faster than they’d notice a broken lamp.

- **Insects** are everywhere, but not as nuisance so much as architecture and messaging. Midges become moving curtains. Dragonflies patrol lanes like bright, dry punctuation. Beetles turn leaf litter the way street cleaners turn trash—noise included.

And then there are the ones that feel like they belong to the town’s secret metabolism:

- **Leaf-bundle things**—small, quiet, easily mistaken for wet debris until they unfold. They move with unnerving synchronization, as if several bodies share a single timing signal. They linger near the reed-conduits and weeping rocks, feeding on what leaks: warmth, mineral residues, stray energy gradients. People don’t usually kill them. They treat them as indicators, the way miners once treated canaries: if the leaf-bundles gather too thickly, something is “bleeding” in the infrastructure; if they scatter, a district may be starving.

Whether these creatures are native, engineered, or a third category that only exists in places where biology and technology stopped being separate—no one entirely agrees. The town doesn’t label them; it routes around them, and sometimes, subtly, it routes them away.

### The domestic layer: what “home” means here
Homes are less like sealed boxes and more like **appointments with shelter**.

A family might “live” in a cluster of raised pads where the surface mat is reinforced and warm. Their roof is a leaf-laminate that beads water and shivers when wind shifts, and it can be rolled back, regrown, or thickened depending on season and crowding. Interior walls are hedges trained for privacy, but privacy is never absolute; it’s negotiated acoustically, visually, aromatically.

Cooking smells are green and mineral: steamed tubers, fish, fungus-based broths, fermented reed-sugars. Storage is done by coolness more than dryness. Things are kept in hanging baskets that breathe, in submerged pots anchored under benches, in root-pockets that maintain a steady temperature. Fire exists, but it’s treated carefully—often small, contained, and sometimes replaced by the quiet heat that seeps from certain stones or from the town’s hotter root-runs.

The town’s social life happens in those deliberately empty clearings—breathing spaces where the marsh is allowed to be itself. Markets appear and vanish. Gatherings are timed to the town’s rinse-cycles, so you’ll see a plaza dry itself, hold a crowd, then slowly wet again as the infrastructure decides the day’s use is done.

### The outsiders: why most don’t stay
Visitors come—traders, pilgrims, scientists, refugees from drier, harsher grids. The town can host them; it can even accommodate a surprising amount of weight for a little while. But most outsiders feel, sooner or later, the pressure of living without hard boundaries.

The ground edits your traces. The paths change. What you thought you understood yesterday is revised by morning. The infrastructure acknowledges you—lamp-clusters brightening, moss gripping, canals parting in small courtesies—but it does not *stabilize around you* until you learn its habits.

To stay, you have to accept a kind of citizenship that is mostly attention: noticing, yielding, asking rather than forcing.

### What truly lives there
If you need one sentence: this is a town inhabited by people and animals **inside a larger living municipal body**, where plants are not scenery but law, road, roof, memory, and mood.

But the truer answer is messier: what lives there is a negotiated turbulence—many species, many intelligences, many small hungers and daily routines—all agreeing, over and over, to keep everything light enough to float on a world that never quite dries.
