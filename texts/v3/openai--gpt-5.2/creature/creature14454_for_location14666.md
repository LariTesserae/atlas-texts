What lives in the bowl at first doesn’t present itself as *animals* so much as *processes with skin*.

The first residents are the ones you would only notice by the way they change the rules. A film on stone that makes water bead instead of sheet. A velvet of algae so fine it looks like a shadow until a cloud thins and it flashes green. Pale bacterial veils in the coldest seams of the peat, stitching oxygenless pockets to oxygened ones, turning decay into a slow, sweet fermentation. If you pull up a handful of moss and look closely, you find it isn’t one moss but a council: strands that hold water, strands that shed it, strands that acidify the surface so the wrong roots won’t take. They live like that—by deciding what can happen next.

The “weeping” stones have their own tenants. In the hairline cracks and the undersides that bead against gravity, there are lichens that don’t look like lichens at all—more like old varnish, smoke-stained and glossy. They are thin, almost polite, but they make the rock *wick*. Where they are thickest, the stone sweats more readily, as if the lichen is teaching the basalt how to drink cloud and exhale it drop by drop. In the resonant cavities under shelves, fungi thread through the softer layers and knit mineral dust into felted curtains; they tremble when the spillway’s note hits the right pitch, releasing a faint peppery scent that is less “mushroom” than “stone warmed by rain.”

On the floating shelves of sphagnum and root-felt, life is built in layers like roofing.

The upper layer is bright with wetness: small sedges, wax-leaf rafts, the star-shaped tooth-edged plants that cup fog and gain weight by the hour. Those stars aren’t carnivorous in the simple sense, but they are opportunists. Gnats and midges land on the cupped leaves to drink; the leaves hold them longer than they should, not sticky, just *reluctant to let go*. When the fog lifts, you can find the husks of insects dried into the leaf’s fine teeth, and the plant looks a shade darker for days afterward. It doesn’t trap often—it doesn’t need to. It just takes what the air offers.

Below that bright layer is the working dark: springy peat packed with half-decayed fronds and the fine root-hair of a hundred plants. Here live the engineers—wormlike detritivores that are not quite earthworms and not quite leeches, translucent enough to show the black of their gut, slow enough that you only notice them when you stop moving and stare. Tiny crustaceans flicker in the saturated channels, making the peat fizz with minute motion. When you hear the soft popping of bubbles, it is often their doing: little breaths released from pockets they have opened, or methane unlatched from an old seam by a burrow.

In the shallow pools—door-sized, clear, never stagnant—the animals are mostly larval and mostly patient. Needle-bodied nymphs of dragonflies lurk among submerged stems, their eyes like beads of riverstone. Caddisfly larvae build cases not from sand, but from moss fragments and mica-flakes, so their houses glitter when the sun hits at the right angle. There are snails with shells thin as fingernails that graze on the invisible films coating the pebbles; you can tell where they’ve been because the stones look newly polished.

The insects you hear more than see are not one voice but several, braided.

There is the steady rasp—an insect that lives down in the sedges and sings without moving, as if the sound is simply part of its respiration. The basin amplifies it and then softens it again with fog, so it seems to come from “over there” even when you stand directly above it. When a gust shifts the spillway’s roar into a held tone, these rasping insects sometimes fall silent all at once, as if they can’t compete with the basin’s note and won’t try. Then, a few minutes later, one begins again, carefully, and the others answer in staggered time.

There are also the sudden wing-bursts: cloud-moths and small, dark butterflies that keep their bodies dry by never quite landing flat. They touch leaves only at edges; they drink from bromeliad cups like thieves, darting away the moment a droplet falls too loudly.

Spiders live here the way everything else does: adjusted to dripping. Their webs are not stretched wide between trunks but hung in short, sheltered spans beneath ledges and fern-umbrellas, angled so beads of water roll off without collapsing the silk. In morning fog they look like necklaces abandoned in the air. The spiders themselves are small, pale, and slow to run; they rely on the basin’s damp to keep their silk elastic and on the quiet to keep prey from noticing the trap until it is too late.

The frogs are real, but they behave as if the basin taught them restraint.

You don’t get choruses. You get spacing. One call from the far side of a pool, answered—after a long pause—by another from the rim of a different pool. The calls are low and rounded, not the sharp peeping of pond-frogs in crowded wetlands. If you follow the sound you find frogs that seem made for wet air rather than open water: small bodies, long toes for gripping moss, skin that takes on the color of whatever it’s pressed against. Sometimes you see only the subtle rise and fall of a throat under a fern’s shadow, like a second kind of drip.

There are salamanders too, in the shadiest corners where stone faces stay cold under warm air. They keep to the “weeping” rock, their bellies pressed to the slickness, mouths opening and closing in time with the faint internal trickle. Their eggs are laid not in pools but in the persistent wet at the rock’s underside—clusters of clear pearls that never quite dry because the stone never quite stops exhaling.

Birds treat the basin as a crossing, not a home. They pass overhead in single arcs, as you noticed, but even that passing is part of the bowl’s population: the basin is a watering hole for creatures that don’t want to land. Swifts skim the humid air and drink on the wing, cutting through fog like needles through cloth. Once, if you are still enough, you might see a pale hawk-like bird hover briefly above the spillway notch, not hunting so much as letting the updraft comb water across its feathers.

The larger animals that leave only a compact shape at the edge of vision are the basin’s most careful inhabitants, and their caution feels like etiquette rather than fear. A small mountain deer—or something deer-adjacent, with short legs and a heavier chest than you expect—comes in at dusk to lick mineral taste from the weeping rocks. It steps onto peat platforms the way you would step on thin ice: testing, listening, distributing its weight so the ground doesn’t answer too loudly. Its hooves are splayed slightly, adapted for softness; even so, it avoids the newest mats and follows routes that have thickened over years of repetition.

There are also hunters, but not the kind that announce themselves. A mustelid-sized predator with a coat that goes almost black when wet moves along the stone ribs and disappears behind moss that looks too thin to hide anything. You don’t see it often because it doesn’t often need to move; it can wait. When the basin is quiet enough to hear tiny collapses in moss, you can sometimes hear a different kind of sound: a soft, deliberate patter that stops the instant you notice it.

Yet the most pervasive inhabitant isn’t an individual creature at all. It’s the basin’s *circulation*—the sense that water is doing more than following gravity.

If you stand in one place long enough, you start to feel that the fog is not merely arriving. It is being *received*. The epiphytes on stone faces lift their roots as though tasting the air; the star-plants cup upward; even the pale-lichened trunks seem to brighten slightly where condensation prefers them. Drops form where you’d expect them—but also where you wouldn’t, on undersides and sheltered faces, as if the direction of “down” has been negotiated by capillaries and pores and living films.

Taken together, all these residents make something like a distributed organ: moss as lung, peat as stomach, stone as bone, spillway as pulse. Nothing here hurries, and nothing here multiplies into a crowd. The bowl doesn’t allow a frenzy of life; it allows a *steadiness* of life, tuned to the long work of gathering cloud, holding it, refining it, and letting it go again.

If people exist in this world, they might say the basin has a spirit, because it behaves like one: consistent, self-contained, responsive to breath and weather, uninterested in being owned. But you can also understand it without names. What lives here is an ecology that has learned to be quiet so it can hear the water thinking.
