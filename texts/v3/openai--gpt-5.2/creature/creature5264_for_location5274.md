What lives on the upland is not a single community but a stacked occupation—life using life using ruin using heat. If you stand in it long enough, you begin to notice that the plateau doesn’t host inhabitants so much as it *conducts* them: every creature here is a solution to wetness, darkness, vibration, and the constant, low promise of warm ground.

### The first residents: the eaters of metal, the drinkers of stone  
Before you meet anything with eyes, you meet the films.

On every pipe and girder and rivet-head there are bacterial skins that treat rust as pasture. They do not merely stain runoff orange; they *organize* it. Their colonies feather out in branching maps that follow rivulets and bolt-lines, and where they thicken the metal pits into lace, creating micro-caves that stay warm and wet even when the fog thins. Those pits become nurseries for larvae and grazing pads for mites, and then—because nothing stays simple here—fungi take the pits as anchor points and build slow, luminous scaffolding over the metal.

The plateau’s oldest chemistry lives in the mud: anaerobic mats that bubble softly in the peat and smell faintly sweet, as if the earth is fermenting. They feed on what sinks and what leaks—leaf rot, geothermal minerals, the faint, persistent hydrocarbons that wash off machinery—and in return they give the upland its particular warmth, the kind that feels stored rather than sun-made.

If the plateau has a “ground floor,” it is that: heat bleeding upward through fractures, and life forming a membrane over it to hold it close.

### The plants that don’t reach for sun, only for *surface*  
Vegetation here doesn’t climb toward brightness; it climbs toward *air*. Leaves are engineered less like solar panels and more like gutters. They are broad and stiff enough to shed constant condensation, but cupped or ribbed so that drip-lines fall where the plant wants them to fall—down the trunk, into the root-wads, onto the moss that feeds it. You can often tell what a plant is doing by how it makes water behave.

Vines are not decorative; they are structural. They bind trees into wind-resistant bundles, lash themselves to pylons and cableways, and turn old industrial corridors into living suspension bridges. Their stems are rope-thick and slightly warm to the touch near vent fields, as if they’ve learned to steal the plateau’s geothermal budget.

Mosses and liverworts are the plateau’s true fabric. Some are so dense on basalt faces that the rock looks padded. Some specialize in iron-rich seepage and grow in dark, glossy tongues down ruined walls. A few types—those that colonize insulators and substation roofs—seem almost domesticated by vibration, growing thickest where the hum never stops, as if steady sound has become a kind of climate.

And everywhere, fungi make the darkness legible: constant low glow along fallen logs, along root-knuckles, along the interior ribs of dead leaves. In places the glow is reactive—brightening with thunder or with the sudden churn of a pump—so the upland flashes with brief, involuntary illumination, like a body responding to touch.

### The insects that turn air into a crowded room  
Insect life is not “abundant”; it is infrastructural.

There are midges that hatch in such numbers from warm seep pools that they become weather, a drifting layer that thickens the fog. There are beetles with sealed wing-cases that raft across oil sheens without breaking surface tension, skating the runoff lines around pump houses as if those lines were roads laid for them. There are moth-things that don’t navigate by stars (there are none) but by the rhythm of industrial lamps—circling not the light itself, but the *flicker rate*, as though the plateau’s grid has become a substitute sky.

Some of the most successful flyers aren’t flyers at all: they are jumpers and fallers. They throw themselves into the fog and let damp air carry them, using fine sensory hairs to read turbulence the way fish read current. When they land, it is with that small, wet slap you hear in the dark and cannot place.

And then there are the luminous ones: insects that don’t flash for romance so much as for coordination. Their synchronized sheets aren’t pretty signals; they are moving maps—“this corridor is safe,” “this log is feeding,” “this predator is passing.” In a world where sight is short-range and sound is messy, light becomes punctuation.

### The amphibians and fish that treat the plateau as one big shoreline  
Almost everything here lives as if it is never far from water—because it isn’t.

Amphibians are the plateau’s loudest citizens. Some cling to vertical wet rock and call with throats like drums, their voices pitched to travel through fog without being swallowed. Others live in the warm margins of geothermal seeps and carry heat in their bodies; when they hop through the undergrowth you can sometimes feel it in the air, a small displacement of warmth.

In the braided rivers and flooded root-lattices there are fish adapted to dimness and tannin-stain, with pale lateral lines and slow, deliberate movement. They shelter beneath the woven roots of trees standing knee-deep in black water, and when the upland floods quietly—as it does, over and over—those fish spread through the forest as if the forest were simply another channel.

There are eels too, or things eel-shaped, that follow warmth rather than current. They coil in culverts, in drainage pipes, in the submerged mouths of old conduits, and when a factory releases a pulse of hot effluent, you can watch the water itself change behavior as they gather—small eddies and sudden silences in places that were flowing.

### The mammals that travel by wire, trunk, and tremor  
The gliding mammals are not rare; they are merely hard to catch with the eye. They are built for short launches between trunks and pylons—fur that sheds water, membranes that don’t flap but *hold*, and claws that can bite into moss-slick bark or rusted steel. They nest where warmth leaks: under roof overhangs of pump houses, inside hollow metal frames colonized by vines, behind the false walls of substations where the hum is constant and predators hesitate.

The antlered browsers—heavy, patient things—feed on moss gardens and fungal shelves the way deer feed on leaves elsewhere. Their antlers are not just weapons; they are tools for parting fern umbrellas, for scraping lichen off rock, for levering rotten beams loose to get at the bright, soft rot beneath. They move through marsh-edge with practiced care, hoofprints filling instantly with tea-colored water that steams faintly on cold nights.

Predators here are less about speed and more about *reading*. Long-bodied hunters press themselves to the ground to listen through mud, using belly and jaw as sensor arrays. They pause often, not indecisive but processing—the plateau’s floor is a bulletin board of vibration: the distant patience of pumps, the footfalls of browsers, the frantic patter of insects, the subtle hollow ring that says “this patch of ground is thin.”

Some predators have their own dim flank-glow, not to illuminate prey but to manipulate attention. A slow pulse can freeze a skittish animal long enough for the hunter to close the last meters. In the fog, a pulse can also be a warning to other predators: *mine*.

### The birds that aren’t really birds (and the ones that are)  
If there are true birds, they are specialists: short-winged, heavy-feathered creatures that launch from high perches and drop into the fog to snatch insects and return by memorized routes. Their calls are blunt and repetitive—less song than sonar marker—because beauty isn’t useful when the canopy eats sound.

More common are the *perchers*: batlike things, gliders, and something in between, that cling to cables and beam edges. They treat the plateau’s industrial skeleton as cliff-face. At night, when the grid is loudest and the lamps halo, you can sometimes see dozens of pale shapes hanging upside down under a trestle, their bodies arranged to catch rising warmth.

### The plateau’s other big inhabitants: the works, and the people who learned to be another species  
Industry lives here the way a reef lives in a warm sea: not as an intrusion that remains separate, but as a structure that becomes habitat and is itself inhabited.

Some of the plateau’s facilities are still staffed, though “staffed” looks different than in clearer lands. The workers who stay up here long-term tend to be the kinds of people who can live without horizons. They learn the plateau by *signature*—the pitch of a specific turbine, the cadence of a particular drip field, the way a certain cable sings when the load is heavy. They don’t say “north” and “south” as often as they say “toward the hot pumps” or “past the loud marsh” or “downwind of the ozone vents.”

Their settlements are usually not towns so much as nodes: pump houses with bunks above the wet line; maintenance sheds on stilts; switchyards with insulated walkways; clusters of containers welded into sheltered courtyards where lantern-light doesn’t immediately vanish into fog. They hang reflective tags and chimes along routes—not for decoration, but because the plateau is navigated by ear as much as by sight. A safe path is a path that *answers* when you call.

They wear gear that assumes constant saturation: coated cloth that doesn’t pretend to stay dry, boots that drain rather than seal, masks for spores and metallic haze. Many carry light in ways that don’t rely on flame: battery lamps in tight hoods, chemical sticks, cages of cultivated glow-fungus fed with scraps. You can sometimes see their markers before you see them—dim, disciplined points of light that hold steady rather than pulsing.

And they are not separate from the ecology. Their trash becomes substrate. Their heat leaks into the ground and changes what can grow. Their buildings create overhang caves where gliders roost and where amphibians lay eggs in condensation trays. A pump’s vibration becomes camouflage for burrowers. A warm discharge channel becomes a breeding creek that never freezes.

Some workers talk about the upland as if it listens. Not mysticism, exactly—more the practical superstition that comes from living in a place where fog has moods and where everything responds to everything else. They will tell you to speak before you enter a dense fern stand, not because the forest is offended, but because the plateau is crowded and blind things deserve warning.

### Scavengers, opportunists, and the things that use humans as weather  
Where there are people, there are followers.

Small mammals trail maintenance routes to feed on dropped rations and the insects drawn to lamp halos. Nesting creatures build in the warm cavities behind panels. Amphibians learn that the underside of certain walkways drips reliably even in “drier” weeks. Predators learn the rhythm of shift changes and wait in the ferns where footsteps funnel.

Even the plants take advantage: vines thicken around rails because the gravel bed drains just enough to keep roots oxygenated; moss gardens flourish on the leeward sides of buildings where wind-driven spores collect; sound-reactive lichens brighten around generator pads, turning machinery into a dimly glowing shoreline.

### What *kind* of life this makes  
If you try to summarize the plateau’s inhabitants, you end up with rules rather than species:

- Nothing here expects dryness, so everything is built to breathe while wet.  
- Nothing here expects clear light, so communication is sound, pulse, scent, and touch.  
- Nothing here wastes warmth; it is gathered, hoarded, shared, stolen.  
- Nothing here is truly solitary, because every surface becomes community.  
- Industry does not sit on top of the ecology—it is one of the plateau’s organs now, pumping, humming, leaking, providing structure and heat the way basalt and root do.

So what lives there?

A whole continent of damp, dark adaptation: iron-eating bacteria and glow-fungi writing slow constellations across ruin; insects turning fog into traffic; amphibians shouting over turbines; gliders nesting in the warm ribs of buildings; browsers gardening moss; predators listening through mud; and human crews moving between beacons, half engineers and half weather-animals, keeping the machines alive while the plateau patiently, relentlessly, makes the machines part of itself.
