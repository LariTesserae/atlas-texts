At first it seems like nothing lives here in the way your reflexes expect life to announce itself. No quick bodies. No heat-signatures that dart. No little negotiations in the dust. The pocket feels *unpeopled*—a kiln left running after everyone went home.

Then the longer you stand in it, the more you realize the inhabitants have simply chosen (or been forced into) a tempo and a chemistry that your animal brain keeps mistaking for weather and materials.

### The obvious residents: the dry makers
What you called vegetation isn’t just decoration around ruins; it’s the dominant citizenry, and it has the calm authority of something that arrived before the buildings and will remain after they’re rewritten into rubble.

**Saltwood** are not “trees” so much as slow pressure-engines. Their laminated plates aren’t only structure; they’re *actuators*. Each tiny click on a heat pulse is a mechanical decision: opening a seam, tightening a spiral, shifting load from one anchoring wedge to another. A grove is a field of metronomes, and in the aggregate their ticking stops being random. When the vents breathe in a familiar pattern, the grove answers with a familiar counter-pattern, the way a stand of reeds answers a river.

**Amber vines** are the archivists and masons. The film they exude is sticky on purpose—an appetite for the air’s offerings—and when it thickens into curtains it isn’t only catching dust; it’s sorting it. Layer by layer, the vine lays down bands with different inclusions: carbon-rich soot, pale salts, metal oxides in rare streaks, sulfur crystals like embedded stars. In cross-section, an old curtain reads like a book of seasons: storm months, lull months, years when the vents ran hot and years when the ceiling cracked and let in more light.

They also do something a little unsettling for a “plant”: they *seal.* When a dome fissures, the vine grows toward the draft with the confidence of a thing that can feel pressure differentials the way you feel pain. It lays varnish into the crack, hardens it, lays another, hardens it again—until the building becomes a jar again. Not out of sentiment, but because enclosed volumes are useful: controlled air is food.

**Fan-palms** are the lungs and the ears. Their ridged faces comb the moving atmosphere, their bladders store ionic syrup like a battery, and their whole posture is that of an organism that expects the world to arrive as *flow*. They tilt toward vents the way other leaves tilt toward light. If you watch long enough you can catch a slow reorientation—subtle enough you’ll doubt yourself—when a vent’s chemistry changes. They’re not following warmth alone; they’re following *the right kind of air.*

Between them, these three forms make the city look lush, but it’s a very particular lushness: not green hunger, not wet abundance—more like a workshop that never shuts down.

### The hidden residents: the ones too small to be missing
The pocket is full of life you can’t see without thinking like a chemist.

Every glossy amber sheet, every salt-feather efflorescence on a wall, every mineral gel pad in a crack hosts **dry biofilms**—colonies that don’t need liquid water sloshing around to do their work. They live in micro-layers where resins hold just enough mobility for ions to pass, where salts can melt on a microscopic scale under a heat pulse and then freeze again, trapping gradients like stored breath.

They are the reason the air tastes the way it does—sharp, metallic, match-struck. They eat what the vents give: reduced sulfur compounds, ammonia traces, carbon monoxide, stray organics that drift down the seam from the surface world. They metabolize aggressively, not into softness, but into **crystals and varnishes**—hard outputs that suit a dry place.

If you scrape a wall where the pale mineral “glow” returns light, you’ll find the glow isn’t just reflective stone. It’s a living glaze: mineral precipitates laid down in precise structures that scatter what little light exists. The pocket’s dimness is not merely tolerated by its residents; in places it has been *engineered*.

### The city’s true inhabitant: a distributed, listening thing
What lives here most completely is not any single grove or curtain or palm. It’s the *network* that has braided itself through the old infrastructure until the distinction between “ruin” and “organism” stops being reliable.

The canals of molten salts and sulfur-rich fluids are the obvious bloodstream. Plants lean into them and drink heat and trace minerals, yes—but the canals also carry **signals**. A change in composition upstream propagates downstream. A new vent pulse shifts redox gradients. The residents respond not with motion, but with *state changes*: resin softening, plates clicking open, palms yawing a few degrees, biofilms changing what they precipitate.

And the pocket is loud enough to matter.

Sound here is not a byproduct; it’s a medium. Those organ-pipe towers, the whistle arrays, the clay plaques that ring when gusts strike—they become a language substrate for anything that can sense vibration. The living varnishes and mineral gels are excellent at that. Some of them incorporate piezoelectric salts naturally; some build them the way shells build nacre, layer by layer, until a wall panel becomes a sensor.

So the “inhabitant” that begins to emerge is something you could call a **choir** if you needed a metaphor: a distributed ecology that listens to wind-notes and answers with changes in stiffness, aperture, resonance. In one district the vines thicken a curtain to damp a corridor’s howl; in another a stand of saltwood slowly opens its plates to turn clicking into a denser, steadier rattle. The city becomes an instrument, and the ecology becomes the musician—without hands, without a brain you could point to, but with feedback loops old enough to be indistinguishable from intention.

If you suspect that the rhythmic papery rustle at the entrance seam is “too regular to be sand,” you’re probably right. There are places where amber curtains have grown across narrow throats and now behave like valves. They slap and relax in time with pressure changes as the pocket breathes—an unplanned diaphragm that, over centuries, has become reliable enough for the rest of the system to depend on.

### How they reproduce and move without “animals”
Nothing here needs to run.

Reproduction is done by **shedding and sailing**: brittle flakes of resin film, dust-grit wrapped in varnish, saltwood plate slivers that detach during a particularly harsh pulse. They don’t crawl; they *ride.* The pocket’s circulating currents carry them into new cracks, onto new walls, into the lee of a new terrace. There they wake when the chemistry is right, reconstituting into a new curtain edge or a new gel pad.

Movement, when it happens at a scale you can notice, is structural: a curtain migrates an inch over a season as it thickens on one edge and erodes on the other; a palm-field slowly rotates its collective face toward a vent that has “learned” a new path; a saltwood avenue advances along a buried foundation line because the stone underneath is fractionally cooler and therefore less hostile to a particular resin phase.

Even the “editing” of buildings is part of their life cycle. A dome is not a relic to them; it’s a cavity to be stabilized, a pressure pocket to be managed, a place where dust can be trapped and sorted and turned into usable gradients.

### Their fear and their defense: water as predator
Water is not merely unhelpful here; it is a kind of invasive violence. The inhabitants treat it the way a forest might treat flame: not a tool, not a gift, but a thing that annihilates the rules they are built from.

Where the ecosystem has had to guard itself—near the seam, near rare ceiling cracks—it has evolved a harsh kind of immune response. Some resins incorporate minerals that, on contact with moisture, **react exothermically** or generate corrosive byproducts. Not because anyone “designed” a trap, but because in a world where water kills, anything that turns water into immediate, localized disaster survives better than anything that merely suffers politely.

That is why the pocket stays pure: it is not only secluded; it is self-protecting.

### What about people?
If there were once builders here, they are not the current tenants in any ordinary sense. The city’s ongoing motion does not have the signature of maintenance—no repairs made against entropy, only adaptations made *with* it. Whatever intelligence is present now is of the slow, distributed kind: the kind that can live in varnish layers and salt gradients, in the way a vent pulse “means” something because the whole basin has evolved to respond to it.

The closest thing to a “someone” is the basin-wide system itself: an ecology that has colonized architecture until architecture is just one of its tissues.

It lives by harvesting wind the way river cities harvest current; it lives by storing heat the way wet forests store water; it lives by turning dust into structure and structure back into dust. It is lush because it is industrious. It is noisy because it is listening. And it is alone of animals not because life failed here, but because life chose a form that doesn’t need to hurry.
