What lives here is, first, the structure itself—not as a metaphor, not as scenery, but as a single distributed animal-plant-lichen-fungus consortium old enough to have forgotten its starting shape.

It has no one body. It is buttress and beam and leaf-mat and root-lattice, and also the things that seal it: the fungal skins that knit cracks shut, the resin glands that flood a stress-fracture before it can propagate, the velvet moss that lines a vent not because moss “likes damp,” but because the vent needs a throttle and the moss is a living baffle. When stormlight runs the canopy veins into that faint map, you are seeing its attention: water diverted, sugars shunted, heat stored and released as needed. The place does not think like a person; it *regulates* like a lung. It *remembers* like grain remembers a bend.

And because it is warm and wet and endlessly surfaced, it grows tenants the way a reef does: in layers that become communities that become organs.

### The near-vent grazers and the mat-keepers
On terraces close to heat seams, where condensation forms around your hand, the “herd-things” are less like deer than like careful, muscular clippers: split hooves that can pinch slick bark, knees that lock to rest on an incline, teeth designed for shaving succulent mats without tearing their root-felt. They graze in slow, synchronized arcs, not because they are social in the usual way, but because the mats they feed on respond to pressure—release sweetness where many hooves tread, toughen where hooves strike sharply. The herd learns the mats’ moods and, in time, the mats learn the herd.

On their hides live pale-line symbionts: not pigment, but thin, branching colonies of something between lichen and skin-flora that mimic the architecture’s vein-language. The colonies drink their sweat and, when the animal presses against a wall to scratch, leave behind a faint chemical “signature” that says *safe*, *food*, *water is here*. It is graffiti made of microbiome.

Their predators do not so much chase as *correct*. A stalker in a buttress-cavity waits for the moment when a gravity-lean shifts and one animal misplaces its clamp-hoof; the predator’s strike is less a pounce than a timed interruption. Many kills look like accidents from a distance: a quiet slip, a soft collapse into moss. The ecosystem is tuned to avoid noise; drama is damped.

### The air between: drift-colonies and current-riders
In the wide empty volumes—those engineered corridors of wind—the gliders aren’t one lineage so much as a set of solutions that converged on the same trick: let the architecture do the work.

Some are true animals with bones like reed and membranes veined with valves. They launch from warm vents, circle once to taste the updraft’s chemistry, then fold into long, economical sails and *disappear* into the dim. They do not need to flap because the place is always exhaling somewhere.

Others are colonial drifters: dozens, sometimes hundreds of thumb-sized bodies braided together by secreted threads into one kite-shape. Individually they are simple—mouth, gut, cling-hooks—but as a colony they are a moving net. They pass through fog banks and come out heavier with water and spores; they skim close to lichen panels and comb pollen off the surface without landing. When a colony needs to turn, it doesn’t steer like a bird; it changes which bodies tense, which slacken, reweighting itself like a living sailboat.

These colonies are hunted by things that don’t look like hunters at all: thin, ribbonlike “banners” that hang from beam-boughs, apparently decorative until a glider brushes them and the banner becomes a mouth. The banner doesn’t snap; it *sticks*, and digestion begins as quiet warmth.

### The veins: jointed filters and the slow, blind carpenters
In the water-grooves live your eel-things—jointed, hand-sided, patient. They are not swimmers; they are *climbers* adapted to flowing film. Each “hand” along their flank can close into a seal-ring, letting them hold position against a pulse, then release in sequence to inch forward. Their mouths rasp biofilm off the groove walls, and their waste, rich with spores, feeds the groove’s cilia-moss.

Below them, in deeper hollows where pools collect warm and sweet, live the slow carpenters: larval forms as thick as your wrist, pale and nearly translucent, that eat resin nodules and excrete a harder, glassier resin in return. They are one of the reasons “erosion hasn’t dismantled anything.” Where stress makes bead-scree, these larvae colonize it, ingest the loose pieces, and cement them into a new, load-bearing crust. The architecture tolerates their burrows the way a tree tolerates sapwood beetles: damage that becomes strength.

They are mostly blind. They navigate by vibration—the deep, slow pump of sap, the tremor of footfalls. If you stand still long enough, one will surface under the water-skin and press its head against the pool rim as if listening to the world with its whole body.

### The hush-predators and the shadow-lenders
Because sound dies, the loud hunters are selected against. The dominant predators here are tuned to pressure and light’s misbehavior.

There are “lichen-faced” ambushers whose faces are truly colonized—camouflage grown, not worn. When they open, their mouths don’t gape; they *unzip* along seams that match bark-plates. Their strike is a suction pulse more than a bite, using the same physics the place uses to move water: a sudden pressure drop that pulls prey off its footing.

Above canopy-level light, where storm pulses make shadows misbehave, live the shadow-lenders: small, black-bodied things that nest on the underside of lichen panels and feed on the brief detachments—on the tiny thermodynamic gradients when a shadow slides. They are not supernatural; they are exquisitely specialized scavengers of an anomaly. When stormlight comes, they swarm in silence, and afterward you may notice that your own shadow looks slightly “thinner,” as if a smear of it has been grazed away.

### The people (if you keep walking)
If there are makers now, you do not meet them often because they do not live in crowds. Their homes are not villages so much as *stations*—chosen load points and water nodes where the living machine can be persuaded, patched, or redirected.

They are sometimes called graftwrights by outsiders, but among themselves the distinction is finer: those who tie (cord-binders), those who cut (scar-makers), those who coax (weight-singers), those who listen (pulse-readers). None of these are metaphors. Their primary literacy is not writing; it is growth.

A graftwright carries:

- a set of obsidian flakes and resin handles for shaving cambium clean without bruising it,
- bone needles for stitching vine-skins together,
- spools of fiber drawn from certain bark layers that swell when wet and tighten into a living knot,
- and a small hollow gourd of ferment-sap used to “warm” reluctant joints and encourage sealing.

They do not fight the architecture; they negotiate with it. If a corridor’s gravity leans wrong for a planned route, they don’t build a railing—they seed a particular moss along one wall whose water weight will, over seasons, pull the lean into a safer angle. If a bridge-vine’s pulse becomes irregular, they don’t replace it; they graft in a secondary “heart” vine alongside, then spend nights sleeping on the span to feel whether the rhythms entrain.

Their communication is mostly tactile and percussive: knuckle taps on rootstone that carry far through the structure, foot-drums on certain resonant ribs, palm-presses against warm walls to “read” condensation patterns. In a place where air is a muffler, wood becomes a telephone.

You see their presence in the small, intimate interventions that don’t break the place’s aesthetic:

- bark peeled in long clean strips, exposing a pale underlayer where a new graft will take,
- ladders made of tied roots that are *grown into* the vent’s throat so they don’t chafe,
- shell-clusters hung under wick-ropes not as decoration but as drip-counters—each shell a different pitch when struck by falling water, a way to hear flow rates without looking,
- and, rarely, a “quiet fire”: a ferment heap of sap-rot and fungus that produces heat with almost no smoke, used to keep a sick joint from cooling and cracking.

They are careful with population for the same reason the place feels empty: too many bodies mean too much chewing, too much pruning, too much waste heat in the wrong chambers. They space themselves like components, not like citizens—one caretaker per basin, one listener per tower, sometimes miles between.

Children, when there are any, learn routes the way other children learn streets. They are taught where distance lies, where it lies, and where it lies *to you*. They are taught not “don’t go there,” but “if you must cross the corridor where your hands look far away, keep one palm on the rib with the three knots; it will pull your sense back into your body.”

### The small things that make the whole possible
The dominant life here is not the dramatic life but the infrastructural life:

- spore-motes that bloom after rain, briefly turning fog banks into living nurseries,
- mites that live under bark-plates and eat the first rot so deeper rot can’t take hold,
- thread-fungi that run like stitching through every joint, turning damage into seam,
- and the leaf-mat “valves” that open and close their pores on a schedule that makes the weather behave.

If you sleep here—truly sleep, long enough to be woken by cycles instead of fear—you start to sense that most creatures are not competing against the place. They are *cooperating with its maintenance*, because maintenance is the most abundant niche.

The grazers prune. The jointed filters keep veins clean. The resin-larvae re-cement scree. The banner-mouths remove excess drift. Even the predators keep herd patterns moving so no terrace is overgrazed. It is not harmony; it is a machine that has learned to recruit every appetite.

And over all of it, the oldest resident is the quiet itself: not emptiness, but a design choice that became a climate. Noise would be wasted energy in wet air and soft surfaces. So life here has become private, tactile, absorbed—written in pressure, in seepage, in the slow confidence of a bridge-vine tightening under your feet as a pulse passes through, reassuring itself, and incidentally reassuring you, that the world is still holding.
