What lives here is not a single ecosystem so much as a negotiated truce between **heat, water, stone, and appetite**—and the truce has been in place long enough that it has grown traditions.

At first you notice the *small* lives, because they are literally on the surface of everything. Then you notice the lives that move. Then, only once you’ve listened long enough, you start to pick out the lives that *keep the whole plateau from choking on itself*.

## The first residents: films, skins, and the city’s invisible pasture
Every wet face in this basin is a pantry.

- On basalt and old concrete, **mineral-eating microbial carpets** spread in layers so thin they only announce themselves when light skates across them: a mother-of-pearl slick one day, a chalky bloom the next. Some are iron-bright and leave rust-colored tears below bolts; others are pale and greasy, breaking into islands when a draft hits.
- In warm channels and settling basins, the films become **thicker, ropey, almost architectural**—gelatinous curtains that wave in slow water, trapping grit and turning it into edible grit. They smell metallic and faintly sweet, like warmed coins.
- In places where exhaust and rainwater mix, the films grow in **stratified bands** keyed to chemistry: sulfur-tolerant near vents, ammonia-feeders near drainage outfalls, iron-oxidizers along the weeping rock cuts. To an experienced eye, the walls are not “dirty”; they’re a **map of gradients**.

This is the plateau’s substitute for leaf and grass. It is why the city can be so loud with animals without ever showing a blade of green.

## The grazers: the slow crowds along canals, roofs, and foundations
Once you know the films are food, the grazers make immediate sense. They are the plateau’s herds—just distributed vertically, draped over infrastructure instead of spread across fields.

### Canal raspers
Along the runoff canals, especially where the water is shallow and warm, there are **broad-backed, many-limbed raspers** that move with the patience of something that has never needed to outrun wind in tall grass.

- Some are hand-sized, living in dense lines like living brush heads, their mouthparts making a soft sandpaper sound as they file slick stone clean.
- The larger ones—dog-sized—carry **pebble-like armor** that is not just camouflage but *diet made visible*: mineral-stiffened plates with a dull metallic sheen. When they turn, their bellies flash pale, and you see rows of specialized scraping teeth.
- They travel in habits, not flocks: the same route along the same canal wall, timed to when the microbial sheen is thickest. Their migration is a maintenance schedule written in muscle memory.

In a city without plants, these animals do a job a lawn would have done in another world: they keep the surfaces from becoming too thickly coated, too slippery, too chemically “hot.” They are sanitation as much as they are livestock—whether anyone claims them or not.

### Roof-lickers and vent-clingers
Above, on sheet metal and concrete terraces, live lighter-bodied grazers that treat warmth as pasture.

- They cling around vents in **tight colonies**, picking at the pearly skins where steam condenses. Some have hook-feet that click against metal; others have pads that leave brief foggy prints.
- When fog thickens, you can hear their feeding as a faint, synchronized patter—tiny impacts, tiny tears, like rain that has learned intent.

Because roofs are safer than canals, these colonies can get enormous. The city tolerates them until they clog a grate or bridge a sensor; then the tolerance becomes work.

### Tunnel filterers
Below, in service flows and warm water channels, there are **pale swimmers** that live in the city’s bloodstream.

- They filter bloom from the water with feathery gill structures, leaving a visible wake of clearer flow behind them.
- Their bodies are soft-looking but not delicate: pressure-adapted, cold-to-warm tolerant, able to survive sudden chemical pulses when some plant upstream flushes a line.

They are one of the reasons the tunnels don’t become anaerobic death-traps. When the filterers vanish from a district, people notice—first in smell, then in corrosion, then in sickness.

## The scavengers: the ones that keep the system from stalling
Any place this wet, this dense, and this crowded with animals risks becoming clogged by its own dead. The plateau avoids that not by cleanliness, but by **specialists**.

- In alley drains and under catwalks, small, quick scavengers—many-legged, flat-bodied—rush any spill of protein. They can strip a carcass to mineral in a night, leaving only the parts that even they can’t use: dense bone, tough shell, the occasional metal-stained tooth.
- Around factory districts, heavier scavengers arrive when shift sirens change. They know when waste falls. They move like municipal workers: unhurried, hard to startle, always with an eye on escape routes.

Their presence is not optional. Without them, the plateau would drown in its own richness.

## The predators: silence with a territory
Predators here are not “wild” in the romantic sense. They are **structural**. They have learned to read the city the way the city reads pressure and flow.

- They nest in the **warm voids**: behind cladding, above transformers, inside bridge undersides where heat rises and rain can’t reach.
- Their bodies tend to be compact and powerful, built for **short, decisive movement** in tight corridors. Long pursuits don’t exist here; ambush does.
- The signs they leave are practical: a scrape of clean stone where something heavy repeatedly launches; a drain-mouth littered with neatly opened shells; the sudden absence of the smaller sounds that normally make an alley feel alive.

In this ecology, “quiet” is a predator’s most reliable camouflage because the city is otherwise never quiet.

## The fliers: weather made of wings
The air is busy, and a lot of that busyness is alive.

Some fliers are small enough to be mistaken for drifting soot until they change direction too sharply. Others are big enough to make you duck when they cross a narrow street between buildings.

- They roost on cable runs, under eaves, in the lee of cooling towers where updrafts are steady.
- Many have **water-shedding skins**: not feathers, not fur, but surfaces that let droplets bead and roll, keeping them from becoming heavy in fog.
- They time their movements to the city’s thermals. A district with fresh waste heat becomes, briefly, a sky-road.

When breeding season hits a neighborhood, it doesn’t just get louder—it gets *thicker*, as if the air itself has gained mass.

## The biggest animals: slow processions of heat
There are, in a few corridors and outlying mudfields, animals large enough to feel like part of the infrastructure.

They move in long, unbroken patterns from one warm district to the next, drawn by the plateau’s heat map. Their size is not flamboyant; it is functional. Big bodies hold warmth. Big bodies tolerate the thin air better. Big bodies can carry mineral armor without paying too much in energy.

People don’t often see them up close unless something has gone wrong—unless a canal gate sticks, a district floods, or a maintenance schedule slips and an animal’s habitual route collides with human plans.

Then you learn how the city deals with them: not with panic, but with procedure.

## Humans: the maintenance civilization
Humans live here the way the city lives here: **layered, patched, and always accounting for water**.

They are not separate from the fauna; they are one more tier in the food web, and they behave like it.

### How they move and dress
- They prefer catwalks and overhead runs not for romance but because ground-level is a negotiation with mud, seep, and scavengers.
- Clothing is designed for constant wet: oiled fabrics, layered wraps that can be dried quickly near vents, boots built to resist both water and mineral abrasion.
- Many wear **breath aids** for exertion—not because the air is toxic, but because high, warm thinness makes hard work expensive. You hear the soft rhythm of valves in stairwells.

### What they eat, and what they “farm”
In the absence of plants, people become cultivators of the things that replace plants.

- They **manage microbial growth** the way other cultures manage crops: encouraging certain films in warm channels, harvesting thicker blooms from settling basins, feeding them into fermentation systems that turn slickness into protein and shelf-stable paste.
- They harvest grazers—especially the roof colonies and canal raspers—selectively, the way coastal peoples harvest shellfish: taking enough to feed a district, leaving enough to keep the surfaces clean and the population stable.
- They render fats from scavenger catches, extract mineral salts from efflorescences, and treat “waste” as a resource stream because here, waste is literally what feeds the base of the ecology.

Cuisine tends to be smoky-salty, rich in minerals, heavy on fermented tangs that cut the metallic undertone of the water. People develop preferences for the output of certain districts the way others prefer valley wines: “north canal bloom” versus “tower-basin skim,” each with its own aftertaste.

### What they value
Their culture prizes what keeps you alive in a place that never dries:

- **Listening** as a civic skill. Children learn the sound of a healthy pump the way children elsewhere learn birdsong. A wrong rhythm in a turbine is as alarming as a scream.
- **Sealing, draining, venting** as everyday craft. The best builders are those who can guide water where it wants to go without pretending it can be banished.
- **Heat etiquette.** Warmth is currency, and there are customs around it: how close you can sleep to a vent, who maintains a district’s public heat exchangers, what counts as stealing warmth, what counts as sharing it.

### Their relationship with the animals
People here are not sentimental about fauna, but neither are they casually cruel. They live in constant proximity to hungry things; sentimentality would get them killed, and indifference would get the city clogged.

Instead there is a practiced, almost bureaucratic respect:

- Certain predators are tolerated because they keep the grazer populations healthy and prevent disease blooms. Others are removed because they’ve learned to hunt humans.
- Scavengers are protected in some districts the way working cats are protected in old port cities: not because they’re beloved, but because the alternative is rot.
- There are workers whose job is basically diplomacy with animal habit: guiding large migrators away from vulnerable infrastructure, relocating nests that block vents, timing repairs to avoid breeding cycles that would turn a catwalk into a war zone.

## Domestic and commensal life: the animals that moved in with people
In a damp city, the boundary between “inside” and “outside” is porous, and so is the boundary between “wild” and “kept.”

People keep small raspers in controlled troughs to maintain certain microbial harvests. They raise filterers in warm tanks to seed tunnel flows after a chemical purge. They tolerate tiny vent-clingers in kitchens because those animals keep condensation skins from getting too thick and dropping into food.

And there are the *true* hangers-on: the creatures that live off human shedding—skin, hair, fiber, crumbs—so embedded in habitation that they’re part of what “home” smells like.

## What it all adds up to: a city that is also a reef
If you stand still long enough, you realize the plateau is not running like a forest or a grassland. It’s running like a **wet industrial reef**:

- Microbes lay down the base layer.
- Grazers keep it edible and thin.
- Predators keep grazers moving.
- Scavengers keep everything from drowning in its own dead.
- Humans maintain the flow of heat and water that allows the whole structure to persist.

What lives here, in other words, is a **single interlocked organism** made of stone and metal and breath and hunger—an inhabited machine that never gets to be clean, only balanced.
