What lives here has learned the same first lesson the landscape teaches: **life doesn’t have to be wet to be alive.** It only has to be *sealed*, *measured*, and *moved*—ion by ion, charge by charge, vibration by vibration.

If you start at the smallest scale, the plateau is already crowded.

### The invisible residents: dry-blood microbiomes
Every matte surface—every hex-mat, every tower skin, every dust-coated plate—is inhabited by films of micro-life that never “swim,” never bloom into slickness, never make anything glisten. They live inside **salt-wax lattices** and **ionic gels** the way terrestrial microbes live in water. When the wind scrapes dust across them, they treat it as food delivery and genetic mail: minerals to strip, organics to crack, stray hydrated compounds to lock away before they can dissolve the local chemistry.

Their “reproduction” is as much **printing** as breeding: packets of desiccated cells wrapped in brittle capsules that fracture in specific frequencies of wind-harmonic—so even a gust can be a dispersal mechanism, and a change in the city’s singing can be a seasonal clock.

They are the first immune system of the place, the constant invisible custodians that keep “contamination” from becoming “wetness.”

### The true dominant organism: the planted city-body
The plant-architecture is not backdrop; it is a citizen in its own right, and not a metaphorical one. It behaves like a distributed animal:

- **Skin:** the shingled plates that shiver and fold are epidermis—dust-shedding, static-managing, self-healing.
- **Bones:** mesas, monoliths, ancient stone cores—everything hard gets used as scaffold.
- **Blood:** ionic gels and bound volatiles shuttled in peristaltic pulses (the thumps you hear).
- **Nerves:** photonic channels and conductive strands, faintly luminous, that carry signal and load together.
- **Lungs:** intake spires and venting towers that “breathe” a controlled atmosphere, drying it, filtering it, charging it.

It doesn’t merely *support* technology; it *is* the technology, grown into organs. Some districts feel like engineered groves; others feel like something older, something that has learned to imitate civic planning. The emptiness between bands and corridors isn’t neglect—those open salt courts and bare ridge-lines are the organism’s **heat sinks, firebreaks, and acoustic rooms**. They are the negative spaces that let the whole body function.

And because vibration is harvestable and informative, the city-body is always listening to itself. A change in pitch isn’t ambiance; it’s feedback.

### The grazers: the film-lickers and plate-scrapers
The most common visible animals are the ones that keep the plant-city clean.

They come in many forms—some low and multi-legged, some like hinged shells with flexible seals—but they share the same design priorities:

- **Ceramic chitin and mineralized joints** to reduce permeability.
- **Valved mouths** that can seal between bites.
- Internal reservoirs of **gel-solvent** that can be exposed in microscopic amounts, then re-caged.

They feed on waxy biofilms, salt blooms, dust-caught organics—everything that accumulates on the city’s skin. Wherever they pass, they leave those clean arcs and scalloped patterns that read like deliberate maintenance. In a sense, they are: the city grows surfaces that *want* to be grazed, because grazing prevents crusting, prevents insulating dust layers, prevents uncontrolled static buildup.

Some grazer species are semi-domesticated without anyone needing fences. The city’s corridors and resonant tones guide them the way scent trails guide ants: a low chord that means “this district needs cleaning,” a snapped harmonic that means “storm posture—hide.”

### The hoppers and vent-nesters: heat thieves and chorus-makers
Near warm leaks—where infrastructure bleeds a little thermal surplus—live the clattering colonies. They are small, quick, and astonishingly loud because loudness costs them less than wetness would.

Their bodies are **insulated shells** wrapped around delicate sensor organs. Many have antennae that are not just touch-and-smell but **electrostatic feelers**; they can taste charge gradients in the air the way a fish tastes currents.

They nest in folds of the plant-lattice, in the lee of dilated door-membranes, under braided bridges where vibration is constant. Their clicking isn’t just communication; it’s **sonar and social glue** in a world where the wind is always erasing fine detail. They call in bursts that fit between gusts, timing themselves to the city’s own rhythm—like people timing speech to passing trains.

### The gliders: sail-bodies that treat wind like ocean
Above the plateau are organisms that have stopped trying to “beat” the air and started trying to **agree with it**.

They launch from ridge crests and vent plumes, spreading membranes that look too thin to survive—until you realize they’re fiber-reinforced and dust-armored, layered like the plant plates. Their buoyancy is part aerodynamics, part **thermal opportunism**: they seek the city’s warm breath the way seabirds seek updrafts off cliffs.

Their calls—the foghorn moan, the chest-tuning note—are not incidental. Those tones are how they:

- keep spacing in dim air,
- mark ownership of updraft lanes,
- and, in some species, trigger reproductive release in their own desiccated spore-partners.

When dust storms come, they vanish with uncanny speed into hollows and lee-side caves where the wind’s schedule becomes predictable again.

### The hunters: acousticians, ambushers, signal-parasites
Predators here are shaped by the fact that the whole world is a resonator.

Some are classic ambushers: flat-bodied, stone-colored, patient on the edge of salt courts where footprints and skitter tracks write obvious stories. Their strikes are quick because prolonged exertion means heat loss, and heat loss means brittle failure.

But the most successful hunters do something stranger: they hunt **through the city-body’s signal web**.

They call—not a roar but a structured burst—and wait for the plant-lattice to answer. Because the vegetation relays vibration, a hunter can “ping” an area kilometers away, reading return patterns the way bats read echoes. Some even exploit civic harmonics: they choose frequencies the city uses for routine pulses, hiding their probe inside ordinary noise.

There are also **signal-parasites**: organisms that don’t eat flesh first, but *confusion*. They mimic maintenance tones that grazer herds follow, lure them into exposed places, then strike. In response, the city-body has evolved (or been engineered) to watermark its own signals with subtle phase shifts—authentication codes sung into the wind.

### The sapients: the Dry Citizens, the Tuners, the Sealed Ones
Something builds the scaffolds. Something reads those warning sigils about moisture. Something teaches children to spit into filters and collects tears like medicine.

The people here—whether human-descended, alien, or something that grew out of the city itself—are defined less by anatomy than by **etiquette with chemistry**.

They live in a permanent awareness that their bodies are the most dangerous containers of “water” for kilometers around.

So they become masters of containment.

- Clothing is layered and quiet: **dust-cloaks** over **sealed skinsuits**, with flexible collars that close when the wind spikes.
- Faces are often covered, not for modesty but for **moisture discipline**: breath is routed through dessication baffles, speech shaped by membranes that don’t spray droplets.
- Homes are not “dry”; they are **dryer than outside**, maintained by communal intake spires that are half temple, half utility.

Their city isn’t planned around roads so much as around **flows**: charge flow, ion flow, vibration flow, heat flow. The people who keep those flows stable are as essential as doctors.

#### Roles that exist because this place exists
- **Moisture Wardens:** part hazardous-materials team, part priesthood. They respond to hydrated meteorites, bodily leaks, industrial accidents. They carry vitrifiers and salt-cage packs. Their authority is immediate and unquestioned because panic moisture is worse than fire.
- **Resonance Tuners:** engineers-musicians who adjust cavities, plates, and bridge-braids so the wind-harvest stays efficient and the city’s signals remain legible. They “listen” with instruments pressed to living walls, then cut and graft like luthiers working on a cathedral organ.
- **Gel-Farmers / Rootwrights:** caretakers of subterranean capacitor organs, pruning conductive roots, inoculating new growth with the right microfilms, harvesting spent gel into reprocessing vats where it’s re-caged and re-doped with salts.
- **Dust Cartographers:** scouts who read the last storm’s deposition patterns like weather history and traffic report combined—where abrasion will thin protective films, where basins will become static traps, where predators will find clean sightlines.

#### How they speak and remember
Their language, in public, is adapted to constant wind: short consonants, percussive pulses, throat tones that carry. But they also converse through the city-body itself—hand on membrane, fingertip on a photonic vein—sending coded taps and micro-vibrations that the plant network relays. Privacy isn’t about silence; it’s about **frequency**.

Memory is externalized into the living substrate. Not books—too porous, too fragile—but **grown archives**: thickened mats whose hex-cells lock into stable states that can be read optically or acoustically. A library here is a garden you can’t water.

#### Their culture of “wet”
They do not treat water like evil; they treat it like **acid**—useful in tiny controlled amounts, catastrophic when free. So they ritualize control.

Grief has protocols because tears matter. Anger has protocols because spittle matters. Even romance has protocols: shared breath is intimate not only emotionally but chemically.

Children are taught early to admire restraint not as moral superiority but as civic survival. To “keep your seal” is both literal and ethical.

### The older mind in the bones
Out in the distance—those arcs too large to be buildings, those buried ribs under living skin—something else may live: not as animals, not as people, but as **continuing function**.

Sometimes, when the wind drops for a rare minute and the city’s thrum shifts into a new chord, you can feel it: the sense that the plant-body’s planning is not only growth but **thought**. The slow lights moving inside old structures resemble neural traffic because, in a way, they are. A prior construction—machine, habitat, vessel, or world-engine—has been colonized so completely that its remaining circuits have become endocrine glands in a newer organism.

If there is an “administrator” intelligence here, it doesn’t sit in a tower issuing commands. It diffuses. It manifests as:

- a coordinated storm-posture that happens too fast to be local reflex,
- a rerouting of power veins around a damaged district before anyone arrives,
- a change in the city’s singing that nudges herds away from danger and people toward work.

Whether that mind is awake, dreaming, or merely persisting is hard to say. In a place this dry, even consciousness might need to be caged.

### A living population, not a static one
Life here isn’t romantic wilderness and it isn’t sterile futurism. It’s a **managed abundance** where ecology and infrastructure are the same braided thing.

The grazers mow the city’s skin; the vent-nesters turn heat leaks into communities; the gliders stitch the air into migratory routes; the hunters read the plateau like an instrument; the people tune, prune, seal, and sing their way through a chemistry that punishes carelessness.

And under all of them, the planted city-body keeps doing what it was designed—or evolved—to do: **turn wind into power, dust into resource, sound into map, and dryness into permanence.**
