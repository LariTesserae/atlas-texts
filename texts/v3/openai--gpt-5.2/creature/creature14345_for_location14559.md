What lives here is not a community in the usual sense—no chased calories, no teeth, no wings, no busy inventory of bodies moving through air. It’s a set of surface-negotiations: films that cling, mats that seal, towers that stand still and let the world pass through them as gas and light.

Life on this plateau is less an *inhabitant* than a *finish*—the green is a functional coating laid down on a machine that is also a landscape.

And when you get close enough to stop calling it “vegetation,” it starts to separate into kinds.

## 1) The first residents: the skin that makes the ground livable
At the boundary where mineral meets atmosphere, there’s almost always a layer you don’t notice at first because it doesn’t announce itself as organism. It’s a **sheen**—a dry, faintly iridescent bloom in the microcracks of basalt-like plates and across the salt-ceramic polygons. In angled light it looks like a misbehaving reflection; in shadow it reads as nothing at all.

This is the plateau’s oldest life, and it behaves like **living flux**.

- It sits inside the cooling cracks and the porous crust the way resin sits in wood grain.
- It tolerates heat and desiccation not by “holding water,” but by using **oily ionic slurries** and waxy, conductive gels as its internal medium.
- It feeds on what the vents supply: reduced gases, trace organics, sulfur compounds, metal-bearing aerosols. It doesn’t need seasons; it needs throughput.

It grows in the same geometry the plateau prefers: along seams, along polygon edges, along ridges where gas rises and minerals precipitate. In some places you can trace a ventline from kilometers away because the life on it is subtly darker—pigments tuned not just to light but to chemistry, to the exact recipe of what’s coming up from below.

This layer doesn’t merely survive on the rock. It **conditions** it. The crusts that look “manufactured” are partly manufactured by these organisms: they bind dust that would otherwise stay loose, they catalyze precipitation at the surface, and they leave behind mineralized residues that become tomorrow’s matte plates.

If the plateau is a factory, this is the part that keeps the pipes from scaling in the wrong places—by choosing where the scale forms.

## 2) The mats: felt that breathes
The green felted mats are the first obvious “plantlike” residents, and they are as much architecture as organism: dense, springy, warm to the touch in the way compost is warm—except there’s no rot here, no wetness, no animal traffic to turn it.

Their underside is the important side.

Where you expect roots, they have a **contact mesh**: millions of stiffened fibers that press into pores and microfractures, tuned to capture **vapor and supercritical seepage** the way other ecosystems capture runoff. Not drinking—*filtering*. They sit on the crust like a membrane on an intake.

Those reflective flecks in the mat are not decoration. They’re built-in hardware:

- tiny mineral plates that act as **heat spreaders** and light-shapers,
- conductive grains that let the mat run slow electrochemical gradients across its body,
- and in some species, crystalline inclusions that function like **batteries**, storing charge during high vent-output and releasing it across still periods.

The mats do two things at once: harvest the air, and **seal the ground**. Where they dominate, the salt-ceramic polygons are tighter, their edges less crumbly—as if the mats are stitching the surface closed against loss.

They reproduce without needing wind or water. When a mat patch ages, it becomes brittle at its edges and begins to **flake** in thin, light chips. Those chips are not dead. They carry dormant nodes that wake when they land across a warm seam or chemically favorable crust. Dispersal here is mostly a matter of tiny lift—thermal breath from vents, electrostatic cling, the faintest pressure changes in an atmosphere that rarely commits to wind.

You can read the plateau’s slow gas plumbing by watching where the mats choose to be thick.

## 3) The fronds and tubes: plumbing without water
Rising from the felt are the translucent stalks and fronds that look like plants only because “plant” is the nearest word for anything that grows upward and stays put.

They are **chimneys** as much as bodies.

Their inner ribs form channels, yes—but the “sap” is a thin, resinous solvent whose job is not to transport water but to move:

- dissolved hydrocarbons and salts,
- reactive sulfur and nitrogen compounds,
- suspended conductive particles,
- and occasionally, captured aerosols of metal that will later be locked into tissue like rebar.

When you split a stalk and the resin film evaporates sluggishly, you’re watching a solvent designed for this plateau’s rules: stable in dry heat, unwilling to flash off, able to carry ions without needing a drop of water.

These stalks are often communal. What looks like a single clump is sometimes a **stacked consortium**:

- a basal mat providing chemical intake and electrical buffering,
- tube-fronds extending surface area into clean air for gas exchange,
- and embedded microbial layers that sit in between like circuitry—regulating redox reactions the way enzymes regulate metabolism elsewhere.

Their green is not purely “photosynthetic green.” It’s a working pigment, tuned to manage **radiation and temperature** and to skim energy even when sunlight is not the most reliable input. Some stalks look greener at dawn and duller at noon because their pigments *shift state*—closing into heat-shedding configurations as the ground returns its stored warmth.

They do not flutter because movement is expensive and unnecessary. Their strength is in **static efficiency**.

## 4) The canes and panels: glass-grown towers
The tall jade canes and fan-panels are the plateau’s most striking inhabitants, and also its most alien: they look like botany imitating manufactured material, or manufactured material discovering it can reproduce.

They are **mineral-skeleton organisms**—not plants that happen to mineralize, but lineages that use precipitation as a growth method. Where other life builds cellulose and lignin, these build frameworks of silicate and salt ceramics, laid down in laminae so regular they resemble poured glass.

They stand only where the chemistry is steady enough to guarantee supply. Their roots—if you can call them that—are narrow couplers pinned to vent margins and warm seams, tapping constant outflow.

They orient with “near-perfect discipline” because they are essentially living radiators and collectors:

- some panels maximize light at low angles, then “lock” their internal state to dump heat when the sun is high;
- others are tuned less to sunlight than to **gas flow**, positioned to skim a thin layer of vent-fed volatiles that hugs the ground before dispersing.

Up close, their surfaces are not smooth. They are ribbed with microscopic fins, like heat sinks, and those fins host thin microbial films—the same foundational residents—turning the panel into a vertical reef for chemistry.

They don’t need to sway. They’re engineered to endure stillness: flexible where thermal stress would crack them, rigid everywhere else. When they die, they don’t rot. They **devitrify**—their tissues slowly lose their living organization and become pure brittle mineral. The plateau gains another shard field, another terrace-worth of raw material.

The boundary between “organism” and “geology” is not a line here. It’s a gradient.

## 5) The quiet symmetries: life cycles without animals
With no grazers, no pollinators, no burrowers, no decomposers in the familiar sense, the residents handle their own endings.

They senesce in controlled ways:

- Mats “turn off” sections and let them mineralize into protective crust.
- Tube-fronds pinch themselves shut and harden, becoming scaffolds for the next generation.
- Panels crack along pre-designed fault lines, shedding viable splinters that can anchor elsewhere.

Nutrients don’t circulate through food webs; they circulate through **phase changes** and **settling**—from vapor to film to mineral to film again. The plateau’s living things are less like a pyramid and more like a set of filters in series, each tuned to a different thickness of air, a different temperature band, a different chemical ratio.

There is competition, but it’s quiet and spatial. A mat will overrun a ceramic flat not by speed but by **sealing it**, depriving rival films of access to seepage. A panel will “win” a site by casting a narrow strip of shade that changes surface temperature just enough to favor its own associated microbes. Territories are thermal and chemical first; geometry follows.

## 6) What “fauna” becomes when it doesn’t become animals
If you insist on the idea of fauna—something that behaves like an animal—this place refuses you. Nothing hunts, nothing flees, nothing crosses the open spaces in a hurry.

But the plateau does have motion inside it.

The closest thing to “behavior” is how these organisms modulate their internal chemistry in response to the vents: pigments shifting, conductive pathways opening and closing, surfaces becoming more or less reflective. At night, some mats grow slightly warmer, not from external heat but from **metabolic throttling**—using stored gradients when the sun is gone and the vent supply is steady.

If there are movers here, they are microscopic and transient—cells surfing along films, drifting on thermal breaths from cracks—never becoming bodies large enough to leave a track or cast a second kind of shadow.

The silence isn’t emptiness. It’s a different scale of life.

## 7) The plateau’s true resident: a coupled system
After a while, the question “what lives here?” becomes harder to separate from “what is the place doing?”

Because the residents don’t merely occupy the plateau; they complete it.

- Vents provide warm gas and trace feedstock.
- Minerals precipitate in clean layers.
- Organisms intercept those layers, regulate where they form, and convert some of the chemical throughput into structured green tissue.
- That tissue mineralizes back into crust and plate.
- The new crust changes the next round of vent margins and capillary pathways.

A closed loop, not just ecologically but architecturally.

What lives here, then, is a **living manufacturing line**: a set of organisms evolved to thrive where stability is the only weather, where dryness is not an adversity but a given, and where “lushness” means having enough energy and enough feedstock to keep building thin green machinery across an unfinished world.
