They live like you’d expect cliff-dwellers to live if cliff-dwelling weren’t a romance but a municipal requirement: in layers, in routes, in schedules that assume the world will be slightly wrong tomorrow and workable anyway.

### The people who can’t afford to be surprised

The most visible residents are the ones with harness marks on their bodies and a permanent inventory running behind their eyes. They move with the peculiar economy of people who have spent years in a place where a misstep isn’t a lesson, it’s an obituary.

They are not all “workers” in the broad, heroic sense outsiders like to use. They are specialists in *continuity*:

- **Rail and cable crews** who treat the city like a constantly detuning instrument. They don’t just repair: they re-tension, re-level, re-measure. Their hands learn the feel of a cable that has begun to disagree with the distance it claims to span. They speak in numbers, but the numbers are always attached to dates.
- **Pumpwrights and condensate-fitters** who understand the city’s water as a negotiated phenomenon. They know which surfaces sweat reliably, which drip-lines have migrated, which fin arrays “catch” fog with the least argument from the air. Their work is half plumbing, half weathercraft.
- **Foundry and generator hall crews** who live with soot under the nails and a faint tremor in the bones from standing near constant vibration. They are the ones who smell like hot oil even in the cold, and who can tell you which machine is failing by the character of its thrum traveling through young stone.
- **Platform-masons**—still called masons even when they’re bolting prefabricated plate—who specialize in building “convincing” structures: things that persuade curing rock to hold still. They have folk rules about where to set anchors (not near bright mineral veins; not where your lantern’s edge-sketching looks too crisp; never in stone that remembers footprints).

They share a culture of small cautions. Everyone knocks on railings before leaning. Everyone tests a stair by stomping the first tread twice and listening, not for strength but for the *reply*. Children learn early that sound is sometimes a liar, but it is still worth hearing because it tells you what lies are currently fashionable.

These people aren’t superstitious in a simple way; they are procedural. Their prayers look like checklists.

### The ones who map what won’t sit still

If the city has an aristocracy, it is not old money so much as **old measurements**.

There are surveyors who aren’t surveyors in the textbook sense but in the local sense: men and women with calibrated lanterns, plumb-bobs that have been modified and re-modified, string-lines treated with oils so they don’t stiffen in the cold. Their notebooks are thick with cross-outs. They travel in pairs because a single witness is considered an opinion.

They’re employed by offices with names that sound like jokes until you need them: the Bureau of Span Compliance, the Office of Buoyancy Ratings, the Department of Auditory Irregularities. The paperwork is real, stamped and archived, because the city has learned that the only way to live with a world that shifts is to become *better at remembering* than the world is at staying consistent.

These mappers are quietly resented and quietly essential. Resented because they can close a bridge with an orange placard and a signature. Essential because they can reopen it with another placard and another signature, and that is the closest thing to certainty most residents will ever receive.

They also develop a peculiar intimacy with the mountain. Not affection—something more like professional acquaintance. They speak of districts the way sailors speak of currents. They do not say “that stairwell is haunted.” They say, “that stairwell is running light this season.”

### Couriers, climbers, and the art of shortcuts

Between terraces, across voids, through elevator shafts that sometimes deliver you one level off from where you expected, there is a class of resident whose job is to treat inconsistency as a resource.

The **couriers** are the city’s connective tissue. Their bodies are wiry, their fingers callused from rope and rail, and their faces have the calm look of people who are always doing two kinds of navigation at once: the official routes on the map, and the unofficial routes that exist only when the mountain is in a certain mood.

They know the listening walls, but they don’t trust them. They use them the way you’d use a rumor: to save time, to avoid a crowded platform, to learn that a freight cable has stopped singing and should be avoided. They carry small charms not because they believe in magic, but because they believe in *habits*—and habits calm the nervous system, which matters when the bridge decides to be longer today.

Some of them become **wrong-elevator specialists**. It’s never an advertised skill, but everyone knows someone who can “miss” an elevator the right way: step out before the doors fully open, press a button with a chipped thumbnail, keep their eyes on the lantern beam as if it were a compass needle. These are the people who come back with stories of lower catwalks that aren’t in the registry, of wet metal corridors that smell like older industry, of fog with structure.

Most don’t go looking for the under-plan. But in a city where the bottom is an argument, you get a few residents whose curiosity becomes a vocation.

### The damp-gardeners and the shrine-keepers

Where condensate gathers, people gather too—not only because water is useful, but because the green pockets feel like the only places where the mountain’s unfinishedness is generous rather than indifferent.

There are **drip-gardeners** who cultivate moss and fern the way others cultivate grain. They don’t plant so much as *encourage*: redirect a gutter, roughen a beam to hold spores, shade a ledge with scavenged sheet metal, hang a mesh curtain to coax fog into a particular corner. Their gardens are not ornamental; they are pantries, pharmacies, and humid refuges from metal-dry air.

In those same pockets live the **shrine-keepers**, often gardeners and pumpwrights and widows and anyone else who spends time where the city sweats. The shrines aren’t grand: a bowl where condensate collects, a little rack of rusted tools, a knot of wire shaped into something almost like a hand. Offerings are practical—fresh cloth, a newly sharpened blade, a strip of insulating rubber—things you’d want if you were a place that constantly chews at infrastructure.

They don’t talk about “worship” unless pressed. They talk about being *polite*. The mountain is conversational, they say, in the way a dangerous neighbor can be conversational: it answers if you address it correctly.

A few shrine-keepers become something else: listeners. Not to gossip, but to the places where sound comes wrong. They will tell you, gently, not to repeat what you heard in the rock until you know whether it belongs to the present.

### Children who grow up vertical

The children are the easiest proof that this isn’t merely a worksite—it’s a home.

They play games that would look like training exercises elsewhere: racing along catwalks while touching every third support post, counting steps on a stair this week and checking again next week, daring each other to stand at the edge of a buoyancy corridor and drop pebbles to see how long the hesitation lasts. They can tell districts apart by wind-sound alone: the particular keening of a truss gap, the drum of a loose plate, the way a certain pipe forest turns gusts into a low organ note.

Their scraped knees and rope-burned palms are normal. So is their precocious caution. The games have rules, and the rules are half safety, half cosmology:
- Always clip in before you laugh.
- Never chase a ball into fog without a tether.
- If the lantern beam cuts off like a wall, turn around—someone else can be brave tomorrow.

They learn early that the city’s “edge” isn’t a line; it’s a condition.

### The animals that have made industry into habitat

If you look up, you see that the city is also a cliff-face rookery, a warm-breathing cavern system, a layered reef.

- **Birds** are everywhere, but not in a single shape. There are the small, agile ones that treat cableways as branches, hopping and fluttering along the lines with an electrician’s confidence. There are heavier birds that ride the updrafts between terraces and vanish into cloudbanks, only to reappear at sudden angles as if the fog has doors. There are colony birds under bridge decks whose droppings become mineral crusts—so much so that some residents quietly harvest the stuff for industrial use, scraping it like ore.
- **Cable-mammals**—long-limbed, narrow-bodied creatures with clever hands and feet that seem to understand grating—live in the interstices: behind warm ventilation stacks, inside disused conduit runs, under the lips of platforms where wind can’t reach. They steal bright things. They learn which warning paint is still fresh and which is only memory.
- **Amphibians** thrive in drip-lines where they should not be able to. In the cool dim, slick-bodied and fearless, they treat condenser runoff as a river and the moss mats as an entire continent. Their skins sometimes pick up a faint metallic sheen from the air and the mineral dust, as if the city is slowly plating them.
- **Insects** are the city’s constant weather. At night—night as a deepening pewter—lamp halos fill with wingbeats. Some are ordinary scavengers; some are attracted to bioluminescent fungi and live their whole lives inside those green pockets like moths inside lantern glass.

And the bioluminescence isn’t only fungus, though fungus is the most blatant. There are larvae that pulse with slow internal light, and small creeping things in the moss that flash when disturbed, turning a gardener’s careful hand into a moving constellation.

The city pretends these animals are incidental. It issues sanitation orders. It posts signs. But everyone knows that half the place’s vitality comes from the way life has colonized the warm leaks and damp seams of industry.

### The residents you can’t put on a census

Finally there are the ones locals talk around rather than about. Not because naming them invokes them, but because naming them forces you to claim you understand.

Some are simply people living off-map: squatters in unfinished projects, families in maintenance voids, crews who “temporarily” occupy a disused warehouse for ten years because it sits in a stable patch of reality. They pay rent in repairs. They belong to the city the way moss belongs to a beam: unofficially, undeniably.

But some are stranger.

There are **fog-shapes** in the vertical basins that don’t behave like wind eddies. The eye insists on interpreting them—street-grids, archways, long corridors of darker mist—because the human mind hates a formless down. Most residents learn to let the interpretation happen without believing it. A few don’t.

There are **echoes that arrive with personality**—not just sounds out of place, but tones that seem to lean close, as if the mountain is trying on voices. In certain listening walls, you can hear work songs that no one sings anymore, spoken names of platforms that were never officially built, laughter that fades into something like static.

And then there are the **under-plan believers** who don’t act like zealots so much as urban explorers with a theology. They trade hand-painted arrows, chalk marks that only mean something on certain days, and warnings about elevators that “skip.” They swear there are lower districts that mirror the upper ones imperfectly: foundries running cold, gardens made of pale fungus instead of fern, rails that lead somewhere that feels like home but isn’t registered under any bureau.

Whether those districts exist as places, or as the mountain’s misfiled intentions, or as a collective story given weight by repetition—no one can prove. That’s part of the point.

### What truly lives there

If you stay long enough, you realize the most accurate answer isn’t a list of species or trades. What lives there is a kind of personhood shared across organisms and systems: a population—human, animal, fungal, mechanical—that has adapted to a world that is still setting.

They live by **tethering**: ropes, rails, schedules, forms, rituals, routes, gardens, songs. They tether water out of fog. They tether themselves to catwalks. They tether meaning to dates on maps.

And under all of them, like a huge slow animal that hasn’t decided what shape it wants, the young mountain lives too—curing, shifting, conducting sound through its hollow seams, letting distances stretch and compress like muscles. Not malevolent. Not benevolent. Simply present in a way that demands response.

The city’s inhabitants have answered, over generations, with a life that is always clipped in, always listening, and never entirely convinced that “down” is only a direction.
