What lives here doesn’t arrive as silhouettes against the sky. It declares itself sideways—in what gets used up, what gets left behind, what repeats with too much consistency to be mere weather.

### The first residents: the films that aren’t quite organisms

Around the warm seeps and the shaded sweating faces of boulders, the iridescent mats are the basin’s primary population, and “mat” is only the least-wrong word for them. They’re not leaflike, not fibrous, not cellular in the familiar way; up close they read as a *finish* the rock has learned to grow.

They begin as a haze at the edge of condensation: a faint, greasy-looking interference sheen that appears where vapor cools and mineral dust settles. Over weeks it thickens into something you can lift with a fingernail—except it doesn’t lift so much as it *lets go*, reluctantly, like damp paper that prefers the stone. Its body is layered: a top skin that repels sudden wetting, a middle that holds water as bound moisture, and a bottom that keys into pores and microcracks with adhesive threads made of polymerized salts.

It lives by taking advantage of the basin’s steady contradictions:

- **Heat gradients** are food: not warmth in itself, but the reliable movement of vapor condensing and re-evaporating, which lets the film harvest ions as they migrate.
- **Electrochemistry** is food: where the “machine-rock” leaks charge, the film aligns and grows along invisible fields, thickening in bands that match the patterns of static in the air.
- **Metal availability** is food: it binds copper, nickel, iron into dull chelates, and in doing so it both detoxifies its own environment and lays down the first ingredients of future lattice.

It reproduces without hurry. When it dries beyond recovery it doesn’t “die” so much as *become infrastructure*: a powdery residue of salts and complex organics that roughens the stone, making the next generation easier to anchor. Every film is both organism and paving stone.

If you watch long enough—really watch, with the patience the basin demands—you can see its motion. Not crawling, not waving: a slow change in gloss, the subtle retreat from exposed ridges, the deliberate thickening in a crescent where a shadow falls at noon. Its behavior is a kind of positioning, like a sundial that feeds.

### The second residents: the machine-rock itself, older than any individual living thing

There are places where it’s hard to keep the categories separate. The nickel-lined hexagonal pores, the sintered sheets, the branching channels—these don’t merely *host* life. They participate in it, as if the basin long ago grew a set of mineral organs and then forgot the difference between geology and metabolism.

The “fans” you hear are not necessarily machines in a chamber; they may be **convection systems** tuned into stable loops by the lattice’s geometry. The periodic hisses, the relay-clicks, the grind-without-drift—those can be read as the basin’s **valving**, the way a lung makes noise when it keeps itself open.

In places, the lattice is coated with the same iridescent polymer the films make. In other places the lattice seems to *make* its own coatings—thin conductive skins that tarnish, shed, and regrow in a cycle that matches the scheduled hisses. It is not conscious, but it is persistent in the way reefs are persistent: a structure that maintains conditions for its own continuation.

And it “feeds” too, if you allow the word to stretch:

- It consumes **chemical disequilibria**: oxidizing here, reducing there, forever keeping a gradient alive.
- It consumes **water movement**: not rivers, but capillary creep, thin films in cracks, the slow breathing of moisture through porous stone.
- It consumes **time**: because time is what lets small advantages compound into architecture.

If the basin has a dominant organism, it may be this: a planet-slow assemblage of conductive pores, warm channels, and self-maintaining chemistry that just happens to resemble industry when heard from the surface.

### The small animals: mineral drinkers and charge-followers

Where there is a reliable surface wetness, even in millimeter-thin form, there are grazers.

**The pale arthropod-things** are the most visible. They look like mites or tiny isopods made from translucent wax, their joints and plates dusted with crystals as if the basin has begun to fossilize them before they’re finished living. They don’t seek puddles; puddles are too temporary, too exposed. They go to *wet stone*.

Their mouths are not built for chewing plants—there are none—but for scraping and licking: a rasping pad that can harvest the film without tearing it all away, leaving enough for regrowth. They drink by pressing their bodies flat so their underside can wick moisture directly. When the air is drier they become miserly with movement, tucking legs close and letting static charge hold dust to their shells as camouflage and insulation.

They navigate partly by texture and partly—strangely—by the basin’s electrical moods. In the weak static zones they wander. In stronger zones they align their paths along the same invisible lines that the films thicken on. Sometimes you find them clustered not around the wettest crack, but around the most *charged* one.

Predation is scarce, but not absent. The basin supports a kind of minimalist food web:

- Film → scraped by arthropods.
- Film and residues → consumed by worms in protected cracks.
- Arthropods and worm-segments → occasionally taken by something rarer and harder to catch sight of.

### The crack fauna: the slow worms that turn film into stone

Near warm vents and in deep fissures, **silica-sheathed worms** live where temperatures are steady and the air is always half-wet. They look like pale cords lacquered in glass, their bodies segmented not with obvious rings but with changes in sheen—as if their skins are being varnished continuously.

They feed with a combination of grazing and chemistry. A worm will press its front end to the film, exude a thin solvent that loosens polymers and trapped ions, then absorb the resulting slurry. As it moves, it leaves behind a trail that hardens: not slime, but a faint glaze of silica and bound salts. Over long periods, their movement becomes part of the basin’s surface treatment—another way the land is “finished.”

They reproduce in a manner that suits the place: not swarms, not seasons, but **scheduled releases**. Once in a long while—on the same kind of timetable as the hissing vents—thin pale threads appear near a crack mouth, like a snag of spider silk. They’re egg-cases or bud-casts, hardened in place, waiting for the next reliable pulse of humidity.

The worms are also the basin’s recyclers. They take what the films make, and what the machine-rock leaks, and they compress it into something the rock can keep.

### The rarest residents: things you mostly infer

Some inhabitants are almost all absence and evidence.

There are **shadow-dwelling “stitchers”**—not a confirmed shape so much as a pattern: arthropod clusters that vanish overnight, worm-trails interrupted cleanly, a faint line in the salt polygons where something heavier than a mite crossed and didn’t leave a clear footprint. If you ever see one, it is brief: a ribbon-thin body hugging the ground, moving with a gliding efficiency that suggests it rides the basin’s microcurrents of warm air. It doesn’t hunt often; nothing here can afford excess. When it does, it takes only enough to persist.

There are also **microfauna** you don’t meet with the naked eye: things living within the films, within the residue powders, within the porous lattice itself. Their presence is suggested by changes in color, by the way one patch of sheen resists drying longer than another, by the faint sweetness in the heated clay smell that isn’t purely mineral.

Even the loudness may be, in part, alive: the basin’s acoustic signature could be the byproduct of countless small valves and phase changes triggered by living coatings that open and close pores on a timetable.

### What “living here” means

Nothing in the basin tries to green the world. There is no reaching upward, no colonizing of the horizon, no exuberant conversion of sunlight into bodies. Life here is not expansion; it is *fit*.

The inhabitants are creatures of margins measured in degrees and microns:

- the thin wetness on shaded stone,
- the predictable breath of warm vapor,
- the reliable charge in a nickel-lined pore,
- the slow accumulation of residue that becomes a foothold.

They are quiet because the land is loud. They are small because the processes are vast. And they persist because, in a place where change never arrives dramatically, persistence is the only successful kind of motion.

If you had to name the basin’s true resident, it wouldn’t be the mite or the worm or even the film. It would be the *system*—a long-stabilized partnership between heat, moisture, mineral lattices, and the few forms of biology that learned to live like chemistry: thin, tenacious, and perfectly timed to a world that has already decided what it will keep doing.
