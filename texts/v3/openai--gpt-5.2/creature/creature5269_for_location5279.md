What lives here is, first of all, the city itself.

Not metaphorically—the district is not a backdrop that hosts life. It is a set of living tissues arranged into streets and platforms and cisterns, and the thing that “inhabits” it is the continuous metabolism that keeps those tissues aligned with purpose. If you stand still long enough, you can feel the residency in small accommodations: a beam that subtly swells where your weight repeats, a leaf-valve that relaxes open because your body heat says “occupied space,” the minute tightening of vine fibers around a press-frame when the machine wakes and asks—through vibration—for bracing.

But that’s only the first layer. Living inside that larger living thing are other lives—some deliberate, some tolerated, some opportunistic. They don’t present as a bustling population. They present as *a caretaking presence*, as if the district is never empty, yet rarely crowded.

### The primary resident: a coordinated vegetal civic body

The structure-plants are not just shaped; they behave like a municipal organism with many organs:

- The condensation ribs are its lungs, taking the night’s breath and turning it into drinkable film.
- The root decks are its tendons—tensioned planes distributing load, deciding where feet may go without being told.
- The seal-plants are its skin, closing when cold, loosening when safe.
- The filter towers are its liver, quietly trapping the dark byproducts of work.

If there is “mind” here, it isn’t centralized. It’s closer to a distributed reflex—responses traveling through cambium and phloem, through graft junctions engineered long ago, through rhythms of pressure and sap flow that have become as regular as shift changes.

The governance you sense—the way growth keeps clearances, the way a tool is held but not eaten—has a physical basis: a citywide memory of stress and use encoded in growth patterns. The repeating leaf-pattern “code” isn’t decoration; it’s a record of instruction and an antenna for it. In the older groves, you can find places where bark has healed over ceramic nodes the way flesh heals around an old splinter: not rejecting them, not forgetting them, incorporating them into the continuing plan.

This is why the district feels “at peace.” Peace, here, means: the system is in equilibrium and knows how to correct itself.

### The second resident: the human (or human-descended) tenders

People do live here—just not in the way cities usually advertise their inhabitants.

They are sparse, partly because the city doesn’t require constant manual control, and partly because the work is specialized: pruning is not simple cutting; wiring is not simply wiring; maintenance is as much listening as fixing. The residents you encounter (if you’re allowed to encounter them) tend to share a set of traits shaped by the place:

- **They move as if negotiating with architecture.** Not hurrying, not timid—simply attentive to what’s load-bearing today and what’s in a growth phase. They step where the root deck is ready to be stepped on, and avoid the springier sections the way you avoid fresh mortar.
- **They carry small, quiet tools.** Not big axes or loud saws. More like narrow grafting blades, hand augers for inserting ceramic dowels, clamps that don’t bite into living bark, tuning-forks or resonant rods used to test whether a beam is hollowing or densifying properly.
- **They smell faintly of resins and mineral dust.** Sap-stain on fingers, filter soot worked into a paste that becomes pigment or sealant, the metallic tang that never quite leaves skin when you live around old flywheels.

Their homes are less “houses” than *pockets of negotiated interior*: chambers within fused groves, sleeping platforms grown deliberately away from major load paths, rooms that breathe. You can tell a dwelling because the plants around it have learned a particular kind of stillness—leaf layers arranged to keep consistent shade, drip routed away from entrances, the living facade pores behaving in a more human-friendly rhythm.

Their culture—what little you can infer from traces—is industrial but not extractive. They don’t “use” the district so much as keep agreements with it.

- Work seems timed to dew and fog: early hours for harvesting moisture, mid-morning for running pumps while the air is still cool, afternoons for dry processes in the kiln yards, evenings for checking growth lines and making small corrections before night swelling begins.
- Fire is treated like an honored hazard. Furnaces are fed carefully, exhaust guided, soot collected like a tax that must be paid and then reinvested.
- They keep distance between workshops not only for safety but for *privacy of process*. Each courtyard is its own discipline: pressing, pumping, filtering, grafting, composting. The spacing reads like a social arrangement as much as an engineering one.

If you meet one, they don’t talk at first as if speech is the default. They look, listen, and often put a hand to living structure the way someone else might check a clock. When they do speak, it’s practical and minimal—names for growth states, warnings about “hungry” fungal strips, instructions like “don’t lean there; it’s learning.”

They may not consider themselves the city’s owners. They behave like resident mechanics inside a huge, ongoing machine that happens to be made of wood and leaf.

### The third resident: fungi as sanitation, archive, and slow intelligence

The district’s lack of animal scavengers gives fungi an enlarged role. They are not incidental rot. They are a managed utility—and, possibly, another layer of “decision-making.”

In the compost courts and under-eaves strips, the fungal growth is cultivated in bands and shelves with the neatness of plumbing. Some of it is obvious: pale threads binding leaf-litter into stable mat, dark shelves digesting scrap wood into soil amendments. Some of it is less obvious until you notice the patterning: mycelial veins that mirror the layout of paths, thickening near workshops, thinning near living quarters, as if the sanitation system has preferences.

The tenders likely use fungi as:

- **A recycler** of plant waste into predictable substrates.
- **A filter partner**, eating what the porous chimneys trap.
- **A diagnostic tool**—certain blooms marking chemical imbalance, certain textures indicating stress in the root decks.

And it’s hard, in a place like this, not to suspect the fungi also function as an archive: a slow record of what’s been spilled, burned, repaired, celebrated. If the plant-city is reflex, the fungal-city is memory—long-term, chemical, patient.

### The tolerated residents: insects, small birds, and the few bold mammals

Because the district is so coordinated, and because decay is managed, the animal life that survives here tends to be either discreet or highly specialized.

- **Solitary pollinators** that work the high canopy layers in brief windows when leaf-pores open and the air currents are “set” for it. They’re not abundant, but they’re reliable—thin-bodied insects that don’t swarm, that keep to the city’s wind-lanes like commuters.
- **Small, cautious birds** that nest where the living facades provide warmth without exposure. They don’t sing much. They flutter and vanish. You mostly know them by the sudden, neat drop of a seed husk on a mineral plate, or the quick shadow crossing filtered light.
- **Leaf-litter scratchers**—tiny things that live in the living mat: beetles, pale mites, perhaps a small, dark, whiskered animal that you never see twice. If anything mammalian is here, it survives by staying out of the city’s way: feeding on fungi in permitted strips, drinking from pocket cistern overflow, retreating before dawn when the tenders move.

Even the pests feel negotiated with. The district does not invite a lot of freeloaders; its surfaces are waxy, its routes are deliberate, its waste is collected. Anything that lives here without being part of the plan has to be exceptionally good at being ignored.

### The other inhabitants: old machines that have become something like citizens

The pump-houses and press-frames are not alive, but they behave like long-tenured residents. They have habits. They take up space with the confidence of things that will still be here next year.

Some of the district’s “population” is this: mechanisms so integrated into bark casings and root beds that the line between tool and organ has blurred. The city makes room for them; in return, they provide rhythm.

When a flywheel turns, it broadcasts a low vibration through its mount. The living material hears that vibration as strain and responds by bracing or relaxing. In that sense, the machines are not just powered; they are *communicating*. They are part of what keeps the district’s schedule.

There may even be machines no one actively remembers how to build from scratch anymore—kept running because the city expects their particular pulse, and because the tenders know that stopping them would not simply end a process; it would alter an ecology.

### Who you’re most likely to *feel* watching you

Visitors often describe a sensation of being observed, but not by eyes.

It comes from the way the district responds to presence: airflow adjusts; pores close; a walkway firms under repeated steps; a vine that would have crossed your path chooses not to. The “watcher” is the coordinated infrastructure taking measurements—pressure, heat, vibration, moisture—and deciding how to remain stable around you.

If there are human watchkeepers, they fit that same pattern: not stationed guards, but people who appear where the city has already noticed a new variable. You won’t hear them approach. You’ll see them already standing in a gap between courtyards, hands stained with sap and soot, posture relaxed in a way that says the living beams beneath their feet recognize them.

They are not hostile. They are careful. This place has survived by being careful—by letting only certain kinds of chaos in, and converting it, slowly, into another habit.

So what lives here?

A municipal plant-body that has learned law; a thin population of arborwrights and machine-tenders who maintain treaties with growth; fungi that perform sanitation and keep long chemical records; a scattering of quiet animals adapted to being exceptions; and a set of old, softly working mechanisms that have become as much a part of the district’s identity as any grove.

It is a city with residents you can’t count easily—because most of them don’t have faces, and most of them don’t need to be seen to be present.
