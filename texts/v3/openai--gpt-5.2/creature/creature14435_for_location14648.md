They live like people who have moved into a mouth that might still be chewing.

At first you think “survivors,” because everything about the trench reads as aftermath—raw stone, new cracks, the delayed drip of rain from a world above. But the longer you stay, the more that word fails. Survivors don’t usually build this densely, this deliberately. Survivors don’t cut stairways into young strata and then teach their children which footfalls are “light” and which are “loud.” What lives here is less a remnant than a kind of craft: a population that has made attentiveness into citizenship.

### The people: quiet artisans of an unstable home
They are mostly small-scale builders and maintainers because the place demands it. Not everyone swings a hammer, but everyone understands load.

You can see it in their bodies. Shoulders roped with the muscle of hauling baskets up ladders. Hands with pale stone-dust ground into the creases. Shins bruised from narrow steps. Their balance is different than yours—trained by walkways that flex, by roofs that are also streets, by the habit of looking up before looking forward. Even their stillness is practiced: when grit starts to rattle down a wall, they freeze without drama, as if pausing is simply another tool.

They talk softly not out of politeness but out of ingrained caution and acoustics. Their voices are shaped by the trench’s sound-eating: clipped phrases, close-range speech, meaning carried in gestures and eye flicks and the way a person turns their head toward a noise. When they do raise their voices, it isn’t anger—it’s warning, and it has a standard vocabulary everyone recognizes: *pebble-run*, *wet line*, *hollow*, *shift*.

Their clothing is made for abrasion and sudden damp: layered wraps, thick-soled sandals, elbow guards of hide, headscarves that can become a sling, a filter, a rope pad. Most wear a cord belt with small, specific things that tell you what kind of life they lead—bone wedges, a lump of clay for seals, a chalk shard for marking fresh cracks, a little tapping stone for listening.

### They don’t have one profession; they have roles
Down here, work is less “job” than “function in a living structure.”

- **Listeners** (not always an official title) are the ones who test walls by sound and touch. Some are simply experienced stoneworkers; others have become uncanny at it, able to tell you whether a terrace has gone *soft* behind its face. People treat them the way you’d treat a medic: with respect, impatience, and relief when they arrive.

- **Water-runners** are half plumber, half diplomat. They open and close channels, argue over drip rights, skim silt from cisterns, and decide—sometimes on short notice—whether a seep is a blessing or a threat. In a place where water appears as a delayed truth, they are the ones who turn rumor into schedule.

- **Shorers** and **tie-men** specialize in timber and rope. Their work is invisible until it isn’t. They know which posts are sacrificial, which knots hold in damp, how to distribute weight across stone that hasn’t decided what it is. Their neighborhoods smell faintly of resin, cordage fiber, and old sweat.

- **Dust-kiln folk** and **mud fitters** keep walls tight with mortar that always looks a little wet. They have the patience for slow curing in cool air and the eye for hairline separation. They are the ones who can look at a seam and say: that will open by next dusk.

- **Salvagers** go out after the trench gives its gifts—fresh fall, clean faces, gravel seams that have finally slumped loose. They work with baskets and sled-runners and the kind of caution you only get when you’ve seen a small slide become a street collapse.

- **Terrace-growers** are rare and treated accordingly. Their ledges look like miracles: shallow soil coaxed into holding tough greens and reed bundles. Their hands smell of mineral tang and crushed herbs. People barter with them the way other cities barter with jewelers.

And threaded through all of it are the people who make the city feel like more than a worksite: cooks who know how to stretch stored grain with bitter greens; storytellers who can hold a room without raising their voice; elders who can point at a wall and tell you, from memory, how it used to look before the last settling.

### How they organize: a society stacked like the city
Their map is vertical. “Above” and “below” are not metaphors; they are addresses, politics, and weather.

Upper terraces get more light and earlier warmth. Lower pockets get more seep, more cool, more damp—better for some storage, worse for some lungs. People argue about elevation the way other places argue about borders. There are whole micro-neighborhoods defined by a particular stair, a rope bridge, a cistern, the angle of an overhang that keeps a walkway dry.

Rights are practical: who may draw from which cistern, who maintains which flue, whose doorway sits under whose balcony and therefore whose roof-sweep can send dust into whose stewpot. Conflict isn’t absent, but it tends to be procedural—because impulsiveness is expensive here. Even fights, when they happen, are pulled short by the awareness of what vibration can do.

They have rules that are close to laws, and laws that are close to physics. Some are taught to children in the same tone as “don’t run with a blade”:

- Don’t cut into untested stone.
- Don’t let water wander.
- Don’t store heavy loads over a fresh face.
- Don’t sleep beneath a new crack.
- Don’t ignore a change in drip sound.

That last one matters more than outsiders understand. People here can tell time by water: a drip that goes from *glass* to *mud* means silt; from *steady* to *skipping* means a channel has partially clogged; from *cold* to *warm* means the sun has shifted and a condensation line is about to vanish.

### Their temperament: cautious, stubborn, communal by necessity
They are not cheerful in a loud way. Their humor is dry, their affection understated. They share tools the way sailors share knots—by borrowing, returning, and silently noting how a person treats what isn’t theirs.

In the evenings, when the rim takes the sun early and the city folds into shadow, the trench becomes intimate. Lamps are few; most light is contained: reflected off pale stone, pooled around a doorway, glinting in a cistern’s black mouth. This is when you hear the social life: low talk in clustered rooms, soft singing that doesn’t try to fill the air, the knock-code some neighborhoods use to avoid shouting down a stair slot.

They are also, in a way that surprises visitors, hopeful. Not naïvely—more like people who have made “tomorrow” into a maintenance plan. They keep building because the alternative isn’t leaving; it’s being buried.

### What else lives there: the small, pale, and adapted
Big animals don’t thrive in a city made of ledges and slots, but a few kinds of life have learned the trench’s rules.

- **Goats** with careful hooves and sharp patience, used like living cranes—carrying light loads, climbing where ladders would flex too much. They wear soft wraps on their bells (if they have bells at all) because unnecessary sound is frowned upon.

- **Dogs** that move like locals: pausing when stone sighs, picking routes by smell and footfeel. They’re lean, dust-colored, and trained to find people after small collapses, to alert without barking much.

- **Pigeons** and **pale wall-birds** that nest in high holes and feed on scraps, insects, and whatever the rim winds happen to bring down. Their droppings are collected where it can be done safely—nothing is wasted if it can be turned into soil.

- **Bats** that skim the sky-strip at dusk, reliable as a clock. Some residents read their patterns the way farmers read clouds: a thick early flight means insects have hatched in a damp pocket; a scattered flight can mean wind above.

- **Insects** that belong to the wet seams—thin-legged, quick-vanishing. They aren’t loved, but they’re watched, because swarms can indicate a new seep or a rot pocket in stored goods.

- In the lowest, permanently damp basins, **tiny amphibians** appear like a secret that becomes visible only when conditions are right. Children treat them as luck; adults treat them as data.

Vegetation, sparse as it is, also feels like a resident: lichen that writes moisture trails on stone, moss harvested like cloth, reeds tended like a resource more precious than meat.

### What they worship (if “worship” is the word)
Not all of them share a religion, and not all of them would call what they do religious, but there are reverences you can’t miss.

They respect the **Rim** the way coastal people respect the sea: it is the source of weather, danger, and trade, and it is always present even when not seen. They respect **Gravity** the way other cultures respect law: impersonal, inexhaustible, never negotiable. They respect **Attention** as a virtue with teeth—because in this city, noticing is the difference between a nuisance and a funeral.

There are small rituals that are more like habits than ceremonies: touching a wall before sleeping beneath it; leaving a pebble on a newly marked crack to see if it vibrates loose; pouring the first cup of drawn water back onto a designated “thanks stone” near a cistern; keeping a household’s heaviest jar on the lowest shelf no matter how inconvenient.

### And the city changes them, as they change it
If you stay long enough, you begin to see that what lives here is not just a population inside a trench. It is a relationship.

The trench is unfinished and keeps becoming; so do they. They build in ways that assume revision. They plan for slumps the way other places plan for seasons. Their architecture is not a claim of permanence but a conversation with an active landform: *hold here, release there, redirect this, brace that, listen always*.

So who lives here?

People whose main resource is not metal or timber or even water—though they need all of those—but *skill at reading small changes*. A city-full of readers: of stone tone, of drip rhythm, of dust settling, of the faint pressure of wind making its way down. And around them, the modest companion-life that can survive on ledges and damp rumors: goats, bats, birds, moss, lichen, insects—everything that can make a living without demanding the world be stable.

They live like that: stacked, quiet, stubborn, and awake.
