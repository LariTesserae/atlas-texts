What lives there is not a population so much as a single, ongoing decision to be alive.

At the scale you first notice—leaf, root, hanging braid—the cavern looks like it has been colonized by plants the way any damp hollow gets colonized: opportunists reaching for water and purchase. But the longer you stay, the more that explanation peels away. These aren’t separate organisms competing for footholds. The “vegetation” is a continuous body that has learned to masquerade as an ecosystem.

It has parts, and those parts have different tempers, but they don’t truly behave like individuals. They behave like organs.

### The Lattice (the main inhabitant)
The dominant life here is the hanging architecture itself: the braided columns, the tensioned membranes, the wall-mats and floor-islands. If you try to name it as a plant, it slips your category. It grows like roots, drinks like moss, holds itself like muscle, and *listens* like skin.

- **The columns** are its bones and tendons: thick ropes of pale tissue that split, rejoin, and bear load. They are not merely anchored into stone; they are pegged into the cavern’s misbehavior—fastened to those repeating points where distance stutters and a “here” overlaps another “here.” In places, a single column can be felt in two positions at once: pluck it gently and the vibration answers from a spot your eyes insist is separate.

- **The membranes** are its lungs and instruments. They span the void in slow, patient arcs, tightening when the air dries, slackening when moisture rises, and *ringing* when the cavern exhales through them. Their surfaces are not leaf in the usual way—more like living film, veined with channels that can close and open to direct condensation. In a dry spell they become miserly, sealing pores; after a loud sound they grow suddenly generous, beading and releasing droplets as if noise has paid for water.

- **The wall-carpets** are its stomach and workshop. Where limestone is fresh and sharp, the mats creep over it with a purpose that looks almost hungry. They don’t just cling—they *process*. Mineral dust becomes something green can use. Clay smears become something that holds moisture. The youngest fractures get lined within hours not because spores happened to land there, but because the Lattice shifts attention toward new surface the way a tongue shifts toward a cut.

If you could see it in cross-section, the entire hanging garden would resolve into a single continuous vascular logic: slow pressure pulses, directional flow, deliberate hoarding and release. The cavern’s “weather” is not weather. It’s physiology.

### How it feeds (and what it’s really doing with light)
The borrowed brightness isn’t a gift falling into the cavern; it’s a behavior the Lattice performs.

Somewhere in its tissues—especially near those bruised-thin ceiling patches—it carries structures that behave like optical grafts: not luminescent, not reflective, but *parasitic on distance*. Light isn’t traveling down a hole; it’s being *pulled across a shortcut* the way water is pulled up a stem.

It’s telling that the light is ordinary. Ordinary light is stable light. Stable light means the knot in the world is being held in a familiar orientation—pinned, trained, prevented from slipping into stranger geometries. The Lattice doesn’t just use the pocket’s instability. It uses living mass to *stabilize* it.

From that, a suspicion forms: the cavern is not home to the plants by chance. The plants may be what makes “here” remain here.

### Its senses (sound as touch, attention as weather)
There are no eyes in it, but it has an exquisitely distributed sense of disturbance.

Sound changes the place because sound is one of the ways the Lattice measures you. The membranes are tympana. The braided columns carry vibration like nerves. A clap does not merely echo; it becomes a map the organism can read—distances, volumes, moving bodies.

That’s why the cavern responds:

- Loudness producing condensation is not magic; it’s a reflex: pores opening, surfaces wetting, droplets shaken loose to change humidity and damping—an attempt to quiet the air the way a body quiets an irritation.
- Sudden silence making leaves relax is not “calm”; it’s the organism returning to baseline tension when the disturbance has passed.
- Those subsonic groans you feel are sometimes the rock settling, yes—but sometimes they are the Lattice shifting its own load, redistributing weight to keep a seam from widening too far.

And then there is the other lever the place seems to have: **attention**. If you stare at the bruised ceiling until your eyes try to make “sky” out of stone, the Lattice subtly reorients fibers there, as if your focused looking is a pressure it can use. It is not that it wants to be seen; it wants to know what kind of seeing you are. Different minds make different weather.

### Reproduction (not seeds, but shedding and re-tying)
The Lattice doesn’t rely on animal pollinators, and it doesn’t scatter seeds in any straightforward sense. It propagates in three ways:

1. **Membrane snaps**: when a sheet tightens to a threshold, it can pop free at a seam and recoil, flinging a dust of spores and micro-cuttings into the air currents it itself produces. Some of those specks land in rock cracks; some land in the wrong “layer” of space and vanish from your view without ever truly leaving.

2. **Root-braid budding**: thick columns sometimes grow nodules—pale knots that look like tumors until they split and unfurl into new cords. These budded cords do not seek soil; they seek *overlap points* in the cavern’s geometry, places where anchoring does more than anchor.

3. **Wall-mat fission**: in damp cycles, the living carpet can simply… divide. A patch you watched all day will, after a pressure shift, appear as two patches with a narrow strip of bare stone between them—stone that looks older than it should, as if the split didn’t just happen but has always been there. The organism uses the pocket’s reality-editing the way a vine uses a trellis.

### The lesser residents (animals that live in its margins)
Animals are not absent; they are simply not central. They exist the way barnacles exist on a whale: real, persistent, but not steering.

- **Translucent beetles and mites**: small, patient scavengers that graze on biofilm and shed tissue. They live in the protected seams where wall-mats meet bare rock, emerging when condensation makes surfaces slick enough to travel. Their bodies are thin enough that the borrowed light passes through them, giving them the eerie look of moving glass.

- **Clay-colored amphibians**: not common, but stable. They keep to puddle basins and the succulent islands around them. Their stillness reads like wariness, but it’s also an adaptation to a place where vibration means “noticed.” When they do move, they move during the cavern’s own louder breathing, hiding footfalls under the general roar.

- **Root-dwellers**: something small and quick inhabits the braided columns—more felt than seen. Sometimes you hear the scrabble inside a cord and watch a subtle tightening run along it like a swallow passing through a snake. Whether these are insect-like burrowers or the Lattice’s own mobile nodules is hard to say; the distinction blurs, because the organism tolerates and even seems to *use* them to keep channels clear.

Predation exists but is understated: glossy leaves that hold fast, warm-seeking root tips that twitch, membranes that fold when something lingers too long beneath them. The place doesn’t need dramatic killers; it only needs to make sure nothing eats the wrong parts faster than they regrow.

### The decomposers (the quiet engine you don’t see)
Under the green is a second kingdom: fungal threads and bacterial slicks that make the mineral edible.

You catch them in the smells—the sour-green undertone, the sudden sweetness after a wet episode, the metallic tang near fresh fractures. In some crevices, you can see faint, powdery blooms where the wall-carpets thin. Those are not failed plants; they’re the partners that turn limestone and clay into feedstock. Without them the Lattice would be beautiful but hungry. With them it becomes patient.

### The visitors with hands (not residents, but acknowledged)
Someone does come. Not often, and not long.

The evidence is in the *quality* of the scars: a clean cut that implies a sharp stone edge; a harvested patch taken with restraint; a path that appears and then softens as the groundcover rebounds.

If you imagine them, imagine people who have learned that you don’t “take” from this place—you negotiate with it the way you negotiate with weather:

- They speak only when they want water to come.
- They keep their steps inside existing crush-lines so the mats don’t have to decide whether to erase or remember.
- They cut certain vines not for food but for fiber, certain leaf-sheets not for shelter but for their strange property of holding tension and sound.
- They leave quickly, because lingering makes the Lattice curious, and curiosity here is not safe. Curiosity changes humidity. Curiosity shifts distances.

The Lattice does not chase them. It does something more unsettling: it *accommodates* them in small ways, like an old tree accommodating a habitual climber by thickening bark along the route.

### What the cavern is, when you add it all up
A cave like this should be a dead end without an entrance: a sealed pocket with limited energy. But this one is bright, wet in pulses, and continually expanding into fresh rock. That only makes sense if its main inhabitant is not merely living *in* the pocket, but living *as a solution to the pocket’s problem*.

The knot in the world wants to slip. The young rock wants to crack further. The borrowed light wants to come from somewhere that isn’t here. The Lattice ties all of that together and calls it metabolism.

So what lives there is:

- a plant-body that uses sound as touch and geometry as scaffolding,
- a thin fringe of animals that have learned to be quiet in a place that listens,
- and the lingering possibility that the “ordinary” light is not ordinary at all, but the steady gaze of a distant daytime the Lattice has learned to keep on a leash.

If you return after time away, you may find different leaf shapes, different tensions in the membranes, a new anchor column grown precisely where the rock has decided to split. The life here does not preserve a habitat; it preserves a *stability*—and it will change everything else to do it.
