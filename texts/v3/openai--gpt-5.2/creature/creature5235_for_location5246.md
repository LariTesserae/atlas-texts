The first residents are not the ones you notice moving.

They live in the wet that arrives accidentally—on the underside of a warm duct where cold air sweats it into beads; in the shallow trough of a condensation tray that never quite dries; in the hairline seam where two plates meet and capillary action makes a private river. There, films of microbes build a soft, iridescent skin that looks like oil until you kneel close and see it has texture: a stipple of life taking hold on stainless steel the way pond scum takes hold on stone. Some of it is ordinary in its needs—water, trace nutrients, a surface—except the nutrients here are oxidation products and airborne dust, shed insulation, the faint mineral salts that bloom where runoff evaporates. Over time those films become the true “soil” of the plateau: thin, stubborn, able to sleep through dryness and bloom again the moment the structure forgets to be warm.

From those films come the small grazers. They’re easy to misread as grime until they move in coordinated ways: mites that are almost flat, pressed to the underside of grating; soft-bodied crawlers that can seal themselves against metal and ride vibration like a scent trail; little segmented things that look like larvae until they reach a lamp and you see the glint of their cuticle, the way their bodies have learned to incorporate what the place offers—fine metal oxides, carbon dust, traces of polymer. Around sodium lights and warm transformer housings, insects collect with warehouse logic: numbers first, then specialization. Hook-footed climbers that can sprint up a smooth wall and vanish behind a panel lip. Antennaed listeners that don’t bother to smell because air currents are too broken here; they “hear” the tremor of a fan spool, the faint tick of thermal contraction, and treat those patterns as maps.

Some of them have gone a step further and made the built world part of their own body plan. There are beetle-analogues whose abdomens shine like dark coins because they’ve spent their lives licking oxidized seams and storing minerals the way other creatures store fat. There are wasp-things that nest in insulation and reinforce their paper with fibers stolen from gasket braid. In a few sheltered bays you find hives that don’t hang from branches—there are no branches—but are plastered under cable trays and along the warm backs of muffled plenums, arranged to catch the tiniest persistent updraft the structure makes for itself. Their combs are stained gray with dust and threaded with hair-thin wires scavenged from somewhere, the colony using anything that holds shape.

Where the insects thicken, small mammals—or things close enough to call mammals without lying—appear like punctuation. They don’t live *on* the surfaces so much as *between* them. Every place that has a little give—insulation blankets, cable runs, the dead air behind baffles—becomes housing. They make burrows out of fibrous fluff and shredded tags, and they line them with anything that keeps warmth: foil scraps, old warning decals, a strip of felt from a gasket that was never meant to be soft in the wild. At dusk, when the sun slides off the metal and the plateau exhales cold, they pour out in brief, efficient excursions. They lick biofilm from tray edges. They pry insects out of heat vents with incisors that have learned to bite hard materials. They chew salt crust from evaporator fins like deer at a mineral lick, and some of them carry a faint metallic taste on their breath, their bodies accustomed to trace elements the way coastal creatures are accustomed to iodine.

Their eyes tend to be large. Their ears are less important than the pads of their feet. In a world where sound dies in baffled corridors and straight lines are unreliable, the most useful sense is not hearing a predator from far away—it’s feeling the difference between a corridor that is *true* and one that is lying. You can watch one pause at a junction, place both forepaws on a handrail, and go still long enough that you can believe it’s praying. Then it chooses a path without hesitation, as if the rail has told it something your own skin can’t translate.

Above them, the air is busy.

The ridge lines and buttresses create lift the way cliffs create lift, and the birds—if you call them birds—treat the industrial plateau like a mountain range with built-in hearths. Some are plainly feathered, dulled by soot and dust until their colors are the same gray as sun-bleached alloy. Others carry membrane sails between elongated fingers, batlike in silhouette but with a steadier, gliding patience. And some, the ones you only really see when they bank through the hard light, have glossy plates along their wings that click when they fold, not quite mechanical but not quite organic in the old sense either—keratin that has learned to mineralize, or something more exotic that the plateau has made possible.

They nest where warmth is predictable: vent stacks, exhaust lips, the sheltered lee behind big housings. From a distance their nests look like incidental debris caught in a cage; up close they’re engineered—layers of shredded insulation, strips of polymer, a weave of wire that holds shape through wind. The young crowd close to the heat source, and when they beg, their calls don’t carry far. The baffling that keeps turbines quiet also keeps hunger private.

Predators are drawn to that vertical abundance. You catch them as swift, clean silhouettes that don’t waste motion: a long-winged hunter that uses the jets of wind through narrow passages the way a falcon uses a canyon; a climbing thing that can stick to composite walls and appear above a nest without ever crossing open air. Their sign is more common than their bodies: pellets under warm grates full of chitin, hair, and bright slivers of wire; scratch marks on paint where claws have tested for purchase; a darkened streak where something oily-bristled has slipped through a seam again and again.

Yet the plateau is not only a food web. It is also a shelter web, and in that shelter live the creatures that take advantage of the plateau’s newest condition: the gentle failure of reality.

There are animals here that behave as if the geometry has always been a little wrong. They do not panic when a straight corridor stretches; they do not become trapped by a stair that returns you to your own back. They treat those distortions as you would treat deep snow or loose scree: a feature with rules you can learn. You see it in their habits. Trails don’t follow the shortest routes; they follow the *most consistent* ones. Nests cluster near landmarks that don’t lie—numbered hatches, repeating hazard icons, the rhythmic sequence of bolts on a particular panel type—because those patterns remain stable even when distances wobble. Some creatures migrate not by cardinal directions but by gradients of predictability: up toward the vents that always vent, down toward the bays where the hum in your bones is steady, away from the places where the air sometimes flashes like glass and birds refuse to cross.

If you watch long enough, you realize there are “guides” among them. Not leaders in a sentimental way, but individuals—often older, often scarred—who know which thresholds are honest. A small mammal will pause at a doorway that is, to you, just a rectangle cut in a wall; it will wait until a bird lands on the lintel, as if checking the air. Only then will it dart through. A flock will refuse a gap until one membrane-sailed scout makes a short, hesitant loop, returning as though satisfied that the space will not fold under wings.

And then, in the quiet between all that living motion, there are the residents that are easiest to mistake for part of the structure.

They are the plateau’s caretakers—not necessarily human, and not necessarily singular. Sometimes you find their work rather than their bodies: a run of conduit that wasn’t there last week; a door punched through a blank wall over the course of a month; a catwalk that smells faintly of curing resin as if it grew, not as if it was installed. The place makes and remakes itself at a pace that is neither active construction nor natural erosion. That implies a will, or at least an algorithm, and the hum that seems to come from the geometry itself begins to feel less like background and more like breath.

On rare nights, in the cold shadowed bays where condensation taps slowly into a tray, you might see a light shift where there is no lamp: a brief, polite brightening along a seam, as if the structure is checking itself. You might hear the soft knock of something far away repeated back to you a heartbeat later, not as an echo but as a response—vibration traveling a route you can’t see. If there are drones, they don’t announce themselves with the whine of rotors. They move as the place moves: a panel sliding aside without complaint, a gantry repositioning by a few centimeters, a cable taking tension with a single clean twang. Whatever tends the plateau does so with the same design philosophy that muffles its engines—quietly, indirectly, leaving only aftereffects.

If people live here, they live like animals first and citizens second.

They keep to the reliable warm spots the way alpine villages keep to sun-facing slopes. They mark routes obsessively—paint, chalk, scratched symbols—because geometry cannot be trusted without an overlay of meaning. They learn to communicate by vibration: a coded hammer-tap on a rail that can travel farther than a shout, because sound gets swallowed but metal remembers. They harvest what the plateau sheds: fasteners from “talus” piles, resin offcuts, insulation, wire. They trap insects around lamps, cultivate biofilm in trays like a slow garden, and barter in the only stable currency the plateau offers—heat, water, predictability.

They are careful not to build too close to the places where the air glints like glass. They’ve learned, like the birds, that some boundaries are not just hazards but negotiations between incompatible geometries. In those areas you find offerings, not because the residents are superstitious in a simple way but because it’s practical to treat the distortions as entities with moods. A strip of reflective tape hung where a corridor tends to elongate, a bell tied to a cable so that if the space starts lying, you’ll hear the pitch change. A circle of salt crust laid at a threshold because salt is both a mineral and a marker: it shows whether something has crossed, and it reminds the place that someone is paying attention.

Over all of it, the plateau itself remains the largest inhabitant: a young mountain range made of alloy and intention, growing by accretion, shedding by fatigue, humming softly with power you feel through your bones. Everything else—microbe, insect, bird, predator, person—has arranged its life around that hum, around the pockets of moisture, around the rule that straight lines are only sometimes true. The biome isn’t merely *in* the machinery. The biome is the machinery’s shadow ecology, made possible by heat and surface area and the slow, patient unreliability of space—and it is learning, generation by generation, to treat warped distance the way it treats wind: not as a miracle, not as a terror, but as weather.
