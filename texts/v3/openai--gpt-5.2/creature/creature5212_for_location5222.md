What lives here is not one community so much as a set of tactics for enduring a heavy, shifting dark.

The first residents are the ones you don’t quite see as “animals” at all—because they don’t announce themselves with shape. They live as sheen and residue.

On the basalt backs and along the sheltered sides of gravel bars, microbial films do the real settling. They take the constant drizzle of organics—flakes of dead plankton, shredded kelp threads, the faint leaching of minerals from old rock—and stitch it into something local. Up close they’re not a uniform slime but a patchwork: slicker where the current scours clean, fuzzier where sediment can stay long enough to be colonized, speckled with tiny gas blisters that form and then vanish when pressure and flow change their mind. Some patches feed on the slow chemistry of stone and seep; others on whatever falls. Their colors are mostly the colors of absence: milk-glass, bruised grey, the faint tan of silt embedded in gel.

Where there’s even a modest increase in light—shallower ridges, or those rare hours when the surface mood is generous—algae and short, tough fronds appear like stubborn hair on a scalp. They don’t “grow upward” so much as they spread sideways and keep their center of gravity low. Their holdfasts are the real organism: pale knots gripping rock, tolerating abrasion the way trees tolerate wind. Anything tall is temporary. It grows, it becomes a sail, it is eventually ripped and carried away, and somewhere downcurrent a fragment finds a new stone and tries again.

In the sediment plains, life hides by becoming the same texture as the ground.

There are worms that make their homes out of the bottom itself—tubes that look like nothing but a slightly more deliberate clumping of mud. When the water is calm they extend delicate feeding fans, combing the passing current for edible grit. When the turbidity pulse comes, they retract in a single practiced motion and the plain becomes blank again.

There are small crustaceans—amphipods and isopods—built like moving punctuation marks. They don’t swim so much as hop and cling: a quick dash across exposed basalt, then flat against it, then still. Their colors are the local palette: grey-brown, silt-dusted, sometimes translucent enough that you see their guts darken after a meal. They eat what everything here eats: what falls, what rots slowly, what can be scraped.

Scattered through the basins are animals that make the bottom negotiable by accepting it as their element. Burrowers don’t fight the puffing silt; they exploit it. A soft-bodied creature can disappear without digging in the way a shovel digs—just a steady, patient wedging and a subtle shimmy until it is covered. In calm moments you might notice the betrayals: a pair of holes like pinpricks where something ventilates its burrow, a faint ridge where a body moved beneath the surface, a brief exhale of darker mud from a hidden siphon.

Filter-feeders exist, but they are careful about where they place themselves, because the difference between “food-rich current” and “grinding storm” is timing and a few hand’s-breadths of depth. You find them in lee zones: behind a rounded ridge, under an overhang where the water still moves but the heavier grains don’t hammer as hard. Sponges that look like stone until you touch them. Anemones that keep their tentacles short and thick, more like fists than feathers, closing when silt turns the water into sandpaper. Small bivalves embedded in firmer patches, their shells already scuffed to the same dull finish as the gravel.

Then, moving through all this, there are the drifters—organisms that treat the water column not as empty space but as a slow conveyor.

Comb jellies are among the most visible simply because they catch what little light exists. In a world that has forgotten color, their ciliary rows still make faint, disciplined flashes when you’re at the right angle: not rainbows, more like the suggestion of glass. They pulse like a thought forming and unforming. They are mostly water in water, and because of that they can afford to be nearly invisible until the current turns them.

Jellyfish appear as problems rather than presences: a faint bell, a sudden sting to anything careless enough to brush the trailing filaments, then the bell is gone again into the particulate dark. They don’t “hunt” the way a fish hunts. They wait in the moving layers and let the layers do the work.

Somewhere between bottom and midwater are the fish that have learned not to waste.

They are not plentiful, but they feel *decisive*. A lanternfish-like body, narrow enough to offer little drag, with photophores that don’t blaze so much as mutter—small points of regulated light used at close range, for recognition, for confusing a pursuer, for luring something just dumb enough to approach. In this turbidity, vision is a short-range sense anyway; their real reading is done through pressure and vibration. The lateral line is their map: they can feel a gravel bed shifting before a stone audibly ticks, can sense an approaching body by the way it edits the currents.

These fish do not patrol; they station-keep. They take advantage of the stacked flows the way a bird takes advantage of thermals—rising a little into one layer, sliding into another, letting opposing currents cancel out movement so they can hover with almost no effort. When they move, it is because they have chosen to end one situation and begin another. One flick, one acceleration, then stillness again.

Along the bottom you sometimes find the heavier-bodied ones: codlike, sculpinlike, built to rest on silt without sinking, their fins spread like snowshoes. They taste the world. Barbels, sensitive lips, mouths that can vacuum a mouthful of mud and sort it for life by touch. Their camouflage is not pattern so much as *accumulation*: a skin that holds silt, mottling that looks exactly like “what the water leaves behind.”

Predators exist, but even predation here is restrained by the cost of commotion.

A larger hunter—something sharklike or eel-long—moves with the patience of deep time. It does not chase through sediment clouds because a chase makes blindness for both hunter and prey. It uses senses that don’t care about turbidity: electroreception, pressure changes, the low-frequency thrum of struggling muscle carried through water. It follows the edges of disturbances: the seam where a turbidity flow meets clearer water, the downcurrent trail of leaked amino acids from an injured body. When it strikes, it is not a flurry. It is one controlled contact, then a retreat to let the prey weaken.

Scavengers are more common than hunters, because death arrives here predictably—from cold, from hunger, from storms above that translate into crushing rearrangements below. When a carcass reaches the bottom, it becomes a temporary landmark. Small crustaceans gather first, then larger ones; worms arrive as if summoned. The microbial film blooms into a richer, darker slick. In a place where nothing persists long in human terms, a carcass is one of the few events that can be “visited” over days: a rotating cast of eaters taking turns as the currents allow.

And then there are the residents that make the hints of “use.”

Not architecture—nothing here rewards permanence—but brief intelligence expressed as leverage.

Cephalopods fit this world well: soft enough not to crack under pressure changes, clever enough to make choices in a landscape that erases tracks. An octopus can live in a pocket under basalt, plugging the entrance with a stone not because it is building, but because it is editing the flow—keeping silt out, keeping scent in, keeping teeth out. If you find a rock placed just so, or a bone wedge jammed where it shouldn’t naturally lodge, it may be the work of an animal that understands “this object changes the problem.”

They do not leave towers or walls; they leave *moments of arrangement*. A pile of shell fragments dragged into a slight hollow because the hollow is safer to eat in. A stone struck against another not to shape it into a tool for tomorrow, but to crack something open *now*. The sea later takes the evidence and distributes it back into randomness, but for a while the act exists as a deviation from the usual drift.

There may also be visitors from above—surface mammals that dive deep enough to become ghosts down here. A seal or a deep-diving whale doesn’t belong to the bottom, but it intersects with it. A seal might drive prey into a basin and pin it against the silt, leaving a brief scar where the bottom puffed and resettled. A whale’s passing is felt more than seen: a distant pressure wave, a subtle re-sorting of suspended particles, a slow rain of disturbed detritus after it goes.

So the place is “sparsely populated” only if you count bodies the way you count birds in daylight.

In truth it is crowded with persistence: films that re-form after every scouring, burrowers that rebuild after every collapse, drifters that repopulate the water column as soon as the current changes its mind, fish that make a living out of hovering in opposing flows, and occasional minds—animal, not civic—that understand the only durable act here is a small one performed at exactly the right time.

Life here doesn’t decorate the landscape. It *adheres* to it, infiltrates it, waits inside it—until the water shifts, and the whole community shifts with it, quiet as sediment settling, present as weight.
