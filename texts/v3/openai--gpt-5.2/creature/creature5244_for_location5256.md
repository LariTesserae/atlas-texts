They call it a city, because there are addresses and councils and arguments you can lose in public. But what lives there is closer to an assemblage—an intertidal colony that learned to bill you for water rights and noise.

Life in that wet band comes in layers, and each layer thinks it’s the real one.

## The obvious inhabitants: people who grew up with water in their bones
The humans here don’t read as “amphibious,” exactly. They read as *trained by permanency of damp*.

Their clothes are built around it: capillary hems that wick rain away from joints, collars with salt-shedding textures, sleeves that don’t cling when you brush a sweating wall. Most people wear footwear that can tolerate alternating grip—dry composite, oiled metal, algae-slick stone, shallow film-water—without that tiny hesitation that gets you killed in a dense crowd. You can often tell who’s new because they stop walking when the surface changes; locals don’t. Their gait already contains the city’s slipperiness.

Skin care is civic infrastructure. The air salts you. The water salts you. Metal dust salts you. People carry little neutralizer strips like old-world lip balm, swipe it along knuckles and under eyes, wipe the residue on their trousers without thinking. Nails are kept short because every hand is a maintenance hand at some point—prying a panel, reseating a gasket, slapping a leak-seal patch over a sweating seam until a service swarm arrives.

Language, too, adapts. Directional speech includes *height* and *shelter* by default: not “meet at the corner,” but “meet at the corner under the intake,” “two levels above the thin-water lane,” “west side where the wind doesn’t pipe.” Time is weather. Plans are tides. Apologies are phrased like forecasts: “I thought the surge would hold.”

There are communities shaped not by ethnicity first but by relationship to motion:

- **Deckwalkers** (the mid-level pedestrians and couriers) know the stable routes and the shifting ones. They memorize the moods of certain corridors: when the acoustic panels will open, when the rain-sheets will dump, which bridge always shivers at dusk.
- **Sumpfolk** live low, in the districts where door thresholds are dams and the ground is an agreement. They are often pump-literate, filter-literate, alarm-literate—able to diagnose a neighborhood’s health by the sound of a throat-clearing gurgle.
- **Heatwardens** work around exchangers and seawall chambers, where warm brine and machinery heat make their own fog. They smell faintly of minerals baked into fabric, and their hair is often cropped close because humidity makes everything else a liability.
- **Bonders and patchers** are artisans and emergency responders at once: the people who know which old stone arch can take a new composite band, which ceramic lattice will recrystallize if you wet it and warm it, which crack is cosmetic and which crack is a story about subsidence.

And threaded through all of them is the social practice that outsiders mistake for impatience: the constant micro-negotiation of right-of-way. In a place with no empty space, courtesy is a fluid dynamic. You learn to *flow* without stopping, to trade priority with a look, to let a stranger pass because you recognize the storm front in their shoulders.

## The domestic layer: companions that can survive the city
Traditional pets exist, but only the ones that can tolerate brine, noise, and perpetual damp—or the ones rebuilt for it.

Small household animals tend to be **slick-coated, salt-tolerant**, with ears that seal easily and paws that handle grates. Some are engineered “cousins” of old mammals: sharp-eyed, clever, not quite feral, with the unapologetic confidence of something that has never known a lawn.

People keep:

- **Cable-runners**: long-bodied, quick little mammals (or mammal-analogs) trained to retrieve dropped items from maintenance gaps, deliver messages through service chases, or simply hunt the endless insects. They wear tiny ID plates because otherwise the city will claim them as part of the general scavenger ecology.
- **Vent-roost birds**: compact, tough birds that nest in warm louvered cavities and return at night like commuters. Their droppings are a controlled nuisance—collected, processed, turned into feedstock—because nothing is allowed to be merely waste.
- **Low-light companion bats** (in districts that tolerate them): kept less as “pets” than as respected housemates, insect controllers, and omens of seasonal swarms.

Ownership is porous. An animal might sleep under your heating grate for a week, then decide a market canopy has better scraps and live there until the rains change. People accept this. In a reef, territory is a negotiation, not a deed.

## The uninvited, indispensable citizens: scavengers and drain-law
Where you have constant water and constant waste-streams, you get an entire civilization of things that live *in the interfaces*.

Storm drains here aren’t empty pipes; they’re *habitats* that the city manages the way a farmer manages soil.

- **Crustacean scavengers** click in the dark, armored against grit, able to crawl through filter baskets and return loaded with whatever fell through. Some are natural, some are “helped”—bred for appetite, for tolerance to chemical swings, for a life cycle that matches maintenance schedules. Their breeding seasons are marked on municipal calendars because they change clog behavior.
- **Sump eels / canal hunters**—sleek predators that treat warm canals like rivers. They’re bold enough to strike at the edge of a runoff lane, then vanish under a platform where their bodies can’t be followed. Children learn not to trail their fingers in certain waters the way children elsewhere learn not to touch a hot stove.
- **Grate-mice and panel-gnawers**—small mammals that chew not for hunger but for nest-building. They are hated, trapped, respected, studied. There are districts where people leave them sacrificial fibers so they stop chewing cables. There are districts that run silent wars against them with ultrasonic fields and bait that sterilizes rather than kills.

And then there are the insects—because heat and moisture make them inevitable, and light makes them congregate. Here, insect life is less a “problem” than a regulated weather.

Some neighborhoods schedule **light-dimming cycles** not for energy savings but to reshape insect movement, to keep swarms from colliding with transit wakes. Restaurants hang heat curtains that create invisible borders insects hate to cross. You can tell an upscale market by the way its air *feels*—slightly different pressure, slightly different thermal gradient, engineered to keep wings away from open food.

## The city’s grazers: biofilm browsers and wall-reef life
Without plants, the city’s “green” is pale—frostlike biofilms and fungal mats on shaded stone, the soft bloom on a canal wall that looks like mineral until you watch it *thicken* over a week.

Those films are alive, and they’re not merely tolerated. They’re farmed, harvested, and sometimes guarded.

- **Biofilm browsers**—small, damp-loving creatures with rasping mouthparts (some snail-like, some more arthropod) that graze along canal faces and undersides of decks. In some districts they’re encouraged because they keep surfaces from becoming dangerously slick; in others they’re controlled because they can interfere with sensor arrays.
- **Moldwright fungi**—not the fluffy household horror, but engineered mats that digest specific waste compounds and exude binders useful for sealants. People who work with them wear gloves that look like medical gear until you realize they’re also inoculation tools: touch a crack, seed the right organism, come back in a day to a healed seam.

To outsiders, it looks like neglect. To residents, it’s husbandry.

## The aerial population: things that ride wakes and pressure
Above the decks, the sky belongs to animals that learned to fly in a built canyon where the air itself is shaped.

There are **wake-riders**—birds that can time a mag-line’s passage and surf the pressure change like ocean swell. You see them lift without flapping when a transit convoy rounds a corner, then peel away at the last second into a side-street draft.

There are **roof-bats** and **duct-bats**: different subcultures of the same general form, one roosting under solar decking where it’s warm and stable, the other deep in vent architecture where the air tastes like metal. Their guano feeds microbial farms; their presence is tracked as part of pest control.

And there are always **flying scavengers**—the opportunists that have no loyalty except to calories. They are brutally intelligent, able to recognize the sound of a market stall opening or the pattern of a refuse drone’s route. They will take food from hands that hesitate.

Predation is part of the skyline. A sudden silence in a flock means a hunter moved through. People barely look up unless the impact comes close enough to spray them.

## The machine-life: drones, swarms, and infrastructural fauna
The city is so dense, and so continuously repairing itself, that machines behave like an animal class.

They are everywhere, but not all are “owned” in any simple way.

- **Service swarms** cling to walls like barnacles until called, then peel off in a glittering sheet to sinter a panel, print a brace, chew away rot. They have the focused, unemotional aggression of ants.
- **Filter-skimmers** patrol thin-water lanes, tiny machines that look like flat stones until they glide, sip dust-laced water, and spit it into slots that lead to treatment.
- **Pipe-crawlers** live inside the city’s intestines, scraping, inspecting, tagging corrosion with chemical paint that later swarms can read.
- **Security kites**—soft-bodied aerial drones that hover in busy districts, more like jellyfish than helicopters, listening for gunshots (rare, because sound is too public) and for structural failure (common enough to matter). They move slowly because the air is crowded, and because speed is violence in a place with so many bodies.

Some machines are municipal, some corporate, some personal. Some are orphans: forgotten assets that found a niche and never got decommissioned. People sometimes feed them power the way you feed a stray animal—because a familiar drone that recognizes you is safer than a hungry unknown that mistakes you for an obstacle.

The boundary between “robot” and “wildlife” is not philosophical here; it’s practical. If it has a predictable route, the city designs around it. If it starts behaving unpredictably, someone either adopts it or deletes it.

## The hidden majority: microbes, chemosynth, and the slow politics of rot
What truly lives here—the biomass you never quite see—is microbial.

The city runs on imported feedstocks and chemical gradients, which means the base of its ecology isn’t sunlight; it’s **waste heat, dissolved compounds, and engineered metabolism**.

- In warm canals, **chemo-fed microbial mats** bloom in thin layers, converting certain pollutants into harvestable biomass. They’re managed like crops: scraped at intervals, monitored for mutation drift, occasionally “reset” with a sterilization pulse if they start producing the wrong byproducts.
- In old stone districts, **lithic microbes** live in the capillaries of sedimentary slabs, not just tolerating the weeping groundwater but using it—forming mineral crusts that can either protect surfaces or crack them, depending on which strain dominates.
- On metal, **corrosion ecologies** form: communities that accelerate rust in controlled areas to stabilize other areas, creating sacrificial skins. The city doesn’t merely fight corrosion; it *steers* it.

People who work in public health here think like ecologists, not like exterminators. Outbreaks are often not single pathogens but **community shifts**: a canal biofilm turns too protein-rich and suddenly supports a new insect boom; a ventilation mat changes composition and starts irritating lungs; a drain-crust becomes too brittle and flakes into a filter cascade.

The most powerful “animals” in the city may be these invisible consortia, because they decide whether the wetness is merely inconvenient or actively lethal.

## The institutional organism: the city that behaves like a creature
There is also something that lives here that isn’t any single animal, machine, or person: the *coordinating intelligence* embedded in flow systems.

Not a god-mind, not a single AI with a name you can curse, but a distributed set of municipal algorithms, corporate optimizers, and legacy control systems that collectively do what nervous systems do:

- sense pressure changes in seawall plates,
- reroute people the way blood reroutes around a clot,
- decide which districts get to stay dry during a surge and which must yield,
- throttle sound baffles here and open them there,
- move modular buildings a few meters a day like muscles shifting posture.

Residents relate to this “city-nervousness” the way sailors relate to the sea: as something you can learn, bargain with, sometimes outsmart, never fully command.

There are people—call them **interpreters**—whose entire job is reading the city’s moods: watching the subtle shifts in pedestrian-light lanes, listening to pump rhythms, predicting where the next forced flow will happen. They sell this as safety, as profit, as art. A good interpreter can lead you through three kilometers of density and keep your ears from ringing once.

## What it feels like, up close: a reef that eats, heals, and remembers
If you stand still long enough—rare, because standing still is itself an act—you notice that everything living here is adapted to one principle: **nothing is stable, so survival is coordination**.

Animals coordinate with architecture. People coordinate with sound and tide. Machines coordinate with microfractures. Microbes coordinate with waste.

And the city, in return, doesn’t offer pastoral comfort. It offers niches.

A warm vent grate where bats can roost.
A market’s under-deck where cable-runners can nest.
A seawall chamber where scavengers feed on whatever the tide brings.
A shaded canal face where biofilm can grow thick enough to be harvested.
A pocket under an overpass where, for thirty seconds between transit convoys, a person can hear their own breathing and remember they are an animal too.

That is what lives there: not a singular population, but an interlocking set of hungers and repairs. A crowded, wet ecology that uses metal as cliff, concrete as reef, noise as weather, and motion as law.
