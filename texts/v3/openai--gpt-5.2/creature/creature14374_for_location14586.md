Life here is not a flourish, it’s a set of solutions—quiet bodies and quiet minds shaped by cold water that won’t leave.

At first you notice the *small* things, because they are everywhere and they’re the real foundation: films, crusts, skins.

### The first residents: what lives in the wetness itself
On bare rock that looks sterile from a distance, the plateau is already occupied.

- **Stone-slick microbial mats** live wherever water pauses: in the shallow pans, in the seams of basalt plates, in the braided rills that can’t decide where to go. They tint the ground in bruised greens and smoky ambers, and they make the plateau smell faintly metallic after rain. When a cold snap comes, they don’t “die” so much as fold inward—chemistry slowed to a careful near-stop—then wake again when meltwater returns.
- **Lichen-cities** colonize exposed ridges. Up close, they’re intricate: pale rosettes and crusts that trap moisture out of fog, holding it against stone the way a sponge holds a secret. They are old in a land that otherwise feels young, and they are one of the few things that don’t have to move to survive here.
- In the marsh shelves, **threadlike algae and fungal lattices** braid through sedge roots and engineered fill, binding the soil together. They’re part of why the marsh clings to the plateau’s edge instead of simply washing away; the ecosystem is literally stitching itself to the land.

These low-level residents are the plateau’s true “green.” They are not decorative; they are infrastructure. Everything else eats, lives in, or times itself by them.

### Invertebrate abundance: the quiet engine
If you watch any shallow water long enough, it reveals the plateau’s real density.

- **Ice-tolerant insect analogues** breed in astonishing numbers in water that is barely water—thin, cold, temporary sheets. Their life cycles are fast and opportunistic. Eggs survive freeze; larvae graze microbial mats; adults rise in brief, synchronized hatches that look like smoke lifting from puddles.
- Along city drainage veins and warm runoff channels, there are **ventline midges** and **condensate gnats** that live almost entirely on the boundary between liquid and air. They aren’t drawn to human food so much as to human *heat*: the stable humidity near conduits, the steady trickle beneath transit decks.
- In the mineral flats, where nothing obvious grows, small **detritivores** live under stones and within gravel fans, feeding on windblown organics, shed lichen, dead insects, and the thin rain of urban residue that makes it out into the open.

The ecosystem’s soundlessness makes this easy to miss. There’s no summer roar of life—just a constant, muted cycling: hatch, graze, hide, freeze, restart.

### Amphibious and semi-aquatic creatures: the ones who treat ice as a season, not a barrier
The plateau has a class of animals built around a simple trick: *partial surrender*.

- **Freeze-runners**—amphibious creatures with wide feet and flexible joints—move between marsh pans and rock pools. In the coldest nights, they let extremities stiffen and ice over while keeping core tissues protected. By morning they are mobile again, as if sleep has an extra stage here.
- **Marsh-browsers**—low, long-bodied herbivores—graze sedge mats and microbial films. They’re not large, but they travel in big, steady groups, leaving narrow, repeated paths that become semi-permanent lines in the marsh, like informal roads.
- Under the thinnest ice there are **darkwater filterers**, pale things that pulse slowly and feed on suspended life. They congregate near where the city’s warmed channels enter marsh catchments, turning infrastructure runoff into food web.

These animals don’t fight the plateau’s constant freeze-thaw. They synchronize with it.

### The grazers: herds built for distance and slick stone
Out on the open rock, where the plateau is most itself, you find the big movers: lean herds that make the emptiness feel inhabited.

- They have **broad, splayed hooves** (or hoof-analog pads) with textured edges that grip wet basalt and thin ice the way a tire grips rain-slick pavement.
- Their bodies run “cool” by default—dense insulation over a metabolism that doesn’t waste heat in showy bursts. When they need speed, it’s economical, not dramatic.
- They travel on habitual routes that thread between districts, causeways, and marsh shelves, using the city’s raised lines as windbreaks without becoming dependent on them.

If you stand near an elevated transit line at dawn, you sometimes see a herd moving under it—not spooked, just purposeful—using the shadowed strip where the ice is more predictable.

### Predators: pressure rather than spectacle
Predation here isn’t a chase across open ground; it’s an understanding of time and fatigue.

- **Plateau stalkers** follow herds at a distance for days, pressing them toward harder crossings or away from the best forage. They’re patient, intelligent, and astonishingly quiet—built for long surveillance in cold air that carries sound too well.
- In the marsh margins, **reed-hunters** work like accountants: methodical steps, sudden stillness, then a precise strike into sedge or shallow water.
- Around the city, smaller **edge predators** patrol ventilation gardens and maintenance zones, taking the insects and scavengers that congregate where warmth leaks. They’ve learned the geometry of structures: where an animal must pause to climb, where runoff channels funnel movement, where a light pole casts a shadow that briefly hides approach.

The city doesn’t “attract predators” so much as it creates an edge—an ecological shoreline—where hunting becomes efficient.

### Birds and the sky-life: disciplined flight in big weather
The plateau makes the sky feel close in a strange way, and birds treat it as a set of corridors.

- **Long-legged waders** own the marsh shelves. They move with the same restraint as everything else here, their calls sparse and far-carrying.
- On windy days, **ridge-gliders**—broad-winged scavengers—ride the uplift along unfinished escarpments. They watch herds, watch the city’s waste-sealed perimeters, watch maintenance swarms the way gulls might watch fishing boats.
- In warmer seams near vents and courtyards, small **heatline perchers** nest where condensation feeds insect life. They’re the most “urban” birds: bold enough to live close, quiet enough not to draw attention.

At night, when the city keeps its lights down, migration becomes visible in the old way: silhouettes crossing a star-field, choosing darkness because darkness is safer.

### Urban scavengers: the ones who learn schedules
The city is not loud, but it is regular. Some animals become fluent in that regularity.

- **Quick-footed gleaners**—foxlike, mustelidlike, or something entirely local—move along the undersides of decks and the lee of service housings. They don’t raid trash (there isn’t much to raid); they hunt insects, take the occasional small vertebrate, steal nesting material, drink from managed trickles.
- **Plaza-skirters**—small herd animals adapted to stone—graze the moss seams and hardy grasses around heat leaks. They’ve learned where people don’t walk, where the ground stays least icy, and which surfaces hum faintly with warmth.
- **Civic opportunists** (the most successful scavengers) have a particular talent: they can tell the difference between a maintenance drone and a living threat, between a passing commuter and a person who will throw something. They survive by being correctly indifferent.

Nothing here becomes a pest in the old, messy way; the city’s sealing and discipline prevent that. But plenty of life becomes *adjacent*—living in the margins of design.

### The hidden partner: machines as a kind of resident life
The maintenance swarms are not “alive,” and yet they function like a resident species.

They are the plateau’s thin immune system: micro-drones that reapply hydrophobic coatings, heat a drainage vein just enough to keep it moving, stitch a crack before freeze-thaw can widen it, vacuum up organic buildup where it might attract the wrong imbalance of insects. You rarely see them directly; you see their spoor in reverse—surfaces that should have failed but haven’t.

Over time, animals incorporate them into their mental maps: they avoid certain hums, follow others, time crossings to the moments when traffic is light and maintenance is done. In this place, technology becomes another weather pattern.

### People: the species that learned to live like the plateau
The human presence (or whoever the builders are, if not human) doesn’t dominate by noise or density. It dominates by *pattern*.

The locals tend to share traits you see echoed in the ecosystem:

- **Patience as a bodily habit.** People walk like they expect the ground to change its mind—feet placed with care, momentum held in reserve. They are rarely in a hurry across open spaces; vastness trains its own pace.
- **Layered clothing and layered planning.** Everything is modular: garments designed to vent moisture without losing heat; tools that can be used in gloves; transport routines with redundancies because weather will interrupt someone’s clean schedule sooner or later.
- **A cultural respect for drainage and circulation.** Children learn early where not to pour water, where to knock ice away, how to read the city’s channels the way someone elsewhere reads traffic lights. People talk about water the way sailors talk about wind: not as an inconvenience, as a fact that can be worked with.
- **Low-light etiquette.** Night lighting is directed and minimal, so citizens develop a practiced darkness-awareness. They use reflective markers, subtle beacons, narrow cones of light; they don’t flood the sky because the sky is part of safety here.

Social life clusters in sheltered nodes—transit hubs, warmed courtyards, indoor markets—then stretches thin again across causeways and long straight lines. Communities are less about a continuous neighborhood and more about *strings of familiar stations* across distance.

And because the city never fully excludes the nonhuman world, people grow up literate in it. They know the migration corridors. They know which waders mean the marsh is about to freeze hard. They know that the sudden absence of insects around a vent means a maintenance cycle has changed something—or that a predator has moved in.

### What “belongs” here: an ecosystem of restraint
If you keep looking, the plateau stops feeling empty and starts feeling densely inhabited by things that have all agreed on certain rules:

- Don’t waste energy.
- Don’t assume dryness.
- Don’t assume still ground.
- Don’t announce yourself loudly, because sound carries.
- Move when you must, rest when you can, and use whatever warmth exists—sun, vent, body, rock seam—without ever depending on it completely.

The place is full of lives that don’t advertise themselves. Standing at the boundary where a district’s pavement ends and marsh begins, you can watch them all at once: tiny ripples in a puddle that isn’t deep enough to matter until it suddenly does; a line of waders stepping like careful machines; a herd sliding over stone with practiced certainty; a predator keeping time at the edge of sight; a person pausing to read the sheen of ice on a drainage channel; and, beneath everything, the quiet work of mats, roots, and maintenance swarms—keeping the plateau’s wet cold world continuously, stubbornly habitable.
