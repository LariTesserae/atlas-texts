What lives there does not present itself as individuals.

At first it seems like a single organism wearing many costumes—mats, columns, curtains, domes—until you spend time close enough to notice the seams: the slight differences in sheen and temperature, the way one surface drinks water greedily while the adjacent one sheds it, the faint shift in sour-sweet odors that marks a different chemistry at work. The plain is a community, but it behaves like an apparatus. It is not “a forest” so much as a wet, warm metabolism that has learned to stand up in places and lie flat in others.

### The smallest residents: the makers of gradients
The true citizens are microscopic and everywhere, because this world runs on what you cannot see.

In the peat and gel layers live dense consortia of bacteria and archaea that treat the ground like a staircase of electron donors and acceptors. A few centimeters down, oxygen is gone; deeper, even nitrate and sulfate become precious; deeper still, the system breathes carbon itself—fermentation that tastes sweet on the air, methanogenesis that you meet as a soft bubble and a faint metallic tang. These microbes do not “bloom” or “crash.” They hold their populations at steady ratios the way a well-tuned chemical plant holds pressures in its pipes.

Threaded through them are filamentous bacteria—pale, hairlike, almost fungal in habit—that form living bridges between zones that should not easily share chemistry. They are the first hint that conduction matters here: thin biological wires, conductive sheaths, biofilms that move electrons the way other places move pollen.

And then there are the biofilms on basalt itself: slick skins that colonize every sharp plane. They are dark, not from pigments to catch sunlight but from minerals they precipitate into themselves—iron oxides, manganese crusts—turning rock faces into slow batteries. If you peel back a scrap (it resists, elastic), the underside smells sharper, more mineral; it has been eating the stone not for building blocks but for charge.

### Fungi: the quiet infrastructure
Fungi are not a guild here; they are the connective tissue of the whole place.

They live in the rootmats as lattices, in the dead layers as warm lace, in the hollow stems as soft internal ribbing. Some are decomposers in the ordinary sense, turning plant shed into peat and peat into broth. Others are partners, embedded so intimately in living tissues that it becomes hard to say where plant ends and fungus begins: hyphae running alongside conductive bands, hyphae lining capillary channels, hyphae forming valves that swell shut when humidity crosses a threshold.

Many of them are melanized—black-fleshed—not for UV protection (there isn’t much UV to fear) but because dark polymers bind metals well and tolerate constant wet. Their bodies are less like mushrooms and more like seals and gaskets: they exist to manage leakage, to keep the system’s slow flows from becoming diffuse.

When something “dies” here, it doesn’t fall; it relaxes. A sheath slumps, a wall of fibrous tissue softens, and within hours it has been infiltrated. Decay is not a phase; it is a service running continuously beneath the visible forms, like a second circulation.

### The dominant macrolife: plants that are not satisfied with light
The big structures—the ones you mistake for trees and vines—are plants only by analogy. They are builders of plumbing, not ornaments of photosynthesis.

They make themselves out of laminated fibers that remain wet without rotting, impregnated with waxes that don’t repel water so much as direct it. Their “bark” is often a skin of overlapping membranes: one layer porous, one layer slick, one layer that swells into a seal. Touch them and you feel that they are engineered for controlled wetness, not for dryness.

They come in several recognizable forms, though each blurs into the next:

#### 1) The column-groves (load-bearing organisms)
These are the pylons: thick stems that don’t aspire to height so much as to *support.* They branch everywhere because branching is bracing; their arches and buttresses knit into a low ceiling that carries the weight of the canopy-mass like a bridge carries a roadbed.

Inside, their tissues are divided into channels—some for water, some for gas, some for ions. You can sometimes see the ghost of that internal architecture from the outside: ribs that repeat at regular spacing, bands of darker mineral inclusion running like structural steel. They don’t creak, even when they shift; the movements are so slow and water-damped they register as pressure changes more than motion.

These columns are the place’s bones, but they are alive the way bone is alive: inhabited, porous, always remodeling.

#### 2) The felt mats (heat harvesters and circulators)
In the basins live the dark felts—thick mats that look simple until you notice they are layered. The top is a fine nap that catches condensation and holds it in place long enough to be routed downward. Beneath is a denser layer rich in microbes and mineral grains. Beneath that, an insulating raft of dead-but-not-gone fibers that traps geothermal warmth the way a blanket traps body heat.

Their function is to create gradients: slightly warmer below, slightly cooler above, enough to keep water moving without wind. They are the place’s heart-muscle, pumping by physics. If you stand in one basin long enough, you can feel the air change with their cycle: a subtle exhale of warmer dampness, a pause, then a cooler settling as water sinks elsewhere. The “schedule” is set by vapor pressure and membrane flex, but the mats are built to make those thresholds reliable.

#### 3) The curtain-growths (vertical condensation engines)
Where basalt rises, life turns into hanging systems: curtains of rootlike tissue draped down rock faces, their surfaces textured with microgrooves that guide water into channels. These are not roots seeking soil; they are collectors living off the rock’s constant weeping.

Some curtains include stiffened strands—cables more than vines—reinforced with mineral bands. They act as both support and conduit, delivering water and dissolved metals back into the peat network below. The curtains make cliffs into reservoirs without ever storing water in a single place; they store it in *movement.*

#### 4) The domes and plate-fields (electrochemical organs)
The most alien forms are the low domes: broad, pressed leaf-fields edged with mineral seams. They feel warmer, and the air around them feels subtly wrong, as if your skin is part of the circuit just by being there.

These are not flowers or fruits. They are organs for charge management. Across their surfaces, redox reactions are staged with obsessive regularity: oxygen-rich pockets above, oxygen-poor mud below; metal-rich seep water meeting acidic decay products; microbial films acting as catalysts. The plants provide the geometry—huge wet surface area, separated layers, controlled diffusion—so that the landscape can behave like a slow power station.

Here “growth” means adding more electrode area. Expansion is not conquest for light, but for potential difference.

### How they reproduce without animals
Life here spreads like stain and weld.

Spores exist, yes, but they are not flung into air with the hope of a ride. They are released when humidity reaches a precise threshold and films of water become continuous enough to carry them. Many plants reproduce by fusing mats—two networks touch and, over time, become one network, their conductive tissues aligning, their fungi negotiating compatibility. It is less mating than *merging of systems.*

Some structures make enclosed reproductive chambers that never open to the air: sacs that fill with warm condensate, dissolve an internal membrane, exchange gametes by contact, then reseal. The “flower” is a gasketed vessel. The “pollinator” is physics.

### What they eat, what they exhale
They eat stone, seep, and rot.

- From basalt: iron, manganese, trace metals—used not as nutrients alone but as functional materials, deposited in rings and bands that change conductivity and stiffness.
- From peat: organic acids, fermentation products, the slow soup of breakdown.
- From water: dissolved gases, faint oxygen pulses managed like a resource.

They exhale moisture (the cloud roof is their breath), small steady amounts of methane and sulfur compounds from the underlayers, and—most importantly—*structure.* The output is architecture: filters, wicks, condensers, baffles, valves. The ecosystem’s “biomass” is largely plumbing and scaffolding built to keep chemistry in motion.

### The social order of a place with no animals
Without bite marks or burrows, competition is not violent; it is geometric.

Species win by holding a gradient steady, by making a better seal, by routing water a fraction more efficiently, by precipitating a metal in a way that discourages a neighbor’s fungi. Territory is not defended with toxins so much as with *hydrology:* a mat that can keep its surface slightly drier (or slightly wetter) excludes organisms tuned to the opposite. A column-grove that can maintain oxygen pockets in its tissues becomes a refuge for certain microbes, which in turn power its electrochemistry, which in turn lets it mineralize stronger supports. Advantage is a loop.

The nearest thing to “behavior” is the opening and closing of membranes: pore-fields that swell shut in overly saturated air, then relax when evaporation has pulled the boundary layer thin; chambers that fill, then siphon; tissue-curtains that change their droop by a few millimeters as internal pressure shifts. In a world without events, these tiny motions are the drama.

### What you would call them, if you had to name them
If a human taxonomy tried to pin them down, it would keep failing, because the important distinctions here aren’t between plant and fungus, or organism and environment. They are between *functions.*

- **Condensers:** leaf-fields that harvest ceiling-drip and route it.
- **Circulators:** basin mats that turn stagnant water into slow currents.
- **Electrodes:** mineral-banded tissues that trade electrons with the peat.
- **Seals:** fungal skins and membrane layers that prevent valuable gradients from dissipating.
- **Frameworks:** column-groves that hold up the canopy-mass and maintain gas pockets.

And beneath all of it, everywhere, the microbial workforce that makes the gradients worth engineering in the first place.

So what lives there is not a chorus of creatures but a single ongoing project: wet life exploiting steadiness. A world that discovered, early, that if nothing is ever dry and nothing ever disturbed, the best way to survive is to become a machine made of moss and fiber and metal—and then to spread until the machine is the landscape.
