What lives up there is not a list of species so much as a set of occupations. The upland is full of workers—organisms that treat rock face and fog bank the way lowland life treats soil and river. They don’t merely endure the place; they complete its circuits.

### The first residents: skins that make a world habitable
Before anything with a spine matters, the upland is claimed by **films**.

- **Stone-skins**: ultra-thin biofilms—slick as breath on glass—spread over basalt and fresh mineral seams. In sun they go dark and glossy, drinking light hard and fast; under cloud they pale and switch metabolism, stripping trace metals from seepage. They don’t look like “plants” so much as a change in how the rock reflects.

- **Rime-lace lichens**: layered rosettes and crusts that grow in strict bands according to exposure, like tide marks on a cliff. Each band is a different alliance: one tolerates brutal UV and desiccation, another specializes in mineral-rich damp, another in the brief warmth of noon rock.

- **Velvet mats**: mosslike poured carpets that can lift and settle with moisture, as if they have lungs. They creep over scree and lock it together grain by grain. On a young mountain they are the first real “engineering”: they slow the constant, patient downhill flow and make terraces where nothing was stable enough to stand.

These skins already feel “industrial” because they behave like coatings and gaskets: sealing, insulating, regulating. Where they thicken, they change the mountain’s chemistry—pH, mineral availability, the very rate at which the stone weathers. The upland’s lushness begins as an alteration of surfaces.

### The vent-felts: living insulation around the planet’s seams
Around warm cracks and cold exhaling fissures live the things you noticed first as “living gaskets”:

- **Seamfelts**: pale, fibrous colonies neither comfortably fungus nor plant. Up close, they are millions of hair-fine filaments bound in a felted mass, each filament hosting internal symbionts that feed on chemical gradients—sulfur, iron, hydrogen traces, heat itself as difference. They grow in rings because they are literally tuned to the vent’s edge: too hot kills them, too cold starves them.

Seamfelts do three jobs at once:
1. **Thermal moderation**: they keep the vent “in range,” trapping heat where it benefits their symbionts and shedding it in cold breath-ribbons that condense fog.
2. **Water rationing**: they soak and release moisture slowly, turning brief melt and condensation into long availability.
3. **Mineral capture**: they precipitate minerals into their own structure; old felts become softly mineralized banks, a strange in-between of organism and deposit.

Where seamfelts have persisted for centuries, you get whole vent-corridors that look upholstered—soft ridges, padded lips of fissures, pale berms that steer meltwater like levees.

### The hanging forests: epiphytes that drink the air
In sheltered amphitheaters and under overhangs, the upland grows **forests that don’t need soil**.

- **Cup-fronds**: translucent, cupped epiphytes that fog-beads cling to. They have micro-grooves that wick water into their tissues, and their undersides are warm-toned—designed to absorb radiant heat from rock.

- **Sponge-braids**: ropey, dangling growths with a texture like resilient foam. They are living reservoirs, and other organisms treat them as infrastructure: nesting, feeding, hiding, even “tapping” them.

- **Resin-dark groves**: where trees manage to exist, they are compact and defensive—short, thick-trunked, needles or small leathery leaves, bark dense with resin. They are less a forest than a cluster of wind-shelters. Their branches are crowded with parasites and tenants; their job is to make still air.

The hanging forests are where the upland feels most crowded in three dimensions—except even here, the real crowding is on the bark and the underside of leaves, on the needle surfaces, on the thin boundary layer of still air that clings to every object.

### Grazers that can eat stone and milk fog
Large animals here are not “above” the mineral economy. They are built to harvest it.

- **Scree-hoof herds**: stocky, furred ungulate-analogues with splayed, broad feet and extra dewclaws for talus. Their coats are structured to *hold* frost as a layer—insulation you can see. They travel along contour lines, not because it’s easier walking, but because the best forage is a band: where fog drags across a slope at the same hour each day.

Their mouths and teeth are the most telling adaptation. They have:
- **Enamel ridges like cutting tools**, hardened with mineral inclusions.
- A habit of grazing not just on mats but on **mineral ribs** left by seepage, scraping them in parallel grooves as neat as machining.

- **Fog-muzzlers**: smaller, deerlike browsers that feed by combing dew and fog from epiphytes and needle clusters. Their lips and tongues are fringed—built to strip water and microfauna together. They move with the weather’s pulse: active when fog is thick, resting when sun turns everything too reflective and dry.

Where the herds pass, they don’t merely eat; they **maintain flow**: trampling mats into denser, water-holding carpets; fertilizing thin soils; opening channels that become repeatable paths for meltwater. Their migrations are slow infrastructure projects.

### Burrowers that swim through moss
Under the velvet mats, the upland has an almost aquatic layer.

- **Mat-swimmers**: long-bodied burrowers that move by undulating through moss the way eels move through weed beds. Their tunnels don’t stay open; the mat closes behind them, leaving only a traveling ripple and, later, a faint line of aerated growth.

They feed on:
- rootlike holdfasts,
- soft-bodied microfauna,
- the nutrient-rich edges where seamfelt water meets moss terraces.

Their waste is a fertilizer that never forms piles—just a distributed enrichment that keeps the mats lush without deep soil.

### The “insects”: burst-swarms and hidden carpets
If “insect” still applies, it applies as a strategy: appear fast, vanish faster.

- **Sunburst fliers**: swarms that emerge when sunlight hits black rock and makes instant updrafts. They are tiny, reflective, and coordinated, riding thermal lines in looping circuits around ridges. They do not simply mate and feed; they **carry**—spores, seeds, microscopic eggs—caught on barbs, electrostatic fuzz, sticky footpads. Their circuits are pollination routes and cargo loops.

- **Crack-dwellers**: when cloud shadow passes, the upland seems to empty. It hasn’t. The small life retracts into fissures and under-plates, where temperatures are stable and predators can’t see.

- **Understone carpets**: flip a rock and you find not a handful of beetles but a living fabric—many species layered: grazers on biofilm, predators on grazers, scavengers processing droppings and shed skins. They are the upland’s compost system, slow because cold makes everything honest, but relentless.

### Predators as landscape features
Predation here feels like infrastructure because predators are often *built into the same surface logic* as everything else.

- **Hummock-mouths**: ambush organisms that mimic mossy mounds until they flex. They aren’t plants; they’re muscular sacs anchored into mats, with trigger hairs sensitive to vibration and warmth. They take mat-swimmers, small grazers, sometimes even a careless limb from a larger animal. After feeding, they re-inflate slowly, re-growing their disguise from captured nutrients.

- **Cliff siphoners**: sessile or semi-sessile hunters that anchor in cliff cavities and cast filament nets into fog. The nets are almost invisible until they bead with moisture and trapped microfauna. Some siphoners reel in the net like fishing line; others simply digest what sticks, net and all, then re-grow.

- **Ridge-stalkers**: lean, long-limbed predators that move on scree as if it were firm ground. Their feet are flexible, spreading to distribute weight. They hunt by listening—this is a loud land, but it is loud with patterns, and these predators know the difference between “frost crack” and “hoof shift.”

They rarely chase. They cut off. They wait at the narrow points—the passes where fog-rivers pour through, the gullies where herds must funnel—and let the upland’s geometry do the running.

### The sky is inhabited, not traversed
The upland’s air is thin, bright, and full.

- **Ripplewings**: bird-outlined creatures that hover without flapping, wings rippling like cloth in a steady wind. Their bones are latticed, their muscles tuned for continuous fine adjustment, not bursts. They feed on the sunburst swarms and on airborne plankton—microfauna lifted from mats and carried on fog.

- **Cartilage kites**: drifting forms like living sails—skin stretched over flexible struts. They ride the updrafts off sunlit rock faces, barely spending energy. Their mouths are simple openings with filtering fringes. They breed on cliff faces, clinging in clustered nurseries that look like scalloped rock until they open and close.

- **Cliff-nesters**: whatever counts as “bird” here nests in cavities peppering the walls. Those cavities are rarely empty. Some are excavated by beaked diggers. Some are actually *grown*—the cliff face softened and reshaped by mineral-dissolving secretions from generations of residents, a biological quarrying that makes the wall look full of eyes and mouths.

The air guild is also the upland’s messenger system. Seeds and spores don’t need to find soil; they need to find a surface with the right film already on it. The flyers and kites ensure that new stone doesn’t stay bare for long.

### The slow rulers: organisms measured in decades
Not everything is frantic. Some life matches the mountains’ patience.

- **Bank-builders**: old seamfelts and mineral-precipitating slimes that build ridges over centuries, redirecting trickles into dependable damp zones. Whole “gardens” form downstream: sponge-braids, moss terraces, and the grazers that depend on them. If you map the upland’s fertility, it often traces the ghost of an ancient vent.

- **Glacier-edge communities**: along granular ice tongues, there are belts of organisms specialized for freeze-thaw—dark patches that warm the ice, accelerate local melt, and create microstreams that feed moss mats. These communities migrate with the glacier’s inching advance, leaving behind a patterned wake of colonization.

- **Stone-boring symbiotes**: microscopic alliances that chew into raw mineral seams, widening cracks by fractions, making more surface, making more habitat. They are why the upland’s “newness” doesn’t stay sterile; it becomes workable.

### If there is a single dominant creature, it’s the coupled system
Nothing up there sits outside the machine-feeling because the machine is real: rock uplift and venting; frost’s nightly labor; fog’s daily circulation; mineral seepage and precipitation. The living things don’t just inhabit that—they **tune** it.

The seamfelts keep vents productive instead of catastrophic. The moss mats stabilize scree into terraces. The herds grind mineral ribs into edible dust and fertilize the thinness. The swarms move reproduction like freight. The predators keep pathways from becoming overused, forcing movement that prevents any one surface from being stripped bare.

So what lives there, ultimately, is a kind of distributed organism made of many bodies: a loud, layered workforce of skins and grazers and flyers and vent-fed felts, all adapted to a world where the true resources are not forests and rivers, but **surfaces and gradients**—light intensity, temperature difference, chemical leak, the brief presence of water as fog on stone.

Stand still long enough and you can feel it operating around you: not a wilderness trying to become something else, but a high, cold ecology perfectly content to remain a working skin over young rock—busy, crowded, and patient.
