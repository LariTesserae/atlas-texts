At first it seems like nothing lives here—because nothing *insists* on itself in the usual way. There are no weed-beds to wave, no floating mats, no birds sketching the horizon into something familiar. The basin doesn’t advertise its biosphere with green. It hides it in clear water and in the small stubbornness of surfaces.

Life begins as a mistake the chemistry keeps making.

On any hard thing—basalt teeth, glass plates, the galvanized skin of a pylon—there is a shine that is not just wetness. It is a laminate: microcrystals laid down grain by grain where the water is supersaturated, and inside that mineral skin a living varnish that tolerates what plants can’t. Not so much cells with ambitions as colonies that behave like weathering. They don’t “grow” toward light; they spread wherever the boundary conditions are right: a seam that leaks warmth, a ripple in the metal where eddies pause, the narrow belt where wave-spray salts the rock with precise regularity. Their colors are faint because pigment is expensive in a world that offers no soft nutrients—pewter, bone-white, a rust-red bruise around iron, an oil-slick violet where trace metals make odd chemistry possible.

They are the basin’s first citizens, and they live by making the basin more itself: turning dissolved metal into crust, turning energy gradients into thin sheets of order. If you scrape them away, they return in the same patterns, as predictable as frost.

On those films feed the grazers, and they are the first animals you learn to *recognize*.

They aren’t bottom-dwellers in the comforting sense—there is too much vertical wall and too little “bottom”—so they cling and skate. Their bodies are dense and blunt, built like stone tools: a muscular slab with a ventral mouth that can seal to rock, and a rasping belt of mineral-hard teeth that shaves the varnish and its crystal shelter down to clean substrate. Some are solitary, patient enough to work a cliff face centimeter by centimeter, leaving pale arcs like tool marks. Others travel in slow companies across the drowned saddles where pylon-tops break the surface, using the metal spines as a ladder: feed, release, drift, reattach. Their skin is armored not with bone but with layered precipitate—shell made from the very water they live in—so that over years the individual becomes a moving sample of local chemistry. In this basin, “old” animals look increasingly like artifacts.

The grazers are also the first to tell you the machines matter, because they prefer them. Not for mystery, but for physics: warm housings grow thicker films; electrical leakage (the faint, clean bite in the water) draws certain microbes the way a spring draws salts; vibration keeps surfaces from sealing over completely. A turbine cage becomes a perennial feeding ground, and the grazers learn its geometry until their routes around it look ritualistic.

Then you notice the drifters—because they make the emptiness *move*.

In midwater, where sunlight still falls without color, there are translucent kites with bodies like stretched glass and internal threads that catch the light into thin rainbows. They don’t pulse like jellyfish, not against that steady current; they fly it. Mineral fibers stiffen their membranes, giving them shape without much muscle. Their feeding is delicate: sieving the “clean” water for what little it offers—microcrystal dust, bacterial flakes, the tiniest larvae—then excreting it as denser pellets that fall like slow hail into the deep. Around them, the water is full of almost-invisible companion life: small ribbon-things and speck-creatures that ride the pressure wake of a drifter the way birds use a ship’s wind.

Where drifters are fragile, the basin’s fish—if that word can be forgiven—are all function.

They appear as sudden density changes in the cathedral: a kilometer-scale flicker of silver bodies that hold formation the way iron filings hold a field. Their skin is reflective not for beauty but for sameness; in clear water, camouflage is a matter of refusing to be a single point. They don’t have bright markings because there is no reef to advertise to, no vegetation to hide in. Instead they have eyes tuned to the basin’s unchanging light and a lateral-line sense so acute it treats the constant roar as a reference signal. The steady wind-chop, the periodic boom of cliff-strikes, the turbine hum—these are not “noise” to them but coordinates. They live inside a sound-map so stable that a new object is immediately suspicious.

That is why so many of them move like thought: abrupt, collective, and then gone. To hesitate is to be outlined.

The predators are not rare, because in a place with few places to hide, hunting is a matter of *computation* and *commitment*.

Some are built for speed: long-bodied, pale as bone, with fins that fold tight to reduce drag and then flare for instant turns. They hunt the schools by making the first disruption—an infrasound thump that panics bodies into scattering—then choose targets by the micro-delays in the fleeing shockwaves. Others are shock-hunters themselves: thick, muscular animals that snap their torsos like loaded springs, releasing a pressure pulse that stuns prey at a distance. You don’t see the strike so much as you see the consequences ripple through a school: a sudden gap, a slow tumble of a few bodies, and then the predator arrives to collect what the water has already decided.

And there are the listeners.

They are larger, slower, and unsettlingly precise—creatures that fill the basin with patterned calls and read the returns the way a bat reads a cave, except their cave is a nation-sized bowl of water and rock and metal. The constant roar doesn’t blind them; it *helps*. Their calls are not loud compared to the wind, but they are placed in the quiet seams between the basin’s steady frequencies, and because the background is stable, any deviation is stark. A drifting drifter, a grazing herd moving along a pylon, a loose bolt vibrating differently on a turbine cage—all of it shines in their acoustic world.

If you follow one down, you understand why the deep feels like entering the casing of a machine: the listeners prefer it where the surface chaos has been filtered into low pressure. Their bodies are dark and heavy, their tissues adapted to cold clarity, their eyes reduced to light-sensing scars. They navigate by returning echoes off trench walls that look freshly cut, and they patrol boundaries—warm seam against cold brine interface—as though those boundaries are valuable.

The trenches have their own inhabitants, and they are the basin’s strangest because they live where stability becomes *layering*.

At the shimmering interfaces where supercooled brine meets warmer water and refuses to mix, there are ribbon organisms that hang across the boundary like suspended hair. They are not plants, but they function like them: they harvest chemical disequilibrium. One end in the brine, one end in the warmer layer, and along their length a slow conduction of ions and energy. They grow in curtains that don’t sway much because currents are predictable; they merely tremble in place, delicate and tireless. Small animals shelter in them—not to hide from sight (there is too much clarity) but to hide from *sound*, because the curtains scatter and damp certain frequencies. In a basin where everything is heard, anything that can blur an echo becomes a refuge.

On the trench floors—where “floor” might mean a tilted glass plate or a field of basalt columns—there are scavengers that look improvised: soft-bodied things with mineral grit embedded in their flesh, creeping along fractures and eating what falls. They are slow because there is no hurry in a world without seasons. Their reproduction is timed not to spring or summer but to rhythms in the machine network: a faint change in turbine vibration, a predictable pulse of warm water from a regulator intake, a pressure oscillation that arrives every so many days as the basin equalizes itself. Their larvae rise into the water column exactly when the currents are most likely to carry them to new surfaces. In this place, life doesn’t trust the sky; it trusts the engine.

Which brings you, inevitably, to the question the basin keeps asking without speaking: *who maintains the maintenance?*

At first you can believe it is all old automation—structures bolted to rock, buoys that nod, turbines that hum because they were built to hum. But the network behaves like it is being watched. Not in a dramatic, sentient way, but in a way that feels like attention: seals replaced before they fail, intakes cleared before they choke, output adjusted so the sea level remains pressed to the rim with uncanny fidelity.

The custodians, when you finally infer them, are not a surface people with boats and harbors. There is nowhere to build that sort of life here. They are a distributed presence, more like an ecology than a city.

Some of that presence is purely mechanical: maintenance drones that live in pressure housings and travel along cables like limpets, emerging in the quiet hours (if “hours” means anything under constant light) to brush mineral lace from vents and re-tension anchor lines. You don’t notice them at a distance because they are shaped to be uninteresting—dark, small, with movements that mimic debris. But up close you can see the intentionality in how they avoid stirring the water too much, how they pause to let silt settle before reseating a panel, how they retreat when large listeners approach, as if even the machines have learned that anything that changes the soundscape too sharply is dangerous.

Other parts of the custodian presence are alive.

Around warm housings and electrical fields, there are colonies of organisms that have become, over generations, symbionts of infrastructure. They don’t merely tolerate the metal; they *need* its gradients. Some are microbial mats that regulate corrosion by consuming certain ions and excreting others, effectively plating the machines in protective mineral. Some are small grazing-guardians—armored mouth-things that feed on the most destructive crusts first, keeping intakes from sealing. In exchange they get warmth, steady flow, and a place where predators’ echoes are complicated.

And then—rarely—you encounter something that does not fit neatly into “animal” or “tool.”

A large, slow-moving form that approaches a buoy-cable, wraps it in flexible limbs, and listens. Not listening like a predator listens for prey, but listening the way a mechanic listens to an engine. Its body is pale and smooth, with patches of embedded crystal that act as resonators. It presses itself to the cable, to the metal skin, and you can feel (if you are close enough to feel anything through the roar) the subtle adjustments it makes: tightening, loosening, shifting a weight, cleaning a joint with a rasp-mouth that looks borrowed from a grazer but used with delicacy. It does not eat the machine. It tends it, and when it is done it drifts away into the clear distance as if the task is simply part of the basin’s metabolism.

Whether these tenders are engineered descendants of something older, or a native life that discovered the advantage of partnership with regulation, the result is the same: the basin has developed organisms that treat infrastructure as habitat the way other seas produce reefs. The machines are not foreign objects anymore; they are skeleton and circulatory system, and life has moved into them.

So what lives here is not a single emblematic beast or a lonely outpost community. It is a hierarchy of adaptations to steadiness:

- **Surface-skin chemistries** that grow like mineral weather.
- **Hard-mouthed grazers** that turn varnish into motion and keep rock faces newly clean.
- **Midwater fliers and schooling bodies** that use a stable soundscape as navigation.
- **Predators that hunt by pressure and pattern**, because sight is too honest here.
- **Boundary-harvesters in the trenches** that live on gradients the planet refuses to let collapse.
- **Machine-symbionts and tenders**—some mechanical, some biological—that make regulation into an ecosystem.

In a world where the wave strikes the same cliff at the same angle for decades, the dominant survival trait is not opportunism. It is *alignment*. Everything that persists has learned to fit itself into a constant engine without jamming it—has learned, in effect, to become one more stable frequency in the basin’s ongoing roar.
