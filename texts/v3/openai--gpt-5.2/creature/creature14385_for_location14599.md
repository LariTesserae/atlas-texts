What lives there is not a single species so much as an agreement—an interlocked citizenship of tissues, salts, and careful breath. If you insist on “inhabitants,” you can point to animals that move and look back at you; but the longer you stand inside that ring of young cliffs, the more the place teaches you that the primary resident is the settlement itself: a composite organism made of many organisms, each one shaped by the same dry law and each one quietly enforcing it.

### The rooted citizens: column-keepers and mat-weavers

The tall columns are the obvious bodies, the ones that claim vertical space the way trees do elsewhere. Up close they are less trunk than stack: segmented reservoirs, each joint a sealed module with its own ration of bound moisture, its own mineral reinforcement. The parchment sheaths are not dead bark; they’re the city’s first layer of weather—an exfoliating, self-renewing skin that flakes off in thin sheets the way mica does, taking dust and microbes with it.

Their fronds—thick, translucent blades—are working surfaces. By day they are solar collectors and shade-makers; by night they become dew-thieves, micro-tilting to present the right texture to the slightest increase in humidity. Their veins of amber resin are not merely plumbing. Resin is antiseptic, adhesive, and structural; it’s what the city makes when it needs something to become permanent in a place that cannot afford rot.

Between the columns, the mats are the real density. They read at first like groundcover, but they behave like infrastructure: springy, waxed, gritty, stitched with roots and fungal cords that are more like cable than mycelium. Some mats are “public”—kept low, combed, and uniform to serve as flooring that traps dust without becoming loose. Others are “private,” bulking upward into low berms and lips around openings, shaping airflow and guiding any accidental condensation away from vulnerable walls and storage bodies.

Those beadlike nodules in the mats are not decorative. Some are salt glands: little valves that migrate mineral out of the tissues and onto the surface where it can harden into protective crust. Some are symbiotic houses—tiny domes that open and close for minute insects, offering wax and receiving waste heat, trace nutrients, and the scraped cleanings of the insect’s own grooming.

### The living cisterns: gel-bearers and the politics of moisture

The pale gourd-like sacs embedded in the ground are the closest thing the city has to treasure. They are storage organisms—slow, stationary, and heavily defended, not by teeth but by chemistry. Their skins are uninviting: wax over mineral over wax again, dusted with bitter salts that discourage casual chewing. Inside is not water but a cool matrix that *refuses* to become water. It holds vapor as structure.

They are tended. Not in a sentimental way, but with the constant small interventions of a place that cannot let its savings leak. Their rims are kept clear of loose powder; the surrounding mats are trained into raised gutters. The air above them feels subtly different—slightly denser, faintly sweet with resin and something alkaline—as if the gel-bearers are always bargaining with the atmosphere, taking a molecule here, releasing one there, never enough to wet anything, always enough to keep the city from cracking.

In dry years, these bodies shrink by millimeters. In better years they swell just enough that the ground around them shows a new crescent of white crust, as if the place is marking its own prosperity in tidelines that never involved a tide.

### The small, everywhere folk: dust-silent insects and pellet-masons

The pearl-colored insects you glimpse are not one kind. They are a guild.

Some are harvesters—minute, soft-bodied, almost larval in their simplicity—moving in threadlike lines to graze films of microbes off wax and mineral. They leave behind almost nothing except a cleaner surface and the faintest change in sheen.

Some are carriers, with hard casings that look like pale ceramic. They move with purpose along the swept corridors, stopping at junctions as if the junctions have meaning. Their mouths work constantly: scraping, tasting, kneading dust with secretion into pellets. These are the pellet-masons, and their work is everywhere in the porous pumice-like walls. They don’t “build” as an event; they build as metabolism. A wall is not erected; it accretes.

Watch one long enough and you’ll see the rules it obeys without ever being told: it avoids the tacky night-surfaces when pores are open; it prefers to place pellets where airflow will cure them cleanly; it won’t deposit on a patch of mat that has not been groomed to hydrophobic perfection. Their building is constrained by moisture discipline, as strict as any engineering code.

There are also the ones that look like they are doing nothing—standing still under fronds, angled oddly, antennae barely moving. These are listeners. They read vibration through the plant tissues, reading the city the way a spider reads a web. When something shifts—a settling stone fin, a new crack propagating with the slow patience of cooling rock—these insects redistribute, and within hours the carrier lines change course. Buttressing begins where it is needed, not because anyone issued a command, but because the listeners *alter the traffic of work* by touch, scent, and vibration.

If there is intelligence here that feels most like governance, it lives in that loop.

### The mid-sized movers: felt-footed watchers and seam-keepers

The ash-blue lizardlike animals are not mere predators in a food web; they are maintenance in motion.

Their felted feet are for silence, yes, but also for adhesion on waxed surfaces and porous mineral. Their frilled scales are thermoregulatory flags, opening dark to drink heat quickly, closing pale to reflect it—an economy of warming that wastes nothing on heavy breathing. They spend long periods immobile on columns and walls, not asleep so much as *in patience*, watching insect traffic, listening for the faint tremors that signal a cavity becoming unstable.

They hunt, but their hunting is neat. A quick tongue, a small crunch, no spraying fluids, no tearing that would wet a surface with fresh body water. After feeding they groom obsessively, scraping residue off their mouths and forelimbs, polishing themselves back into hydrophobic order. Their droppings are dry pellets—valuable, collected by smaller insects and glued into walls, mineral-rich and already desiccated.

In some courts you’ll find them in pairs, pressed close along a warm basalt plate, trading slow movements: one lifts a frill, the other mirrors; one shifts, the other follows. It reads like bonding, and it probably is, but it is also thermodynamic thrift—two bodies sharing a boundary layer of warmth, reducing the need for either to spend moisture on temperature control.

They are seam-keepers. Where plant lattices meet dust-cement, where a chimney slit flexes too wide, where a niche lip begins to crumble—those are the places these animals spend their time. They cannot repair with hands, but their presence changes the behavior of the insect guilds: a lizard’s skin carries oils and salts that discourage some microbes and invite others; its scent marks a zone as watched, and watched zones get maintained.

### The high shadows: gliders, hush-voices, and the outer ring

Above the canopies, the larger winged things are mostly absence: shadow without sound, a soft click that seems too small to belong to a big body. They are not built for flapping; they are built for riding the slow drafts that the city’s architecture generates—warm air rising through braided chimneys, cooler air sliding down corridor canyons.

They nest in the stacked niches near the boundary cliffs, where the wind is a little freer but still tamed by the broken ring. Their nests are dry composites: woven fiber, resin, dust-cement, lined not with feathers but with shed parchment sheaths and mats of microfibers stolen from the city’s own flooring. Theft here is not crime; it is circulation. The city tolerates it because it benefits: gliders carry spores on their bellies; they drop dry pellets of waste that are rich in nitrogen; they remove surplus insects and thus prevent any one guild from blooming into a moisture-expensive swarm.

Their calls are close-range because distance is dangerous. A loud call implies wasted breath; a long-range call implies a world where sound can travel without being absorbed by wax and porous mineral. These gliders speak the acoustics of their home: clicks, rasps, short notes meant to be caught by a nearby ear and not offered to the open air.

### The hidden majority: fungi-cords, dust-eaters, and the city’s digestion

Underfoot, the fungal cords are not simply decomposers. They are dry-adapted chemists, turning dead fiber into usable structure without ever letting it become wet decay. When something dies here, it becomes scaffold: salts crystallize in its tissues, resins harden, fibers desiccate. The fungi colonize that scaffold slowly, breaking it down into powder that can be carried, glued, or pressed into new crust.

In the porous walls, in the velvet-lined chimneys, in the cavities that “open like mouths and then close,” there are communities of microbes and tiny invertebrates that function like lungs and gut flora. Dust is filtered, sorted, stored. Air is cleaned. Nutrients are cycled without slurry, without seep, without the lavish dissolving that liquid water would allow.

That is perhaps the most alien richness: the city does not run on flow; it runs on *exchange*. Molecule by molecule. Grain by grain. Heat by heat.

### Who, then, is the resident?

If you wait long enough, you start to see that the “inhabitants” are arranged less like a list of species and more like organs.

- The columns and fronds are the roof and the solar skin.
- The mats are flooring, gutters, insulation, and trap.
- The gel-bearers are savings accounts and drought insurance.
- The insects are masons, sweepers, couriers, and sensors.
- The lizardlike seam-keepers are inspectors and quiet enforcement.
- The gliders are the city’s link to the boundary air—its scattering mechanism, its pruning hand.

And threaded through all of it is a distributed coordination that doesn’t need tools or spoken plans: behavior guided by texture, scent, temperature, and vibration—rules embodied so completely that they feel like ethics.

In a wetter world, “community” often means sharing abundance. Here, community means preventing catastrophe. It means making sure nothing becomes liquid.

So what lives there is a civilization of dryness: not barren, not fragile, but exquisitely disciplined—lush as a library, dense as a reef, and as quietly busy as a body keeping itself alive in a place where the wrong kind of rain would be ruin.
