Life here doesn’t present itself as individuals first. It presents itself as a *system*—a set of hungers that have learned to ride water and charge the way other places ride sunlight.

At the smallest scale the inhabitants aren’t “creatures” so much as arrangements: films, veils, skins that keep being born and torn away, then born again. The first resident is a chemistry that has learned to metabolize.

### The dark curtains on the stone

Those glossy conductive mats are not one organism. They’re a stacked community, a laminated city built out of necessity.

Closest to the spray are the breathers: bacteria and archaea that want oxygen, clinging where the mist keeps replenishing it. Beneath them, pressed into the places water runs without aeration, are the ones that can’t tolerate oxygen at all—the deep-fermenters, the iron-reducers, the sulfur-workers. Between layers, the boundary is sharp: a fraction of a millimeter where the world flips from “air” to “underwater” without changing location.

What makes this community distinctive isn’t merely that it lives on rock. It lives on *gradient*—oxygen above, anoxia below, iron between—and it lives on the fact that the rock itself keeps worrying at its own seams. The quartz discharges are like tiny weather events that never stop: minute lightning, constant enough to be predictable. Over time, the mats have become not just tolerant of that input but structured around it.

If you could peel a strip of the curtain (and it would resist, like wet leather), you’d find it threaded with mineral needles and bacterial sheaths arranged along flow lines—an accidental wiring diagram that repeats because it works. Charge migrates through it the way warmth migrates through fur. When it releases, the arc isn’t a dramatic bolt; it’s a pinprick, a brief stitch of blue-white that leaves no scar but changes behavior all around it: insects pause, fish flinch, the mist momentarily becomes a lit volume.

The mats grow with a particular habit: they do not colonize randomly. They “prefer” edges—ribs of basalt, seams of iron, lip-lines where spray breaks into droplets. Over years the stone begins to look dressed, not by hands but by a living drapery that keeps being re-hemmed by floods.

They are the primary producers of this pocket. The rest of the basin is, in a sense, their animals.

### The grazers you almost mistake for grit

Where the mats thicken, the wall seems peppered with moving specks. These are the first obvious consumers: pale, wingless crawlers that look like animated seeds until you watch them anchor and rasp.

They have hard mouthparts made for scraping biofilm, and they move with a rhythm that suggests they’ve learned the timing of the place. When a siphon dumps and the spray turns violent, they flatten into the film; when the flow relaxes, they lift and feed again. Their bodies often carry a dusting of iron precipitate, so that in a brief arc-light they glitter as if dusted with metal filings.

Among them are threadlike worms and soft-bodied slugs no longer than a fingernail—translucent enough that you can see dark gut-lines pulsing as they process the mat. Some excrete beads of concentrated mineral waste that roll into cracks and become nuclei for new crystal growth, which in turn becomes new scaffold for the next generation of film. In this way the grazers don’t just eat the curtain; they help rebuild it, turning digestion into architecture.

The true aerial insects—midges, stoneflies, and a few cold-tolerant moths—treat the chamber as a hatchery and a shield. The constant roar masks wingbeat and warning call. Their larvae cling to the wet walls and ledges, and when they emerge they don’t rise into open sky so much as ricochet through mist, mate fast, and drop eggs back into noise.

### The pool-keepers: newts and the quiet side basins

In the slower vats and side pools live the basin’s amphibians: dark, thick-skinned newts that seem carved out of peat. They are not abundant everywhere—only where circulation is gentle and the mats’ metabolism puts off a faint, local warmth, just enough to keep the water from going utterly inert.

These newts are patient in a way that feels specific to this room. They will hold motionless on a submerged ledge while the whole chamber trembles. Their eyes are small and glossy, adapted to grayness; their hunting seems guided as much by vibration as by sight. When the siphon coughs and the current surges, they tuck in and let debris pass over them like weather.

Their eggs are laid where most pondlife would never risk it: on the underside of wet stone close to the curtain-film, in places where the conductive mat keeps microbial predators at bay—either by chemistry or by the tiny, periodic discharges that make the immediate surface an unpleasant place for soft-bodied scavengers. The newts have learned, over generations, that the “electric wall” is safer nursery than a quiet pond.

### Fish that live in a place that won’t hold still

The stream’s captured run is full of quick-bodied fish that hold station in turbulence—small, dark-backed shapes that hover in eddies and vanish when you try to focus. Their fins are broad for their size, built to grip moving water the way hands grip a rope. They feed on larvae swept from the walls, on drowned midges, on anything dislodged when a curtain of biofilm peels away.

In the deeper vats are the scavengers that give the chamber its hint of something older than the surface world: pale, eel-like bodies with slow mouths and an unhurried persistence. They are not necessarily huge, but in a pocket-scale basin they feel outsized—too much muscle for such a contained room. They wedge themselves under ledges where silt collects, and they vacuum the basin clean of what the falls kill.

When the arcs flash, these eels briefly look plated—light running along their wet backs in a way that suggests metal rather than skin. Some carry freckles of magnetite in their tissues—dark grains that may help them orient in a place where sound is constant and landmarks shift seasonally. Whether they *sense* the chamber’s electrical moods or merely endure them is hard to tell, but they behave differently after big dumps: they come out, as if oxygen and agitation mean opportunity.

### Birds that use the roar as camouflage

Above the wet stone, where a human voice would be swallowed, small birds move like thrown shadows.

Dippers are the obvious ones, built for this—compact, dark, fearless of water. They bob on slick ledges and then plunge into the run as if the cold were irrelevant. The chamber’s constant noise gives them a kind of invisibility: predators that hunt by ear have a harder time isolating their movements, and their own calls don’t carry beyond the rim.

Alongside them are small thrushes and brown, spray-tolerant passerines that work the willow fringe and the dripping roots. They feed in bursts, darting to snatch midges out of air pockets, then vanishing into sedge where the ground is too unstable for a heavier animal to pounce cleanly.

In nesting season they use the chamber’s odd infrastructure. A dipper nest here is often wedged behind a fall, where the spray keeps parasites down and the constant motion confuses anything trying to approach. It’s a risky bet—one slump, one rerouted channel, and the nest is erased—but the birds that succeed raise young in a place few rivals will tolerate.

### The rim-walkers: mammals as traces and habits

Larger mammals do not live *in* the basin so much as around it, using it as resource and boundary.

Fox-sized predators make paths along the rim because the edge is where prey must pass: birds commuting between willows and ledges, rodents skirting wet ground, amphibians dispersing after rains. The animals that patrol here learn the geography of collapse—where the ground gives, where it holds—and they move with a careful, practiced lightness that leaves narrow pressed trails rather than churned mud.

Deer test the place, as you’ve seen, but do not commit. The basin is too uncertain: a hoof can find nothing but sponge and cold. They browse the willow fringe and retreat.

Small rodents and shrews do better. They exploit the warm breath of the chamber—slightly higher temperature along certain cracks—and build hidden runs in the moss that stay unfrozen longer than the surrounding plain. Their lives are fast, almost frantic, timed to insect swarms and the brief windows when the ground is firm enough to carry them without soaking.

### A resident that is not one body

If there is a “who” that belongs to this place more than any single animal, it is the curtain itself—the living circuit as a distributed organism.

Not intelligent in any human sense, not planning, not watching—yet it has behaviors that read as preference and response. It grows where the gradient is steep. It retreats where flow turns abrasive. It thickens along the routes that siphons make reliable, and it colonizes fresh fractures quickly, as if the moment stone is newly exposed it becomes available not just as surface but as opportunity.

Its shedding is part of its reproduction. When a sheet peels away and drifts into a vat, it doesn’t merely die. Chunks survive, seed other ledges, reattach in calmer eddies, and begin laying down their mineral scaffolding again. The place is constantly rearranging its own wiring.

The animals, in turn, have adapted to that distributed resident as if it were weather:

- insects feed on it and time their lives to its regrowth;
- newts use it as nursery shelter;
- fish use its peelings as drifting food and cover;
- birds use the noise and spray it helps generate as concealment.

So the basin is inhabited the way a machine is inhabited: not by a dominant beast at the center, but by many lives arranged around a repeating set of forces—water falling, rock cracking, charge gathering, mist condensing—each creature shaped to fit a moving niche.

Stand long enough and you can feel the difference between a place that merely contains life and a place that is, in its own slow, cyclical way, *alive*: not a single heart, but many small pulses nested inside one another—the siphon’s cough, the arc’s stitch of light, the sudden hatch of midges, the silent glide of an eel in black water—each answering the basin’s cold work with its own.
