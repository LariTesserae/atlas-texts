What lives there is not *in* the city so much as *as* the city, and the longer you stay the more that distinction becomes unusable.

At first you keep looking for bodies—anything with a center of mass and an obvious intention—because the layout reads as habitation: routes, thresholds, stacked dwellings, public basins, an edge that frays into hinterland. But the “residents” are not shaped like residents. They are shaped like solutions.

### The first inhabitants: the builders that never stop being built
The tallest fused columns—the tower-groves with the shingled bark—are not individual trees in any useful sense. They’re cooperatives, multiple trunks agreeing to share load and then forgetting where one ends. Their “skin” is a repeating pattern because repetition is how they remember: scales laid down in sequences that encode stress histories the way rings encode seasons. When a gust tunnels through an avenue and sets a colonnade singing, the nearest towers subtly adjust their cambium—thickening on the windward side, loosening on the lee—so the next gust will strike a different note.

These column-cooperatives are slow, but not passive. They are the city’s long-term institutions: archives, buttresses, territorial claims. They don’t move, so they negotiate by *being in the way*—by deciding where rigidity will exist tomorrow.

Around them, faster-growing vine-architectures behave like municipal workers who never go home. Road-mats are not laid once; they are continually re-laid, resurfaced, widened, narrowed, textured. The springiness underfoot is not cushioning—it’s attention. A road is a thickened response to repeated disturbance, and when you walk it you are literally feeding it pattern: compression, heat, vibration. In return the road keeps itself open not out of welcome but because an open channel is a reliable way to distribute strain through the grid.

The pods that stack like apartments are closer to *organs* than buildings. They inflate when humidity spikes, then slowly deflate as they bleed moisture into adjoining tissues. Some are storage. Some are pressure regulators. Some are deliberate weak points—sacrificial blisters meant to rupture so the larger structure doesn’t. When a facade “molts,” that’s not decay; it’s planned shedding, a way of clearing fungal load, salt buildup, or outdated geometry. The city maintains itself the way skin does, by constantly replacing itself.

### The second inhabitants: the hidden network that makes it one place
The pale fungal threads in the root-mat are not merely supporting cast. They are the city’s continuity.

Plants can grow fast, but they forget fast too: new tissue is optimistic, eager, structurally naive. The fungal lattice is older, cooler, and far more conservative. It braids through the peat and xylem like an accountant and a librarian at once—buffering nutrients, smoothing chemical spikes, and, most importantly here, carrying *signals* in ways that don’t require open air.

In a city with no animals, “news” doesn’t arrive on wings or feet. It arrives as gradients: sugar surpluses, hormone pulses, mineral deficits, tiny shifts in pH. The fungal web turns those into coherent, traveling messages. Where the towers record stress as bark pattern, the fungal layer records it as distribution: a slow rerouting of resource flows away from collapse-prone districts and into reinforcement zones, like a budget quietly rewritten at night.

You can feel this layer if you stand still long enough. The ground’s closing hiss after a footstep isn’t just elasticity; it’s the mat re-lacing itself, restoring conductivity—water, ions, information. It does not like gaps. Gaps are not “space,” they are broken circuits.

### The third inhabitants: the light-editors and sound-keepers
The leaves that “edit” sunlight are the city’s sensory surface, and they have had time to become sophisticated about it. Their translucence isn’t only about photosynthesis; it’s about *routing illumination* the way the road-mats route pressure.

In the brighter-than-expected corridors, the walls are functioning like light pipes—tissues tuned to guide certain wavelengths deeper while rejecting others, turning the urban interior into a layered greenhouse where different districts receive different spectra on purpose. Those drifting shadows that snap back are not supernatural trickery so much as moving shutters: overlapping leaf-planes shifting microseconds out of sync, creating the illusion of detached darkness the way a crowd can produce a wave with no single person being “the wave.”

And then there is the sound—because in this place sound is not an effect, it is an inhabitant.

The crackles, pops, groans, and braiding frictions are mechanical, yes, but they are also *organized*. Resonance travels farther and faster through wood and tensioned fiber than chemicals can diffuse. A change in pitch can be detected across blocks as a structural issue or a successful reinforcement. The city uses vibrational signatures the way a social species uses calls: not to express emotion, exactly, but to coordinate thresholds.

When the noise surges into that mass tuning-snap, it’s often a district coming into alignment—many fibers increasing tension until a collective release resets the geometry. The “rhythmic, municipal” periods are real shift-changes: phases when growth focuses on surfacing roads, then on thickening arches, then on sealing cavities, cycling like an organism alternating between digestion and exertion.

### The civic center: where the city is most like a self
In the basin with the shivering membrane, you are as close as you’ll get to meeting something that could be called a single being.

That membrane is not a floor. It is a tympanum, an amplifier, a mixing surface. Vibrations from terraces, colonnades, canals, and tower-groves converge here and are converted into a composite signal—an acoustic map of the city’s current tensions. The roof’s pulsing green-gold is not decorative illumination; it is feedback: a visible output of internal state, like blushing made architectural.

If the city has a “mind,” it is not located in a head. It is distributed across these coupled systems:

- **Road-mats and vine-ways** as peripheral nerves—fast, reactive, reshaping with contact.
- **Tower-groves** as bones and memory—slow, load-bearing, persistent.
- **Fungal braids** as deep brainstem—homeostatic, integrative, stubborn.
- **Light-editing canopies** as eyes—patterning input, controlling what counts as “day” in different neighborhoods.
- **The basin membrane** as an ear and a parliament—where the city listens hard enough to become coherent.

Standing there, the force on your chest is not merely volume. It is coupling: your own body’s vibrations being taken up, sampled, and folded into the city’s ongoing internal conversation. You are momentarily part of the circulatory system the way a pebble becomes part of a river’s turbulence.

### The outskirts: where the inhabitants become plural again
At the edge, where roads dissolve into braided mats and towers into knuckled groves, the city’s unity loosens. Not into wilderness exactly—there is no “outside nature” here—but into competing growth-forms.

Different clades assert themselves:

- **Sponge-thickets** that prioritize water capture, swelling whenever the air turns wet and starving everything around them of drip.
- **Ridge-lifters** that heave the young land upward by aggressive root expansion, creating new topography simply to create new stress to grow toward.
- **Curtain-epiphytes** that drape over structural vines, stealing light and forcing the host to thicken or reroute.
- **Valve-roots** that open and close with startling decisiveness, rationing flow as if water were a currency.

This is where the city “reproduces,” if that word applies: not by seeds you can see, not by dispersal on animals, but by *annexation*. A successful growth-form finds a persistent stress pattern—recurring condensation, a reliable shear in the wind, a contradiction in light—and elaborates it until it becomes a new knot, then a corridor, then a district. The outskirts are full of prototypes. The center is what survived.

### Who lives there, finally
No birds. No insects. No mammals. And yet: a dense population.

The inhabitants are architectures that metabolize, streets that learn your weight, reservoirs that behave like civic utilities, and a deep mesh that remembers long enough to make the whole basin feel like one preoccupied entity. The city’s “citizens” are not individuals you can point to; they are *roles*—reinforcers, weavers, storers, listeners—performed by tissues that can change their job mid-growth.

It isn’t empty. It’s just that the life has no faces, only functions—and it speaks not in voices but in tension, wet pop, creak, and the continual, disciplined thunder of shape being made and remade.
