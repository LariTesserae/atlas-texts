At first the only residents you can honestly *name* are the cowardly ones: the thin skins and dustings that live by keeping their heads down.

A smear of algae that never commits to being a mat because the next hour will sand it off again. Moss that grows in the only places the spray can reach but the turbulence can’t quite tear—dark felt in hairline cracks, as if the mountain has started to bruise and the bruise decided to photosynthesize. Pale, sharp grasses that don’t so much *grow* as practice being bent.

Life here begins as adhesion: anything that can hold on, does. Anything that can’t, becomes part of the water’s clarity.

But that accounting—plant, microbe, scrap of animal—is only the inventory at the edges. The thing that actually lives here lives in the middle, where nothing can stay put.

### The pocket-sea is inhabited by a motion
In calmer places you can point to a creature and say: *there, that one.* Here the resident is the behavior itself.

The spiral of bubbles you called “not an animal but a pattern” is the closest the pocket comes to showing you its body. Those spirals are not random aeration. They recur with preference. They form too neatly in the same few corridors of the cleft, tighten as if pulled by a hand, then vanish at a depth that isn’t a depth so much as an *elsewhere* in the rock.

If you watch long enough, you start to notice that the turbulence is not uniform. It has habits.

- A counter-rotation that always appears when the sun clears a particular spur, even if the wind is wrong for it.
- A thrum that rises whenever a crack on the east wall weeps meltwater.
- A smooth pause—heartbeat-flat—whenever you step too close with a regular cadence, as though something has lifted its head to listen.

It isn’t that the water has become sentient in any tidy, human way. It’s that the pocket-sea behaves like a single organ: all the violence coordinated, all the noise part of one ongoing internal conversation.

The mineral rind that forms and shatters along the contact line is its skin—skin it can’t keep, skin it keeps trying to grow anyway. The filaments of suspended sediment that briefly arrange into lattices are its nerves, sketching and erasing the same diagrams like a thought that can’t be finished before it is interrupted.

It feeds on difference: on temperature gradients, on the thinness of the air, on the rock’s odd attraction, on the wind that sometimes turns the entire cleft into an instrument. And it feeds—more unnervingly—on *rhythm*.

That “memory for motion” isn’t a metaphor to the pocket. It is appetite and defense in the same mechanism. A repeated pattern in its boundary—three sharp taps, a steady chant, metal struck against stone—becomes a handle it can grip. It catches the cadence the way a whirlpool catches a twig, and then it uses it to pull harder.

If you wanted to be superstitious you’d say it learns. If you wanted to be scientific you’d say it entrains. In practice the difference doesn’t matter: the pocket responds like something that remembers being harmed.

### The small creatures are either scouts or shed cells
The quick silver fish-shape is real, but it feels more like an argument than an organism—an answer thrown back at the question of the place.

It never swims in the way fish swim in lakes. It *spends* itself through the turbulence: held still by a fin-flick against a torrent, then released and flung, then somehow not dashed to paste against rock. When it wants to cross the pocket it doesn’t travel through open water; it rides the seams between vortices, slipping along the quietest lines in the chaos as if it can see the flow the way you see a path.

It appears singly because a school would be a pattern, and patterns are dangerous here. Even the pocket seems to dislike the idea of many small bodies moving together; the moment two silver shapes share a route, the water roughens as if offended by the symmetry.

The thumb-sized bell-bodied drifter is even stranger. It doesn’t pulse like a jellyfish. It *waits*—a translucent comma suspended in violence—and then snaps away in a direction that makes no sense unless it is reacting to pressure changes you can’t feel. It behaves less like prey and more like a sensor: a living barometer that the pocket uses to taste its own shifts.

When one of these bells dies, it doesn’t wash up. It simply stops being where it was, drawn into the same fold in the cleft that makes the bottom lie. If you pry at the geology and imagine a hidden channel, you can tell yourself it was carried off. If you stay longer, you begin to suspect the pocket reclaims its own instruments.

Even the algae patches, constantly erased and recolonized, read like this: not a stable community but a recurring film, like the lining of a throat that heals fast because it is always being abraded.

### There is a watcher that isn’t made of flesh
Sometimes—especially when wind blows across the mouth of the cleft and the water begins to sing in that bottle-wail—the turbulence aligns into a brief, unsettling coherence.

Not stillness. Coherence.

The visible eddies stop looking like random folds and start looking like *attention* turned in a direction. The clarity becomes a lens you can feel on your skin, as though the pocket has found an angle that makes you easier to see.

In those moments, the “emptiness” you sensed in the water stops feeling like lack and starts feeling like deliberate clearing: a chamber kept swept so that any intrusion is obvious.

If the pocket has a mind, it is distributed. It is not behind a pair of eyes; it is in the whole circulating volume. Its thoughts are vortices forming and tearing apart. Its moods are surges. Its voice is resonance. The deep thrumming you feel in your teeth is not just acoustics—it is the pocket’s way of holding itself together, humming its own boundaries into being.

It is young in the same way the stone is young: not naive, but raw. It hasn’t learned the softened behavior of old water that has been in the same basin long enough to develop manners. This one is newly anchored, newly constrained, and constantly testing its constraints.

You could call it a spirit if you need the comfort of a category. But it acts more like an emergent creature made of physics—an organism whose tissues are flow, whose bones are the magnet-listening rock, whose blood is sky-water stolen and kept upright.

### The human inhabitants are not settlers; they are attendants
The ropes and anchors and slate kneeling-platform make sense once you accept that nobody comes here to live *in* the pocket. They come to live beside it, briefly, in the way you live beside a fire: close enough to be warmed or burned, never close enough to forget what it is.

The people who return—because the repeat-placed anchors imply return—are a kind of listener. They do not bring metal tools unless they wrap them. They do not speak in strings of even syllables. They do not walk with a steady gait near the mouth of the cleft. Their knowledge is practical but also ritualized, because in a place where rhythm becomes fuel, superstition is just safety that learned to sing.

They build “uninteresting” structures for a reason: any regularity is an invitation. Anything too engineered becomes a melody the pocket can catch and amplify. So their shelter is lopsided on purpose. Their knots are varied. Their tallies on the rock are not decoration but data—records of when the pocket surged outward, when it reversed, when it entered that unnerving heartbeat-smooth pause.

What do they do here?

They bargain at the edges.

- They collect drip-water not because they lack water, but because the drip is *not the pocket*—it is unclaimed, unlistening, safe to swallow.
- They take mineral crust shards and salt-dust the way other people take ash from a sacred fire: proof of contact, a material that has been in the pocket’s skin.
- They leave offerings that do not clang or chant: a twist of fiber, a smear of fat, a pinch of ground shell—things that dissolve without pattern.

If you ever met one of them down in ordinary valleys, you might think them simply weathered climbers, or shamans, or obsessive geologists. Up here, beside the cleft, you understand they are something else: attendants of a newborn rule in the world, people who have decided that a dangerous anomaly is worth learning the etiquette of.

They do not worship the pocket-sea as benevolent. They treat it the way you treat a powerful animal that has not yet decided what you are: with respect that contains calculation, and caution that contains curiosity.

### What “lives there,” in the end
If you insist on one answer, it is this:

A young thing made of water and constraint lives there—a turbulence-organism anchored to raw stone, feeding on gradients and sound, defending itself by remembering rhythm. Around it cling the first colonists: algae, moss, a few razor grasses, and a handful of small creatures adapted either to exploit its violence or to serve as its sensing organs. And skirting all of it are rare humans who have learned that the only way to survive in the pocket’s presence is to become irregular, quiet, and forgettable.

The pocket-sea is not empty. It is *occupied*—by a living motion that is still deciding what kinds of life it will allow to accumulate without tearing them back into water.
