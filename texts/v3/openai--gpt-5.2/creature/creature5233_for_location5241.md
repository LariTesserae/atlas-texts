What lives there is, at first, easy to mistake for what *doesn’t*.

From a visitor’s pace—eyes forward, collar up—the city seems inhabited mostly by wet stone: terraces darkening and paling, fog nesting in stairwells, the patient stitching of channels and cistern mouths. The life you expect to announce itself with noise does not. Doors don’t bang; markets don’t shout. Even animals, when they appear, seem to be practicing quiet as a local custom.

But if you stop long enough for your hearing to adjust, you realize the city is full of residents who have made a home inside the same rules as the water: don’t resist, redirect; don’t hurry, persist; don’t shine, endure.

### The first citizens: films, crusts, and the soft takeover of surfaces
The oldest and most widespread inhabitants are not people.

Mosses live here like a second masonry—low, tight mats that choose corners the sun can’t keep. They don’t carpet so much as *seal*: a green-black edging that holds grit in place, that drinks the drip before it splashes, that turns a hairline crack into a nursery for more life. Lichens write slowly across facades, pale as ash or sulfur, their maps expanding season by season until a wall looks dusted with frost even in midsummer.

Algae claim the shaded runnels, where water always passes but never rinses hard. In those places the stone is slick as an old spoon, and the city’s stair-cutters have learned to score steps with shallow texture—safe paths worn into the building itself, renewed when the green becomes too confident.

Salt and mineral blooms live here, too—white lace that appears overnight where damp has evaporated. People read those blooms the way lowland folk read clouds: a warning, a promise, a hint about what is happening inside the walls. A sudden flowering of salt on an interior corridor means a seep has found a new route. A darkening stripe on an exterior buttress means the rock has begun to weep there again.

This is the city’s baseline ecology: a thin skin of patient organisms that treat stone not as dead matter but as a long-term landscape, and treat water as a courier that never stops coming.

### Small animals that have learned to be brief
Where there is constant damp without standing water, you don’t get the obvious creatures of ponds and rivers. You get specialists.

Under flat stones and behind the fitted lids of cistern mouths live beetles that prefer cold and dim, their bodies matte as charcoal. Pale, slow things inhabit the hollows where rooms have been carved into cliff—soft-bodied scavengers that never need to outrun anything because there is so little to chase them. Near the marsh-edge, where sedge and stone interlock, there are quick mammals that show themselves as a moving V in the grass and then are gone: not a population that dominates, but one that passes through the city’s outskirts the way wind passes through a colonnade.

The birds are fewer than you’d expect and more consequential. A high, solitary glider might be a scavenger, a messenger, or simply an animal using the plateau’s lift. But when it crosses the city’s sky, people notice. Rooftops have perches that look like decoration until you see the thin traces of use: a smear, a feather, a dropped bone. Some quarters keep small, dark birds in stone-walled lofts—not for song (there isn’t much), but for dung to feed moss beds, for feathers, for the simple reassurance of a living thing that returns.

And there are the creatures you only infer: the faint tracks in a film of condensation on a parapet at dawn; the sudden absence of midges above a wet stairwell because something invisible has been eating them; the way certain alleys always smell faintly of musk though no animal is ever seen there.

Nothing here expands into abundance. Life conserves itself. It lives narrow and deep.

### People shaped by condensation and weight
Then there are the humans—numerous, distributed, and at first strangely hard to spot because their city does not force them into streets at the same time.

They move like maintenance rather than commerce: one person with a shoulder yoke carrying two lidded jars; two elders balancing a long stone slate on a padded frame; a trio in felted cloaks pausing at a gutter to clear a leafless clump of wind-blown grass. Their clothing is not bright. Dyes don’t hold well in constant damp, and anything that tries to be vivid becomes a liability—mud-stained, algae-marked, or simply out of place in a world of slate and bone-color stone. So they dress in grays, deep browns, the green-black of boiled wool. Their fabrics are thick and forgiving. Their shoes are built for slickness: soft soles that grip, replaceable wraps, resin-treated stitching.

They smell, faintly, of wet mineral, smoke that never fully dries out of cloth, and the sharp clean note of certain mosses used as lining. Some wear cords of braided reed or sedge at the wrist—practical ties that can become handles, seals, or emergency lashings. Hair is often kept short or braided close; loose hair drinks fog and drips cold down the neck.

Their faces tend to be weather-sanded rather than weather-burned: not cracked from heat, but steadied by chill. They squint less than lowland folk, because glare is rare; instead they look with a kind of patient focus, as if trained by fog to trust outlines and movement more than detail.

### The city’s true occupation: tending flow
Most people here, regardless of what they call their work, are employed by water.

There are channel-keepers who walk assigned routes at dawn, listening with the back of their minds for wrong sounds: a drip that has become a trickle, a trickle that has become silence. They carry thin rods for clearing silt, a palm-sized stone hammer for tapping—listening to the note of a wall to tell if it’s hollowed by seep.

There are cistern-masters who know the taste of stored fog-water versus stored rainwater, who can tell by smell if a covered run has begun to grow the wrong kind of slick life. In some districts, the cistern lids are carved with marks—simple, deep lines—showing which households draw from which storage, and on what days, and how much must be left for the public basins.

There are stone-fitters whose status is not measured by monuments but by *repairs that don’t look like repairs*. Their pride is a terrace that stopped slumping ten years ago and has not needed mention since. They work with wedges, levers, rollers, and an expertise in weight that functions like an invisible machine. Children learn early to read slope the way others learn arithmetic: where water will go, where it will pause, where it will undermine.

There are algae-scrapers and moss-cutters—less prestigious perhaps, but essential. They harvest certain mats for insulation, for packing jar lids, for wound dressing. They know which green is safe and which is the beginning of rot inside a wall.

And because the city has no engines, transport is a choreography of bodies and gravity. Goods move on sledges that slide easily on damp stone. Barrels are rolled along shallow-grooved ramps designed centuries ago for that purpose. Staircases have side channels not only for water but for ropes—haul lines anchored to stone horns polished smooth by hands.

### Food that doesn’t pretend the climate is different
What they eat is what can tolerate a cool, wet world without trees.

There are hardy grains grown where the plateau gives a rare broad shelf of soil—short-stemmed, heavy-headed, harvested quickly between rains. There are tubers and roots from the marsh-edge, tough-skinned and bland until smoked. There are reed seeds, ground into meal. There are mushrooms cultivated in carved chambers where the air is always damp and the temperature barely shifts; some are pale and meaty, some thin and bitter and used like medicine.

Protein comes in small, reliable forms: kept birds, traded dried fish from lower, rivered lands, occasional meat from animals trapped at the margins, and a steady reliance on legumes when they can be made to grow. Oils are precious. Smoke is common. Fermentation is an everyday technology: crocks in cool corners, stone-lidded jars in alcoves where dripwater keeps the air from drying out.

The city has scents of food, but muted—more smoke and sour than spice. A cooking quarter is recognizable not by aroma billowing out, but by the slight warmth in the fog near certain vents, and the thin line of soot above a stone chimney.

### A culture that listens
Because sound is subdued, people here become careful with it.

They speak without wasting breath; not whispering exactly, but keeping syllables dense. In some neighborhoods, conversation happens at thresholds where the fog is less thick, because inside certain courtyards voices seem to vanish into stone and damp and come back wrong.

They have signals designed for a muffled world: knocks on specific stones that carry vibration; water-marks scratched beside doors; hanging bundles of reed that move even when the wind is too gentle to be felt. In fog, a person might trail a hand along the wall not only for guidance but for news—feeling whether the surface is newly damp (a fresh seep), newly dry (a cleared channel), newly slick (algae blooming).

Timekeeping here is often domestic and hydrologic. People notice when the first evening condensation darkens the parapets; when the drips start in a certain order; when a known weeping seam begins earlier than usual. There are public rituals that look, from outside, like superstition but function as inspection: a seasonal walk along a ridge wall, everyone touching the stones, everyone looking for changes.

### The unseen residents: vacancy, memory, and the city’s long breath
The city contains emptiness like a necessary organ.

There are sealed doors that no one opens because the rooms behind them are too wet now, or too unstable, or simply no longer needed. Those spaces become habitats for the quiet animals and the slow plants. A corridor abandoned to seep becomes a moss farm. A courtyard nobody visits becomes a sediment trap that keeps silt from choking the lower channels.

And then there is the sense—hard to name without making it mystical—that the city itself behaves like a living thing that has learned to coexist with its environment instead of trying to conquer it. Not because it thinks, but because generations have trained it through form: every slope an instruction, every terrace a compromise, every repaired buttress a memory of near-failure made permanent.

If there are beliefs here, they are less about gods than about *process*. Water is not worshiped as a personality; it is respected as a law. Stone is not romanticized; it is trusted and monitored like an elder who can be depended upon but must be checked on.

### Who, finally, lives there
So: what lives there is a layered citizenry.

- A skin of lichens, algae, mosses, and mineral blooms that claim the damp and convert it into slow growth.
- Small, economical animals that exist as traces and quick motions, plus a few birds whose rare crossings matter.
- People who have built an urban life around gravity and seepage—engineers in wool and stone-dust, gardeners of fungus and moss, readers of stains and drips, carriers and fitters and keepers of flow.
- And a city-organism made of terraces and channels that does what it was taught to do: receive water, divide it, store it, and let it go—day after cool day—without ever pretending the world will become dry.
