What lives there at first seems like the answer you already have: not a population, but a practice.

The terrace is inhabited the way a ship under slow construction is inhabited—by the few people who know where the tools are kept, which sounds mean danger, and how to coax a half-finished thing through weather without pretending it’s finished. They don’t “occupy” buildings so much as they keep certain roofs from becoming rot, certain stairways from becoming ramps, certain walls from becoming rubble before the mountain has even decided whether it will permit walls at all.

You meet them indirectly. A broom’s scrape that never becomes footsteps. A pulley’s complaint that stops the moment you look up. A mallet striking stone with the unhurried cadence of someone who has learned that impatience here is the quickest way to break a chisel—or to wake the wrong seam in the rock.

Then, slowly, you begin to see them.

They’re not many, and they don’t move like townsfolk. They move like surveyors in a place that keeps revising its measurements. Most are dressed for wet rather than cold: layered plant-fiber and wool kept waxed with resin, hoods that shed mist, wraps that can be retied into knee-high gaiters when the steps film over. Their hands are the first thing you notice—broad, nicked, always busy—because hands matter more than faces in a city where nothing stays true long enough for ornament to mean much.

They carry small, specific objects:

- charcoal sticks and flat slates for marking “still warm” stone, for leaving quick warnings that will smear in rain but hold long enough to matter;  
- little wedges of wood already shaved to standard angles, carried the way other people carry coins;  
- short lengths of reed cord and lashings kept in damp pouches so they won’t dry and snap;  
- a knuckle-sized stone hammer with a handle wrapped in resin-soaked cloth, because bare wood swells and loosens in the constant wet.

They don’t build in straight lines if they can avoid it. That isn’t an aesthetic; it’s caution. Curves forgive settling. Arcs distribute argument. Their scaffolds are ribby and over-braced, not because they expect storms, but because they expect the ground to make up its mind halfway through the week.

If you try to name them—builders, masons, gardeners—you keep being wrong by a few degrees. Their work is always half stonework and half waterwork. They do not only cut channels; they *teach* water what to be. They roughen a slab so damp will climb it. They set porous stones like wicks where moisture should gather. They plant moss not as decoration but as infrastructure: a living gasket in joints that can’t afford mortar, a soft ruler that reveals motion by the way it tears.

The city’s “agriculture” is theirs, too, and it makes the place feel less empty once you understand what you’re looking at. Those terrace beds and troughs are not mere greenery; they’re pantries, ropeworks, pharmacies, and test instruments. The people who live here are always tending something that isn’t food *until it is*: fungi grown on soaked bark in shaded alleys; reed patches for basketry and lashings; thick-leaved plants whose sap seals clay pipe joints; mosses chosen for how they drink and how they die.

Their households are hard to spot because they avoid committing to a single building. Rooms are used like tools: a dryish cell for storing grain brought up from lower valleys, a warmer alcove near a vent fissure for raising mushrooms, a high platform under an unfinished arch for sleeping where the mist thins. A “home” is a route through the terrace—a sequence of places that are good *this season*.

They also live by intervals. That’s the other trick. The terrace is designed for gatherings because gatherings really do happen—just not in the way a market happens. Twice a year, sometimes once, sometimes three times if the rock has been particularly argumentative, groups arrive from elsewhere: crews who know the old ring of towers by inherited muscle memory, who come up with baskets of kiln-fired pipe sections, with dull metal hinges, with fresh timber. They work fast and communally, as if racing the mountain’s patience, and then they leave again, as if staying too long would tempt the place to count them as permanent weight.

The few who remain year-round are stewards more than citizens. Their authority—if it can be called that—is practical. It belongs to whoever remembers which plaza slab rose a finger-width last winter, which grate coughs mist when stepped on, which parapet’s plantings are a sign that water is collecting where it must not. They speak to each other in gestures as much as in words, partly because the roar makes conversation a shared strain, and partly because habit has taught them that what you *point at* here matters more than what you *say*.

And then there are the other residents—the ones the stewards never pretend to own.

The plants, first: not a wild lushness, but an opportunistic one, as if the mountain’s youth produces surfaces that invite colonization. Ferns here aren’t just ferns; they’re seam-finders. Moss spreads in patterns that reveal the direction of “uphill” damp, mapping invisible flows better than any chalk line. Vines try cracks the way a hand tries door handles. Small trees grow twisted not from wind but from periodic re-leveling: roots adapting to beds that subtly tilt, trunks correcting for a ground that won’t stay agreed upon.

Insects exist, but sparsely, like the first draft of an ecosystem. What there is tends to be small and specialized: pale-bodied gnats that rise in brief clouds from the warmest fissures; beetles that live under wet stone where fungi have begun; tight-winged moths that treat the terrace beds like islands in a fog sea. You don’t hear a summer chorus; you hear individual ticks and buzzes as distinct as single tools being set down.

The amphibians—if that’s what they are—are more certain. In the wet shadows where the stone sweats, there are slow, soft-bodied things the color of unglazed clay, with skins that shine not with slime but with perpetual condensation. Some have toes; some have none. They move like a thought changing its mind: a brief shift, then stillness, then a retreat into a crack that you could swear wasn’t there until they used it. The stewards don’t hunt them. They sometimes relocate them with a flat piece of bark, gently, the way you move a useful spider out of a bathhouse. It’s not affection exactly; it’s recognition that these creatures are part of how damp is managed here. They eat what would otherwise become infestation. They belong to the terrace as much as the grates do.

Above, the birds are not city birds. They are cliff birds: solitary, dark, making use of updrafts and vanishing into cloud as if cloud were a doorway. When one lands—rarely—it chooses unfinished stone, not trees. It stands for a moment as if listening, then lifts away again, unwilling to be pinned to any surface that might shift under its feet.

Below, in the voids that swallow the channel water, something else lives—not a single monster, not a clear myth made flesh, but a layered presence. The roar itself has biology in it, or at least something that feels biological: the sense of steady exhalation, the rhythm beneath the constant noise. In the hollows under the terrace there are slick walls beaded with mineral blooms, and mats of fungus that do not need sunlight. If you follow a grate’s throat with a lamp, you sometimes catch a faint shimmer—tiny cave-dwellers, blind and white, shrimp-like or worm-like, retreating into film-thin currents. The stewards know these things exist the way sailors know barnacles exist: not as companions, but as the inevitable life that claims any surface where water and time cooperate.

And there is one more kind of resident, harder to describe because it isn’t exactly “creature” and yet it behaves like one: the mountain-in-the-act-of-being-born.

People here talk about stone as if it had moods, but not in a superstitious, theatrical way. More like a craftsperson talks about wood grain: with respect for its tendencies. Certain seams are “restless.” Certain blocks are “green,” meaning not just new-cut but still deciding how to bear weight. The stewards test rock by sound—tapping and listening for the dull note that means a cavity, the bright ring that means tension. They leave offerings sometimes, but the offerings are practical: a bowl set under a weeping face, a carved channel to keep a seep from undermining a stair, a ring of moss planted to stabilize a joint. Their reverence looks like maintenance.

When water runs uphill, they do not gape at it. They adjust their work to it, as if the place has its own local gravity and you either learn it or you lose your cisterns. Children—there are a few, though you might not notice them at first—grow up thinking of damp as a direction rather than a consequence. They learn early which stones collect moisture on the “wrong” side, and they can trace invisible flows with a fingertip the way other children trace rivers on maps.

So what lives there is, finally, a small, patient human craft-culture nested inside a very young landscape—stewards and seasonal crews who treat architecture as a negotiation with geology; plants cultivated as tools; sparse insects and quiet amphibians that thrive in permanent mist; and a whole underside of pale, water-fed life in the terrace’s hollows.

And above and below all of it, the most populous inhabitant of the place is its unfinishedness: a mountain still making itself, loud with its own breath, letting a city exist on its skin only as long as the city remains willing to move with it.
