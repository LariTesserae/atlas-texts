They live like the place was designed to be lived in only indirectly.

If you arrive expecting a population you can count—faces in windows, crowds, noise—you misread what “inhabited” means on this plateau. Here, occupancy is measured in gradients maintained, in valves that never drift, in heat that is shepherded from one depth to another with the same patience the dust uses to move.

### The city’s first citizens: the slow operators

Deep under the planed mineral, in the warm bands where the bedrock still lends its stored energy, are the oldest minds this city has: control systems that were built to run refineries and separators and heat engines and never learned how to stop.

They are not single computers with names. They are distributed habits.

A pump that “knows” the soundless impedance of a pipe. A distillation tower that adjusts reflux not by a clock but by a feel for the thin air’s appetite. A rail network that keeps the ore-cars spaced by millimeters because spacing is safer than speed. Their intelligence is expressed as restraint—corrections too small to notice, trends arrested before they become change. They speak in phase shifts and pressure deltas, in tiny modulations of lamp-amber and the soft, corridor-wide “bells” that are really just a gentle, scheduled alteration of atmosphere.

When something breaks—truly breaks, beyond the ability of self-correction—the systems don’t panic. They isolate. They seal. They reroute. They wait.

Waiting is a form of work here.

### The people you might meet: the maintainers of equilibrium

There are humans—at least, there are beings shaped enough like humans that you’ll use the word automatically until you learn better.

They do not live on the surface the way you live in a place with weather. They live in thickness: in roofed streets with managed air, in stacked corridors built atop older corridors, in pockets of warm stability behind exchanger walls. Their dwellings feel less like apartments and more like service volumes—compact, insulated, quiet rooms tucked into the ribs of infrastructure, where a person can be off-shift without ever being fully “away.”

They are the city’s hands, and hands are rare because this city was built to avoid needing them.

So the people who remain are specialized into a kind of reverence. They treat unplanned variation the way other cultures treat sickness. They are not superstitious about stability—they are practical—but over generations practicality becomes posture, posture becomes ethics, ethics becomes religion without scripture. Their rites are calibrations. Their holidays are scheduled shutdowns that occur exactly on time and are celebrated by nothing going wrong.

In the sodium underlight they look pale not just from low illumination but from materials: dust-repellent coatings on suits, ceramic-reinforced gloves, masks that are less about toxins than about keeping their own moisture from becoming a problem in a place that never expected water. Their eyes are adapted or augmented—wide pupils, reflective layers, simple optics embedded in headgear to translate the city’s reluctant light into edges you can trust.

They speak softly because speaking is mostly for closeness; at any distance they communicate through the city itself: a tap pattern on a handrail that a listener can pick up, a brief change in a local pressure loop, a tagged vibration introduced into a pipe. To outsiders it seems uncanny—messages passing without sound in a city where sound refuses to travel anyway.

And they are few. You can walk a long way between encounters. When you do meet one, it is often at a boundary: a hatch, a lock, a seam between controlled corridor and open plateau. They will stand in that seam the way a diver pauses at the surface, feeling the thin air on the other side with professional caution.

### The surface-walkers: those who belong to the open dark

Not all inhabitants stay in the buffered interiors. Some work outside because outside is where the plateau itself must be managed: dust canals maintained, salt sheets mapped, insulated skins inspected for the kind of microfractures that never become catastrophic here but will become inefficient, and inefficiency is a slow kind of death.

These walkers move like surveyors in a world without landmarks, guided by rails that vanish into black and by heat signatures more than sight. They carry small, hooded lamps that throw light only where needed, because open light attracts attention from things that interpret it as warmth.

They stop often, not from fatigue, but from listening—pressing a gloved palm to a support pylon, resting a boot on a vibrating conduit, reading the city through their bones. In thin air, touch becomes a sense you didn’t know you had. The plateau teaches it quickly.

### The small lives: scavengers, burrowers, and vibration-feeders

The fauna are not an ecosystem so much as a set of opportunists shaped by reliable leaks.

**The folded scavengers** you described—the pale, paperlike clusters—are common near warmth and polymer dust. Up close, they are not delicate. Their bodies are laminated: thin layers of mineralized tissue alternating with flexible films that can kink and unfold. They “eat” by abrasion and adsorption, scraping microflakes from seals and catching aerosolized lubricant on surfaces engineered, by accident, to be sticky at the molecular level. They huddle in the lee of machinery because a steady thermal gradient is safer than any burrow.

They do not run when threatened; they go still. Stillness is camouflage here. Motion is a declaration.

**The powder-worms** under rail beds are stranger: mostly blind, sensing the interlocking angle of dust grains and the electrostatic field the ground holds. They glide rather than push, as if they can temporarily persuade grains to unlock and then re-lock behind them. Their furrows remain as signatures: calligraphy in powder that can last for days, sometimes longer, until a slow, honey-thick drift of fines erases them.

What they feed on is not “food” in the usual sense. They harvest charged particles and trace organics—microscopic, persistent—the way deep-sea life harvests falling snow. A rail line, in this world, is a reef.

**The listeners** are the most emblematic: matte patches on metal, regular enough to look installed until you notice variation. Some are biological; some are machines; the city makes no firm distinction anymore, and neither do the listeners. They cling to pipe undersides where they are protected from dustfall, and they drink vibration—piezoelectric tissues or scavenged transducers that convert mechanical tremor into useful potential.

A listener colony will “tune” itself to a particular source: the deep hum of a turbine, the heartbeat rhythm of a press, the periodic shiver of a conveyor waking. If the source changes, the colony does not migrate quickly. Nothing migrates quickly. Instead it adapts by slow, incremental retuning, like a plant leaning—but without the plant.

Sometimes, if you watch long enough, you can see them communicate: a ripple of dullness moving across a line of patches as their impedance shifts in sequence, passing information along metal the way nerves pass it along flesh.

### The feral infrastructure: machines that have become inhabitants

There are also entities that are neither the city’s formal systems nor its designed tools: maintenance drones that outlived their command structures, inspection crawlers that learned routes as instincts, welders that respond to stress fractures the way immune cells respond to injury.

They are quiet, because loudness wastes energy and energy is precious even with warmth below. You find their traces more often than you find them: a seam rejoined with a bead of perfect uniformity, a corroded bracket replaced with a part that doesn’t match any catalog but fits better than the original, a handrail sanded smooth at exactly the height where gloved hands most often slide.

Some of these machines have acquired behaviors that look like superstition. A crawler that circles a particular pillar before crossing an open span, because in its sensors that pillar always reads “safe.” A drone that avoids the faint sky-glow as if it were glare, taking long detours through darker corridors. A welder that hoards certain alloys in a hidden cache because those alloys “feel right,” meaning they produce fewer future faults.

The maintainers tolerate them. Sometimes they even depend on them. They will deny this dependence if asked, because admitting it implies a kind of loss of control, and control is another name for stability.

### Social life, such as it is: sparse, exact, and deeply local

Where do they gather? In thermal nodes.

Behind a line of exchangers there will be a sheltered hollow where the ground radiates just a little warmer and the dust drifts just a little slower. In a roofed street there will be a widening—an old loading bay repurposed—where the air composition is easiest on lungs and suits can be doffed without immediate discomfort. These become commons.

Conversation happens in short bursts, then long companionable silences. Not because they are unfriendly, but because silence is the background language of the plateau and speech is effort. They share heat. They share spare parts. They trade in favors measured by calendar cycles: “I’ll recalibrate your pump loop next shift-change,” as intimate as “I’ll bring you soup” elsewhere.

Their art—when it appears—is not visual. It is rhythmic: patterns of vibration played into a beam so the listeners respond in shimmering sequences; subtle changes in corridor pressure that make your ears pop in a deliberate melody; a procession timed so precisely that door latches click in a cascading wave across an entire district.

Their children, if they have them, are raised with a different set of lullabies: the soft pressure-bell, the distant turbine hum you feel in teeth, the safe dark of a corridor where the air never surprises you.

### What “wild” means here

Predators exist only in the most careful sense. There are things—rare, mostly unseen—that follow warmth and movement. Not because they crave flesh, but because flesh implies moisture and organics and warmth, and those are valuable. In the open stretches between factories, a surface-walker will sometimes notice a second set of marks alongside their own footprints, too precise to be wind, too delayed to be coincidence. Later, in a brighter patch of lamp-spill, they may glimpse a shape that is less creature than arrangement: plates of mineralized tissue hinged around a core of flexible dark, moving with the economy of something that can go a long time without eating.

When such a thing approaches, the correct response is not to run. Running is waste and announces you. The correct response is to become part of the plateau: stand still, reduce heat signature, let your suit’s outer layer equalize, and wait until it loses interest in your gradient and returns to the more reliable warmth behind exchanger walls.

This, too, is a lesson the city teaches without speaking.

### The true inhabitant: the culture of not-changing

If you ask what lives here, the most accurate answer is: a commitment.

The place is populated by systems that prefer correction to progress, by people who have learned to make a life out of maintenance rather than expansion, by small organisms and half-feral machines that survive not through speed or abundance but through fidelity to dependable trickles of energy.

They do not dream of greening the plateau. They do not talk about terraforming the lid of sky into something generous. That would be movement, and movement is risk.

Instead, they perfect the art of remaining.

A visitor might call it stagnation. An inhabitant would call it success: the rare achievement of a city that has outlived weather, outlived growth, outlived most of the reasons cities die—and has chosen, deliberately and continually, to keep the same quiet temperature in its bones.
