What lives here is mostly not *in* the architecture but *of* it—organisms whose bodies have become the place’s notion of “ground,” and a long-cultivated menagerie that treats those bodies the way land animals treat soil, reef, cliff, and forest all at once.

### The first residents: the Weave

The load-mats, spines, and canopies aren’t separate species so much as one continuous habit of life: a consortium that has forgotten the difference between individual and terrain.

Up close, the “stone” resolves into layers—tough, lignified sheets stitched with living fiber. The uppermost skin is a patient velvet of microfilaments that reads flow the way grasses read wind. Beneath it is a structural laminate: cords as thick as your arm braided into ribs, ribs fused into panels, panels grown together at seams that never quite stop being wounds. In cross-section the mats are full of slow plumbing—channels that move brine, sugars, minerals, spores, and chemical messages with the deliberateness of glacier melt.

The Weave doesn’t *plan* in any human sense. It feels.

- Where current scours, it armors: extra lignin, extra calcified co-skeleton, tougher surface films.
- Where a span flexes, it thickens the ribs and redistributes load the way a tree makes reaction wood.
- Where corridors are meant to stay clear, it exudes inhibitory chemistry—not poison so much as discouragement, a pervasive “not here” that pushes sprouting tissue back toward buttress and pillar.

Its intelligence is architectural: stress maps, vibration maps, pressure gradients remembered as growth rings. If you could watch it on the right timescale, you’d see it correcting itself constantly—micro-failures absorbed, creases healed, popped calcification replaced by a fresh seam that glows faintly as it knits.

In places the Weave is old enough to have moods: broad districts that “prefer” certain flows, that keep corridors clean not just through chemistry but through the way their canopies are tensioned, how they breathe the water and make the water behave.

### The seam-cities: where the small life concentrates

Every seam is a border and a banquet.

The faint blue outline comes from films—chemosynthetic microbes and cooperative algae-analogs living on the chemical gradient that exists wherever two growth-plates meet. They are the true sunlight of this world: energy that doesn’t arrive from above, but from difference—between tissues, between pressures, between what leaks and what is hungry.

On those films live the first grazers:

- **Raspers**: flat, slate-colored bodies with toothy belts that move like conveyor tracks. They skim the mats in slow, tireless circuits, leaving pale trails that fade as the biofilm regrows. Their mouths are also their feet; their feeding is their locomotion.
- **Knock-shells**: hard-shelled crustacean analogs that make the percussive tapping you hear. They don’t just eat; they *test*—tapping for hollow spots where a seam is healing, prying up flakes of calcified skin to get at the softer, richer layer beneath. They are tolerated because the Weave benefits from their pruning. Overgrazing triggers a chemical “shiver” in the mat—an irritant pulse that sends them elsewhere.

Among the grazers are the cleaners and the thieves: threadlike worms that steal dissolved nutrients directly from living tissue, and tiny shrimp-swarms that harvest the grazers’ waste, turning it into a fine, sinking snow.

And threaded through all of it are the seam’s little guardians: small, almost transparent hunters that can’t afford speed, only timing. They sit under lips and folds, sensing vibration through the structure. When a grazer’s rasping rhythm falters, they strike—less a chase than a sudden closing of distance.

### The corridor-fauna: citizens of the open dark

The open water “streets” are not empty. They’re *disciplined*.

There are **lane-schools**: fishlike bodies with lateral fins tuned to the corridor’s stable flow. They travel in narrow bands where the current is smoothest, holding station by making tiny corrections, never wasting energy on drama. Their eyes are reduced or shuttered; instead, their bodies are latticed with pressure sensors. They read the city by its noise—knowing where they are by which chord the corridor hums.

Some of these schools are not prey so much as maintenance crews. They feed on suspended detritus and on the drifting microbial floc that would otherwise collect in eddies. They keep the corridors clear simply by existing in the right numbers.

Above them drift the **frill-mantas** you sensed in the higher vaults: huge, slow filterers with fluted mouths and comb-like gill arrays. They don’t swim; they *position*. Their bodies are shaped like listening dishes, using the corridor’s resonance to find the densest fall of nutrient snow. When they pass, the water’s sound changes—overtones dampened, the roar briefly softened—as if a thick curtain moved across a cathedral organ.

Between lanes move **lantern drifters**: gelatinous forms carrying cold light in internal sacs. They’re not bright enough to reveal the architecture; they’re bright enough to advertise to the right partners. Their glow pulses in patterns that match the corridor’s harmonics, as if they’re trying to be heard in light the way everything else is heard in sound.

### The pillar-communities: vertical neighborhoods

Each spine—kilometers apart, but unavoidable once you find one—is an axis of attachment.

Along the pillar’s shaded side grow **filter-fans**: frondlike animals or animal-plant hybrids that open and close with the current’s breathing, sifting particulates and spores. On the faster side cling **calcifiers** that turn dissolved minerals into knobby armor, adding stiffness to the spine in exchange for a constant flow of nutrients.

There are also **hinge-creatures** that live only where the pillar meets canopy: thick-bodied things with gripping tendrils, built to ride motion. They eat the fresh seam tissue that appears whenever the canopy adjusts its tension. Their populations rise and fall with micro-repairs: a living indicator of stress you can’t see.

And high up, where the water is quieter, you sometimes find the **float-nests**—slow colonies that bud off the canopy and hang like heavy curtains. They’re nursery-grounds: their dense fibers trap drifting young of many species, giving them a safe eddy until they’re large enough to join the lanes or settle into the mats.

### The shadows under overhangs: predators that don’t break the peace

Predators here are not built for pursuit. They’re built for *the single correct moment*.

Under the dimmest lips of the canopies wait **fold-hunters**: long-bodied, ribbon-muscled creatures that can snap themselves into a tight curve, creating a local pressure collapse that yanks prey a body-length closer. The strike is the “quick fold of water” you felt—an edit, not an event. Their mouths are small, their throats expandable. They swallow without tearing, because tearing would waste.

There are also **door-jaw lurkers** embedded in the living floor: creatures that grow their own camouflage by encouraging the Weave to overgrow their backs. Their trap is vibration-triggered; their presence is discovered only when the mat’s seam-light briefly stutters—an involuntary flare as the Weave registers a sudden bite of stress.

Even these hunters are part of the equilibrium. They remove the weak, yes, but more importantly they keep traffic patterns stable. Lane-schools avoid certain alcoves; frill-mantas drift a little higher. The city’s flow persists.

### The decomposers: the reason nothing is wasted

The basin’s stability depends on a workforce you almost never see: organisms that make death into architecture again.

In the detritus snowfall live **flake-eaters**—tiny arthropod analogs that shred falling matter into finer particles. Below them are **gelatinous digesters** that absorb dissolved organics directly, turning waste into their own slow growth and into the microbial bloom that feeds the seam-films.

Where a large creature dies, it becomes a temporary neighborhood. Scavengers arrive not in frenzy but in order:

1. first the lane-schools that nibble loose tissue,
2. then the knock-shells that pry and rasp,
3. then the digesters that blanket what remains.

Within days—on this world’s scale of “days,” meaning a timespan you feel as a subtle change in the corridor’s voice—the carcass is stitched into the Weave. Bones, if they exist, are colonized, softened, calcified over, turned into another rib in a span that will not collapse.

### The “town centers”: where the basin’s life gets large

Far out, where corridors intersect and the roar becomes chordal, the architecture rises into layered domes—platforms stacked like dark lily pads. These are not plazas crowded with bodies; they are *confluences*.

Here the Weave is thickest, and its seam-glow is slightly more continuous—not brighter, but more connected, like a line of script rather than scattered punctuation. The domes host species that need many currents at once:

- **conductor-colonies**: broad, disk-like organisms that can stiffen or relax in sections, subtly retuning the flow through an intersection. They don’t control the basin, but they smooth its breathing, preventing eddies from becoming chaos. When a conductor-colony shifts, the sound changes first; the water’s chord resolves into a different harmony.
- **spore-blooms**: rare reproductive events where the Weave releases clouds of gametes or spores that shimmer faintly as they react with the seam chemistry. For a brief span the city looks dusted with cold stars. Nothing rushes to the light; everything simply feeds a little better for a while.

In these centers, you also meet the basin’s largest grazers: **plate-herds**—massive, armored skimmers that move in slow processions across the upper mats. They are as much gardening tool as animal. Their passage polishes surfaces, clips excess growth, keeps the corridors from narrowing. The Weave “wants” them the way a forest wants fire: not as catastrophe, but as maintenance.

### The final resident: the basin’s long memory

If there is a single presence that feels like *who* lives here, it is the Weave’s continuity.

Not a mind in a skull. A memory in fiber. A city that repairs itself without ever having learned the idea of ruin.

Everything else—raspers, knock-shells, lane-schools, frill-mantas, fold-hunters, digesters—are ways of making that continuity true. They are the moving parts of a stability so old it has become an environment, a set of rules with bodies for punctuation.

And when you listen long enough, you realize the loudness isn’t noise at all. It’s communication on the only scale that matters here: pressure and resonance, the basin talking to itself through its living architecture, and all its inhabitants answering by staying in rhythm.
