What lives there is not a roster of species so much as a single crowded method for being alive—a continent that has learned to occupy itself the way a reef does, except the reef is made of wet air, fresh basalt, and tissue.

At first you want to call it *plants*, because it’s green-adjacent and it grows in sheets and pillars. But the longer you imagine walking inside it, the less “plant” feels like the right container. It is vegetation the way a city is “buildings”: true, but missing the point. The inhabitants are layers of function—catch, wick, store, route, seal, signal—stacked so thickly that an individual organism has trouble existing as an individual at all.

### The surface-dweller: the felt that pretends to be ground
The thing you would step on is a living laminate.

- The topmost layer is a compressible, saturated skin—mosslike in texture but engineered in behavior. Press it and water doesn’t merely squish out; it *answers*, raising a sheen in a controlled bloom as pores dilate and microchannels align to relieve pressure without tearing.
- Beneath that is a mesh of root-ropes and fungal cords that behaves less like “roots in soil” and more like tendon in flesh: braided, load-bearing, always under some measured tension.
- In the dim pockets where water sheets under translucent mats, the mats aren’t passive covers. They’re lenses and valves—regulating evaporation, trapping warmth from fermentation below, and deciding (in the only way they need to decide) when a puddle becomes a reservoir and when it becomes vapor to be spent elsewhere.

This felt is an inhabitant in its own right: it is where most life is, because it is where gradients meet—water/air, mineral/organism, cold stone/warm decay.

### The vertical-dwellers: hollow columns that are more organ than trunk
Those house-wide “trees” are not trying to be trees. They are grown plumbing, grown acoustics, grown weather-interfaces.

Inside each column is a packed interior of ribs, baffles, and wet membranes:

- Some chambers are dedicated to moving water—not up a single stem, but through communal lift: capillary stacks, osmotic pumps, and sequences of one-way valves that trade time for elevation.
- Some are dedicated to air: intake slits that close when the wind carries too much cold, exhaust vents that open to dump humidity into a fog-bank the way lungs exhale into cloth.
- Some are dedicated to sound and vibration. The columns’ hollowness isn’t emptiness; it is a habitat for resonance. Pressure fronts turn them into instruments, and the “hum” you hear is partly the landscape playing itself, partly the landscape *using* that playing—vibration harvested by tissues that stiffen, flex, and convert mechanical oscillation into chemical work.

If you could cut one open (you can’t, not without it closing around the wound), you’d find surfaces that look like cartilage and sponge and leaf all at once—wet, ribbed, incredibly thin—built for exchange rather than strength.

### The mist-catchers: leaf as membrane, membrane as net
Across ridgelines and saddles live the organisms that treat fog as prey.

They don’t have broad, sun-hungry canopies; they have *geometry*:

- Silica-edged sheets that encourage droplets to coalesce and run in chosen directions.
- Velvet-black panels that drink what light exists and turn it into just enough heat to make condensation happen a centimeter away, on a cooler surface positioned to catch it.
- Fronds grown as overlapping scales—each scale a microclimate, each microclimate a bead-factory.

They “eat” the air’s water the way other ecosystems eat insects. And because there are no animals to steal it, the only contest is between neighboring membranes: who can make the air give up its moisture first.

### The mineral-eaters: microbes that begin the claim
Where the basalt weeps and the glassy foams are still young, the first residents are not green. They are films.

Chemolithotrophs—bacteria and archaea that treat new rock as food—spread as pale slicks inside fractures and along seep-lines. They harvest energy from iron, sulfur, and whatever else the newborn stone offers, and their waste products become invitations:

- acids that roughen the fresh surfaces;
- sticky polymers that hold the first dust;
- mineral crusts that give later tissues something to grip.

They are the reason bare rock never stays bare. Before anything with “leaves” arrives, the stone is already inhabited by a microscopic workforce changing the rules of the surface.

### The recyclers: fungi that make “soil” unnecessary
With no animals to chew and scatter, the role of breaking-down is taken to an extreme. Fungi here don’t wait for things to die; they negotiate with living tissue continuously.

Threaded through the root-web is a mycelial infrastructure that does three jobs at once:

1. **Immediate composting**: any shed skin, sloughed membrane, or snapped fiber is digested almost as soon as it falls, heat blooming in patches from the chemical rush.
2. **Distribution**: nutrients don’t trickle; they are routed. The fungal cords act like freight lines, moving phosphorus, nitrogen, trace metals—whatever the network is short on—sideways under ridges faster than surface water can manage.
3. **Defense and control**: because competition is pore-and-valve, fungi become both weapon and treaty. Some mycelia secrete inhibitors that keep a neighboring mat thin; others form mutualisms, exchanging minerals for sugars with a reliability that looks almost contractual.

In a world without mouths, decomposition becomes a form of conversation: who is allowed to keep their carbon, who must give it back, and how quickly.

### The signalers: tissues that treat weather as a nervous system
The crackle you can almost hear is not metaphor. There are electroactive inhabitants—plant tissues and microbial partners that hold charge, leak it, and read the leak.

Along certain membranes, charges build when mist strikes, when wind shears past, when droplets crawl and separate. That charge doesn’t light the world; it *coordinates* it:

- pores opening in one valley because a pressure change was “felt” along a ridge;
- transpiration throttling back because air downstream is already saturated;
- a controlled venting of gas because too much tension has accumulated in a conduit wall.

Sometimes this coordination shows as thin luminous threads in fog—brief discharges tracing motion like a pencil stroke in wet air. They are not a display. They are a side effect of regulation, like the smell of sap when a branch bends.

### The parasites and opportunists: life that lives on life because it can
Even in a near-monoculture of purpose, there are thieves.

There are pale, threadlike siphoners that don’t photosynthesize at all. They tap into the main mats and drink processed sugars, paying nothing back except perhaps a toxin that discourages other parasites from crowding the same host. There are blister-organisms that inflate under a neighbor’s membrane, stealing fog-capture surface area until the host responds by thickening waxes or shedding the blister layer entirely.

Predation exists; it is just slow, intimate, and hydraulic.

### The “individuals” that aren’t quite individuals
In places—especially where new terrain has formed quickly—you do get something like discrete organisms: pioneer pads on stone shelves, isolated columns establishing the first chimneys, small translucent bowls that harvest dripwater and seed a mat around themselves.

But those are temporary. The moment the network thickens, the distinction between one and another blurs. Connections fuse. Fungal cords bridge. Root-ropes splice. Electrical gradients unify. What began as separate colonists becomes tissue in a larger body.

If there is an intelligence here, it is not located. It is distributed the way homeostasis is distributed in an animal: sensed everywhere, acted on locally, coherent only when you step back far enough.

### What’s missing—and what replaces it
No animals means no eyes looking back at you, no sudden movements, no quick deaths. The ecosystem replaces that with different kinds of presence:

- **Motion without locomotion**: peristaltic swelling of mats, slow tightening of fibers, leaf-panels rotating by turgor, an entire saddle “closing” its pores as a windfront arrives.
- **Reproduction without courtship**: spore pulses timed to humidity cliffs; seed-packets that travel as inert cargo in vapor streams; fragments that detach and are immediately accepted elsewhere because “elsewhere” is the same architecture with a different pressure profile.
- **Territory without borders**: ridges that change composition like gears turning—gradual until suddenly decisive, when a new suite of membranes outcompetes the old by altering the local weather just enough.

So what lives there is a living skin that refuses vacancy, plus the innumerable specialized lives inside that skin: rock-eaters in the cracks, fungal freight lines underfoot, mist-nets overhead, hollow-column organs singing to the wind, and electrochemical whisperers using rain and static as language.

Stand in it long enough and “inhabitants” stops meaning *things* and starts meaning *processes with bodies*: condensation made muscular, erosion postponed by occupation, weather made into metabolism. The continent is alive the way a swamp is alive, the way a body is alive—except scaled until the only “animal” is the place itself.
