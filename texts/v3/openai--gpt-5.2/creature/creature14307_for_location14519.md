What lives in the cleft is not a single creature so much as a stacked economy of bodies, each one built around the same three facts: the walls are vertical, the light is rationed, and the air is always moving.

At the rim it announces itself as flight.

Bats are the population you feel first, before you properly see them. They don’t simply “roost” here; they use the crack as a lung. In the upper dark they hang in crowded pleats from the stone’s seams—thousands, sometimes tens of thousands—each animal occupying a thumb-sized claim in a mineral crease that holds the day’s leftover warmth. Their fur is dusted the color of the rock, not by pigment but by habit: they live in a constant drift of fine grit and dried guano, and the mountain slowly powders everything into the same matte palette.

When they move, it’s less like a flock and more like a release valve. Individuals peel away, test the draft with a few tight wingbeats, then commit to the upflow that the cleft provides. They don’t need much room; their whole body language is made for narrow air. Their sonar clicks—too high to be heard as sound—still register as pressure, a faint, persistent tapping you feel in the hinge of your jaw if you stand at the edge long enough. Even their mistakes are adapted: when one clips the wall, it doesn’t tumble. It skitters, catches, clings, as if every inch of its anatomy has been rehearsed against stone.

Above them, nearer the ribbon of sky, the day-shift takes over—swifts, sometimes nightjars, sometimes both in the same hour, exploiting the warm updraft like a conveyor belt of insects. In bright weather they are almost invisible against the thin sky, but you can identify them by how they turn: abrupt, stitched angles for swifts; softer, wider scoops for nightjars. They never land where you’d expect a bird to land. They cling sideways to near-vertical faces, or settle in slots so shallow they seem painted on. If there are nests up there, they are not built in the human idea of building: a smear of saliva and grit, a few stolen fibers, a depression in guano hardened like plaster by repeated use.

Then the walls begin to resolve into their own neighborhoods.

Every ledge is worked. The shelves are terraced not by intention but by accumulation: guano lays down in layers, dries, cracks, slides a little, and catches again on a roughness in the basalt. Over years it forms steps—soft-looking from above, but crusted and sharp-edged where ammonia has burned it into a kind of mineralized soil. These terraces are the true ground of the cleft, and they host the creatures that turn waste into food.

Beetles patrol there in slow, armored certainty—dull-backed, black-brown, their wing cases ridged to shed condensation. They are not flashy decomposers; they are custodians. Where a fresh drop lands, they arrive. Where a dead moth falls, they arrive. Mites ride them like dust. Fungal threads—white, almost luminous in the dim—stitch through the guano crust and turn it into something plants could use if plants were ever allowed more than a toehold.

Cave crickets live in the looser rubble near the bottom, where falling grit collects. You don’t see them so much as you hear their paper-scrape movements, the dry rasp of legs finding purchase on talus. Their bodies are pale, nearly translucent in the scant light, as if pigment is a luxury they have long since abandoned. When startled they do not leap outward; they throw themselves into the nearest crack, because every creature here learns early that open air is a corridor for hunters.

The hunters are patient and light-footed.

Thin snakes—stone-colored, ribboning through fissures—anchor themselves in warm seams and wait. They do not chase in the usual way; they exploit the cleft’s lanes of moving air. When insects lift on a thermal pulse, the snake is already positioned where the current feeds. Their scales are small and matte, built to reduce friction against rock. Their eyes, in the deeper dark, look like wet pebbles: reflective, economical, always open.

Owls come as specialists, not residents, but when they arrive the whole crack seems to tighten. They hunt the vertical space with wings held half-folded, making themselves narrow enough to thread past protrusions. Their flight is almost silent even here, even with the constant noise; they use the cleft’s wind the way a swimmer uses an undertow. You might never see one, but you will feel the sudden subtraction it causes—the way the bat hush becomes more frantic for a minute, the way the insect noise dulls as if someone has put a hand over the mouth of the place.

There are smaller predators too: lizards that live like punctuation on the walls.

They press themselves flat where the stone is warmed from within, their bellies taking the heat directly, their backs the exact shade of basalt in gloom. They can hold still for an unreasonable length of time, their throats barely moving, eyes half-lidded. Then, without any visible acceleration, they are elsewhere—gone into a fracture, or snapped onto a moth that has blundered close, or clinging upside down under an overhang with toes splayed like soft clamps. Some have pale patches along the jawline that catch what little light exists, a kind of accidental signal in the dark; others are uniformly muted, pure camouflage.

And in the water pockets—those black bowls slick with algae that looks like oil—there is a whole separate citizenship.

The “shrimp-like” things are real, but they are not oceanic intruders; they are an answer to the cleft’s strange hydrology. Tiny crustaceans, nearly clear, pulse in the thinnest films of water and graze on microbial mats. When a drip strikes the basin they scatter as if the sound has a shape they can feel. Tadpole-forms—sometimes true larvae, sometimes just the suggestion of movement in darkness—worry the margins where oxygen is richest. If you watch long enough, you realize the basins are not stable ponds but timed events: they swell during condensation-heavy nights, shrink during dry drafts, refill in rare rains. The inhabitants live in cycles of feast and almost-nothing, surviving in cracks as eggs, cysts, or dormant bodies until the next wet hour reanimates them.

The algae and bacteria down there are as important as any winged thing above. They are what make the water smell faintly sour-sweet under the ammonia. They coat stone with a slickness that changes how everything moves. They form dark, velvet sheens on the lips of overhangs where droplets are born, and they convert mineral trace elements into food at a pace that seems impossible until you remember how long the cleft has been doing this.

Plants exist, but as refusal rather than flourish.

Moss grows where the walls sweat most reliably, in strips so thin they look like shadow until you touch them. It is nearly black—not dead, just adapted to low light and constant damp, each leaf a miniature cup that holds a bead of water. Ferns hang from fractures where dust has collected enough to make a smear of soil, their fronds narrow and sparse, every segment shaped as if it has learned to be content with less. Lichen occupies the in-between: gray-green by day, indistinguishable from stone by night, spreading slowly across faces that are never fully dry and never fully lit. In the guano-rich terraces near the upper roosts, there are occasional surprise sprigs of grass—stunted, yellowed, heroic—taking advantage of nitrogen that has no other place to go.

All of it—the bats, the beetles, the snakes, the moss—depends on the cleft’s breath.

Warm air rises from below with a steadiness that feels like physiology rather than weather. It carries insects up from wherever they hatch. It carries the bats’ scent and sound upward and out. It creates the nightly sweating of the walls, the beads of condensation that feed basins and moss alike. The cleft doesn’t merely contain an ecosystem; it engineers one by forcing every life cycle to align with a vertical current.

There are also visitors whose presence is mostly absence.

Certain birds come not to nest but to display, gathering polished pebbles into little heaps on damp ledges where the stones can gleam. In the dim the arrangement reads like intention because it is intention, but it isn’t construction—no shaping, no joining, only selection and repetition until a ledge becomes a stage. These birds leave before you learn their faces. Their proof is in the piles, in a few dropped feathers stuck to tacky guano crust, in the way a particular ledge is worn smoother by many small feet returning to the same spot across generations.

And everywhere, threaded through every niche, are the small lives that make the place function: parasites in fur, flies whose larvae thrive in dung, spiders that stretch pale nets between protrusions where the updraft delivers prey, worms in the wettest guano that turn it to crumb. They are the quiet majority, the uncounted laborers that keep the cleft from choking on its own abundance.

So if you ask what lives there, the honest answer keeps changing as you descend.

Near the top: wing and speed, bodies that treat the crack like a sky.  
Midway: stone-keepers and ambush hunters, lives flattened into seams and shelves.  
Lower down: water-in-the-dark, a whole fauna scaled to droplets and bowls.  
All of it: the conversion of heat into motion, motion into sound, sound into a kind of map that every resident reads with its own senses.

The place feels crowded because it is crowded, but it isn’t chaotic. It’s layered. Each creature has learned exactly how much of the cleft it needs—an overhang the width of a hand, a seam that holds warmth, a basin that lasts one night—and it takes only that, over and over, until the mountain’s wound becomes less like damage and more like a long-standing, humming organ of life.
