What lives here is not a set of bodies moving through space so much as a set of occupations being performed continuously: gripping, sealing, dissolving, threading, storing, shedding. It’s an ecology made of surfaces and patience. If you wait for a creature to arrive, you’ll miss the residents entirely, because the residents are already everywhere the rock is willing to be touched.

At first it looks like nothing lives—only the mineral glare of freshly opened basalt and the constant, industrial insistence of wind and venting steam. But as your eyes stop searching for motion and start reading texture, you find that the place is crowded. It’s a crowd that doesn’t walk. It spreads.

### The first residents: the film-makers
The newest stone—those lighter seams, those freshly split plates—gets claimed by things that don’t need soil and don’t mind being rinsed every hour.

There are **biofilms** so thin they register as a change in gloss rather than color: microbial skins that make the rock look slightly lacquered when the mist thins. They gather in the micro-shelters you’d overlook: the lee side of a basalt rib, the underside of a shard, the hairline crack where condensation pauses before it falls. Some are dark, almost soot-dark, blending with the basalt; others are faintly green-black, like the suggestion of algae where the dim light is just barely usable.

They live by taking what the cleft gives freely and relentlessly—water, minerals, dissolved gases—and by enduring the violence of being exposed. When the wind scours and the vents exhale, they don’t flee. They tighten, slow, and then resume. Their work is quiet but foundational: they are the first softening of hardness, the first step in turning “new rock” into “something that can be lived on.”

In the places where steam breathes out regularly, the rock can host **heat-tolerant, mineral-loving microbes** that treat the vent’s rhythm like a tide. When the vapor thickens, they drink. When it thins, they hold what they can in slick matrices and wait.

### The ash-painters: lichens as slow machinery
Above and around those films, the cleft is marked by **lichens**—not showy, not the bright foliose plates you might expect on old cliffs, but the kinds that look like weathering itself.

They arrive as **pale crusts** that seem like spilled ash or salt bloom, and in time they become a map: faint islands spreading outward from pits and flaws in the basalt. Up close, they’re not simple stains. They’re tight cities—fungus and algae (or cyanobacteria) bound together, making a living membrane that can endure being alternately drowned in mist and dried by sudden gust.

Their job here is both colonization and demolition. They hold on with chemical insistence, exuding acids so mild you’d never call them corrosive—until you realize the stone under a lichen patch roughens faster, grains loosening, the surface turning from razor-fresh to faintly friable. In a young cleft where “anything loose eventually moves,” lichens are the ones that make looseness possible.

They don’t need animals. They don’t need anyone’s permission. Wind brings them what they require: moisture, a trace of dust, a moment of light. And wind carries away their fragments and spores, sowing the next patch in whatever nook stays damp long enough.

### The sponges and blankets: mosses that turn angles into beds
Where a ledge breaks the wind and holds condensation like breath held in a mouth, **moss** becomes the dominant architecture.

The moss here isn’t decorative. It’s structural. It takes the knife-edges of scree and makes them feel padded; it takes vertical faces and makes them seem furred. There are cushions that form on slightly flatter shelves—dense enough that droplets don’t splash off but sink in, disappear, and then reappear lower down as slow seep. There are also mats that behave like wet felt, stitched over gravel so that the talus looks stable until the whole sheet slumps a few centimeters with the next tremor.

Mosses in this cleft live by hoarding. They hoard water in their cells and between their leaves; they hoard minerals caught from vapor; they hoard time. They can go glassy and still during a harsh gust and then, with the next calm, resume that subtle, persistent green swelling.

And in their bodies they build the first real “ground.” Not soil the way a meadow knows it, but a darkening layer of held grit and decaying plant matter: the beginning of a sponge that will someday, if the cleft lasts, become a floor.

### The hanging curtains: liverworts and the art of staying damp
Where the rock sweats continuously—under overhangs, along trickle paths—**liverworts** hang in sheets and drape the stone like heavy cloth. They love the stable humidity the way lungs love oxygen. Their surfaces are slick, their edges scalloped, their bodies built to keep a film of water close.

They do not need depth. They do not need roots. They need only persistence of dampness, and the cleft supplies it like a law. Liverworts turn walls into living wallpaper, and in doing so they change the wall’s behavior: water that would have fallen cleanly instead spreads, clings, and feeds a longer reach of green.

### The dominant bulk: ferns as the cleft’s breathing
If anything here looks like it “owns” the place, it’s the **ferns**—not scattered individuals but banks of them, layered until the air becomes crowded with fronds.

They occupy the eddies. They pack the hollows. They form thick, springy barricades where the slope briefly relents. In that dim, uniform glow, their greens are deep and wet-looking, almost black in places, and their fronds act like instruments: each gust bends them into a unified bowing, and when they rebound they fling droplets outward in fine sprays. The cleft is full of that sound—drop after drop—so steady it becomes part of the vent’s mechanical rhythm.

Their reproduction matches the place’s rules: they make **spores**, and the wind is their courier. When a gust hits a mature bank at the right angle, the air briefly holds a smoky haze that isn’t smoke at all—just countless tiny futures, lifted and swallowed by mist. No pollinator has to arrive. No seed has to tempt a mouth. Their life cycle is built for a world that is mostly air and wet rock.

And because there is no browsing—no chewed edges, no insect stippling—the ferns here grow into an uninterrupted density. Old fronds collapse and remain, not shredded quickly into crumbs but layered into a dark, wet archive that slowly becomes compost.

### The recyclers and the invisible architecture: fungi as nervous system
Under the ferns, under the moss, threaded through the talus where damp persists, **fungi** are the true connective tissue of the cleft.

Some are obvious: shelf fungi stacked like dull, pale brackets beneath dripping ledges; small, waxy caps emerging after a particularly saturated week; delicate, translucent growths that appear where steam lingers and then vanish when the vent’s rhythm changes.

Most are not obvious. Most are **mycelium**: pale threads in the compost, fine cords bridging stone to stone, webbing that makes the dark soil smell richly of “cold” rather than rot. They are the ones that take the place’s accumulation—fallen fronds, dead moss tips, lichen flakes, microbial mats—and turn it back into usable matter. They are the ones that make nutrients mobile in a world where mobility is otherwise just gravity.

In a cleft with no animals, fungi also fill roles animals often assist with. They open pathways through dead plant layers; they distribute resources; they enforce a kind of balance by taking advantage of anything that grows too thick and then collapses. They are the living interpretation of the place’s loudness: not a roar, but continuous work.

### The smallest residents: the slow fauna that isn’t “fauna”
Even in a place described as animal-less, life still comes in scales that challenge the word “animal.” This cleft can plausibly be absent of anything you would call a creature—no insects, no birds, no mammals, no visible grazers—yet full of *minuscule presences* that are more like processes than individuals: bacteria, archaea, microscopic algae, spores, dormant cells carried in by wind and then either killed or incorporated into the resident films.

Whether or not any microscopic animals persist here (tardigrades, nematodes, mites) is a question the place seems to answer with its own severity: if they exist at all, they would be intermittent, accidental, and never foundational. The cleft is built to run without them. The system doesn’t leave open jobs that require legs.

### What “lives” here besides organisms: the cleft itself
Spend long enough in this hanging wound and you start to feel that the most dominant resident is the **fracture**—not as an abstract geological feature, but as an active participant.

The cleft’s walls decide where dampness gathers and therefore where moss can thicken. The vents decide where mineral-rich vapor deposits new chemistry and therefore where certain films can thrive. The wind decides where spores land and whether they desiccate or take hold. The talus decides what gets buried, what gets exposed, what gets reset.

The place is young enough that its physical changes happen on a human timescale: a ledge cracks, a plate falls, a slope re-angles, and suddenly an entire patch of moss is gone while a new scar of raw rock is available. The residents are adapted not to stability but to *repeated erasure*. They are specialists in returning.

### The community, taken as a whole
So whoever lives here is a layered, interlocking assembly:

- **Microbial skins** that begin the softening of stone.
- **Lichens** that paint the basalt and chemically pry it open.
- **Mosses and liverworts** that hold water, catch grit, and make the first beds.
- **Ferns** that turn constant condensation into constant growth, using wind as their only messenger.
- **Fungi** that stitch the whole thing together, recycling, redistributing, quietly maintaining the closed loop.
- And above them all, governing them without intention: **wind, mist, gravity, heat**, the cleft’s own “machinery.”

It’s a world with no eyes looking back at you, no calls answering your presence, no sign of hunger moving through the understory. But it is not empty. It is occupied the way a workshop is occupied when no one is speaking: by ongoing labor, by materials in transformation, by an engine that runs on physics and time—turning fresh rock into soil, soil into green, and green back into soil again, loudly, continuously, in the dark.
