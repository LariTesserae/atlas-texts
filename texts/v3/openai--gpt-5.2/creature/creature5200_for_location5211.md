What lives in the basin is not a “community” the way a green world builds one. It’s a workforce—layered, redundant, mostly hidden—built to keep going when the only reliable inputs are cold, water, pressure, and stone.

At first you think the residents are the loud bodies in the fog: the herds, the flocks, the things that chase them. But the real locals are the ones you can’t point at. The ones that live as rules.

### The first inhabitants: skins that eat rock and write conditions
Every exposed surface has a tenancy.

The slick, oil-looking sheens are microbial mats tuned to minerals instead of sunlight. They don’t bloom up; they spread sideways in microns, laying down films that change the wetting of the stone—making water bead where it should sheet, making it creep uphill for a few centimeters before surrendering. They “feed” by catalyzing tiny redox reactions on iron-dark films, by concentrating salts, by stealing energy from the slow, constant exchange between mineral seams and waterlogged clay. When the ground builds a voltage gradient, these mats respond the way a pond responds to wind: the whole skin shifts, patterns tightening into faint striations aligned with invisible currents.

Under them is the basin’s fungus—if fungus is even the right word. No caps, no stalks, nothing that reaches. It lives as a lattice in pores and cracks, threads fattening with absorbed water and then shrinking until the stone makes its quiet, knuckled pops. Some patches leave the rock faintly warm because the lattice is doing chemistry that releases heat in pinpricks: not enough to steam, just enough to make thin ice flex instead of snap. In the Fracture Shift these lattices are the first to “wake,” not by motion but by tightening their grip, helping certain shelves of clay hold together while others slump. The basin’s patchwork stability is, in a literal sense, glued on.

Between sheen and lattice there are smaller tenants—wormlike things you almost never see whole. They live in the boundary between skin and stone, in the film of water that never quite drains. They graze the microbial layer the way a snail grazes algae, leaving clean, matte trails on glossy rock. When the ground arcs—those brief blue stitches—some of these thread-animals coil into tighter loops and go still, not from fear but from saturation, as if the electricity is a meal too rich to keep moving through.

This is where the “industrial” sound begins. Not from any single creature, but from millions of small acts repeated: drip rhythms, skin contractions, micro-fractures, electric snapping, the soft abrasion of things scraping films off stone.

### The grazers: bodies built to harvest a coating
Then come the animals you notice as animals.

The dominant grazers are not browsers; they are cleaners. They move as if the ground is a tool that must be worked.

Some are low-slung and wide, built like living sledges: thick hides, fat layered in sheets, legs that don’t lift high so much as *press* down and *roll* forward. Their feet are not hooves but pads with stiffened rims, spreading their weight like snowshoes and sealing briefly to slick clay before peeling free with a wet clap. In a place where every step can liquefy the ground, their locomotion is as much about not *breaking* the surface as it is about crossing it.

Their mouths are the basin’s true grazing apparatus. Broad keratin plates—scrapers—run along the lower jaw, and when they feed you can hear it: a steady, gritty rasp like a file drawn across metal. They skim mineral films, lift salt blooms, and strip microbial sheens down to bare varnish. The stripped rock is immediately “new” again: raw enough for skins to recolonize, charged differently, wetting differently. Their feeding is landscaping.

Others have mineralized teeth arranged not for chewing plant fiber but for grinding crust. They take in salt, clay, and film; they excrete pellets that are strangely regular—little composite bricks of processed sediment that harden as they cool and dry, adding texture to otherwise slick flats. In some areas those pellets accumulate into low berms that redirect thin floods. Grazers build infrastructure by accident.

They travel in tight formations for the reason you already suspect: the lead animals test the ground and compact it, the ones behind step into the stability created by the first. When a line has passed, what remains is not merely a trail but a temporary roadway—compressed clay, squeezed water, shifted electrical potentials. The basin “remembers repetitions,” and herds are the basin’s way of repeating itself on schedule.

A few of these grazers have conductive ridges—callused, dark bands along neck or flank that look like stains until you see them purposefully pressed to certain stones. At battery-flats they kneel or slump down, bodies contacting the mineral skin, and shudder in a way that is neither pain nor pleasure exactly. More like a deep reflex: a voltage-gradient drinking. Afterward they rise and move with new coordination, as if their internal clocks have been reset to the basin’s shifts.

### The flyers: vent-riders and sound-printers
Above the herds are the basin’s winged things, and even they don’t behave like birds from a solar world.

Their wings are built for shivering flight, not flapping—thin membranes stiffened by struts that can vibrate rapidly, letting them “catch” the basin’s constant upwellings and the warm ropes of vent mist. They spend less energy on lift and more on steering through a three-dimensional map of temperature and humidity gradients.

Their calls are tool-sounds because their throats are tuned to resonate in wet air: clicks, ratchet-clacks, long moans with an edge like metal under tension. They do not sing to fill space; they sing to *probe* it. In fog that edits distance, sound is the only reliable tape measure, and these flyers have learned to throw calls that return with information.

You can sometimes watch a flock tilt into a vent’s exhale and hear their cries “double,” as if answered by the ground. That second call is not a ghost. It’s the basin’s skins—microbial films and porous stone—taking the vibration and holding it for a beat, like a fingerprint left on wet clay. Flyers return to places where their sounds hold longer; those places tend to be stable. Their ecology includes acoustics as habitat.

Their nests—when you find them—are not in trees, obviously, but in the lee of low ridges where fog movement is predictable. They use woven strips of shed hide, fungal threads pulled from cracks, and the stiff, mineralized whiskers of other creatures. The nests are damp, warm-ish, and faintly conductive. Eggs incubate partly on body heat and partly on the slow warmth of chemical skins beneath them.

### The predators: hunters of the uncertain
Predators here don’t have cover, so they have something else: the basin’s inconsistencies.

There are pursuit-hunters that look, in outline, like the grazers’ lean nightmare—taller, lighter, long-limbed with feet that can either spread wide or narrow into points for firmer ground. Their heads carry sensory structures that read voltage gradients the way a dog reads scent. They run not toward prey so much as toward *routes*—the hum-troughs, the battery seams, the places where reflections lead by half a heartbeat.

They hunt by driving herds toward zones where the basin’s editing becomes dangerous: flats where distance deletes itself and a straight line becomes a loop; shallow bowls where a crust of ice flexes until weight makes it fail; corridors of fog that “over-sharpen” and tempt a panicked animal into speed, which here is another name for sinking.

Some predators don’t kill with teeth at all. They harry until a grazer breaks formation. A single animal off the living bridge is an animal stepping onto untested ground. The basin does the rest. In that sense the apex predator is often the Drain Shift itself, and the flesh-eaters are opportunists who arrive like workers at a spill.

There are also smaller, stranger hunters—flat, raylike things that move under water-skins so shallow they look like wet stone until they lurch. They detect the tiny electric fields of muscles through the saturated clay and strike upward, not to drag prey down (there’s rarely depth for that) but to startle it into a bad step, a break in rhythm. They feed on the injured and the stuck.

And always there are scavengers—quiet, tireless—slender-bodied animals that follow the herds at a distance and pick clean whatever the basin takes. Their jaws are designed to crack mineralized hide and gnaw cartilage. Their droppings seed the microbial skins with nutrients that are otherwise scarce. Even death is a form of fertilizing here, just not a green one.

### The “engineers”: creatures that tune currents and keep the factory running
The basin has animals that don’t fit neatly as grazer or predator: the ones that behave like maintenance.

In the humming troughs you’ll sometimes see slow, deliberate movers—armored, low, almost arthropod-like—pressing their bodies into the clay and vibrating. They aren’t merely traveling; they are *coupling* themselves to the ground’s resonance. Where they linger, the hum changes pitch. Water drains differently; arcs snap more often. Other animals arrive and follow the altered path as if it has become brighter.

These engineers feed on the basin’s energy flows: heat gradients, electrical potentials, the chemical richness of freshly exposed mineral faces. Their bodies hold plates of magnetite or other conductive minerals embedded in tissue, not as armor but as instruments. They can short small potentials locally, smoothing a dangerous gradient, or sharpen it, making a seam “louder” to voltage-sensing creatures. In a world without built structures, they are the closest thing to infrastructure managers.

When the Vent Shift begins, these engineers often move first, massing near exhale-points. Their activity seems to “prime” the vents: mist ropes thicken, and the upwelling steadies into something winged things can ride. It’s hard to tell whether they cause the change or simply know the schedule with terrifying accuracy. In this basin, cause and prediction can look identical.

### The fog: not a resident, not not a resident
Over all of them is the presence that doesn’t have a body you can corner: the fog that edits.

Whether it’s alive in any strict sense is the wrong question; it behaves like a distributed organ of the basin. It responds to repetition. It creates corridors. It corrects distances that feel like errors. It returns calls improved.

Animals treat it as an active factor the way a desert animal treats sun—something you can’t fight, only negotiate with. Grazers keep formation because fog makes individuals disappear. Flyers throw calls because fog changes what “far” means. Predators watch for the moment fog offers an over-sharp corridor, because prey will trust it and run.

If you stay long enough you begin to see that the fog doesn’t merely obscure; it *selects*. It favors patterns that have happened before. Trails become easier because the basin—through compacted clay, recolonized skins, altered electrical gradients, and fog’s own strange preference—rewards repetition with coherence. The residents have evolved to exploit that preference. In a place where facts slip, habit becomes a survival strategy.

### The basin as organism: a living shift schedule
Taken together, the inhabitants form something like a single, slow body.

- The microbial skins are its metabolism: harvesting energy from minerals and currents, changing how water behaves.
- The fungal lattices are its connective tissue: gripping stone, releasing heat in small doses, controlling which surfaces fail.
- The grazers are its hands: scraping, compacting, building trails, exposing new “fuel” for skins.
- The flyers are its sensors: probing the fog with sound, moving information across the basin in patterns of calls and returns.
- The predators are its culling reflex: converting mistakes—breaks in formation, bad steps—into nutrient cycling.
- The engineers are its nervous tuning: adjusting resonances, shepherding traffic into humming troughs, shaping the invisible map.

Nothing here is ornamental. Even beauty—ice flexing like metal, arcs stitching between stones, droplets marching into lines—feels like a byproduct of function.

So when you ask what lives in the lowlands, the honest answer is: a factory ecosystem that manufactures stability out of instability, and an entire cast of creatures evolved not to conquer the basin’s wrongness, but to *use* it. The loudness is just the audible edge of that work. Silence would mean the shifts had stopped, and stopping is the one behavior nothing here can afford.
