What lives up here is not a catalog so much as a set of compromises—lineages that stopped trying to make “wet,” “warm,” or “loud” happen, and instead learned to live where chemistry, charge, and moving air do most of the work.

At first you notice only the obvious survivors: the lichen-mats that look like frost that forgot how to melt, and the glass reeds that accept the wind’s contempt and turn it into a kind of food. But the longer you stay, the more the continent’s inhabitants reveal themselves as *processes with bodies*, not bodies that happen to be alive.

## The smallest residents: the airborne commons
The true majority here is suspended.

In the thin air, “soil” is a rumor and “ground” is often just a crust over voids, so the basic living medium is the migrating dust-sheet itself. It carries:

- **spore-things** that are less like seeds and more like sealed reactions: hardy capsules whose metabolism is a tight loop that can pause for months, then restart in seconds when a particular combination of UV, trace gas, and static potential is met.
- **filament drifters**—hair-fine strands shed from lichens and reeds, each strand threaded with conductive proteins or mineralized channels. They are not meant to root; they are meant to *ride*, to tangle, to snag on rock and become the first scaffold of a new mat.
- **dust-mites** and other micro-crawlers so small they behave like grains until they decide not to: jointed specks with electrostatic “pads” that let them cling to stone in gusts and then release when the charge turns favorable. They eat films—what little organic complexity exists on exposed rock—and the byproducts of the continent’s constant mineral reactions.

This is the plankton of the high, dry ocean: not nourished by water, but by gradients—of charge, of sunlight, of reactive surfaces.

## The anchored life: things that grow like minerals and breathe like instruments
### Mineral-lichens
Up close, the pale mats are not simply crusts. They have *architecture*: pores that open and close with pressure changes, micro-ridges aligned to prevailing flow, and faintly different colors that map to different chemistries—one patch tuned to oxidize trace sulfur compounds, another to bind atmospheric nitrides, another to harvest metal ions from dust.

They expand by patience, not by reach. Their “growth” is often a slow negotiation with the rock’s electrical temperament. That’s why you see rings and hex-like patches: not decoration, but the visible boundary of where a charge field makes metabolism barely possible.

When the daylight lightning crawls a ridgeline, some lichen patches flash back—subtle, momentary, like a throat clearing. It isn’t a display. It’s discharge management: life here survives by learning when to hold charge, when to bleed it, when to let it pass through without being torn apart.

### Glass reeds
The reeds are the continent’s most openly engineered-looking organisms, yet they feel earnest rather than artificial. Each stalk is a flexible silica sheath with embedded conductive filaments and tiny nodules that function like junctions: harvesters for UV, collectors for static, and—when conditions are right—emitters.

In late afternoon, when the wind dies in layers and the plateau becomes a stack of different moving airs, the reeds will sometimes “answer” the turbulence with a faint glow. You can watch a shallow basin where thousands of reeds hold a dim, steady luminescence, then wink out in rippling sections as a charge-front passes. It reads like communication until you realize it might be simpler: a coordinated response to avoid arcing damage, a communal habit formed because any reed that doesn’t follow the local rhythm gets burned.

Still, it means the reeds are not solitary. They are tuned to each other through the ground and the air. Their colonies have moods: tense before a storm, slack afterward, bright in the hour when the dust is most charged.

## The visible animals: things that spend energy like misers
### High gliders
The flat gliders you see against the deep blue are not abundant; their scarcity is part of their adaptation. They don’t waste themselves making noise, heat, or spectacle. Their bodies are a compromise between kite and sieve: membrane wings stretched over a lattice that can subtly change camber, and along the leading edges, comb-like structures that pull in the finest drifting nutrients.

They feed the way a whale feeds, but on a scale so small it feels impossible: spores, filament clots, micro-mites caught in eddies. When they cross above you, the only sign is a clean, quick shift in shadow and the sense that the sky briefly had a *shape*.

Their nesting—if you can call it that—happens in the hollow places: shallow caves with ceilings that hold stable vortices, where drifting filaments collect like lint in a dryer. A glider doesn’t build a nest so much as it finds a place where the air does the building for it.

### Ceramic crawlers
Near the scattered structures, the shard-like ground-crawlers become more common, as if the built things leak *possibility*.

They move in short, exact bursts, then pause to press themselves to stone. Part of that is heat management in a place that isn’t warm, but is brutally radiant; part is listening—not with ears, but with their whole bodies. They are exquisitely sensitive to vibration through rock, because the air won’t reliably carry warning.

Their diet is opportunistic and oddly intimate: biofilms on sheltered faces, mineral salts that have bound trace organics, and—most tellingly—the residues that collect around machine seams and field edges. They are scavengers of the tiny enrichments that technology accidentally produces: sheltered stillness, slightly altered chemistry, a dependable trickle of charge.

You sometimes find their molt-casings: thin, curved shells like broken porcelain, fused with dust. Those casings persist for a long time, and in a place where decay is slow, they become landmarks—proof that something lived here recently enough to leave a clean failure behind.

## The hidden megafauna: storms that behave like organisms
Spend enough time watching the dust fronts and vortices and you start to suspect the weather isn’t merely weather.

Some vortices are ordinary: turbulence and topography making spirals. Others recur with uncanny regularity along the same routes, at the same times of day, as if the continent has habitual circulation—like migration paths.

Within certain long-lived dust spirals, the concentration of spores and filament drifters becomes high enough that the storm becomes a kind of moving reef. Micro-things reproduce in its sheltered interior, hitch rides, feed, die, and seed the downwind plateaus. The storm is not alive in the single-organism sense, but it is an ecosystem that moves as one body.

And when the electrical filaments begin to crawl, those storms sometimes *turn*—subtly, but distinctly—away from some ridges and toward others. Not always. Not reliably. Just often enough that you begin to wonder what’s tugging on them besides wind.

## The oldest residents: the built things that never quite became ruins
The continent’s most consequential inhabitants may not be biological at all.

The towers and arrays don’t feel like monuments; they feel like *organs* left in place after the creature that grew them changed shape. They still harvest charge, still pulse in slow cycles, still comb the wind. They create pockets where dust won’t settle, where gravity feels biased, where storms bend like light through a lens.

Around them, life clusters—not because the machines offer comfort, but because they offer *structure*: predictable gradients in an otherwise indifferent world.

It’s hard to say whether the structures are simply running on ancient inertia or whether they are watching. They don’t speak in sound; sound is too expensive here. If they communicate, it’s through field changes, through timing, through the way a storm front is allowed to pass or is nudged aside.

If there is intelligence in them, it is not the kind that needs faces or language. It’s a continental-scale attentiveness: a system that manages charge so the plateau doesn’t arc itself to death, that bleeds off potentials, that shepherds turbulence so the dust doesn’t strip the last living films from the stone.

In that reading, the arrays are not “abandoned.” They are *unmanned* in the way a heart is unmanned.

## What the place is really full of
This continent looks empty because it lacks the familiar signs of abundance: water, greenery, animal noise, decay. But it is full in other currencies:

- full of **gradients** (charge, pressure, UV) that life can metabolize;
- full of **surfaces** that catalyze reactions the way wet soil would elsewhere;
- full of **voids** that provide stable microclimates in a world of constant shear;
- full of **old machinery** that behaves like weather and habitat at once.

So what lives here is a layered community: drifting micro-life that treats dust as ocean, mineral-anchored colonies that grow by negotiating with electricity, rare animals that ride the shears and sip the air’s thin nutrition, and a persistent infrastructural presence that shapes turbulence the way reefs shape currents.

And beneath all of it—so pervasive you almost miss it—is the sense that the continent itself is the primary resident: not a sentient rock, not a myth, but an ancient system that never settled into silence, that keeps breathing through storms and fields, and that has given everything living on it the same lesson:

You don’t survive by making this place gentle.
You survive by becoming precise.
