What lives here is not the kind of life that arrives with suitcases. It arrives with checklists.

At first, the inhabitants feel like an extension of the district’s own physiology—things that move because something somewhere needs to keep moving. You can stand in one corridor and convince yourself there is nobody at all, only the managed hush of fans and the patient discipline of pumps. Then, slowly, you begin to notice that the place has *behaviors* that aren’t purely mechanical: choices about where warmth is allowed to pool, where damp is tolerated, where light is withheld. Those choices have caretakers. They have enforcers. They have opportunists. They have parasites that are allowed to exist because the system needs them, and parasites that are hunted because they will eat the system alive.

### The first residents: the small machines that patrol the seams
Before you meet a person, you meet the things that work while people sleep.

They’re not dramatic robots—no gleaming shells, no cinematic articulation—because everything down here is judged by whether it can survive heat, mineral grit, and a film of sweat-salt on every surface.

- Small inspection crawlers that cling to pipe bundles with magnet pads, pausing at taped labels to blink a sensor over a barcode, then moving on with the careful patience of insects that are not allowed to exist. Their motors are quiet on purpose; loud vibration loosens dust, and dust is a kind of weather here.
- Valve-actuator collars that live permanently around certain wheels—dull, scuffed rings with a single small indicator light. They don’t decide anything on their own; they just remove the need for a human hand in places where a human hand would burn.
- Drip listeners: cheap puck-shaped sensors stuck under joints, under flanges, under that one “temporary fix” everyone pretends is permanent. They don’t stop leaks. They simply make leaking *legible*, and in this district legibility is half of control.

They’re always slightly filthy. Nothing down here stays clean. But they are maintained the way a surgeon maintains instruments: not shiny, just *reliable*. A crawler that fails in a hot duct doesn’t die; it becomes a blockage, then a heat trap, then a reason a whole corridor has to be shut for an hour that nobody has to spare.

So the machines are treated like minor citizens—counted, tracked, swapped out without ceremony. Their presence is one reason you feel that strange absence of animal life: the ecosystem has been replaced with monitoring.

### The second residents: the people who dress like they belong to the infrastructure
When you finally see a person, they don’t read as a visitor. They read as a moving piece of the plant.

They wear layers that look wrong until you understand the climate: light cloth against the skin to keep sweat from becoming glue, then a tougher outer layer that can take abrasion from concrete corners that are still sharp. Gloves that aren’t for cleanliness but for *heat memory*—metal holds a burn long after the air feels bearable. Boots with soles chosen for wet steel grating as much as for stone.

Their light is never bright. Their headlamps are hooded, dimmed, often filtered amber to match the sodium wash. Not because they can’t afford better, but because glare makes you careless. Brightness would flatten the shadows that hide steam leaks and wetness on rails. Brightness would make you forget that a corridor can change pressure the moment a bulkhead opens.

The people move as if every movement has an effect downstream.

- They keep shoulders in, tools tethered, hair covered. Not from modesty, but from the logic of spinning fans and exposed couplings.
- They touch handrails with the backs of knuckles first, reading temperature before committing skin.
- They pause at junctions and listen—not to hear a voice, but to hear the district’s *tone*: the hum that says load is steady, the faint new hiss that says a seal has started to talk.

They talk, but not like surface people do. In many stretches they don’t waste sound at all. They use taps on metal (distinct from the drip percussion), hand signals, brief radio clicks that carry codes more than sentences. Where voices do happen, they’re low and practical—words shaped to travel only as far as necessary.

They’re organized like a utility, not like a neighborhood. There are shifts, coverage maps, responsibilities assigned to routes:

- **Intake** crews who treat every incoming flow like a potential contamination event.
- **Bleed** crews who know the language of pressure relief: the safe sigh, the dangerous one, the one that means a gauge is lying.
- **Scrub** crews who handle the chemistry and the living filters, careful and half-feared because they deal with the only sanctioned “wild” things down here.
- **Reclaim** crews who live in the borderland between resource and rot, whose hands always smell faintly of treated water no matter how often they wash.

If there are dwellings tucked under catwalks, they don’t personalize them much. Not because the people lack sentiment, but because dust will take any softness you offer it and turn it into a sponge for odor. A few laminated photos, maybe, sealed under plastic. A small charm hung inside a locker where it won’t gather grit. Clothes hung where a vent blows slightly cooler, because comfort is engineered in centimeters.

Their bodies show the place, subtly. Skin that stays slightly slick because the air never fully dries it. Pale pressure marks from respirators and masks—more for particulate than for poison. A certain careful hydration, the way they swallow as if drinking is a scheduled maintenance act. Nails kept short because grime under nails becomes a health problem when you work around reclaim water. Eyes that don’t widen easily in dim light because they’ve learned that brightness is rare and usually a warning.

There are no children running in the corridors. That isn’t a moral judgment; it’s an engineering outcome. This district is too young, too hot, too close to its own hazards. If families exist in the larger world, this is where adults go to earn credits or pay debts, where they do their hours and leave. The people who stay long-term are the ones who have either chosen the rhythm or been shaped by it until another rhythm feels false.

### The third residents: the quiet authority in the panels
Every control panel with its faint green blink is a kind of resident too.

Not a personality in a human sense—more like a distributed attention. The district remembers its own tolerances. It logs every tagged drip, every pressure cycle, every pump that draws a fraction too much current. It doesn’t need to be a sentient overseer to feel like something that watches; it only needs to be consistent.

People here treat alarms the way you treat an animal that can bite: with respect and a certain wariness. They don’t silence alerts casually. They don’t override without leaving a trail. The system is not a god, but it is a witness, and in a place built in a hurry, witnesses matter.

Sometimes the panels do things that feel almost like preference: dimming one corridor further, throttling a fan in a way that makes dust strata shift like curtains. Whether that’s algorithm or habit, it becomes part of the culture. Workers learn to interpret the district’s moods. They say things like, “It’s running warm today,” as if speaking of a body rather than equipment.

### The fourth residents: the sanctioned life—cultures that scrub, eat, and seal
Down here, “biology” isn’t pets or pests. It’s process.

In the warm wet corners—especially near **SCRUB** and **RECLAIM**—there are bioreactors and filter beds that hold living colonies: bacteria that eat specific contaminants, engineered mats that precipitate minerals out of solution, mycelial threads that bind fine particulates the way felt binds fibers.

These aren’t allowed to spread freely. They are housed, fed, measured, harvested. They are life treated as infrastructure.

Still, they leak into the margins. A chalky mat creeps a few centimeters beyond a gasket. A pale film appears where condensate drips repeatedly, nourished by trace organics in the water. In the reservoir pocket, where the paddle turns and the mineral shoreline grows sharp, there are places the deposits look less like crystal and more like something built: layered, faintly patterned, as if the district is growing teeth.

The workers have names for these growths that are half-joke, half-warning. They scrape them when they must, and sometimes they don’t—because certain films, certain colonies, actually *help* by sealing micro-leaks or consuming something that would otherwise corrode metal faster. The line between maintenance and cultivation blurs. Down here, you can be both gardener and exterminator in the same hour.

### The fifth residents: the unsanctioned life—what survives despite policy
Even with sterilants, even with heat, even with the engineered airflow, the district cannot be perfectly purified. It is too young, too busy, too full of seams.

So there are the quiet squatters of the microbial world: the rust-associated bacteria that accelerate orange bloom on bolts in damp corners, the threadlike fungi that take the fibrous insulation as a slow meal, the biofilms that make a handrail feel slick in a way that isn’t just condensation.

These are treated like adversaries, but also like weather. You don’t hate humidity; you manage it. You don’t moralize corrosion; you budget for it. The people here learn which stains are harmless and which are the beginning of a shutdown.

They mark the dangerous ones with paint. They quarantine a corridor by closing a bulkhead and rebalancing pressure. They come back with masks and scrapers and sealed bags, moving methodically, like surgeons removing something that will grow back if you leave even a trace.

### What this makes, together: a society of caretakers under a lid
Taken as a whole, the inhabitants aren’t a community in the cozy sense. They’re a maintenance ecology.

- Machines that patrol and report.
- People who interpret, repair, and enforce rules that keep the district from becoming its own disaster.
- A quiet oversight embedded in sensors and control logic.
- Living cultures raised like crops to clean water and air.
- And the persistent marginal life that arrives whenever heat and damp shake hands.

No animals, yes—but not emptiness. A different kind of crowding: the density of systems depending on each other, the intimacy of corridors where one person’s mistake becomes everyone’s heat problem, everyone’s water problem, everyone’s corrosion problem.

If you stand still long enough, you can feel how the residents think of themselves. Not heroic, not doomed—*necessary*. They live under something heavy, and their lives have adapted to that weight. They become careful. They become quiet. They become fluent in small changes: a drip’s tempo, a fan’s pitch, a new tide mark on concrete.

And in a place this young—edges still sharp, cut marks still legible—that fluency is the closest thing to belonging.
