At first you think the basin is mostly empty of *creatures* and crowded only with things—stems, ropes, timber, the slow architecture of kelp and lashings. Then you spend enough hours moving through it to realize you’ve been mistaking quiet for absence.

Life up here doesn’t announce itself with speed. It holds. It yields. It waits until you’re close enough that you can’t pretend you’re alone.

### The water’s oldest residents: the patient makers
Before you meet anyone who calls themselves a citizen, you meet what actually keeps the city possible: the films.

They’re on everything that stays still for more than a day—slick skins of microbes and mineral-eaters that turn raw stone into something root can grip and rope can bite into without slipping. In the dim, those films look like nothing: a softening of edges, a faint pearly sheen. Up close they’re busy with chemistry. They drink the vent-breath and exhale a chalky persistence, laying down the first brittle crusts that become chimneys, shelves, terraces.

People talk about the basin “deciding” where things belong. Part of that is physics—temperature bands, salinity gradients, the gentle stubbornness of buoyancy. But part of it is these living skins that change the water by living in it: making some depths taste wrong to some bodies, making other depths feel like relief. The sensation in your teeth when you cross an invisible band isn’t superstition; it’s your nerves registering a new balance of dissolved metals, gases, and microbial byproducts. The basin doesn’t think like a person—if it thinks at all—but it *does* have preferences, and those preferences are distributed across billions of small hungers.

Where the films are thickest, plants grow in cleaner lines. Where they thin, the kelp becomes ragged, and even ropes seem to fret and loosen as if they’ve lost conviction.

### The green citizens: trained plants that behave like buildings
The vegetation isn’t just background. It’s infrastructure with a metabolism.

Some of it is ordinary in form—broad blades, root-tangles, floating leaf-mats—but not in behavior. In this basin, people have been selecting and shaping plants the way land towns breed sheepdogs and fruit trees.

There are kelp strains that grow stiffer ribs so walls can be cut from them, cured, and stitched into panels that flex with current instead of fighting it. There are vine-things that thicken where they’re repeatedly tied, producing natural “knuckles” that hold knots without slipping. There are wax-leaf mats that heal fast when punctured, making them perfect for roof-gardens and for sealing the mouths of chambers that keep a pocket of managed air.

In some districts the “buildings” are as alive as any garden. You can tell the older ones because they’ve learned the city’s rhythms: shutters that open at the hour the current is gentle, curtains of reed that droop when siltfall is thick, root-bundles that creep toward warm stone as if seeking a hearth.

And sometimes, late in the dim, you’ll notice a wall *moving* in a way wood never would: not swaying, but slowly re-placing itself—tension redistributing, fibers tightening and slackening as though the house is resettling its own posture. People don’t call that haunting. They call it a well-trained home.

### The scarce animals: singular bodies, treated like weather
Fauna exist the way comets exist: not as constant traffic, but as sightings that become stories.

A pale eel appears often enough that it has nicknames in multiple neighborhoods. It threads the kelp with such unhurried certainty that children sometimes follow it the way children elsewhere follow a stray dog—keeping just far enough back not to be disrespectful. No one tries to catch it. Catching it would be like catching a rumor. It is allowed to be itself because its presence is considered a sign that the basin is still habitable.

Thumb-sized crustaceans cling to posts and anchor-stones like punctuation marks. People don’t eat them. They watch where the crustaceans choose to settle, because the creatures are more reliable than instruments at detecting subtle shifts in the “intention layers.” When the crustaceans abandon a line of pylons, the tether-guild checks the anchors.

Translucent medusae drift through sometimes, each one an event. In the rare clear-column days, their bells catch light and briefly turn the city’s dim into stained glass. A few households keep small ones in netted courtyards as living barometers: when the medusae rise, a pressure band is changing; when they sink, a vent-field has begun to breathe differently.

There are also grazers in the gardens—snails, soft-bodied cutters, slow mouth-things that rasp algae from mats. They’re tended like livestock not because they’re valuable as meat (though sometimes they are), but because they are valuable as *stability*. A garden with grazers stays clean; a garden without them chokes on its own generosity.

And in the deepest lanes, down where the vents make the water feel “thoughtful,” there are things people don’t inventory aloud: shadows that are too large to be any known animal, moving at the basin’s pace rather than the city’s. When those pass, the current changes slightly afterward, as if the water remembers a shape.

### The people: breath-hoarders, knot-makers, and depth-listeners
When you finally meet the inhabitants who built this place on purpose, the first surprise is how ordinary they can look—until you notice the ordinary has been tuned.

They are not fish. They are not merfolk in any clean mythic sense. They are humans (or close enough that the differences read as adaptations rather than a separate species), and the basin has taught their bodies to negotiate with it.

Their chests tend toward breadth, not for strength but for storage. Many have a slight thickening along the ribs where cartilage has become more stubborn over generations—useful when your days involve pressure changes that aren’t just pressure. Their ears are small or close-set; their sinuses, if you learn their faces, seem shaped for fewer trapped spaces. Their fingers are often scarred with the pale lines of rope-burn and resin-work, and the pads of their hands are toughened the way a climber’s would be—except the “rock” here is wet cordage and living stem.

Their eyes look a little too patient. In the dim they don’t waste focus. They look for edges and drift and the faint language of suspended silt. They recognize neighbors not by silhouette alone but by the way someone holds their buoyancy—each person’s habitual angle in the water, the way they kick or don’t, the tension they carry in their tether-lines.

They rarely speak at a distance. Conversation is close, forehead-to-forehead or cheek near ear, or conducted in a signed language adapted to three dimensions: gestures that include depth, tilt, and the direction of current. Touch is not intimacy by default here; touch is navigation. You grab a shoulder to stop yourself from drifting through someone’s hanging garden. You press your palm to a friend’s wrist to ask, *are you steady?* and the answer is given in pressure back.

#### What they wear
Cloth is tricky underwater, so they wear surfaces rather than fabrics: layered hides, resin-stiffened mats, braided fiber that swells tight. Most people carry:

- a belt of weights that can be reconfigured quickly (never a single heavy anchor—always many small decisions),
- a coil of cordage with standardized knots marked by texture so they can be identified by feel,
- a small bladder or two for air—precious enough that even a child learns not to play with one.

Some have mouthpieces that look like simple reeds but function as valves; some have nasal clips; some have none and simply control their breath like musicians. The richer households have repaired metal fittings on their gear—tiny rings, buckles, hook-blades—each one kept for decades, each one with a history.

#### What they eat
Their food is mostly plant: kelp cured and fermented until it becomes chewy and sour; wax-leaf greens scraped clean by grazers; starchy root-things cultivated where the mineral floor allows it; and, in vent-districts, bacterial mats harvested carefully from warm stone—food that tastes faintly metallic and strangely comforting, like the basin feeding you back.

Meat appears rarely: a snail harvest, a lucky catch of something that wandered in, the occasional eel *only* if it dies or strands itself. Predation is not a default relationship here; it’s an interruption. Many people treat eating an animal as a solemn act, not moralized, just acknowledged as uncommon.

#### What they value
On land you might hoard gold or salt. Here the currency is more practical and more intimate:

- **air**, in bladders whose seals are checked like prayers;
- **resin** and sealants, because a leak is a slow disaster;
- **cordage** of known quality, because bad rope is betrayal;
- **seed-mats** and cuttings of reliable plant strains;
- **knowledge of the layers**—who can tell, without instruments, when the basin is about to “change its mind.”

The last of these makes a class of people who are treated with an unusual kind of respect: depth-listeners. They are not priests exactly. They are interpreters of sensation. Some claim they can “hear” the bands; others simply have bodies sensitive to minute changes and the discipline to admit what they feel without turning it into theater. When a depth-listener says, *don’t sleep below the third terrace tonight,* most people obey without needing an explanation.

### How they organize themselves: guilds shaped like currents
Because everything is negotiable and nothing is final, authority here is less about law and more about maintenance.

There are anchor-keepers who inspect weights and the stone-work around vent chimneys. There are drift-gatherers who range the gyres and return with wood, relics, and sometimes stories of distant structures being slowly swallowed or newly revealed. There are gardeners whose work is half botany and half engineering: training living walls, pruning kelp so it doesn’t redirect a current into someone else’s home, breeding mats that make better light.

And there are air-wardens—quiet people in warm chambers—who oversee the communal stores and the rules around them. Not because they are tyrants, but because air is too easy to romanticize and too lethal to manage casually. You can be generous with food. You cannot be generous with breath without accounting.

Disputes aren’t shouted. They’re negotiated like everything else: by meeting in netted plazas where no one can drift away mid-sentence, by laying out cord samples or broken seals as evidence, by calling a depth-listener to confirm whether a misfortune was negligence or the basin’s slow whim.

### What they believe (without always saying they believe it)
If there is a religion here, it doesn’t require gods. It requires attention.

Most people behave as if the basin is a partner in every action. Not benevolent, not malicious—just involved. They’ll thank a vent-field for warmth the way you might thank a stove, and they’ll apologize to a current for blocking it the way you might apologize for stepping in someone’s path. These are not metaphors to them; they are manners adapted to a world where ignoring the environment has delayed but severe consequences.

They tell stories that sound like myths but function like manuals. Tales of neighborhoods that tried to “fix” their depth permanently and were slowly unraveled. Legends about a platform that refused to be re-tied and eventually floated up into the surface gardens, tearing them open like a wound. Children learn caution in the same breath as wonder.

And threaded through all of it is a shared, almost unspoken identity: *we are not the first inhabitants.* Not because they know a prior civilization, though relics sometimes rise from the basin floor, wood too old to be theirs, cordage tied in unfamiliar hands. But because the basin itself—its microbes, its plants, its slow mineral breath—behaves like something that was here before any human decision and will be here after.

### The truest resident: the unfinishedness itself
If you stay long enough, the question “who lives here?” stops pointing only at bodies.

The city lives here, in the sense that it metabolizes: it repairs itself, sheds old skins, grows new passages. The vegetation lives here, clearly. The rare animals live here like punctuation. The people live here by making agreements with depth and tying those agreements into rope and routine.

But most of all, *becoming* lives here.

A place where rocks are still deciding what kind of rocks they are will never let its inhabitants pretend they are finished either. So the people cultivate a particular temperament: not optimism, not resignation—something more technical and more tender.

They practice being temporary without being careless. They build homes meant to move. They raise children to read currents the way other children read streets. They treat breath as a shared resource and time as a building material. And when the basin shifts—when a terrace band lowers by the width of a hand, when an anchor’s “comfortable level” changes—they don’t call it catastrophe first.

They call it information.

Then they get out the rope. They put their hands on the living walls. They listen with their teeth and their bones for where the water wants things to be. And they re-make the city—slowly, deliberately—as if that is what living has always meant.
