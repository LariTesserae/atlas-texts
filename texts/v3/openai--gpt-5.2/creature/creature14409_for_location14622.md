What lives there is not a single species so much as a *stack* of lives, each one taking a different cut of the same harsh abundance: heat leaks, mineral dust, polymer scraps, vibration, and the one thing everyone hoards—breathable air that isn’t full of knives.

At first, you notice the smallest residents because they’re the only ones that seem truly at ease.

### The first citizens: the films and the dust-bodies  
In sheltered seams—where a coolant line sweats an almost-imperceptible slick, where an exhaust grate stays warm even when the rest of the terrace is cold—there’s a dark sheen that doesn’t read as growth until you watch long enough. It’s a living varnish that doesn’t reach upward, doesn’t flower, doesn’t waste energy on anything that could be torn away by turbulence. It spreads by patience and chemistry: a slow, matte migration along metal grain and stone pores, building itself from reactive residues and trace water stolen from air-reclaimers’ vents.

On top of that varnish move the dust-eaters: pale, jointed things so small they’re easy to mistake for drifting grit until they turn as one, synchronized to pressure shifts. They aren’t pretty, but they’re *precise*. They comb the city the way lichens comb a rock face elsewhere—filtering, concentrating, leaving behind a thinner, cleaner surface and tiny pellets that other mouths can use.

They have their own weather sense. Before a particulate bloom, they disappear—vanishing into gasket shadows and bolt-holes—because they can feel the coming abrasion in the charge of the rails.

### The second citizens: the scavengers that make a city possible  
Then you meet the organisms that have learned the city’s true crop is *manufactured leftovers*.

Scrap-gnawers live where humans (or whoever built this place) have learned to stop seeing them as merely vermin. They’re heavy, plated, and slow in a way that reads like confidence rather than laziness. Their mouthparts are grinders tuned not for bone but for sealant and soot-cake and old insulation. They will chew the wrong thing if they’re hungry—fresh polymer couplings, heat-shield fabric, the edge of a boot—so people bait them with compacted waste bricks and guide them like living sanitation.

Cable-nesters are harder to tolerate and harder to evict. They find warmth the way a compass finds north: electrical junctions, transformer housings, the backs of luminaires where the metal stays a degree above the air. They weave with what the city sheds—wire whiskers, torn filter mesh, the softest thing this world produces: frayed insulation fiber. In a pinch, they’ll steal hair from sleeping beings and braid it in, because hair is one of the few truly flexible materials that arrives already made.

Their young are the reason bright light is rare. Strong illumination draws the quiet flocks—and the quiet flocks draw what hunts them.

### The quiet flocks, and what follows them  
The gliding silhouettes that pass through on jetting wind aren’t birds, but they occupy the same ecological job: fast movers, mass travelers, opportunists. Their bodies are jointed more than they are muscled; their wings are membranes that stiffen and slacken with electrostatic control. They use the pocket’s turbulence as a lift system, catching pressure differentials with uncanny economy.

They don’t land for long. When they do, it’s on grating edges and cable runs, claws ticking like a light rain that never happens here.

And because they exist, there are predators that don’t look like predators until you realize what they’re tracking.

Some of those hunters are simply larger versions of the scavengers—armored, patient, with a sense for heat plumes and nest-smell. Others are stranger: pale, half-reclusive bodies that stay close to the chimney’s warm exhale, where the air is easiest to read because it is always moving. They don’t chase so much as *position* themselves, becoming part of the infrastructure in the same way the city itself has.

A traveler learns an unpleasant rule: the most dangerous things here are the ones that can afford to be still.

### And then—higher up, behind the grates—there are the ones who built the truce  
If there are “citizens” in the social sense, you don’t first encounter them as faces. You encounter them as *maintenance decisions*.

A railing that has been re-ground not once but many times, its edges softened by deliberate labor in a place that doesn’t soften anything by accident. A valve handle wrapped in three different generations of insulating tape, each layer labeled in a different hand. A ladder rung replaced with a composite that doesn’t spark, because someone once died to a static discharge and nobody wanted the same story twice.

When you finally see them, they look like a people designed by the environment as much as by ancestry.

They are wrapped, sealed, and layered—not for drama but for survival. Their clothing is anti-static and dust-shedding, stitched with conductive threads that bleed charge into ground straps. Their masks are not merely respirators; they’re reclaimers: exhalation routed through cartridges that pull moisture, heat, and trace chemicals back into personal reservoirs. In a world where a droplet is “an accident,” a person’s breath is part of the municipal water budget.

Their skin—when you glimpse it at a wrist seal or an ill-timed gap—is often marked by the place: fine fissures of dryness, pale scarring where dust abrasion got past filters, small burn freckles from arc-flashes. Many of them wear ear protection not just against noise, but against the pressure changes that can make the inner ear feel like it’s being twisted.

You can’t tell at a glance whether they are human, or human-descended, or something that became “people” by living here long enough. In low light, their silhouettes are similar: compact bodies built for ladders and narrow walkways, hands that prize grip over ornament, posture that’s always negotiating a harness attachment point. Some have augmentations that seem less like luxury and more like local clothing: forearm plates that double as tool docks, ocular lenses that polarize against arc-flare, throat implants that monitor oxygen saturation in thin air.

If they have a name for themselves, it will translate poorly. Outsiders would call them bolt-keepers, or terracefolk, or simply miners. They are none of those words alone.

They are *tenders of instability*.

### Their culture is not built around stories; it’s built around checks  
They live by rituals that look like habits until you understand that habit is what stands between a ledge and a fatal fall.

- They touch railings with bare knuckles through a conductive pad to read vibration.  
- They listen to cables as if listening to weather, because in this pocket, tension is forecast.  
- They keep ink and paint not for art (though art exists) but for marking shifts—today the crack is this long, tomorrow it is longer, and the city will respond accordingly.  

Their calendar isn’t months; it’s dust seasons, chimney moods, and the long slow argument the mountain has with itself.

Socially, they group by *systems*, not by streets. There are air-keepers who maintain the reclaimers and decide how much oxygen can be “spent” on industry that day. There are heat-mappers whose job is to walk the tiers and feel for leaks—because warmth is life, but too much warmth in the wrong place is fire without water to end it. There are riggers who treat every bridge and platform as a living animal that must be fed attention or it will throw you.

And there are the ones who negotiate with the nonhuman residents, because negotiation is cheaper than extermination.

A scrap-gnawer colony is moved, not destroyed, the way another city might reroute a river. Cable-nester nests are harvested carefully for their woven insulation, then replaced with decoy hollows so the junction boxes stay clear. Dust-eaters are encouraged with mineral “salt” baits at intake corners, because clean air intakes matter more than pride.

The people do not see themselves as separate from the pocket’s ecology. They can’t afford that luxury. Their industry made a food web; now they live inside it.

### What they fear is not death; it’s cascading failure  
They fear the moment when small shifts synchronize.

A gust pattern that turns a cable run into a resonating instrument.  
A static-heavy dust season that makes every handrail a threat.  
A pressure pulse from the chimney that arrives like a slow footstep through the cliff and knocks a thousand fasteners into the same tiny looseness.

When that happens, the city responds like a body mounting an immune response: shutters clatter down; lights dim further; elevators are locked at safe tiers; the air-keepers tighten the reclaimers; children and elders are moved away from exterior walkways; riggers run with wrenches and strain gauges like medics.

And through all of it, the nonhuman residents respond too—dust-eaters vanishing into seams, quiet flocks going suddenly absent, scrap-gnawers hunkering down as if listening.

In those moments, you realize the pocket is not a backdrop. It is an active participant. Everything living there has learned to read it the way coastal people read tides.

### If you stay long enough, the city stops feeling like it’s “in” the mountain  
It starts to feel like the mountain has grown an organ.

The chimney is a throat. The conveyors are intestines. The warm tiers are a heart with a disciplined beat. The people—wrapped in their reclaiming masks, hands always finding purchase, eyes trained to notice misalignment—are not conquerors of the cliff. They are its nervous system: sensing, correcting, adapting.

And threaded through them all, from the slow black bio-skin to the gliding flocks to the sapient tenders of bolts and breath, is the same shared law:

Life here doesn’t bloom.  
It *clings*, it *filters*, it *reuses*.  
It survives by being good at what the pocket offers—heat, residue, movement—and by never pretending the place will one day become gentle.
