What lives there is easy to miss because it rarely *moves* in the way your body means by movement. It does not cross a street. It does not flee your footsteps. It does not hunt. It doesn’t even make a show of being alive.

It occupies the place the way humidity occupies air: everywhere, unevenly, according to rules that aren’t personal but still feel selective.

### 1) The first inhabitants: the films that aren’t quite plants

On the downwind faces of pillars and in the seams where basalt meets clay, the gray-green “afterthought” isn’t an afterthought at all—it’s the city’s most ordinary living skin.

Up close it’s not moss and not algae in the familiar sense. It’s a layered biofilm: thin communities that build themselves the way the walls build themselves, by accretion. It has strata.

- A top layer that tolerates the heat and the constant wet: slick when touched, almost soapy, resisting your finger like it would rather shear than tear.
- Beneath it, darker sheets—matte, faintly fibrous—where the chemistry is doing the work photosynthesis cannot. They eat stone in slow mouthfuls: sulfur compounds, iron traces, whatever minerals are dissolved into that metallic-tasting air.
- A basal layer that isn’t soft at all, more like varnish: a mineralized “floor” the colony lays down as it goes, turning yesterday’s living surface into tomorrow’s substrate.

They don’t reach upward because there’s nothing to reach for. They spread sideways because that’s what the city rewards: surface area, contact, adhesion. In the rare places where pale fronds appear, they’re less like leaves than like feelers—translucent blades that are not seeking light but sampling moisture gradients. When humidity peaks they relax, flatten, and almost vanish; when the air thins (as much as it ever does here), they stiffen as if listening.

They don’t attract animals because they were never meant to. They’re built for a world where being eaten is improbable and being dissolved is constant.

### 2) The second inhabitants: salt that behaves like bone

Salt blooms are not just chemistry decorating architecture. The city grows them the way a body grows callus.

A corner where water repeatedly hesitates will develop a lace of mineral crust: white, yellowed, faintly pink. It looks ornamental until you realize it’s functional. These filigrees:

- stiffen edges that would otherwise slump;
- redirect runoff into established channels;
- seal hairline fissures when the ground swells.

When humidity rises, the blooms soften and partially dissolve. When humidity falls, they re-crystallize—but not identically. Each cycle remembers stress. Each new crust repeats the old pattern with small corrections, like bone remodeling around a long-healed break.

If you break a salt ridge with your boot, it does not feel like snapping brittle candy; it feels like cracking a thin shell over something damp that immediately tries to close. Within a few cycles the break is rimmed, then bridged. A different pattern grows there, as though the city has marked the wound and is repairing it with a slightly different stitch.

### 3) The third inhabitants: the “streets” themselves—capillary organs in basalt and clay

The corridors aren’t built to be walked; they’re built to *conduct*.

Basalt slabs, porous when dry, become a network of capillaries when wet. Clay underlayers swell and relax like muscle tissue, opening and closing microchannels. The grid—those repeating right angles—begins to read less like a plan and more like anatomy:

- long straight “avenues” are preferred flow lines, places where seepage is most efficient;
- gutters that were never installed are simply the easiest drains the system discovered;
- plazas are basins: pressure equalizers for a saturated shallow body.

The city’s quiet, heavy settling is not merely weight. It’s adjustment. Saturation shifts, load redistributes, and the stone answers with a slow flex you feel through your soles. Over time those adjustments become *regular*. Not clockwork—something more like breathing.

It is hard, standing in it, not to interpret the hiss of steam and the irregular drip as a kind of language. If it is, it is not speaking *to you*. It is speaking to itself: feedback between heat, water, and mineral.

### 4) The fourth inhabitants: the drops that hesitate

In the tighter district—where condensation never stops—water behaves as if the air has learned to hold it a fraction too long.

Drops form, swell, and then…do not fall.

They hover, slide sideways, join other droplets at improbable angles. They choose seams. They find old joints and move along them with the slow certainty of things following nerves.

These drops are not individually intelligent, but they are not random either. Each one is a carrier: dissolved minerals, microscopic spores, fragments of film. A droplet is a traveling colony-in-waiting. It delivers chemistry, seeds, and salt to exact locations—because the surfaces themselves “ask” for it by being slightly cooler, slightly rougher, slightly more thirsty.

When a bead slides sideways instead of down, it’s obeying a local rule the way a blood cell obeys a vessel. The city’s geometry is off-kilter; gravity is sometimes negotiated. Water has adapted to that. Water is the courier that makes the misalignment livable.

### 5) The last inhabitants: the misaligned reflections

The mirror-black puddles in the deep district are not mere pools. They are interfaces.

They show you the street behind you because they are not reflecting *this* arrangement of space; they are reflecting an adjacent one—the city’s other fit, the version that sits a fraction of a turn away from itself. The place’s seams are not just cracks in stone; they’re boundaries between near-identical layouts.

Something lives in that near-space, or rather: something *is able to inhabit both*.

It does not climb out. It does not take a body. It expresses itself as continuity across the mismatch: the way an avenue returns to itself; the way sound arrives early or late; the way an archway refuses to be reached until you stop wanting it.

If the biofilms are the city’s skin, and the salt is its bones, and the capillary grid is its organs, then this misalignment is its nervous system—the part that lets it maintain coherence even when its own map doesn’t agree with itself.

You can feel it watching not through eyes but through correction. The city doesn’t notice you as a person. It notices you as a disturbance: a warm, stepping pocket of displaced water and air that introduces new pressure and new temperature into a finely balanced cycle. And then, quietly, it compensates.

A drip that was irregular becomes regular for a while.
A puddle edge grows too clean.
A salt bloom thickens on the side you keep brushing past.

Not to trap you. Not to greet you. Simply because the system is alive enough to heal around what passes through it.

### What, then, lives there?

A city that is also a wetland organ: self-building, self-sealing, self-correcting.

Life in it is mostly:
- **microbial and mineral**, slow as deposition;
- **distributed**, with no single creature to point at;
- **expressed as process**, not as motion.

It’s a place where “inhabitant” means *whatever can persist in heat, brine, darkness, and warped distance*—and what persists best is not an animal, but a collaboration between water and stone that has learned, over countless rain pulses, how to keep itself going.

The emptiness you hear—the spacious quiet—isn’t the absence of life.

It’s life without bodies loud enough to announce themselves.
