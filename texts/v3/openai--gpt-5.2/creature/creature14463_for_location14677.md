What lives here is not a single population so much as a layered tenancy—each layer taking a different meaning from the same slow water, the same silt, the same leftover geometry. If you stay long enough, you begin to notice that “inhabited” doesn’t mean “busy.” It means *claimed*, in small, persistent ways.

At first it seems like nothing lives here except corrosion.

The district is full of the kinds of motion that don’t feel alive until you’ve watched them for a while: a warm ribbon rising from a grate with the patience of a plant; a lamp that never quite stops humming; the steady, uncountable labor of salt undoing metal. The machine that the neighborhood has become—leaking brine, holding its thermal layers, making its private weather—behaves like an organism that lost its mind but kept its autonomic functions. It circulates. It sheds. It maintains boundaries. It *breathes* with the tide through broken culverts.

And once you admit that, you start to see its dependents.

### The first residents: films, crusts, and the upholstered metal
Life here begins as a skin.

Bacterial films take the paint as if the paint were a kind of soil. They spread in the lee of handrails and under the lips of bolted signage, where the current combs gently and never scrapes clean. They aren’t bright, not in this light—more a faint internal pearl, a low-grade willingness to glow when disturbed. Touch a panel and the bloom answers, briefly, like a shy animal blinking.

Some of them eat what the district produces: iron, sulfur, the faint chemistry of old industry still seeping from seams. They turn rust into habitat and habitat back into chemistry. Where the interior rain falls in thin curtains, these films grow differently—cleaner edges, brighter patches—as if the droplets are a kind of seasonal event. The rain doesn’t nourish so much as *edit*: it selects what can cling and what will be washed away.

Mineral bloom joins them, a second, not-quite-living tenant—bone-pale, velvety, encrusting the undersides of ledges. In places the two become inseparable: biofilm trapped under mineral lace, mineral lace anchored by biofilm. It’s hard to say where biology ends and geology begins; that ambiguity is one of the district’s signatures.

### The silt people: burrowers without burrows
If you watch the fines long enough, you notice they’re not uniformly still.

The silt shifts in slow drifts, but also in small, local collapses—tiny sinkings and puffs that happen without any visible current change. Under the settled ash of decades, there are channels. Not the big trenches that used to be roads, but hairline paths: worm-things, slender and almost transparent, pushing through as if the silt were water and the water were air. They don’t make permanent tunnels—the fines won’t hold them—so their homes are more like habits than structures.

What they eat is mostly what falls: flocculent particles, dead gel colonies, the perpetual snow of grit rubbed from walls. Their presence is easiest to see under a working lamp: a brief stippling of movement, a patch of silt that looks as though it’s thinking.

Where warm plumes rise, the silt life concentrates. Heat makes chemistry faster. Faster chemistry makes more edible gradients. In the mild warmth near a cracked vault, the bottom is slightly more alive—not lush, never lush, but *busier*, the way a kitchen floor is busier than a hallway.

### The boundary-layer hunters: fish that live on a line
The clearest “animals” in the way people usually mean are the ones that use the district’s strangest rule: the layered water.

Small pale fish—nearly uncolored, as if the sea forgot to finish them—hang along the shimmering boundary where cold clarity meets warmer haze. They do it with minimal effort, bodies angled just so, letting buoyancy do the work. They pick at what collects on that line: drifting microorganisms, fine organic threads, the occasional speck of something richer that has no business being here and arrived by accident.

They are not schooling fish. Schooling would require abundance. They appear singly or in pairs, spaced apart like punctuation. When startled they don’t bolt into open water; they vanish into infrastructure—through a cable bundle, behind a rib of collapsed wall—where the geometry breaks pursuit into a thousand wrong guesses.

There are longer things, too: eel-thin, jointless silhouettes that thread through the lattice as if the lattice were a reef. They don’t *swim* so much as *solve* space, choosing gaps with unsettling precision. If you only glimpse them, you can tell yourself they were hoses. If you glimpse them twice, you stop believing that.

### Drifters that snag and bloom: the soft colonies
The district receives visitors on the tide—gelatinous colonies that arrive as if carried in from another ocean entirely.

They catch on rebar and chain links and the teeth of corroded grating, and for a while they make the place look ornamented. Their weak pulsing is less like a heartbeat than like a lamp trying to remember how to be bright. Some carry faint luminescence; in the blue-black they look like slow sparks pinned in place.

They do not settle permanently. The next strong inhale of the bowl, the next exhale, the next shift in the district’s private currents—gone. What they leave behind is not a shell but a smear of nourishment, a brief enrichment that wakes the films and silt-feeders into a small, local feast.

In that way, the drifters function like weather, too: not residents, but a recurring season.

### The machines that never stopped being hungry
There are also occupants that aren’t alive and still behave like they are.

Some pumps and sealed loops still move fluid, and not always cleanly. They leak warmth in the same way a body leaks heat, creating microclimates that everything else arranges itself around. A warm jet from a grate is a kind of hearth; a heat exchanger’s slow output is a kind of sun. The district’s few still-burning lamps are not just light sources but *territory*, each one defining a cone of readable space that becomes a feeding ground for whatever can tolerate illumination.

And there are sensors—old industrial ones, newer improvised ones—encased in cages, strapped to columns, mounted where someone once needed data. Some are dead. Some are alive in the limited way a forgotten instrument can be alive: blinking, recording, emitting a tone no ear here is tuned to hear. Around them, growth patterns differ. Biofilms thicken where there’s a faint vibration. Small fish hover where a low-frequency hum gathers plankton into swirls. The machines, by continuing, accidentally farm the ecology.

### The air-pocket dwellers: life in the inverted ceilings
The district’s trapped gas pockets are rooms turned upside down: ceilings that became floors, floors that became lids.

In those mirrored bubbles, certain small things thrive—tiny crustaceans and insectlike swimmers that need a place to cling where silt can’t smother them. They graze on the scum line at the water-air interface, living under the bubble like bats under a bridge. When the interior rain forms, it forms there first—condensing, beading, shedding—so these pockets are both shelter and weather engine.

Sometimes the bubble itself becomes a boundary worth defending. A fish will hold station under it, mouth angled up, picking at whatever collects at the line. Something eel-thin will patrol the perimeter, not entering, just ensuring the bubble remains a private ceiling.

If a bubble is disturbed—if it wobbles and belches a little gas—every resident reacts as if the room has shifted its furniture.

### The rare large guests: people who don’t move like fish
Humans come here, but not as a community in the usual sense. More like comets.

They arrive in quiet craft, or in careful pairs, following mooring lines as if following scripture. Their lights are different—whiter, more decisive—and they carry with them a kind of temporary gravity. Silt wakes under their fin strokes. The district’s living skin retracts where it can and endures where it must.

What they do varies. Some salvage: they cut cable, unbolt sealed housings, retrieve instruments that should not still be working. Some come to check the domes and cages that were added after the flood—pressure rooms that can still be entered, still used as staging points, still kept nominally safe. A few come for reasons that look like maintenance but feel like devotion: they clear a grate; they replace a fuse; they grease a hinge that no longer needs to swing. They behave as if keeping the district’s autonomic functions going is an obligation inherited with no paperwork.

They never stay long. The place doesn’t welcome lungs.

But the district remembers them anyway: in the scraped-clean arcs where a tool passed; in the sudden new brightness of disturbed phosphor; in the way certain fish learn that a particular kind of light means particles will rise and food will follow.

### The real dominant resident: a distributed patience
If you try to name what *most* lives here, you end up naming something that isn’t an animal and isn’t a machine, but a collaboration between the two and the water itself.

It’s the district’s tendency to keep making conditions—layers, rain, warmth pockets—and for other things to adapt to those conditions until they become a web of dependence. The inhabitants are specialists in low energy: feeders on gradients, hangers-on of boundary lines, scavengers of whatever the tide forgets.

Nothing here expands quickly. Nothing here conquers.

What lives here is what can accept a ceiling of moving water, a street made of silt, and a light that arrives mostly as rumor—and still find, in that hush, a way to persist.
