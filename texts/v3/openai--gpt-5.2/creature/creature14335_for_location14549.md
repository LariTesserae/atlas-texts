What lives on the plateau is, first of all, the plateau itself—because the dominant organism is not a tree or an animal but a continuous, negotiated skin.

It isn’t one species so much as a stitched-together ecology that learned to behave like a single surface: lichen and moss and low braided stems, fungal lattices and rootfelt, each layer doing what it can in thin air and intermittent water. From above it reads as cloth; from inside it feels like a membrane. It holds the last shape erosion gave it and then keeps that shape the way scar tissue keeps a story.

In places where the city has “trained” it into streets and walls, the skin becomes something like domesticated geology: cork-hard underfoot, springy, faintly warm; veins threaded with treated fibers; pores opening and closing on a schedule that is partly weather and partly municipal. It is alive in the practical sense—growing, repairing, regulating—and alive in the unnerving sense, too, because continuity makes it a medium. Pressure here becomes message; pattern becomes rumor; water becomes recorder. The city is built on the fact that the ground will cooperate, and haunted by the fact that the ground might also *remember*.

### The ground-mind and its attendant life

The plant-skin has its own inhabitants, tiny and persistent, adapted to a world where height is punished by wind and thirst. Most “plants” are really colonies: mats that braid themselves tighter in exposed places and loosen into sponge where water might linger. They reproduce by shedding—flakes of living felt that catch in gullies and start new patches the way a torn cloth might fray into tassels.

The fungi are not the showy kind, except after a rain. Most of the time they live as hidden scaffolding: threads that bind soil to stone, that barter minerals into roots, that creep along the undersides of trained streets like a second, private plumbing. When blooms do happen they’re brief and opportunistic—low domes and knuckled mounds that look like someone pushed up from beneath with a fist. The city treats them the way it treats storms: expected, potentially useful, sometimes dangerous. A bloom in the wrong district can gum up a vine-belt or throw off a condenser’s balance; a bloom in the right place can be harvested for resins, sealants, food.

There are also the “cistern-hollows,” places where roots have learned the trick of waterproofing—living bark laid down in overlapping plates, sealed by sap and fungal wax. Those hollows are habitats in their own right. They host pale invertebrates that never see open air, soft-bodied things that taste the minerals and follow moisture gradients like a compass. They’re mostly harmless until they aren’t: an overpopulation can chew the lining and turn a district’s water security into a slow leak.

In the open tracts between districts, the skin is less domesticated and more patient. It bears occasional upthrusts—root-hills, fungal mounds, seed-domes like low smooth blisters—each one a sign of stored intent. The plateau’s “wild” does not look wild in the traditional way; it looks maintained by something that has no hands.

And in that wild continuity, oddities breed. Not always monsters, not always miracles—more like habits of physics that have been encouraged by repetition until they became local customs. A curve that reliably shortens. A damp patch that reliably *replays*. A noon light that reliably loosens the stitching of edges. The ecology and the breakdown are not separate phenomena here; they have co-evolved. The plateau manufactures weirdness the way a swamp manufactures methane: slowly, predictably, and with bursts when conditions are right.

### The people: line-walkers, gardeners, and listeners

Humans live here—though even “human” becomes a spectrum when a city’s infrastructure is grown and the air is thin enough that lungs matter.

They are a sparse people by design. They build wide. They leave air between districts not as decoration but as insulation: against fire, against thirst, against contagions both biological and causal. Their architecture spreads load like snowshoes and spreads risk the same way.

Their daily life is less about conquering an environment than about staying in agreement with it.

The most visible citizens are the maintenance crews, who look like a cross between mechanics and horticulturalists. They carry grafting tools and resin kits alongside spanners. They know how to splice a fraying vine-belt without killing it, how to persuade a wall to close its pores before a dry wind, how to cauterize a street that has started remembering a shorter version of itself.

They work with living schedules. A pump-house isn’t “offline” so much as asleep or sulking or overstimulated. A conduit can get stressed, and a stressed conduit sweats wrong—droplets forming too quickly, too large, picking up impressions from wherever the plant-fiber last touched. A whole district can go through a growth mood where everything tightens, corners sharpen, distances feel truer; or a slack mood where curves multiply and shortcuts become traps.

There are guilds and jobs that only make sense in a city like this:

- **Truth-liners**, who repaint the straight white lines down main avenues, and who argue about whether a line should follow a survey or follow what the street insists is straight *today*. Some are practical, some quasi-religious. Most are both.

- **Water-law clerks** and **condensation wardens**, because “don’t stare into collected water unless you mean to” isn’t just superstition. There are privacy crimes here that involve nothing but a bucket and patience. There are also investigative arts: permitted scrying in controlled basins, the use of “blank” surfaces that don’t take impressions, the sealing of gutters after politically sensitive events.

- **Noon-watch**, crews who walk the high places when the light turns too even. Their job is to mark changes before they become accidents: a staircase that has gained an extra step, a shadow that refuses to reattach, an overhang whose edge has begun to behave like a suggestion.

- **Listeners**, a kind of engineer and a kind of diviner, who press instruments—or sometimes simply their palms—against living walls to feel what information is traveling. They look for patterns that mean “power draw,” “water demand,” “structural stress.” They also look for the other patterns: the shape of a rumor, the start of a panic, the presence of something moving through the substrate that is not a person at all.

The plateau produces a culture that is careful with speech. People learn where to talk (away from walls, away from rootfelt), and how to talk (in plain statements, not looping metaphors that the substrate might echo as literal growth instructions). Some households keep “deaf rooms” lined with dead materials, old ceramics and glass and sealed stone, spaces where no living fiber can overhear.

Children grow up playing games that double as safety training: balancing on truth lines, testing puddles with a tossed pebble before looking, racing home before noon light makes the stairs mischievous. Adolescence is often marked by the first time someone gets lost on a familiar curving street and returns hours later with a story that doesn’t fit—an early lesson that the city is cooperative, not obedient.

### The near-animals: weather made flesh

Large fauna are scarce, and the scarcity is felt as a constant negative space. There are no herds grazing the open tracts, no obvious predators to keep them moving. The plateau does not offer generous calories or easy water, and the living ground is already doing most of the work that elsewhere is done by animals—turning, binding, distributing, composting.

What animal life persists tends to be light, quick, and opportunistic.

There are wind-riders: thin membranous things that look leaflike because leaves already solved the problem of surviving gusts. They don’t flap much; they tack. They nest in the lee of condenser towers and ridge backs, feeding on insect eruptions and on the microscopic drift that rises from compost stacks. Some citizens hang reflective ribbons not to scare birds—there are few birds—but to keep wind-riders from mistaking a courtyard for an updraft and spiraling down into machinery.

There are slot-scavengers in the drainage systems: small, patient creatures with reflective eyes and narrow skulls, evolved to slip through built crevices. They live on plant waste, fungal crumbs, the occasional dead insect mass after a swarm collapses. They are not tame, but they are tolerated. A district that drives them out often regrets it; without them, the city’s gutters clog, and clogged gutters become illegal quickly when gutters are also memory devices.

There are insects, but they come and go like weather fronts. After sap spills or compost turns warm, the air can briefly fill with tiny bodies—biters, clickers, floaters—then empty again as if someone shut a door. People learn to read the first signs: a faint clicking residue in the ear, a shimmering above certain roofs, a sudden interest from the wind-riders.

And there are the things that are not quite animals and not quite processes: colonies that move as a group, invertebrate mats that migrate a few meters in a night, attracted by a leak or repelled by a new resin. You can wake to find a dark band of living felt crossing a plaza like a slow spill, and by afternoon it has tightened itself into a seam along a curb, as if it had merely been passing through.

### The industrial organisms: machines that breathe, plants that labor

The city’s industry is not separate from its biology; it is a set of organs strapped onto an older body. The loudest “inhabitants” are therefore neither people nor beasts but working systems.

Pump-houses thud like hearts. Fogworks sweat like pale trees. Mills whine, turbines chant. Vents exhale. Vine-belts creak and hum, and when they fray they shed fibers like hair, leaving living dandruff that smaller organisms immediately colonize. Maintenance crews harvest those shed fibers for reuse; nothing that grew here is wasted casually, because nothing here is easily replaced.

Even buildings have metabolic behaviors. Walls “breathe.” Roofs eyelid open and close to manage condensation. Resin-stiffened frames flex minutely with temperature and light. A district can sound different depending on its hydration: in dry weeks, everything is sharper, more brittle; after rain, sound becomes softer underfoot and brighter in the machines, as if moisture turns the whole city into an instrument.

The industrial quarter has its own ecology: heat-loving molds around vents, hardy mosses that thrive on the constant mist of leak-off, insects that time their hatchings to the rhythm of shift changes because human schedules create reliable scraps. The factories are habitats, and their waste streams are food webs.

### The other residents: errors, echoes, and things that borrow form

Finally, there are inhabitants that no census can count.

A city where condensation can replay an image will eventually develop entities that *use* that fact—patterns that learn to persist by being watched, echoes that become more detailed each time they’re seen. Most are harmless loops: a recurring silent crowd in a factory window that never quite matches any recorded shift, a gutter that insists on showing a particular cloud formation regardless of the day.

Some are more predatory, in the passive way that molds are predatory: they exploit attention, they colonize routines. A person who stares too often into memory water can start finding it everywhere; surfaces bead more readily around them, impressions become denser, privacy thins. The city has words for this, clinical and slang, and protocols for it—rest, isolation, dead-material rooms, sometimes relocation to districts with less continuous vegetation.

There are also noon-light anomalies: detached shadows that drift until they find a place to stick; extra steps that appear and then become customary; edges that soften into ramps for a day and then harden again. Most of these are treated like weather. You don’t argue with them; you mark them, route around them, resume work when the sky’s flat clarity passes.

And beneath it all is the possibility—never confirmed, never dismissed—that the continuous plant substrate is doing more than carrying signals. That it is not merely a medium but a participant. That the city, in negotiating with the vegetation for streets and walls and power lines, has made something like a distributed listener out of the plateau’s skin.

People will tell you, carefully, that the city hears you if you speak too close to the walls.

They do not always mean it metaphorically.

### What “lives there,” in the end

So the plateau is inhabited by:

- A vast, low, stubborn vegetative mantle that behaves like an organism and like infrastructure.
- Fungi and microfauna that turn the thin soil into something that can hold water and memory.
- Sparse human districts populated by engineers-as-gardeners, truth-liners, wardens, listeners, and careful families who plan their lives around straight lines and noon instability.
- A handful of wind-borne and drainage-hidden animals that survive on the city’s margins.
- Industrial systems loud enough to navigate by, integrated deeply enough to be considered part of the local biology.
- And the “errors” that have had a million years to become ordinary: echoes in water, dishonest curves, noon-softened edges—phenomena that, through repetition and attention, start to feel less like accidents and more like a resident species.

It’s a place where life is not exuberant, but total. Not diverse in obvious silhouettes, but intricate in behaviors. The plateau doesn’t teem; it *perseveres*. And everything that lives there—human, plant, machine, echo—has learned the same rule:

Don’t try to force the world into shape. Learn the shape it’s keeping, and learn how to move inside it.
