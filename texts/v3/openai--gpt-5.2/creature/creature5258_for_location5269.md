What lives here is not a list of species so much as a single way of being, repeated until it looks like a crowd.

At first you call it “plants” because they are rooted in place, tiered, layered, photosomething. But the longer you stand in the pocket and let the constant machinery write its tempo into your bones, the less botanical the inhabitants feel. They are not trying to reach light. They are trying to complete a circuit.

### The ground: the Mat that remembers
The thing underfoot—the laminated, resilient flooring that is also alive—is the oldest resident, or at least the one that has rewritten the pocket most thoroughly. It is a mat-organism built from mineral grit, polymer-fiber, and salt crust, fused into sheets that behave like a skin.

It grows the way a callus grows: not by pushing outward into new territory, but by thickening where stress repeats and thinning where it doesn’t. Every step you take becomes data in its body. The “rebound” you feel isn’t elasticity in the ordinary sense; it’s the mat settling back into a pressure-map it prefers, the way a tongue returns to a familiar tooth-gap.

If you peel a corner back—if you can find a corner that hasn’t already sealed itself to rock—you don’t find soil. You find laminae: pale layers that crunch like dried paint, dark layers that smear like graphite, clear layers that snap like brittle plastic. Between them are hair-thin seams of active chemistry: films that smell of solvent and stone, where the mat digests its own dead and re-precipitates it as structure. Decay here is not rot but reprocessing.

It has no visible organs. Its “mouth” is surface area. Its “blood” is a slow, glass-clear gel that migrates a few millimeters at a time and then hardens into a new seam.

### The reeds: bundled-cable stems that drink air
Above the mat rise the stiff stems—those cable-bundles that lift themselves into the airflow. They look like reeds because that’s the closest shape-language we have, but reeds are about water and sway. These are about pressure.

Their bodies are sheathed in that resin-skin you noticed: a polymerized epidermis that does not breathe in and out so much as admit and refuse. Along their length are valves you can’t quite see until the compressor pulse comes and the stems tighten, minutely, in unison. When they stiffen, a faint ticking runs through the pocket: not insect noise, but synchronized microvalves stepping through a cycle.

If you cut one (and the place gives you no encouragement to try), it doesn’t bleed. It emits a clear viscous film that seals the wound the way a machine gasket seals—fast, neat, chemically indifferent. The cut end becomes glassy within minutes, and the plant reroutes internally as if injury were just another kind of scheduled shutdown.

These stems feed the way lungs feed: not on oxygen, but on whatever thin reactants the ductwork provides—trace organics, ammonia-like compounds, ozone-edges from microscopic arcing, mineral aerosols abraded off the cliff and carried in obedient currents. Inside, the stems are not full of sap-vessels but membranes and baffles. Gas goes in, gets sorted, and comes out slightly different.

### The hanging panels: scale-curtains that turn waste into light
The broad panels pinned to the walls—thousands of overlapping scales—are less like leaves and more like radiators or filters. Each scale has a hard, varnished face and a softer underside dusted with that steady bluish shimmer.

It isn’t a lure. Nothing comes. It isn’t a signal. Nothing answers.

The glow reads as a metabolic compromise: if you can’t afford warmth and you can’t keep water, you get rid of energy in the only ways left. Some of it becomes chemistry. Some of it becomes structure. Some of it leaks out as photons because photons are cheap compared to heat loss in thin, hostile air. The light is so uniform because it’s not performing; it’s venting.

These panels are also the pocket’s archive. Their scales grow by layering—thin mineral films laid down from the air, resin films laid down from within—so each panel is a slow accretion of the pocket’s composition over decades and centuries. If you knew how to read them, you could tell when the machinery ran hotter, when the duct air carried more metal, when the mountain exhaled different salts.

### The threaders: resin-spinners that build the pocket’s skeleton
In the dimmer cracks, where rock meets old steel, live the organisms that make the threads: clear filaments drawn out of their bodies and fixed to metal and stone. They do not catch prey. They do not net anything living.

They are builders, and the pocket is their construction site.

The threads brace catwalk fragments, seal microgaps, drape over sharp edges to blunt them, and stitch loose debris into the mat so nothing shifts. They are moisture-barriers in a place where moisture is an error state. They are insulation. They are also, subtly, wiring: some of the thicker cords have embedded conductive salts, and when they lie across a vibrating conduit you can feel—if you put your fingers close without touching—a faint static prickle.

The threaders don’t move in a way you can track. They extend, set, and stop. Days later, a thread you thought was slack is taut. Months later, a brace has been replaced with a new one laid a fraction of a degree differently, as if the pocket were forever re-tensioning itself around its own hum.

### The tar-lichen: the black gloss that eats old electricity
On broken glass insulators and rusted junction boxes, the glossy black “lichen” is another kind of resident: a skin-thin colony that thrives where trace current and mineral dust meet. It looks like poured tar because it spreads like liquid and hardens like lacquer, but it’s alive in the way a battery is alive—gradient-dependent, surface-bound.

It loves edges: the snapped ceramic, the chipped glass, the corroded copper. Where a phosphor strip glows behind a grate, the tar-lichen thickens nearest the faintest leakage, as if it can taste electrons the way ordinary life tastes sugar.

If the pocket has anything resembling a scavenger, it is this: not eating bodies, but eating the last usable differences in an abandoned system.

### The unseen majority: films in the ducts, colonies in the seams
The most abundant inhabitants are the ones you don’t really see because they are too thin to cast shadows.

Every surface that vibrates has a resident biofilm tuned to that vibration. In the conduits, a membrane grows like a second lining—soft, pale, tubular—whose job is to condition the passing gas: to strip, to concentrate, to neutralize, to exhale back something the reeds and panels can use. In bolt-holes and around ladder rungs, microscopic colonies live in the resin-salt composites, cycling minerals the way tidepools cycle salt—except the tide here is pressure, and the sea is air.

These organisms are why the pocket can be lush without being damp. They hoard what little liquid chemistry exists inside sealed tissues and films, never letting it bead, never letting it evaporate, treating “wetness” as waste.

### And the closest thing to an animal: the pocket itself, as a distributed body
Nothing here moves with appetite. Nothing has eyes. But the pocket is not inert.

When the compressor pulse comes, everything answers: tubes stiffen, scales click, threads tighten, mats subtly lift and settle. It’s hard not to feel that you’re standing inside a single, distributed organism whose nervous system is mechanical rhythm.

Whether that “organism” has intention is the wrong question. It has coordination. It has persistence. It has a homeostasis so complete it looks like design even if it began as accident.

If there is a “mind” in the pocket, it is not a thinker hiding in the sealed hatch. It is the loop itself: fan-tone as heartbeat, pressure as breath, resin as scar tissue, salt as bone. The inhabitants have learned the shapes of conduits and vent-covers the way a shoreline creature learns the shape of a tidepool—by surviving only where that shape repeats.

So what lives here?

A lung made of plant and polymer.
A skin made of mineral and solvent.
A reef built on dead industry.
A community that doesn’t hunt or flee because its predator—weather, wetness, change—has been excluded, and its sun is a machine that never stops turning.
