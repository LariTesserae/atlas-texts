What lives here is mostly not a cast of creatures so much as a way of being alive in public—alive while exposed to glare, to wet, to noise. The place selects for organisms that can either become architecture or hide beneath it, and for any animal clever enough to treat the landscape not as “ground” and “air” but as a layered machine of surfaces, pores, and resonant chambers.

### The dominant resident: the living substrate

The mats are not one plant. Up close they resolve into a stitched commons: tiny scale-leaves from one lineage, filamentous threads from another, gelatinous films where algae and bacteria do the slow accounting of minerals and light. In the top few centimeters the mats behave like skin—photosynthetic, textured, shedding water in a controlled sheen. Beneath that, they behave like muscle and lung: they swell, vent, tighten, relax.

They are full of engineered conveniences that no engineer designed.

- **Pores that open and close** with humidity changes, not as stomata do on a leaf but as valves do on a pressure system. When wind leans on a mat field, the whole surface can “bleed” air in thousands of tiny hisses, preventing lift the way a parachute has spill-holes.
- **Mineral teeth**—little hard nodules precipitated from seep-water—that anchor the raft into cracks in the young stone. These are the mat’s nails. In places you can find the evidence of a failed grip: a chunk of mat torn loose, the mineral teeth still embedded in it like grit in a root ball.
- **Slow internal canals** that move water not downhill but toward need: into pillar bases, toward drying edges, away from saltier seeps. The mats don’t drain so much as *allocate*.

The mats are habitat first and plant second. Most of what “lives here” lives inside them.

Under the buoyant carpet is a dim, buffered world of steady temperatures and muffled sound. There are pale, sightless things down there—small arthropods that feel their way through fiber by vibration, wormlike grazers that rasp biofilm, soft-bodied filterers that pulse in place and sift the constant seep-water. Nothing down there needs speed. The mat makes the weather irrelevant; the wet is guaranteed, the light is rationed, the predators are few.

If you cut a cross-section (people sometimes do, for diagnostics, for harvest quotas), the mat shows layers like a manufactured product:
- a bright green photic skin,
- a spongy middle full of air pockets and stored water,
- and a dark underside where decomposition is slow, oxygen is scarce, and the chemistry turns patient and sour.

That underside is where the mat makes its bargains with stone—acidifying a seam, softening a mineral edge, freeing nutrients. The land is young; the mats are how it learns to be soil.

### The pillars: wind organs with vascular intention

The pillars look like plants until you stand close enough to hear their internal work. Their thick stalks are latticed, not solid—bundles of fiber arranged around hollow cores. The wind makes those cores sing, but the singing is not merely incidental. The pillars *benefit* from the wind.

They drink by noise.

Not literally, but functionally: the resonant cavities create pressure differences that pull mist-laden air through hanging sheets and into condensing surfaces. In calm weather the pillars are thirstier; in a gale they are lush. Their leaves are less like blades and more like membranes—broad, tough, veined with channels that route water toward the pillar’s base, feeding the mat around it like a cistern feeds a town.

Around each pillar is a ring community. The pillar’s base is too wet, too oxygen-poor, too chemically active for ordinary roots. So the surrounding lower plants specialize in:
- **oxygenating the moat-water** with slow bubbling photosynthesis,
- **binding silt** into firm rims,
- and **capturing salts** so the pillar isn’t poisoned by its own drinking.

If the mat is a raft, the pillar is a mast and rain-catcher, and the whole cluster is a self-maintaining installation.

And because these clusters are spaced—deliberate-seeming—each one becomes a landmark to every moving animal in the region. They are islands not only in the mineral gaps, but in the mental maps of anything that navigates.

### The small fauna: tuned to brightness and sound

Large animals can’t afford to be careless here. The openness is too exposing, and the constant roar erases the informational advantage of listening. So the creatures that persist are either:

1) **Wind-users**, who treat the air as a road; or  
2) **Under-mat dwellers**, who treat the mat as a ceiling.

The pale birds you glimpse are not built for long, flapping flight. They are shaped for control: dense plumage that resists wetting, broad tails for braking, and a stern, economical nervous system that can read wind shear the way a fish reads current. They don’t sing—there’s no point. Their calls are short, physical noises: clicks, snaps, the sound of wings struck in a particular pattern. If they communicate, they do it at close range inside the hanging vine-sheets where the wind’s voice is damped.

Their nests (when there are nests) are not cups but **pockets**—stitched into the undersides of leaf-membranes, insulated by the pillar’s warmth and protected from the sky’s glare. The eggs are few and thick-shelled, tolerant of chill and wet. The chicks fledge quickly or not at all.

Insects exist, but they behave as if sound is predation. Many are low-frequency sensitive—feeling the world’s vibration through their legs—and they avoid the open shelves where the wind turns stone into drumhead. The ones you meet are either:
- **tiny and quiet**, living in the humid calm between mat fibers, or
- **boldly armored**, capable of clinging to slick rock and feeding on mineral biofilms that nothing else can use.

Some are transparent or milk-pale, not from cave evolution but because pigment is expensive under such harsh light and constant washing. Others are dark and glossy, using melanin as both armor and heat-capture, warming themselves in sudden patches of noon sun.

There are also things you almost never see: small amphibian-like bodies that never fully leave water, sliding through under-mat channels; predatory larvae that ambush by sensing pressure changes; minute crustaceans in the shallow pans, turning those isolated tremoring surfaces into whole, self-contained food webs.

The signature of animal life here is not footprint or call but **patterned disturbance**: a section of mat that has been combed in one direction; a vine-sheet with a line of neat punctures; a set of tiny, repeated splashes where something rose for air in the same spot, again and again.

### The unseen multitudes: the chemists

If you asked what *truly* lives here, what owns the place in terms of numbers and influence, you would end up talking about microbes.

The constant seepage through raw mineral seams makes the region a laboratory for bacteria and fungi that can eat stone, precipitate metal, and turn thin trickles into nutrient streams. They paint the rock in subtle colors: rust lines, black slicks, iridescent sheens that are not oil but life.

Some of these microbes are partners of the mats and pillars; others are opportunists that colonize any wet surface, including metal and concrete. They are the reason “stainless” becomes filmed and why bolts grow furry halos. They don’t corrode everything quickly enough to look like ruin, but they are always negotiating with human materials, slowly rewriting surfaces into something more hospitable.

There are even microbial communities that live in the *sound* indirectly: they thrive in consistently aerated foam-lines where wind-driven turbulence keeps oxygen high. The land’s music sorts chemistry. Where the wind whistles hardest, the biofilms are different.

### The human presence: listeners, tenders, and those who stay indoors

Humans do not “settle” this place so much as maintain a series of footholds. The industrial stations imply a kind of residency that is intermittent but persistent: crews rotating in, specialists arriving for a season, technicians who know a particular pump’s cough the way a shepherd knows a particular goat’s limp.

The people who live here longest—weeks, months—acquire habits that feel like adaptations.

They move by **walkways** where possible, not because the ground is impassable but because the mats are alive and valued and because stepping wrong can break a floating field’s anchor and start a slow drift. They learn to read the land’s wetness the way others read snow: a darker rim means depth; a trembling sheen means a skin over a pocket; a certain moss texture means “safe” or “sink.”

They wear gear designed as much for sound as for water. The wind’s chord can exhaust you. Long-term residents prefer sheltered corridors between pump houses and pillar clusters, using vegetation as baffles even when it fails to make the world quiet. They speak close. They touch shoulders to be understood. A lot of communication is gesture, habit, and scheduled check-ins over cable rather than shouted conversation.

There are three broad types of human occupant:

- **Tenders**, who treat the stations as living things that need daily attention: cleaning intake screens, clearing biofilm from certain valves while cultivating it in others, adjusting clamps around pillars so growth is guided rather than damaged. Tenders have a practical intimacy with wetness. They can tell you which seep runs “sweet” and which will scale a pipe in a month.

- **Harvest crews**, who arrive when the mats are thickest and the fibers at their strongest. They don’t clear-cut; they shear, skim, take only what regrows. Their work is closer to forestry than farming, but with the ethics of reef management: take too much and the whole system shifts.

- **Listeners**, though they may not call themselves that: engineers and ecologists who monitor the region by its sound. Because the place is so consistently noisy, change becomes audible. A turbine’s pitch drifting into the wind’s register means bearing wear. A pillar’s boom acquiring a rasp means internal collapse or insect infestation. A mat field’s hiss pattern changing means drought stress or gas buildup. People here develop an ear for this the way sailors develop an ear for weather in rigging.

Some stations have a permanent caretaker or two. Their rooms are small, sealed, warmed by machinery waste heat, always fogging at the edges. Personal objects are weighted. Papers are laminated. Tools are hung to dry but never truly dry.

And there are rumors—there are always rumors in places like this—of someone who left a rotation and didn’t go back down, who lives in the gap between two ridge stations, trading fiber and filtered water for parts, sleeping under a pillar’s leaf-sheets because the constant noise makes solitude feel less like loneliness. Whether such a person exists matters less than the fact that the landscape makes the story plausible.

### The integrated residents: life that uses the machines, and machines that learn the life

Over time, the boundary between “infrastructure” and “ecosystem” thins.

Certain bird species nest preferentially on the lee side of turbine housings because the vibration deters some predators and the waste heat dries feathers. Some insects lay eggs in cable insulation where warmth and steady humidity mimic under-mat conditions. Biofilms colonize intake grates in ways that improve filtration—until they clog it—so crews cultivate a controlled slime the way brewers cultivate yeast, scraping and reseeding.

The clamps and rings around pillars are not neutral. They become new surfaces. The mats creep up concrete footings, stitch themselves around bolts, and in doing so they stabilize structures against frost heave and saturated ground shift. Some stations quietly rely on this. You can find places where a walkway’s “foundation” is, in practice, a living mat thickened into a load-bearing pad.

The machines, in turn, alter selection pressures. Pillars near harvesters grow tougher fibers. Mats near pump stations evolve more robust anchoring nodules to resist the microcurrents created by intake flow. You can walk from one installation to another and see the plants change slightly in form, like dialects.

Nothing here is dramatic in the way of predators or monsters. The drama is in persistence: in how life occupies raw geology so quickly that stone barely gets a chance to be empty, and in how humans, arriving late, do not tame the place but become another set of careful constraints within it.

### If you stand still long enough

If you stand in a bright mineral gap and refuse to move until your eyes stop searching for “animals,” you start to notice that the region’s true inhabitants are processes wearing bodies.

A mat field lifts and settles with the wind like a slow lung. A pillar’s hanging membranes darken as they load with water, then lighten as they drain, like a sail being reefed and released. A shallow pan’s surface trembles not from breeze but from something beneath rising to sip air and sinking again.

Even the humans—if they cross the open rock—move like a maintenance routine more than like explorers, stepping from island to island, checking, adjusting, listening.

What lives here is whatever can tolerate being continuously rinsed and continuously sung to, whatever can make use of wetness without drowning in it, whatever can treat noise as weather and brightness as pressure. The land is still being assembled, yes—but its residents are not waiting for completion. They are the assembly.
