What lives here begins too small to see.

At first it’s only the sense that every wet surface is *busy*—that the shine on the stone isn’t just water, it’s a population. The rocks wear a living glaze: films of algae and bacteria and things that are neither quite plant nor fungus, layered like skins. If you drag a fingernail across the blue-black stone you don’t get grit; you get a slick peel, a translucent ribbon that snaps back into place, and within minutes the scraped line clouds over again as the film reknits.

In the thinnest water—where a “puddle” is only a lens over rock—there are motes that move against any current you can imagine. Not larvae, not dust: seed-sized grazers with bodies like commas, flexing through the warm sheet in sudden darts. They rasp at the biofilm with mouthparts too fine to be visible and leave behind paler trails that vanish as the film swells back.

This is the first kind of resident: the **mat-makers**, the ones that turn stone into habitat without turning it into soil.

They exude gels that hold water as if it were stitched into fabric. They precipitate minerals in soft, stubborn ways—little crusts, little freckles—that don’t build up into hard limestone or crumble into sand, but hover in between: a perpetual “about to become rock” texture. Their waste is food for the next layer. Their bodies are the next layer.

And under all of it, threaded through the felt of roots and microbial sheets, there is a second presence—wider than any single organism, hard to point at because it’s mostly *connections*: a pale, corded network like mycelium, but tougher, more elastic, more deliberate. If you lift a corner of the wet felt you can sometimes see it tightening, drawing inward, as if the ground doesn’t like being opened.

### The plants: arrested giants and hungry flowers

Above the films and cords, the greenery looks exuberant until you realize how many of its forms are compromises—growth that has been allowed to spread but not to *commit*.

There are trees, but they are not ambitious. They thicken. They brace. They make surface. Their bark is sealed and glossy as if varnished by constant wet, and on that bark live whole gardens: liverworts stacked like scalloped shingles, orchids that never grow tall but keep producing thick, water-filled leaves, and long curtains of moss that drip in steady punctuation. The trees seem less like individuals and more like **anchors**—posts driven into a living carpet so that everything else can hang, crawl, and pool.

Lower down, the dominant plants are the ones that treat water as architecture.

- Broad-leafed platforms with waxy skins channel condensation into their own roots and into basins formed at their stems.
- Ferns unfurl into perfect, mature symmetry and then *stop*, holding their fronds indefinitely, neither collapsing nor converting into woody height.
- Vines extend, yes, but they do it like fishing line paying out: reaching, looping, re-looping, never quite strangling their supports, never quite making the leap from curtain to conquest.

The flowers are not bright; they are engineered. Many are cups with slick inner walls, collecting rain that isn’t rain. Insects slide in, drown, soften, and the petals close slightly—not snapping shut, just leaning—until the body has been absorbed. The scent is subtle but insistent: spice over rot, sweetness under green, a note that pulls small things in and keeps them there.

The plants here do not merely tolerate the crowding; they seem built for it. Leaves overlap in ways that create safe undersides—humid caverns where eggs can be glued and never desiccate. Stems are often ridged or hooked, inviting other plants to use them as ladders. It’s a vegetation that has evolved to live as a **single interlocked mass**, less a forest than a woven object.

And that woven object is not passive. When something tears it—when a branch breaks, when an animal rips through—the wound doesn’t gape. It beads. A clear sap wells up, thickens, turns cloudy with microbial bloom, and within a day the tear has a new seam: flexible, damp, scar-like, stronger than the original tissue.

### The insects: air as habitat, sound as weather

The air is full because the air is *livable*. Warmth, moisture, and constant plant surface mean every height is an ecosystem.

Closest to the water sheets are the skimmers—thin-bodied, long-legged insects that touch down and lift off as if the water were solid enough to take a suggestion. Their feet leave brief dimples that rebound instantly. There are also midges and gnats that form columns in the dim, each swarm a moving organ: they rise and sink as if breathing, and when a predator passes the column tightens into a dense knot and then relaxes again.

On the leaves are the chew-and-lick guilds: beetles with heavy shells and surprisingly delicate mouthparts; caterpillars that graze in slow, meticulous arcs; ants that don’t so much march as **flow**, carrying pale, soft pellets—fungus-food—back toward the breathing mounds.

The constant chorus isn’t just many species calling; it’s many species *working*. Wingbeats, stridulations, clicks from mandibles, the papery patter of something shedding a skin and letting it fall. In this place, sound functions like a second humidity: always present, coating everything.

And because there is no real wind, pheromones and spores linger. Many insects here navigate by smell more than sight. Some have feathery antennae so broad they look like extra wings, constantly sampling the soup of air for specific threads: sap of this plant, rot of that pool, alarm of that ant line, musk of that frog.

### The amphibians and water-things: bodies made for warmth

The frogs are everywhere, but they’re not all “frogs” in the way you expect.

Some are squat and heavy, the size of a human hand, with skin like wet stone and calls that thump through your chest. Others are tiny and loud, perched on leaf-tips to broadcast into a world that doesn’t carry sound far. A few never leave the water sheets at all—flat-bodied, almost fishlike, with limbs that seem ornamental until they launch themselves in sudden, snapping leaps to seize passing insects.

In the runnels live things that keep the channels honest: eel-thin predators that can thread upstream through water that barely moves, their bodies pressing against the slick rock as if adhesion mattered more than swimming. They hunt by vibration. A footstep nearby makes them vanish under the microbial glaze, leaving only a tremble in the water and then nothing.

There are also grazers: tadpole-forms that never fully become frogs, remaining as suction-mouthed, algae-scraping juveniles for years, their metamorphosis delayed indefinitely. It’s as if “becoming” is optional here, and many creatures opt to stay in the stage that fits the held world best.

### The birds (and the almost-birds): short cries, quick lives, constant watching

The canopy isn’t high, so the flying residents move through corridors rather than open sky. They flash between trunks and vine curtains with wings that make a soft tearing sound against wet leaves.

They don’t sing long songs. They call like alarms, like coordinates, like brief declarations of territory that don’t need to last because everything is so close. Many have oversized eyes and matte plumage that drinks the dim light. Some wear colors you only see when they tilt: oil-slick greens, bruised purples, the dull gold of damp pollen.

They eat insects, fruit, frogs, each other’s eggs. Nests are everywhere but often hidden under leaf platforms or suspended beneath branches in woven sacs that drip steadily—designed to stay wet without molding, designed to shed parasites into the water below where something else will eat them.

Not all of them are true birds. Some are gliders—membrane-winged climbers that launch from trunks and steer through the humidity like thrown cloth. Their calls are more like clicks, and they land with splayed toes that cling to wet bark without slipping.

### The larger animals: few, careful, built to pass without breaking

Big herbivores would normally be the architects of a wet lowland—trampling, wallowing, carving paths into mud—but here they exist as if under constraint.

There are creatures that resemble small deer or tapirs in silhouette, but with wide, splayed feet and skin that sheds water like polished fruit. They move slowly, placing their weight with practiced precision, choosing the root-mesh mats that will rebound instead of tear. When startled they don’t crash away; they freeze, then slip into the vegetation with surprising silence, leaving the foliage to close behind them.

Predators do exist at this scale, but they are ambushers rather than chasers. A long-bodied hunter lies half-submerged in a shallow sheet, eyes just above the surface, body almost indistinguishable from rock shine. Another waits in the vine curtains—limbs folded, skin patterned like fungal blotches—and strikes with a single efficient motion. There are no long pursuits. The terrain doesn’t reward them, and the system seems to dislike damage that spreads.

Scavengers are constant: crabs that shouldn’t be this far inland, pale snails the size of a fist, roach-like things that strip a carcass down to clean bone in hours. But even bones don’t last long. They soften. They’re bored through. They’re converted into calcium for eggshells, for fungal nodules, for the mineral freckles that dull-glint in the stone.

### The mound-builders: houses that are lungs

Those termite-like mounds are not simply nests; they behave like organs pushed up from underground.

They are warm to the touch. They sweat. Their surfaces open and close with tiny pores that you can’t see move, but you can see the effect: a faint shimmering of humid air rising in steady exhalations. Inside, insects (and insect-adjacent things) cultivate fungus gardens the color of old milk. They feed the fungus leaf pulp and dead bodies; the fungus feeds them; both feed the surrounding mat. The mounds vent gases, regulate moisture, and—most importantly—keep decomposition *cycling* rather than finishing.

If you listen with your ear against a mound you hear a low, continuous rustle, like distant rain: millions of small bodies working, chewing, grooming, passing pellets hand-to-mouth in the dark.

### The true resident: the binder beneath everything

After a while the question stops being “what species are here?” and becomes “what is holding this all in place?”

Because the most consequential inhabitant isn’t a frog or a vine or a predator. It’s the **binder**: that distributed, interwoven living system in the ground-mat and on the stone skin.

It behaves like a fungus in some ways—threaded, networked, hungry for sugars—but it also behaves like a reef organism, mineralizing and sealing without building upward. It senses disturbance as pressure changes and chemical shifts. Where something begins to cut—where water tries to gather enough force to carve, where a root pushes hard enough to crack—this binder responds by thickening, by exuding gel, by stitching the attempted change back into the equilibrium.

It does not prevent life from being violent in the small ways—predation, competition, constant eating. It prevents violence in the large ways: the kind that would let one process become a lever.

- When water starts to focus into a cutting flow, the binder swells along the channel bed, making it slicker, broader, more like a vein than a knife.
- When plant litter threatens to become true soil, the binder accelerates the loop—turning rot into feedstock for growth before it can compress into history.
- When a plant tries to become a tree that would shade everything else and start a succession, the chemistry in the mat shifts. Growth hormones change. The plant’s next stage never quite triggers. It remains lush, mature, *finished*—but not transformative.

You can see hints of it at work in the way wounds seal, in the way puddles keep their depth, in the way stones stay smooth. It’s as if the landscape has an immune system whose purpose is not to keep invaders out, but to keep *time* from taking hold.

Whether this binder is “one organism” or a conspiracy of many—microbes, fungi, root symbionts, mineral-precipitating films—doesn’t feel like a meaningful distinction once you’ve watched the ground respond. It is a resident in the same way a mind is a resident: not located in one place, but expressed everywhere.

### How the inhabitants meet you

Nothing here greets you. The place doesn’t flare into alarm at your presence; it simply adjusts around you.

Insects sample you immediately—landing, tasting salt, lifting off. Tiny leeches test the edge of your boot. Spores cling to sweat. The sounds shift in small bands as predators notice new vibration patterns and the prey-things compress their swarms tighter. A frog stops calling for three seconds and then resumes, because the system cannot afford silence.

If you cut a vine, sap beads and clouds; within minutes, gnats gather to drink it, and ants arrive to harvest the sugary leak, and the wound begins to seal under a bloom of microbes that smell faintly like yeast. If you dig, the felt resists in one cohesive sheet, and the pale cords beneath tighten, drawing the edges inward as if the ground is trying to close its own skin.

These are the residents, ultimately: countless individual lives crowding every surface, and beneath them the quiet, binding life that makes sure none of their struggles ever add up to a new chapter.

A chorus of creatures, yes.

But also a single, vast habit of staying the same.
