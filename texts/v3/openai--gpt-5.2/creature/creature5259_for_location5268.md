What lives up there is not a single species so much as a solution—life pressed into a set of constraints until it stops looking like “an ecosystem” and starts looking like a continuous, crowded mechanism. If you try to picture individuals first, you miss it. The plateau reads, at scale, as one organism with many skins: a dry sea that circulates, feeds, sheds, and heals itself along the mountain’s slow pulse.

At the very bottom of it—before claws, before armor, before anything with a face—there is the living varnish.

### The living varnish (the first occupancy)
Around the vents and along hairline fissures, the stone carries a film so thin it doesn’t have thickness until it’s disturbed. It’s dark not because it’s “black,” but because it eats light the way it eats chemistry. It lays itself down where two things meet: hot gas and cooler rock, sulfur breath and metallic crust, heat from below and ash-dark from above. It doesn’t need water the way surface life does; it hoards what little hydrogen it can steal from gases, binds it in stubborn oils, and makes its own slow internal wetness—sealed, recycled, never offered to the air.

When it is healthy it looks like polish. When it is stressed it crazes into microscopic scales, and the plateau takes on a faint, patchy sheen as if someone has oiled the world and then wiped it almost clean.

This varnish is grazed constantly. Not in the way a meadow is grazed—no leisurely mouths, no visible bites—but by abrasion: raspers shaving the surface, leaving behind stone that looks freshly unvarnished and strangely naked, as if it ought to sting in the heat.

### The raspers (armored conveyors, patient eaters)
The most common “large” bodies are the ones you already half-saw: low, plated creatures whose backs are segmented into overlapping shields. Their motion is not a scuttle but a ripple, each segment lifting and setting down in sequence, so that the whole animal advances like a slow belt pulled over hidden rollers.

Underneath, they aren’t soft. Their undersides are a field of hooklets and gripping pads hardened with mineral salts—enough to cling to warm basalt as if the stone were textured cloth. Their mouths are not at the front where you’d expect; they’re tucked under the leading plates, protected from grit and from the heat jets that would dry anything exposed. A rasper feeds by laying its belly to the rock and working a ring of mineral teeth in tiny circles, polishing the varnish off the surface and swallowing both film and the finest loosened grains of crust.

They don’t drink. Their bodies are sealed and lacquered; their internal solvent is a thick, oily brine that behaves like molten salt more than blood. When one is cracked, the smell isn’t rot—it’s a hot, sharp note like resin on a stove, plus the bitter tang of metals.

Raspers aren’t solitary. They like contact. In the murk they park against one another in stacks and drifts, using shared warmth and shared darkness as a kind of shelter. A piled mass of them can look like a single boulder until it shifts as a whole, each shell responding to some signal you can’t hear.

### The bead-eyed mats (the “sand” that is alive)
Between the plates, in the cracks, along the edges of depressions, the plateau’s true density moves: thousands of hard points the size of seeds, each a little body with a hard, glossy carapace and eyes like polished beads. They do not behave like a swarm so much as a fluid that has learned purpose. They pour around obstacles, split and rejoin, and leave behind trails that look like wind-marks until you watch them form against the wind.

They’re the plateau’s scavengers and its janitors. They find a fracture in a shell, a flake of shed plate, a dead wing, and they don’t so much “eat” it as disassemble it: releasing small amounts of solvent, softening edges, carrying grains away inside themselves. They are how nothing is wasted in a place where replacement is expensive.

When they communicate, it’s mostly by touch: antennae tapping, bodies bumping, the faintest vibration transmitted through stone. In a quiet that deep, even a tremor is loud—so they keep it small and local, like whispered hands.

### The drifters (thin wings, heat-sailing bodies)
Above the ground layer, always near but rarely seen clearly, are the membrane-winged things. Their wings are not built for flapping in thin air. They are built like sails: papery, translucent, with stiffened veins that can cup or flatten to catch a rising stream. Their bodies are narrow and dense, and their legs end in tiny grapples for landing on hot rims and sheer walls.

They are economical to the point of austerity. They climb on thermals from vents, spiral in slow columns, then peel off and glide to another warm seam. They make almost no sound—just that brief tick you noticed when two membranes brush.

They don’t glow to be pretty. When a drifter shows light, it’s usually internal process leaking through: a dim chemical bruise under the thorax that flares when it’s stressed, when it needs to be found by its own kind in a gust, or when a predator’s shadow crosses it too closely.

The drifters are the plateau’s messengers in the only way the plateau permits: they carry chemical traces on their feet and bellies, picking up the scent of one fissure and laying it at another, turning heat-roads into a connected map.

### The long-limbed geometry crawlers (predators that measure)
Then there are the ones that make the place feel watched even when you can’t see far enough to be watched: the long-limbed crawlers that move as if the ground is fragile. They are taller than everything else, but not tall—knee-high at most, often less—yet their shape grants them authority. Their bodies are narrow, lifted, with legs that articulate in many joints and end in pads that can mute their contact with the stone. They place each foot as if following an invisible grid. They never commit all their weight at once.

Their antennae are long and restless. They do not seek scent in air; they seek vibration in rock. They pause with their feelers laid against a warm slab, and you can almost imagine them reading the plateau’s traffic like script: the distant rasping of grazers, the minute shift of bead-mats, the hollow movement of something heavy crossing a crystal collar.

When they strike, it is not a lunge accompanied by sound. It is a sudden collapse of distance—legs folding and extending in a single, silent geometry—and then stillness, the prey held under the predator’s body where it can be opened with careful, efficient tools.

What tools? Not fangs, usually. A predator here can’t afford to spray wet venom or spill internal fluid. Many of them use dry chemistry: a heated secretion that sets like glue, a powdery enzyme carried in a pouch and dusted onto a seam, a clamping mouthpart that can crack lacquer without spilling what’s inside.

### The dissolvers (slow mouths for hard waste)
Somewhere between scavenger and predator are the dissolvers: squat, blunt creatures that look like moving knobs of stone until they unfold. They are drawn to dead bodies, to shed shells, to crystal collars thick enough to matter. Their mouths are wide and ridged, and they feed by heat and patience—pressing themselves against material and leaking just enough solvent to soften it, then rasping it up into their own sealed guts.

They’re part of why the plateau’s remnants look “assimilated.” Anything left long enough becomes edible surface. Even old stone rings get licked clean by dissolvers over years, edges rounded not only by erosion but by hunger.

### Reproduction: eggs like sealed minerals
Nothing up there can leave its young exposed to air for long. Eggs are not laid in nests of plant matter; there is no such luxury. They are sealed capsules tucked into warmth: wedged in fissures that breathe gently, glued under overhangs where gas flows but grit does not scour, hidden beneath piles of raspers whose bodies form a living incubator.

Many young hatch as something else—small, pale versions that don’t yet have lacquered shells. For a brief stage they are soft by necessity, protected by the crowd. Then, after feeding on varnish and mineral salts, they harden, darken, and seal, joining the plateau’s immense armored majority.

Metamorphosis is common because specialization is everything here. One body can’t afford to do all tasks at once. A feeder becomes a drifter; a scavenger becomes a rasper; a soft larva becomes a hard point in the bead-eyed mats. The plateau doesn’t need individuals to persist; it needs roles to remain filled.

### The “rules” and the wardens (why predators don’t strike at the arch)
The place also has something rarer than any body: enforced restraint.

That basalt-plate arch—the suggestion of a doorway—functions like an artery. Traffic funnels through it, and the stone beneath is clean not because wind sweeps it, but because it is constantly, obsessively used. Over time, a path becomes more than a path. It becomes a law written into pheromone and vibration.

There are creatures that seem built to maintain that law.

They are not the biggest animals on the plateau, but they behave like something with jurisdiction: dog-sized, low and thick, with heavier armor than the raspers and a head shield shaped like a wedge. Their plates are dull until they choose otherwise—until they heat themselves along interior channels and make their seams faintly glow, a warning without sound.

These wardens (if you can call them that) patrol near the arch and other old constructions. They do not chase prey. They do not graze. Their mouths are adapted for gripping and pushing, not eating: they can hook under a stone, lever it, and set it back. They can shove two fighting bodies apart. They can block a predator’s approach with their own mass and armor, and they can do it without escalating into the wasteful chaos of a general fight.

How do they “tell” others not to hunt there? Not by threat displays alone. By laying down a chemical boundary that lingers on rock—dry, bitter, persistent—and by producing a low-frequency tremor through their bodies that disrupts the subtle vibration-reading predators rely on. Inside the radius of that tremor, the plateau becomes noisy in the wrong way. Hunting becomes inaccurate. A strike might fail; a failed strike is a cost few predators can afford.

So the predators learn. Or rather, the ones that don’t learn leave fewer descendants.

The arch becomes safe not because it is sacred in any human sense, but because it is a stabilized pattern: a long-standing agreement between many species, maintained by a few that have evolved into the role of keeping traffic flowing.

### The built remnants: adopted architecture, remembered heat
Those old rings, steps, cairns, and slabs are not inert to the plateau’s life. They are incorporated.

A low stone ring becomes a corral not because anyone “decides” it should, but because it changes wind at ground level, traps warm air for a few degrees longer, and concentrates chemical traces. Once enough bodies use it, it becomes part of the migration map. The plateau remembers it. Even if the ring collapses, the memory persists as scent and habit, and the creatures will circle the same patch of ground for generations as if the stones were still standing.

The smooth ledge at the top of the steps—the place that looks touched by many hands—has probably been touched by many kinds of appendage. It is a gathering point. Not for ceremony, necessarily, but for exchange: drifters landing to swap traces, bead-mats meeting and sorting themselves like poured grains, wardens pausing to “listen” into the rock for shifts in the mountain’s pulse.

If there was once another builder here—something that made steps on purpose, stacked basalt plates with intention—the current inhabitants treat those artifacts the way reef life treats shipwrecks: as structure worth colonizing, as geometry that creates new currents.

And yet, in the way the arch is kept clean, in the way some slabs are left ungnawed while others are polished down to fresh stone, you can feel a kind of continuity. Not reverence exactly. More like inherited infrastructure. The plateau’s life behaves as though it has been living inside a framework longer than its current forms have existed.

### The hidden majority: the plateau’s “nervous system”
If you stay long enough, you begin to notice that many of the smallest movements are not random. The bead-eyed mats pause in unison. Raspers shift their weight at the same time across distances you can barely see. Drifters alter their spirals as if a signal passed through the air, but the air is stingy with signals.

The signal is in the rock.

Beneath the mineral skins and varnish, the plateau is threaded with living lines: thin, wormlike bodies in the cracks—blind, heat-seeking, coated in their own sealant—whose entire existence is to carry chemistry and information between warm points. They are not impressive to see, which is why they are powerful. They act like nerves in a body: distributing trace compounds, keeping the varnish healthy, warning of changes in venting, coordinating migrations when the mountain’s breath shifts.

A predator can kill a rasper. A storm of ash can choke drifters. But the crack-thread network endures because it is everywhere the stone is fractured—which is to say, everywhere.

That is why the place feels too small to contain what lives in it. The life is not only on top of the plateau; it is *in* it, threaded through it, using it as both habitat and organ.

### What, finally, lives there
A crowded hierarchy of sealed, mineral-bodied animals; swarms that behave like purposeful sediment; sail-winged drifters that turn heat into travel; predators that hunt by geometry and vibration; dissolvers that digest the plateau’s waste back into usable chemistry; wardens that enforce quiet corridors; and beneath it all a thin, patient, crack-dwelling network that makes the whole system cohere.

They don’t acknowledge an intruder because they cannot afford to. Attention is an expense. Sound is an expense. Even light is rationed.

What they do instead is more unsettling: they incorporate you the way they incorporate everything. Your boots become an obstacle to flow. Your warmth becomes a gradient. Your scent becomes a temporary landmark. If you stood still long enough, they would route around you with the same calm inevitability with which they route around a boulder—until, eventually, the plateau decided whether you were merely in the way, or whether you were made of something worth taking apart.
