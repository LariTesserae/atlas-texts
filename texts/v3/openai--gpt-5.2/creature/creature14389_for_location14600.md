What lives in the bowl is not a single inhabitant so much as a set of long-lived occupations—guilds of organisms that behave like trades in a town whose only currency is moisture and whose only law is persistence.

At first you notice the “citizens” that are easiest to name.

There are the mats: mosses that do not so much *grow* as *thicken*, each season laying another felted layer over the last until the rock is padded, insulated, and faintly springy underfoot. If you peel back a corner (you shouldn’t, but you can imagine it), the underside is darker, wetter, warmer—a slow compost that smells almost sweet in a place that otherwise smells like stone and cold metal. The mat is a blanket and a reservoir and, in wind corridors, a kind of sieve: it catches mineral dust shaken loose by vibration and holds it where water can dissolve it.

There are the lichens: the mapmakers. They do not compete by height because nothing can afford height here; they compete by *claim*. They spread in careful circles, each colony marking out a district whose boundary is decided by millimeters of advantage—an extra hour of shade, a slightly rougher grain of granite, a seam of darker basalt that warms faster in sun. Some are crusts so thin they look like discoloration until you realize the “stain” has edges, logic, neighborhoods. Some are frilled and leafy, lifting tiny folds to harvest fog the way a sail catches wind. In the quiet pockets, away from the loudest corridors, they dominate like old families in sheltered courtyards—slow, patient, hard to dislodge.

There are shrubs: not shrubs as you’d meet them below treeline, but shrubs that have been coached by weather into domes and walls. Their stems braid inward. Their leaves are small, dark, waxed; each one is a refusal to lose water. Under a dome the air is different—less moving, faintly warmer, smelling of resin and damp soil rather than open stone. These domes are the bowl’s rooms within the room, and other lives rent space there: seedlings that would die exposed, insects that would freeze in open wind, fungi that need a steadier humidity.

Then, after you’ve accepted that plants are the architecture, you begin to notice the lives that hide inside that architecture.

There are the fungal networks—more present than visible. They lace the moss like rebar and thread the shrub roots like wiring. Where roots have “bridged” gaps between terraces, fungi are the unseen rivets at the anchor points, turning separate roots into composite beams. In some places the fungal growth is so thorough that the soil is less soil than a living fabric: grains of grit stitched together with hyphae, unable to slide the way loose scree would. The fungi are not merely decomposers here; they are engineers of cohesion. They decide which cracks become gardens by deciding where nutrients can travel.

Within the moss live the small, durable animals that most landscapes forget to count: tardigrades that wait out drought by becoming almost nothing, rotifers waking when fog rewets them, springtails that hop through the velvet like fleas through carpet. There are mites in the lichen crusts, nematodes in the thin films of water that appear and vanish daily. Their whole world is the thickness of a fingernail, and it is enough—because it is stable, because it repeats.

Along the water threads—those bright, reckless rivulets—are the brief-lived opportunists: algae that bloom in a thin green sheen on stone when the fog is generous, microbial films that make the rocks slick in the sun, tiny larval insects in the rare pools that are deep enough not to freeze solid. The pools have their own quiet ecology: cold-tolerant invertebrates, bacteria that can metabolize in near-dark at the bottom, and the occasional drowned wing or needle that becomes an event of nutrition, a feast that lasts weeks in a place where nothing arrives quickly.

And then there are the animals big enough to cast a moving shadow.

The pale raven is not a resident in the way the moss is a resident; it is a commuter, a courier, a thief of whatever can be stolen. It reads the bowl as a street plan of wind: dropping in low to avoid the rim’s roar, tacking between ribs, riding the architectural howling with the ease of something that has learned the city’s traffic patterns. When it calls, the sound doesn’t feel like a voice added to the place; it feels like the place briefly speaking in a different consonant.

The alpine fox—if fox is what it truly is—is the bowl’s rumor made flesh. It moves as if it is borrowing space rather than occupying it, stepping only where stone is bare or moss is thick enough not to betray a scent trail. It hunts what there is to hunt: an unwary bird, a pocket of insects, perhaps the occasional small mammal that dares the terraces for a season and then vanishes. It does not mark territory with confidence. It passes through, taking advantage of the bowl’s rooms and corridors the way weather does.

Sometimes, rarely, ungulates leave tracks—goat or deer—careful, linear, as though the animal held its breath while crossing. They don’t stay. There is too little forage that isn’t defended by cold and wind, and too few easy exits if weather closes the rim.

But the bowl’s truest inhabitant—the one that makes it feel less like a place and more like a settled presence—is the thing you can’t point at.

It is the bowl as a tuned system: rock cavities that breathe, fluted ribs that chord the wind, root-bridges that hum when gusts strike their tension just right, moss that thickens where vibration shakes dust into its fibers, lichens that hold the quiet corners the way libraries hold silence. Over long time the living and the nonliving have arranged themselves into feedback loops: sound shapes moisture; moisture shapes growth; growth shapes airflow; airflow shapes sound again.

If you insist on a single “who,” it is not an animal and not a plant. It is a composite citizen: a superorganism made of granite, fog, fungi, and patience.

You can feel how it lives by what it rewards.

It rewards anything that can become infrastructure without intending to: a root that happens to span a crack and is then reinforced by years of surviving vibration; a shrub that leans into gale until leaning becomes its final posture and that posture becomes a windbreak for a hundred smaller lives; a lichen colony that finds a niche in the acoustic shadow of a rib and holds it for decades because nothing louder can outcompete it there. Even the “singing rock” is part of the living, not because stone is alive, but because life has learned to make use of the stone’s breath—placing itself where the exhale keeps frost from sealing a cavity, where the resonance keeps water moving, where the temperature swings are gentler by a few degrees.

So the bowl is inhabited the way an old city is inhabited when its builders are gone: by maintenance, by adaptation, by countless small tenancies that add up to something coherent.

Leave it, and you don’t feel that you’ve left an empty landscape. You feel that you’ve stepped out of an enclosed, ongoing conversation—between wind and rib, drip and hollow, root and gap—where the locals are not faces at windows but moss thickening in a loud corridor, lichen holding a quiet wall, fungus tightening its grip on soil, and a raven passing through like a piece of moving punctuation.
