What lives here is not an inventory so much as a set of tactics.

This high country doesn’t reward anything that tries to *occupy* space in the usual way—by building tall, spreading wide, staking a claim. The distances between usable patches are too deliberate-feeling, too often swept clean or reset overnight. So the residents become specialists in **edges**, in **intervals**, in **persistence without presence**. They live in cracks, under slabs, inside windless pockets that last an hour, in the few degrees of warmth that leak from certain stones, in the thin, repeatable mercy of midday.

And because the land itself behaves like a machine—phase changes, pressure changes, conveyance—its inhabitants do too. Not coldly. Efficiently. They’re shaped around cycles rather than growth.

### The first residents: films, crusts, and the slow claim of chemistry
Before you notice anything that moves, you notice what *won’t let go*.

On raw rock faces, life begins as a **skin**: microbial films you only infer from the way certain stones hold water differently, darken sooner, dry later. In sheltered hollows the rock has that faintly slick, living feel—like it’s been handled. These microbes don’t “green” the landscape; they **condition** it, making raw mineral slightly more hospitable, slightly less brand-new.

Then come the **lichens**, the true flag of residence here. They’re not decoration; they’re settlement. They spread as pale cartography across basalt ribs and granitic plates, their colors a restrained chemistry set: sulfur-yellow where the substrate offers it, gray-green where water pauses, ink-black where the sun never strengthens. In places, the lichen crust is the only thing binding the glassy grit into something that can hold.

Where water lingers—where the land sighs underfoot—**mosses** take over, not as soft cushions but as compressed pads, felted and practical. They are less “plant” than **sponge-and-anchor**, catching sediment, holding warmth by keeping water from leaving quickly. They turn temporary seepage into a location.

### Plants as heat-hoarders and mapmakers of hidden water
There are no trees because there is no tolerance here for leverage. Anything tall becomes a handle for wind.

So the vegetation stays close, round, inward:

- **Cushion plants** that look like stones until you kneel. They are little heat batteries, storing a few degrees of warmth in their density, surviving by reducing surface area and by making their own windbreak.
- **Braided grasses** that don’t so much “grow” as trace plumbing. They line melt seams and warm fractures as if someone drew the watershed in thread.
- **Tiny flowering bursts** in the briefest window—days when the cloud lid thins and insects wake. The flowers are small, blunt, almost impatient: open, offer, close. This is not a place for extravagance.

Taken together, the plants form a patchwork that reads like a diagram: if you see green, you’re seeing **water’s decisions**.

### Invertebrates: the brief workforce
Insects are here the way sparks are here—intermittent but real.

On stones warmed by seep-water, **dark flies** gather in clusters that make the rock look stained until they lift. They feed fast and breed faster, compressing whole generations into the narrow, damp intervals between storms or freezes. In the moss pads, **beetles** and mite-like scavengers move through the felted layers, breaking down what little organic matter exists. You don’t see a lot of them at once; you see evidence: tiny holes, frass-like dust, the sudden softening of a dead tuft of grass into usable soil.

There are also the quiet, cold-tolerant kinds: springtail-like jumpers in the saturated grit, larvae that overwinter under ice with antifreeze in their bodies, things that spend most of their lives *not* being active and then abruptly are, whenever the thermostat crosses an invisible line.

They are the connectors, turning plant tissue into something the next level up can live on.

### The talus residents: small mammals that treat rock as habitat, not obstacle
In the poured chutes of scree and the boulder fields that haven’t learned roundness yet, small mammals live with a competence that feels like a local law.

Pika-like creatures make the talus into a city: **tunnels of negative space** between stones, sheltered from wind, buffered from temperature swings. Their presence shows up as neatness—clipped grass stacked into tight haypiles wedged under overhangs; little runways in the grit; droppings placed in habitual spots. They are the ones who make the landscape feel “inhabited” even when you see nothing moving, because their work remains visible.

They also become one of the place’s main nutrient engines. They cut vegetation from seep-lines and carry it into the rock. They move nitrogen and carbon uphill and into crevices where plants can’t reach, and predators can’t always follow.

When the fog drops low, you might hear a single, dry call—more a notification than a song—then nothing, as if the mountain swallowed it.

### The ledge-walkers: ungulates engineered for vertical distance
The large herbivores here—goat- or sheep-analogues—seem less like animals placed into the terrain and more like the terrain briefly deciding to move.

They travel the long shelves and impossible angles with a calm born of anatomy: hooves that bite small edges, a center of gravity built for slopes, coats that shed wet and hold warmth, lungs that treat thin air as normal. They don’t fight the wind; they use it, turning their bodies broadside only when they need stability, tucking into lee pockets when the gusts pulse like machinery.

Their tracks are narrow but influential. In saturated ground they cut grooves that briefly become streams during rain. Along ridges they pack snow into lines that later guide meltwater. Even their droppings matter disproportionately: little dark packages of fertility dropped into a world that otherwise starves soil.

They are also faithful to boundaries—the seam between snow and stone, the margin of a warm seep, the fringe of a cliff where wind keeps the ground exposed. This makes them predictable in the way weather is predictable: you can’t schedule it, but you can learn where it likes to happen.

### Predators: made of patience and borrowed pathways
Predators here don’t announce themselves. They have no reason to.

There are the obvious aerial hunters—eagles or eagle-like raptors—who use the plateau’s updrafts as if the air were a conveyor belt. When one passes, it’s often registered first as a change in the birds’ behavior rather than a shape in the sky: a sudden flattening, a collective quiet.

And there are the ground predators, cat-like or wolfish in silhouette, that travel the same hidden lines the meltwater chooses: shallow gullies, fog-softened folds, the sides of boulder fields where footing is good but visibility is poor. They hunt by **reading movement against stillness**. In a place with so much open space between things, surprise comes from timing, from using mist, from appearing exactly when a herd has to cross an exposed shelf.

They leave little behind—no dens you find easily, no bones piled like trophies. Any mess would be wasted opportunity; scavengers are too efficient, weather too constant.

### Birds: the most visible citizens of the empty volume
The air is where the abundance shows.

There are swifts high above the plateaus, cutting through wet light with wingbeats you almost never hear. There are gull-like scavengers that patrol cliff colonies and talus margins, opportunists that understand the landscape’s resets: a cornice break means exposure, a rockfall means injury, a storm means someone will misstep.

On certain cliffs, colonies gather in numbers that make the sky look populated even when the ground looks empty. Yet even there the sound is restrained—less raucous shorebird clamor and more a constant, papery rustle, the friction of many bodies moving in damp air.

These birds do something important for the whole system: they bring the outside in. Their guano is concentrated nutrient; their dropped scraps seed insect blooms; their bodies carry parasites and spores from lower elevations. They are one of the few ways this high country gets imported richness.

### The warm fractures: life gathered around slight exceptions
The most crowded places are the ones that break the rules by a few degrees.

Where water emerges warmer than it should, even barely, the ground behaves differently: it stays pliable, it resists full freeze, it keeps tiny channels open. Around these seeps, you find the densest moss, the earliest insect activity, the most frequent hoofprints. In bad weather, you may find birds hunkered near them, and in clear weather you may find predators checking them like a schedule.

Nothing here needs miracles. A slight warmth is enough. A difference of **two degrees** is an ecosystem.

### How they endure the cycles (because the cycles are the true landlord)
Every resident shares certain traits, even across categories:

- **Low profiles**: not just physically, but behaviorally. They avoid becoming the tallest thing in any scene.
- **Water-proofing and water-hoarding**: coats that shed, skins that resist saturation, plants that trap water without rotting.
- **Rapid use of short windows**: growth, breeding, feeding—everything happens in bursts when conditions permit.
- **Fallback modes**: torpor, burrowing, clustering, hunkering in lee zones, riding out storms as if they were just another night.

In winter—or in the nearly-winter that passes for much of the year—the community doesn’t vanish. It compresses. Life moves from the open shelves into the talus, from the plateau into the fractures, from visible motion into stored heat and stored food.

### What “belongs” here, in the end
What lives here is a network of creatures and plants that accept that the land is still being assembled.

They don’t build monuments. They don’t tame distance. They **thread it**—moving along seams, surviving in pockets, treating mist as cover and rock as shelter and warm water as wealth. Their abundance doesn’t fill the emptiness; it **rides** it, the way the birds ride the updrafts and the glaciers ride gravity.

If you stand still long enough, the place stops looking empty. Not because more things appear, but because you start noticing the true pattern of residence: small, repeated marks of use—tracks that become streams, haypiles that become soil, lichen that becomes a foothold for moss, moss that becomes a platform for insects, insects that become the brief currency of flight.

The inhabitants are not trying to make the high country gentle.

They are making it *livable*, one boundary at a time.
