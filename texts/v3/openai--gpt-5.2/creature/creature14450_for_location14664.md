What lives there is not a single population, but a negotiated crowd—an arrangement between stone that is still cooling, air that has opinions, and a city that learned early that survival is mostly etiquette.

### The basin itself (the oldest resident, though still young)
The first inhabitant is the cirque as a body: a place still deciding its final posture. It lives in minute motions—fresh ribs of rock lifting like knuckles under the moss, hairline fractures breathing cold out of the mountain’s lungs, boulders that wake up with a new split down the middle.

It is not sentient in a way that speaks, but it is responsive in a way that remembers. It “knows” weight. It “knows” vibration. It teaches through consequence: step too hard in the wrong season and you’ll feel the warning travel under your feet hours before a talus fan decides to rearrange itself. The people don’t call that superstition. They call it listening.

### The air-moods (not quite weather, not quite animal)
Something else lives here that isn’t flesh the way a goat is flesh. The atmosphere behaves like a colony-creature: moods that gather, thin, drift, and return. After rain, when the fog thickens, the basin fills with that rising sheet of tiny filament-tailed bodies—neither gnats nor spores, but something between: living attention-stir.

They don’t bite. They don’t poison. They *tune*.

In their presence, the basin becomes more easily impressed, like a surface that takes fingerprints. Smell travels cleaner and farther. Small sounds register as if they were larger. Hidden things wake up. It’s as though the swarm is the basin’s sensory organ moving through its own space, sampling everything.

The city treats these swarms the way coastal towns treat tides: not as a threat, but as a condition you build manners around. Doors get veiled; conversations shrink down to taps and breath; certain work pauses because you don’t do loud human projects while the air is “looking.”

### The green pressure (plants as colonists, plants as architecture)
Vegetation is not background here; it is an insurgency.

Moss comes first, thick enough to soften stone edges into rounded syllables. Ferns follow, unrolling slowly like something tasting the air with its whole body. Vines are the basin’s handwriting—testing every crack, crossing bare rock with deliberate strokes until they find purchase and then, patient as a lever, they widen it over seasons.

Some plants have learned the city. They grow with human intention braided into them:

- Roof-gardens whose roots stitch thatch into a single mat.
- Vines trained as load-bearing ties, their living tension replacing nails.
- Broad-leaf canopies cultivated to deepen the dim in “kept shadow” places, as if darkness is a crop.

What lives in the plants is as important as the plants themselves: beetles layered under bark like hidden shingles; pale amphibians in leaf-cupped pools; moths that only commit to flight when light is at its most reluctant.

### The obvious animals (and the way they’ve been edited by quiet)
There are cliff-runners—shaggy, narrow-bodied herbivores with hooves or pads built for columnar ribs, animals that climb as if gravity is negotiable. They move in short, economical bursts and then stop completely, becoming rock-colored punctuation on the walls.

There are predators that keep the understory combed: long-bodied, low-slung hunters whose coats drink light, whose paws find the firm places in moss without asking permission. They don’t announce themselves. They appear the way a thought appears—sudden, already in motion, already vanishing.

Birds are present, but subdued. They do not waste sound on song. Their language is functional: wing-flicks, throat clicks, wood taps. They communicate in a manner shaped by a place where volume is a kind of spending.

And everywhere, the innumerable small citizens: mites, beetles, thin-legged insects that seem to be built from condensation and patience; soft-bodied things that live in the cold bowls of pooled water; seedpod-splitters and fungus-eaters working the margins of every surface. Their noises are so small you only recognize them once the larger noises have been trained out of your expectations.

### The shadow-tenants (the ones the city makes room for)
Then there are the residents no one points at directly.

In deliberately dark pockets—under overhangs, beneath platforms, inside the deeper ribs of scaffolding—you sometimes see movement that refuses detail: a silhouette where empty air should be, a passing thickness, a suggestion of a body made from the absence of reflected light.

The city does not treat these as ghosts in the storybook sense. They are treated like a kind of wildlife: acknowledged, accommodated, given corridors.

Those kept-dark spaces function like shrines and like crossings. People slow down there. They don’t carry lamps in. They don’t argue, don’t laugh loudly, don’t bring the sharp edges of themselves into it. The darkness is maintained as a resource—part sanctuary, part boundary marker, part treaty.

Whether the shadow-tenants are predators, protectors, or simply another expression of the basin’s living attention is something even long residents disagree on. But everyone agrees on the practical truth: if you disrespect those pockets, the basin becomes less forgiving. Not vindictive—just less willing to ignore you.

### The people (a culture built from restraint)
The human settlement is small because the bowl is small, but it is dense in craft.

These are people who have learned to build as if the land is still tender. Their city climbs and folds rather than spreads: terraces pinned into stone with wedges, platforms cantilevered where rock allows, ladders stitched between levels like vascular tissue. They rely on plant-work as much as carpentry—training living supports, braiding fiber cord, letting roots and vines do structural labor over time.

They are not ascetics, but they practice a kind of everyday discipline:

- **Soft footwear and softer walking.** Children are taught how to place a foot so moss doesn’t squeal slickly and stone doesn’t answer.
- **Quiet technologies.** Message-cords that can be tugged in patterns; muted clay chimes; tapping codes on posts worn smooth by thousands of hands. Trained insects carrying scent-markers between platforms—communication that rides the basin’s cleanly separated smells.
- **Weather-craft.** Gutters of hollowed wood for fog-drip; channels cut into stone that only run for ten minutes at a time and are therefore made to waste nothing; cistern jars that are shaded to keep water cold and sweet.
- **Death as participation.** Bodies placed in woven cradles suspended in certain trees, offered to carrion-beetles and careful birds. It isn’t spectacle. It’s composting with witnesses, a way of staying inside the basin’s cycles instead of pretending you can stand outside them.

They do not govern by proclamation so much as by custom. The most respected people are often not the loudest leaders but the best listeners: those who can tell, by the feel underfoot and the tension in the air, when a stone-fall is likely; those who can read the coming of the filament-swarm by a shift in how smells separate; those who know which shadows are crossings and which are only shade.

### The city as a single organism (built of many species)
If you stand back, the settlement itself looks alive in the same way a reef looks alive: not because any one house breathes, but because the whole structure is a habitat layered on habitat.

Warmth leaks from compost terraces in faint steam. Insects nest in the lashings. Small birds roost under bark shingles. Vines travel from roof to roof like neighbors exchanging hands. Human paths are also animal corridors by agreement—maintained emptiness so movement can happen without constant collision.

The “rules” of the basin—sound draws response, atmosphere has moods—have shaped the people into something like a local adaptation. Not weaker, not cowed. Simply precise.

### What you feel, if you try to summarize a population that won’t be summarized
A high, dim amphitheater of young stone hosts a community that treats attention as a physical force.

Living things here are not merely present; they are *careful*. The beetle closes its wing-cases with a wet click that sounds like a door being shut gently. The predator parts the brush without tearing it. The human child learns not to shout because shouting isn’t forbidden—it’s expensive.

And over all of them, threaded through fog and drip and the faint never-pluming smoke, something like a shared understanding holds: the basin is still becoming. Everything that lives inside it behaves as though the becoming can hear.
