What lives there is, first, the city itself—because in a place where water is routed like blood and pressure is timed like breath, “inhabitant” starts to mean *anything that persists by keeping pace with the cycles*.

The machines are not merely active; they are *habitable*. Every pump-note, every gate-drop, every warm jet is a repeating season. A living thing doesn’t need sunlight if it can predict a timetable. It doesn’t need soil if it can find a flange that always sweats the same chemistry. It doesn’t need a meadow if it has a baffle-shadow where turbulence forgets to reach. The city provides those, bluntly and without kindness, and what lives here has learned to treat brutality as climate.

### The first residents: films, stains, and the city’s slow appetite
On the sheltered faces of beams—places where the flow skims but doesn’t scour—you find the thinnest possible ecologies: skins that look like nothing until you tilt your light and realize the “damp” has texture.

They’re not lush mats; they’re negotiations. Biofilms that behave like varnish. Communities of bacteria that make a living on what the city bleeds: iron, manganese, hydrogen, trace organics leached from old seals and insulation. They take turns.

- When a valve cycle brings in more oxygenated water for a while, iron-oxidizers bloom as a faint tan haze, leaving delicate, smoky streaks like fingerprints on the concrete.
- When a compartment stagnates and turns chemically tired, sulfate reducers and other anaerobes move in and the smell (if you could smell underwater here) would be of old pennies and eggs and wet stone.
- In seams where warmer water seeps, the biofilm thickens slightly and becomes *slick*, as if the wall has been greased. Not to help you—simply because the chemistry is steady enough that “slightly thicker than a stain” is allowed.

These microbes don’t decorate. They *eat the city in patterns.* Where the water’s turbulence is relentless, they’re sanded down to nothing. Where the flow calms for even an hour each cycle, they return. They are the city’s slow pulse made visible: a living tide line that crawls a few millimeters, dies, crawls again.

In some corners, that black felt of packed silt isn’t purely mineral. It’s partly biological—dead film, shed cells, flocculated residues from decades of tiny lives finding the only way to accumulate in a place designed to prevent accumulation. It is the closest thing this city has to soil, and it is treated as such by anything that needs a bed to hide in.

### Clingers: animals that are shaped by holding on
The commonest animals are not swimmers. Swimmers get claimed by the center. The survivors are those that can become, temporarily, *part of the infrastructure.*

You see them as exceptions at first—one pale crustacean on a rivet head, one limpet-like shell on the underside of a handrail—but then you notice the logic: every animal is either a hook, a clamp, a wedge, or a thread.

There are small, blind arthropods—shrimp-kin, amphipods, things with too many joints—whose bodies are flattened not for speed but for reduced drag. Their legs end in absurd, delicate grapples. They live on the downstream sides of bolts and weld beads the way cliff insects live in wind: by knowing exactly where the air (here, the water) goes quiet.

They feed in brief windows. When a gate shifts and a long-quiet duct coughs out a pulse of particulate, they release their grips in unison—not to drift, but to *hop* from anchor to anchor, snatching at whatever the city has dislodged. Then they clamp down again before the next pressure change turns the corridor into a cannon.

Some have learned the city’s “weather” well enough that their migrations look like maintenance routes. They move along the hazard-striped edges, along the predictable laminar strips near walls, never crossing the loud open spaces. In a different world you’d call it instinct. Here it looks like reading a manual.

### Riders: creatures that treat turbulence like terrain
There are swimmers, but they are not built for swimming *against* anything. They are built to read the city the way gliders read sky.

The translucent fish you glimpsed—more nerve and gel than body—are part of a small guild of “riders.” They have oversized lateral-line organs and delicate fins that don’t so much propel as *trim*. Their muscles are modest. Their skill is in interpretation.

A rider will hover at the boundary between two flows—the shimmering membrane where temperature snaps and sound changes timbre—and make minute adjustments to stay pinned to that invisible edge. It feeds on whatever collects there: microfauna, flecks of organic residue, bacterial clumps torn free from walls. To cross a boundary is to pay a cost in energy and risk; to live on the boundary is to let the city bring food past your mouth.

Some riders are solitary. Others move in sparse, widely spaced chains—never schools, because schooling implies safety in numbers, and here numbers are just more mass for the current to spend. The spacing is practical: if debris comes through, it doesn’t take them all.

Every now and then you find a dead one plastered against a grate like a wet leaf. That, too, is part of the habitat: the city generates corpses the way a reef generates shells. Something else will pick at it, quickly, before the next cycle peels it away.

### The bubble harvesters: life that follows gas like light
In that district of strapped tanks and metered releases, the ecology changes—not richer, but more *organized*, as if the city’s chemistry briefly becomes legible enough for specialization.

When the infrastructure releases its beads of gas, a whole minor economy wakes up.

Tiny snails or snail-analogues—pale, almost glassy—cling near the vents. They aren’t grazing plants; they’re grazing films that grow better there because the gas changes the redox balance on the metal. Small worms thread in and out of mineral accretions, not eating stone but eating what *coats* stone when gas is present. Even the clinger-crustaceans congregate, not because it’s safe (it isn’t), but because it’s predictable: the bubble release is an event they can time their risks around.

A bubble is not just buoyancy; it’s information. It tells you a valve up-system has cycled. It tells you turbulence will change in a few minutes. Creatures here don’t have calendars. They have *bubbles.*

### Predators: not kings, just opportunists with patience
A place this harsh doesn’t support apex anything for long, but it does allow ambush—especially ambush that uses the architecture.

There are eel-thin hunters that live in pipe forests and cable trays, their bodies ribboning in the lee of beams. They don’t chase. They wait for a pressure shift to push prey into a narrowing corridor, then they snap once and retreat to their bolt-hole before the current can punish them for moving.

And there are things you notice only as absences: a run of clinger colonies suddenly gone from a favored rail; a drift of tiny shells cracked neatly, as if opened by a tool rather than crushed by chance. Predation here doesn’t leave gore. It leaves *cleaned* places.

Occasionally, something larger passes a doorway fast enough that the only certain detail is the way the sound changes—how the pump-thunder briefly gains a soft, living occlusion, as if a body just moved through the bass note. Those larger things might be residents. Or they might be visitors that enter when the seam opens, take what they can, and flee before the city rearranges its weather.

The city doesn’t care which. It is equally dangerous to both.

### The scavenged machines: the half-living maintenance that never got recalled
Not all inhabitants are biological, and not all of the non-biological ones are the city’s original organs.

Here and there, embedded in accretion like fossils, are the remains of tools and drones: an old inspection crawler fused to a catwalk, an ROV frame stripped of buoyancy foam, its thrusters long since seized. Some are dead as stones.

But a few are not.

A sealed housing can survive astonishingly long in the right pocket. A battery can be replenished (slowly, poorly) if it’s connected to the right galvanic mess. A timer can keep time badly and still keep it. In a city that already runs on faithful, stupid cycles, even broken automation can become a kind of animal.

You might see a lamp that shouldn’t still be lit, strobing faintly not because it’s failing, but because something upstream resets it every thirty-seven minutes. You might hear a *click*—not a relay in a panel, but a small mechanism in a corroded box that still opens and closes a vent the size of your fist. That vent creates a tiny, reliable eddy. That eddy becomes a feeding ground. Life gathers around the machine the way life gathers around a warm stone.

If you stay long enough, you begin to suspect a more unnerving possibility: that some of the city’s cycles persist *because* biofilms have colonized sensors and narrowed or widened certain thresholds. Not intelligence—nothing so clean—but feedback. The city “breathes” partly because living films have become gaskets, partly because mineral growth has become a cam, partly because corrosion has turned one timing into another. The inhabitants do not merely endure the mechanisms; in small, blind ways, they have become part of the mechanism’s continued behavior.

### The listeners: creatures for whom sound is not background
In a place where sound is the first law, the most successful senses are those that treat vibration as sight.

Everything living here listens with its whole body. The clingers can feel, through a bolt, a change in flow ten meters away. Riders detect the approach of a gate closure as a change in the city’s harmonics—an alteration in the standing waves that form in long corridors. Even the microbial films respond: there are bands where growth is thicker not because the chemistry is better, but because the vibration is steadier—because steady vibration keeps silt from settling on them, keeps competitors from taking hold, keeps their thin world from being smothered.

If there is anything like a “chorus,” it isn’t made of calls. It’s made of synchronized stillness. Whole stretches of small life will clamp down at once when the bass note shifts, as if the city has spoken a syllable meaning *incoming violence.*

### What you don’t find: comfort, greenery, permanence
There are no gardens. No waving fields. No soft animal abundance. Nothing here is allowed to get big without being taxed by drag, noise, and mechanical indifference. The city is constantly editing its residents.

So what lives there tends to be:
- small, or flat, or threadlike;
- attached rather than free;
- opportunistic rather than territorial;
- timed to cycles rather than seasons.

And yet it isn’t barren. It’s simply *strict.*

Life occupies this place the way a mold occupies a hinge: not by transforming it into something hospitable, but by finding the precise tolerances where it can exist without being torn off. The inhabitants are not building a new city atop the old one. They are learning to live in the wound without closing it.

If you press your hand to a vibrating wall in a rare quiet nook, you can imagine the city as an enormous animal and its residents as parasites, symbionts, skin flora—an ecosystem clinging to a heartbeat that doesn’t know it’s being used. Then a gate shifts somewhere, the water flinches, the rules rewrite themselves, and every living thing resumes the single most important action in this place:

hold on.
