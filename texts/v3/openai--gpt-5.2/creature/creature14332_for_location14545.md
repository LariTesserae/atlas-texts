Life down there begins so thin you can mistake it for stain.

It isn’t moss, isn’t lichen, isn’t anything that looks like hunger made visible. It’s a glaze—microbial films that cling where the stone sweats the right gases, where a hairline crack leaks a breath of sulfur or a whisper of carbon. They grow like patience: molecule by molecule, trading electrons the way surface life trades sunlight. Up close, the film has texture only because the mineral dust sticks to it; it’s less a carpet than a varnish that has learned a few tricks.

Everything else in the undercontinent is built on the fact that this varnish exists, and on the fact that almost nothing is allowed to be wet.

## The film-eaters: the first multitude

The most common animals are small enough that a person reads them as motion in the powder rather than as bodies.

Some are hard-shelled raspers—flat, low things that move like living scrapers. They cling to stone with pads that seal against the dust, and they feed by abrading that microbial sheen with mouthparts made of interlocking mineralized ridges. Where they’ve grazed, the rock looks newly broken, paler, as if someone cleaned it with a cloth.

Others are powder-swimmers that never need a “burrow” in the way surface animals do. The dunes are their ocean. Their bodies are shaped to flow through grains: wedge-fronted, ribbed, with valve-slits along their sides that open only when they must vent heat or scent. When disturbed they vanish without drama—one moment a little ridge of powder is moving against gravity, the next it is still, and the animal is simply elsewhere in the dune.

There are congregations you only learn to see indirectly. In some chambers the dust has a faint combed pattern, as if a careful hand had dragged fingers through it over and over. Those are feeding grounds: thousands of small grazers passing the same ledges night after not-night, polishing stone by their collective persistence.

They do not sing. But they click, and their clicking—dry, repetitive, almost like the settling of cooling ceramic—becomes part of the undercontinent’s background once you know to listen for it.

## The hunters of chokepoints

Predation here is not a chase; it’s geometry.

The most successful predators are long-bodied and spare, all limb and hinge. They choose places where the world forces movement into a narrow shape: a corridor that pinches, a seam you must sidle through, a ledge that makes you step with care. They wait with their bodies flattened into cracks, sensing vibration through legs spread like antennae. Their eyes, if they have them, are vestigial; what they “see” is the map a footstep draws in stone.

When they strike, it’s as if stillness was only ever a pose. A joint unfolds, a hook-limb snaps shut, a mouth like a split in armor clamps and injects a solvent that is not water—an oily, reactive fluid the predator makes and hoards the way other creatures hoard moisture. It softens membranes, it unlocks salts, it turns sealed life into accessible chemistry.

They leave little behind. In a place that mummifies, scavengers are efficient: smaller cutters arrive in minutes, then powder-swimmers, then the patient grazers that take even the last traces of film that blooms briefly on the leavings.

## The larger walkers: heat-followers and route-keepers

Where the chambers widen enough to allow a body to turn around, you find the undercontinent’s “large” animals—dog- to pony-sized, sometimes bigger in the rare cathedral voids.

They are built like moving containers. Thick plates or leathery skins layered over insulating fat. Wide, blunt heads that can shove aside rubble without exposing soft tissue. Their undersides are often pale and smooth, shaped to conserve internal dampness; their backs collect dust and carry it like camouflage.

They navigate by heat and by the planet’s tiny electrical moods. Many have broad thermal membranes—flaps or sails that can close tight in dry corridors and open near vents to drink warmth without drinking water. Their migrations are slow and astonishingly consistent. In a landscape that barely changes, the safest paths become inheritance. You can tell a traditional route because the powder is packed and faintly glazed with compacted pellets; because the walls at shoulder height are polished by passing flanks; because old shed plates lie in the same corners decade after decade.

They are not domesticated everywhere, but they are *known* everywhere. Smaller creatures draft behind them to take advantage of stirred dust and exposed film. Predators respect them the way you respect a boulder: not as a threat, but as something that can ruin your day if you misjudge it.

And sometimes—this is where the built marks begin to make sense—you see that these big walkers carry traces of deliberate handling: a notch filed into a plate for a tether to sit; a healed puncture where a soft-metal hook once pierced; pigment dust rubbed into a ridge to mark ownership or lineage.

## The ones who build: the Dry-Carriers

The makers are not everywhere at once. Their presence is like the presence of ships on a vast sea: you find harbors, not continents of settlement.

At first it’s hard to decide what they are, because the undercontinent pressures you into thinking in silhouettes—anything sapient must be either huge to matter, or tiny to survive. They are neither. They are roughly human-sized, but not built like humans. They are *sealed* the way everything here must be sealed.

They wear their biology the way surface people wear clothing: layers designed to keep an inside from becoming an outside.

- Their skin—where it shows—is dull and dense, with a waxed look, the pores almost invisible.  
- Their eyes are small or protected, sometimes covered by a clear membrane that can be shuttered against dust. In some lineages the eyes are secondary; in others they are keen but always treated as fragile.  
- Their faces are built around breath-management: narrow nostrils, internal baffles, sometimes external masks even when naked, because exhaling in this place is a kind of spending.

They smell faintly of heated stone and of whatever internal solvent their bodies rely on—an oily tang, resinous, sometimes metallic. When they bleed (rarely), it is thick and reluctant, reluctant to evaporate but eager to cake into protective scab.

You learn quickly that they do not travel “light.” To go anywhere is to carry your own atmosphere.

They bring ceramic canisters sealed with pitch-like compounds; bladders of reclaimed moisture; oil-reservoirs; folding wraps that can be pressed against the face to condense breath back into the body. Their tools are simple in principle—lever, wedge, hammer—but obsessively designed around sparing the soft and preserving the sealed. Handles are wide to accommodate gloves. Fastenings are redundant. Edges are kept for stone, not for flesh.

### How they move and speak

They move like locals: slow, deliberate, tuned to vibration.

In a chamber where dust lies deep, they step in the old footprints, not out of sentiment but because compressing fresh powder costs moisture and energy and invites attention. In corridors they touch the walls constantly—not for balance alone, but to read what the stone is saying. A hand pressed to a seam can feel the difference between safe stress and the kind that means a shelf of plates may decide to become a landslide tomorrow.

Their speech is not optimized for open air, because open air is scarce. Much of it is made for contact: knuckle-taps, heel-drums, finger-scrapes that travel along the rock and return as patterns. They can “throw” a message through a wall the way a surface person throws a voice across a room, using crystals as amplifiers when they find them—piezoelectric seams turned into crude, enduring telegraphs.

When they do use breath-voice, it is quiet and economical. Consonants do more work than vowels. Words are clipped, as if every syllable is taxed.

### What they eat, and what they value

They do not farm in the surface sense. Their equivalent of agriculture is stewardship of vents and films.

They maintain certain chambers the way a coastal people maintains tide pools: not by planting, but by keeping conditions stable. A ring of stones around a vent is not only a windbreak; it is a climate device, meant to shape where mineral-laden gas cools and where microbial varnish thickens. A line of cairns is not only navigation; it is a soft fence, guiding migrating grazers through a place where the makers can predictably harvest without collapsing the herd.

Their diet is a careful mix of:

- dried animal tissues ground into meal and reconstituted internally with saved solvent,  
- mineral-rich crusts scraped from deposition terraces,  
- the occasional luxury of fresh fat from a large walker, eaten reverently because it is both food and fuel and sealant.

They waste almost nothing. Their “trash” is sorted: pellets to feed powder-swimmers, shells to become armor plates or bowls, tendon-fibers to twist into rope that won’t rot because nothing rots quickly here.

They value thickness, redundancy, and promises that last.

A fired-clay bowl is wealth not because it is pretty but because it holds a boundary between wet and dry. A copper hook is wealth because it means you can trust a rope in a fissure where slipping would cost you an entire journey’s stored breath. A marked wall is wealth because it is information that will still be true when you come back—if you come back at all.

### Social shape: not cities, but routes

They are not a people of dense towns; the undercontinent does not reward permanence in one spot unless the vent is exceptionally generous and the stone exceptionally stable.

Instead they are a network of routes and obligations. Clans—or their equivalent—are often defined by which corridors they maintain, which waystations they keep stocked, which shelves they arrange shells upon as ledger and offering at once. A waystation might be “inhabited” only for a few days each long cycle, then left alone for months, yet still be considered part of someone’s home in the same way a ship considers the sea-lanes part of its domain.

Their conflicts are rarely wars of territory in the surface sense. They are disputes over access to stable vents, over the right to harvest certain herds during certain temperature phases, over whose cairns count as law in a given canyon of dark. Sabotage here is simple and terrifying: knock down a marker. Spill dust into a sealed pit. Chip the lip of a bowl so it leaks just enough to ruin someone’s reserves over time.

Their ethics, accordingly, are built around preservation. You do not damage the world thoughtlessly because the world will not heal quickly, and because everyone’s survival is bound up in other people’s past care.

### What they believe (because you can’t live here without believing something)

They treat heat the way surface cultures treat sunlight: not merely as comfort, but as order.

Vents are not gods, usually, but they are *ancestors* in the sense that they make life possible and set the rhythm of migration. The planet’s deep thrum is listened to the way others listen to weather; it is interpreted, not superstitiously so much as pragmatically, because reading it keeps you alive.

Many of their marks—handprints in dust, symbols scratched into soft crust—are records of passage: “we were sealed and we remained sealed,” “this route held,” “this vent was kind,” “this crack took three lives, do not trust it.” Some are prayers, but even prayers here are functional: requests for steadiness, for the stone to remain honest.

Offerings are often dry: arranged shells, carefully sorted pellets, a smear of resin on a cairn, a strip of fat left where a migrating herd must pass. Nothing that would tempt evaporation.

## Other presences you only learn to name later

Once you’ve spent enough time down there, you start to notice that not all “life” is animal-shaped.

There are chambers where the piezoelectric shimmer is strong and constant, where crystals under stress produce faint light and, more importantly to local creatures, faint electric fields. In those places, the microbial varnish grows differently—denser, stranger, threaded with metallic filaments. Grazers congregate. Predators lurk. The makers sometimes avoid these halls or treat them as taboo, not because they are haunted but because the fields confuse navigation and scramble the usual stone-speech.

And there are slow phenomena that sit somewhere between ecology and geology: mats that spread only along a particular mineral contact and then stop as if respecting a boundary; salt-fans that grow in shapes so regular they look intentional until you watch long enough to see the vent’s breathing pattern writing them.

The undercontinent produces these half-alive structures the way a reef produces architecture—except the reef is rock, and the tide is heat and gas.

## What “lives there,” in the end

A layered persistence.

- A skim of chemistry that refuses to die, feeding on gradients too slight for surface senses.  
- A vast multitude of sealed small bodies turning powder into pathways and pathways into tradition.  
- Larger walkers that carry heat from chamber to chamber like slow-moving seasons.  
- Predators that turn silence into a weapon by mastering chokepoints.  
- And the Dry-Carriers, the route-keepers, who have learned that in a world without rain the most precious thing you can do is keep a boundary intact—around a bowl, around a vent, around a promise scratched into dust that may outlast the person who made it.

They do not make the undercontinent friendly. They make it *inhabited*—dense with intentions as well as bodies—so that when you stand in the dark with your lamp and think you are alone, the feeling is not that something is watching you with eyes, but that the stone itself is full of listeners.
