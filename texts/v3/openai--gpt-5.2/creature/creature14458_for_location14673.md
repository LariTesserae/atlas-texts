They call themselves a dry people, and it’s half a joke and half a prayer.

Up close, you stop thinking of “inhabitants” as a single category. The basin doesn’t permit that simplification. Here, what lives is layered the way the city is layered: chemistry on stone, mouths on chemistry, hunters on mouths, and—threaded through all of it—humans building temporary dryness out of constant wet.

### The first residents: the films
Before you notice anything with eyes, you notice the living varnish.

Every pale terrace has a slick to it that isn’t just water. Bacterial mats—thin as breath, stubborn as hide—grow wherever warm mineral water spreads and cools. They come in subtle colors the fog doesn’t quite let you see until you’re close: smoked-yellow, bruised violet, iron-red. They are the basin’s “meadow,” but they don’t wave. They cling. When you scrape them, they smell like pennies and eggs and clean rot.

And they are not passive. They change the stone under them. They make some surfaces gummy, some glassy, some brittle. A local can tell you where to step by the look of a sheen—where the film is harmless, where it’s slick as fat, where it hides a crust too young to carry weight.

This is the first kind of life the city learns to manage, because everything else is downstream of it.

### The grazers: moving mouths
Then you see the grazers, and you understand why the terraces never stay clean.

They’re small enough to ignore until you don’t: slick-bodied colonies like living commas, shell-backed raspers with soft undersides, things that look like mollusks until they unfold jointed legs and scuttle with sudden certainty. They move in tides of their own, dense in safe shallows, sparse near vents too hot or too sour.

They eat the films. They leave behind combed stone—fine parallel scratches—so common on older slabs that the city’s masons imitate it when they want a surface less slippery. The grazers are also the basin’s first domesticates, though nobody here would call it that. People lure them the way you “lure” condensation: with placement.

A strip of warm tufa set beside a walkway becomes a feeding lane; a shallow tray kept at the right temperature becomes a living brush that cleans itself. Children learn early which grazers you can lift without getting bit, which ones will sting with a hidden spine, which ones you never touch because they carry a caustic slime that will eat the calluses off your palms.

### The swarm-life: water that isn’t just water
In the terraces and canals, the water itself has texture.

Crustacean swarms turn a pool into something like drifting sand. If you watch long enough, you can see their decisions—darkening under a shadow, thinning where the water warms, gathering in a corner where a dead thing has started to dissolve. Some of them flash faintly when disturbed: not bright bioluminescence, more like a reflexive pulse, a stutter of green or milk-blue.

There are eels too, but “eel” doesn’t cover it. They are long, blunt-headed, patterned like mineral veins. They rise to breathe with a quiet patience, noses breaking the surface with the casualness of a habitual act. They are eaten, and they are feared, and they are used. Their skins cure into a tough, water-shedding leather that replaces the absent bark and canvas of other cities.

People don’t fish the way you fish a river. They harvest the way you harvest a hot spring: with screens, with traps that can be checked by touch in fog, with pens built where the basin’s steady processes do most of the work.

### The scavengers: the city’s second workforce
Above the waterline, the city has its own cleaning crews that nobody pays.

Shell-backed skitterers live under platforms and in the seams where mineral growth forms little ledges. They eat scraps, offal, droppings, dead grazers washed up in a wrack-line of pale shells. They leave behind pellets that dry into fuel if you know how to collect them without being swarmed. They shed mineralized scales that get swept into corners and reused as grit for polishing and grinding.

The pale birds are the loudest of the visible scavengers. They aren’t graceful. They’re built for fog: broad wings for slow lift, thick necks, hooked beaks meant for pulling flesh from tight places. Their eyes look washed out until they turn their heads and you see the focus: they can find a carcass from a long way off in half-visibility, guided by smell and habit more than sight.

They fight constantly, and the fights are part of the city’s soundscape the way hammering is. A market can be measured by how many birds are perched above it, waiting for the moment someone turns their back on a waste bucket.

### The wall-owners: amphibians in the gaps
There are larger animals that feel like neighbors, whether you want them to or not.

The dog-sized amphibians claim warm vents and the wet undersides of stairs. Their feet are wide, their toes spread like starfish, and they cling to mineral the way a hand clings to a rung. They are not pets, but they are tolerated; they eat the smaller things that would otherwise infest storage racks, and they are predictable in their own way.

They nest where the steam is steady. Their eggs are kept warm by geology, and the city builds around that fact. In some neighborhoods, a vent is not just heat; it’s a rookery, a protected source of meat and hide and oil. People learn the calls the way they learn the hiss of a safe seep versus the sharper exhalation of a thin crust. The amphibians’ voices—low, throat-deep, endlessly repeated—become part of navigation. You can find your way home by listening for a particular chorus under a particular bridge.

### The humans: builders of dryness, keepers of rates
And then there are the people, who are not the masters of this place so much as its most complicated adaptation.

They live like maintenance given names.

Their bodies tell you where they’re from. Skin toughened by perpetual damp, hair kept short or bound tight because loose hair drinks fog until it’s heavy. Nails kept trimmed because anything under them becomes a paste of mineral and grit. Clothes layered for warmth not against cold but against wet: cured hides, eel-skin panels, shell-inlaid collars that shed drips away from the neck.

They smell faintly of brine and fat-smoke. Everyone does.

Their lives are organized around the basin’s fixed rates. In other cities you might schedule around weather; here you schedule around accretion. Around the narrowing of a channel. Around when a walkway will become too slick unless it’s scraped. Around the predictable hour when a certain vent’s steam thickens enough to make an alley blind.

So the city’s most honored work is not conquest or artistry—it’s keeping geometry stable.

There are crews whose whole job is to shave the city back to its intended shape:

- **Scrapers**, who walk with bone-bladed tools and buckets, taking new stone off step edges before steps vanish.
- **Channel-keepers**, who listen with their ears close to the ground for the hollow drum of water where it shouldn’t be, and dig relief grooves before a platform lifts.
- **Rope-menders**, because damp rope fails by surprise, and a surprise here is usually a fall into warm mud that pretends to be solid.
- **Gutterwrights**, who make and remake the city’s drip-lines—shell gutters, clay troughs, mineral-furred chutes—so that “roof” means something.

Nothing is allowed to become final. Finality is what kills you here.

#### How they build community
The fog changes what “neighbor” means. You can’t see far enough to be indifferent.

Etiquette becomes infrastructure: you announce yourself on bridges; you keep lamps low; you don’t run unless there’s fire, because running is what you do when the ground turns treacherous and you want others to clear.

Voices become identities. People recognize each other by cadence and footfall—the particular slap-suck rhythm of someone who favors one leg, the hollow knock of a certain kind of boot sole. Names are used often, not for intimacy but for certainty: you call a name to make a body real in the fog.

And because there are no seasons to force celebration, celebration is scheduled socially: hatchings, migrations, the opening of a new terrace that’s finally crusted enough to carry a market.

#### What they eat
They eat what the basin offers, but they don’t pretend it’s abundant in a gentle way.

They eat grazers dried into crisp sheets, eels smoked over sulfurous fuel that makes the meat taste sharp and lasting, amphibian flesh stewed with mineral salt. They harvest fat like a currency—rendered, stored, guarded—because fat is light and heat and lamp and fire in one substance.

They drink water that has been coaxed into being drinkable: condensed from steam, collected from higher runnels before it turns brackish, filtered through porous tufa and shell charcoal. A child learns early that not all clear water is safe, and not all brown water is deadly; you learn by who is still alive.

#### What they believe
Religion here tends to be practical, even when it’s ornate.

They venerate steadiness: the vents that never surge, the terraces that keep their edges, the slow white growth that turns today’s tool-mark into tomorrow’s buried history. If they have gods, they are more like **rates** than personalities—patrons of deposition, guardians of thin crust, keepers of the fog.

Bones appear in their shrines not as death-mementos but as building material that remembers. A rib becomes an arch. A skull becomes a lamp housing. The dead are not kept separate from the city’s function; they are integrated the way everything is integrated, sealed under new mineral, made part of the structure that holds others above the water.

### The collaborations: life that lives *with* the city
Some creatures aren’t simply tolerated or hunted; they are recruited.

- Certain grazers are encouraged to clean specific stones, kept in shallow “gardens” where they can be harvested and replaced.
- Some shell-backed scavengers are allowed under granaries because they deter worse infestations.
- Amphibian rookeries become neighborhood features, protected like wells.

Even the bioluminescent swarms in canals are, in a way, municipal. People time travel by them: you can tell how recently a boat passed, how busy a channel is tonight, whether a predator moved through and panicked the light into a ragged pulse.

The city isn’t separate from its fauna; it’s one more surface that gets colonized, one more pattern the basin’s life learns to use.

### What it feels like, standing among them
If you stand still long enough in this place, you realize the inhabitants are not defined by what they are but by what they keep doing.

The grazers keep rasping. The swarms keep shifting. The birds keep screaming. The amphibians keep calling. The vents keep hissing. The people keep scraping, mending, hauling, shouting names through fog, building “dry” out of bone and stone and habit.

No one here expects permanence, but everyone here expects continuity. They don’t live on land. They live on an agreement they renegotiate every day with water and mineral and the dense, hungry life between.
