What lives here starts small enough to be mistaken for weather.

Before there are animals, before there are people, there are the skins: the opalescent films that look like spilled light and behave like appetite. They are colonies more than individuals—membranes of microbes and mineral-binding chemistry braided together—thin where the flats are poor, thick and almost leathery where the seep-water comes up warm and loaded. They don’t “grow” upward so much as they spread and fold, learning the contours of stone the way a tongue learns a tooth. In the morning they can be tight, glassy, reluctant; by noon they loosen, wrinkle, and drift toward heat gradients as if the ground were gently tilting them.

The city measures them the way other places measure crops. Inspectors tap at stained marker posts and note the color shift—green-blue means a copper-rich week, violet means the silica is winning, a faint gold scum means the skins are about to clog every intake in the district unless someone scrapes channels today. Children are taught early not to step where the sheen looks “too perfect,” because that’s where the surface is thinner than it looks and the crust can punch through into warm slurry.

From the skins comes most of everything else.

### The grazers: stone-shelled herds that turn film into muscle
Along the terraced basins move the plated herds—low, broad animals with overlapping scutes that genuinely resemble river stones, each plate edged with a pale mineral line where old layers met new. From a distance they could be part of the terrace itself until they shift and the whole “rock” decides to walk.

They feed with patience. A grazer will brace itself on splayed, clawed feet and set its mouth to the slick. The rasping is mostly felt through the soles of your boots, not heard: a vibration like sandpaper used underwater. They leave behind scraped arcs—pale scars on dark rock—that re-darken as the skins knit back over the exposed mineral.

They are social in a quiet way: they don’t call, but they keep spacing with little shoulder touches, plate to plate, as if sound would be too expensive. When a storm curtain comes down, they will turn their backs to it, let the rain sluice grit from between their plates, and then resume feeding as though the interruption never happened.

People in the city treat the herds like a combination of livestock, hazard, and neighbor. There are lanes for them across certain districts—wide sinter tiles with shallow channels on either side so runoff doesn’t pool under their weight. At dusk, when the light stays bright and the streets look wet even when they aren’t, you can watch an entire herd cross between housing blocks, their armor clicking so softly that it becomes a kind of communal clock.

### The small quick things: scavengers that belong to the drains
Where grazers leave crumbs—flakes of skin, bits of mineral, sloughed plate-edges—there are the many-legged scavengers that flicker across wet stone and vanish into infrastructure. They live where the city pretends not to notice life: inside curb channels, under grates, behind the ceramic ribs of condensation towers.

They’re fast enough to feel like an optical effect. You see one, then you see the pattern of its wake: tiny ripples on a film, a brief tremor in a bead of water, a sudden clearing where algae-like sheen was moments before. Their bodies are built for humidity—jointed, sealed, hard-shelled—yet they still avoid direct sun, slipping into the cool underworld of drains as soon as a shadow moves the wrong way.

Municipal crews design around them now. Some grates have a fine lattice not because the city fears trash, but because it learned what happens when juvenile scavengers get pulled into pumps: clogs, die-offs, and then the skins bloom uncontrolled because the cleanup crew has been vacuumed away.

### The channel-dwellers: filterers and the ribbon predators
In the braided runoff—engineered gutters that behave like rivers and natural channels that have been convinced to hold a straight line—there are fishlike things that aren’t quite fish. They have wide mouths for filtering, internal bladders that let them hover in absurdly shallow water, and a habit of schooling precisely at the seam where warm effluent meets cold rain. In that mixing zone the microscopic world explodes: free-floating bits of skin, mineral-laced droplets, bacteria that thrive on the chemistry of meeting waters.

The filterers look harmless until you see how efficiently they strip a channel clean. A busy school can turn a milky, living stream into something nearly transparent over the span of a block, leaving behind a faint metallic tang and a quiet, satisfied eddying.

And then there are the ribbon-bodied predators. They don’t read as “animals” at first. They lie against undersides of sinter ledges and the shadowed lips of terrace dams, patterned like mineral deposits—banded, speckled, deceptively inert. Their strike is so fast it registers as absence: a sudden tear in the water’s surface, a brief quiver through the channel stones, then nothing but settling ripples and a few drifting scales that might be mineral flecks if you didn’t know better.

Even human movement adjusts to them. In some canal districts people don’t run—not out of fear of slipping, though that’s part of it, but because sudden footfalls send vibration down the tile and that vibration can trigger feeding strikes in shallow water, which splashes the channels, which spreads skins, which clogs grates. The ecosystem makes municipal policy by force of consequence.

### The hanging colonies: glass combs that drink air
Above street level, under bridges and along the shaded faces of buildings, are colonies that look like delicate architecture someone forgot to claim: translucent combs, fronds, and curtains with a watery shine. They are animals—soft-bodied, pulsing, full of channels that move air and moisture through them.

They make use of the city’s very habits. Condenser fins bead water; the combs cluster beneath them. Pipe forests sweat mineral-laced steam; the combs bloom in their lee. On mornings after rain they can be so heavy with gathered moisture that they drip steadily, turning alleys into shimmering bead-curtains you have to walk through with your collar up and your mouth shut.

People harvest from them carefully—never the whole colony, never during certain weeks—taking the dried, mineral-rich “shed” that forms when a comb constricts and sloughs off a layer. That shed becomes everything from filter media to medicinal salts to the glaze on the pale sinter tiles that make streets shed water just a little more reliably.

In return, the city leaves them dark places to live. There are bridges designed with intentional underhangs, buildings whose lower faces are roughened—not for aesthetics, but to give the combs grip. It’s hard to tell where urban planning ends and husbandry begins.

### The humans: quiet-engineers, skin-farmers, and the people who listen to stone
People do live here, and they have the look of inhabitants of a bright damp place: skin weathered not by cold but by glare, hair kept short or tightly wrapped because humidity makes everything heavy, clothes built in layers that can breathe while still shedding water. Fabrics are treated with mineral oils; seams are sealed like the joints of rail pylons. Footwear matters more than fashion—soft soles that don’t ring on sinter, treads that grip when the tile decides to become glass.

Their culture is shaped by what the plateau rewards.

- **They speak softly, not from politeness but from habit.** Voices feel private here because the land eats sound, and people come to expect privacy even outdoors. Arguments don’t escalate into shouting as often because shouting doesn’t travel; it just sits around your head and makes you feel foolish.
- **They learn vibration etiquette.** The “quiet corridors” aren’t sentimental conservation zones; they’re navigational sanctuaries for species that read the world through stone. In those corridors, people walk with a particular pace, carts have specific wheel materials, and machinery is banned not because it’s loud, but because it is *felt* too far away.
- **They are water-accountants by necessity.** Everyone understands condensation surfaces, drainage grades, and the danger of a blocked channel the way coastal people understand tides. Buildings sit on jackable frames and the residents know how to read the inspection pylons—those tide-like lines of groundwater history—like farmers read cloud fronts.
- **They practice a kind of daily maintenance that borders on ritual.** Scrape the intake screens. Check the grate lattices. Clear the ceramic ribs. Recoat the rail joints. Not because anyone loves chores, but because damp and mineral will take anything that isn’t defended.

Occupations have grown up that barely exist elsewhere. There are **drainwrights** who can hear a blocked channel by the way their footsteps change tone on tile. There are **skin-cultivators** who “seed” certain basins by placing mineral wafers that encourage the opalescent films to thicken into harvestable sheets instead of clogging pipes. There are **patina-smiths** who work with alloys that “heal,” teaching a metal to grow the tight glossy layer that seals scratches before corrosion can take hold.

Food follows the same logic: less “agriculture,” more managed ecology. People eat pressed cakes made from processed skins (neutral-tasting until you season them with salts from comb-shed). They eat the filterers from the channels, smoked over low heat so the flesh keeps its mineral tang without turning bitter. In some districts they eat grazer meat—rare, expensive, and regulated—because the herds are as much part of the plateau’s stability as they are a resource. A city that overharvests its grazers gets punished not by moral backlash but by mechanical failure: skins overgrow, intakes clog, terraces change chemistry, channels shift.

### The other residents: the things that live in seams and rules
There are also lives that aren’t easily categorized.

- In the warm cracks near old vents, there are **tiny heat-lovers**—wormlike and translucent—that spend their lives in steam-breathing stone, feeding on minerals precipitating out of vapor. People sometimes call them “threadlings,” and miners consider them an omen: where threadlings are thick, the ground is unstable but rich.
- On certain bright flats, when fog climbs upward in wall-shadows, you can find **floating specks** in the mist—minute bladdered organisms that spend a few hours aloft during the sharpest thermal gradients, then settle back onto wet stone. They’re less like insects and more like airborne plankton.
- In places where voices sound “kept,” close to your mouth even in open air, some residents swear there are **listening hollows** in the basalt—pockets in the porous rock that trap sound and release it later as faint pressure changes. Whether that’s geology or something alive that uses vibration like scent, nobody agrees. People simply lower their voices near old vents, just in case.

### A city as an ecosystem, not a shelter
The most truthful way to describe who lives here is that no one does alone.

The city is not set down on the plateau like a lid; it is threaded into a water-loop and a mineral metabolism. The skins feed the grazers; the grazers keep the skins from smothering stone; the scavengers clean what falls away; the filterers strain the runoff; the ribbon predators keep the filterers honest; the hanging combs turn humid air into drips that keep alleys wet; the people harvest, redirect, forbid, and maintain—acting less like conquerors than like one more regulatory layer in a system that will continue with or without them.

Even the laws read that way: not commandments, but boundary conditions. Don’t divert this pool. Don’t send engines down that corridor. Don’t harvest comb-shed during the heavy-drip weeks. Don’t scrape skins to bare stone in breeding season. Not out of reverence—though some do develop reverence—but because the plateau enforces reciprocity with clogged pipes, corroded beams, sinking crust, and sudden flooding that appears from nowhere but condensation.

So what lives here is a quiet abundance: chemistry that learned to cling, animals that learned to armor, and people who learned that survival in a bright wet highland is mostly the art of not interrupting the loops that keep you alive.
