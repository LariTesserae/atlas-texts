What lives here is not a set of occupants so much as an occupant that has mistaken itself—successfully—for terrain.

At first it reads as flora because your eye keeps looking for trunks and leaves, for the familiar rhetoric of growth. But the longer you stay, the harder it is to keep calling it *plants* in the ordinary sense, because there are no individuals to point to, no bodies with edges. The plateau is a single extended organism whose “organs” are districts and whose “skin” is civic engineering: load-bearing bark, pressure-rated membranes, heat-shedding fronds, hinge-joints made of callus and carbon fiber. It does not sit *in* the desert; it is the desert’s answer to itself—life rearranged into something that can afford to be dry.

It lives the way a furnace lives: by managing gradients.

Its hungers are not water-hunger or sugar-hunger, but differential-hunger. Heat where heat shouldn’t pool. Pressure where pressure would rupture. Dust where dust can be fixed into skeleton. Slag where slag can be chelated, softened, made useful. Its rootwork is not a search for moisture—there is none to find—but a slow, persistent mining, a chemistry of claws. It grips mineral like a problem to solve, and solves it by turning it into ribs.

Inside, the “sap” is not a pastoral thing. It is a suite of fluids with jobs: viscous resins that behave like adhesives until they behave like ceramics; clear solvents that strip and sterilize; brines that are brines only because “molten salt” is too honest a name for something that moves like blood. These fluids do not circulate to keep a body hydrated. They circulate to keep a body *sealed*. A leak is not merely loss—it is heresy. So every conduit has reflexes: swelling collars, instant-sintering plugs, tissues that flash-cure in air like shame.

And because it cannot afford spills, it cannot afford casual mouths.

There are no animals because an animal is, by definition, a mobile bag of water. A mammal would be an exposed reservoir. An insect would be a puncture risk. A bird would be a contaminant with opinions about where to land. Even microbes—real ones, wet ones—would be an insurgency. The plateau does not merely fail to support them; it actively edits them out. Any stray organic softness that arrives (a blown carcass from lower altitudes, a clump of lichens carried up in a storm) is treated like defective material: dried, sealed, rendered. Not eaten in any recognizable way—processed.

So what *does* move with intent?

The plateau’s movement is mostly slow and architectural: thickening here, relaxing there, shifting load paths the way an animal shifts its weight—but on the scale of neighborhoods. You feel it in the nightly rise of a floor that is not swelling from moisture but from pressure scheduling. You see it in a wall that has gained a new strut by morning, grown into place like a bone callus after a fracture.

Yet there are quicker things, too, and they complicate the picture.

Embedded in the plant-mass are the machines that were never meant to be separate. Some are recognizably industrial—presses, turbines, beltways—but they behave less like tools and more like symbiotic organs. They wake on cues the way glands wake: a heat threshold crossed, a pressure demand signaled, a chemical permission granted. Their housings are grown, their bearings fed, their waste captured, their vibration damped by tissues evolved (or engineered) to love silence.

Where those machines need fine work—where a press must be cleaned, where a conduit must be inspected, where a mold must be re-lined—the plateau does not release animals into itself. It releases *workers* that are not quite organisms in the way your instincts mean.

They are microscopic and multitudinous, yes, but “microscopic” here doesn’t mean bacterial. It means distributed. It means you never meet one as a creature; you meet them as an effect: a surface that becomes slick with catalytic film, a clogged pore that clears as if it decided to, a resin that sets with uncanny uniformity because a billion enzyme-like agents insisted it do so. They live in the dry way the plateau lives: in locked microcells, in gels that never become free liquid, in compartments that open only when they can close again without loss. They don’t roam. They are deployed.

There are also larger maintenance presences—not animals, not exactly machines—things that move because the plateau needs movement but refuses the vulnerabilities of flesh.

In certain corridors you can watch a seam unlace itself and produce a cutter: a rigid, chitinless implement grown from the wall and hardened with mineral dust, articulated by fiber bundles that behave like tendons without containing any water worth stealing. It advances on quiet hinges, performs one precise excision—a bulge of “wild” tissue shaved flush, a swollen duct opened and re-sleeved—then retracts, not triumphantly but dutifully, leaving the corridor smoother and the system calmer. Later the place will digest the cutter back into feedstock, as if tools here are only temporary arrangements of the same underlying body.

Communication happens without sound and without light. The plateau speaks in:

- pressure pulses that travel through ribbed streets like drumbeats you feel in your molars,
- heat waves routed as messages (“send surplus,” “shed now,” “brake growth”),
- chemical gradients that are less scent than instruction.

Sometimes, if you stand where a wall meets a floor and let your attention go soft, you can sense a kind of attention in return—not a mind watching you, but a system registering a perturbation. Not curiosity. Calibration. You are a heat source, a moving obstruction, a minor draft in a tightly budgeted world. The plateau accounts for you the way it accounts for a new duct: by deciding what you must not be allowed to cause.

And then there is the strangest inhabitant of all: *memory*.

It lives here not as stories but as layers. Old designs planed down into rounded ridges. Deprecated conduits sealed and left in place because removal would risk an opening. Fossilized scaffolds that still bear load because—after enough rebuild cycles—nothing is purely obsolete; everything is reinforcement for something else. The plateau remembers like a tree remembers drought: in thickness, in scar tissue, in altered grain. The industry that built it may be gone, but its scheduling persists as physiology. Its rules have become instincts.

If there were once caretakers who walked these corridors with clipboards and lamps, they are not here now as bodies. What remains of them is procedural: growth calendars embedded in hormones, pruning geometries encoded in where the cutters emerge, safety interlocks that treat “light” like a contaminant and “liquid” like an explosion.

So the answer—what lives here—is a city-sized plant-machinery continuum that has taken on the duties of an ecosystem and the temperament of a factory. It does not host wildlife; it *is* the wildlife, pared down to what can survive without spilling. It is a desert’s only resident: not a thing enduring scarcity, but a thing engineered until scarcity is irrelevant—busy, sealed, and dreaming in heat.
