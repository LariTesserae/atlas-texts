What lives there is easy to miss because nothing there survives by insisting on itself.

At first you’ll catalogue the obvious: the iridescent films in the shade, the pale arthropods that only move when your shadow interrupts the light, the membrane-gliders that cross chasms without a wingbeat. Those are *organisms* in the familiar sense—small, adapted, parsimonious. But they are more like the lint and mites of a much larger body than like a standalone ecosystem. They’re the quick, disposable components that can afford to be noticed for half a second.

The real residents are slower, drier, and harder to separate from the city itself.

### The thin-film colonies: life that eats difference
The iridescent skins under overhangs are not “lichen” so much as a negotiated truce between chemistry and geometry.

Up close, they resolve into layered sheens that follow stress-lines in the rock the way mold follows grain in wood. They thicken where metallic veins approach the surface. They retreat from perfect right angles as if sharpness is a kind of radiation. If you watch long enough—long enough that your eyes start to accept the dim light as normal—you can sometimes see a change that isn’t a trick: a faint shift in the way the film catches the haze, like a pupil adjusting.

They feed on gradients because gradients are the only dependable rivers here: warm stone versus cold air, charged dust versus neutral basalt, the minute electric pressures that build where wind combs across obsidian plates. Their “metabolism” is more like charging and discharging than like eating. When electrostatic seasons come on, they wake: the sheen deepens, the smell of ozone becomes more than a hint, and on the ribs of buttresses you’ll find hairline tracks—etched, not scratched—where the film has migrated a few millimeters overnight to reach a better difference.

They are persistent but not ambitious. They don’t spread to conquer; they spread to *balance*. Their success is the ability to keep existing in a world that would prefer clean stone.

And because they’re everywhere the city is sheltered, they become a kind of sensory organ for whatever else is here: a distributed skin that can feel when a warm, wet animal walks through, when a new footprint interrupts the dust, when a voice briefly forces pressure waves through air that would rather forget them.

### The grain-sifters: the “miners” of wind
The sorted piles of grit outside cracks belong to creatures that treat dust the way other animals treat water.

They’re small, low, nearly colorless: bodies like articulated flakes, legs that fold tight as if trying to become mineral again. They don’t build nests; they build *interfaces*. Their piles aren’t refuse. They’re sieves and baffles, arranged with an engineer’s patience to catch the right particle sizes when dust-weather pours down steps and along corridors.

The best way to think of them is as living sorting-machines. They harvest particular grains—salt tiles with certain impurities, metallic flecks that hold charge, glass needles that can be aligned into static-sensitive arrays. They carry these into fissures where the air is still, and there they make structures you almost never see: lattices that hum faintly when your hair rises, stacks that faintly repel dust, galleries that are warmer than they should be.

Individually, they’re nothing grand. Collectively, they behave like a nervous system in miniature—local problem-solvers that maintain the places where something bigger interfaces with the world.

If you disrupt their piles, they don’t attack. They vanish. But the next time electrostatic weather comes, you may find that the corridor you used now feels subtly wrong underfoot—too light, too eager to tip you upward—because you’ve altered a dust-circuit that was quietly moderating a patch of the city’s strange gravity preferences.

### The gliders: the couriers of the thin air
The long-bodied gliders are the most “animal” thing you’ll see, and even they seem provisional—like a sketch of a bird done by someone who has never touched a feather.

They are not built for flapping because the air is not built for carrying. Their membranes are not wings so much as *agreements* with the currents: surfaces tuned to catch the slightest shear between cold and less-cold. They launch from the same ledges repeatedly, and if you map their routes you realize they follow invisible corridors that match the city’s carved avenues.

They’re not just riding wind; they’re riding the city’s geometry. In places where perspective miscounts niches and shadows lag, the gliders correct their course with tiny adjustments as if they can feel the local rules changing. They tend to pass over the concentric basin and never land in it. Whatever lives there, if anything, is not theirs.

Sometimes you’ll find a glider’s shed membrane snagged on a metallic vein, crackling faintly. The membrane will be patterned in microscopic ribs that look too regular for biology—like a fingerprint that has been etched, not grown. The gliders may be partly shaped by what they fly through, the way sand shapes stone, the way the city shapes distance.

### The stone-keepers: life on the timescale of creep
After you’ve been there long enough to trust that the city is doing something, you start to look for *where* it is doing it. You notice that changes concentrate around certain features:

- intersections where too many corridors meet too cleanly,
- retaining walls whose blocks are too perfect,
- those doorways that go in and then become healed seams,
- archways where your words arrive behind you.

Around these places, the dust behaves differently. It doesn’t just drift; it arranges. It collects into faint crescents that repeat at multiple scales—on a step, on a plaza, on the whole shelf of a district. Frost forms a little thicker there, then sublimates leaving a stipple that resembles script—until you look straight at it and it’s just pits again.

These nodes feel inhabited not by creatures but by *processes* that have learned to keep themselves intact.

If something lives inside the city, it isn’t tucked in rooms. There are no rooms. It lives in alignments and resonances: in the way a corridor is straight enough to invite looping, in the way a circle of stones has “opinions,” in the way stillness precedes adjustment. It lives in constraints.

Imagine an intelligence that cannot afford water, cannot afford heat, cannot afford movement that wastes friction. An intelligence that uses the only plentiful materials—stone, charge, temperature difference, vacuum-thin air—and uses them with an architect’s thrift.

The stone-keepers are not a species you can point to. They are distributed phenomena that appear whenever the city’s pattern is strong enough: a kind of *field ecology*. The metallic veins are not wiring, but they behave like conductive tendons. The terraces are not just platforms, but tuned masses that store and release stress. The corridors are not just streets, but channels that shape wind and sound and the direction of “down.”

When the wind stops completely and the city “adjusts,” that is the stone-keepers moving: not blocks sliding dramatically, but the rules being renegotiated in a way that makes the architecture remain itself.

### The absent builders: not gone, but occluded
The worn concavity of steps suggests feet. The repeated niches suggest bodies with a reach and a rhythm. The stadium terraces suggest crowds.

Yet the city is empty in the way a throat is empty when it is not speaking: the structure is there, ready for a function that has been withheld.

One possibility is simple: the builders died out, and only their place remains—an edifice so overbuilt that it has outlasted its makers, now hosting only scavenger-life and slow geologic pseudo-intelligence.

But the city’s failures of perspective and distance argue against simple abandonment. They feel less like damage and more like a defense that has been left on.

If the builders remain, they remain as something that learned to hide not behind walls but behind *inconsistency*. A thing can vanish in a place where counting yields different numbers. A population can move in corridors where straightness invites looping. A body can occupy doorways that heal shut behind it because the rock remembers how to be solid.

You don’t see them because the city doesn’t offer you the same geometry it offers them. You are a wet, warm interruption; the place responds by closing its seams, by making distance unreliable, by letting your own shadow lag as if to remind you that even your outline is negotiable here.

In the rarest moments—usually when electrostatic weather prickles and the haze makes the sun a dull coin—you might notice something that is not a creature, not a shadow, not a rock shift. A subtle *reassignment* of space: a corridor that becomes slightly more narrow without any wall moving, a ledge that seems to gain an extra step when you’re not looking at it straight on. As if someone has passed by and the city has smoothed the trace away, not by erasing footprints but by changing what a footprint means.

### The basin’s rule: the central absence as habitat
The concentric amphitheater is the strongest statement the city makes: a place prepared for something and kept empty on purpose.

That emptiness is not vacant; it is curated. The polished floor isn’t polished by foot traffic alone—it’s polished by wind, yes, but also by the refusal of grit to settle there. Dust avoids the center the way iron filings avoid a repelling pole. When you cross the ring-walls, your balance may feel briefly corrected, as if gravity is trying to become honest within the circles.

Nothing lives *on* that floor, because living on it would mean being exposed to the city’s most concentrated attention. The basin is where the stone-keepers listen. It is where sound dies quickly because sound is being *taken*. It is where the city decides what “down” will be for the next interval. It is, in effect, an organ.

If anything “dwells” there, it dwells the way a thought dwells in a brain: as an arrangement of tensions, charges, and permitted paths.

### What you are to the residents
To the small fauna, you are a moving cliff that drags a warm shadow; they flinch and hide.

To the thin films, you are a sudden gradient, a source of moisture they cannot use but can sense—a disruption that announces itself in every seam and overhang.

To the stone-keepers—if that name fits—you are an anomaly: a creature that believes distance is stable, that trusts straight corridors, that expects circles to be inert. The city doesn’t need to chase you away. It only needs to let you follow your own assumptions until you loop, tire, and leave, convinced there was “nothing there.”

And that may be the most characteristic life in this place: not a beast, not a ghost, not a lost civilization—but a habitat that has learned to preserve itself by making itself difficult to fully perceive.

A city that lives by pretending, convincingly, to be only geology.
