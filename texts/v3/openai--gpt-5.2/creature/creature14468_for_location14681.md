They live the way barnacles live—by making a decision against gravity and then spending their whole lives defending it.

At first you assume “they” must be workers, because the place reads as work made solid: catwalks like sentences, pipe runs like arteries, vents that never stop exhaling. But the longer you stand still, the more the city refuses the idea of a single kind of resident. It hosts layers of life the way it hosts ledges: some obvious, some half-hidden, some so slow you only notice them by what they change.

### The people: a high, warm, dry breed of citizen
Most of the inhabitants are human or human-adjacent in the practical sense—bipedal, tool-using, social—but adapted by habit into something that feels specific to this altitude and this heat.

They are not built like desert nomads, lean and airy. The retained warmth changes the calculus: you don’t need to outrun the sun so much as survive contact with everything it has preheated. They move with a cautious economy, not from laziness but from a deep bodily knowledge that every unnecessary touch is a small injury. Sleeves are not about modesty here; they are about surfaces.

Their clothing tends to be layered in a way that looks paradoxical in a warm place: outer shells that are pale, mineral-dull, reflective; inner fabrics that wick and seal. Gloves are common. So are wraps at the neck and jaw, not for cold but for dust and metallic grit. Many wear thin eye shields or tinted lenses that aren’t about glare so much as particulate—because the air holds its fines the way still water holds silt.

You can often tell a local by their hands. The fingertips are callused in a particular pattern: not the blunt, uniform callus of field labor, but the precise abrasions of climbing and gripping: cable, rail, ladder rung, the edge of a ceramic panel. The city trains everyone, quietly, to be at least a little bit vertical.

And their speech is tuned to the thin air. It isn’t whispered, exactly, but placed. People don’t throw words. They speak as if the space beyond a few meters belongs to someone else—because it does. Sound doesn’t carry; privacy is cheap; eavesdropping requires proximity. That shapes manners. You don’t shout for someone across a transit deck. You send a runner, or a signal through the city’s own nervous system.

#### Their professions are the city’s organs
A place like this does not have “jobs” so much as roles in a metabolism.

- **Anchorsmiths / Bolters**: They are part structural engineer, part climber. They read rock the way sailors read wind. Their tools are percussion drills, resin injectors, torque wrenches that can be operated one-handed while hanging from a harness. They work in pairs or threes because the terrain can change its mind while you’re inside it.

- **Heatwrights**: Not just plumbers of hot pipes, but people who understand heat as a fluid with politics. They reroute thermal flow, add shielding, build shade that is really a survival corridor, decide what can be allowed to run warm beside a walkway and what must be buried in stone. They know, intimately, where you can safely put your palm.

- **Water clerks / Condensers**: Some are machinists who build dew-catch meshes and sweating ceramic walls; others are accountants in the literal sense—keepers of inventories. They measure water in the way other cities measure currency. A gifted condenser is respected like a gifted doctor: they can bring comfort, they can avert rationing, they can “make weather” in miniature.

- **Listeners**: The teams with contact microphones. In another place they’d be eccentrics. Here they’re a municipal necessity. They press sensors to walls, to ribs of steel, to bare basalt. They wear headsets not to hear music but to hear stress: faint pings, microcracks, the long creak of settling plates. Their status is strange—part engineer, part augur—because they deal in predictions about a ground that still behaves like cooling glass.

- **Saltwrights / Efflorescence crews**: They scrape, harvest, and manage the mineral blooms. Salt here is not just nuisance; it’s data (where moisture has traveled), hazard (where it will eat metal), and resource (chemistry stock, industrial feed). They talk about crust patterns the way gardeners talk about soil.

Daily life is choreographed around the city’s rules. Children learn early not to sit on bare stone, not to grab sunlit railings, not to run without scanning for cable lines at head height. They learn to step over gutters and never into them—because gutters are not trash channels but part of the water mind.

And because moisture creates comfort, the city has “cool lanes”: narrow paths under condensing rigs where the air is fractionally kinder. People linger there the way people elsewhere linger under trees. That absence—no plants, yet an equivalent desire for a shaded, living-feeling corridor—shapes their social geometry. Meetings happen in damp pockets. Arguments cool off literally.

### The city’s imported life: animals as provisions, not neighbors
Most animal life here is brought in on purpose, sealed in systems, treated like equipment.

If there are mammals, they are kept in interior facilities where heat can be managed and water can be recycled. You don’t see herds. You see crates and carrier modules. Protein is an industrial product more often than it is husbandry. The city doesn’t waste water on animals that can’t pay it back.

What survives outside is not charismatic.

- Small **heat-tolerant scavengers**—the clicking, mineral-shelled “insects”—live where residue collects: service trenches, behind insulation, under platforms where condensation drips and then evaporates leaving trace organics and salts. They are slow, deliberate, almost mechanical, and the locals regard them with the same mild annoyance they reserve for corrosion: a natural process that must be managed.

- There may be **micro-fauna** in the cisterns: tiny, pale things like threads or specks that thrive in mineral-rich water. They are not pests so much as indicators. Water clerks know which cistern “tastes wrong,” which tank has developed a living slick on its baffles.

No birds, as you noticed—no easy life in open air. The few creatures that can ride thermals at this height either pass through without engaging, or they have learned to treat the city as a cliff face that occasionally offers warmth. If you ever see one land, it will not perch on a railing; it will cling to a shaded rock seam where the heat is tolerable and the wind is minimal.

### The “plants”: living films that behave like weather
The nearest thing to flora is that iridescent skin on shaded ceramic.

It is less like moss and more like a responsive membrane: a chemistry that remembers being alive. In damp pockets it spreads until it meets a boundary it can’t digest—paint, certain alloys, glassy coatings—then it stops with a clean edge as if it respects property lines. When the humidity drops it tightens, dulls, and in some places seems to retreat into invisibility, leaving only a faint stain.

The city does not name it as a plant, but some residents treat it the way people elsewhere treat lichen: a sign that a surface has been left alone long enough for time to take an interest. A listener might glance at a film’s sheen and know a corridor has been unusually damp. A saltwright might read it as a warning: moisture has been here repeatedly; salts will follow.

There are rumors, inevitably, that the film is not merely opportunistic but engineered—something introduced long ago to seal microcracks, to regulate humidity, to make the city sweat more efficiently. Whether that’s true or not, people have learned to live alongside it as if it were part of the infrastructure’s immune system.

### The machines: quiet inhabitants with schedules of their own
This is also a city where machines count as residents, not because they are sentient, but because they have continuity, territory, and behavior.

The slow presses, the heat exchangers, the condensation rigs—each has a rhythm that becomes neighborhood character. A certain stairwell is “the one that ticks.” A certain alley is “the one that always smells like iron after rain.” People navigate by these traits the way others navigate by storefronts.

Maintenance is not an occasional activity; it is a form of citizenship. To ignore a vibration is a social failing. To leave a loose bolt is as shameful as leaving a door unlocked in a storm.

And because so much machinery is fused into stone, it gains something like permanence. A press installed into a cliff becomes, over decades, part of the cliff’s story. Families will speak of machines the way they speak of elders: temperamental, reliable, requiring care, older than their own memories.

### The most native resident: the unfinished mountain itself
If you stay long enough, you begin to feel that the dominant inhabitant is not human, film, scavenger, or machine. It is the ground—still cooling, still deciding.

The rock is not passive here. It pings. It contracts. It grows hairline fissures with edges too clean to be called erosion. It accepts bolts, then later seems to reject them by shifting half a millimeter. It warms itself from within and holds that warmth as if reluctant to let it go.

The locals treat this not as a mystical presence but as a neighbor with moods. They don’t say “the street is unsafe”; they say, in effect, “that face is moving.” They map stress the way coastal towns map tides. They build with the assumption that stone is an active participant, and that the city’s true long-term conversation is not with weather—weather is intermittent, manageable—but with a geology still in the middle of becoming.

So what lives there?

A people trained into careful verticality, who treat water as sacred inventory and heat as a permanent condition; a thin ecology of films and clicking scavengers that feed on the city’s leftovers; a population of machines whose quiet rhythms define neighborhoods; and beneath them all, the youngest resident of the city—the mountain range itself—alive in the only way stone can be: slow, insistent, and never quite finished.
