At first you’d swear nothing lives here.

That’s the trick the basin plays: it removes all the usual proofs—green, feather, fur, scat, burrow—until “alive” feels like a word meant for lower altitudes. Up here the world looks pared down to the raw materials: light, stone, ice, and the thin, structured air between them.

But stay long enough for the place to show its habits, and you begin to meet its residents. None of them arrive with softness. None of them spread. Everything that persists does so by becoming narrow, patient, and hard to see.

### The first inhabitants: the Outline-Makers
They are not animals, not even organisms in any familiar sense. They are behaviors that have learned to repeat themselves so faithfully that they take on the weight of character.

They live on edges.

They are the ones who choose where moisture will collect, where rime will begin, where a ridge will grow a white comb in ten minutes while a broad slab stays bare. They do not move like wind—wind is too blunt. They move like preference: a bias in the local physics, a rule the basin enforces so consistently it becomes a kind of handwriting.

You can watch them work:

- A fog spills in. For a moment everything is equally wet in the mind’s eye.
- Then, almost immediately, the knife-lines brighten—beads forming first on the thinnest points, each droplet a tiny lens that clings and refuses to spread.
- Hoarfrost follows the beads, not everywhere, but in a strict, crown-like line that makes the terrain look newly sharpened.

The Outline-Makers’ architecture is temporary, but it is not random. It returns to the same corners and lips again and again, as if those points are mouths and the fog is food.

If the place has a temperament, it’s theirs: selective, emphatic, more interested in boundaries than surfaces. They don’t “want” anything the way creatures want, but they enforce a rule so strongly that passing humans learn to treat the rule as intention. Read the fringes, or get surprised.

### The second inhabitants: the Skin-in-the-Stone
When you kneel to inspect one of those iridescent films—dry as dust, gone by noon, returning after certain fogs—you’re looking at the basin’s nearest thing to flesh.

Not on the surface, exactly. The surface is too exposed, too scoured clean. The living part—if we allow ourselves that word—retreats into structure:

- into the pores of quartzite where light can penetrate a millimeter without heat,
- into hairline fractures where meltwater pauses before freezing,
- into the sheltered undersides of wind-polished faces where crystals grow and sublimate without being sandblasted.

It exists as a thin community of chemistry: microbes that don’t need leaves because they don’t live by catching sunlight in the open. They live by stealing what the mountain is already doing—feeding on trace minerals, making use of brief wetness, turning oxidation into a slow metabolism.

They rarely announce themselves with color. Instead they leave *effects*:

- a faint petrol sheen that isn’t oily,
- a shift in how frost adheres to one stone versus its neighbor,
- a delicate chalk-bloom after fog that looks like mineral efflorescence until you realize it forms in repeating patches, favoring the same micro-shelters year after year.

Their lives are measured in interruptions. Minutes of moisture. Seconds above freezing. Decades of waiting.

If you could shrink yourself small enough, you’d find that the “sterile” basin has neighborhoods: microscopic zones where temperature gradients and condensation patterns create just enough regularity for persistence. The Skin-in-the-Stone doesn’t spread outward; it deepens. It learns the interior of rock the way burrowing animals learn earth.

### The third inhabitants: the Carried-Here
Not everything that lives here was born here.

The wind brings passengers the way it brings grit: without malice, without care. Most of them die immediately—the place is too bright, too dry, too spare. But some arrive already adapted to being almost nothing.

They live as dormant possibilities:

- spores lodged in mica glitter,
- cysts tucked into the cemented crust of compacted grit,
- tiny, tough-bodied drifters—so small you could mistake them for a moving fleck—caught in a pocket of old snow and preserved more than killed.

They do not establish themselves as a visible ecosystem. They’re more like a seed bank that never becomes a meadow. On the rare day the basin’s central depression holds water long enough to soften the crust, there may be a brief, almost unmeasurable stirring: a microscopic bloom, an awakening, a feeding so fast and small it leaves no trace except that the water darkens slightly, as if it remembered something.

Then the mirror skins over. The moment ends. The Carried-Here return to their true state, which is endurance rather than activity.

They are the basin’s way of refusing to be absolutely closed.

### The fourth inhabitants: the Stone-Listeners (human, but not settled)
The shelter under the overhang is not a home in the ordinary sense. It doesn’t hold heat; it holds *pause*. It’s built to let a body wait out a cloud cycle, to crouch while rime thickens on the ridge, to listen for the small clack that means the slope is shedding.

The people who use it don’t live up here continuously—few could—but some return often enough that the basin recognizes them as a recurring phenomenon, like fog.

They are a thin tradition rather than a population:

- the cairn-builders who maintain the line across the ridge, choosing stones for fit the way others choose words for a sentence;
- the ones who tested the quartz vein, not to mine it—nothing up here is easy enough for greed—but to *know* it, to see if the mountain carried something worth carrying down;
- the one who tied the rope around the rock horn and left it, not because they meant to abandon it, but because the place taught them a rule: you don’t argue with weather, you negotiate by retreating.

If there is a “local” human presence, it’s not a resident body but a resident practice: stacking, sheltering, marking, leaving minimal soot in a stone dish and taking everything else back down. A kind of respectful trespass repeated across years.

And the basin changes them. People who come here more than once begin to move like the place moves: carefully, in short decisions, attentive to edges.

### The oldest inhabitant: the Basin itself, as a slow animal
Eventually you realize that calling the place “a landscape” is almost a category error. It behaves too much like a body with a metabolism.

Its circulation is not blood but phase change:

- fog condenses and becomes beads,
- beads become hoarfrost,
- hoarfrost collapses into trickles,
- trickles seep into cracks and become ice again,
- ice pries the cracks wider, making more places for water to hide.

Its breathing is the cloud sheet that spills in and lifts away.

Its nerves are the temperature gradients you feel as you step from sun into fissure—how instantly the air changes, how sharply the cold is compartmentalized, as if the basin has internal rooms and valves.

It feeds, in its own way, on what it can digest: it eats stone by turning it into grit, then sorting the grit into drifts, then cementing it into crust, then breaking that crust again. It does not hurry. It does not waste effort. It is always, quietly, taking itself apart—and in that dismantling, maintaining its shape.

This is why the place doesn’t feel dead even without plants or animals. Its resident is a process with a long memory.

### What “community” means up here
Down below, life spreads by softening the world—soil forming, roots splitting, leaves shading, nests gathering. Up here, whatever persists does the opposite. It survives by becoming:

- thin enough to hide in stone,
- tough enough to be carried by wind,
- brief enough to live in moments of melt,
- selective enough to thrive only on edges.

So the basin’s inhabitants are not a chorus of creatures. They’re a small set of specialists—some biological, some physical, some human—arranged like the terrain itself: crowded, exact, and tightly bounded.

If you want to know what lives here, look where the moisture chooses to begin. The life of the place—what little there is that qualifies as life, and what else behaves like it—always starts at the knife-lines, the corners, the points.

The basin is an opened seam, and its residents are seam-dwellers: things that can make a living in the narrow difference between wet and dry, sun and shadow, stone and air.
