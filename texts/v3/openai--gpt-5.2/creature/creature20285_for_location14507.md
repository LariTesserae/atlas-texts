What lives up here is, first, the plateau itself—if you allow “itself” to mean an argument made of many throats.

Down in the buoyant dark, below the green skin and its soaked padding, there is a continuous underside of life: peat that is not dead so much as *paused*, and fungi that treat “paused” as just another diet. White threads and translucent cords lace through the drowned leaf-layers, turning fallen wood into a slow, sweet slurry and knitting it back into structure. In the black water under the floating mats, bacteria do the patient work of eating without air; their byproducts feed other mouths you never see. Even stone participates: its smooth domes are filmed with algae and lichens so thin they look like dampness until you notice the faint, deliberate patterns—growth written in slow spirals where mineral seeps out.

If you watch long enough, you learn the first residents are not the visible ones but the *processes*: decomposition as architecture, fermentation as warmth, capillary lift as circulation. The plateau’s “ground” is a living membrane with thickness, and it has moods—taut in a dry spell, slack and buoyant after weeks of cloud.

Above that membrane, the marsh-forest is crowded with citizens whose bodies are less important than their attachments.

There are plants that never really stand alone. Their roots don’t anchor so much as *join*, threading into the communal mat and sharing water and sugars the way muscle fibers share load. Many of the braided-column “trees” are not single organisms at all but long marriages: stems fused when they were pliant, now indistinguishable, their bark grown over old seams like scar tissue that decided to be useful. Epiphytes—tank-plants, orchids, fern-rosettes, mossy cups—turn every branch into a street of small reservoirs. Those reservoirs are the plateau’s most common rooms. They hold rain, fallen pollen, drowned insects, shed skin; they are kitchens and nurseries and graves in the same palm-sized bowl.

In those bowls live the first conspicuous animals: cup-amphibians with slick skin the color of wet bark, their eggs strung like beads along the waterline where air and water meet. They do not lay and leave. A parent will sit in the cup until it becomes part of the scenery, so still that the only proof of life is the tiny widening of pupils when a shadow passes. Some carry water in their bodies, moving from cup to cup during drier days, topping up nurseries like careful stewards. Others farm their own algae—scraping it, stirring it, keeping the water sweet enough for tadpoles that never need a stream.

The insects are not a separate category here; they’re a surface condition.

There are moths with wings that look like mildew bloom and stick to trunks as if they were just another patch of growth. Beetles wear domed shells that bead water perfectly; a whole branch can glitter with them in the dimness, each one a moving droplet until it isn’t. Long-legged walkers skate on pools so still you swear they must be walking on leaf-gloss, their feet dimpling the surface tension without the discourtesy of ripples. Ants run the underside of the mat—out of sight—carrying fungal gardens like little mobile compost-hearts. You’ll find their work as clean, sudden channels in decaying wood and as neat piles of seed husks wedged into root forks like offerings.

Predators exist, but they have learned the plateau’s etiquette: no drama, no noise that isn’t locally absorbed.

There are thread-thin leeches in the warm shallows of the basins, so transparent you notice them only when your skin remembers them later. There are ambush hunters that look like broken twigs until the twig bends the wrong way: mantis-things, slow as dripping water, exploding into motion only once. Higher up, in the understory gloom, something with too many joints climbs through vine-cables and never touches the same surface twice; it keeps its weight distributed, like the plateau prefers. And in the canopy’s dim upper stories, there are snakes that don’t *hang* so much as become a length of living vine, their scales matte and moss-flecked, their stillness a kind of camouflage the whole place approves of.

The gliders are the plateau’s most obvious warm-blooded inhabitants, and even they behave like a secret.

They launch from high boughs with a soft, deliberate unfurling—skin stretched between forelimb and flank, sometimes between hindlimb and tail, sometimes with additional fringes like the edges of leaves. They don’t scream. They don’t snap branches. They drop into the warm air and let the plateau’s slow convection carry them, turning with the gentlest shifts of their wrists. Their fur is short and water-shedding, often dappled to match lichens; their eyes are tuned to contrast in dim green, to movement behind veils. They eat what the plateau makes abundant: fruit that is more water than flesh; insects that are more protein than resistance; occasionally the eggs of cup-amphibians, taken with a quickness that leaves no splash.

Some of them live in family knots in hollow braided columns, lined with peeled moss and the shed skins of epiphyte leaves. You’ll find the nests only if you know what “wrong” looks like in a place where everything is overgrown: a patch of bark rubbed smooth; a particular loop of vine that has been bent and held in a way wind could never manage; a faint, persistent smell like bruised fruit in the air after rain.

Birds are present, but not as landmarks. They’re the plateau’s punctuation.

Small, dark birds with wide tails flit through the layered mid-story like thrown shadows. Their calls are short, dulled notes that seem designed not to travel. There are also heavier things—broad-winged perchers that never fully reveal themselves, only the tremor of leaves as they settle. The plateau is so absorbent that even a large bird can feel like a rumor, and perhaps that’s why their voices sound less like songs than like periodic checks that the fog is still listening.

All of this would be “just” an ecosystem—lush, saturated, strange—if it did not also behave like a coordinated body.

The plateau’s living lattice shares resources with a generosity that borders on intention. If a braided column is damaged, neighboring stems thicken on the windward side *before* the next storm season, as if reinforcement ripples outward through the root-skein. When an epiphyte cup becomes crowded with larvae, the plant that holds it produces more nectar at a different hour, luring predators that keep the nursery from tipping into rot. There are chemical conversations everywhere: warning sours on the air after a leaf is chewed; sweet signals that invite certain ants to patrol; bitter pulses that slow fungal spread in one patch while encouraging it in another. In clear pools where the pale root-tendons are visible, you can sometimes see the faint, rhythmic tightening of root-hairs—micromovements timed to temperature shifts, like a breathing you weren’t meant to notice.

And then there are the stones.

The ring on the rise does not belong to any plant, but it has been accepted into the plant’s grammar so completely that you could mistake it for a natural feature—until you try to imagine erosion placing boulders with such careful gaps. The line of slabs along the basin’s lip reads like a sentence half-erased by moss. The low wall in the clearing has been upholstered so patiently that it feels less like a ruin than like a scar healed into a new shape.

Who lives in relation to those stones is harder to say, because they have taken the plateau’s lesson to heart: life here hides in density, and intelligence—if it exists—would be foolish to announce itself loudly.

Sometimes, at the edge of a high clearing where wind finally arrives, you find the smallest signs of hands: not cut branches, not felled trunks, but *redirected* growth. A vine that has been trained into a handle-loop around a standing slab. A narrow corridor through epiphyte thickets that stays open despite the place’s hunger for closure, maintained by subtle stripping and reweaving rather than clearing. A bundle of shed bark and dried moss tucked under a stone overhang where it will stay just dry enough to catch a spark—except there is no charcoal, no ash, no old burn-scar on the surrounding green.

The inhabitants who make such gestures, when they are seen at all, are almost never seen directly.

They move like the gliders in their respect for quiet, but they are not gliders. They are small—human-sized or smaller—built to distribute weight: long-toed feet that press the mat without sinking, hands that grip living lattice without breaking it. They wear the plateau the way other people wear cloth: layered capes of woven root-fiber, netted hoods that shed water, ornaments made from polished seedpods and the translucent shells of beetles. Their skin, if it is skin, is often stained green-brown by the plants they touch; their scent is masked with crushed leaves and fungal resin.

They do not build houses in the usual sense. Their homes are borrowings: hollows inside braided columns, sheltered saddles between root-buttresses, platforms of living lattice encouraged to thicken until they can carry sleeping bodies. In the wettest basins they use the floating mats themselves—anchoring them with weighted vines, weighting them with stones placed just so, turning buoyant ground into slow-moving rafts that shift with water level but do not drift away.

They are not farmers; the plateau would not tolerate plow-lines. Instead they are *tenders* of certain relationships. They encourage fruiting vines along known routes. They transplant epiphytes from overcrowded branches to new scaffold-trees. They break open seedpods at particular times so the right rodents and beetles carry the seeds into the right damp pockets. They harvest carefully from the cups—larvae, algae, sometimes the frog-parents’ shed skins—taking just enough that the cup remains a nursery next season.

Their most visible work is with stone, because stone is the only material here that resists being entirely rewritten by growth.

A ring of boulders becomes a meeting place not because it is empty but because it is *stable*: a geometry the mat can climb but not erase. Standing slabs on a basin’s lip become boundary markers not of ownership but of obligation—places where the tenders agree not to overharvest, not to disturb a nesting colony, not to redirect a trickle that feeds a downstream peat-hollow. The low wall becomes a backstop for water in heavy seasons, slowing a sheet-flow into a soak, feeding the capillary works that keep the plateau’s internal rain going.

If you listen for these tenders, you won’t hear language in the ordinary way. Their communication suits the plateau: soft clicks that carry only a few meters, throat-hums pitched to resonate through root-wood, hand-signs made close to the body where fog and leaves won’t steal them. Sometimes they use the plateau’s own mechanisms as message: a particular vine looped twice around a slab means “safe crossing”; a cup-plant turned and re-seated in its fork means “nesting above”; a stone set into the mat at an angle tells you where the ground is thin and the old rock will bruise a careless foot.

And there is another possibility, whispered by the way the whole system coheres: that these tenders are not separate from the plateau so much as one of its mobile organs.

If the root-skein has a kind of awareness—distributed, chemical, slow—then a creature that moves through it, feeds it, prunes it, carries its spores and seeds, could be as much a part of its cognition as a hand is part of a body. The plateau cannot walk to the stone ring, but something can. The plateau cannot lift a slab back upright, but something can. The plateau cannot choose, quickly, where to apply pressure in a coming storm, but something that reads wind and water can place a rock today that changes flow for decades.

So what lives here?

A superabundance of small lives nested in water-cups and moss, insects that make the forest’s surfaces feel inhabited even when nothing moves, amphibians that treat plants as cradles, gliders that treat air as a corridor, birds that treat fog as cover, predators that have learned restraint, fungi and microbes that turn death into scaffolding.

And, threaded through all of it, a rare, quiet people—or a set of quiet behaviors performed by whatever the plateau has shaped to serve as its hands—whose oldest craft is not building against nature, but placing the minimum stone in the minimum place so that the living architecture can keep solving itself.

They do not dominate. They persist, like the highlands: not rising, not conquering, simply continuing—patient enough that the forest can grow over their work without erasing it, patient enough that their work can still be read centuries later as a slight, deliberate change in the land’s grammar.
