What lives here starts small, as if life has had to learn to be thin before it can afford to be lush.

First there is the film—an ecology that doesn’t announce itself as living until you watch it long enough. The pale salt blooms in seams are not just mineral accidents; some of them creep, regrouping after a sluice-run, thickening where hands brush the same corner day after day. The green copper blush on hinges comes and goes in patterns that follow warmth, like a bruise that remembers the shape of a palm. In a few sheltered pockets—where a vent sighs just a little warmer, where tallow has been wiped off a tool handle a hundred times—there are smudges that eat what little is offered: soot, skin oil, lime dust. They do not spread like mold in a house; they hold their territory like lichen on a cliff, slow and conditional, living on the idea of “enough.”

From that film come the smallest animals: tile-lice that look like pale grains until they move; thread-thin worms in cistern lips that only exist for the hour after condensation; mothlike things that never learned to fly well because flight has too many edges here and too many sudden downdrafts. They live on shedings—paste crumbs, flour dust, the starch of rope sizing, the mineral froth left when water evaporates. They are not plentiful, but they are persistent, and their persistence is what feeds the next rung up.

The darting beam-runner you glimpse is one of those next rungs: a long-limbed, almost hairless climber with wide, soft pads on its feet (the only softness it can count on is the softness it carries). It knows the underside of catwalks the way a field animal knows grass—routes of warmth above a working chamber, routes of drip below a sweating plate. It doesn’t search for plants; it searches for *use*: a rope wrap where insects collect, a felted handrail where skin flakes and salt gather, a vent grate where warm air draws edible dust into a tidy line. Its nest is not a burrow but a gap: insulation scraps, fiber mats, stolen tassels of cord teased into a pocket between two beams.

Bats live here too, but they behave as if the architecture has trained them into restraint. Their roosts are small, tucked under brackets that don’t vibrate when the winches turn. They fly short, precise loops—less hunting across open sky than skimming along corridors of echo. Their guano makes one of the few truly “fertile” substances in the whole place, so it gets noticed. Someone always notices.

In the canals and sumps, where water becomes an appointment rather than a season, there are things that can afford to be bigger. The eel-shapes in the lower ribbons are not river eels; they are cistern-eels: blunt-headed, dark-skinned, built to push through narrow ceramic troughs and tolerate sudden drought. They wait out the dry stretches in wet cracks, in saturated rope bundles, in the black mud that collects behind a gate that doesn’t seal perfectly. When the sluices run, they move like a tool being drawn—deliberate, economical, leaving only the brief signature of ripples against tile. Sometimes, if you stand with a lantern low, you can see their eyes catch the light like rivet heads.

Above all of this—over it, around it, threaded through it—are the people. Or things like people.

They do not inhabit “buildings” so much as *functions*. You find them where a mechanism needs a body: the treadmill chambers, the bellows platforms, the counterweight shafts. They work in rhythms that match the place’s acoustics, because here sound is not background—it’s navigation, warning, and proof of life. A crew on a winch knows another crew is still turning two districts away by the rope’s tone as it passes through pulleys. A repairer can tell a beam is failing not by sight but by the change in creak when the damp leaves it.

They are builders, but not in the romantic sense of raising monuments. Their craft is maintenance-as-survival. They carry wedges, lashings, tin straps, pegs; their pride is in making a sag stop sagging without having to replace the whole span. Their hands are always dusty with lime, always nicked from wire or wood splinters. Many of them wrap their palms and knuckles in felt or rope fiber—not for comfort, but to preserve skin. Skin is a tool here, and tools are precious.

There are districts of specialists, though “district” is too solid a word for how they distribute themselves.

- **Rope-people** live close to tension. They sleep near anchor points and wake when the pitch of a line changes. Their children learn knots before they learn letters. They talk by tapping: a knuckle code on posts and rails, because voice gets stolen and thrown by the architecture.
- **Tile-setters** live in the basins and canal mouths, where moisture makes everything honest. They smell faintly of wet clay even when dry. Their aprons are stiff with grout dust. They can lay a patch by lantern-light that won’t lift when the next sluice runs.
- **Lamp-keepers** move like careful ghosts, tending the small, shielded lights. They know which niches breathe warm and which ones steal heat. They ration wick, hoard ember-coal like seed, and can read the entire place’s mood by how flames lean in the wind-tunnels.
- **Wayfinders**—the rarest, most quietly valued—make maps that are half geometry, half ritual. In the corridors that fold, they walk the center line with eyes half closed, counting paces, listening to the echo shift. They carry chalk, but they also carry little sounders: a bead on a string, a tongue-click, a tapping rod. They don’t just mark where a path goes; they mark how it behaves.

Food is not grown here; it is allocated, imported, or coaxed from accidents. Workers eat dense pastes and dried slabs brought in on carts that complain over plankways. They chew candied resin for energy when the treadmills need turning. In some hidden pockets, people cultivate the fungal-like smudges deliberately—scraping them into jars, feeding them tallow and warmth until they thicken into something like a sour spread. It isn’t abundant, but it is *local*, and that makes it valuable in a place where “local” usually means stone and metal.

Water creates a calendar, and the calendar makes a culture. When condensation starts, you’ll see buckets appear under known drips like mushrooms after rain—placed by habit, claimed by scratch-marks, defended with sharp words. When a sluice gate is due to open, certain lanes clear, and those who know the routes of permission step aside without being told. There are arguments, sometimes fights, but more often there’s a practiced choreography: the place is too loud for prolonged conflict, too tight for grudges that block passage. You learn quickly that nothing here forgives a body left in the wrong corridor.

There are also those who live *between* the official processes: scavengers and listeners. They haunt the half-finished staircases, the braced stubs, the platforms that “will be something later.” They collect what the constant repairs shed—offcuts of bronze strap, snapped pegs, discarded lashings, the rare fallen rivet like a coin. Some of them trade in information rather than material: which corridor is misbehaving this week, which folded passage is delivering you to the wrong district, which canal gate is sticking and will create a puddle worth claiming. In a landscape where the world is made of intention, knowing someone else’s intention is a form of wealth.

Not everything that lives here is easily classed as animal or person, either.

The built environment has its own semi-life: habits that have become so consistent they feel like will. A beam that always sweats before the drip-cycle peaks. A corridor where echoes arrive early, as if space is impatient. A canal stretch where water hesitates, trembling level, and then chooses left more often than right. People speak of these things the way sailors speak of currents—as personalities without faces. They appease them with small acts: a wedge placed *just so*, a ritual tightening of a strap, a lantern left burning in a niche that “likes light.” Skeptics call it superstition; the practical call it good maintenance. In this place, the difference is mostly tone.

Predation exists, but it is opportunistic and architectural. The cistern-eels take whatever falls in when water runs. The beam-runners steal eggs from bat-roosts. In the deepest stacked levels, there are rumors of larger things—pale, patient, built to live where the sound is heavier and the air is colder—but those rumors tend to follow broken lanterns and missing repair crews, and missing crews are common enough without needing monsters to explain them.

What defines the inhabitants, more than any single species, is that they’ve adapted to a world where *nothing is simply ground*. They don’t think in terms of fields or forests; they think in terms of load, route, access, permission. Home is not a patch of earth but a reliable pocket of warmth near a vent, a sleeping shelf that doesn’t vibrate, a corner where the drip is predictable and the echo tells you who is coming.

They live as the place lives: by bracing, patching, listening, and making do with what the structure is willing to shed. And because the landscape is still coming into being, so are they—less a settled population than a continuing response to an engine that is being assembled around them.
