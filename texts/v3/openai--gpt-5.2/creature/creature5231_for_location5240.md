What lives here is, first, the wetness itself—water as behavior rather than as a thing you can point to. It occupies every crack and joint, turns every surface into a slow mirror, and makes the plateau feel inhabited even when it is empty. If you were looking for the usual signs—tracks, scat, chewed stems, the sudden flick of something startled out of cover—you would conclude, correctly, that the land has never learned that kind of company. The place is populous in other ways: by films, by mats, by colonies, by patient human hands.

### The non-animal multitudes

Along the seep lines and in the shallow trays, life is mostly flat and adherent—organisms that treat rock as a home and water as a road.

- **Algae and cyanobacteria** are the first color you notice when you stop looking for trees. They varnish the basalt where it stays constantly wet, making the stone look freshly oiled. In some basins they form skins so thin you only see them when they tear under a ripple; in others they thicken into soft, dark-green quilts that can be lifted in sheets and laid on racks to dry. The city farms them without needing to call it farming: widen a shallow pan, smooth it, keep the inflow steady, shade it with reed lattice when the light grows too featureless-bright. Harvest happens with wooden combs and patient rinsing.

- **Mosses and liverworts** take the places that are neither fully submerged nor truly dry—those perpetual “almost” surfaces. They grow like upholstery, making the plateau’s youth feel older than it is, as if someone has already softened the rock with cloth.

- **Fungi** do the work that, elsewhere, is done loudly. In storage courts where plant matter is stacked under cover, you can smell the specific sweetness of rot that is not failure but process. Pale fruiting bodies appear at the bases of reed bundles and in the seams of old timber bridges. The people cut some of them for food, leave others alone as if they were part of the city’s plumbing—another way of moving nutrients along.

- **Bacteria** are everywhere and nowhere, shaping the flavor of the place. Without insects to spoil, without animals to introduce sudden chaos, fermentation becomes the main engine of change. What would be compost elsewhere is here a slow, controlled turning: tubs that warm gently, crocks that burp once and then go still, clay-lined pits that make sourness into something dependable.

This is an ecology of surfaces and persistence. Nothing sprints; everything spreads.

### The human presence: a quiet, maintenance-built people

The humans here do not live *against* the plateau. They live as if the plateau is a partner that speaks one sentence—*stay wet*—over and over, and their whole culture is an elaborate, elegant reply.

From a distance you might not notice them at all, because they do not need to announce themselves. There are no herds to manage, no hunting parties, no barking dogs, no bird-scaring clappers in fields. Movement is sparse and purposeful: one figure crossing a causeway with a shoulder pole, another kneeling at a canal edge with a scraper, three more in a cistern court rolling a stone plug like a careful game.

Up close, they look like people who have grown up in air that is always touching them.

- **Skin and hair** are perpetually damp at the edges—behind ears, at the nape, at wrists where sleeves cling. Hair is often kept bound, not for fashion first but because loose hair becomes a wick. You see braids flattened by humidity, knots wrapped in resin-thread, headcloths that are changed often.

- **Clothing** favors layers that accept water rather than pretend to repel it. Woven reed-fiber and bark cloth are common, treated with plant oils that don’t make them waterproof so much as less eager to stay soaked. Cloaks are short and stiffened, designed to keep the shoulders warm without becoming heavy. Sandals are rare; most people wear thin-soled shoes with replaceable uppers, because everything that touches the ground eventually turns to softness.

- **Hands** tell you who is valued. Fingers are callused in specific places: thumb pads worn by rope-work, nails abraded by stone-scraping, knuckles scarred by tile-setting. A person’s role is visible the way a sailor’s is visible, but without salt.

They speak softly, not from timidity but because the world is quiet enough that loudness feels like waste. When voices rise—at a basin court, in a market lane—the sound doesn’t carry far in the damp air, so gatherings feel intimate even when they’re not.

### What they eat when there are no animals

In a land with no birds, no fish, no insects, and no grazing animals, diet becomes a kind of engineering.

- **Tubers and water-loving roots** are the dependable calories: grown in saturated beds bordered by low stone, harvested by hand so the beds aren’t churned into soup. The varieties are selected for being content in constant wet, for storing well without needing dryness.

- **Reeds** are both food and material. The tender inner shoots are eaten fresh; older stems are processed into fiber, mats, basketry, roofing, and the ubiquitous screens that make rooms breathable without becoming cold.

- **Algae cakes, fermented greens, and fungal foods** provide what meat would elsewhere: dense nutrition, strong flavors, the sense of something “satisfying” that isn’t just starch. The city smells faintly of sour and clean at once—like pickling without vinegar, like bread without ovens.

Fire is used cautiously and rarely. Heat is captured more than created: sheltered clay stoves, charcoal kept dry in sealed jars, steam used when open flame would sputter and sulk. Meals are often warmed rather than cooked hard, and a lot of food is prepared by time—soaking, souring, drying under roof-eaves where condensation is guided away.

### Their occupations: keepers of slope, keepers of seep

If you tried to name the professions that matter here, many of them would sound, elsewhere, like “chores.” Here they are civic pillars.

- **Canal-keepers** walk the veins of the city daily. They clear algae where it will cause slip, leave it where it will seal. They know the sound of a healthy runnel (a near-silent, steady glide) and can hear when a channel has begun to silt by the way the water hesitates.

- **Stone-fitters** are the city’s quiet aristocrats. In a world where mortar is a suggestion and swelling chink is the true seal, good stonework is moral work. They can read the plateau’s fractures the way others read grain in wood: which crack will widen, which will stay obedient, where a terrace wall should step back to let the ground have its small decisions.

- **Plug-carvers and stopper-makers**—a specialty that exists because the infrastructure depends on it. Wooden stoppers are cut not just to size but to swell correctly when wet, to be removable without splintering, to last just long enough before they must be replaced.

- **Reed-workers** supply half the city’s softness: floor mats that keep feet from slipping on stone, wall panels that drink condensation, ropes that stay strong when damp, scaffolds that can be rebuilt every season without anyone mourning the loss of “permanent” timber.

- **Fermenters** are both cooks and custodians. In a society where time does the transforming, they become time’s managers—tasting, sealing, moving crocks from warm to cool, teaching apprentices the exact point when “green” becomes “good.”

Children learn by being given water tasks early—carry, pour, sweep channels, listen. The first civic lesson is that water is never “out there.” It is always in the next joint, the next step, the next breath.

### How they organize themselves: a city run like a watershed

The plateau’s predictability produces a social structure that values steadiness over heroics.

Authority tends to collect around maintenance rather than conquest. The closest thing to a ruling class is a set of councils or guilds responsible for flows: who opens which cistern plug, who gets first draw when algae blooms are thick, which district’s basin is due for re-lining. Disputes are less about land boundaries (there is space) and more about *gradients*: whose runoff crosses whose court, whose terrace wall redirects seepage toward whose foundation.

Public squares are large because water needs room—and because conflict, in a quiet city, is best handled in open air where everyone can see the channel lines and point to the facts of slope and seep. Argument here is often done with gestures toward stone: not “I want,” but “if we do this, the basin will silt.”

Their ceremonies, when they have them, are subdued and practical. A new canal segment is opened with a crowd watching the first thin sheet of water take its route, not with cheers but with a collective exhale—as if listening for whether it will behave. A repaired bridge is walked across slowly by many feet, not to test it in secret but to let the timber learn its load.

### Their inner life: what they believe in, given this world

In places with animals, people borrow metaphors from flight and hunt and song. Here, meaning comes from wet stone and slow accumulation.

They tend to think in terms of **paths** and **settling**. Patience is not framed as virtue so much as realism: everything in their world works by incremental overflow. Memory is often described as a basin—what you keep, what you let pass on, what silts up if you don’t tend it.

If they have sacred things, they are likely not distant gods but local rules: the reliability of gravity, the honesty of water (it will always show you where the lowest point is), the danger of ignoring small changes. A person who can “read” a new seep before it becomes a problem is treated with a respect that borders on reverence.

Death, too, is less dramatic without predators. Bodies return to the city’s slow chemistry. Burial might mean placement in specially lined pits where decomposition is managed cleanly, or exposure in reed-woven chambers where fungi and time do their work without smell becoming offense. The dead become part of the same system as roof runoff and basin overflow: handled with care, directed, never wasted.

### The city as a living thing

If you stand in one of the cistern courts long enough, you start to sense that the primary inhabitant is not any single person but the **collective practice**—the way many small, repeated actions keep the whole arrangement from slumping into swamp.

A lone traveler might see only stillness and absence. A resident sees a thousand ongoing adjustments: a reed screen shifted to change drying time; a silt line scraped before it becomes a ridge; a roof tile re-set so condensation drips into the runnel rather than the doorway. Their lives are made of these edits. Not a battle, not a romance—an intimacy with a plateau that refuses to be dry and refuses, also, to be dramatic.

So what lives here?

A warm, wet highland that has never hosted animals; a thick, self-sufficient community of plants and microbes; and a human city that has evolved into something like a second watershed—stone and clay shaped into habit, inhabited by people who have learned to measure time by the sound of a drip changing its mind.
