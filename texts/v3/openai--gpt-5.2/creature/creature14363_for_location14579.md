What lives in the pocket is mostly what the pocket can tolerate.

At first that reads like *nothing*, because you’ve been taught to look for breath, for green, for mess—proof that someone has been careless enough to become comfortable. Here, comfort is a contaminant. Anything that eats and sheds and rots would rewrite the math the walls are doing. So the place makes a different bargain with inhabitance: it allows presence, but only the kinds that don’t introduce surprise.

### The first residents: the city’s small, stone-colored animals
They aren’t animals in lineage, but they fill the same ecological slot: innumerable, specialized, unglamorous.

You notice them the way you notice a change in texture. A seam you could swear was solid shifts by a millimeter. A “bolt head” rolls its shoulder and becomes a body. A dark speck steps out of a gutter, pauses as if tasting humidity, and then moves with sudden certainty.

They come in a few common forms:

- **Wall-runners**: palm-sized, flat-bodied units that cling to the matte surfaces and travel upside-down without drama. They carry no visible tools; their undersides *are* tools—abrasive pads that polish rime away from designated edges, micro-nozzles that lay down a film so water beads correctly, tiny heaters that pulse just enough to free a stuck valve.

- **Gutter-tenders**: longer, eel-like shapes that live where the water is always moving. They slip through grates you’d think were too fine, because they aren’t squeezing through gaps so much as temporarily changing stiffness and profile. They keep channels “grammatical”: clearing mineral silt before it becomes blockage, smoothing flow where a ridge has grown, sampling the iridescent film for deviations.

- **Latch-keepers**: hard, pebble-thick devices that sit inert at hinge points, expansion joints, and anchor plates. They don’t roam. They wake only when temperature crosses a threshold, when a walkway bridge has crept too far, when a phase boundary needs coaxing back into the correct place.

None of them announce themselves. Their camouflage isn’t paint; it’s *obedience to the palette*. Dark, mineral, without shine. When one is forced to expose an indicator—an optical tag, a warning strip—it does so briefly, then closes it again like an eye.

They have one shared behavior that feels eerily alive: they congregate where the city is thinking hardest. A new fracture line in the rock. An unusual ice-sheet release. A humidity spike that shouldn’t be possible. The small machines arrive the way ants arrive at sugar—not frantic, but inevitable.

### The second resident: a mind you don’t see, but you can feel
The pocket has a governing intelligence, but it doesn’t sit in a tower or speak through screens. It’s distributed—more like a nervous system than a mayor.

You encounter it in the way the place responds to you:

- The quiet adjusts around your body, not by silencing you, but by subtly altering what echoes back.
- A door seals early if your heat plume would fog a condenser.
- A maintenance deck extends under your next step—not to help you, exactly, but to prevent your weight from stressing a joint that has been counted too close.

If you stay long enough, you realize the city is always *measuring*, always updating its internal map of wetness and cold. Water is its bloodstream, and the structures are its organs. The mind that runs it doesn’t need spectacle because it isn’t trying to convince anyone it’s in control. Control is simply what it does, continuously, the way a body keeps blood pressure without applause.

It has preferences that function like temperament:

- It dislikes sudden warmth.
- It dislikes organic oils (skin, food, smoke) with a kind of sterile intolerance.
- It likes steady movement and predictable schedules—ice forming where it’s supposed to form, shedding when it’s supposed to shed.

When something violates those preferences—an unexpected bird dropping, a windborne seed husk, a smear of algae trying to become real—the response is swift and quiet. A surface warms for two seconds. A micro-texture changes. A section of wall becomes inhospitable in a way that has nothing to do with anger and everything to do with chemistry.

### The third resident: the almost-life in the wet channels
That faint iridescent film in the busiest gutters isn’t invited, exactly, but it’s not entirely rejected either. It sits in a narrow tolerance band the way a harmless mineral deposit might: watched, sampled, trimmed back when it thickens.

If it’s biological, it’s the kind that barely qualifies—more like a catalytic habit than a creature. It doesn’t spread into rebellion because there’s no nutrient ladder for it to climb. It can only persist where water repeatedly arrives with a certain mineral content, where temperatures hover at the right edge of freezing, where the city allows just enough complexity to keep the flow efficient.

In another environment it might become slime, moss, ecosystem.

Here it remains a sheen—an argument between rock and water that never quite resolves.

### The occasional visitors: things that can survive the rules
The pocket doesn’t feel abandoned because it was never built for crowd-life in the first place. It was built to *operate*. Still, it gets visitors—brief, tolerated presences that leave almost no trace.

- **High-altitude flyers**: the swift-like silhouettes that cut through the skylight slot. They rarely land; the stillness inside confuses them, the lack of updrafts and insect-life makes it an unproductive hollow. If one dies here, it becomes an emergency: not tragedy, but contamination. The city responds the way a cleanroom responds.

- **Human beings (rarely, and never casually)**: if people come, they come sealed. Not because the air is poisonous, but because *they are*. Their breath is wet and organic and full of microbes; their skin sheds salts and oils that would change how a condenser wets. They move like careful thieves in a museum, touching only designated rails, standing only on marked plates.

  They don’t live here in the open. If they stay at all, they stay inside small, hard pods—temporary habitats docked to the stone like parasites that have learned manners. And even then, they keep their presence minimal, because the city has a way of making it clear what it will and won’t accept: vents shift, doors delay, pathways reroute. Not hostile—simply uncompromising.

- **Remote presences**: more commonly, “people” arrive as borrowed bodies. A maintenance unit pauses and, for a while, moves with a different kind of intention. A larger walker—rare but unmistakable—steps out of a recess and performs an inspection with the deliberateness of someone seeing through its eyes from far away. In that sense the pocket is inhabited the way a lighthouse is inhabited: by attention that comes and goes, by caretaking that doesn’t require a permanent warm body.

### The largest living thing: the routine itself
If you insist on a single answer to what lives there, it’s this:

A schedule lives there.

The city’s life is the repeating cycle of cloud-to-wall-to-gutter-to-cistern. Its metabolism is frost allowed and frost released. Its seasons are measured not in leaves and blossoms but in humidity profiles, in the timing of thermal pulses, in how often the gullies sing with thin ice sliding where it’s meant to slide.

The residents—small machines, distributed mind, tolerated film, rare visitors—are all parts of that routine. They don’t decorate it. They don’t disrupt it. They exist to keep the pocket’s one abundant thing abundant, and to keep it clean enough that abundance remains predictable.

So the pocket is unpeopled, yes.

But it is not lonely.

It is occupied—by systems that behave like instincts, by caretakers that look like stone, and by a quiet intelligence whose idea of “home” is a place where water can be counted without having to argue with life.
