What lives here learns the place the way a land animal learns weather: not as scenery, but as law.

At first you think “nothing that could be called a resident”—just the incidental tenancy of small bodies taking advantage of all that made surface. Then the longer you let your lamp hover and your hands drift along the seams, the clearer it becomes that this is not a reef that happened to grow on architecture. It is architecture that learned to be a reef without ever surrendering the habits of being built. The inhabitants are not merely adapted to structure; many of them behave as if structure is a second skeleton, something you wear by touching it correctly.

### The primary citizen: the film that eats stone
The most numerous life here is almost invisible: the skins in the joints, the breath-thin bacterial mats that make the complex feel *maintained*. They are not exuberant. They don’t plume or wave. They hold tight to corners where leaching is richest, and they work by patience—oxidizing trace metals, cracking salts, turning the place’s slow corrosion into food.

They make flakes, and the flakes are currency. Not metaphorically: nearly every mouth in this district is built to either harvest the mats directly or to harvest what harvests them. When the crusts thicken and release, the “snowfall” does not drift far. It settles into intended traps: troughs, ribs, scalloped thresholds. A city that feeds itself by letting its own walls shed.

The films are also memory in a place without light. They spread preferentially along old traffic routes where oils and waste once touched stone; they are more eager in the grooves your fingers keep finding, less so on untouched faces. Even now, long after the tool was last lifted, the skins still outline where hands *used* to be.

### Colonies that fit the grooves like joinery
On every surface that offers purchase—especially where block meets block—there are dense colonies of anchored soft bodies: tunicate-like filter-feeders whose siphons are not randomly arranged but keyed to the geometry. Their attachment pads match the slight convexity of the blocks; their mouths sit neatly in the carved grooves that funnel water during the complex’s slow “breaths.”

They live in neighborhoods.

A ledge will have a continuous band of them, all opening and closing in delayed unison, not because they are “one organism” but because they’re listening to the same pressure changes and responding the same way. When the water level begins to shift, you can watch the band tighten like a belt—siphons narrowing, bodies dimpling in—so the flow doesn’t tear them.

When they feed, they make almost no sound, but they make *texture*: a faint tremor in the water, a soft, collective flex. In a place where sound dies young, that texture is legible. Other creatures read it the way birds read wind.

### The climbers: jointed, armored, deliberate
Between the colonies move the crustacean analogues—hand-sized and larger, hard-backed, jointed, able to cling to vertical faces with a confidence that makes you suspect gravity is more negotiable here than you were taught.

Their claws are not general tools. They are shaped like specialized keys: curved to bite the regular grain, angled to lock into the shallow repeating texture of the blocks. When they climb, they don’t “scramble.” They *index* themselves—click, settle, click, settle—finding the pattern the way a safe dial finds notches.

They are the ones that tap.

Not constantly, not loudly: brief sequences against ceramic, a code designed for this place’s acoustics. Too sharp a sound would smear; too long a signal would be wasted. So they speak in punctuations, in little percussive packets, often answered not by sound but by a faint change in someone’s lantern-organ or by a brush of antennae in a doorway. Many of them carry bioluminescent patches, but the light is miserly—blue bruises, amber pinpricks—only enough to confirm identity at arm’s length.

They farm without plants. You’ll see them pausing at seams, “raking” with a forelimb that has a comb of chitin teeth, lifting mat-flakes into a mouth hidden under armor. You’ll see them guard certain joints more aggressively than others, and you realize those seams are not random: they’re reliable sources, like wells.

### Corridor fish and niche predators
The fish are built for rooms, not oceans—thick through the middle, fins that can brake and reverse, bodies that can pivot in narrow throats without scraping. Some are blind, their heads blunt and scarred in ways that suggest constant intimate contact with walls. Others have eyes like mirrors, huge pupils that return your lamp with an unhelpful glare.

They feed like city scavengers. Many graze the skins or pick parasites from larger bodies. A few are true predators, but their predation is architectural: they don’t chase; they *wait* where waiting pays.

A doorway is an ambush machine. A gap behind a screen is a blind. A carved groove becomes a guide rail for a sudden strike. You’ll learn that when a fish vanishes, it didn’t “swim away.” It folded into a recess you hadn’t noticed because the recess is shaped like every other recess until it contains intent.

### The living infrastructure: the one that breathes through ribs
The broad leathery creature wedged into the floor lattice is not an oddity; it’s a category. There are others—sometimes smaller, sometimes so large they occupy a whole chamber the way a furnace occupies a room.

They are not merely animals living *in* the build; they are animals that seem designed to use the build as an organ. The rib grids, the channels, the smoothened slots—those aren’t just drainage or ornament. They are gill-ways.

These creatures pulse slowly, moving water with minimal turbulence, turning the complex’s breathing into a regulated exchange. Waste catches where their currents are weakest and gets eaten by the films and filter colonies. In return, the place stays habitable: warm, dense, stable. They are ecosystem and pump in one body.

Their eyes—when they open them—do not shine. They drink light instead of reflecting it, as if reflection would be too loud a gesture here. And they are tolerant of touch, not because they are tame, but because touch is how everything in this place knows what it is.

### The hidden residents: small, fast, and almost certainly watching
There are smaller things too quick to identify cleanly: ribbonlike swimmers that ghost through beams, thread-thin worms that live inside the mineral braid of old cord, tiny shelled mites that harvest the powder in seams and leave trails of cleaned ceramic behind them.

And then there are the eyes.

Not the obvious mirror-eyes of fish, but fields of tiny reflective points that vanish when you look directly. They cluster at the edge of your lamp’s circle, in the screenwork, above doorframes, in the fur of stalactite-minerals where water trickles too slowly to notice. The vanish is not magic. It’s behavior: they angle away, close shutters, flatten themselves, turn their reflective surfaces to matte. They have learned that attention is a form of predation in a close-range world.

### The question of “who”: whether anything like people remain
The tool behind the slats is the strongest smell of personhood—not because it’s sophisticated, but because it is *placed*.

The place itself preserves intention the way it preserves knots: by mineral replacement, by refusing disturbance. That means one of two things is true:

1) The makers are gone, and the ecosystem has been living on their leftover order for so long that even abandoned gestures remain legible; or  
2) The makers—or something descended from them—still operate here on timescales you aren’t used to calling “active.”

The tapping suggests the second possibility, but it doesn’t have to be language. It could be territorial code between crustacean clans, or mating signal, or simple navigation—“occupied, occupied, safe.” Yet some patterns repeat with the same spacing you’d expect from a trained habit rather than instinct, and they often occur near thresholds that matter: baffles, valves, the throats between chambers.

If there is an intelligence here, it is not a bright-eyed, tool-in-hand intelligence. It is an intelligence that lives by adjacency and maintenance:

- Bodies that rarely travel far, because travel is expensive when sound and light don’t carry.
- Communication that rides the architecture (vibration, pressure changes) rather than the water.
- Work that looks like “stillness” until you realize stillness is how you avoid wasting food, stirring silt, alerting predators, or breaking the delicate peace of the mats.
- A culture of positions: *this ledge is mine, this seam feeds us, this valve must not clog, this screen must hang a certain way.*

You can imagine them—if they exist—not as a crowd, but as a few, each attached to a circuit of chambers the way a beekeeper is attached to hives. They would be pale or armored or both, with hands or hand-analogues built for gripping the regular grain, eyes that see little but feel everything, and a sense of time calibrated to the complex’s long breathing.

They would not light the place. Light is wasteful, and waste is loud.

### How the residents relate to the “breathing” of the complex
Every living thing here is synced, loosely, to the slow rise and fall of water—this handspan tide that is not a tide but a valve cycle.

When the level begins to shift:

- Filter colonies tighten and wait out the flow.
- Climbers wedge themselves into known notches, locking their bodies so they don’t have to spend energy holding on.
- Corridor fish retreat into alcoves that are shaped, suspiciously, like fish bodies—old resting hollows made by repetition.
- Predators choose doorways that will become more effective as currents change.
- The mats in certain seams brighten microscopically (not with light, but with activity), taking advantage of fresh mineral exposure as pressure changes flex the joints.

If you could stay long enough, you’d notice this cycle is not just tolerated; it’s used. The architecture breathes, the animals behave, the films feed, the whole district doing what it does with the calm coordination of something that has never needed sun.

### What “home” means in a world that never learned green
Nothing here is wild in the sense of sprawling. It’s all *placed*—if not by hands, then by the inheritance of hands. Life has become a form of occupancy: a right to a seam, a ledge, a notch, a ribbed grid that breathes.

The residents are, collectively, the continuation of the construction by other means.

They don’t build upward into forests. They build inward into joints, into skins, into mineral braids that replace cord, into polished corners made by centuries of brushing past. They make the complex more itself: quieter, smoother, more sealed, more densely alive in the dark.

And if something like a caretaker still moves here—slow, rare, patient enough to let a tool remain untouched until the moment it’s needed—it will not announce itself with lanterns or speech. It will announce itself the way the place announces its boundaries: by touch first, then by the faintest change in how your sound dies, then by the feeling that the architecture, for a moment, is not merely holding water.

It is listening back.
