What lives here is not one population but a layering of tenancies—some with names, some without, some that do not think of themselves as “living” until you come close enough to be counted as company.

### The city itself, first: an inhabitant made of rules
Before you meet anyone with a face, you meet the resident intelligence distributed through matter. It does not present as a central mind. It is a habit of coordination.

It lives in the way the thin pavement-panels pulse, not to illuminate but to *agree*; in the cables braided into corners like nerves laid down during construction and never removed; in the walls that “read” your glove as patterned resistance. It is an organism whose cells are conduits, whose bloodstream is synchronization, whose immune response is a mild lateral gravity tug that clears a corridor of loose objects before a seam shifts.

It remembers by surface: a matte patch on a handrail where palms have passed in similar moods; a doorway that aligns more readily after being touched; a corner that becomes more corner-like because enough attention has been paid to it at the right hour. If it has a language, it is spoken in phase changes—frost that composes and erases itself above the plinth, condensation that beads in grids when an adjustment is imminent, shadows that lag by a fraction as if thought is slightly slower than sight.

This is not a hostile resident. It is unfinished. It is practicing being consistent.

### The visible humans: seam-keepers, not crowds
People live here, but in a way that makes “population” feel like the wrong unit. You see them in ones and twos, rarely in groups larger than a handful, moving with the calm competence of those who have stopped expecting the world to hold still.

They dress for cold that steals heat without drama: layered cloth that looks ordinary until you notice it never flutters, as if it has learned to ignore air currents that may not be reliable; gloves with fingertip patches of slightly different material for touching lintels, railings, and reference points; boots that do not squeak or click, soles tuned to surfaces that sometimes behave like disciplined granules rather than poured stone.

Their faces are often half-covered—not for anonymity, but to keep breath from writing temporary weather onto their own vision. Their eyes do a lot of work. They look the way divers look: scanning for the shape of an unseen current.

They have the city’s etiquette in their bodies:
- pausing a fraction before crossing the plaza’s seam, like stepping over a threshold you can’t photograph;
- holding a strap tighter when passing certain ribbed conduits because the sideways tug can arrive as gently as a thought;
- choosing routes not by shortest distance but by *most stable agreement* between blocks.

They speak softly, because sound doesn’t travel far anyway, and because loud insistence feels rude in a place where geometry is negotiable. Conversation is full of practical verbs—*align, settle, mark, wait, follow*—and of time qualifiers that would sound like superstition elsewhere but are simple scheduling here: “before the second groan,” “while the gutter ice is layered,” “when the bridge is gridding.”

They are not tourists. They are caretakers by necessity.

Some are openly technical: you’ll see them in narrow service corridors, palms on a vibrating railing, listening with their skin for a high-frequency message. They carry small tools that are less “wrenches” than *politeness devices*—things for persuading a seam to hold, for giving a corner permission to remain a corner, for feeding a cable-joint the right warmth so it stops ticking.

Others are archivists of a stranger kind. Because the city repeats junctions and folds its grid, reliable maps are hard to keep as ink-on-paper. The locals keep maps as habits: sequences of touches, angles of approach, the memory of a certain smell of ozone and mineral dust after the thin rain. A practiced resident can “read” a street by how frost chooses to form on its handrail.

And some are simply dwellers—people who have made homes in the dense lattice because it is quiet, because the city holds darkness rather than fights it, because the world here changes slowly enough that you can learn its moods. Their domestic life adapts: furniture weighted so it won’t drift during a preference shift; shelves with lips, cups with broader bases, doors that are trained with repeated touch to become doors again when needed.

They do not conquer the place. They negotiate with it daily, and over years that negotiation becomes a culture.

### The unseen workforce: distributed systems that behave like fauna
A second population moves through the infrastructure the way small mammals move through hedgerows—present mostly as evidence.

In ventilation seams you sometimes hear the faint scratching: not frantic, not scared, just busy. Whatever makes that sound is small enough to vanish into a crack and disciplined enough to stop moving the moment it risks being perceived. It might be biological, it might be mechanical, it might be a hybrid that the city’s ecology produced because a sealed conduit is as good a burrow as any.

These are the city’s little custodians: dust-gatherers that leave crescents that make hidden slopes readable; thread-thin things that tug a cable into better contact; minute agents that maintain the “disciplined surface” of granules held by invisible constraint. They don’t announce themselves as robots. They behave more like insects that evolved inside an archive: silent, task-focused, and polite enough to withdraw from your attention.

You can infer their routes by what changes without explanation:
- a patch of wall that becomes slightly more matte over a week, as if groomed;
- a conduit that stops whispering for an hour and then resumes on a steadier note;
- a recurring motif in the lichen-like patterns that looks less like random growth and more like a maintenance signature.

### Microbial and mineral residents: life that learned to write with structure
The most abundant life here is thin, slow, and exact.

In damp pockets beneath walkways, the iridescent biofilms shimmer not like oil but like layered engineering. They respond when you move your head—changing color with angle—not as a trick but as a literal record of stratification. If you watch long enough, you realize they aren’t merely surviving on neglect; they are exploiting the city’s local laws, making use of condensation that comes in pockets and gravity that sometimes slides sideways.

The lichens (if “lichen” is even the right word) do not blotch. They repeat. They settle into motifs as if the alloys are instructing them where to anchor, which suggests a partnership: substrate offering information, biology answering with pattern. Their “growth” looks like calibration—an organism behaving as a sensor array because in this place that is a viable niche.

Even the stone is a resident in a young mood. Mineral lines align and wander and re-align, practicing being stone. In a city where time does not distribute evenly, a rock can be both newborn and ancient along the same curb. That instability is not just background; it is habitat. Microbes take up residence in the hesitation.

### The rare animals: visitors that don’t always stay animals
Fauna are scarce, but not absent—more like shy, intermittent edits the city permits.

Three-toed tracks appear in frost and then stop at a wall, as if the animal walked into a seam and chose a different state. Birds pass overhead as silhouettes through narrow sky-canyons and never land where you can see them. If something larger lives here, it has learned the chief survival strategy of this basin: *do not insist on a single shape.*

The locals treat these animals with the same calm respect they give to shifting geometry. They don’t chase them. They don’t try to photograph them. In a place where observation can stiffen corners into corners, attention feels like a kind of pressure.

### The plaza-crossers: a guild of people trained to step between versions
Near the center—where the plaza changes width depending on how you enter—there is a small class of residents whose whole work is crossing and keeping crossings possible.

They are easy to miss because they look like anyone else until you watch their gait: a careful, habitual stepping as if they are counting invisible tiles. They know where the seam is not by sight but by a bodily sense of tilt, the way a sailor knows when a deck has changed angle by a degree.

They carry no banners. Their authority is procedural. They leave marks that are not marks: a touch of oil on lintel, a breath held at the right moment so condensation doesn’t declare a wrong boundary, a pause that lets a hallway decide to be the same length on the way back.

To outsiders this seems like mysticism. To them it is maintenance.

### What they believe—without turning it into religion
If you ask why they stay, answers vary, but they orbit a shared recognition: this city is not ruin and not utopia. It is an experiment still in its gentle phase, a settlement built inside a world that is curing.

Some speak of it as shelter: the cold keeps decay slow; the quiet keeps minds from fraying. Some speak of it as duty: someone has to keep the laws local enough to live by. Some speak of it as opportunity: where reality stratifies, you can sometimes retrieve what other places have lost—data, matter, even time—caught in seams like sediment.

But most don’t make speeches. They live by rules because rules are how you keep your cup from sliding uphill for a finger’s length and then shattering.

They are, in the end, a people who have learned that stability is not a given. It is a relationship. And the city—dense, cold, damp in pockets, humming softly with distributed intention—seems willing to be in relationship back, as long as you pay in attention and move with care.
