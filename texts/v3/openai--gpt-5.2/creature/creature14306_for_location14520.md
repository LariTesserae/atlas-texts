What lives here is whatever can make peace with three rulers: noise, cold, and moving air.

At first glance that seems like it ought to mean “almost nothing.” And in the ordinary sense—things you’d recognize as wildlife, or a human settlement with streets and voices—you’d be right. But if you stop expecting life to announce itself with motion and sound, you notice that the basin is full of inhabitants. They simply live at the speed and scale the place permits.

### The dominant resident: the mat that isn’t one plant

The “vegetation” is less a collection of discrete organisms than a negotiated surface: a layered skin of lichens, algae, mosslike growths, and fungi, all braided into something that behaves like a single, opportunistic material.

Up close it has structure. A dull, felted top layer—designed to catch fog and hold a boundary of stiller air—over a denser underlayer that grips stone and metal with root-hairs and adhesive threads. In the seams where warm ducts sweat, the mat is thicker, darker, more gelatinous, as if it’s allowed to keep more water there. In the dry, high-blast corridors it thins into a scab: flat, stingy, nearly mineral, present mostly because it can’t quite be removed.

It doesn’t “spread” the way a garden plant spreads, by taking territory through height or canopy. It spreads by learning every microclimate the machinery creates—every lee-side pocket behind a beam, every temperature gradient along a cable, every place condensation pauses before becoming drip. The mat is a cartographer of airflow. If you could read it the way you read rings in wood, you’d see weeks of humidification, days of increased fan speed, a recent freeze-up in a duct that rerouted moisture into a new seam.

In some areas it has become a kind of infrastructure, but not because it’s benevolent. It’s simply the only thing that can remain reliably present. Where humans once strung temporary ropes or netting, the plant-fungal braid has copied the function: fibers thickened, sap stiffened, then the whole span taken over by living tension. It makes bridges between supports because supports are where heat leaks and where condensation collects—life follows the same logic as engineering.

If there’s an intelligence to it, it’s not the kind that plans. It is the intelligence of persistence: thousands of tiny responses to tiny changes, so that over time the basin’s surfaces look less like excavated rock and more like they’ve been upholstered.

### The small, hidden consumers: the things that eat the mat

Anything larger than a hand would struggle here, but small bodies do well precisely because the place is mostly edges—film-thin water, microns of frost, dust trapped in rivets, nutrient traces on handrails.

In the sheltered folds of the mats are the grazers: pale mites, springtail-like jumpers that you almost never see move because they freeze when vibration changes, and soft-bodied larvae that spend their lives pressed against damp surfaces, shaving off biofilm and fungal threads. Their presence is what makes those “neat grazed patches,” little scalloped bites in an otherwise continuous skin.

Where water collects, the basin supports a different set of residents: wormlike scavengers under ice, midge larvae in the thin oxygen band where slow water meets cold air, hardy little crustacean forms that tolerate long periods of stillness. They don’t thrive in abundance; they endure. They wait out dryness the way desert animals do, except the “drought” here is moving air that steals moisture instead of sun that evaporates it.

Even bacteria and mineral-loving microbes are part of the visible ecology. Some of the staining on rock—iron-dark fans and rust-colored halos around bolts—comes from living chemistry, communities that make a meal of trace metals in seepwater. In a place this young and raw, where fresh-cut stone is everywhere, there’s a lot for that kind of life to do.

Predators exist, but they’re minimal and local: a centipede-thin hunter in a warm equipment bay, a beetle that has learned to live in the insulation of a cable run, feeding on the grazers that feed on the mat. The food chain is short because the energy budget is short.

### The rarest animals: the ones that visit by accident

There are almost no deliberate visitors from the surface ecosystem. Not birds, not mammals, not anything that needs quiet, warmth, or open foraging.

What does arrive comes as stowaways: eggs in mud on a boot, larvae in packaging, a spider in a crate corner that survives just long enough to make a small geometry of webbing in a calm niche—then disappears when the air dries, or the temperature dips, or the roar shakes its sense of “safe.” The basin doesn’t welcome them; it merely fails to kill them immediately.

That harsh selectivity is part of why the place feels empty. Life is present, but it doesn’t broadcast itself with abundance.

### The intermittent residents: the humans who can tolerate it

Human beings live here only in the way sailors “live” at sea: on shifts, in pockets, in structures designed to keep the environment from being fully true. Their homes are not in the basin itself but in insulated rooms inside pump houses and service sheds—boxes within the box—where the roar is reduced to a low physical pressure and the air is warmer by a few stolen degrees.

They’re maintenance workers first. Air is law in this place, and laws require caretakers: people who clear ice from louvers, replace filter media, reseal pressure doors, coax a turbine bearing through another month. Their tools are practical—scrapers, sealants, portable heaters, vibration meters—and their habits are shaped by the constant sound. They don’t shout; shouting is theatrical. They communicate by touch on sleeves, by light signals, by practiced hand language, by writing on fogged visors, by leaning close enough to trade breath.

Over time they develop a particular bodily awareness: not “hearing,” exactly, but an intimate reading of vibration through bone and glove. They know when a fan is slightly off-balance because the catwalk feels different. They know when a duct has iced up because the pitch changes in a way you don’t hear so much as *feel in your jaw*. They become sensitive to the basin the way people who live near rail yards become sensitive to trains without noticing they are.

Some of them also tend the living growth, whether officially or just because it’s impossible not to. If you want a cable to stay accessible, you strip the mat back. If you want a slope to stop shedding gravel onto a walkway, you encourage the runners to knit it. There’s a quiet horticulture here, but it looks like maintenance: pruning a braided span so it doesn’t torque a support, guiding growth onto a sacrificial panel so it doesn’t creep into a vent intake, packing a warm leak with absorbent fiber that the mat will later colonize and turn into a gasket of its own.

You can tell where people have been not by footprints (the noise makes the place feel trackless) but by *clearances*: the clean rectangles around control panels, the scraped handholds where gloves have worn the growth smooth, the tidy channels where someone has repeatedly pushed back the same encroaching skin.

They are not numerous. The basin is too loud, too cold, too indifferent to support a crowd. The humans here live as islands, just as their structures do.

### And then, in a way that’s easy to miss: the basin itself behaves like an organism

Nothing supernatural is required for this. A system can be “alive” in the sense of having appetites and rhythms even if it’s made of steel and rock.

The industrial lungs regulate what every other resident can do. When airflow changes, the mat rearranges itself over weeks. When warmth collects in a transformer bay, insects concentrate there. When a duct seal fails and humidity rises, a new patch of life appears as if the basin has grown a bruise. The place eats electricity and exhales wind; it sweats condensation; it sheds ice. It is a machine that accidentally makes habitats, and a habitat that cannot exist without its machine.

So what lives here?

A living upholstery of slow, stubborn growth; a thin society of tiny grazers and colder-water scavengers; and, passing through like careful ghosts, a few humans trained to work without voices. All of them are shaped by the same constant pressure: the roar that makes the basin feel less like a space you occupy and more like a weather you endure.
