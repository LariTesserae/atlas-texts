What lives here is mostly not a cast of creatures so much as a set of occupations.

The district still acts like a town: things have addresses, routes, habits; there are neighborhoods with different rules. But the citizens are not the ones it was built for. Its inhabitants are the kinds of lives that can make use of warm, metal-rich water, slow currents, and architecture that has turned into a trellis.

### 1) The first residents: films, crusts, and the quiet chemistry of surfaces
Before you meet anything with eyes, you meet the city’s skin.

Every beam and pane is inhabited by layered films—bacterial sheets, mineralizing colonies, algae that don’t form waving “plants” so much as patient, velvety coatings. They live where the flow is right: on the upstream edges of pylons, along the lips of ducts, around bolt heads that create miniature eddies.

In the better-lit lanes these films are greened, stippled, gardenlike. In the darker service corridors they go nearly black, slick as old oil. Where water chemistry turns strange—metallic taste, low oxygen—the films become thicker and more specialized, laying down pale mineral scabs that seal cracks and seams. They do it the way reefs do it, but with an industrial accent: calcium and iron and whatever else the district leaches, set down into collars and sleeves and enamel.

They are the district’s true population density. Everything else lives on them, in them, or because of them.

### 2) The “plants”: brine-trained vines that build with the city
The lush growth is not one species but a guild: fronds, ribbons, feather-plumes, and root-mats that have learned to treat steel like driftwood and rebar like mangrove.

They don’t just occupy space. They engineer it.

- **Load-bearing mats** strap collapsed walkways into hammocks, not by strength alone but by distributing weight through a whole knitted sheet of fibers and mineral grit. The mats are full of pockets—tiny sheltered rooms where silt stays oxygenated and edible.
- **Pipe-climbers** follow warmth and trace elements the way land vines follow sun. They thicken around warm conduits, making those corridors feel more alive and, paradoxically, less hospitable to animal life; the water there can be too charged, too metallic, too chemically “busy.”
- **Curtain growths** hang in doorways and stairwells and behave like slow valves. With the tide they open, with slack water they close, turning the district into a place of permissions: passable now, sealed later, open again when the city exhales.

In places, the vegetation does something stranger: hair-fine filaments align as if combed. You can watch it if you’re patient—threads turning together, not toward light, but toward invisible lines in the water. They are sensitive to the district’s faint electrical habits and have begun to use those habits as orientation, like a second gravity.

### 3) The rare fauna: careful animals that live like trespassers
There should be more fish. There isn’t. The ones that remain behave like they’re surviving in a half-finished world.

They live in the margins—along the edges of the brightest corridors, near the ring of pylons where the open sea can be reached quickly.

- **Needle-thin eels** keep to pipe runs and cable braids. They are not bold hunters; they are opportunists that take what the city provides: larvae shaken loose from plant curtains, tiny shrimp that graze on films, the occasional corpse of something that misjudged an anoxic pocket.
- **Shrimp-like flickers** appear in the lamp-glow and vanish the moment you look directly at them. They are the district’s street-sweepers, feeding on the fine living dust of the biofilms.
- **One-off fish**—underfed, pale, often scarred—use the architecture like cover. They wait at the mouths of culverts as if those were doorways in a predator’s house.
- **A solitary ray** is the closest thing the district has to grace. It travels the long corridors the way a commuter might: not hunting so much as moving through familiar routes, skimming above sediment drifts that are sometimes safe and sometimes quietly toxic.

Predators don’t establish themselves easily here. The warm water promises abundance, but the abundance is chemically complicated—good for plants and microbes, not always good for a body that needs stable oxygen and clean tissue.

So the animals that live here are either small enough to exploit the microhabitats, or cautious enough to leave when the city’s chemistry shifts.

### 4) The scavenger you don’t notice at first: anoxic-pocket life
In the dead bowls—black silt, slow bubbles—life still persists, but it is the kind that does not advertise itself.

There are worms and larval forms that tolerate low oxygen, living in the boundary layer where the plant roots leak just enough breathable water. There are microbes that feed on what sinks: rust, old fuel residues, the steady rain of decaying plant matter. They produce the district’s quiet burps of gas and its occasional metallic tang.

These zones are not empty; they are simply inhabited by metabolisms that work like long sentences: slow, continuous, hard to interrupt.

### 5) The electrical symbionts: organisms that use the city’s faint current
Because the central machinery still turns, the district has a subtle field—patchy, inconsistent, but real. That creates a niche you don’t find in ordinary reefs.

Some of the filament plants and certain bacterial mats have become **electrotropic**: they grow along charged gradients and lay down conductive slime-sheaths that make those gradients more predictable. The result is a feedback loop: the city’s weak electricity shapes growth; the growth, in turn, shapes how the electricity leaks and travels.

In a few corridors the effect is strong enough to feel like a presence, not because anything is thinking at you, but because the environment begins to behave coherently: filaments all leaning the same way, lamps flickering in sympathy, silt sparkling briefly when disturbed as if the water resents being touched.

If there is a “mind” here, it is not a brain. It is a distributed responsiveness—field, growth, current, and chemistry making a system that reacts the way a living tissue reacts.

### 6) The last citizens of the old town: machines that keep half-promises
The sluice gate that opens monthly is not alone.

In the deeper service decks there are mechanisms that never got the memo that the world ended.

- A **pressure regulator** that still hunts for equilibrium, clicking softly behind a panel that has become a miniature reef wall.
- A **timing cam** that still completes its rotations, not because it’s accurate, but because it’s stubborn.
- Relays that still **tick** when corrosion hasn’t quite claimed them, their contacts cleaned, improbably, by gentle motion and mineral plating.

They do not “live,” but they behave like residents: they have routines, they affect the neighborhood, they create predictable events that the biological inhabitants have learned to anticipate. When the sluice gate flushes a channel and the water clears, the curtain-growths lift and the grazing animals briefly venture deeper, as if the district is holding an open market day.

### 7) The thing that misleads you: not a monster, but a habit of the place
The district’s refusal to be mapped is also inhabited—by slow-moving curtains, by repeating industrial geometry, by light that blurs edges, by pockets of thicker water that bend motion and stretch glow.

But it’s more than optics.

The vegetation learns. Not in the dramatic way of an animal, but in the incremental way of a system that gets reinforced by repetition. Routes that get used—by currents, by occasional animals, by the monthly flush—stay open. Routes that don’t get used silt up and grow over. Over time, the district develops *preferred paths* that aren’t the same as its original streets.

That’s why you keep arriving at “almost the same” corner. The city isn’t rearranging itself quickly; it’s selecting which version of itself is easiest to move through today. In that sense, the district is inhabited by its own circulation patterns the way a body is inhabited by blood.

### Who lives here, in the end
- A dense population of films and mats that turn corrosion and runoff into food.
- Plant-forms that don’t merely grow but *structurally participate*, making architecture into ecology.
- Small, wary animals that treat the place as a series of safe rooms rather than a home.
- Anaerobic life that keeps the deep silt quietly working.
- Electrotropic growths that have begun to use the city’s faint charge as a compass.
- Machines that aren’t alive, yet still contribute routines—events that other lives time themselves around.

It feels quiet because none of these residents need speed. The district is inhabited by things that can afford to be patient: life that builds by millimeters, feeds on thin gradients, and treats centuries the way surface creatures treat weather.
