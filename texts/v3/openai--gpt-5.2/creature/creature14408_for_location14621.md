The first residents are the ones you only meet by what they do to everything else.

In the mud—warm, fine-grained, never quite settled—there is a continual turning. Bacteria and archaea that don’t need much oxygen make their living in the drowned layers, trading in sulfur and iron, exhaling faintly sour, metallic notes that seep up through root-mats. Where the ground has breath—around the dark spikes of pneumatophores and the spongier hummocks—other microbes take over, faster, hungrier, shredding fallen leaf and wood into sugars and heat. Fungi are everywhere at once: lacework in the litter, cords inside dead trunks, pale blooms on bark that appear overnight and collapse by afternoon, leaving only a stain and a dusting of spores. Nothing here is allowed to remain merely “dead” for long; it is claimed, softened, disassembled, and handed along.

From that constant digestion, the plants build the architecture.

The tall trees are not singular pillars so much as negotiated towers—straight trunks rising until they can find a seam of light, then exploding into broad, overlapping crowns that lock together into the roof. Many of them stand on flared buttresses or splayed roots that grip the saturated ground like hands pressed into mud. In the wettest pockets, trees solve the problem of drowning by lifting their own breathing holes: pneumatophores like a scattered field of blunt pencils, each one a small insistence that air is still part of the bargain. Palms and broad-leafed understory trees fill the dim middle, their leaves tuned for scarcity—wide, glossy, built to catch what little falls through. Along every trunk, the “ground” climbs upward: epiphytes rooting in pockets of rot, bromeliads cupping water in their leaf-bowls, orchids that live on humidity and dust, ferns nested in the crooks where branches meet. Vines do not merely drape; they stitch. Some are thin as cord and braided into nets; others are thick, muscular ropes that loop down to touch the earth and then ascend again, turning one tree into a ladder for another. Stranglers begin as small, polite plants high in a branch-fork, then slowly become the branch-fork, then become the tree.

The basin’s open water—never fully “open,” always shouldered by grass, roots, and fallen wood—has its own plant life that behaves more like film and fringe than like meadow. Algae and bacteria slick the still surfaces. Floating leaves collect pollen and soot alike. In the shallows, sedges and emergent grasses make combs that slow the brown ribbon of current, trapping leaves until the water becomes a broth of tannins and decomposing sweetness.

Then come the small, innumerable animals that make the place feel crowded even when you see nothing moving.

Ants are a major form of weather here. Some are tiny and constant, moving in glossy lines along branch-highways; some are larger, solitary, more deliberate, inspecting the underside of leaves as if reading. Termites turn wood into soil with a patience that looks like stillness. Beetles live in every stage of rot: in the green world scraping fungus from leaf surfaces; in the brown world boring, tunneling, pupating inside fallen logs; in the in-between world of bark, where their larvae mine quiet galleries. Mosquitoes, midges, and moths hatch from the water-pockets and leaf-cups in pulses that feel timed to the basin’s own breathing. Wasps patrol with purpose, touching leaves the way hands check doorways, locating caterpillars you won’t notice until you see a leaf folded into a shelter. Spiders suspend themselves in careful geometries—orb webs that tremble like instruments when something blunders in, and messy, dense webs in the crooks of roots where the air is always wet.

In the leaf litter and the root-tangle, the amphibians and small reptiles are less a “fauna” than a texture. Tiny frogs—some no bigger than a fingernail—sit like living punctuation on damp leaves, their skin the color of moss, tea, or warning-bright yellow. They feed on ants and mites and become, in turn, food. Skinks and geckos haunt bark and fallen trunks, flashing away in short, efficient bursts. Snakes are present mostly as absences: a sudden vacancy in insect sound, a quick ripple through shallow water, a curve of scaled body revealed only when it takes a shaft of dim light and briefly returns it as a humid sheen.

The water holds its own set of lives, half-hidden by tannin and silt. Fish move like rumors, most visible only when they disturb a floating leaf or when a school briefly silver-flashes and then is gone. In the slowest pools, larvae breathe through siphons or gills, preparing their transformations. Crabs and freshwater shrimp work the margins, pulling at dead plant matter, turning it into smaller pieces, keeping the edge of the water from becoming still for too long. Water striders stitch the surface with their feet, and in doing so they break the thin iridescent skins that sometimes form—those fragile, rainbow sheens that speak quietly of the basin’s shared air with the machine-world beyond.

Birds are above you more often than in front of you. In the canopy there are fruit-eaters that travel by sound—soft thuds of wings, the crack of a seed, a brief, practical call—then vanish behind leaves that look like they were designed to conceal. In the mid-story, insect hunters make short, surgical movements: a pause, a tilt of the head, a dart and a snap. Near water, you’ll catch longer shapes: herons or storks standing with their patience on display, jacana-like birds stepping across floating plants with toes splayed wide, kingfishers that appear as a streak of color and then become still again. At night, the birds change but do not leave; they become owls and nightjars and other wide-mouthed hunters that take moths out of the air. Some sit so motionless they seem like part of the bark until the moment their eyes shift.

Mammals are the place’s least-seen certainty. They are there in the paths that avoid the worst suction, in the faint compression of leaf litter where something heavy passed without panic, in the sudden hush that moves like a shadow through the understory. Small ones work the night: rodents that climb and gnaw, insectivores nosing through the damp, bats drawing precise loops through the canopy-lanes and taking the air’s insects as if harvesting a crop. Larger ones move more sparingly. There are hoofed animals—deer-like shapes, peccary-like bands, creatures that have learned the difference between firm ground and the places that eat ankles—and they maintain quiet corridors through the basin by sheer repetition. Predators follow those corridors with even less sound: cats that place their feet as if the ground itself were listening; snakes that can take a bird in the dark; crocodilians or caiman-like bodies in the slow water, present as a pair of eyeshine low to the surface and a subtle rearrangement of floating leaves.

Everything feeds on everything else, but not with frenzy. The rules here favor steady exchange over spectacle. Carrion does not last long enough to announce itself; it becomes a gathering—flies, beetles, ants, then larger scavengers, then fungi, then nothing you can point to but richer soil. A fallen trunk becomes not an obstacle but a neighborhood: mosses first, then mushrooms, then invertebrates, then seedlings rooting in the softened wood, then the trunk itself losing the last of its identity and rejoining the ground.

And at the basin’s margins, where the canopy loosens into tall grass and scattered trees, different kinds of bodies become common. Grazers and browsers come out more openly there, taking advantage of the light-grown abundance. The air is louder with insects, and the sky—when you can see it—hosts birds that prefer distance and horizon. The boundary between dense forest and open plain isn’t a line; it’s a gradation of strategies: shade-adapted plants giving way to sun-tolerant ones, ambush predators giving way to coursers, creatures of pooled water giving way to creatures that can cross dry stretches between puddles.

No people live inside the green roof. If anyone enters, they do so briefly and leave little—no straight edges, no cut marks, no persistent scent of smoke.

And yet something else “lives” here by influence: the far-off industry that never arrives in person but still participates. It sends occasional soot that settles on the top leaves like fine ash. It sends faint petroleum sheens that form and fracture on puddles. It sends a low, throaty hum that becomes another background animal—distant, indifferent, not answered, simply absorbed into the basin’s layered quiet. The organisms don’t recognize it as a threat in any dramatic way; they incorporate it the way they incorporate everything: as particles to be washed, films to be broken, toxins to be tolerated or succumbed to, one more ingredient in the perpetual, crowded chemistry of staying alive.

What lives here, in the end, is not a single emblematic beast or tree but an entire system that has learned to occupy every available centimeter—mud, bark, leaf, air—without leaving emptiness. The basin is full the way a lung is full: not stuffed, but working, exchanging, never truly at rest.
