What lives in the throat are not “animals and plants” in the ordinary order so much as a set of strategies that have found the one chamber where they make sense. Everything in there has learned the same lessons: hang on, don’t waste motion, and let the machine feed you.

### The first residents: skins, films, and things that drink metal
Before you notice anything that looks like a creature, the place announces its oldest inhabitants in the way surfaces behave.

There are biofilms in every seam where warm air meets cooler plate—thin, iridescent slicks that bloom and recede with the valve-rains. They don’t read as “slime” so much as a second finish laid over the steel: clear when young, then clouded, then stained where they’ve metabolized oil residues into acids and sugars. Some of them are hydrocarbon-eaters, slow and tireless, living on what the machine sweats—hot mist, lubricant breath, the faint metallic diet of abrasion dust. Where they are thickest, rust doesn’t spread the same way; it darkens, stabilizes, becomes a hard bruise rather than flake. The metal there looks *cured*.

In the lower levels, on drain channels and the backs of damp braces, there are fungal mats that are more cable than mushroom—hair-fine hyphae threading felt panels and ceramic blocks, knitting growing medium into something that can’t shake loose. They’re the quiet infrastructure of the ecology: if you tore away the lushness, you’d find these pale threads still holding, still routing moisture along gradients too small to notice.

And threaded through both—almost impossible to see until you know to look—are conductive plant-fibers and mineralized root sheaths that have learned to tolerate stray fields. Not “wires,” but living tissues that take advantage of warmth and vibration the way a river plant takes advantage of current. In places they make the moss behave like a single sheet: tug one corner and the whole patch responds, not quickly, but unmistakably.

### The plants that don’t need ground
The greenery isn’t opportunistic wilderness; it’s an architecture-language the ecosystem speaks fluently now.

The mosses are the main body. Up near the lip they grow like upholstery—dense, rectangular where they were originally panel-grown, then frayed into tongues and scallops by decades of airflow. Down low they become sponges: thick pillows around drains and warm housings, constantly damp, acting as filters. In the middle bands they’re pragmatic—only where an overlap makes a ledge, only where a bolt head catches silt, only where vibration keeps condensation coming.

The vines are the climbers and the surveyors. They don’t wrap everything equally; they prefer humming conduits, resonant braces, lightly warm ribs. Their tendrils are sensitive enough that a pipe that *sounds* alive is treated as a better trellis than a silent one. In old cable trays they braid themselves with abandoned wiring until it’s hard to say what is plant and what is insulation—one becomes reinforcement for the other.

Near the top, when the valves open and the soft rain falls, flowers appear that are less about beauty than timing: pale, tough, wax-skinned blossoms that open into the damp interval and close again, conserving moisture as if they’ve learned the machine’s schedule the way a desert plant learns dawn.

### The seam-fauna: grazers, cleaners, and the ones that ride vibration
In the cracks and under the flanges are the small bodies that make the whole place feel inhabited even when you don’t see movement.

There are beetles and mites that graze moss the way livestock graze pasture—slow, numerous, almost invisible until a vibration shift makes them adjust in a shimmering line. There are springtail-like things that launch and land with absurd precision on wet metal, timed to the drip rhythm. There are larvae that live in the gritty felt panels and emerge only during valve-rains, when the air briefly cools and the surfaces go slick.

The pollinators aren’t loud; most of them can’t afford to be. Thick-bodied flyers with short wings climb the updraft like work, not flight, and navigate by pressure changes and by the subtle static halos around active junctions. They hover in the warm breath of vents until their whole bodies tremble, then drop like stones into curtains of leaves where they can rest out of the main flow.

Spiders live here too, but their webs aren’t the classic radial kind; they can’t be, not in that constant wind and tremor. Their silk is laid as guy-lines and hammocks in sheltered angles—under catwalk lips, behind ladder rails—built to flex with vibration rather than break. Some of them anchor threads to the very edges that sing with the machine, letting the tension changes tell them when something has landed.

### The birds (and near-birds) that speak in light
In housings that used to be purely mechanical—fan cowls, duct elbows, the dead spaces behind control panels—things shaped like birds nest in cups made of insulation fiber, braided wire, and dried vine strips. Their nests don’t fall apart because they’re built like shock absorbers: resilient, springy, designed to move.

They don’t sing; there’s no point. Instead they communicate with surfaces. Some have reflective throat membranes they pulse like shutters, catching ordinary daylight and throwing it in deliberate flashes. Others raise and lower feather-plates that read like tiny signal flags. Courtship becomes a slow semaphore. Arguments are a staccato of light.

Their young learn early not to fight the updraft. They hop into it, let it lift them, then fold and drop onto the next rail with practiced economy. Flight in the throat is less “soar” than “use what’s already moving.”

### The catwalk mammals: edge-born, sure-footed, almost casual about the fall
There are small mammals that treat the grating as ground because it’s the only ground they’ve ever needed. Broad pads, hooked claws for mesh, oily fur that sheds condensation. They move in the same patient way the place changes—no sudden sprints unless cornered, no wasted leaps. They know which rails hum too hard to balance on and which ones are quiet enough to sleep against.

They eat what the machine provides indirectly: seeds from the upper growth, insects shaken loose by vibration, mineral salts licked from drip-lines, the occasional soft fungus peeled from a damp bracket. In side bays they cache things in toolboxes colonized by moss—little hoards of dry fiber, flower heads, beetle husks.

Predation exists, but it’s adapted too. There are sleek, ferret-like hunters that use the vine curtains as cover and the roaring airflow as camouflage; you don’t hear them come, you notice the sudden absence of small movement on a moss patch, the way a cluster of beetles freezes as if the metal itself warned them.

### The machines that never fully left: dormant workers turned habitat
Some residents aren’t biological.

In quiet cavities off the shaft there are maintenance drones and inspection crawlers—old, paint-chipped, half-seized—wedged under coils of hose or hanging by a joint that never retracted. They’re nests now. Insects lay eggs in their housings. Moss grows on their casings where condensation beads. Birds tuck wire and fiber into their open panels. Occasionally, after a valve-rain, one will twitch—battery chemistry briefly revived, a sensor blinking once in a reflex that means nothing and yet changes the behavior of nearby life. Pollinators will circle that blink the way they circle a flower: not because it’s pretty, but because in this place, any signal might be a resource.

And there are the valve systems themselves, still obeying an old program. The soft rains are a kind of heartbeat the ecosystem has built its calendar around. The “schedule” is as much a resident as any animal.

### The thing that feels most like an inhabitant: a living map of warmth and tremor
If you stay long enough, it becomes hard to believe the residents are separate.

The thickest mosses, the conductive-root nets, the fungal cables, the hydrocarbon films—together they behave like a single slow organism draped over an industrial skeleton. It grows toward warmth and vibration, backs away from toxic runoff until bacteria adapt, thickens where the air reliably rises, thins where plates stay cold and smooth. It doesn’t need intention to look intentional. The throat feeds it with heat, moisture, and tremor, and it answers by stabilizing surfaces, holding grit in place, buffering drips, turning harsh metal into something that can be lived on.

Animals move through it the way fish move through kelp: grazing, nesting, hunting, cleaning—never fully separate from the structure that makes them possible.

So what lives there is a community built out of the machine’s habits: light that’s ordinary, noise that’s law, water that arrives by valve instead of cloud. A vertical garden, yes—but also a patient, many-bodied tenant that has learned to treat a working industrial organ as its native terrain, and to make the act of functioning feel, over time, like a kind of ecology’s steady prayer.
