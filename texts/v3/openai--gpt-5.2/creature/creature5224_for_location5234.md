What lives here is mostly what can live *on purpose-built surfaces*—life that doesn’t need soil, doesn’t mind metal, and has learned to treat heat gradients and maintenance schedules the way a forest creature treats seasons.

At first it seems like nothing lives here at all. The basin reads as an unfinished organ: wet steel, poured pads, the regular fever of machines. But the longer you stay, the clearer it becomes that “inhabited” doesn’t mean “occupied.” It means *used*—by bodies you can see, by bodies you can’t, and by systems that have become creaturely just from having to persist.

### The first residents: the skin-life

The true dominant lifeform is a thin, patient skin.

It’s in the drains first—dark velvets in the corners where the cleaning spray never hits at the right angle. It’s in the seam-lines of paneling, where two materials meet and a micron-wide capillary keeps a standing drink for days. It’s on the underside of catwalks, because the underside is a cave: stable temperature, less airflow, more time for droplets to sit and become meals.

Up close, this isn’t one organism but a treaty:

- **Biofilms** that look like stains until a strobe catches them and you see the subtle contouring—ripples and dendritic borders where colonies have advanced and retreated with humidity cycles. Some are iron-eating, leaving orange blooms; some are oil-tolerant, turning slick residues into a faint soap smell; some are pale and chalky, thriving where mineral-rich condensate dries and re-wets.
- **Fungal plates** in the neglected places: under stairs, behind insulation skirts, inside cable-tray shadows. They don’t sprawl; they *choose*. They bloom in small, confident geometries—flat fans and scalloped shelves—then die back as soon as airflow changes or a heat exchanger comes online and makes their niche too dry for a week.
- **Thread-fringes** around light leaks: hair-thin growths that live on the edge of illumination, where photons are reliable but heat isn’t. They seem decorative until you realize they’re positioned like nets, catching airborne dust and feeding on the nutrient film it brings.

These organisms don’t compete the way surface plants do. They partition the basin by **chemistry** and **temperature boundary**. They know the difference between condensate from a hot duct (warm, clean, short-lived) and runoff from a coolant line (cold, mineral-laden, persistent). Their “roots” are adhesion proteins and micro-texture, and their seasons are defined by maintenance rounds.

When a scrub team finally comes through, you can find the afterimage of their old colonies in the way surfaces age differently: a slightly darker sheen, a faint pattern of pitting where they held water against steel for too long. The place wears their history like fingerprints.

### The small, fast animals: the sump scavengers and pipe-clingers

Where there is standing water—squared sumps, catch pans, shallow pooled belts around heat zones—there are things that move.

They are easy to miss because they’ve evolved around vibration and strobe-light. They don’t freeze; they *phase*—a jerk into stillness, then a sudden dart that looks like a glitch in your eye.

- In the sumps live the **pallid scavengers**, shrimp-like and quick. They gather where particulate settles: flakes of rust, dead biofilm, shed filter fibers, insect husks. Under a lamp they look almost translucent, as if they were built from the same milky plastic as cable insulation. They move in short, decisive bursts—always toward edges, always toward shadow. If you stand still, you can watch them graze: front limbs combing the slime layer in small arcs, leaving clean trails that close again as the film relaxes.
- On warm pipes are the **translucent clingers**—insect analogues with thin shells like peeled mica. They grip by microhooks or suction pads; you’ll see them only when a warning beacon strobes and their bodies flash silver for an instant. They feed on the pipe-sweat ecosystem: the thin microbial films and mineral residues that condense, dry, and re-wet. When a pipe cycles hotter than usual, they migrate in a quiet, shimmering band to a neighboring run, as if the facility’s temperature map is printed in their instincts.
- In the cable forests and duct seams are the **lint-feeders**, smaller still—things that live in the trapped drifts of filter fiber and zip-tie tails where water has combed debris into nests. They are less “insect” than “moving speck,” but their presence shows up in the patterns: fibers cut into shorter lengths, little pellets of processed grit, the tidy hollowed-out centers of what should be inert fluff.

Nothing here sings; nothing here calls. The soundscape is too loud for voices to be useful. These animals communicate by proximity, by chemistry, by the choreography of retreat.

### The watcher in the rafters: the heat-percher

Sometimes there is a larger thing—bird-sized, but not quite a bird in the way you mean it.

It chooses the *updrafts*. It perches where hot air rises from a process bank and meets the colder ceiling layer, creating a stable thermal ledge. The creature is built for this environment: narrow body, tight-feathered or skin-sheathed surfaces that shed water rather than soak it, eyes that don’t mind glare. It stays still for long stretches, because stillness is camouflage against constant motion.

It watches the basin like it understands routes: where service corridors open, where drones pass, where the lights brighten briefly before a gate seals. It doesn’t hunt dramatically. It drops.

A quick glide down, a precise snatch at the edge of a sump or along a warm pipe, then back up into the scaffolding shadows where human eyes don’t like to go. If it leaves evidence, it isn’t nests and feathers so much as *small, clean gaps* in the local populations—one less clinger on a pipe, a sudden emptiness in a sump corner.

You never see two at once. That might mean they’re solitary. Or it might mean the basin has room for only one that’s learned the map.

### The machines that count as fauna

The most visible inhabitants are the ones that don’t pretend to be alive and yet behave as if they are cautious.

- **Maintenance crawlers** with legged gaits that make sense on wet grating and uneven module berms. They move like crabs designed by an engineer: slow, center-of-mass low, always testing with a front limb before committing weight. They pause at puddle edges not because they’re afraid, but because their sensors are reading conductivity, depth, and contamination levels before they decide whether to wade.
- **Wheeled runners** that work the long empty pads, tracing invisible grids. They are the basin’s small herbivores: not predators, not guards, but routine keepers. They carry spray nozzles, UV lamps, squeegee arms, tiny vacuum mouths that drink grit from seams. Their paths are practical, but over time they become *trails*—cleaner lanes in a world otherwise glossed with moisture.
- **Hanging pods**—sensor cocoons that dangle from cable trays like fruit. Some are inert; some pivot slightly when air pressure changes. When you stand under them you get the feeling of being measured in more dimensions than you can feel: heat signature, particulate plume, vibration footprint through the floor.

These machines don’t merely operate here; they *adapt* in the limited way algorithms adapt. When a leak becomes constant, a crawler begins visiting that trench more often. When algae thickens in a cooling corridor, UV units linger longer. Over months, their behavior makes patterns that resemble territory.

And sometimes you can tell when one has encountered something unexpected—its movement becomes less efficient, more exploratory. It circles. It repeats. It waits for instructions that don’t come, then makes a choice anyway. That’s when they look most alive: not when they perform their job, but when the job no longer fits the world.

### The rare humans: brief ghosts in high-vis

If people work here, they do it like divers entering a wreck.

You don’t find them as a constant presence. You find their **signatures**:

- fresh scuffs on new deck plate where heavy boots have pivoted,
- a handprint in condensation on a railing that no one touches unless they’re tired,
- the smell of warmed plastic and sweat trapped in a sealed corridor after a crew has passed through and the air rerouted behind them,
- tape flags on cables, bright as tropical birds in this gray wet.

When humans appear, it’s in pulses—shift crews, inspection teams, welders at the far edge of the light. They move with practiced economy and with the distracted caution of people who know the environment is indifferent. Their eyes don’t roam the way yours do; they track gauges, listen for bearing noise, watch for the wrong shimmer of a leak.

They don’t stay long in open bays. They prefer the catwalks and service planes because the catwalks have rules: rails, lines, known distances. The basin floor is too large, too echoing, too much like being inside a throat.

To the small fauna, humans are weather: a sudden vibration, a storm of light, a chemical fog of cleaners, then absence. The ecosystem has learned to hunker down until the boots pass.

### The largest inhabitant: the basin itself

Over time you start to feel that the facility is not just a container for life; it is the dominant organism, and everything else—microbe, shrimp-thing, perched watcher, crawling drone, occasional human—is living *in its habits*.

Its metabolism is flow: intake, compression, exhaust; fill, drain, flush. Its moods are pressure changes and fan ramps. Its immune system is alarms and rerouted ventilation, UV cycles, purge sprays. It doesn’t care about individuals; it cares about thresholds.

And yet it creates niches the way a body creates organs. Warm banks become feeding grounds. Cold trenches become refuges. The thin hanging chains that guide dripping water become ladders for clingers and perches for the watcher. A neglected stair becomes a fungal garden until the day a work order finally remembers it.

So what lives here is a layered community of opportunists—soft life in the seams, small life in the water, one or two larger things that have learned the heat-map, and a steady fauna of machines—under the rule of a single vast working presence that never sleeps, only cycles.
