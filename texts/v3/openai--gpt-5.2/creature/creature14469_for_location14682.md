What lives here is not one population but a stacked arrangement, like the terraces themselves: life that walks the bright plates, life that clings to the seams, life that swims the black chambers, and—hardest to name—life that *is* the bias in the materials, the tendency of the place to return to its intended shape.

### The surface-dwellers: creatures that learned the grammar of tile
In the open glare, where there is no “ground” to dig into, the animals live as if the world were a mosaic cliff laid flat.

Small amphibians are the most honest citizens of the basin. They are built for adhesion and patience: bellies that flatten to warm clay, toes that splay into the ribbing of plates, eyelids that blink sideways against salt-bright reflection. They don’t simply hunt; they *station* themselves. Each chooses a particular overflow lip or perpetual drip and becomes part of its rhythm, taking insects as they arrive, retreating when a basin “decides” to pull water in faster. Their territories are not circles of earth but chains of seams—crack A to crack B to the shadow beneath a buttress.

Insects make a second skin over the construction. The metallic-winged dragonfly analogues don’t roam randomly; they hold straight lines between planted plots as if those lines were drawn for them. Beetles live between plates like tenants in row houses. Some are resin-black, some chalk-pale to match limestone, and a few carry a faint dusting of green algae, turning them into moving bits of grout. There are moths that rest on maker’s marks—handprints, spirals, diagonal slashes—not because they recognize meaning, but because those impressed symbols condense moisture a fraction more readily, and a damp tile is a cooler tile. The moths gather where physics is slightly “edited.”

The larger herbivores—those long-legged grazers with split paddle-hooves—move with the delicacy of something that knows it is walking on a lid. Their gait is a kind of reading: pause, test, place, shift weight. They favor the lattices over black water because the slats flex in a way fired clay does not, and their joints seem to prefer that give. When they feed, they do it at the edges of the planted islands, harvesting fast-growing reeds and vine tips without stepping fully into the gardens. They keep the plots trimmed the way wind might, if wind here had any voice.

Predators are present but never triumphant. Sleek, low animals—half shadow, half muscle—patrol the boundaries the way a gardener’s eye patrols a bed. They take what is offered at the edges: an unwary amphibian, a fledgling bird, an injured grazer. They do not pursue across the open plates in long chases; the place swallows loud speed. Instead, they appear where the system already narrows: a kink in a channel, a turn in the terraces that feels like it was built to avoid something. Ambush fits the basin’s quiet.

Birds live in the architecture the way marsh birds live in reeds. They nest in the old scaffolding holes and half-finished pylons, turning toolmarks and peg-slots into cradle points. Many are light-bodied and broad-winged, suited to short, controlled flights that don’t rely on strong wind. Their calls are intimate, shaped by a world that refuses to carry sound: notes that are less announcement than close-range coordination. Some species have almost stopped singing and instead communicate with wing-flicks, throat-pulses, and the soft slap of feet on wet tile.

### The garden-keepers: life that behaves as if it was hired
The planted plots look wild until you watch them long enough to see that they are *maintained*—not in the human sense of pruning and weeding, but in the basin’s sense: growth that expands to a limit and halts, decay that begins and then politely gives up.

The vines that root into stone seams are not merely opportunists; they are sealants. Their root tips exude a mineral-rich gel that hardens where water would otherwise creep. They lace the joints like living caulk, then stop as if they have reached the edge of their assignment. Moss forms bands exactly where condensation needs to be harvested and nowhere else. Ferns in hanging baskets unfurl, shed spores, and then hold steady, neither overtaking their frames nor dwindling away. The whole green population behaves like a consortium trained to fit a blueprint they’ve never seen.

Among the plants are things that feel like deliberate inventions—reeds whose blossoms open and close in response not to light but to particular stone compositions, as though “limestone near a vent” is their sun. Creepers that crawl in right angles along the grid do so because their tendrils are hypersensitive to the microtemperature differences between tile face and seam; they turn where the heat turns. Their geometry is not obedience to human design so much as literal responsiveness to the basin’s hidden instructions.

### The underworld: chambers that are not empty
Beneath the lattices and inspection shafts, the basin is darker, cooler, and more crowded than the surface suggests.

The cistern fish are the most visible inhabitants down there, but they are only the quick flashes above a more patient ecology. There are blind shrimp-like things that graze algae off resin-lined walls. There are snails with thin, translucent shells that rasp mineral films, leaving clean arcs like erased pencil marks. In the deepest still water, you find slow-bodied, sacklike filter feeders attached to stone as if they were fixtures. They open and close in time with the basin’s own pulses—intake, seep, release—so that the water is constantly “polished.”

Root tunnels form a second architecture. Some plant beds have grown downward more than outward, sending pale, ropey roots through designed apertures and into the humid cavities. There, the roots braid with fungal networks that do the work of soil in a place with no soil: they break down whatever organic matter is allowed to exist, then stop before it becomes stain, before it becomes accumulation. Even dead things are processed into cleanliness.

If you listen at an inspection shaft you can sometimes hear a soft ticking, not quite sound, more like the sensation of something adjusting. That ticking comes from the smallest and most consequential residents: mineral-building colonies—lichenlike, bacterial, neither clearly plant nor clearly fungus—that live in hairline cracks. They “eat” seepage, precipitate lime, and lay down new material grain by grain. When a tile fractures, they bloom along the break and knit it shut. Their work is the basin’s immune response.

### The shy engineers: beings that don’t like the open sky
Whether the original builders are gone or simply absent from the day is hard to tell, because the place has a habit of hiding its hands. But there are signs of a people—or something people-shaped—that still moves through the porous underside.

They do not live on the exposed terraces where the light pins everything flat. They prefer the inspection galleries, the under-walk cavities, the shaded pockets behind unfinished masonry. Their dwellings, when you find them, are not huts on ground (there is no ground) but nests of joinery: reed frames wedged into stone slots, woven panels sealed with wax, hammocks strung in places where condensation drips predictably. They use the basin like a set of rooms already furnished with water and temperature.

You almost never see them at distance. The shimmer and the sound-damping make long observation useless. Instead you notice their presence in the small corrections: a channel mouth cleared of leaf litter that the wind should have missed, a spill-lip propped open with a ceramic shim, a new reed mat laid over a softened section of lattice. Their labor is not expansion but calibration.

When you do meet one, it is usually at a seam—exactly where the world feels like it might be “between pages.” They are slight and wrapped against wet heat, with skin that takes on the color of whatever surrounds it: lime-grey, clay-bone, pitch-dark. Their tools are quiet: wooden wedges, bone awls, braided cords. They carry little bundles of powdered ceramic and char—the manufactured “mud”—and they sprinkle it into trays or cracks like offering and repair at once.

Their communication fits the basin. They don’t shout; they touch. They tap on tile with knuckles in short codes that travel through the structure better than through air. They pass messages by placing small objects—pebbles, broken tile corners, seed pods—on particular maker’s marks, knowing which symbols will keep condensation and thus keep the message visible longer. In a world where sound won’t carry, they’ve learned to make meaning out of wetness and placement.

They do not seem like rulers of the place. They seem like attendants of something larger, respectful of boundaries they did not invent.

### The true resident: the basin’s intention
Under all these lives is the strangest inhabitant: not an animal, not a person, but a *preference* embedded in matter.

Water here remembers where it is meant to go. Distances here fold back on themselves in small, polite loops. Decay begins and then turns aside. Those aren’t just quirks; they behave like the reflexes of a body. The terraces are the skeleton, the channels the veins, the gardens the organs—and the “rule” that systems return to their designed state is the nervous system, quiet but everywhere.

If there is a mind to it, it is not a mind that thinks in words. It thinks in flows and thresholds: keep this basin full to this lip, keep this lattice springy but unbroken, keep moss to this band, keep sound close, keep the whole wide room private. It is less a consciousness than a set of vows the world keeps repeating.

Everything that lives here—frog, moth, grazer, seam-colony, shy engineer—has adapted not only to wet heat and handbuilt surfaces, but to that underlying intention. Predators hunt along designed chokepoints because the basin itself shapes movement. Plants stop at invisible borders because the basin rewards equilibrium. The people below maintain rather than conquer because conquest would be corrected, gently and endlessly, back into the shape the place prefers.

So the answer is: a constructed wetland full of ordinary lives made strange by their landlord. The landlord is not a king in a tower, but a basin-sized insistence: that the world, having once been assembled, should stay assembled—hot, wet, lush, quiet—forever.
