What lives up here is not a single “fauna list” so much as a set of strategies—ways of having a body in a place that is young, sharp, bright, and unreliable. The basin selects for lives that can pause, tuck away, cling, and then—when the brief window opens—spend everything quickly.

### The first residents: the clinging green that makes soil out of stone
Before there are animals in any meaningful number, there are the makers of footing.

**Crusts and films** live on the sun-facing slabs: lichen that looks like spilled paint, algae that only reveals itself when the rock sweats, microbial skins in the lee of boulders where the wind’s sandblasting is gentler. They don’t “cover” the mountain so much as annotate it—small colonies in places where the rock’s chemistry and the drip-lines agree. When they die, they become the beginning of grit that can hold water for an extra hour.

In the sheltered geometry—the underside of an overhang, the seam where two plates of stone meet—**mosses and liverworts** gather like stored humidity. Their great talent is not greenness but *containment*: they hold warmth close to the surface and keep the day’s melt from leaving too fast. You can tell where the fog likes to pause because that’s where the moss is thickest, darker, almost glossy, and where the rock smells faintly alive.

Then there are the **cushion plants**, the true architects. They don’t grow upward; they grow *inward*, knitting themselves into domes dense enough to resist wind and cold. In summer they bloom briefly—tiny flowers pressed close to the surface, as if even beauty has to crouch here. Their roots are less “roots” than negotiations with cracks, patiently widening hairlines over decades.

Where a little dust has collected behind a rock lip and snow lingers long enough to melt slowly, **dwarf conifers** live like the memory of a forest. They are shrubs in posture but trees in persistence: tight, resin-scented, with needles that can take abrasion and desiccation. Some grow in rings where an older center has died and new growth has crept outward, leaving a hollow that stays warmer than the surrounding air.

And in the deepest, wettest secrets—cracks fed by fog-drip—you find **ferns** so thin and dark they seem drawn rather than grown. They exist because the mountain gives them a private water schedule: not enough for a stream, but enough for a drip.

All of these plants share a quiet common behavior: they make the basin *more habitable than it ought to be*, creating pockets where insects can overwinter, where a burrow won’t collapse immediately, where hooves can find something besides sliding stone.

### The small, many lives: insects and the fast economy of brief warmth
When warmth arrives, it doesn’t spread evenly; it sparks.

Along damp seams in the rock, **gnats and midges** hatch in sudden, concentrated clouds—dark specks over a line of green. Their life cycle is tuned to hours. They rise, mate, feed, and fall back into crevices before the temperature drops again. Some are so cold-tolerant they can move at the edge of freezing, crawling rather than flying when the air is too thin or too sharp.

Near snowbeds and melt-trickles, **stonefly- and caddis-like larvae** cling under pebbles or in tiny gravelly channels that exist only for part of a day. Their presence is easy to miss because the “stream” is sometimes only a wetness moving through stones. If you lift a rock at the right time, you find the proof: wriggling bodies built to anchor themselves against sudden, temporary flow.

There are **bees and hoverfly-like pollinators**, but they behave differently than lowland insects: they patrol very specific routes between cushion-plant domes, returning again and again to the same heat-favored ledges as if those ledges were whole meadows. In good years, the air above a moss shelf can hum—softly, and strangely local, because the hush of the basin eats the sound almost as soon as it’s made.

### The burrowers: the basin’s breathing under the scree
Where the mountain offers a little softness—fines washed down between rocks, a terrace of wind-deposited grit—**marmot-like burrowers** live in colonies that are less about numbers than about *infrastructure*. Their tunnels knit the basin’s shallow soils, creating hidden ventilation and drainage, turning brief surface wetness into longer subsurface damp.

They are compact, thick-furred, and vigilant. Their eyes and posture do the work that loud alarm calls might do elsewhere. In this place where sound falls out of the air, warning becomes a choreography: one animal rises; others freeze; heads angle upslope in unison. They use the landscape as an amplifier—choosing lookout stones that give a wide view because hearing is unreliable.

They feed fast and selectively. In the short green season they prune moss edges, nibble at the richest cushion plants (never too deeply; killing a cushion is like burning a pantry), and strip seed heads from hardy alpine flowers. They carry stems and dried moss to line chambers that must stay above a certain cold threshold to be survivable.

Their winter is not “sleep” so much as calculated disappearance. When the basin becomes almost purely mineral again, the burrows hold a remnant warmth and the memory of summer’s moisture.

### The grazers: hooves that read vertical stone
The most visible large residents are the **goat- or ibex-like grazers**, but “resident” here means something like *regular visitor with routes*. They treat the basin as a series of safe lines: morning shelves that catch sun, midday ledges with wind-shadow, afternoon descents into hollows where ice won’t glaze the rock underfoot.

Their bodies are built for the place’s contradictions. They carry dense coats, but their faces look leaner, more angular; the air is thin and the cold is dry. Their hooves are not just tough—they are *precise*: hard outer rims with softer centers that can grip a slope that looks impossible. They step with an economy that fits the hush: one click, then stillness, as if they know excess noise is wasted.

They browse rather than graze. They take tips of conifer, lichens off accessible rock, the edges of moss shelves, the flowering tops of cushion plants when available. They know where fog-drip feeds a green seam and will climb to it even if it means crossing a chute of shifting stones, because those seams are the basin’s scattered orchards.

Their social life is partly visual: stares held a beat too long, body angles that signal agitation, the sudden collective lift of heads when something moves upslope. In a place that steals mid-range sound, they lean on sight and on the shared habit of watching what others are watching.

### The threaders-through: small predators and the boulder-field logic
Between stones, where the light can’t quite reach and the cold is steadier, **long-bodied predators** live—mustelid-like, quick, silent, and opportunistic. They hunt the burrowers and whatever else the basin briefly offers: nesting birds, insects concentrated at wet seams, weak young grazers if chance allows.

Their world is a map of gaps. They slip into fissures the way water does, appearing and vanishing without the basin having to “announce” them. Where another landscape would carry the scrabble of claws on rock, here the sound often dies a handspan from the source. That quiet is not just an atmosphere; it is part of their hunting advantage. They pause often, not to listen in the usual way, but to watch: a ripple of fur in a marmot colony, the slight shift of a goat’s posture.

Some of these predators cache food deep under rock plates, where the cold keeps it from spoiling and the scent is trapped. In that way they use the basin’s refrigeration as a tool.

Higher still and more occasional, there are **fox- or lynx-like visitors** that come up in certain seasons, following the grazers. You might never see them. You see instead the sign: a narrow track in a dusted patch of scree that avoids open slabs, scat placed on a flat rock like a claim, tufts of fur snagged on a thorny dwarf conifer.

### The aerial residents: the watchers who own the updrafts
Above the bowl, **raptors** ride the air as if the ridges were built for them—which, in a way, they are. They circle with minimal effort, leaning into updrafts that peel off sun-warmed cliffs.

Their hunting here is patient, because opportunities are spaced out. When they commit, it’s abrupt: a fold of wings, a drop like a thrown knife. Their shadows pass over the basin in brief eclipses, and the ground-life reacts more reliably to that moving darkness than to any sound.

There are also **high birds** that nest where stone offers a thin ledge and a view: chough-like corvids, cliff swallows that find a niche under an overhang, small finch-like birds that live on seeds and insects. Their calls, when they make them, are often sharper and simpler than lowland songs—notes that either cut through the strange acoustics or aren’t meant to travel far at all.

Some birds here have learned to communicate by **movement**: wing-flicks, short hopping displays, the choice of perch—because the air does not reliably carry the full message of voice.

### The hidden amphibians of rock: things that live on moisture itself
In the wettest cracks, where meltwater disappears into fissures and returns as cold breath, there can be **small salamander- or frog-like creatures**—not in open pools, but in the narrow world beneath stones where dampness persists. They are not abundant, but they are stubborn. Their skins are dark, almost black, to drink warmth when it exists; they move slowly, conserving water, and retreat at the slightest drying of the air.

If the basin is truly as young and raw as it feels, these creatures may be the descendants of a lower, wetter time, stranded upward and now surviving only where fog-drip imitates the old conditions.

### The human inhabitants: borrowers, maintainers, and the ones who read animals
People don’t “live” here the way they live in valleys. They **keep** it.

There are those who come up seasonally: shepherds or hunters, rope-menders and cairn-builders, pilgrims and messengers. Their presence is recorded in practical architecture: a shelter that fits a crouching body, a line strung across a gap where falling would be absolute, a series of cairns that show the least treacherous route through moving stone.

The humans who use this basin have a particular kind of competence: not domination, but *relationship*. They watch goats to understand slope stability. They read marmot posture to anticipate an unseen raptor. They pause often, because the basin’s odd hush makes rushing feel stupid—you won’t hear the warning you’re used to hearing.

Their tools are chosen for cold and repairability. Knots that can be tied with numbed fingers. Fire-making kits that work in thin air. Waxed thread for stitching hide. They blacken the inside of the shelter not out of carelessness but because smoke is one of the few things that briefly changes the basin’s sterile smell, marking “here was warmth.”

And because sound behaves strangely, they develop customs that favor **visibility** over shouting: bright cloth tied to poles, hand signals, meeting points that can be seen from afar. Even their prayers or tallies carved into stone are, in a sense, communication methods adapted to a place where voices don’t carry.

Some of them leave offerings that are more ecological than spiritual: a handful of grain scattered on a ledge late in the season, not to feed “the mountain,” but to keep certain birds around that help locate weather changes or carcasses. In such a spare system, even small human acts can ripple.

### A community woven from spacing
What’s most distinctive about this basin’s inhabitants is that they don’t fill space; they **occupy intervals**.

- The green lives in seams.
- The insects live in hours.
- The burrowers live in hidden volumes under rock.
- The grazers live on routes.
- The predators live in gaps.
- The birds live in air-lines.
- The humans live in borrowed corners and brief seasons.

And the hush shapes all of them. In a landscape where sound often drops out of existence, life becomes less about calling and more about **watching**—posture, shadow, sudden stillness, the way a line of goats tilts its heads at once. The basin’s residents are not loud because loudness has poor returns here. They survive by reading what’s visible and by building their lives in the few places the mountain briefly agrees to be generous.
