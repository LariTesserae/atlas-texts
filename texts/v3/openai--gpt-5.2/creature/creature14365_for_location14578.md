The first inhabitants you meet are not the ones that look back.

They are the mats underfoot, the ribs of grown arches, the braided down-trunks that hang like patient chandeliers. In a world where the stone is still deciding what shape it wants to be, the vegetation has taken on the work of decision. It lives less like a garden and more like an organ: sensing strain through the rock, sensing moisture through thin cold air, sensing vibration as if vibration were scent. It doesn’t surge upward to claim the volume; it spreads itself into sheet and tendon and scaffold, occupying the places where touch happens—the ledges, the slopes, the seams between boulders. If you could look at the underworld the way the underworld looks at itself, you’d see a nervous system more than a forest.

The “grass” skin—fibrous, tough, faintly warm against the palm—is a colonial thing. Not one plant, but a quilt of tight-packed stalks and threads, each strand specialized: some drink mineral fog, some glue to stone, some store cold water as ice-latticed cartilage. When the bright tides come, the mat doesn’t simply grow; it *reconfigures*. Pores open. Resin ducts uncork. Buds that have held still for months snap into motion in an hour, and the floor becomes subtly different under your boots—firmer, springier, more adhesive at the edges of pits where collapse is likely.

The down-trunks are the other great presence: slow, braided columns that descend from the roof in deliberate spirals, their outer bark hard and pale, their inner tissue dark and thirsty. They are not rooted the way aboveground trees are rooted; their “roots” are sprawling combs pressed into roof-seams, threaded through crystal-veins and salt beds where condensation forms. They feed by interception: catching wet, catching spores, catching stray mineral dust, catching the thin blue glow and converting it into long, slow sugars. Around each trunk there is a radius of altered weather. Air currents straighten. Humidity lingers. When a grove decides to shed, it does so in coordination—not intelligence exactly, but timing—releasing reflective spore-dust that rides convection like a message.

That dust is not merely reproductive. In this place, spores are also insulation, signal, irritant, currency. There are chambers where the walls wear old layers of it like frost, and other chambers where everything living has learned to keep its pores shut because a wrong breath will chalk the lungs for days.

Between mats and trunks live the “useful” plants: the ones that make tools possible. Vines with fibers that don’t rot in cold damp. Sheath-leaves that dry into waterproof shingles when lacquered with sap. Resin-cups—those tough-lipped funnels—that condense water and hold it with a lip that seals in frost. There are creepers that stitch rubble into stable slopes, and there are creepers that *do the opposite*, prying at fault lines and swelling in cracks until a wall is encouraged to fail, making new raw faces for colonization. The underworld’s vegetation is not benevolent. It is infrastructural in the same way bone is infrastructural: it supports, and it reshapes by breaking.

Only after you’ve started to think of the plants as the true population do you notice the smaller movers—the ones that make the place feel watched.

They don’t announce themselves with sound. Sound is expensive here.

There are pale crawlers in the mat, jointed and low, with bodies half armored and half fibrous. They look like crustaceans until they pause and you realize their “legs” are not limbs so much as bundled root-knots that stiffen and soften in sequence. They graze on the mat’s surface biofilm, on sporefall, on the mineral salts that bead where condensation dries. They leave behind thin, sinuous paths where the mat tissue has been combed and re-knit. When threatened they don’t flee far; they press down and become texture, tucking into the mat as if sinking into snow.

There are gliders overhead—batlike, but not mammals, not quite anything you’d name cleanly. Their wings are translucent membranes veined with dark threads, wide enough to catch the slow loops of air. Their heads are beaked for scraping resin from trunks and for gouging at the soft edges of moisture-cups. They land rarely, and when they do they land carefully, because a careless impact is a shout in a world that listens. Their roosts are often in the lee of down-trunk braids where the airflow is steady; their droppings seed little gardens of decomposer growth on ledges—dark velvet patches that drink light and heat and break down what the cold would otherwise preserve.

In the lakes and trenches—where water collects like an afterthought—there are things that don’t look alive until they move. Pale ribbons in the silt that wave without current, filtering minerals. Reef-fronds that are plants until they retract like animals when a shadow passes overhead. Small, hard-shelled swimmers under the ice, blind or nearly blind, navigating by pressure and taste. Now and then a thin black stream carries a drift of shed spores downstream like a slow comet tail, and whatever lives in that stream has learned to eat the dust that makes lungs ache.

And yes: sometimes there is something larger.

The “traveling sheet” you glimpsed between columns might be a colony creature—an organism that is many individuals knitted into a single, sail-like membrane. It catches convective updrafts, then drops itself like fabric onto a new surface when it tastes the right humidity. Where it lands, it clings and goes still, becoming wall or ceiling decoration until the next change in weather loosens it again. Some sheets are purely plant; some have mouths along their edges, or hooks, or patches of dark tissue that look suspiciously like eyes without being eyes. Nothing in this underworld is obligated to fit one category.

Predators exist, but they are subtle about it. A thing that hunts here does not roar. It waits where sound will betray prey, and it uses touch, vibration, and the faint chemistry of breath-mist.

Then you find the other inhabitants: the ones who leave smoke stains.

They are not numerous, and the underworld keeps them small on purpose. Where collapse can erase a district overnight and a spore-storm can make a chamber unbreathable, permanence is a kind of arrogance. The people who survive here—call them whatever you like; they have many names for themselves, and most of those names are gestures—live as moving knots of familiarity in a continent of changing corridors.

At first, their presence is only in workmanship. A cut made cleanly through living branch and sealed with resin so the plant doesn’t bleed itself dry. A rope bridge whose vines have been twisted in alternating directions so it doesn’t unravel when frozen. A platform that is not nailed together—there is almost no metal for nails—but *trained*: living ribs guided over years into a shape that holds weight, then pruned so it stays.

When you finally meet them, it is likely at the edge of a chamber rather than in its center. Their shelters prefer alcoves where ceiling-rain doesn’t strike directly, where smoke can rise and be swallowed by cold stone, where the mat is thick enough to insulate.

They tend to be slight not because they are weak, but because the underworld punishes excess. Muscle is weight; fat is fuel you must feed; loud footsteps are danger. Their skins are often pale in the roof’s cold luminance, but not uniformly—there are stains and tints from resins, minerals, and spore pigments rubbed into the body like protective cosmetics. Many keep their hair cropped or bound tight; loose hair collects spore-dust, and spore-dust is not just mess, it is sickness, it is rash, it is the slow clogging of breath.

Their eyes are adapted less to darkness than to glare: the bright here is honest, relentless, and blue. You see goggles sometimes—frames of bent root-wood fitted with thin plates of clear ice-crystal or lacquered membrane, replaced frequently because they craze and scratch. You see scarves and face wraps even in “warm” chambers, not for warmth alone but as filters. A proper wrap is layered: fibrous mat-lint, then a resin-stiffened outer skin that can be scraped clean.

They speak, but not like aboveground people speak.

They learn early that the underworld answers noise. So their languages grow sideways into quiet. Conversation is often done close, with breath kept low. There is a rich vocabulary of hand signs shaped for low visibility—broad gestures for “fog bank,” “bright tide,” “spore shed,” “unstable roof,” “sleep plant,” “good resin.” When they must communicate at distance, they do it by *not* vibrating the air: by line pulls on vine-cords strung between posts, by mirror-flashes of crystal, by the timed opening of a lantern-cup plant that releases a brief plume of scented steam. In a pinch they will hum, but even humming is chosen carefully—certain frequencies make the mats thicken or the tendrils reach.

Their tools are exactly what the place allows. Crystal-edged knives carried in sheath-leaves. Scrapers of mineral flake set into resin handles. Hooks of horn or carved down-trunk bark, used for climbing and for harvesting moisture-cups without spilling. They prize metal when they can get it—old hammered scraps traded from somewhere above or from older ruins deeper down—but metal is rare enough to be heirloom, and the cold makes it bite the skin like ice.

Their food is a negotiation with scarcity. They harvest starch nodules from mat-thick patches (you can feel the difference underfoot; it has a certain give, a certain stored fullness). They chew resin in small amounts for calories and to soothe lungs, though too much resin gums the gut and makes the body sluggish. They trap crawlers in low basket snares pressed into mat-runways, and they take gliders when they can—more often eggs than adults, because an adult glider is hard to catch without noise. They collect sporefall in cloths during safe sheds, then wash and ferment it into a paste that tastes like minerals and smoke; it is nutritious but risky, and only certain families know which spores can be eaten without fever.

They are not farmers in the sense of planting rows, but they *tend*. They guide vine growth into bridges. They encourage mat thickening on safe routes by tamping it and feeding it certain ash. They prune aggressive tendrils away from camp walls, not because the tendrils are malicious, but because if you let them test and taste you long enough they will decide you are part of the architecture. In some camps, you see “sleep posts”: stakes driven into the mat, ringed with resin. People tie themselves lightly to them at night, because the mat can subtly shift in bright tide and a sleeping body can drift toward a crevasse like a raft on a slow river.

Their social map is not drawn in borders but in timing. A “district” is a set of chambers whose bright tides sync, whose ceiling-rains cycle together, whose convective winds run predictably. People belong to routes more than to places. They know when a far ceiling is about to sweat by the taste of the air two days away. They know which trunk-groves will spore on the third bright pulse after a tremor. They plan marriages, gatherings, trades around these pulses, meeting at certain stable ledges when the roof is bright enough for long travel and dry enough for safe fire.

Fire itself is a controlled insult to the underworld. They burn sparingly, in pits lined with stone and damp mat to keep the heat from provoking unwanted growth. The smoke stains you saw are not accidental; they are part of a practice. Smoke discourages some tendrils. Smoke tells other travelers, quietly, that this alcove is recently human. In a few shelters you can find old smoke patterns deliberately made: handprints, spirals, maps of safe crossings drawn in soot on pale stone—marks that will last until the next collapse or until a velvet wall-plant decides to eat them.

Their beliefs are hard to separate from their weather sense. The roof’s glow is treated as a mood, a breath, sometimes an injury. Bright tides are not “good” or “bad”; they are *awake* times, times when the world is touchy and fertile. People tell stories about the ceiling flexing like the ribs of a sleeping animal. They make offerings sometimes—not to gods, exactly, but to the place’s listening: a pinch of ground resin scattered softly into a crevice before crossing, a deliberate silence held for a minute under a particularly stressed crystal seam.

They are cautious with strangers, but not cruel. Isolation here is default; trust is cultivated like a bridge: slowly, with redundancy. A newcomer is watched for how they walk (do they make the mat shiver?), for how they breathe (do they cough chalk?), for whether they flinch at the wrong sounds. Hospitality, when it is offered, is practical: a face wrap soaked in resin-water, a strip of dried sheath-leaf, a place near the warmest part of the fire where your boots will not freeze stiff.

And they are, in their way, builders as much as the plants are.

Not builders of cities. Builders of spans. Builders of temporary safety. Builders of relationships with living surfaces that can be persuaded but never owned. If you stay long enough you begin to see their settlements as another growth form of the underworld: a small cluster of walls and bridges and smoke-stains that appears during a stable season, then is dismantled, moved, or simply absorbed back into mat and vine when the ceiling changes its mind.

So what lives there is not one thing.

A luminous, mineral-driven canopy that behaves like a second sky. A plant infrastructure that acts like skin, tendon, and bone. Sparse, secretive animals that have learned to move without claiming attention. And small, fragile groups of people who have learned the most difficult local skill of all: to make a home in a place that hears you, answers you, and never promises to stay the same shape twice.
