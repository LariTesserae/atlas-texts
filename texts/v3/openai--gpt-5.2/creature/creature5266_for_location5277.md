Life up here does not announce itself by moving through the open.

It announces itself by *staying true*—by a canal that is still the right depth to catch fog, by a windbreak that has been re-lashed before the old fiber can fail, by a terrace wall whose stones have been returned—patiently, perfectly—to their long argument with frost. The plateau is not inhabited the way a valley is inhabited, with noise and herds and smoke. It is inhabited the way a tendon is inhabited by tension: you feel it most at the points where it holds.

### What the land lets you see (and what it doesn’t)
If you come looking for occupants as you would elsewhere—tracks, refuse piles, dung, butchered bone—you will keep concluding the cities are empty.

But then you begin to notice the kinds of traces that only appear when there is *no one else* making them:

- **No opportunistic scavenging.** A strip of dried moss-paste left on a stone stays there until weather takes it. Nothing nibbles the edges.
- **No birds to steal fiber.** The windbreaks fray only at their own pace, and always in the same stress points—then, suddenly, they’re repaired.
- **No animal paths.** Yet there are paths: the causeways are worn smooth by something that repeats a route with devotion.

The cities are maintained not in bursts but in a continuous, low-pressure way, as if the act of living here is simply the act of keeping the plateau’s thin rules from tearing too wide.

### The inhabitants: the ones who are present by being quiet
You eventually accept that the plateau’s dwellers are not absent; they are merely *uninsistent*.

When they appear in the open (and on some weeks, they do), it is rarely with the decisiveness of creatures built around heat and hunger. They move like something optimized for not wasting breath: slow, sure, without the restless micro-adjustments of animals that evolved under constant predation and constant noise.

At a distance, a figure can be mistaken for a bundled screen-post or a roll of matting someone left upright. Closer, the illusion fails—not because they are dramatic, but because their details are *too* congruent with the place:

- Their outer layers resemble the city’s own materials: **felted moss-cloth, fungal leather, reed lattice**, basalt dust worked into seams as if to keep wind from finding purchase.
- Skin, where it shows, is often **pale and dry-looking**, but not cracked; it has the muted resilience of lichen surfaces—built to endure long exposure rather than heal quickly.
- They carry little in their hands. Tools are light: **bone- or wood-scrapers, fiber cords, wedge-stones**, short blades of rare metal kept like heirloom punctuation rather than workday grammar.

You might expect voices. You rarely get them.

Their speech—when it happens at all—tends to be close-range, economical, as if sound is a resource that shouldn’t be spilled into a landscape that cannot be trusted to echo properly. Much of their coordination is done through **touch and placement**: a knot tied in a particular way, a reed panel rotated, a small cairn shifted one stone to the left. The city is full of messages that look like maintenance.

And even that may be only the surface form.

### The older resident: the distributed mind under the mats
Spend time in the damp basins, where the ground has that subtle elasticity, and a different possibility presses itself forward: that the most continuous inhabitant here is not bipedal at all.

The fungal networks are not merely decomposition. They are *infrastructure*—and perhaps, in a place where animal economies never took hold, infrastructure had time to become something like governance.

Under the moss plains and terrace strips, the mycelium forms sheets, cords, and knots so extensive that “organism” stops being a helpful scale. In the cities, it is densest at the boundaries—along canal linings, under tower rings, at the lips of terraces—exactly where the plateau’s rules thin.

If the plateau has a long memory, this is where it would live: in a cold, patient mesh that can:

- **store water** in gels and sponges without needing a heart to pump it,
- **share nutrients** without needing mouths,
- **signal** without needing sound—via chemistry, pressure, temperature shifts.

In that reading, the visible dwellers—the quiet walkers in moss-cloth—are not the main body of the civilization. They are its *hands*: temporary, grown or gathered when fine work must be done aboveground, then returned to the larger continuity.

It would explain the peculiar intimacy of the upkeep: as if the city repairs itself, but prefers sometimes to do so with fingers.

### How they live without animals
Their daily economy is built from what this plateau offers in abundance: slow growth, cold moisture, stone that holds shape, and fog that arrives like a tide.

They eat what does not require chasing.

- **Moss-farm harvests**, dried and rehydrated into pastes; some are bitter, some faintly sweet with mineral salts.
- **Fungal cakes**, grown in shaded vaults where condensation is guided down basalt ribs into troughs.
- **Reed pith and lichen crusts**, processed carefully—soaked, fermented, leached—because nothing here can afford to waste calories on poison.

Fire is rare and small. Heat is expensive at altitude, and besides: stone and wet fiber don’t love it. Instead, they use the plateau’s steadier energies:

- frost as a tool for cracking and tightening,
- wind as a dryer and a cleanser,
- condensation as a daily harvest more reliable than rain.

If they need protein, it doesn’t come from beasts; it comes from microbial craft—cultures tended the way other worlds tend goats.

### Homes that look like parts of the city
You don’t find houses the way you expect. You find *thickened places*.

A terrace wall that is slightly warmer on the lee side. A ring-tower whose interior mist smells faintly of fermentation. A canal segment where the stone lining is newer and the moss mat is clipped to a neat edge. These are inhabited not because someone is always visible there, but because the place has been tuned repeatedly to support bodies that are conserving heat and breath.

Where they sleep (when they sleep in anything like our sense), it is often:

- **half-buried chambers** lined with moss-felt and reed, where temperature changes lag behind the outside world,
- **under-tower hollows** that collect and store fog-water in cistern stone,
- **mat-vaults**—rooms roofed with living vegetation layers, kept alive because living mats insulate better than dead ones.

Doors are light. Thresholds matter. Edges are powerful, and their architecture treats every edge like a tool.

### Their relationship to the plateau’s bending rules
A people who stay here for generations don’t “solve” distance elasticity and unreliable echoes. They develop etiquette for it.

They travel in windows.

There are weeks when the roads agree to be roads, when a canal’s curve does not turn you back toward your starting point, when the horizon behaves. These times are marked not with calendars of moons and harvests—those would be too loud for this ecology—but with subtler clocks:

- the first week the towers “drink” mist before dawn,
- the cycle when certain lichens darken at their rims,
- the period when drips in specific canal-stones speed up, as if the ground is inhaling.

When the plateau is misaligned, they don’t fight it. They stay closer to home, do work that doesn’t require long straight walks, reinforce boundaries—because boundaries gather events, and in unstable weeks you want those events to happen where you can watch them.

Their navigational markers are not towering signposts (those would become magnets for weirdness), but *humble redundancies*: a particular knot-pattern repeated at waist height on three consecutive screens; a basalt block with a specific face turned outward; a strip of moss cut to reveal pale stone beneath in a shape only locals read as instruction.

### What they value (and what they don’t)
They do not build as if they fear invasion by beasts; there are no pens, no fortified granaries. Their anxieties are different.

They fear waste. They fear abruptness. They fear the plateau’s thinness becoming thinner.

So their culture—if you can call it that, given how much of it may be distributed through underground networks—values:

- **continuity over innovation**, because continuity is what keeps the city aligned with its own past work;
- **maintenance as ceremony**, because a repaired joint is also a renewed contract with frost and gravity;
- **silence as respect**, because the land decides what deserves an echo, and it’s unwise to demand one.

Art exists, but it is difficult to separate from engineering. The most ornate things are often functional: a windbreak weave that sheds fog into a hidden trough; a tower ring whose inner surface is scored to condense mist into clean rivulets; a terrace corner whose stones are chosen for a particular lichen that only grows where people intend to keep walking.

### Meeting them (if the plateau allows it)
If you ever encounter one up close, it may feel less like meeting a stranger and more like being briefly permitted into a system that was already operating perfectly well without you.

They will look at you for a long time—not with suspicion exactly, but with the careful attention of something that has learned the plateau’s rules are conditional. Their eyes, if you can call them that, tend to be dark and unreflective in the glare: built for a world that is mostly matte.

They may touch the edge of your sleeve, not your hand. Edges are powerful. Touching an edge is a way of learning what you gather.

And if you speak, they might answer—perhaps not with words, perhaps with a small adjustment to a nearby screen, a retying of a cord, an invitation expressed as a path cleared of silt. A gesture that says: *you can be here, if you don’t force the place to explain itself.*

Then fog slides through the streets. The towers fill like cups. Drips begin their patient counting again.

And the inhabitants—whether they are walkers in moss-felt, or the vast stitched intelligence under the mats, or both—continue the work of keeping a thin world coherent, one quiet edge at a time.
