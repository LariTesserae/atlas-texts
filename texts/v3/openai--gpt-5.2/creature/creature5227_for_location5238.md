What lives there is, first of all, the city itself—because “city” is the wrong word for something that has lungs, skin, and a metabolism.

The basin is an instrument, yes, but it is also a body: stone ribs, vent-throats, terraces that behave like layered tissue. The gardens are not ornament. They are the organ that makes the rest possible. And the residents—whatever they are in origin—live the way gut-flora lives in an animal: not as passengers, not as owners, but as a necessary layer of function that the larger thing has made room for.

If you stay long enough to stop flinching at the sound, you start to distinguish the living from the built, and then you realize you can’t. The “plants” lace themselves through mortar lines and under thresholds, growing in deliberate mats where a human foot will land and nowhere else. The screens that throw shade are woven half from fiber and half from stiffened fronds that continue to wick vapor while they serve as architecture. Some bridges are still alive at one end, drawing solvent up through their bundled stalks so they don’t become brittle. In places, the city is literally grafted.

### The green: the basin’s true population
The lushness is the majority life.

Up close, the vegetation is less like “trees and shrubs” and more like a set of solutions to the same problem, iterated thousands of times:

- **Wickers**: mats of root-thread that spread sideways like felt. They stitch the rock meal together, keep terraces from slumping, and harvest the stone’s faint exhale. When a wicker-mat is cut, it doesn’t bleed; it *dries* instantly at the wound and then seals with a glassy crust. People prize these mats the way other places prize timber.

- **Blade-stacks**: the jade-bladed rosettes you noticed from afar. Their “leaves” are heat-management devices, opening and closing in coordinated ripples at midday. In the morning they look waxy and inert; by noon they are visibly alive, flexing a fraction with each breath of wind. If you stand still and watch long enough, the whole terrace seems to shimmer with a slow, shared pulse.

- **Crystal fronds**: lattices that aren’t mineral exactly and aren’t cellulose either. Their fibers grow as if they are printing themselves into the air, then harden into a geometry that catches light and makes it useful—throwing it down, scattering it sideways, or breaking it into prismatic shade. They shed fine spore-dust that clings electrostatically to stone; the dust is the beginning of the next frond somewhere else.

- **Resin-bearers**: trunked forms that ooze amber nodules. The resin is not just glue or fuel; it is the city’s *sealant*, the substance that makes porous stone behave. When the basin shifts, when salt blooms threaten to split a seam, resin-bearers are “tapped” and their product is smeared into the right cracks with the casual intimacy of a medic dressing skin.

In the quiet niches—those sudden pockets where the roar drops away—there are also living things that look dead: **white crusts** and **grey velvet films**. These are the slowest residents, microbial or lichenlike, and they are what keeps the city from poisoning itself with its own salts. They eat what the wind deposits, fix what would otherwise become lethal, and leave behind faintly patterned stains that the locals read like weather.

A visitor might think the people maintain the gardens. The longer view is harsher and stranger: the gardens *permit* the people, and would continue without them. The city’s green life is ancient and patient; it is not sentimental about its symbioses.

### The small animals: punctuation, not prose
The fauna is sparse because everything mobile pays a price for being exposed to the wind’s violence and the light’s brutality. But a few lineages have committed, utterly, to this one niche:

- **Click-shelled pollinators**: pale insects with segmented armor that looks dusted in flour. Their joints make audible ticks as they move—useful, in a world where you track one another by vibration. They nest in warm cracks near vent mouths where spores and pollen collect. They are valued enough that some households keep “boxes” of them: not cages, but sheltered stones arranged to encourage a colony to choose that ledge.

- **Solvent-lick lizards**: small, quick, eyes filmed against glare. They lap the cooling exudate from certain blade-stacks and then sleep pressed to warm rock, conserving motion like a precious resource. Their droppings—chalky, mineral-rich—are gathered and crumbled into garden beds. Children are sent to collect it the way other places send children to gather eggs.

- **Wind-skins**: the batlike gliders high in the vent-ring. Hairless, thin as scraps of oiled paper, living on the boundary layer where updraft meets stone. They do not perch. When they must rest, they wedge into the mouths of organ-holes where the pressure holds them like a hand. People call them by a gesture more than a word: two fingers spread and tilted, meaning *that which rides without landing*. Their presence is taken as a sign that the vents are tuned correctly; when the wind-skins vanish, the windwrights go out with rope and mallet to listen for a failing note.

Nothing here wastes water to make flesh. Even animals feel “dry” in their behavior: brief, decisive movements; long stillness; life conducted in the thin margins between too-hot and too-cold.

### The people: wind-adapted, water-taboo, void-dwelling
And then there are the ones you would call people, though they don’t fit neatly into the word unless you let the place rewrite your assumptions.

They are not numerous. The city’s scale makes them look like scattered seeds caught on ledges. But they have been here long enough that their bodies and customs have converged on the same principles as the vegetation: conserve, wick, vent, adjust.

**Their bodies** tend to the practical:

- Skin that is often salt-tolerant and thickened where it matters—hands, soles, lips—because everything you touch here either abrades or desiccates. Many wear resin-smeared wraps not as decoration but as a second epidermis.
- Eyes adapted to glare: narrowed lids, pale membranes, and a habit of looking *past* brightness rather than into it. They learn early to read the world by edge and shadow.
- Breathing shaped by the roar. Infants are soothed not by lullabies but by being held where the wind-note is steady. A “good sleeping ledge” is one where the sound is constant enough that your body stops fighting it.

**Their clothing** is architecture in miniature: layered mats, stiffened fiber, scarves tied tight across the jaw and cheekbones. The scarf is not about dust—it is a brace against vibration, a way of giving the skull something to press against so the world doesn’t rattle your teeth.

**Their daily work** is less about building new things than keeping adjustability alive:

- **Windwrights** tune the city the way a musician tends an instrument. They climb into vents with padded mallets and wedges of resin, listening with their palms against stone. They open and throttle organ-holes with sliding slabs, not to make it quieter—quiet is impossible—but to keep the note from becoming destructive. A wrong resonance can shake grit loose from a ceiling; a mis-angled shutter can turn a courtyard into a sandblaster.

- **Wickers** cultivate and move the root-mats. When a terrace begins to salt over, they don’t “fix the soil”; they roll up the living mat like cloth, carry it to a fresher ledge, and pin it down with bone stakes until it takes hold again. Their craft is closer to textile work than farming.

- **Resiners** harvest amber nodules and trade in precise quantities, because resin is what lets them carry “bound water” safely: gels sealed in wax, damp pastes in resin tubes. These are the city’s medicine and emergency ration. A household’s wealth can be measured in how many sealed tubes it can spare.

- **Stone-readers**—part surveyor, part priest—track salt bloom cycles and the slow creep of mineral stains. They can tell you which stair will become slick next season, which wall will crack if a storm screams at a certain pitch. They don’t predict the future as much as they acknowledge that the basin is always in the middle of becoming something else.

**Their language** is inseparable from the city’s noise. Spoken words exist, but they are intimate, used nose-to-ear in windless niches. Public language is percussive and spatial:

- Codes tapped into resonant posts.
- Stomps on specific stones whose pitch is known.
- Shutter-flicks that slap like punctuation.
- Rope-plucks on bridge-webs, sending vibration ahead of a messenger.

It means the city is constantly “talking” even when no one is present: loose slats clacking, tensioned fibers humming, gourds throbbing in the central cavity. To an outsider it is chaos. To residents it is a readable field—like scent to an animal, like color to a bird.

**Their social structures** grow out of the water taboo and the reverence for void:

- Free water is treated the way a forest city treats open flame: a tool used only in designated, prepared places, handled by specialists, never carried casually. The worst accusation you can level at someone is not “thief” but “spiller.”
- Communal life happens on edges: shared shade-rings, market tables with grooves, workshops on bright ledges where the wind can clear grit from a tool-stroke.
- The rare silent niches function as sanctuaries and courts and hospitals. People go there not to commune with a deity but to re-meet their own bodies. In the sudden absence of the roar, you can hear a heartbeat; that sound becomes, for many, the closest thing to prayer.

**Their art**, inevitably, is tuning. A beautiful object here is one that changes the way wind moves—screens that make a harsher corridor gentle, bridge-webs strung so they sing in a particular mode at dusk, hanging gourd-frames that “answer” a vent’s note. The city is full of unseen composers.

### Something else lives there, too: the distributed mind of maintenance
Over time you notice a pattern that is hard to explain as mere culture.

The residents do not simply *use* the city; they respond to it with the reflexes of an immune system. When a new salt bloom forms, people show up before it becomes dangerous, carrying resin and wedges, as if summoned. When a certain organ-hole’s tone shifts, a windwright arrives without being told, climbing into the rim at the exact hour the temperature difference will make the correction easiest.

At first that seems like attentiveness and tradition.

Then you watch a child, barely old enough to climb, pause on a stair and press their fingertips to a post—eyes unfocused, listening through skin. They choose a different route, tugging an adult with them. Later you learn a vent upstream had begun to shiver that afternoon, a precursor to a stoneflake collapse. The child had no vocabulary for it. They simply *knew*.

Living in constant vibration changes what counts as a sense. The city has taught its inhabitants to think with their hands, their teeth, their soles. In return, they have made themselves into mobile sensors, distributed across terraces, keeping the basin’s lungs open and its skin sealed.

So what lives there?

A wind-city that breathes through rock and sings without rest.
A vast, mineral-wicking green that turns dryness into abundance.
Small, precise animals that survive by obeying the place’s physics.
And people—few, adapted, half-musicians and half-gardeners—who have become, over generations, the city’s way of keeping itself alive.
