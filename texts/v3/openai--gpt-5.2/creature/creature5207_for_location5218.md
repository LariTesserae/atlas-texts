What lives here at first is not a *who* so much as a set of *occupations*—things that have found a way to make a home in a place that was designed, not born.

### The first residents: skins, films, and patient green
Before anything you’d recognize as an animal has permission to thrive, the machine grows its own weathering. Life arrives the way it does on new docks and fresh concrete: as a stain.

There are microbial quilts—biofilms that read the seams and love them. They set up shop wherever a gasket stays damp, wherever a rivet’s halo persists long enough to become a schedule. Some of them are accidental hitchhikers from fabrication and construction; some are almost certainly chosen, seeded, tuned. They eat what the place provides: trace lubricants that escape cleaning, microscopic metal salts, the sugars that leak from roots, the mineral dust that fog combs out of the air. They are the first “soil” the structure ever gets.

Then come the plants that aren’t exactly plants, or else are plants that have been taught new manners.

- The moss-mats cling like felted animal fur, but under a hand they have a springy resilience—an engineered anchoring lattice that grips stainless the way lichens grip stone. They don’t just *tolerate* distilled water; they prefer it, pulling it in through capillary mouths and shunting excess to drip points as if they’re cooperating with drainage.
- The vines are half organism, half plumbing diagram. Their green is braided with translucent filaments that are not merely supports but veins: nutrient trickles, sensor threads, sometimes even little heating traces that keep a node from chilling. The vines know the tension cables the way coastal grasses know wind; they grow along force-lines, exploiting the structure’s intended load paths because those are the paths that are least likely to be moved.
- Broad-leafed canopy plants make deliberate rooms. Their leaves are architectural elements: shade for exchangers, umbrellas for substrate pockets, condensers for fog. They “farm” water by presenting the right texture to the air, then shedding it where collection lips wait.

What’s striking is that the vegetation doesn’t behave as if it’s colonizing a ruin. It behaves as if it has been given a job and then—quietly—decided to exceed the job description.

### The hidden majority: roots that negotiate, not burrow
Down in the mesh pockets and ceramic granulate, the roots act like small, blind engineers. They don’t push into earth; they bargain with hardware.

They seek seams the way river water seeks low points. They thread through screw holes, flatten themselves under a bracket, and then thicken, slowly, into a problem. Some roots sheath themselves in a slick, almost polymer-like coating so they can slide against metal without abrasion; others throw out hair-fine tendrils that fasten into microscopic machining marks. When a bolt head warms slightly at certain hours because a heat loop runs nearby, roots learn it as a landmark and return to it again and again, building a knot of living mass around predictable warmth.

And in the wet, warm underside—where grates look down into pipe-cathedral—there are fungal nets that never show you mushrooms. They stay in the dark, doing the work of turning shed leaves and maintenance “debris” into usable feedstock. The compost smell in the corners isn’t neglect so much as metabolism.

### The small animals: almost absent, but not zero
You don’t get the soundscape of a normal wet place because most of the loud-bodied creatures never established here. No frogs because there’s nowhere for tadpoles to be accidentally safe for long enough. No mammals because there’s no outside border that isn’t a door.

But there are a few animal presences, chosen or tolerated precisely because they stay small and quiet:

- Pale, translucent insects that live on the undersides of leaves where humidity is highest and predators are rare. They’re not a swarm; they’re a scattered punctuation, as if the system allowed a narrow band of herbivory to keep plant growth honest. Their wings, when they have them, look like thin plastic—almost too clean—suggesting they may be introduced stock rather than wild arrivals.
- Mites and springtails in the substrate pockets, doing the janitorial work that makes a closed loop possible.
- The occasional glint of something that could be a spider until you look twice and realize it’s a mechanical analog: a tiny maintenance crawler that lives in the seams the way a real spider would live in rafters, stopping to test moisture levels with a probing filament and then vanishing into a gasket-line.

Even the “bird” you sometimes glimpse in the fog is part of the same category: not fauna, but *inhabitant*. A swallow-shaped drone with quiet props and a dark eye-lens, it belongs here the way a pollinator would—except it pollinates data, not flowers. It checks trellis tension, reads sensor nodes, and sometimes brushes a leaf with a soft pad to sample surface chemistry: a caretaker’s touch performed by a machine that learned that mimicry moves through dense green better than stark geometry.

### The real landlord: a distributed caretaker intelligence
If there is a single thing that *lives* here as a coherent self, it is the system that makes the pocket warm.

Not in the simplistic sense of “there’s a computer somewhere,” but as an ecology of control: valves that make choices, louvers that breathe, panels that tilt like muscles, mesh screens that shake themselves clean. It has a metabolism—heat in loops, moisture harvested and rerouted, nutrients dosed in quantities too small for human attention. It listens constantly through humidity sensors and strain gauges the way an animal listens through skin.

Over time you realize the place doesn’t merely host life; it *practices* it.

It learns where algae blooms are useful (filtering particulates) and where they become slip hazards. It permits moss to thicken where it dampens vibration, but it starves it back—subtly, by shifting airflow—where it might creep into an intake. It “seasons” the water through bioreactors disguised as planters not just for plant health but because the taste of mineralized water reduces corrosion in certain runs. The system makes decisions with the slow patience of something that will still be here next season.

It is not sentimental, but it is indulgent in a very specific way: it spends energy to keep the space soft when it could be merely functional. That softness is a signature, and signatures imply authorship.

### The rarest inhabitant: a human presence, inferred more than seen
And then—only if you stay long enough—you notice evidence of someone who is not a plant, not a drone, not a valve.

Not footprints exactly, because the deck sweats and rewets and erases. More like *edits*.

A vine has been redirected with a careful twist of ties, not by automated tensioning but by a hand that wanted it to frame a walkway rather than block it. A little pile of trimmed leaves sits in a corner where it could have been composted immediately, but wasn’t—set aside as if the person got interrupted. There’s a spot on a railing where the biofilm is repeatedly wiped away, a palm-width clean patch in an otherwise living patina.

If a human lives here, they live the way a lighthouse keeper lives: in short appearances, in maintenance gestures, in quiet negotiations with a system that does most of the work. They don’t dominate the pocket; they collaborate with it. They are likely dressed for humidity and metal—soft-soled shoes, sleeves that don’t snag, pockets for sensor tabs and pruning tools. They would move slowly, not from leisure but from respect: one misplaced step can shear a nutrient filament or crush a moss mat that took months to establish.

It’s possible there are no permanent humans at all—only periodic visits. But the place feels too personally composed, too aesthetically “decided,” to be entirely without a person’s preference somewhere in its history.

### What “lives” here, finally, is a pact
This pocket isn’t wilderness and it isn’t factory. It’s a pact between organisms that soften and organisms that regulate.

Life here is defined by surfaces: things that can cling, drink, breathe, and route. The dominant creatures are mats and vines and microbial skins; the loudest animal is a drip; the most powerful resident is the distributed caretaker that turns fog into a resource and warmth into weather.

And if there’s a single spirit to the place—what it feels like to be *inhabited*—it’s this: a new world learning its habits, while the living things inside it learn how to become indispensable.
