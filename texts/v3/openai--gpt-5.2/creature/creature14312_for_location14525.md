Most of what lives up here does not *go* anywhere.

It *holds*.

In a place where “up” is only a thinning of the medium and “distance” sometimes behaves like a suggestion, the dominant life is the kind that survives by making itself into an answer to flow: catching it, tasting it, slowing it, turning it into tissue, turning tissue back into spores, letting the current do the traveling.

### The resident majority: the layered small

The first inhabitants are the ones you feel before you see—because they are the reason the water has texture.

In the sweeter bands, the micro-life grows in soft veils: blooms of single cells and colonial filaments that clump around mineral ions the way dust clings to humidity. They thicken the medium into that faint syrup sensation, and when the upland-sea “inhales,” they are the first to respond: swelling, dividing, building quick, temporary architectures—gelatinous lattices that slow silt and then collapse when the chemistry shifts. The collapsing is one source of the perpetual quiet crackle: not only bubbles, but these fleeting micro-reefs popping apart as their own metabolism changes the pH around them.

Lower, where the water is a touch more acidic, different microlayers dominate: carbonate-eaters, silicate-sheath builders, organisms that wear stone the way other worlds’ creatures wear shells. They are the authors of the pale mineral lace that fills cairn-gaps and slab-circles—because they precipitate not as passive deposits but as living decisions: choosing edges, seams, stress-lines, places where flow brings both food and risk.

Over time they create films that are not just coatings but *interfaces*: skins on rock, skins on leaf, skins on older skins. They alter friction. They make some surfaces slick, others velcro-rough. Entire current paths in the forested ridges are guided by what amounts to microbial landscaping.

This is why the place feels densely occupied even when no animal passes: the water is crowded with life that doesn’t read as “creature,” only as viscosity, taste, and faintly biased motion.

### The great forests: buoyant filters that behave like organs

Then come the structures that look like plants only because your mind wants a familiar word.

The tall “trees” are columns of flexible tissue built around a braided core—bundles of translucent fibers shot through with channels. Those channels don’t just move sap-equivalent; they move *density*. The trunks actively pump slightly different salinities and temperatures through themselves, creating micro-buoyancy shifts that let the whole organism stand higher into the lighter bands or sink a little when the medium becomes harsh.

Their hanging sheets—those ribbed membranes—are not leaves in the sunlight sense. They are *filters* and *wings*: they present enormous surface area to flow, catching dissolved organics and microscopic prey, and they can subtly billow or tighten to change the local current. A mature stand is less like a grove and more like a coordinated set of gills.

What makes them “lush” here is not just that they grow; it’s that they grow *together*. Where the ridges are broad-backed, colonies interlace their upper fronds so that a passing front of denser water bends them in synchrony. The bowing is not purely passive. Within the bend, the membranes stiffen along certain ribs, loosening others, as if each column is listening and deciding how much to yield. When the current passes, a rain of spores drifts off—fine, persistent, and adhesive enough to take hold on mineral film or older tissue.

If you watch long enough, you realize the forest is doing something subtler than feeding: it is *editing* the water. It slows silt here, lets it scour there, thickens the medium in one corridor and thins it in another. It makes paths, and then it makes them stop being paths.

### The carpets and mats: the true ground, grown rather than found

On the stone itself, the “moss” quilts are the real proprietors of the upland.

They creep, yes, but they also remember. Not in a mystical way—more in the sense that their growth patterns encode flow histories. A mat that has lived through decades of shifting fronts becomes physically biased: thicker on the side that receives mineral upwelling, more porous where shear tends to arrive, studded with air-filled nodules in places that often go stagnant.

Those nodules are both float and lung-analog: bladders filled with gases produced by fermentation within the deeper layers of the mat. The mat can close valves and hold buoyancy, lifting itself off the rock to let water rush underneath through those hidden galleries. The accelerated flow polishes the bedrock, but it also brings food and oxygen-equivalent, and it discourages would-be grazers—there is little that likes to feed while being sandblasted by a deliberate current.

In hollows where sediments settle, the mats change character. They become gardens of form: rosettes that spiral to manage eddies, fans that orient to capture suspended micro-life, delicate filament fields that vibrate at particular frequencies of flow. Those filaments are sensory arrays as much as feeding structures. Disturb them, and you can watch a wave of contraction ripple across a patch—a local hush, a closing of pores, an exhalation of faintly cloudy chemistry that makes the immediate water less appetizing.

### The animal minority: visitors, not owners

Animals exist here the way sparks exist in damp air: possible, brief, and specialized to the point of looking like exceptions.

They tend to fall into a few repeating strategies.

**1) Ribbon-swimmers (the refraction-thin hunters)**  
The long transparent ones you glimpse are not built for speed so much as for *not being a discrete object*. Their bodies are narrow sheets with a dark internal line—organs packed into a minimal strip—while the rest is clear tissue with a refractive index tuned close to the local medium. In a world where light is already indecisive, they become almost unseeable unless they cross a boundary layer.

They move by sending traveling waves down their length, but the wave is also a *pressure trick*: they can “borrow” motion from a passing current front, slipping along the gradient rather than pushing against the bulk water. When the geometry misbehaves—when a kilometer becomes an hour and then becomes nothing—these swimmers are the ones most likely to take advantage. They patrol the places where the world loops a moment, feeding on whatever accumulates when the medium repeats: stray spores, stunned microfauna, confused grazers that find themselves passing the same frond twice.

**2) Bladder-grazers (the slow rings)**  
The drifting grazers are more buoyancy system than musculature: an outer sac with internal partitions that can be filled with different densities of fluid or gas, letting them hover at a chosen layer boundary. Their “mouth” is a soft ring that seals against a membrane-leaf and scrapes with microscopic hardened ridges—not tearing, but sanding, leaving those pale crescents.

They are patient enough to be dangerous in a slow ecosystem. A grazer can park on a productive frond for hours, taking just enough to avoid triggering the forest’s chemical defenses. They reproduce by budding: a small bladder pinches off and drifts until it finds a mat-hollow rich enough to sustain it. Most never do.

**3) Threaders and mites (the almost-invisible citizens)**  
The true animal abundance is tiny: wormlets that live in the mats’ undergalleries, crustacean-analog mites that graze microbial films, ciliated specks that ride silt like balloons. They’re rarely seen because the medium hides them, but they are the difference between a mat that becomes suffocated in its own detritus and a mat that stays porous and alive.

They are also, quietly, the dispersal mechanism of the forests. Spores stick to them. They carry one stand’s chemistry into another stand’s water. In a place without reliable straight lines, hitchhiking becomes a way to cross the folded map.

**4) The rare big ones (the reason the silence feels watched)**  
Very occasionally, something larger moves—large enough to displace the fronds rather than slip between them. These creatures do not announce themselves with sound. They announce themselves with *changes in current you can feel in your bones*: a pressure easing, a suction, a slow shove.

They tend to be deep-bodied and slow, armored not with plates but with toughened mineral-skins grown from the same precipitates that lace the cairns. They browse, they rest, they drift through ridge-forests like boulders deciding to be alive for a while. They are so few that each one shapes the local ecology simply by existing: a moving hard surface on which films can grow, a traveling reef that carries a community of micro-life across distances that may or may not be distances.

### The stone-marks and the question of “who made them”

The cairns and slab circles do not feel actively tended—yet they also don’t feel entirely abandoned.

Here, “built” and “grown” blur.

The stones were placed by hands—or by manipulators that cared about stacking, alignment, enclosure. But the ongoing *maintenance* is biological. Mineral-lace organisms prefer the sheltered micro-eddies inside stacked gaps. Mat-quilts anchor against upright slabs because the slabs interrupt flow and create reliable turbulence patterns. Over centuries, living growth reinforces the arrangement: binding stones with precipitated seams, cushioning them with fiber, making collapse less likely.

It can give the unsettling impression that the structures are *choosing to remain structures*, even if their original builders are gone.

And there are hints—subtle, never certain—that some life still uses them as reference points. Not by carving new meaning, but by passing through in repeated ways: a ribbon-swimmer that always slips along the same trilithon’s lee-side when the clarity tide arrives; a grazer that returns to a particular cairn as if it anchors a migratory loop; mats that open their undergalleries in the same places, making hidden corridors that line up with stone circles like a nervous system aligning to old bones.

### The oldest inhabitant: the medium itself, as a kind of tissue

Spend long enough in the upland sea and the “presences of another kind” become less poetic and more literal.

The stratified water is not just a setting; it behaves like a coupled organ made of chemistry, heat, micro-life, and suspended mineral. It stores patterns. It replays them. It sometimes refuses to let a moment fully pass.

Where the world loops—where you meet the same stand of fronds again with only the spores rearranged—it is rarely random. Those places often sit at the intersection of three conditions: a persistent density boundary, a forest old enough to have sculpted local flow for ages, and mineral films smooth enough to reduce turbulence into stable, repeating sheets.

In such zones the medium acts like memory foam: pressed by movement, it slowly returns, but not instantly. Tiny delays accumulate. Eddies return to the same shapes. Chemical gradients rebuild in familiar layers. Life that can sense those layers—mats, filaments, the frond-forests—begins to synchronize with them, because synchrony is survival. The place becomes a habit that the ecosystem and the physics share.

If there is anything like “mind” native to the uplands, it is not housed in an animal skull. It is distributed across these repeating patterns: the forests’ coordinated yielding, the mats’ ripple-responses, the microbial films’ selective precipitations, the way clarity tides sweep through like breathing.

Not a single intelligence that speaks.

A residency.

A slow, thick occupancy made of countless living decisions that add up to a landscape that behaves, increasingly, like one organism that happens to contain ridges and valleys inside it.

### What it means to live here

To live in the high submerged uplands is to accept that permanence comes from softness, not hardness.

The inhabitants do not conquer the medium; they tune themselves to its bands. They do not build with metal; they build with mineral skin, with braided fiber, with stacks of stone that biology can reinforce. They do not rely on sight; they rely on touch, taste, viscosity, the angle of a current across a membrane.

And because the place is warm and old and insists on life, what “lives there” is less a cast of creatures and more a continuing occupation by systems: forests that are filters, carpets that are ground, microbes that are weather, rare animals that are fleeting edits in a world whose main story is written by vegetation and water and time.
