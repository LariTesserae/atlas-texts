Life on the upland does not present itself as individuals first. It presents as *flow*—as if the plateau has learned to breathe in slow, scheduled volumes, and the creatures are the valves and bellows.

From a distance you see only the grammar of it: parallel smudges moving along a pale river-corridor; a faint stippling on a bench of gravel where something has been feeding; a darkening patch in a basin where bodies have gathered and the ground has been worked. Only after hours—after your own attention stops looking for drama—do the residents resolve into forms.

## The great herds: the Pressers

The dominant animals are not built like the swift grazers of warmer continents. They are built like endurance made into flesh—massive, quiet, and cooperative in a way that feels less social than *logistical*.

They are called (in the only tongue that matters here: pattern) **Pressers**.

- **Shape:** Low-slung torsos on thick columnar limbs, with shoulders higher than hips so they can lean into grades without changing pace. Their necks are short and powerful, suited to pushing and scraping rather than browsing at height.
- **Skin and insulation:** A double coat: a tight, dark underlayer that holds heat and a longer outer pelt of hollow fibers that sheds rime. In certain light the outer coat looks dusted with ash; in others it looks like old felt.
- **Feet:** Wide, padded, almost silent. The pad is not soft exactly—more like cork: springy, tough, able to conform to gravel without scattering it. On basalt pavements their passage leaves a faint polish rather than a clatter.
- **Mouthparts:** Not a single clean muzzle, but a broad, blunt face with ridged lips and keratin scrapers. They can strip sedge, comb moss from stone, and rake lichen-crust without breaking their own teeth. When they feed, they do not tear; they *abrade*.

A Presser herd is not a crowd. It is a machine with redundancy.

They move in long, braided files along corridors that have been used so long the stones are subtly sorted by weight: larger rounded cobbles at the edges, finer gravel in the middle where the pads have kneaded it for generations. The herd never truly stops. It shifts.

- One band feeds while another rests standing, heads low, bodies angled to share wind shadow even when there is no wind.
- Calves—if you can call the downy, long-legged young that—are kept in the center, where the ground is most compacted and the temperature a fraction kinder.
- Old individuals are not abandoned; they fall back into slower lanes, and the lanes adjust around them.

### What they “make”

They do not merely consume the plateau’s sparse growth. They *engineer access* to what the plateau hides.

**Compaction basins** are their most obvious work. In the dim, at the edge of a shallow bowl, you can find the track-pattern: concentric loops where thousands of bodies have walked in slow circles, pressing wet gravel until the water film rises. The herd then drinks in an order that seems instinctive and precise—no fighting, no rush. When the water is gone, the basin remains as a slightly deepened depression with a crust of fine silt: a future condensation trap.

**Warming scrapes** are subtler. In certain places the herd clears away pale gravel to expose darker stone. The exposed slabs warm marginally under the flat sky; that margin is enough. At dusk, moisture condenses there first. At dawn, the herd returns to lick the invisible water and the salts that ride with it.

If you spend long enough in one place, you realize the herds are not chasing water. They are **coaxing it** out of the plateau’s reluctance.

### How they talk

They rarely call. Instead, they communicate through:

- **Sub-audible vibration:** A deep chest-thrum you feel through your boots when you stand on compacted ground—more pressure than sound. It carries far in stone.
- **Scent-marks in dust:** Not sharp musk, but a faint mineralized odor left where they rub glands against certain rocks—message boards that last because the cold refuses to erase them quickly.
- **Timing:** The most important signal is simply *arrival*. When the herd turns, when it lingers, when it bypasses a basin, every other creature notices.

## The silent flyers: the Tapers and the Undertow

Above the herds, where the cloud ceiling makes the sky feel close, there are wings.

At first they look like defects in the light—ellipses drifting across the gravel’s matte glare. Then you see the deliberate geometry: long, narrow wings with a slight backward sweep, bodies small enough that they seem all wing.

Two kinds dominate.

### The Tapers (scavenger-gliders)

They ride the plateau’s thin thermals with almost no movement, adjusting angle by fractions. Their heads are heavy with hooked beaks, their eyes set forward more than most birds, giving them a faintly intent, almost clerical look.

They do not hunt often; chasing wastes energy. Instead they watch for *events*:

- a lagging Presser,
- a sudden stillness in a herd lane,
- the darkening of gravel where blood has touched.

When a death happens, Tapers appear as if they were already there—which, in truth, they often were. They descend without noise and feed quickly, not with frenzy but with efficiency. They leave little exposed flesh for the cold to hoard.

Their droppings—thick, nutrient-rich, and deposited where updrafts stall—are one of the plateau’s main fertilizers. Under their habitual perches, moss pads grow slightly greener, and wire-shrubs manage an extra season of stiffness before dying.

### The Undertow (herd-shadowers)

Larger, paler, with wings wide enough to briefly blot the already-dim light, these flyers do something stranger: they follow the herds not for carrion, but for **warmth and insects**.

In the brief wet periods, when rills appear and the sedges wake, tiny winged things hatch—gnat-like, almost invisible except as a faint shimmer above damp hollows. The Undertow birds drift low behind the herds, picking these from the air and from the warm boundary layer that forms around thousands of bodies.

They are so quiet that when they finally flap—once, twice, with a slow, heavy stroke—it feels like someone turned a page in a closed room.

## The ground’s second city: burrowers and ice-keepers

Under the gravel skin the plateau is less dead than it looks. The temperature is steadier. The wind cannot abrade. Moisture, when it comes, can be held in pores and tiny lenses of ice.

Here live the **burrowers**, small mammals and insect-analogues that the herds inadvertently support.

### Frostmoles (the aerators)

They are palm-sized, blind or nearly so, with shovel forelimbs and fur so dense it looks like gray velvet. They live in networks just below the freeze line, where the ground is firm enough to hold tunnels but soft enough to move.

Their tunnels do three crucial things:

1. **Aerate** compacted soils so roots can penetrate during wet pulses.
2. **Channel** meltwater sideways, spreading it from a single seep into a broader moist zone.
3. **Store** warmth: their bodies and their nesting material create microclimates that keep certain pockets from fully freezing.

Their ventilation holes dot the flats like pinpricks. After a cold night, each is ringed with frost—tiny white halos that reveal an underground infrastructure.

### Gravel lice (the recyclers)

Not lice in any familiar sense—more like pale, jointed mites that move slowly through the dust, feeding on fungal threads, droppings, and anything that decomposes at the plateau’s reluctant pace.

When a carcass is stripped and the bones remain, these small recyclers are among the few that can make use of what’s left. They do not eat bone; they eat the films, the microscopic greases, the last stubborn proteins that cold would otherwise preserve for years. They are part of why “nothing is wasted quickly” doesn’t become “nothing is wasted at all.”

### Ice-keepers (the hidden constant)

In cracks under overhangs and in shaded stone lips, there are colonies of organisms that live directly on the edge of freezing: dark biofilms, lichen-fungi partnerships, and bacteria that produce antifreeze proteins.

They look like stains until you see their effect. Where they grow, ice forms differently—more layered, less brittle. Their presence subtly increases how much water can be held in a fissure without shattering the rock. Over decades, they help build the plateau’s secret reservoirs: not lakes, not glaciers, but *habits of storage*.

## The scavengers on foot: bone-walkers and rind-eaters

Not all scavengers have wings.

Along the herd corridors, especially near compaction basins where traffic is constant, you sometimes see low, long-bodied animals moving with a cautious economy: **bone-walkers**.

- They are roughly the size of a large dog, but their posture is wrong for pursuit: shoulders high, head low, spine flexible.
- Their fur is sparse and oily, meant more for shedding wet than for insulation; they rely on burrows, herd heat, and patience.
- Their jaws are narrow and strong, adapted to cracking frozen tendon and cartilage.

They are not pack hunters. They are *followers*.

A bone-walker will trail a herd lane for days, pausing at the edges of rest-stations where droppings accumulate and where small burrowers are most active. They pick what the plateau offers: beetle-analogs, the occasional weakened calf, frozen scraps left behind by Tapers, and—most importantly—anything that dies of distance.

There are also **rind-eaters**: squat creatures that look like moving stones, with keratinous mouthplates for scraping lichens and with guts specialized for extracting a few calories from a world of mineral crust. They are slow, armored, and oddly abundant near old basalt pavements where lichen growth is richest. When threatened they do not flee; they simply become indistinguishable from the ground, and in this light that is often enough.

## The plants that behave like systems

Vegetation here is not a carpet; it is a set of *installations*.

- **Crust-lichens** are the plateau’s first language, painting basalt with maps of chemistry. Some are matte and chalky; others have a faint metallic sheen that flashes and then disappears when the cloud light shifts.
- **Moss pads** are the plateau’s sponges, found only where stone geometry creates shelter—concavities, the lee of a slab, the bottom of a shallow bowl that catches condensate.
- **Wire shrubs** look dead until they aren’t. Their leaves are reduced to scales; their stems are jointed, designed to flex without breaking. During the rare wet stretch they produce tiny dull flowers that open and close without scent, as if unwilling to advertise.
- **Sedge threads** occupy the wide “rivers” when they run, anchoring gravel with long fibrous roots. They grow fast when water is present and then shut down into a tough, almost wooden state when it vanishes.

The herds treat these plant pockets the way a city treats reservoirs and fields: not consciously, but reliably. They trample in some places to create future moisture; they avoid others to let moss recover. Their droppings fall along predictable wind shadows; those shadows become the richest soil the plateau will ever allow.

In time, certain basins become greener not because the climate changes, but because traffic patterns do.

## Predation here is mostly geometry

There are predators, but few are built for speed. Speed costs too much.

The plateau’s main killers are:

- **Distance** (the long gaps between usable hollows),
- **mis-timing** (arriving at a basin before compaction has coaxed water up, or after it has been drunk and banked),
- **injury that cannot heal quickly** in cold air that discourages tissue repair,
- **exposure** when cloud seams brighten and everything becomes visible at once.

When something does actively hunt, it tends to do so by *waiting*.

Somewhere among the slate stacks and tilted metamorphic sheets are animals that rarely leave their rock. They are lean, gray, and so still that their fur grows dusted with the same fine grit as the ground. They do not chase herds. They take the lone, the confused, the newborn that strays beyond the moving wall of bodies. Their success rate is low, but the plateau does not require frequent success—only persistence.

## The smallest residents: the ones that make “optimization” possible

If the plateau feels optimized, it is not only because large bodies move like infrastructure. It is also because microscopic lives close the loops.

- In damp moss, **fungal threads** bind soil and hold water a little longer than physics would suggest.
- In burrow networks, **bacteria** fix nitrogen from the thin air, turning it—slowly—into fertility.
- On the underside of stones, **algae-like films** photosynthesize in light so dim it barely seems like light at all, storing energy that will be eaten later by mites, which will be eaten later by burrowers, which will be eaten later by bone-walkers, which will die and return to dust.

Nothing here is fast. But nothing is allowed to be useless for long.

## What it feels like to share the plateau with them

When you stand still long enough, the place stops being empty and starts being occupied in a way that is hard to explain to anyone who thinks occupation is noise.

A herd passes a mile away and you don’t hear it; you feel a soft insistence in your sternum, as if the ground is remembering weight. A Taperglider moves across your field of view and you only notice because a patch of gravel briefly loses a degree of brightness. You kneel beside a moss hollow and see that the moss is not just moss: it is a city of tiny movements, slow as frost, busy as any market.

And through it all the plateau keeps its rules:

Moisture hides, light flattens, distance punishes, and life—without walls, without tools, without banners—performs the work of a whole engineered world.

The residents do not dominate by being loud. They dominate by being *scheduled*. They are the plateau’s only architecture: moving corridors, trampled basins, warmed scrapes, fertilized shadows—an industry made entirely of bodies that learned, over uncountable time, how to make a dim, cold height keep running.
