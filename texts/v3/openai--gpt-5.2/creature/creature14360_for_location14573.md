What lives there is, at first, hard to accept as “life” at all, because it refuses every cue you’ve been trained to look for. No wetness. No greening. No quickness. No heat you can call metabolism. No eyes to meet yours back.

And then you stay long enough to notice that the place is not merely *filled* with organisms.

It is an organism that learned to become a place.

### The load-bearers (the ones you touch first)

The walls and rails are not trees in the usual sense. They are *tensile flora*—growth that evolved under a ceiling it could never outgrow and in a chemistry that would never indulge soft tissues.

Their bodies are bundles: thousands of hair-fine fibers pressed into ropes, ropes laminated into ribs, ribs braided into beams. If you cut one—if you force yourself to—you don’t find rings or pith. You find a dry, layered composite, more like horn and cork than wood, shot through with narrow channels that carry oils rather than sap.

They don’t “drink.” They *dissolve*.

At their roots—if you can call those black, searching seams “roots”—they exude slow solvents that worry at rock: not to make soil, but to free specific ions. Iron darkens them into near-black strength. Sulfur plates them pale and brittle. Salt makes their skin glaze, a protective crust that keeps their precious internal chemistry from evaporating into the city’s constant draft.

They grow where the forces are.

A human builder would put a buttress where a roof wants to fall. These plants do it too, except they do it by feeling the roof *already falling*, in increments so small only a living thing could bother to answer them. Pressure becomes instruction. Strain becomes a map.

And the answer—always—arrives as a slow tightening, a thickening, a new cable laid across an old gap.

### The wind-organs (the ones you hear before you understand)

Some of the hanging cords in the atrium are not simply supports; they are *instruments* the organism grew on purpose. Hollow segments with ribbing; narrow throats that whistle; widened bladders that act as resonance chambers. They are tuned the way a body is tuned—by survival, by accident, by the gentle tyranny of physics repeated for millennia.

These organs do more than make the city loud.

They allow the city to know itself.

A change in airflow is not just weather down here; it is a message. When the outer crack loosens, the draft’s pitch shifts. When a terrace settles a finger’s breadth, a different overtone appears. When an unfamiliar weight steps onto a crust that rings, the vibration returns through the beams—through living tissue—and the organism responds by redistributing tension. Not thought, exactly. Not decision the way you mean it.

More like reflex scaled up to architecture.

You can feel it in your bones when you stand still: a barely perceptible tightening of the nearest rail, as if the place has taken your measure and decided, calmly, to keep holding.

### The sealed bladders (the city’s “blood,” if it has any)

Here and there, built into ribs and arches like knots in a muscle, are the glazed sacs—the bladders you already sensed you shouldn’t puncture.

They’re not water storage. Water is too dangerous: too easily lost, too quickly frozen, too ready to invite the wrong chemistry. The bladders are instead reservoirs of dense oils and volatile solvents: fluids that carry dissolved minerals, that shuttle energy, that function as both antifreeze and battery.

When a beam is stressed, those fluids move.

Slowly, yes—but movement nonetheless: a thick circulation through capillaries so narrow they might as well be memories of veins. The oils are how the organism shares resources between chambers that never meet, between terraces separated by emptiness. They are also how it heals: a breach in one cable can be patched by a seep of resin that hardens into a new layer, dry and glassy, sealing the wound against the city’s endless thirst.

The sharp, resinous smell you described is not perfume. It is alarm and antiseptic at once—the chemical equivalent of a flinch.

### The crystal fans (the ones that feed on the thin air)

In the deepest still pockets, where the draft fades and dust hangs as if nailed in place, the organism makes structures that barely resemble plants at all: slow fans of crystalline tissue, branching like coral, pale as old bone.

These are its lungs—not for oxygen in the way yours needs it, but for *trace harvest*.

They are surfaces tuned to steal from scarcity: to trap molecules that drift by rarely and reluctantly; to bind what little moisture exists as hydration in salts; to take CO₂ and other gases as raw material; to capture metallic dust not as dirt but as diet. Their metabolism is a kind of dry chemosynthesis, patient and exacting, as if the city learned early that sunlight would never come and decided it didn’t deserve to be mourned.

If you brush one, it doesn’t bend. It hums—a faint, brittle vibration—because it is both tissue and mineral, life that has surrendered softness to persist.

### The archive-skins (what the city does with its dead)

Nothing rots here. So the dead don’t disappear; they *accumulate*.

Old surface layers slough off in papery sheets and cling in corners. In side chambers, those skins stack into drifts: membrane over membrane, each one a record of atmosphere and stress. A season when the air carried more salt. A decade of quieter draft. A collapse somewhere far away that sent a tremor through the network and triggered a citywide thickening.

If you look closely—if you have the patience—those layers are not random. The organism sheds strategically, the way a tree sheds bark, the way a snake sheds skin. Deadness is managed, cataloged, kept from fouling the living structure.

The city remembers by stratigraphy.

### The small life you don’t see (because it lives *inside* rock and fiber)

Even if no animals have ever found a foothold here, the city is not alone in itself.

Within the braided beams and along root-seams in reactive stone, there is a whole invisible citizenship: microbial and fungal analogues adapted to dryness—endolithic colonies that live in the first millimeter of rock; symbionts that help dissolve metal-bearing veins; spore-forms that wait as dust for centuries until a microclimate shifts by a degree.

They are the city’s digestion and its immune system. They break down what the main organism cannot, they detoxify what would poison the oils, they knit the boundary between “plant” and “stone” until the distinction stops being useful.

If the big cables are the architecture, these tiny lives are the mortar that is also alive.

### The central knot (the closest thing to a “self”)

Around the central shaft—the throat that never stops breathing—growth becomes thicker, more ropey, more deliberate. Cables converge there not because it’s a sacred center, but because it’s the only reliable gradient in the whole system: the draft, the pressure, the cold, the dustfall.

The organism builds its sensory density where information is richest.

Down there, in the rim’s braided collar, is likely the oldest living tissue: the part that has never been shed, never been allowed to die, because everything else depends on its slow regulation of airflow and tension. Not a brain, but a hub—an evolved inevitability.

If you want to imagine “someone” living here, imagine that: not a creature with a face, but a knot of living load and living breath that has been taking measurements for longer than your language has had a word for measurement.

It doesn’t greet you. It doesn’t hunt you. It doesn’t help you.

It incorporates you—briefly—into its calculations.

### How they reproduce (because a city must make more city)

They cannot fling fruit into sunlight or entice animals that aren’t here. Their reproduction is as dry as everything else:

- **Dust-spores**: microscopic, resin-coated grains released when tension patterns say the outside crack has opened “enough.” They ride the rare outward drafts and vanish into the salt plain, hoping to find another fracture, another void.
- **Mineral seeds**: dense nodules grown around a grain of reactive rock, heavy enough to roll into crevices and survive decades without activity until chemistry wakes them.
- **Clonal grafting**: the most common method—simply growing across gaps, stitching one terrace to another, making offspring that are not separate, just expanded.

It doesn’t seek to become many. It seeks to keep holding.

### What the inhabitants are, in the plainest terms

A single, ancient, dry-adapted botanical superstructure—more scaffold than shrub—living on mineral chemistry and air-traces, built into a city shape by a long-forgotten partnership between slow life and slower geology.

If hands once guided it, those hands are gone. The organism kept the habit anyway: braiding itself into stairs, thickening itself into arches, growing itself into rails where falls would happen. It continues the work because the work is indistinguishable from breathing.

So the answer to “who lives there?” is:

- **The braided plants**, which are also beams and streets and doors.
- **Their hidden symbionts**, which make stone edible and keep the oils clean.
- **The city itself**, as a distributed, listening, tension-reading body that has learned to navigate darkness by sound—sound it makes, sound it measures, sound it uses to remain what it is.

Everything else—every absence of animal track, every untouched plaza—is not emptiness waiting for inhabitants.

It’s the sign that the inhabitants never needed anything that moved fast enough to leave prints.
