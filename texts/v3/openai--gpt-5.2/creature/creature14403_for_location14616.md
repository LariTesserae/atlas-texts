What lives here is not one population so much as a *stack of metabolisms*—each one using the others the way a city uses a river, except the river is dry air, mineral dust, and resin held behind skins.

At first, you notice the obvious citizens: the people who made the lamps and rails and factories, who keep the place from relaxing back into collapse. Then you realize the lamps and rails are only the bright edge of it. Most of the inhabitants are quiet, adhesive, patient things that don’t need attention to win. They were winning long before the first cable was strung.

### The primary resident: the built biome itself
The dominant “organism” is the vegetative substrate—the mats, sheaths, tubes, and buttresses that turned stone into something habitable. It doesn’t behave like a forest. It behaves like an *urban material* that happens to be alive.

- **The ground-mat** is a thick, interlocked weave of fibrous growth and mineral stiffeners. It is springy under weight, but only after a delay; it gives like memory foam because it is constantly redistributing internal pressure and repairing microtears with resin.
- **The wall-sheathing** is older, tougher, more ceramic-skinned. It grows in laminations, each layer engineered (or selected) to do a job: insulation, filtration, load sharing, abrasion resistance, electrical isolation.
- **The “root” analogs** don’t hunt for water. They hunt for *edges*: temperature gradients, mineral seams, stress lines in young stone. They colonize cracks the way vines colonize fences, then lock them shut—unless locking them shut would make a future collapse more catastrophic, in which case they spread the stress, encourage controlled spalling, and “teach” the ceiling to shed small flakes rather than whole slabs.

Even when nothing is happening, the biome is doing slow work: closing a rail-gouge, tightening around a pylon, producing a thin wax bloom that makes dust slide off, opening microscopic pores to breathe in aerosols and exhale a different chemistry.

It is, functionally, the city’s **second infrastructure**. And it is inhabited in the same way pavement is inhabited—by everything that can live in the seams.

### The small citizens: the dry-chemistry invertebrate nations
Where there is plant architecture, there are countless things that learned to live off plant architecture without requiring open water.

- **Origami-winged insects** live in the air layers where the lamps are. Their wing-cases fold and lock, so they can glide through still air and then tuck themselves into cracks during pressure changes or maintenance gusts. Their bodies are lacquered—waxy, matte—so they don’t lose internal fluids.
- **Mineral-raspers** (arthropods that look like burrowers until you see their mouthparts) eat rock the way termites eat wood: not for calories, but to free trace elements and to harvest the biofilms that grow on fresh mineral faces. They leave behind the fine powder that becomes everybody else’s raw material.
- **Resin-crackers**—beetlelike scavengers—specialize in sap-threads and bladder-nodes. They don’t drink in the way surface animals drink; they *chip*, *carry*, and *cache* sweetness, breaking resin into manageable pieces and storing them in dry burrows lined with wax.

These creatures are the city’s constant cleaners and recyclers. They keep waste from accumulating in quiet corners; they turn the accidental byproducts of industry—trace aerosols, catalytic dust, polymer crumbs—into edible gradients. They are everywhere, and you rarely see them unless a light hits at the right angle and a thousand tiny surfaces flash.

### The grazers: herds that farm the mat as they walk
Across the broader shelves move the larger animals: pale-bodied, low to the ground, built for endurance and careful traction rather than speed. They travel in herds not because they fear predators in open plains—there *are* no plains—but because the mat itself rewards coordinated movement.

Their hooves aren’t hard keratin. They’re layered pads with a mineral core, designed to compress the living substrate without tearing it. When they feed, they take only new growth: tips, nodules, the tender parts that are meant to be harvested. The mat responds with overgrowth, thickening where it has been grazed, like a lawn that got a haircut and decided to retaliate by becoming denser.

They leave **temporary dimples** that rebound slowly, and those dimples become brief microhabitats: pockets where dust settles, where insects gather, where seedlings of specialized filtration fronds can germinate in the slight shelter of a depression before the ground forgets it.

### The predators: not monsters, but edits in the light
The predators are less a species category and more a behavioral one: anything that learned to use this upholstered quiet.

Some hunt from within the plant structure, moving through hollow struts and layered sheathing as if the city has ducts meant for them. Others are surface runners, silent on the mat, their bodies long and low, their coloration tuned to look like shadow against industrial light. They rarely announce themselves because announcement wastes energy and invites humans.

You don’t often see the kill. What you see is the *effect*: a sudden, synchronized stillness among insects; a herd tightening its spacing; a ribbon of indicator-seedlights blinking out for a moment as something passes between them and the lamp-run.

Predation here is a form of pruning. It keeps grazers moving, keeps scavengers cautious, keeps the biome from being stripped too aggressively in any one district.

### The human layer: crews, not crowds
People live here, but the place doesn’t carry the signature of mass habitation. It feels like a working district because it is: a settlement built around continuous maintenance, cultivation, and extraction.

They tend to fall into roles that sound like trades and behave like ecology.

- **Graftwrights** (or whatever the local term is) are part structural crew, part horticulturalist. They install steel frames and then “teach” the living sheathing how to hold them—introducing starter cultures, stitching growth-mats, applying catalytic dusts that cue hardening. Their work looks like carpentry from a distance and like surgery up close.
- **Dust-farmers** manage the mineral aerosols: the powder lines, the settling chambers, the root-fields that need specific trace mixes. They smell faintly of slate and resin. Their gloves are always chalked.
- **Resin-tappers** harvest the glassy sap-threads without letting them snap back and foul a work zone. They wind them onto spools like cable, then cure or fractionate them into sealants, adhesives, insulators.
- **Quiet mechanics**—vehicle and rail crews—treat the grown composites as something between metal and muscle. They don’t just replace parts. They *coax* them: warming sections, applying nutrient mist, setting braces that the substrate will later swallow and reinforce.

Their clothing is made for dust and abrasion: sealed seams, matte finishes, filters integrated into collars, boots with soles tuned to the mat so they don’t trigger unnecessary repair responses. They carry little bottles, but they aren’t water bottles in the surface sense—more like rations of controlled solvent, electrolyte gel, sometimes actual water in miserly quantities kept in bladder packs and accounted for like fuel.

Housing exists, but it’s not skyline housing. It’s tucked into the safest contours: rooms grown into buttresses, modular pods bolted into living walls, dormitory bands along transport routes. Interiors are dry, cool, and softly lit. The luxury is not warmth; it’s *stability*—a ceiling that doesn’t pop, a wall that doesn’t decide to sprout a new seam overnight.

There are children, in some districts, but not everywhere. When you do see them, they move like locals move: not loudly, not aimlessly. They’ve learned which mats bruise easily, which fronds sting, which seed-things blink harmlessly and which ones are warning organs for a filtration system. They play along the boundaries where human order and plant order negotiate—clambering on grown ribs that are technically load-bearing, chased off gently by a drone or a tired adult who knows exactly how much weight that rib can take before it decides to thicken.

### The machines that count as fauna
Maintenance drones here don’t feel like intrusions; they feel like another class of insect.

Some are small, skittering units that patrol rails and road composites, tasting the surface with sensors, applying micro-patches of catalyst or resin when they detect stress. Others are slow gliders that drift under the lamps like patient moths, mapping spalls and checking air chemistry. In certain districts you see heavier crawlers—industrial “beetles” with articulated arms—moving in deliberate routes, stopping at marked hazards to install braces that the living substrate will later engulf.

The drones are designed with the biome in mind: rounded surfaces that don’t snag, coatings that don’t poison, static control so they don’t attract dust plumes. A few have even been given **symbiotic interfaces**—ports that open like buds, allowing the plant sheathing to connect and exchange data via embedded conductive fibers.

Whether that makes the biome smarter, or just gives it new ways to respond, is an argument locals don’t bother having out loud. They simply work with what responds.

### The hidden majority: microbes and films doing the real chemistry
Under all of this is the smallest layer, the one that makes “dry” life possible at scale.

There are microbial mats and biofilms that live inside waxy tissues, inside resin chambers, inside the ceramic skins of the plants. They process aerosols into usable compounds. They fix nitrogen equivalents from engineered gases. They detoxify heavy metals by binding them into pellets that scavengers can carry away. They are the city’s sewage system, refinery, and compost heap—without the wetness that would normally make those things obvious.

In some places, those microbes make faint heat; in others, they make the coolness feel sharper by consuming the energy of reactions that would otherwise release warmth. The air tastes “clean” not because nothing is happening, but because the happening has been *contained*.

### How all these inhabitants behave together
The striking thing is not any single creature or trade. It’s the way coexistence is built into the physics of the place.

- The plants absorb sound, so **privacy is the default**, and conflict has to be deliberate.
- The lack of open water forces **everything into sealed exchange**, so waste becomes feedstock almost automatically.
- The young, still-setting geology makes permanence expensive, so the culture—human and nonhuman—values **adaptation over monument**.

Life here doesn’t sprawl; it *interfaces*. Every resident is either a connector (binding, filtering, reinforcing) or a user (grazing, harvesting, transporting). Even the predators are part of the maintenance schedule, keeping the living infrastructure from being overconsumed.

So what lives here?

A city that is also a reef, inhabited by people who think like caretakers of a large animal, and by animals and insects and microbes that treat industry as just another climate. The true resident—the one you feel everywhere—is the combined system: a dry, cool, restrained abundance that has learned to make “inside” into a habitat and “construction” into a form of respiration.
