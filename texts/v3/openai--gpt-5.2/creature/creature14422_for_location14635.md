What lives here has learned the plateau’s first trick: if you cannot soften the world, you must become as hard to notice as the world itself.

From a distance, it seems empty because nothing *stands up* in the usual way. No canopies, no furred crowds, no busy wingbeats. Life, where it exists, has taken the plateau’s shapes—blade, seam, lip, underside—and moved into the places the wind cannot peel clean: the back of a column, the roof of a fissure, the thin line where water clings and cannot decide whether it is air or stone.

### The hidden green that isn’t green

The foundation of the place—its actual “vegetation”—is inside the rock.

In the translucent minerals and pale limestones, just beneath the bright skin, there are endolithic colonies: microorganisms that live a few millimeters down, where glare is filtered into something usable and the wind cannot sandblast them away. They don’t make mats, because mats would be a flag; they make *stains* so faint you’d call them a trick of the light. Some are photosynthetic in the thinnest way imaginable, sipping photons through calcite. Others are lithotrophs that treat the plateau’s chemistry as a pantry: iron films, sulfur traces, the clean tang in fissures—food that isn’t food to you, energy coaxed from stone and air.

They grow as slow as terraces form. They repair themselves rather than expand. When the wet side of a pillar never dries, they don’t colonize the surface; they colonize the *boundary layer*, that microscopic zone where water beads and minerals precipitate and dissolve in the same breath. A biofilm can exist there and still look like nothing more than an extra sheen.

If you want to “see” them, you don’t look for color. You look for behavior: a mineral rim that forms slightly differently on one side of a pool; a faintly softened tick-mark where droplets strike; a spot where the stone’s polish is too uniform, as if someone has been licking it for centuries.

### The harvesters of mist and drip

The next tier of life doesn’t graze in fields. It grazes in *lines*.

There are small things—too small to announce themselves, too quiet to give you the comfort of buzzing—that live in the constant wet. Not insects, not in the familiar sense, but patient, armored microfauna that cling beneath overhangs and inside the wind’s blind corners. Some are like translucent limpets with mineral-crusted backs, indistinguishable from the terraces until they move one fraction of a millimeter to reposition under a drip. Others are threadlike, living ropes that bridge hairline gaps and collect water the way a spiderweb collects dew—except their “silk” is a secretion that mineralizes, becoming part thread and part stone.

They feed on what the water brings: airborne salts, trace organics, and—most of all—the invisible biofilms that ride the drip-paths. They scrape with mouthparts made for polishing. They have no need for speed, because the weather never breaks; their whole ecology assumes tomorrow will be the same as today, and it is.

Reproduction, too, is built for a world without seasons. Instead of bursts and swarms, they shed cysts—hard, glassy seeds of life—that lodge in fissures and wait for a particular geometry of dampness. A new seam begins to weep; a new colony appears, not as a bloom, but as a slight increase in slipperiness.

### The fissure-dwellers: life as a rumor

Then there are the animals you almost see.

In the dark cuts between clints, something uses the stone the way a fish uses water: by knowing every current in it. These are long-bodied, quiet creatures that move by pressure and leverage, not by running—pale or dark depending on whether they spend their lives against mineral light or mineral shadow. They leave those tiny wet prints that end at a pool because they come to drink and to cool their skin, then vanish into cracks too narrow for your hand.

They are not numerous; the plateau cannot support abundance. So they are built for scarcity: slow metabolisms, long lifespans, the ability to go still for days in a cold slot where the wind cannot reach. Their senses privilege vibration over sight. In a place where the air is always singing, they listen to the stone instead: to changes in the plateau’s chord when something heavy steps on a column, to the different drumming when a pool level shifts by a finger’s width, to the soft, telling silence where a fissure stops breathing mineral tang.

Predation exists, but it is restrained, almost courteous. A fissure-hunter does not chase; it waits at a choke point, where every path narrows, where every traveler must squeeze and announce itself with friction.

### The stilted walkers on the ledges

The pale, long-legged shape you glimpse on a far ledge is real, and it survives by refusing to compete with anything that wants the same water.

It is built like a compromise between goat and wader, but more spare than either: high hips, narrow chest, limbs that look too thin until you realize they are mostly tendon and shock-absorbing cartilage. The hooves are not hooves but split pads with mineral-hard edges, able to bite slick stone and also to feel it—texture as information. It walks where the rock is loudest and most exposed because that is where predators have the least leverage and where the constant wind strips scent trails away.

It does not graze. It “grazes” the wet side of pillars, licking the mineral skins and the invisible life embedded there, taking in salts it cannot get anywhere else. Its teeth are ridged like files. Its tongue is tough, almost horned, made for abrasion. When it drinks, it does so at places where droplets strike in steady percussion: reliable inflow, predictable chemistry. It will stand with its body angled to the wind so that its own warmth—what little it has—does not melt rime in a way that would betray it.

It leaves almost nothing behind: a few pellets as dry and pale as chalk, crumbling into minerals, rejoining the plateau’s refusal of mess.

### The ones that ride the chord

Overhead, the dark speck that crosses the glare is not a bird as you know birds. It is closer to a living sail.

It spends most of its life without flapping because flapping is waste. Its wings—if you can call them that—are long membranes stiffened by flexible struts, dark to drink the sun’s thin heat, edged with pale mineral dust that acts like a leading-edge coating. It launches from ridgelines and lives inside the engineered steadiness of the wind, reading the plateau’s updrafts the way a navigator reads a map.

It feeds rarely and carefully. Sometimes it drops to a sheltered bowl where mist condenses and small life concentrates, and it takes fissure-things that have come out to drink. Sometimes it simply skims the surface of a pool, not for water but for the faint protein-slick of microorganisms, sweeping its beak or mouthpart through the microlayer like a net.

Its nesting, if it nests, is in places you would not think to look: not on cliffs where storms might tear it away (storms don’t happen here, but the wind is eternal), but inside chimney-like hollows that resonate at a particular pitch. The plateau’s chord matters. A cavity that sings at the right frequency warns of approaching wings through subtle changes in tone; the nest is an instrument as much as a shelter.

### A food web that is more like plumbing

If you try to diagram life here, it won’t look like a forest or a meadow. It will look like the plateau’s own hydrology:

- **Energy enters** as thin light through stone and as chemistry through mineral films and fissure gases.
- **Primary life** hides in pores, under skins, in the boundary where wet meets air.
- **Grazers** scrape, polish, and filter along drip-lines and pool-lips.
- **Small hunters** patrol constrictions and edges, guided by vibration and humidity.
- **Large bodies** appear only where geometry and habit make survival economical: a ledge that always weeps, a corridor that always shields, a ridge that always lifts.

Everything is adapted to constancy, and that adaptation has a cost: when nothing changes, nothing can afford to be loud. So the plateau’s living things do not compete by multiplying; they compete by enduring—by being hard to dislodge, hard to detect, and slow to require.

The place isn’t barren. It is simply hosting a kind of life that has accepted the plateau’s premise: motion without progress, water without rivers, growth without greenness, survival without spectacle. If you stand long enough—long enough for your own heartbeat to stop arguing with the roar—you can begin to notice the difference between stone that is merely wet and stone that is *being used*.
