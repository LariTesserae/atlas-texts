What lives here is not one population but an arrangement—layers of occupants fitted to layers of access, moisture, heat, and permission. If you try to picture “settlers,” the image keeps slipping, because the primary resident is the system itself: an engineered massif that has acquired a metabolism. Everything else—leaf, feather, boot, circuit—learns to live inside that metabolism the way river life learns a current.

### The first inhabitants: films, crusts, and patient eaters
Before anything with a face, there are the quiet residents that treat construction as habitat.

- **Salt-garden microbes** live along the white blooms at joints. They take the mineral efflorescence as both shield and pantry, building thin biofilms that look like damp varnish when the air is right. In the dry hours they harden and go glassy; in the wet minutes they wake and spread a fraction farther along a seam.
- **Mosses and lichens** aren’t just decoration here—they’re infrastructure’s soft failure mode. They seal hairline cracks, hold dust, trap spores, and create the first real “ground” in places where ground was never permitted. Their mats are threaded with fine invertebrate life: mites, springtails, tiny things that turn corrosion powder and leaf litter into usable soil.
- In shaded spillways and the lips of drains, **algae and iron-bacteria** paint the concrete in greens and rust-reds, a living map of where water *actually* goes versus where diagrams claim it goes.

These residents don’t care who owns the surface. They care about time, texture, and the brief, reliable appearances of moisture—condensation hours, irrigation hours, the warm-breath hours after a vent cycles.

### The green residents: plants that read the machine like weather
The vegetation looks lush because it has learned to interpret infrastructure as climate.

- **Vines and climbers** treat cable trays as riverbanks and handrails as treelines. They are opportunists with excellent memory: once one stem finds a condensate drip, it repeats the route until the whole trellis of the plant is biased toward that point, as if the plant has opinions.
- **Terrace shrubs and small trees** live in those bolted hatches as if each hatch were a planter in a hanging city. Their roots don’t seek “depth”; they seek edges—where engineered soil meets membrane, where tiny leaks keep a corner humid, where heat from below prevents frost.
- **Greenhouse escapees**—spare herbs, aggressive ornamentals, nutrient-hungry hybrids—colonize the fringes of vent outflows. You can sometimes tell what a given longhouse is growing without seeing inside: the scent at the vent, the particular weed flourishing beneath it, the insects that cluster there.

In this place, “wild” doesn’t mean unmanaged. It means the management includes tolerances—room for overflow, for feral success, for a vine to be allowed as long as it doesn’t occlude a sensor.

### The small, fast residents: insects, scavengers, and conduit-dwellers
The loud life—buzzing, clicking, chirring—concentrates where the system sweats.

- **Drain-mouth insects** live at the boundary of water capture: midges and gnats that hatch in the thin wet line of a settling basin, beetles that shelter beneath grates still warm from sun, moths that treat light fixtures as moons that never set.
- **Pollinators** follow schedules. They learn the irrigation cycles the way coastal creatures learn tides; when misters tick on, the air fills with wings.
- **Rodents and small marsupial-like mammals** (whatever the local analog is) don’t dig; they *occupy*. They den in insulated pipe jackets, in empty duct runs, in the dead space behind access panels. Their paths are not along the ground but along routes of cover: conduit bundles, shadowed ledges, the underside of catwalks where the wind is quieter.

Their tools are stolen from human practice: insulation fluff for bedding, shredded safety tape as binding, foil scraps pressed into nest linings to hold warmth.

### The air residents: birds that learned architecture as cliff
If the place sounds alive, it’s because the birds discovered that engineered elevation is a gift.

- **Truss-nesters** wedge their nests behind signage panels and inside the hollow angles of steel. Their young learn edges before they learn branches; they fledge onto handrails and practice balance on cables.
- **Scavengers and thermal riders**—broad-winged, deliberate—own the high tiers. They treat exhaust plumes as reliable uplift. Their shadows are part of the daily clock on certain terraces: a slow pass across planters, a brief hush of smaller birds, then the chatter returns.
- **Tank-side hunters** work the cistern rims, taking insects off the algae line and small fish or amphibian analogs if those have been introduced to keep water clean. (And in a place like this, even “introduced” becomes “native” in a generation.)

The birds here don’t merely live *on* the structures. They use them as instruments: echoes for calls, metal resonance for display, airflow patterns to gain lift.

### The kept residents: animals that are here because someone decided they should be
This place has the feeling of a designed ecology, which means some inhabitants exist as policy.

- In certain terraces you find signs of **biocontrol species**: small lizards in warm equipment bays, predatory insects released to keep greenhouse pests from becoming outbreaks, possibly even microbats in vented vaults where moth populations would otherwise clog filters.
- Around reservoirs there may be **engineered aquatic life**—hardy, low-oxygen tolerant cleaners that eat biofilm and larvae, chosen less for beauty than for not dying when chemistry swings.

These residents are “owned by design” more literally than most: stocked, monitored, replaced if they fail. And yet they still become feral in the margins, slipping the leash of intention without ever leaving the system.

### The human residents: those who have permission, and those who live in its shadows
People are here, but not as a simple town on a ridge. They live the way maintenance lives: distributed, scheduled, keyed to access.

- **Maintenance crews** are the most obvious humans, because the place is full of their touch—polished rails, worn grating, the careful rounding of corners by repeated handling. They move in small teams and carry the place’s scent: oil, warm metal, wet compost, disinfectant. Their routes are ritualized: check the pumps, walk the gutters, clear the strainers, inspect the trays, read the salts at joints like fortune-tellers reading tea leaves.
- **Growers and substrate workers** manage the “borrowed ground.” They know which terrace beds are hungry, which engineered clays are compacting, where the pellets are breaking down too fast. Their work is half horticulture, half materials science: keeping a soil that isn’t soil from forgetting how to behave.
- **Logistics handlers** live by conveyors and lift cages. They are the ones who understand the mountain as a vertical warehouse: which ramps can take a load in heat, which switchbacks ice in the thin air, which corridors echo in ways that mask approaching machinery.
- And then there are the **uncredentialed**—the ones without permission—who still manage to exist in an environment that dislikes unaccounted bodies. They sleep in warm voids near exchangers, in forgotten service corridors that stay dry, in the hollow behind decommissioned panels. They learn the blind angles of cameras the way animals learn a predator’s gaze. They trade in what the system sheds: salvage, spoiled produce, replaced filters, lengths of cable, old insulation.

Even among the permitted, housing isn’t a village so much as pockets: modular quarters tucked into terraces, small habs anchored near work zones, rooms inside the bulk of the structure where the temperature is stable and the noise becomes a constant sea.

### The nonhuman workers: drones, sensors, and small machines that count as fauna
Some inhabitants here are clearly built to live here, and over time they’ve begun to feel like a kind of mechanical wildlife.

- **Inspection crawlers** the size of a shoe or a suitcase move along rails and inside ducts. They appear briefly in a hatch opening like a cautious animal at a burrow, scan, retreat.
- **Pollination drones** in greenhouse districts may drift between rows with a soft, purposeful wobble, leaving a faint static smell.
- **Skimmer bots** patrol reservoir surfaces under floating covers, disturbing the water with small, regular wakes.
- **Valve actuators and smart vents** behave like muscles: opening, closing, sighing, responding to pressure and heat. Standing near them long enough, you stop hearing “machines” and start hearing “breathing.”

Some of these devices fail and remain—dead shells that become shelter, perches, nesting platforms. A broken sensor housing becomes a wasp palace; a stalled crawler becomes a warm rock for a lizard.

### The largest resident: a distributed mind that expresses itself as routine
If there is a single “who” that lives here, it is not a person. It’s the operational intelligence—algorithmic, bureaucratic, and embodied in hardware—that treats the entire mountain as one managed organism.

It lives in:

- pump schedules and load balancing,
- the timing of irrigation that becomes a daily tide,
- the way overflow is permitted as spectacle but not as accident,
- the constant quiet accounting of moisture: capture, lift, ration, reclaim.

This intelligence doesn’t need to be self-aware to feel present. It’s present the way weather is present—inescapable, patterned, decisive. People and animals alike orient themselves to it. Birds time their foraging around the misters. Uncredentialed sleepers choose vents that cycle warm at predictable hours. Maintenance workers read anomalies in sound—one pitch wrong in the turbine’s vowel—as if the mountain has spoken.

### How the inhabitants coexist: a pact written in access panels
Life here doesn’t thrive by overthrowing control. It thrives by nesting inside it.

- The system leaks a little, and the ecology makes those leaks into oases.
- The system creates shade, and the shade becomes habitat.
- The system insists on routes, and those routes become animal trails.
- The system punishes the unplanned, and so everything living becomes excellent at being *just planned enough*—or at hiding in the planned voids.

So what lives here is a full community: salt-eaters and moss, vines and engineered groves, insects tuned to irrigation, birds that treat trusses as cliffs, small mammals that treat conduits as burrows; workers who keep the mountain functioning; drifters who survive on its shed abundance; and the vast, impersonal caretaker-mind that moves water uphill and calls it life.

If you stand still long enough on one of those broad terraces, the place reveals its true population count: not individuals, but cycles—breath, drip, hum, wingbeat, footfall—each one occupying a designed surface as if it were the only kind of ground that ever existed.
