What lives here is arranged the way the land is arranged: by height, by shelter, by the invisible lanes of warmth and charge that run through the rock. If you try to list the residents as if they are separate things—plants, insects, beasts—you can, and you’ll be mostly correct. But you keep circling back to the same impression: the oldest inhabitant is the range itself, and everything else is a kind of accessory organ.

### The first resident: the conducting mountain

The stone is not inert here. It behaves like tissue with a slow metabolism.

Mineral seams—metallic, quartz-threaded, pressure-stressed—carry faint currents that leak out where ridges are sharp and air is thin. Those leaks are the quiet blue crawl of discharge, the hum you can’t quite hear, the way frost avoids certain arcs as if the ground is slightly warmer there (though it isn’t, not in any ordinary sense). If you watch long enough, you realize the pulses of crystal fog from the vents are not “weather” in the usual way. They’re closer to exhalations: timed releases of aerosol and microcrystal, keyed to stress shifting inside the massif.

And around those exhalations, life has built its entire calendar.

In any other country, you’d say the mountains create microclimates. Here, the microclimates feel intentional—not planned, not aware, but regulated: pressure, release; discharge, stillness; damp, then drier; a repeating cycle that the rest of the ecology reads the way coastal creatures read tides.

So the range is the primary resident—not as a mind, but as a machine that has learned, through deep time, to be hospitable to certain chemistries.

### The second resident: the skin—lichen, moss, and the “dark lush”

On the exposed ridges the living layer looks like stain: black-green lichen pasted to basalt, pale crusts stippling granite, islands of growth where the wind scours everything else away. These lichens aren’t merely hardy. They’re *wired* into the mineral behavior. Their mats brighten during discharges, not because they like to glow, but because the light is a byproduct of shunting charge through living membranes that can tolerate it.

Below the ridges, where stone folds into lee-side pockets and slot gullies, the world turns plush. Mosses braid into thick ropes and lattices that can bend under frost and rebound. Their trick is not warmth, but water discipline: they hold damp like hoarded currency. When vent pulses send crystal fog downslope, these mats “drink” by opening microscopic pores in synchrony—an entire patch behaving like a single breathing surface.

The shrubs are stranger still: translucent leaves, glassy with captured frost, as if they’ve decided that freezing is not an emergency but a storage method. Some of these leaves crackle faintly when handled—not brittle, but charged—because their tissues incorporate minerals the way lowland plants incorporate sugars. The shrubs feed off seepage and trace metals, and their faint touch-pulses of light are less like a signal flare and more like a reflex: a protective discharge that discourages the wrong kind of crawler.

Under all of it—under moss, under gravel, in the damp that never quite becomes a stream—the fungal networks run like hidden infrastructure. They glow in long, slow waves that match the mountain’s pulse cycles. In places the glow is so regular it resembles timing, like a heartbeat you can see. The fungi are the true “soil” here: without them, there would be rock and frost and nothing to anchor the thin wealth of moisture.

If the mountain is the engine, this living skin is the interface—the part that turns current and mineral and aerosol into usable food.

### The third resident: the small quiet nation—arthropods and microfauna

The loud animals are rare. The common life is tiny, armored, and disciplined.

Beetles with sealed wing-cases patrol the lichen fields. Their shells aren’t just insulation; they’re moisture vaults. They drink fog the way a sponge drinks a spill—slowly, greedily, without movement that would waste heat. They don’t need to fly because the landscape itself moves: gravel rivers can carry them downslope, and they’ve learned to ride those conveyor slopes deliberately, bracing and releasing in rhythm with the shifting stones.

Spiders walk where your instincts say nothing can walk: on powder snow, across hoarfrost skins, over the slick faces of new ice. Their legs spread weight like snowshoes, and their bodies are tuned to vibration. The mountain “speaks” in minute tremors—cornice settling, ice tension, scree shear—and the spiders read those tremors the way web-spinners elsewhere read a caught insect. They can feel a predator’s approach through the ground long before eyes would catch it.

Moths drift through fog banks like bits of soot. Their wings are matte not for camouflage alone but because gloss wastes heat; matte holds a thin boundary layer of air. They drink moisture directly from fog, then rest on stone where the faintest mineral warmth—or the faintest current—keeps them from slipping fully into torpor.

In the warmer seams, where the rock tastes metallic, the insects are most numerous. Not because it’s “warm,” but because the seam zones are predictable. A pulse will come. A fog will settle. The moss will open. The whole place becomes briefly generous, and the small creatures know exactly where to be when it happens.

### The fourth resident: the far-spaced grazers—herds that treat the land like a map of permissions

Then there are the herds: pale-backed, shaggy ungulates that look almost like the landscape learned to walk.

They move widely and sparsely, not because food is scarce (the hidden gullies can be lush), but because the best feeding is not evenly distributed. They are followers of pattern: vent-pulse slopes, seepage terraces, moss basins that stay damp longest. Their wide, soft hooves are less about snow than about *not damaging the moss machinery*—a broad pressure that compresses without tearing, leaving behind those faint corridors where frost scrapes away and growth lies temporarily flattened.

They navigate by more than sight. You can watch them stop at nothing—no cliff, no crevasse, no scent-mark—and refuse to cross. The boundary is electrical, a gradient in the ground. The herd reads it as clearly as a fence. Sometimes they detour for miles to avoid a zone of wrong charge, as if stepping into it would scramble something inside them.

When they rest, they do so in places that look accidental—lee-side bowls, terraces behind boulders—but if you track enough of those resting sites, you see the rule: always near a seam that conducts, always where a fog pulse will come, always where the ground is slightly “alive.” Their bodies steam faintly not with heat, but with moisture management: exhalation controlled, water conserved, warmth shared in close huddles during the deepest dim.

The herds are not just consumers. They are movers of the system—pressing moss, spreading spores, carrying insect eggs in their fur, transporting the mountain’s living skin from pocket to pocket.

### The fifth resident: the low-slung hunters—predation as pressure, not noise

The predators are built to keep the hush intact.

Long-bodied, close to the ground, with coats that shed ice like oil sheds water, they don’t advertise themselves with voice. Sound is expensive here: thin air doesn’t carry it well, and snow keeps a perfect record. So they communicate in contact—taps through stone, tremors through crust, pauses timed to the herd’s pauses. Their whiskers and footpads are exquisitely sensitive to vibration, and they often hunt by *feeling* migration corridors rather than following scent.

They are patient in a way that seems supernatural until you remember what they live inside: a landscape where processes take hours to do what would take seconds elsewhere, where a gravel river moves continuously but never hurries. The predators match that tempo. They don’t chase so much as position themselves where the system will bring prey: at the edge of a scree conveyor, at a saddle where fog thickens and visibility collapses, beside a seam boundary the herd will avoid until it must pass around it.

When they kill, it’s quick—not as mercy, but as efficiency. Blood is heat, and heat is loss. Even predation here has learned to be economical.

### The sixth resident: the watchers above—birds that borrow the aurora

You might not notice the birds at first because they don’t sing, and because the sky swallows light. But there are aerial lives: dark, broad-winged shapes that ride laminar wind sheets as if the air were solid.

They nest in cliff hollows warmed by conducting seams, lining cracks with moss braids stolen from gullies. Their eyes are adapted not to brightness but to *contrast*—to detecting the slight glow of fungal veins, the faint phosphor of damp moss, the reflective wink of insect shell. Some of them hunt by following bioluminescent “roads” the way coastal birds follow surf lines.

When auroral discharge crawls along a ridge, these birds sometimes spiral near it—not into it, never into it, but close enough that their feathers prickle and lift. The edge of that charge seems to flush insects out of hiding. The birds take advantage without ever making the mistake of thinking the mountain is tame.

### And beneath all those: the hidden majority—microbes, cold algae, and the chemistry that makes food possible

The strangest abundance is invisible.

In the thin films where moisture moves uphill, microbial mats cling to rock faces. They are not simply surviving; they are exploiting a physics that feels slightly shifted. They use mineral gradients the way other life uses sunlight. In cold aerosols settling from vents, there are algae-like cells that can remain dormant as crystals for long periods, then revive briefly when trapped in damp moss.

In those brief revivals, they fix what can be fixed—carbon, nitrogen, trace elements—building the foundation the rest of the food web stands on. The place looks starved of inputs, but the mountain’s currents are an input. The discharges are an input. The vent pulses are an input. The ecology is not solar-powered so much as *geoelectric-powered*, supplemented by what little light the sky permits.

### What the place “is,” when you add it up

If you insist on a single answer to who lives here, the most accurate one is not any animal or plant.

A functioning system lives here: a high, dark, conductive range that acts like a slow engine; a living skin that turns pulse and mineral into growth; a silent nation of small arthropods that keeps matter moving; far-spaced herds that read invisible boundaries; and patient hunters that speak in pressure rather than sound.

Nothing in it seems to have been built for you to notice. Even the bioluminescence is not for display. It’s signage for the inhabitants—moisture here, charge there, pulse coming, danger nearby. You are not a visitor so much as a brief variable introduced into a stable equation.

The residents respond accordingly: not with alarm, not with curiosity, but with minor adjustments. A moth folds its wings tighter. A spider shifts one foot to feel your vibration more clearly. Moss pores close for a beat, then reopen on schedule. Far away, a herd changes its line by a few degrees to avoid the electrical shadow your body makes on the snow.

And the mountains keep working, incorporating you the way they incorporate everything: as a temporary disturbance in airflow, a small warmth on stone, a trace of salt left behind—then, steadily, into the long, quiet machinery of dark abundance.
