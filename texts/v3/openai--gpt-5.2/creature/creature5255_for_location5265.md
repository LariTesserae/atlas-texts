What lives there is not one species so much as a tenancy agreement between soft things and hard things—an occupancy so complete the basin no longer recognizes the concept of “vacant.” If you insist on naming inhabitants, you end up listing processes.

### The first resident: the Mat (a floor that eats)
The most dominant living thing is the underfoot itself: the layered root-felt, peat-sponge, rhizome-braid that has become both ground and gut.

It isn’t just “roots.” It’s an interlocked consortium—plant rhizomes, moss pads, hair-fine feeder threads, and the fungal mesh that stitches them into load-bearing fabric. The mat behaves like an organism the way coral behaves like an organism: countless bodies, one architecture.

- **It feeds downward and upward at once.** Downward into rot, iron, old concrete dust, and the slow drip of whatever minerals leach from the industrial bones. Upward into light and humidity, constantly adding new skin.
- **It’s patient, but it is not passive.** Where something presses—rail, plinth, your boot—it thickens around the pressure points, braiding tighter, making ribs. Where water lingers too long, it lofts itself with trapped gas, raising little hummocks. Where something cracks, it infiltrates and then “heals” the crack with living filler.

If you could see it cut through like a cake, you’d notice the layers are not merely history—they’re strategy. Old, sour, oxygen-poor strata for storage and slow chemistry; newer airy threads above for rapid transport; a top skin that sheds water just enough to keep the whole from liquefying. The mat is the basin’s claim: a statement that even “waterlogged” can be structured, and even “ruin” can be digested.

### The second resident: the Greenwrights (plants that build with what they find)
The plants here don’t just grow *on* things. They grow *with* things, and eventually *as* things.

You can feel the basin’s peculiar intelligence—non-sentient, but adept—in the way stems take advantage of geometry:

- **Rung-vines and rail-wraps** that spiral at consistent pitch, not because they’re measuring, but because the object’s shape offers the easiest repeated purchase. Given enough seasons, “easy purchase” becomes “standard form.”
- **Plate-leaf curtains** that hang like signage, translucent and tough, arranged by airflow and drip-lines. They make shade where shade keeps moisture; they open gaps where light is most reliable. The place is full of these micro-decisions, as if the vegetation is constantly drafting blueprints in response to condensation.
- **Arch-wood lattices**—not trees, not shrubs, but bundled struts grown into stress-bearing curves. They’re what you get when a plant lineage spends generations learning that “up” doesn’t matter as much as “across,” and that collapse is as useful a condition as standing.

Some of these plants have become specialists in industrial adjacency. Their tissues run iron-brown, not merely stained but structurally reinforced with minerals. Their sap is slightly conductive—salty, metallic-tasting if it ever touched your lips—suited to a world where every surface sweats and every object is a damp battery terminal waiting to happen.

They don’t need to be magical to seem uncanny. They only need time, humidity, and the constant presence of shapes that keep offering the same lessons.

### The third resident: the Mortar-Folk (fungi and crusts that replace material)
In the pump-house shadows and along concrete edges, the true engineers are quiet and pale.

Fungus plates climb walls like layered shelves, each one a slow mouth. Lichens and bacterial films fur metal in colors that look like corrosion until you realize the “rust” has veins and a direction of travel. Chalky, living crusts colonize cracks and behave like breathing plaster: soft when wet to insinuate themselves deeper, hard when dry to hold the seam.

They do two kinds of work:

1. **They convert ruin into soil.** Paint, rubber, rotting wood, old insulation: nothing is too synthetic to be at least *occupied*, and occupation becomes degradation becomes incorporation.
2. **They make new surfaces.** Not just cover—*surface.* They create a skin that other roots can grip, that water can sheet over, that spores can stick to. In a basin that has forgotten emptiness, “bare” is an unstable state that gets corrected.

This is part of why the place feels established rather than merely overgrown. The decomposers aren’t trailing behind the plants; they’re in front of them, manufacturing footholds.

### The fourth resident: the Conductors (life that uses electricity the way swamp-life uses sunlight)
On certain mornings the basin feels faintly electrified—not in a dramatic storm sense, but in the small, domestic way an old appliance hums even when it’s “off.”

Here, the inhabitants include things that live on gradients: charge differences, redox chemistry, the tiny opportunities created wherever wet metal meets oxygen-poor muck.

- **Biofilms on copper and steel**—slick, nearly invisible layers that thrive where ions migrate. They turn exposed wire into habitat and make corroding joints into feeding grounds.
- **Sap-conducting vines** that don’t generate power so much as tolerate it, exploit it, and distribute it. A cable becomes a trellis; the trellis becomes a circuit; the circuit becomes a weather system inside the leaves.
- **Static-lick mosses** (you’d call them that if you stayed long enough) that seem to prefer edges where discharges happen: between a vent throat and a wet leaf, between a panel and the air. They grow in little halos around likely contact points, as if enjoying the tiniest sparks.

If there is still a grid remnant, if there are still capacitors quietly holding charge, the basin has incorporated them the way a reef incorporates a shipwreck: not as an anomaly, but as an additional kind of rock.

### The fifth resident: the Breathers (machines that never stopped, and the ecologies inside them)
The pumps and vents are not just leftovers; they are organs that the rest of the basin has learned to rely on.

Inside the vent throats and ductwork live miniature climates: warm, wet, pressurized, periodically refreshed. Root tips invade, then thicken, then die back, then invade again, shaping internal airflow the way nasal turbinates shape breath. Moss turns mesh into felt. Insects—few, but persistent—nest in the drier pockets and ride the pressure changes like tides.

The pump’s thump is a kind of dawn bell for this hidden community. When it cycles, water levels adjust by fractions, oxygen profiles shift, and whole microbial neighborhoods either bloom or starve. That steady “thoom… thoom…” is not background noise; it’s a schedule.

Whether the machine-work is powered by decay gas, a stubborn connection to distant infrastructure, or something as simple as siphon pressure and old design, it has become part of the ecosystem’s calendar. Life here doesn’t worship the machines. It uses them.

### The sixth resident: the Small Witnesses (animals that survive by being unimportant)
Fauna is scarce in the way a well-sealed greenhouse is scarce: there is no obvious room for exuberant animal chaos. But animals are here, living like footnotes.

- **Amphibians**: mud-colored, algae-slick, often motionless on metal because metal stays fractionally warmer or cooler than the mat depending on the hour. They eat what little crawls, and they hide where the dripping is loudest.
- **Snails, leeches, and threadlike larvae** in the slow water lenses that appear under pressure. They don’t need open ponds; the basin provides pockets.
- **Night-movers** that make the narrow paths: something light enough not to break the mat, hungry enough to return on schedule. You might never see it, but you’ll see the clipped leaves—clean bites, consistent height—like a gardener with one rule and endless patience.

Birds come like mistakes: one sharp call, one sudden beat of wings, and then the green closes again. Insects exist mainly as maintenance crews—detritivores and fungus-gnawers—rather than as a bright, noisy layer of life.

Animals don’t run the basin because the basin doesn’t offer them dominance. It offers them niches that open and close with humidity.

### The last resident: the Basin Itself (a hybrid that behaves like a single, slow machine)
If you stay long enough, you stop thinking of the inhabitants as separate.

The mat, the vines, the fungal mortar, the conductive films, the breathing ducts, the slow-maintaining pumps—together they function like a body whose organs were built from two ancestries: botanical and industrial.

- The **plants** provide structure, transport, and relentless claiming.
- The **fungi and films** provide conversion, binding, and surface-making.
- The **machines** provide rhythm and constraint—keeping water in the range where the mat can stay a floor rather than a lake.
- The **electricity**—real, residual, or chemically simulated—provides a subtle nervousness: a way for metal and sap and wet air to interact beyond simple rot.

So what lives there?

A low place that has forgotten how to be empty is inhabited by occupancy itself: by a colony-world that builds floors out of roots, repairs walls with fungus, routes charge through vine, and keeps time by a pump that refuses to die. Everything else—every salamander, every beetle, every rare bird—is permitted as long as it doesn’t demand the basin make room.

The basin does not make room. It makes *more basin.*
