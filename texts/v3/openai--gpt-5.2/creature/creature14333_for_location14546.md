The first and most complete resident is the district itself—not as scenery, but as an organism that has learned to wear “infrastructure” the way other organisms wear bone.

It does not live in the way a tree lives, rooted and singular, and it does not live in the way a city lives, crowded and externalized. It lives the way a nervous system lives: distributed, biased toward circulation, full of thresholds and permissions. The braided walls and load-bearing ropes of stem are not merely plants that happened to grow into useful forms; they are tissue recruited into purpose. The metal housings half-swallowed by cambium are not ruins; they are implants. The seams in the floor are not cracks; they are joints.

### The district as a body: a civic organism with appetite and etiquette
If you stand still long enough, you can feel the place deciding what you are.

Not consciously, not with a face behind the dark—more like an immune response that can also become hospitality. It tests with humidity first, then with scent. It edits your footfalls by subtly changing the spring of a frond under you. It offers light only in the amounts that keep you oriented but not empowered. The air “flavor” shift at the lichen boundary is not atmosphere so much as boundary-fluid: a change in what the district is willing to exchange.

The district eats in slow ways.

- It feeds on minerals sweating out of its new-made stone, pulling trace metals upward the way mangroves pull salt.
- It feeds on work: turbine-turn and pressure differential and heat rejection. Waste heat becomes a warm breath routed into voids where it can lift spores. Vibration becomes a signal that opens valves made of living membrane.
- It feeds on attention in the smallest sense: intention seems to have weight here, and the district has evolved to treat intention as a form of traffic.

That is why thrown objects “don’t get the shortcut” that walking bodies do. Intention is a kind of passport; inert matter is just matter.

If it has a brain, it is not centralized. Its thinking is performed by delays, by consensuses among tissues: one bridge refusing to fold until another has thickened; one corridor staying fog-dense until the atrium has finished exchanging air. Its “decisions” feel like negotiated outcomes rather than commands.

And because it is alive, it has moods—not emotional, but metabolic: nights when the ducts sigh more often, days when everything feels tightened, as if the whole district is bracing against an incoming weather cycle that will only happen under one bay.

### The keepers you don’t see: the mycelial civil service
Under the leaf-skins, under the resin-laminated platforms, in the insulation of ducts and the pale sweating stone, there is a second population that is less visible and arguably more in control: fungal networks engineered (or self-engineered) into something like a civil service.

They are not merely decomposers. They are switchboards.

You see their work as repeating stamped patterns in the laminated “plywood,” as if a manufacturing process got translated into growth. You find their signature in the way a pump will not start until the cambium around the switch agrees: the fungus is the arbitration layer, the part of the district that recognizes demand as legitimate or not. It is not moral. It is procedural. It has rules.

In places where time stutters—where a droplet hangs too long or a wingbeat edits itself—there is often a fine, almost glassy fuzz along the corners of the corridor, a mycelial edging like caulk. The fungus seems to be what seals reality seams, or at least what takes advantage of them. It grows where causality is thin and makes that thinness useful: a delayed droplet becomes a timed release; a compressed distance becomes a maintenance corridor that only “exists” for bodies carrying the right vibration profile.

If you could listen in the right frequency, the mycelium would not sound like speech. It would sound like schedules.

### The animals: tenants, commuters, and tools
The fauna are not just inhabitants; they are integrated. Many of them behave like organs, or like mobile sensors that the district has learned to cultivate.

**Seam-amphibians** (the pale slick ones) travel the open joints in the floor the way blood cells travel capillaries. They are drawn to mineral sweat and condensation and seem to “clean” the seams of films that would otherwise change the friction—an invisible maintenance task. When they cluster, the district often reconfigures soon after, as if their presence is a diagnostic reading: this joint needs attention.

Their eyes are poor; their skin does most of the seeing. They navigate by gradients of cold sap and oxidized metal, pressing their bellies to surfaces the way someone reads braille.

**Long-bodied click-mammals** with oversized ears are everywhere and nowhere. Their clicks never quite become audible to you because they keep their echolocation out of the air and in the structure; they ping through fronds and cambium the way a stethoscope listens through bone. When they run, the “streets” answer them with minute changes in stiffness, returning a map. They look like predators at a glance—sleek, efficient—but most of them are not hunting. They are patrolling, in the way mice patrol walls: testing edges, marking passages, confirming that the district’s agreed-upon boundaries are still agreed.

When they do hunt, they do it with a strange courtesy: they prefer prey that has already been “de-registered” by the district—an insect whose wing-hush has become irregular, a moth that has lost the pattern the lamps recognize.

**Hand-sized moths** are the district’s pollinators and, in a way, its couriers. Their warning-sign wing patterns are not just intimidation; they are labels. Certain flowers open only to certain patterns, and certain lamp-organs open only when those patterns pass close. In some atriums you can watch a moth drift past a sealed slit in a wall and see the slit brighten briefly, as if reading a badge.

When the district “updates” a bridge alignment, moths often appear first, as if they are sent to test the new geometry: not by intention, but by attraction to fresh resin scents and newly exposed sap.

**Fog-fish**—the black sleek creature that swims in waterless canals—are perhaps the strangest tenants because they treat fog as medium. They move by flexing their bodies in slow waves and condensing the fog ahead of them, creating a transient density gradient they can push against. They are not supernatural so much as exquisitely adapted to a place where gas behaves more like fluid.

They hunt in fog by reading heat: not warmth exactly, but the tiny absence-of-cool that living bodies create. When one passes near you, your skin feels the air thicken, and the sound changes, as if the room briefly grew smaller.

Somewhere in the district, these fog-fish breed in pockets where vents exhale warm air into cold shafts, creating permanent fog-wells—nurseries that are also heat exchangers.

**Birdlike nesters** behind turbine casings are the closest thing to “ordinary” wildlife, until you notice what they line their nests with: flakes of patina, strands of mycelium, fibers peeled from vine-cables. Their hatchlings imprint not on parents alone but on vibration signatures. They learn the district’s machine rhythms the way other birds learn songs. A nest that fails to match the turbine’s patient turning often fails entirely; the adults abandon it, as if the district’s heartbeat is part of the reproductive contract.

**Ant colonies** farming fungal mats in duct insulation are not merely opportunists. They are subcontractors. Their fungal gardens are chemically tuned: in one corridor the mats produce a resin that seals leaks; in another they produce a mild anesthetic scent that calms larger animals passing through, reducing panic-movements that would stress load-bearing stems. The ants move like disciplined workers because the work is real.

Predators exist, but none of them dominate. The district does not reward dominance; it rewards fit.

The most successful hunters are those that can read permissions: those that know when a lamp will open, when a seam will stay open long enough, when distance will compress for a pursuing body. Their stealth is not only silence. It is compliance.

### The near-invisible large resident: the one that uses the atriums
There is, finally, a presence that is not often seen and yet feels implied by the cathedral voids: something that requires volume but not clutter, space that is reserved “on purpose.”

You might not call it a creature at first because you rarely see it as a whole. You see effects:

- a slow, deliberate drift of shadows out of sync, not random lag but coordinated delay, like a hand moving through water;
- a sudden alignment of prey-things that is too unanimous to be mere fear;
- a brief opening of multiple lamp-organs in sequence, not in response to footfalls but as if tracing a path through the dark.

When it moves through an atrium, condensation changes tempo. Drips synchronize, then fall out of sync again. The creak of fibers under load becomes more musical, as if the district is tightening itself to provide a route.

If you ever did see it clearly, it might look like a ribbon of articulated darkness, not because it is made of shadow but because it is coated in a surface that holds light the way some walls do—absorbing brightness and releasing it only as memory. Its body would not be massive; the voids are not for its size so much as for its *maneuvering*. It turns like a thought.

It hunts rarely, and when it does, it does not chase. It edits.

A moth will stutter mid-wingbeat—one second missing—and then drop into a seam as if the seam was always there. A click-mammal will stop, not frozen, but “aligned,” and only afterward will you realize that the district has gently redirected it: a bridge has shifted, a corridor has fogged, a scent has been released.

The large resident’s primary prey may not even be flesh. It may feed on irregularities: on things that do not belong in the schedule. It feels less like a monster and more like an enforcement mechanism that evolved into an animal—or an animal recruited into enforcement.

### The absent people: evidence of a species that built, then left the lights on
There are almost no people here, but the place is not post-human in the sense of ruins. It is post-occupancy.

The stamped patterns on platforms, the standardized turbine housings, the ductwork: these are traces of minds that planned with straight edges. They built a district meant to be populated, serviced, run. Then something happened—not necessarily disaster, possibly completion, possibly evacuation, possibly a decision that the district could do better without daily-life scatter.

If any of those builders remain, they are not residents in the ordinary sense. They are visitors who know how to ask.

Very occasionally, you might encounter a figure in the periphery—someone wrapped in matte fabric that refuses the little light available, wearing something on their feet that changes their footfall signature. They do not walk like trespassers. They walk like people entering a laboratory that is also a temple: slow, with deliberate pauses to let the district “read” them.

They carry little, because clutter is not rewarded here. They do not force doors. They wait for openings the way you wait for an elevator: with patience, with an understanding that the system has priority.

If you were to speak to one, you might learn they are not caretakers in the sentimental sense. They are auditors, gardeners of process—checking whether the district is still iterating toward the intended state, whatever that state is. Their tools are scents and vibrations and small offerings of metal salts. Their authority is not dominance but compatibility.

And they are rare enough that the fauna does not organize around them. The animals do not flee or approach; they simply incorporate them as another moving part.

### How it all fits together: a habitat that behaves like governance
What lives here is a layered society of functions:

- a civic plant-body that grows architecture and enforces boundaries as physiology,
- a fungal bureaucracy that turns need into permission and permission into action,
- animals as maintenance crews, pollinators, sensors, and quiet predators,
- at least one large, void-using presence that makes the emptiness feel *occupied* without being crowded,
- and a near-absence of people—enough to leave the taste of intention in the rules, not enough to fill the volume.

The district is not waiting for residents the way an empty apartment waits. It is waiting the way a powered machine waits: already doing its job, already adjusting, already alive—its tenants moving through seams and fog and lamp-slits according to laws that are not written anywhere but are enforced everywhere.

Stand on the plaza long enough and you realize: this is not an abandoned place.

It is a place that has achieved a kind of self-sufficiency, and now admits others only when admitting them improves the circulation.
