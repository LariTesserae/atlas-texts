What lives here is not a cast of creatures so much as a set of occupations the water allows: things that anchor, things that sheet over surfaces, things that turn stone’s slow chemistry into food, things that wait.

At first you call it “vegetation” because that’s the closest word you have for anything lush and rooted, anything that turns a place into a garden. But the longer you look, the more the garden resolves into guilds—different kinds of living that have learned the old builders’ geometry the way a forest learns a mountainside.

### The draped things: ribbon-fronds and cloth-mats
Along the causeways and the edges of channels, where the current still remembers its routes, long fronds fasten themselves in the seams between paving stones. They don’t rise like kelp in sunlit water—nothing here grows with that kind of confidence. Instead they grow as if they’re making do with a ration: broad blades, thin in profile, turned to face whatever weak gray sifts down from above.

They have holdfasts built for masonry. Not the clasping grip of a root in soil, but a patient intrusion: pad-like growths that creep into mortar gaps, then swell until they lock. Over decades they can pry a joint wider, not by force but by persistence—wet expansion, mineral softening, repeated cycles of pressure that are as seasonal here as leaf-fall is elsewhere.

In the lee of buttresses, where the water goes heavy and still, the fronds change character. They become cloth: thick mats that drape down walls and over stair mouths. They aren’t eaten, so they don’t learn to be tough in the way plants learn under teeth. Their defense is simply their setting—cold, dim, and unbothered. They can afford to be soft. When they die, they don’t vanish; they collapse into dark felt and are slowly stitched into the silt, seam by seam, until the architecture has a second skin made of old growth.

### The plume-weeds: filters that don’t need mouths
At the mouths of culverts and the rims of half-buried arches, you see the feathered ones: dense plumes that open into the flow like hands held out. They look like plants because they branch and they sway, but they behave like passive machines.

Their living is interception. The world has very little moving through it—no swimming bodies, no storms of feeding—so what nourishment exists arrives as a thin snowfall: mineral dust, dissolved metal, stray organics leached from timber districts, the faint, slow “breath” of different water masses meeting. The plume-weeds are built to comb that thinness.

Their feathery structure is a compromise with scarcity: maximize surface area, minimize the cost of building it. When the current strengthens along the old canal lines, the plumes lean and tighten, becoming narrow to avoid tearing. When the flow slackens, they open again, more like nets than leaves, relying on time instead of abundance.

### The skin of the place: films, varnishes, and living grout
Most of the biomass here isn’t in the obvious fronds. It’s on the stone itself.

Every block face is slick with a fine, living film—thin as breath, dark as oil. In good light it shows as a sheen that changes when you shift your view, a luster that makes carvings (what’s left of them) look softened, as though the stone is always slightly wet even when it has been wet forever.

This film is where the real work happens. It is made of microorganisms that treat the built world the way a reef treats a coastline: as both habitat and food source. They take energy where they can find it:

- from the tiny redox gradients between one kind of stone and the next,
- from iron and sulfur compounds seeping out of old mortar,
- from the chemical difference between water that has lingered in sealed chambers and water that has been combed by open channels,
- from the faint trace of light in the uppermost districts, when there is any.

Over centuries they lay down varnishes—mineralized coatings that look like patina but are alive, layered, and self-maintaining. In places the “grout” between bricks is no longer the builders’ mortar at all, but living fill: microbial mats that trap silt, bind it with secreted polymers, and slowly turn a crack into a sealed seam. The architecture does not heal, exactly, but it gets edited. Joints become softened, then re-packed. Edges blur into biological cement.

If you had to name a dominant inhabitant, it would be this: the quiet biofilm civilization, everywhere and nowhere, making the entire continent of construction into a slow-reacting organ.

### The timber districts: black wood, swollen with other lives
Where whole neighborhoods are interlocked timbers, the life changes again. Wood in cold water becomes a different kind of stone—swollen, dark, astonishingly persistent. That persistence is a niche.

Here the inhabitants are not the bright, waving kinds but the infiltrators: organisms that can live inside a beam, threading through grain and old peg-holes, turning lignin and whatever organics remain into a long, slow meal. They don’t rot the wood into collapse the way you expect decay to behave in air. They mine it. They hollow it by degrees, leaving a structural honeycomb that can still hold because water supports weight and because nothing is shaking it with urgent movement.

Occasionally you find a plank that has been colonized into something like a reef of its own: raised ridges where inner growth has pressed outward, tiny blistered nodules that open only when the pressure difference is right, releasing a faint cloud of spores or cells that drifts like smoke and then, slowly, settles.

### The “light gardens” and the “dark gardens”
Because light is not a given but a rumor, the vegetation sorts itself by what it can afford.

In the higher, more exposed fields—where the bruise-gray overhead is strongest—the fronds are broader and more olive than black. Growth is still slow, but it has a direction: up, out, into whatever photons arrive. These are the light gardens, and they look almost normal at a glance, if you ignore their somber color and their unchewed perfection.

Deeper, or in districts shadowed by embankments that run for days, the palette shifts toward wine and soot. Here the plants and films lean harder into chemistry. They gather around stone types that leach the right minerals. They thicken at boundaries—where basalt meets limestone, where old fired brick meets later stone—because boundaries create gradients, and gradients are food.

The dark gardens don’t wave as much. They tend to form low, deliberate growth: crusts, cushions, layered sheets that creep rather than reach. In places they build terraces over terraces, mirroring the builders’ own logic—life learning the same low, broad strategy the civilization once used against water.

### Reproduction without hurry
Nothing here needs to be fast to survive, because nothing is chasing it and nothing is racing it for a mouthful. So the inhabitants reproduce in ways that fit the basin’s patience.

Some spread by fragmentation: a strip of mat tears free in a stronger-than-usual current, drifts for a day or a month, and eventually snags in a crack, where it begins again. Some release spores or seed-like cysts only when pressure shifts—when an old chamber finally vents, when a buried canal opens a new route for water. The cues are geological rather than biological: a collapse thud, a slow change in flow, the faint warming of a water mass that has traveled from far away.

The result is that the garden’s “seasons” are not visible as color and bloom but as texture and thickness. A decade might be a growth spurt. A century might be a dieback.

### The inhabitants you notice by their absence
The most striking “species” here is the missing one: anything with a nervous system, anything that would turn this vegetation into food, anything that would kick silt up in panic or courtship or hunger.

Because there are no grazers, there are no short lawns, no cropped edges, no defensive spines perfected by predation. Because there are no scavengers, dead matter doesn’t get redistributed by appetite. It stays where it falls and becomes sedimentary record. The architecture keeps its coverings. The coverings keep their dead.

Over very long time, this makes the basin feel inhabited in a peculiar way: full of life, but not full of events. The living things here do not *happen* so much as *accumulate*. They occupy every crack and face not as individuals but as continuity.

### If the place has a “keeper,” it is the slow composite
Standing over those paving fields, you might look for a single presence—some enduring guardian, some remnant intelligence. What you find instead is a composite inhabitant made of countless quiet metabolisms: fronds that comb the flow, films that turn stone’s chemistry into energy, timber-miners that make black beams into long-term habitat, mats that sew silt into cracks until the built world becomes a sealed, softened continent.

In a world where water is the medium everything belongs to, the dominant life is the kind that can belong without moving—life that makes use of surfaces, gradients, and time. It lives like the architecture lives now: low, broad, negotiated with pressure, expecting to last.
