Life here begins as a mistake the rock makes when it cools.

In the lee of a fin where wind can’t quite scour clean, in the hairline crack that spends a few minutes each dawn colder than the stone around it, in the place where two minerals meet and don’t agree about how to hold their electrons—there is a thinness that isn’t emptiness. A film like tarnish, a bruise of chemistry. If you press your glove to it you don’t feel softness, you feel *grain*, a faint drag as if the surface is slightly more reluctant than bare basalt to let you go.

That reluctance is metabolism.

### The first residents: films, dust, and charge

The oldest inhabitants are not rooted and they don’t drink. They don’t even really *grow* the way a wet-world organism does. They colonize.

- **Gradient-films** spread where day and night pull the temperature back and forth like a slow bellows. Their bodies are layered reactions: one micrometric stratum good at stealing energy from a warm surface, another better at holding ions when the cold arrives. They creep a few millimeters in a season, then sit for a year rearranging themselves.
- **Spore-dust analogs** are not seeds so much as strategies. They ride the wind as dead-looking motes until they land on a conductive patch, or a seam where salts naturally plate out, and then they “wake” by becoming a circuit. If you watch the right cliff at the right hour you can see the proof: a faint crawling of pinprick discharges, like someone dragging wool over stone in the dark.

They don’t make forests. They make *maps*: patchworks on sheltered faces, along the undersides of overhangs, under the lips of glass plateaus where microcracks trap a night’s worth of cold. Their “color” is not for display; it’s an accident of oxidation states and trace metals. Copper-bloom green where a conductive edge benefits them. Rust-brown where iron is plentiful. A violet sheen where a crystalline substrate returns just enough energy to make being alive worthwhile.

Everything above them—every animal, every city-scavenger, every human habit—depends on those slow stains of life.

### The stone-eaters: grazers that refine the ground

The long-legged scrapers you meet at the city’s edge are built the way the landscape is built: to endure abrasion rather than avoid it.

They have the stance of something that expects the ground to fight back. Their legs are jointed in more places than yours, each joint wrapped in tough, flexible sheaths that keep dust out, and their feet are wide pads with radial ribs that deform around shards without slicing. They move quietly not because they are stealthy but because they have learned that kicking up grit costs energy they cannot afford.

Their heads are blunt, almost tool-like. Where a wet-world grazer has lips and tongue, these have **grinding plates** that can press flush to stone. The “teeth” are crystalline: not enamel but something closer to the rock itself, self-sharpening by fracture rather than wear. When they feed, they don’t nibble. They *work*.

They choose sun-warmed faces where the films are most active—where the day’s heat has made ions mobile, where the night’s cold has plated salts into fragile blooms. Their mouths rasp with a low, dry sound like sanding. What they take in is mostly mineral, and their guts are specialized not for digestion but for **sorting**: magnetic tissues that pull out useful metals, bacterial analogs that strip energy-rich compounds, glands that precipitate unwanted salts into pellets.

You can tell where a herd has been by the neatness of it. They leave behind surfaces scraped clean to fresh rock, and their droppings are not dung but *tailings*: pale pellets and gritty scatters, sometimes glittering with rejected crystal. In a world without soil, these tailings matter. Smaller creatures follow them like gleaners follow plows.

There are herds that live entirely wild on the plateaus, timing their movements to the slow migration of dust dunes that bury and uncover feeding faces over years. Closer to the city, some are tolerated or even loosely managed—not domesticated in the tender sense, but *scheduled*. People know which escarpment ledges are safe to share, which weeks the herds will come, which condenser runs should be throttled because too much trace volatile in the air makes the films taste “wrong” and the animals move on.

### The gliders: membranes that drink height

Above the scrapers are the creatures that make the vertical space feel occupied.

They are not birds, but the air has taught them some of the same lessons. They are broad, spare-bodied things with **flexible membranes** stretched between elongated supports. The supports are not bone. They are mineral rods—light, strong, and faintly piezoelectric—grown internally the way a shell grows, fed by whatever trace elements their prey provides.

When they launch, it isn’t with flapping so much as with a decision: a step off a ledge into an updraft, a quick tensioning of membrane that stiffens into an airfoil, and then the body settles into the physics like a boat into a current. At a distance they look like drifting scraps of dark cloth; close enough, you see the membranes have a grain, a subtle pattern of reinforcing veins. They bank with a faint rustle and sometimes, if you’re standing on a sky-bridge, you feel a brief tickle in your teeth as their mineral spars flex and shed static.

They hunt in a way that suits a world with little sound. They don’t rely on cries or chatter. They read the air: the thermal pulse rising off a sunlit wall, the eddy behind a needle spire, the scent-thin trace of warm machine breath that means scavengers will be active.

Their prey is not meat-rich. They take smaller gliders, conduit-skitterers, the young of scrapers when one stumbles into a narrow place it can’t pivot out of. They are economical hunters, and they spend most of their lives *not* hunting: circling, waiting, letting altitude do the work.

At dusk they roost on stone that holds the day’s heat longest. A roosting colony is a strange sight: a cliff face stippled with folded membranes and mineral spines, still as a rack of tools.

### The small folk: scavengers, skitterers, and the city’s shadow ecology

Wherever the city leaks heat, life gathers that can live on heat the way other worlds’ creatures live on sugar.

There are **armored skitterers** that patrol conduits like maintenance workers with no shift end. Their shells are plated with local dust sintered by body warmth into a matte coating that matches whatever surface they spend their life on. They have mouthparts made for licking: not to lap liquid, but to scrape condensed salts and trace organics off warm metal, and to rasp at insulation to reach richer compounds underneath. They are a nuisance in the way insects are a nuisance—persistent, small, hard to eradicate without poisoning everything else.

In the vents of heat exchangers live things that make engineers swear: **felt-nesters**, organisms that weave mineral fibers stolen from filters into insulating pads. They don’t seek comfort; they seek stable gradients. Inside a felt nest, the temperature difference between the hot side and the cool side is gentler, more predictable, and predictability is food. Left unchecked they can clog fins and reduce efficiency, so people do regular “combing,” scraping the nests into sealed bins where they’ll either be processed as bio-waste or, in some neighborhoods, dried and burned in controlled microreactors that reclaim trace elements.

There are also creatures that blur the line between animal and tool.

- **Cable-lickers** (named for what they look like they’re doing) cling to tension lines and sky-bridge guy wires. They detect vibration—traffic, wind, the minute singing of a loaded span—and use it as both warning and meal. Their bodies include pressure-sensitive plates that generate tiny currents under flex. They harvest those currents internally, supplementing a diet of dust and salts. On quiet nights you can sometimes see them as faint dots along a cable, each discharging a minuscule spark when the line thrums.
- **Heat-mites** are almost invisible until they swarm a warm panel. They’re not parasites of flesh so much as parasites of *machines*. Their presence is betrayed by a subtle change in thermal imaging: a warm blur where there should be clean edges. They die quickly if exposed to open air; they need the stable, sheltered microclimate under a plate or inside a duct.

None of these city-scavengers are welcomed, exactly, but no one is surprised by them. The city is an oasis because it creates differences, and differences are what the smallest lives know how to eat.

### Predators and patience: hunters of a slow world

A landscape this open does not favor sprint predators. It favors ambush, patience, and the ability to read vibration through stone.

Out among the needle ranges are hunters sometimes called **shard-stalkers**—sleek, low-bodied animals that can flatten themselves against rock like a shadow made solid. Their hides are layered with reflective mineral flakes that can be shifted by underlying muscles, changing sheen and color just enough to match whatever cliff they’re pressed to. They don’t camouflage like leaves; they camouflage like geology.

They hunt the scrapers not by chasing but by **steering**. A stalker will position itself where a herd must pass—between a feeding face and a known shelter cleft, near a slope where talus is sorted into unstable bands. When the moment comes it drives a targeted panic: a sudden impact against rock that sends a vibration through the ground, a low-frequency pulse that makes legs slip on gravel that seemed stable a heartbeat before. The kill is quick, because in a world where energy is precious, wounds that bleed are wasteful. Their mouths are adapted to pierce and seal, to keep what little internal moisture a body has from spilling away.

The scavenging afterward is its own event. Smaller skitterers arrive. Gliders wheel overhead. Even the mineral films benefit, because a carcass is a rare, temporary reservoir of complex organics and bound water—enough to cause a faint bloom of surface-life on nearby stone in the following weeks.

### The city’s people: residents of seals, schedules, and thrift

Who lives in the terraces and bolted platforms depends on when you arrive.

There are engineers and maintenance crews, yes, because anything built here is in continual negotiation with grit and thermal stress. There are foundry workers and rail-liners, because the planet’s gift is raw material and the punishment is moving it. There are also families, not just transient labor: people who have decided that the thinness and height are home.

They tend to be compact in their habits rather than their bodies. Not necessarily shorter, not necessarily stronger, but *trained*.

They learn to keep their mouths closed when they don’t need to speak, because every exhale is lost volatiles unless it’s captured. They learn to talk close, shoulder-to-shoulder, because sound does not travel and because intimacy is efficient. They carry personal seal kits the way other worlds’ people carry keys: lip and nose gaskets for dust storms, emergency moisture packs, small resin sticks for patching a cracked faceplate on a mask.

Clothing is layered not for rain but for abrasion. Outer fabrics are sacrificial, meant to be sanded away and replaced. Underlayers are sealed and smooth to keep grit from turning every movement into a slow injury. Skin-care here is engineering: oils and barrier films to stop dust from pulling moisture out of pores, mineral salves to treat microabrasions before they become infections.

Their food is not green, and the menu reads like a refinery report.

- **Film-cultures** grown on conductive plates in warm-dark cabinets: harvested as thin sheets, dried, crumbled into stews thickened with mineral flours.
- **Grazers’ products**: not milk in the familiar sense, but a nutrient slurry expressed from glandular sacs—salty, metallic on the tongue, prized because it comes with trace water and complex compounds the human body can’t easily synthesize.
- **Fermented salts**: controlled microbial analogs that turn otherwise useless mineral feedstocks into vitamins and usable energy stores, in sealed crocks that are treated like heirlooms because the cultures are hard-won.

Their water—what little of it exists—is social. It is counted, traded, argued over. There are communal rules about showers (rare), about cooking (condensate-heavy dishes on certain nights), about when you can run a personal humidifier and when you must endure the dry. Children are taught early the etiquette of breath: don’t waste it in laughter you can’t afford; don’t be ashamed to laugh anyway.

Even leisure is adapted. People gather on high terraces at dusk not to watch rivers or clouds but to watch **distance**. To trace the path of a glider until it becomes a speck and then a rumor. To listen to the faint singing of cables and guess, by ear, whether a bridge is loaded evenly. Some keep small pets—usually not furry things, but hardy skitterers tamed by warmth and regular feedings of salts, or little burrowers that live in wall cavities and alert by their agitation when a conduit starts to overheat.

They don’t speak of the land as hostile, not exactly. Hostility implies malice, and this place feels indifferent in a way that becomes almost honest. What they say, when they’re being precise, is that the land is *expensive*. Everything costs. Every mistake costs more.

### Mutualism at the edges: when the city becomes an ecosystem

Over time, the boundary between “urban” and “wild” softens, not with vines but with agreements.

The city’s heat exchangers become night-warm cliffs that attract gliders, and gliders keep certain scavengers in check. Condenser farms pull trace volatiles from the air, changing the chemistry of nearby stone just enough that the mineral films shift composition, which changes the grazing patterns of scrapers, which changes where predators hunt, which changes where people feel safe walking between platforms.

Some neighborhoods intentionally cultivate **spark-film gardens** on sacrificial panels bolted to cliff faces: living sheets tuned to specific trace gases, used as crude environmental sensors. The panels “flicker” differently when certain compounds rise—an early warning for leaks, a sign that a foundry run is venting impurities, a hint that a dust plume from a distant rockfall is rich in something worth collecting.

Maintenance crews learn to read the nonhuman residents the way sailors read weather. A sudden absence of heat-mites from a duct can mean a temperature drop upstream. An unusual congregation of cable-lickers can mean a bridge span is vibrating off-pattern. The city’s people pretend this is just pragmatic observation, but there’s a quiet reverence in it: the acknowledgment that life here is not decoration. It is instrumentation.

### What “abundance” looks like on a world without wetness

Abundance here is not lushness. It is not soft. It is not fast.

It is a thousand tiny lives surviving on differences so small you’d miss them if you weren’t forced, by thirst and thin air, to pay attention. It is herds that eat stone without ever greening the land. It is membranes drifting over voids, patient as weather. It is scavengers turning the city’s waste heat into their own continuance. It is people building terraces and bridges and condensers and then living among the consequences, becoming just another layer of the planet’s slow, abrasive, ingenious ecology.

Stand still long enough on one of the bolted platforms—long enough that your impatience runs out—and you can feel it: not a silence, but a distributed, living economy of grit and charge and warmth. The place is full.

Not with water. With residents. With strategies. With time.
