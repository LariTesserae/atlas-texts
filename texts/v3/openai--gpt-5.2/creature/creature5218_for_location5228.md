What lives on the high plateau is not one thing but an agreement—an old compact between mineral, wind, and bodies that have learned to be more *surface* than *blood*. If you try to picture “an ecosystem” up here the way you would on a wet world, you’ll miss it. Life is not arranged around water, growth, and decay; it’s arranged around *contact*, *abrasion*, and *storage*. Everything that survives has accepted two truths:

1) nothing will wash anything away, and  
2) distance is not fully trustworthy.

So the inhabitants become archivists of matter and cautious negotiators of space.

### The first residents: films, grains, and the patience under stones
The plateau’s real biomass is easy to overlook because it doesn’t stand upright. It clings.

On the shaded underside of a ventifact, where temperature changes are gentle, there’s a slickness that isn’t wet. It’s a living glaze: microbial mats so thin they read as discoloration until you scrape them and your tool comes away with a faintly metallic smear. They metabolize what the wind brings—trace organics, airborne salts, powdered bone—and what the stone itself offers: iron, sulfur, whatever can be coaxed from a lattice one molecule at a time. Their “waste” is the plateau’s soil: a flour-fine mineral-organic dust that behaves like a solid until disturbed because it’s been electrostatically knitted together by countless microscopic secretions.

In cracks where dust settles, other organisms move in: not roots, but *threads*. Filaments like ash-hair that don’t so much anchor a plant as stitch a pocket of powder into a more stable fabric. They’re not green because green is a luxury—chlorophyll is built for abundant light and abundant water. These things are pale, resinous, sometimes faintly crystalline. They don’t drink; they *trap*. A single tuft will slowly accumulate a collar of fine particles around itself, growing not by reaching upward but by thickening its catchment, becoming a living dust-sieve.

If you lift a flat stone in one of these pockets, it’s like turning over a whole city.

### The dust-fauna: the “soil” that moves
The small animals are built like the plateau: armored, matte, and made for grinding. They live *in* dust the way burrowers live in loam, except their burrows are temporary cavities that collapse behind them and leave almost no sign.

They’re not insects exactly; many have too many joints, too many redundant plates, a geometry that looks engineered for surviving sandblasting. Their mouths are rasps and scrapers. They eat mineral skins, microbial films, dried excretions, the last greasy trace left on a bone. They don’t waste water because they don’t have water to waste—what fluid they have is thick, gel-like, held in membranous bladders that only open internally. They exhale almost nothing. They molt seldom, and when they do the shed shell becomes a resource: lighter species chew it into powder; some of the “plants” incorporate it into their dust-hoards the way trees on other worlds incorporate leaf litter.

Their most important role isn’t as prey (though everything eats them). It’s as *turnover*. They keep the plateau from becoming a sealed museum by abrading every surface back into usable particulates. They are the slow hands of recycling.

At this level, you start to sense a theme: nothing up here is allowed to be singular. Everything is made to be taken apart.

### The first large bodies: herd-forms that graze stone
Then come the animals big enough to cast a recognizable shadow—herd creatures that look, at a glance, like an argument between a goat and a crustacean. Long legs, wide feet, a belly held high enough not to drag through drifting powder. Their skins are membranes layered like onion-skin: translucent, tough, and strangely beautiful in oblique light, because you can see the supporting fibers and the faint pattern of underlying vessels.

They don’t graze “plants” in the way you’d expect. They graze *films*. They spend hours nosing along sheltered rock faces and the lee sides of boulders, licking up the living glaze and the mineral salts it traps. Their mouths are wide and ridged. Some carry symbionts in cheek-pouches—little hard-bodied pickers that live on what the herd animal loosens, and in return keep mouth-rot and parasites at bay.

Juveniles are not simply smaller; they are *softer*, and that softness is dangerous. The herd arranges itself to protect it. When they bed down (which is really “stand still with joints locked”), the young are hidden in the middle where windborne grit is less abrasive and predators are less tempted.

Because predators here do not chase for sport. Chase wastes air.

### Predators: efficiency with manners
The plateau’s predators are not flamboyant. Their bodies are lean, sealed, and quiet. Many have reflective patches—mineralized scales that can be angled to shed heat or to match the pale salt sheen. Some have forelimbs ending in pads rather than claws, a concession to noise: a hard claw on polished rock broadcasts your arrival.

The kill, when it happens, is quick and almost ritual. A predator doesn’t want a fight that sprays valuable fluid onto sterile stone. Blood here is more gel than liquid; it dries fast into a dark resin. Predators aim to keep it contained, because any loss is a gift to scavengers and microbes, and gifts draw attention.

And there is always attention.

Not just from other animals, but from the plateau’s other great population: the cliff-nesters.

### The ravine colonies: creatures that speak through stone
In the polished ravine walls—those ceramic planes and impossible niches—live colonies of beings that use the air only as a convenience. They are light-boned, angular, folded out of membrane and spar. Wings that hardly beat because beating is loud and expensive. When they launch, you hear a far-off page-turning rather than a flap.

But their real language is not in air.

They tap. They scrape. They vibrate the rock with claws and throat-sacs and sternum plates. A single individual can send a patterned tremor through a wall and another, a hundred meters away and out of sight, will answer. The ravines become message-lines. The plateau becomes, in places, a resonant instrument.

If you stay long enough in one basin, you begin to feel their communication as a pressure in your teeth and a wrongness in your joints—not pain, but the awareness that the stone is being used as a medium for meaning. The tapping is not random: it has rhythm, call-and-response, the hurried staccato of alarm and the long slow rolls that carry status or count.

These colonies are not merely “birds.” They are a society adapted to three-dimensional living space carved into cliff faces. They farm the windfall: anything that drops into a ravine becomes theirs sooner or later. They know every ledge and how to fall without dying. They keep stores—bones stacked by size, hide sheets pegged under overhangs, dust-cakes pressed and cured in shaded niches. They are the plateau’s librarians of carcass and scrap.

Some outsiders call them *the Tappers*. Not kindly, not unkindly—just descriptively, the way you might name a people by their most audible act.

### The scavenger layers: disciplined hunger
After a kill—or after any death, which is itself an event because bodies do not vanish quickly—arrive the scavengers. They come in sequence, and the sequence rarely varies:

- First, the soft-takers: quick, cautious, able to slice membrane and pull organ-mass before it cures into leathery stiffness.  
- Then, the tendon-chewers: jawed animals that specialize in collagen, in the ropey ligaments that hold joints together.  
- Then, the bone-workers: smaller, patient, equipped with grinding mouthparts, turning skeletal material into powder.  
- Finally, the lickers: tiny things that clean the last trace of salt and resin from the stone where the body lay.

This order is not enforced by dominance so much as by mutual predictability. Noise and fighting attract what no one wants: the things that don’t need to hurry.

### The things that don’t need to hurry
There are inhabitants that are not easily categorized as animal or plant, because they behave more like *processes with bodies*. You notice them around fold-grounds—those basins where the world seems edited, where a standing stone might be one more than it was, where a ridge can duplicate itself faintly.

In such places, you sometimes see a shape that is almost a trick of the eye: a low, dark bulge against pale salt, like a boulder that has decided to be slightly too smooth. It doesn’t move when you look at it. It moves when you look away. Its presence is felt as a misalignment: the sense that your next step might land a fraction too far, or that your shadow is not exactly attached.

Locals treat these as hazards rather than monsters. They have names that translate poorly—words that mean something like *measure-eater* or *step-thief*. The prevailing belief is that these beings are attracted to repeated paths and stable patterns. The plateau is old and composed; it provides the consistent geometry such things can feed on. They don’t eat flesh. They eat *certainty*—the predictable relationship between here and there.

That’s why thrown stones sometimes return: not because of magic, exactly, but because something in the fold-ground has learned the shape of an arc and now repeats it incorrectly, like a song remembered wrong.

Whether these “step-thieves” are animals, spatial parasites, or symptoms of the plateau itself is an argument that never resolves. People who live here don’t care what they are. They care where they are.

### The human presence: small hands, long attention
There are people on the plateau, but they do not dominate it. They don’t build high because the wind will sand anything proud back into humility, and because anything tall becomes a landmark for predators, raiders, and worse—those that hunt patterns.

They live in low shelters, stone-over-stone, roofs scaled like shale. Their craft is adapted to stiffness and abrasion. They make cords from sinew and fibrous column-plants, braided in ways that resist fraying. They favor tools that scrape and bore rather than chop, because chopping is for green wood and there isn’t any. Metal is treasured not because it’s shiny but because it keeps an edge against bone and mineral.

Their culture is navigational. Not in the romantic “we roam” sense—more in the way sailors are always reading the water. Plateau-people read *space*. They carry paired markers. They build cairns in twos, sometimes in threes, and they never trust a lone landmark. They tell stories that are also maps, and the maps include warnings like “do not cross the pale bowl at noon” or “if you see the ridge duplicate, turn back without counting your steps.”

They have etiquette shaped by thin air and crowded life:

- Don’t shout. Shouting is wasteful and invites attention.  
- Don’t run unless you must. Running is panic, and panic is contagious.  
- Don’t throw stones. You might get them back, and not in a way you can predict.  
- Don’t sleep near a fold-ground, because your dreams might start walking before you do.

When they hunt, they hunt with restraint. When they kill, they use everything. Not out of piety—out of simple understanding that decay is slow and the plateau remembers what you leave.

They trade with the Tappers, too, though neither side frames it as trade. Plateau-people leave bundles in agreed places: dust-cakes, cured hide, bone needles. The Tappers leave information: a new crack in the ground, a herd-route altered, a basin where sound is swallowed too much and the stone’s hum has changed.

Information is the richest resource here.

### The plateau’s strangest resident: the held breath itself
After you’ve accounted for films and dust-fauna, for herds and predators, for cliff societies and careful humans, the sense of “crowded openness” still isn’t fully explained. That’s because the plateau’s most pervasive inhabitant is not a creature you can point to.

It is *equilibrium*—a stability so old it has become an active force.

Up here, patterns persist long enough that they begin to behave like structures. Trails become not just worn routes but expectations in the dust. Routes become habits. Habits become something the fold-grounds can latch onto. The plateau doesn’t change much, so the *rules* begin to show through, like an underlayer of paint exposed by endless sanding.

In that sense, the plateau itself feels alive—not as a mind that speaks, but as a system that has developed reflexes. It preserves. It holds. It edits gently, without drama, when something disrupts its composure.

That’s why ruins feel paused instead of abandoned. That’s why carcasses become pantries instead of rot. That’s why distance misbehaves most in the smooth, pale bowls: those are the places where the world has been polished thinnest, and the old stability shows through as a distortion.

What lives here, finally, is everything that can tolerate being part of a long-held breath.

Life that doesn’t rely on water but on dust.  
Societies that don’t rely on speech but on vibration.  
People who don’t rely on permanence of landmarks but on redundancy and caution.  
And lurking within the steadiness, things that feed on the very predictability that makes the plateau feel finished.

If you stand still, you can feel them all at once: a million small raspings under your feet, herds turning like slow weather, taps in the stone like distant code, and underneath it—the second silence, deep as a seam, where the plateau keeps its composure and quietly adjusts the map.
