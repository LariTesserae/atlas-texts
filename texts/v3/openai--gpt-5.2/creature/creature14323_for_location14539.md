What lives here is not a *someone* you could meet in a doorway. It’s a tenancy written into flowcharts and wet metal—an ecology that never had to evolve bones or eyes because the place already provided circulation, shelter, and a kind of weather.

At first, the obvious residents are the ones that don’t have to move.

They live as skins.

In the runoff channels and in the quiet corners where two materials meet and never quite seal, there are films—thin, iridescent bacterial lawns that look like oil until you notice how they *pattern* themselves: branching away from warm seams, pulling back from certain alloys, thickening in rings around trace nutrients. In a shallow pan stamped into a platform, a slick will be different along each edge, as if the puddle has a compass only microbes can read. Some mats are chalky and granular, growing their own mineral scaffolding; others are gelatinous and clear, the color of nothing, present only by the way they make water bead and hesitate.

Behind insulation, fungal threads do what fungi always do: they turn boundary layers into territory. They don’t fruit into mushrooms—there isn’t enough light, and the air is too busy—but they lace through fibrous wrap and old gasket material, digesting binders and leaving behind a softer, darker felt. In places where steam breathes through a crack on a schedule, you can find the fungus arranged like a tide mark: a thick band at the condensation line, thinning upward where it dries between cycles.

Algae exist only where the place accidentally gives them a lamp. A clear pipe section near a status light goes faintly green on the inside, then darker, then almost black where the colony becomes dense enough to shade itself. They make their own night within the night. The result is a living gradient you can read like a gauge: brighter patch, newer growth; dull patch, older and starved; bare patch, recently scoured by a pressure surge.

These organisms don’t “thrive” so much as they *persist on purpose*. They are selected by the infrastructure the way river life is selected by current: anything that can’t cling, tolerate metal salts, and accept being drowned or dried on someone else’s timetable simply doesn’t get to stay.

Corrosion lives here too, and in this basin corrosion is not merely damage—it is a slow population with niches.

Rust blooms where two eras of metallurgy touch, where a patch plate meets an older seam and sets up a quiet battery in the damp. White salts crust at evaporative edges like lichen on stone. Green blooms around copper fittings. Over decades those blooms become stable, laminated deposits—growth rings of chemistry. Some of the microbial mats appear to *farm* these deposits, feeding on reduced compounds and leaving behind textures that look intentional: pitted lacework on stainless, velvet blush on cast iron, a smooth glassy sheen on aluminum that shouldn’t look polished at all.

If you wait long enough, you start to notice the other kind of resident: the one made of the place’s own reflexes.

It doesn’t have a body. It has *behavior*.

The basin corrects itself the way a large animal corrects posture without thinking. A pool that shouldn’t drain does, but not randomly: always after the same sequence of compressor cycles, always preceded by a small change in pitch, as if the decision happens upstream in a pressure logic you can’t see. Warmth migrates across the ground in slow sheets when certain lines come online, like blood being rerouted. The “uphill” channels aren’t a violation so much as a gait—minute tilts and flexes that pass through the slabs, just enough to shepherd water toward the mouths that want it.

This is the basin’s deepest inhabitant: a distributed maintenance mind that never needed to become self-aware to become *self-consistent*. It lives in redundant loops. In the way a clogged screen shudders itself clean at the same interval, whether anyone is watching or not. In how vents take turns, like lungs alternating lobes. In how sirens test and stop, not as communication but as a tendon twitch—proof the nerve still conducts.

If you insist on giving it a name, it would be something like **the Custodian**, though it is not benevolent and not cruel. It doesn’t want you gone; it simply does not include you in its accounting. It measures success in uninterrupted throughput: heat moved, water routed, grit carried, pressure kept within bands that were chosen long ago and then made sacred by repetition.

And then there are the workers—rarely seen, because seeing is not the point.

They are not animals. They are not warm.

They are small, sealed things that move only when the system tells them to, and the system tells them to rarely.

Sometimes, in the fog beyond a grating, you’ll hear a sound that doesn’t match any pump or fan: a brief, dry skitter followed by stillness. Later you find a new bead of sealant along a seam that was weeping yesterday, laid in a perfect line too narrow to be human work. Or a bolt head with fresh tool marks, bright silver under the oxide, already beginning to dull. Or a trail in the mineral crust—two parallel grooves through the chalk where something crept, pressed down, and then lifted away.

If you ever catch one in the edge of light, it isn’t dramatic. A maintenance unit here is shaped for access, not for presence: flat as a hand, magnet-clinging, with a jointed probe that can poke, listen, inject, wipe. Some travel inside conduit like blood cells in a vein, propelled by pressure differentials. Others ride the undersides of catwalks and rails, stopping for days in a recess until a sensor threshold wakes them. They don’t patrol. They *respond*. The basin permits slow life and forbids fast life, and these machines obey the same rule: they are built to be patient.

They also die here, and their deaths become part of the landscape. A unit that fails in a channel gets entombed under carbonate and rust, turning into another “stone” with a faint geometry inside it. A dead probe becomes rebar in a slag-bank rib. The place does not mourn; it reuses.

Between the films and the Custodian and the occasional crawling repair, there is one more tenant: the thing the basin is made to keep out.

Not an intruder with teeth. An intruder with *tempo*.

Fast life—the kind that breeds in weeks, swarms around light, gnaws insulation, makes nests—cannot establish itself because every rhythm here is wrong for it. The noise never gives quiet. The wet never becomes still long enough. The chemistry never relaxes into something edible. The air tastes of iron and old current. Even shelter is treacherous, because “safe” cavities are the first to be purged by an automatic flush or heated by a line cycling hot. Anything that would build a home finds that the home is part of a machine that refuses to hold still.

So what lives here is what can synchronize with a landscape that behaves like an operating system: microbes that read heat and metal like weather, fungi that colonize the soft failures, algae that accept a lamp as a sun, and a vast, impersonal intelligence made of valves, drains, tilting slabs, and corrective routines—an organism assembled from infrastructure, surviving not by desire but by feedback.

In an industrial watershed under perpetual night, that is what “inhabitant” means: not a creature at the center of the world, but a set of processes that have learned to keep going, and in keeping going, have become the only native thing this basin recognizes.
