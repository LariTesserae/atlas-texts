What lives there is, first, the net itself.

Not metaphorically—the dominant organism up in that high, cut-open country is a plant that stopped pretending “ground” was a requirement and began treating *air* as its habitat. The fog-nets and wind-roots are less like trees than like distributed bodies: no single trunk you can point to and name as the individual, but a braided, tensioned anatomy spread across cracks and distances. Their “roots” are not anchors so much as *holds*, wedged into basalt seams with pads that exude a gritty resin, then harden glassy in cold. Their “stems” are cables: layered fibers that swell when wet and cinch tight as they dry, constantly tuning their own span like rigging.

Up close the cable isn’t smooth. It’s furred with comb-hairs—minute, stiff filaments that make fog into beads and beads into flow. Each hair has grooves, and each groove insists water go one direction: inward, toward the thickened nodes. Those nodes are the plant’s organs—storage bulbs, repair-buds, flower-sheaths—huddled in sheltered pockets of stone where wind can’t flay them. When the brief flowering happens, it doesn’t announce itself with color (nothing up here wastes energy on being seen); it announces itself with *chemistry*. The pods split and throw scent into the wet dark: resinous, mineral, sharp as snapped twigs. It is an invitation, a warning, and a coordinate.

Because the nets are an architecture, they acquire tenants the way cliffs acquire ledges: gradually, inevitably, and with a kind of inevitability that looks like design once it’s established.

There are the small things first—the life you only notice when you stop trying to see the landscape as empty space. Pale mites that graze the algae film that grows wherever condensation lingers. Needle-legged insects that live their entire lives in the lee of a braid, drinking droplets as they gather and laying eggs in the softest new growth so the larvae hatch into food. Fungal threads that lace into the plant’s outer layers, not quite disease and not quite symbiont, more like a second skin: they help retain moisture, and in exchange they steal sugars and leave behind faintly phosphorescent waste. On some nights, when the fog glows dull green, it’s those countless fine lives adding up—spores and hyphae and bacterial sheen, a low light born from wet metabolism.

Then come the larger herbivores—the ones that make the net a *thoroughfare*.

They are built for split-second trust in tension. Long-limbed, narrow-bodied climbers with cloven hooves that don’t so much step as clamp: each hoof has a hardened inner ridge that catches braid-fiber without cutting it, and flexible outer pads that seal against wet strands. Their joints are strange by lowland standards—more rotation than hinge—so they can correct mid-step when a cable twists under gust. They move in bursts along habitual routes, because the net itself teaches them where safety is: repeated hoof-falls cause the plant to thicken and toughen in those places, so the animals’ paths become reinforced simply by being used. Their teeth never stop growing because everything they eat is abrasive—leaf-curtains tough as sailcloth, mineral-dusted hairs, bitter pods that trade toxicity for surviving in cold.

They are not silent animals. In fog they maintain contact with short, breathy calls that don’t travel far through wet air—safer that way. But their real speech is weight and rhythm. A herd crossing a span in single file produces a recognizable pattern of tremor: a signature that other creatures read through feet, claws, and the sensitive skins of nodes.

Above them, or sometimes *with* them, ride the gliders.

They roost inside the thickest knuckles of the net—those gnarled places where cable meets cable and the plant has layered itself into a hollowed mass. The gliders are membrane-winged, but their membranes are furred and their bodies run hot for their size. In daylight (when there is any) they are charcoal-colored, disappearing against rock; in fog their fur beads with moisture and makes them look rimed, ghost-pale. They launch in groups because a single animal’s echo-clicks are easy to misread in a landscape of hollows, but a chorus can triangulate the world. When they fly, they don’t *soar* like birds; they drop, bank, and catch, using the net’s spans and the cliff-faces as acoustic reference points. Their clicks are not only navigation—they are social. A good span has a certain resonance, and they will “sing” a route into one another: a sequence of pulses that means, in effect, *this gap is crossable, this hollow eats sound, this corridor will throw you.*

They feed on the net-world’s abundance: insects shaken loose by wind, the soft inner tissue of flower-pods, the fat larvae that hide in warm crevices. In lean times they become opportunists around kills, landing on cable near bone and taking what they can with quick, nervous bites, always ready to drop away into mist.

Predators live here too, but they don’t rule by speed. They rule by *information*.

The most successful hunters are the ones that learned what this place really is: a vibrating web, a continent-scale instrument. They are low-slung, patient creatures with gripping feet—some with claws that hook braid without fraying it, some with adhesive pads that can cling to wet fiber. Their faces are blunt and their ears are small; their true sensing organs are in their limbs and ribs, tuned to detect tremor through contact. They seldom chase. Instead they station themselves at junctions where many spans meet, where every crossing sends news. When a herd moves, the whole net tells on it. When a single animal stumbles, the plant shivers differently, and the predators know *which* shiver means fear.

They communicate by drumming—deliberate thumps in patterned sequences. The net carries these signals farther than voice would, and the wet fog doesn’t swallow them the way it swallows sound in air. A drumming call can coordinate an ambush across a chasm you cannot see into. It can warn a mate. It can claim a route. It can also mislead: experienced hunters will send false tremors, small manufactured panics that herd prey away from a safe span and toward a place where the net is thinner.

This is why you find bones wedged into cable crossings: not only because kills happen there, but because predators *mark* there. Bone becomes a signpost, a rattle in the wind, a reminder that the net remembers.

And then there are the tool-makers—the light-footed people who build as if they know they are temporary guests in a structure older than their stories.

They are not numerous; they cannot be, not in a land where every calorie costs breath. They move in small bands, more kinship than tribe, more apprenticeship than lineage. Their bodies are corded and compact, their lungs trained by altitude into a kind of steady fury. They travel with hook-tools carved from antler and stone, not to conquer the net but to *borrow* it—hooks that grip without slicing, shoes with fibrous soles that spread their weight and read vibration the way a hand reads Braille.

They live in nests rather than houses: half-bowls of woven vine braced to rock, lined with felted leaf and sealed with resin to keep out wet. They favor places where the net forms a natural platform, because they understand what the herbivores and predators understand: repeated stress thickens structure. A camp on a living span becomes safer if it is used carefully and consistently, because the plant responds. So they are deliberate about where they step, how often, in what weather. Their presence is a kind of gardening.

They harvest without stripping. Fog-net fibers are taken from old growth near the edges, where the plant sheds and frays naturally; cutting into a load-bearing braid is taboo not as morality but as physics. Resin pellets are collected from seal-pads and from flowering nodes after the scent is spent. Galls are prized—hard, hollow, already shaped by insect labor into bowls and canteens. Bone needles and braided cordage are common; fire is rare enough that flame becomes a ritual of necessity, not comfort.

Their maps are the hanging dyed strips—not because they can be read by eye in fog, but because they can be *heard*. Different pigments stiffen fiber differently; different patterns fray into different rattles. A line of strips is a sentence you pass through: a sequence of snaps and flutters that says *this is the safe crossing*, or *rockfall here last thaw*, or *predators drum on the third node at night*. When the wind rises, the whole marked route speaks.

They have, too, a practice that looks like worship if you watch from far away and like maintenance if you watch from near: tending the whistle-cairns. Flat black stones stacked with living braid threaded through them, tuned by the size of gaps and the tightness of binding. Each cairn’s pitch changes with ice, humidity, and tension. The people listen to those changes the way sailors listen to rigging. A sudden shift in the line’s melody means a storm organizing over the next ridge, or a pressure drop, or a span taking strain it shouldn’t. They don’t predict weather so much as *hear the land loading itself*.

Among all these lives—net-plant, grazer, glider, hunter, human—the shared adaptation is not strength or speed but fluency in a world made mostly of absence. Nothing here can afford to be heavy in the old way. Everything is either tensile, adhesive, hollow, or social. Bodies become ropes. Communities become networks. Even solitary predators depend on messages moving through the plant.

So what lives there is a chorus of organisms that treat vibration as language, fog as harvest, and the void as ordinary. They do not fill the emptiness; they *span* it, and in spanning it they make a kind of suspended ecology—loud, sensitive, constantly repairing itself, always one thaw or rockfall away from being rewritten.
