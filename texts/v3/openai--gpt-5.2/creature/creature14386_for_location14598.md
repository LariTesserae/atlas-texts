What lives there is not a population so much as a polity of processes—citizenship granted to anything that can hold a shape.

At the smallest scale, the place is crowded.

Every wet surface has its own skin: clear bacterial varnishes that make stone feel waxed; green films that thicken until they become a membrane; pale bio-crusts that look like mineral bloom until you notice they heal when you scuff them. In the warm basins where water stands without ever quite stagnating, the slicks are layered—one species shading another, each stopping at a line as clean as a drawn circle, as though boundaries are part of their metabolism. Even the air has residents: spores that never fully settle, pollen that never fully drifts away, a fine mist of living dust that rides the same convection loops over and over until “floating” becomes its habitat.

Then the mid-scale life declares itself: the city’s green masonry.

Moss here is not an accident; it behaves like a public works material. It grows with a dense, short pile on plazas and steps, and with a longer nap where water needs slowing, and with almost no growth at all on certain load-bearing edges—as if it has learned where slipperiness would be unsafe. Liverworts quilt the undersides of bridges in patterns that repeat too precisely to be explained by chance, each patch aligned with a dripline, each dripline aligned with a note in the city’s constant chord.

Ferns are the city’s curtains and banners: they pour from cracks, but only from the cracks that already exist, never prying a new seam wider. Their rootlets insinuate themselves into old joints and then stop, not from weakness but from obedience to a limit. Vines make doors, not by closing but by defining: a hanging braid that parts when you touch it and re-braids behind you, always to the same twist, always returning to the same arrangement. Broad-leaf plants on terrace edges act like gutters that learned to photosynthesize; their leaves cup mist, shed it backward, and keep the soil line from creeping forward. The hanging marshes are the city’s reservoirs and lungs, spongy with centuries of held water, pulsing softly as air pressure changes—but never swelling beyond their allotted drape.

And beneath all of it—beneath the upholstery of green, beneath the porous tufa and the honeycombed towers—there is the true inhabitant: the threaded dark.

Fungi live here like infrastructure. Not as fruiting mushrooms (those would be too temporary, too declarative), but as mycelial felt stitched through the spongy soils, through the root-mats, through the porous mineral castings. If you peel back a sheet of moss, the underside is webbed in fine white and amber threads that look like nerves. Those threads run into stone and do not stop; they take advantage of every pore in the tufa, every softened seam between fitted blocks, every hairline fracture that water can keep open. Where a crack forms, the mycelium moves in—not to widen it, not to “consume,” but to colonize it as a permanent corridor, sealing its edges with living glue until the crack becomes a new piece of the city’s planned plumbing.

That mycelium is not alone. It is partnered, constantly, with roots.

The plants’ roots here do not explore like roots elsewhere; they draft. They follow existing channels the way a trained hand follows a pattern, reinforcing what is already true about the place. Where a terrace needs buttressing, roots thicken into braided columns. Where a walkway needs railings, woody stems are encouraged into parallel rods and then bound by vine. Where water wants to pool, root-mats flatten and knit until they become a filter bed; where water must keep moving, the mats loosen, leaving a smooth throat lined with algae like a lubricated valve.

The city’s vegetation is not wild. It is civic.

It behaves as though it has internalized building codes: nothing protrudes past a certain plane into the thoroughfares; nothing grows tall enough to shift the balance of heat and humidity beyond its tolerated bands; nothing flowers in a way that would require a visitor. Many plants do flower anyway—quietly, dutifully, in repetitions that never lead to dispersal. Seedheads form, ripen, and drop into the same pockets of soil every cycle, building thickness to a permitted depth and no further. Propagation is allowed only in-place, a kind of inheritance without migration.

So you can walk a street and feel watched without being seen.

The watching is hydraulic. It is the sense that every surface is part of a single sensing organ, because water touches everything and reports everything. A footstep changes pressure; pressure changes flow; flow changes the tension of a root-mat; a root-mat changes the conductivity of the wet stone; the stone answers by humming a little differently. You cannot speak over the roar, but you can’t help adding to it. The place hears by resonance.

That is why the sound feels like more than waterfalls.

Some of the life here is tuned to vibration the way other life is tuned to light. There are biofilms that thicken most readily on the faces of humming walls, growing in faint rippled bands that match the standing waves in the stone. There are lichens—flat, pale, almost metallic in sheen—that prefer the mouths of organ-pipe corridors, where the air’s movement is rhythmic and predictable; they arrange themselves in crescents that sharpen the note, like living baffles. In the hollow towers that throb, the inner surfaces are often lined with a velvety green-black growth that looks like soot but is wet and alive, feeding on the constant energy of oscillation—not “eating sound” in any simple way, but using mechanical stress the way a tidepool organism uses surge, turning the city’s endless vibration into metabolism.

If you stay long enough, you start to suspect that what lives here is not merely adapted to the loops.

It is the author of them.

Not a single mind, not a face behind a window, not an animal hiding because you failed to look in the right crevice. Rather an intelligence made from constraints: from repeated flows, repeated drips, repeated sways, repeated notes. The loops are its thoughts, the stable forms its memories. Where other ecosystems negotiate—predation, flight, hunger, reproduction—this one enforces. It permits growth that can be folded into structure. It permits change only if it can be arrested into an equilibrium. It forbids wandering because wandering would write new geometry, and new geometry would require new decisions, and this place has decided it is done deciding.

That is why there are no animals.

Anything with a boundary of skin and a need that moves it—anything that must range, chase, flee, search—would be incompatible with a world that demands repetition. Even an insect would be an argument: a line drawn across a plaza that didn’t exist before, a ring on water that wasn’t scheduled, a bite taken from a leaf that would force the leaf to improvise. Improvisation is exile here.

And yet—because life is persistent—something like “animalness” still tries to appear, only it has been translated into forms the city can tolerate.

In the warm films on stone you can sometimes see tiny eddies of movement, not darting, not purposeful, but circulating: microscopic grazers that never travel farther than a droplet’s diameter, living their entire lives within a loop of water that rises, beads, falls, and rises again. In the marsh sponges, minute worms might exist in theory, but if they do, they are not wanderers; they are confined to the same centimeter of peat, turning slowly like clock hands, their lives constrained into a fixed orbit. The place allows motion only when motion can become a pattern.

At the largest scale, the city itself is the organism.

Not metaphorically—functionally.

Its terraces are vascular beds. Its stairways are pressure gradients. Its bridges are tendons of trained wood and stone that carry load the way bone carries load, not as dead material but as living composite reinforced by root and fungus. Its cisterns are stomachs that hold water long enough for algae and bacteria to strip it clean. Its humming corridors are lungs that move air by resonance rather than wind. Its hanging wetlands are kidneys and livers, slow chemistry and slower filtration.

What “lives” there, then, is a single continuous habitation made of many species and one overarching habit: to hold.

To hold water without stagnation.  
To hold heat without burning.  
To hold growth without invasion.  
To hold erosion at a finished edge.  
To hold sound until sound becomes structure.

If there were once builders—hands that cut the terraces, minds that learned how to cast stone from mineral water, communities that braided living railings—what remains of them is not a ghost in a doorway. It is a rule that learned how to propagate. The city has become a self-maintaining ordinance written in moss, fungus, root, and wet rock. It does not need inhabitants in the human sense, because it has converted inhabiting into architecture.

And when you enter, you don’t meet anyone.

You meet a system that can include you only as a brief distortion—heat added to air, water displaced in a channel, a new rhythm of footfalls—until it has either absorbed your presence into its constant chord, or quietly encouraged you to leave, so the place can return to the only kind of life it recognizes: the life that repeats exactly, and therefore can last.
