The first residents aren’t animals at all. They’re the soft, thin insistence that arrives anywhere water is kept moving and clean.

On the platework and tower skins, in the lee of bolt heads and under the lips of access hatches, a biofilm lives like a second paint layer: bacteria and archaea that learned to love a world of stainless steel, epoxy, and low-nutrient clarity. It’s not a lush scum—maintenance-grade water won’t allow that—but a felt so fine it’s almost optical, changing the way light slides along a surface. In some places it’s the color of weak tea. In others it’s pale as milk, a mineralizing film that steals dissolved metals and lays them down as a faint crust. If you scrape it, it doesn’t peel; it powders, as if it was always half-idea, half-dust.

Where the system leaks food—trace organics from seals, warm effluent from housings, the slow bleed of nutrients from forgotten silt traps—the film thickens and becomes a foundation. It makes the engineered world biologically legible: a map of where the sea is fed, whether anyone meant to feed it or not.

From that film come the grazers, and they are the first creatures that make the place feel *inhabited* instead of merely wet.

They live in the geometry.

Small snails with blunt, tough shells—more like fasteners than spirals—move along seam gaskets and weld beads the way shoreline snails move along tide lines. Their radulas rasp the microbial felt, leaving clean crescents that take days to blur over again. Isopods wedge themselves into the hex recesses of bolts and hold on through the scheduled tides, their bodies flattened to the same profile as a washer. You learn to spot their colonies because the bolts look inexplicably “alive,” ringed with tiny limbs that retract when the current shifts.

In the corners that were designed to be corners—right angles at bulkhead bases, the inside elbows of conduit supports—detritus gathers, and detritus means worms. Not the romantic kind, not the bright reef kind: thin, pale, contractual animals that extrude mucus nets between protruding casting marks and let the current do the work. They don’t need a bottom; they need a *boundary layer*. The machine-world provides endless boundary.

Then come the animals that turn the infrastructure into a reef, even when it resists being one.

Bivalves find purchase in what was meant for a human hand: ladder rung junctions, lifting eyes, the textured anti-slip squares. Their byssal threads look like someone started to lace the metal shut. Some are translucent, the way organisms get when their world is all uniform light and no hiding; others are dark, pigmented against the glare. They grow fastest near vibration—constant microcurrents bringing food—and slow to a stubborn halt in the deadwater shadows, where the water is too “managed” and too clean to be generous.

There are also things that don’t attach, because the system punishes anything that forgets the next flow cycle is coming.

In the broad open water, the plankton is spare but persistent: tiny crustaceans that time their rise and fall to the artificial noon, drifting upward when the surface luminaires brighten and sinking when the cycle changes, as if they can feel the schedule through their bodies. In the rectangular basins where nothing disturbs the upper layers, gelatinous drifters accumulate—comb-jellies and salps and things that aren’t quite either, transparent chains that turn pump-driven water into slow nutrition. They’re almost invisible until they intersect a shaft of light and the whole water column briefly reveals itself as crowded.

Where those drifters thicken, the first true *feeding structures* appear: filter-feeders that behave like equipment.

Some are anemone-analogues anchored to bolt patterns, their crowns arranged like gaskets, opening and closing with the pulse of flow. Others are colonial animals that build brittle lattices in the wake of pylons—white frameworks that look like mineral deposits until you see them flex, just slightly, in unison. They don’t grow where the current is strongest; they grow where it’s *predictable*. They are creatures of schedule, not weather.

Fish arrive in layers, and each layer treats the architecture differently.

The smallest ones live as if the place is made of edges rather than surfaces. They travel seam-to-seam across the platework, never more than a body length from a gasket line, because a gasket line is both food and road. Their schools turn with the right angles of containment berms, not because they must, but because the water itself turns there; the machine teaches them its corridors the way a river teaches minnows its bends.

Midwater fish—sleek, gray, efficient bodies—form in the quiet zones downstream of intakes, where the water has been pulled smooth. They are the “open sea” inhabitants of a sea that has no coast: they use the towers as orientation points, circling them at set distances like planets around a gravity well, then peeling away to cross the emptiness in disciplined ranks. When a valve opens miles away and a new current line forms, you can watch a whole population reorganize, as if a new wall appeared.

Closer to the machinery, there are specialist fish that look as if they were designed by abrasion.

Their scales are thicker, their fins more scarred, their lateral lines pronounced. They don’t flinch from metal; they have learned what metal means. They hover near warm leaks and oil-sheen eddies, taking the easy calories of whatever the system can’t quite keep contained. Some have darkened mouths from grazing directly on the biofilm that grows richest where lubricant traces are highest—an ecology that would horrify a human engineer and means nothing at all to a hungry animal.

Crustaceans claim the smaller cavities the way a mechanic claims a toolbox drawer.

Crabs live in valve housings and in the empty sleeves of abandoned conduit clamps. They molt inside pipe elbows where the flow is gentle, emerging soft and pale, then hardening under the bright industrial noon as if it were natural sun. Shrimp-like scavengers patrol the drift-dust in basins, sifting rust-flour and polymer grit for edible flecks. Their antennae are long, constantly probing the world’s sharpness; they know by touch what the place is made of, because touch is how you survive a terrain that doesn’t forgive collisions.

Predators appear not as monsters but as solutions.

There are fast hunters that use the straightness. They don’t chase prey into open water; they herd it along bulkhead faces and through service corridors where turning is expensive. You’ll see them wait at the mouth of a narrow gate—where the current accelerates by design—and take whatever is forced to commit. They are patient in a way that feels mechanical, but it’s only adaptation: when the world runs on cycles, ambush becomes a timetable.

Larger predators cruise the deep volumes between districts. They don’t like the loudest zones; the constant bass is a kind of pressure on their senses. They keep to the quieter reaches where the hum becomes background, and when they do enter the machinery-thick regions they do it like divers: purposeful, brief, aimed at known choke points. Their scars tell you the place bites. Their bodies tell you they’ve decided it’s worth it.

Somewhere above all that, mammals—if mammals were ever introduced, or if something filled their niche—treat the towers as reefs and the open water as flight space.

Seal-analogues will sleep in the slow spiral eddies behind pylons, letting the water hold them in place with minimal effort. Their calls don’t scatter into a natural seascape; they strike metal and come back altered, booming and layered, so that a single voice can sound like a crowd. Young ones learn routes the way human workers once did: “from this tower to that intake, under the pipe-bridge with the peeling paint, past the basin where the bubbles rise for kilometers.”

And then there are the residents that are not biological, but are still *inhabitants*—because they persist, and because everything alive has learned to account for them.

In the clearer corridors you sometimes see them first as motion without a body: a distortion line, a small pressure wake. Maintenance drones, long untethered from any human schedule, still run their patterns. Some are slow crawlers that cling to tower skins on magnetic pads, their housings furred with algae and larvae, their lens ports cleaned by their own tiny wipers. Others are torpedo-shaped inspection units that glide the main conduits, their sonar pings faint but regular—another layer of sound in the already crowded water.

They don’t “hunt,” but they startle schools into turning. They don’t “guard,” but they make certain zones feel watched. Creatures have developed etiquette around them: smaller fish draft in their wake the way they draft behind larger animals; crabs retreat when a crawler’s lights sweep a seam; filter-feeders close briefly at the passage of a thruster pulse, reopening when the water settles.

Some drones have failed in useful ways.

A stalled unit becomes habitat—a little island of warmth if its battery still bleeds, a complex object full of cavities and edges. Colonies grow on it faster than on the clean platework, because it offers what the engineered world rarely does: irregularity. In time, it becomes indistinguishable from debris except for the occasional blink of a status LED, still trying to speak to a network that may no longer answer.

If there is a single “who” that lives here most completely, it might be the system itself—not as a mind, but as a persistent behavior.

The managed tides, the scheduled currents, the light that never gets romantic: these are not backdrop. They are the largest organism in the habitat, the one everything else entrains to. Life here doesn’t merely tolerate the machine-sea; it listens to it the way coastal life listens to the moon. Spawning events happen when the flow reverses. Migrations ride the pump pulses. Predation concentrates at valve-openings the way predation concentrates at river mouths.

Even the plants—so sparse, so opportunistic—behave like tenants rather than colonizers. They take what the infrastructure accidentally offers: a bolt hole that catches silt, a seam that leaks nutrients, a shadow that reduces shear stress. They don’t pretend they’ll cover the world; they only need enough footholds to keep existing.

So what lives here is not one emblematic beast, not a single “strange thing,” but a whole set of lives that have learned to read a manufactured ocean as if it were natural:

- films and threads that treat steel as stone,
- grazers that treat gasket seams as pasture,
- filter-feeders that treat scheduled currents as weather,
- fish that treat service corridors as canyons,
- predators that treat gates and accelerations as hunting grounds,
- mammals that treat pylons as islands,
- and machines that treat the water as a workplace long after the workers are gone.

They are all, in their ways, doing the same thing: making a home out of a design that never intended to be one—using the bolts, the hum, the bright noon, the vast empty volume, and the reliable tide of procedure as the raw materials of an ecology.
