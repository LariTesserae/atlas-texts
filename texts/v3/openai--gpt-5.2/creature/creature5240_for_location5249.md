What lives here is whatever can tolerate being perpetually damp without surrendering to it—and whatever can make dampness useful.

At first you think: people, plainly. You catch them in the periphery the way you catch the flicker of a belt or the swing of a valve handle. They’re not absent; they’re simply folded into the same density as everything else. Their bodies move with the district’s rules: careful steps that assume the ground will give, shoulders angled to slip through shoulder-width corridors, hands that automatically touch a wall-vine to feel whether it’s too turgid with water or too dry from waste-heat. Even when you don’t see them, you hear the human layer in the place’s soundscape: a cough answered by another cough; the double-knock of a wrench on metal that means “line’s clear”; short calls that don’t waste breath in warm, heavy air.

But “people” is too blunt a category here. The basin doesn’t house citizens so much as it houses functions—roles that have become hereditary in the way wet habits become permanent.

There are the **pumpwrights**, who live closest to the bass thud of persuasion. Their skin and clothes smell faintly of oil and wet iron; their nails are perpetually dark with grease that never quite dries. They know the district by pressure more than by street: which corridor feels cooler because an elevated tank is drawing down, which platform vibrates because a belt is slipping, which canal eddies wrong because silt has started to knit into a new shape. The pumpwrights have an instinct for bubbles—air caught where it shouldn’t be—and when a line coughs they don’t flinch, they listen, the way a medic listens to a lung.

There are the **binders**, the ones who treat vegetation as structure the way masons treat stone. You can tell where binders live because their ladders are not nailed-on things but grown-in things—rungs coaxed from living stems, then thickened over years until they take weight without complaint. Binders carry blades that never look dramatic: short, curved knives for pruning, narrow saws that grind rather than scream. They don’t cut to remove, they cut to instruct. A wall is not a wall to them; it’s a living plane with a schedule. Too much shade and it will thin; too much heat and it will crack; too much wet and it will fur with rot. Their work is constant, quiet, and visible in what *doesn’t* happen: no sudden collapses, no vines strangling a shaft, no root-bulge rupturing a channel.

Then, close to the sweet-ferment smell, are the **pulpers and pressers**, who turn the district’s excess growth into something that can be shipped, stored, or made into another part of the district. Their platforms are sticky in a way that never fully cleans. They move wet fiber like bakers move dough—lifting, folding, feeding it into rollers. If the city is a body, these are the digestive organs: the ones that make sure nothing grows without also becoming resource.

Above them, threaded through shade, are the **loomkeepers**—not weavers in the romantic sense, but operators of disciplined clicking machines that turn reed and cane into mat, panel, shingle. Their spaces are always warmer and drier than you expect, because humidity ruins tension and tension is everything. They are the ones who curse mildew like a personal enemy and have the sharpest noses for it. Where a binder reads the health of a wall by touch, a loomkeeper reads the health of the whole district by smell: the first hint of sourness in stored fiber, the first hint of sweet rot in a stack of panels, the difference between “this is ferment” and “this is failure.”

And then there are the people who are easiest to miss because they have learned to make themselves narrow: the **heat-runners**, who follow waste-heat pipes and maintain the district’s invisible boundaries. They keep certain corridors just warm enough that moisture won’t settle; they reroute warmth away from fungus-prone corners; they open and close vents so the district doesn’t become a single, stewing lung. Their hands are always testing: palm to pipe, knuckle to a plant-fiber panel to feel if it’s sweating. If you find a place that smells clean-bitter, like ozone and hot insulation, it’s because a heat-runner and an electrician have recently been there.

The **electricians** here don’t look like they belong in a marsh until you realize electricity is one of the few things that can’t be allowed to “just be wet.” They work in insulated loops strung like vines, in boxes warmed deliberately so condensation won’t form. Their tools are wrapped, their pockets are sealed, and they have a certain fastidiousness that reads as moral rather than practical. Their spaces have a different acoustics—less dripping, more hum—and their presence is one of the reasons fauna are scarce: nest-building and exposed cable are a bad combination in any ecosystem.

All of these people live here in layers, because the district itself is layered. Homes are not separate from work; they’re perched over it, tucked into it, grown around it. A family might sleep above the press that feeds them, and you learn the city’s social map by listening: the clicking of looms beneath a lullaby; the pump’s steady thud under conversation; the hiss of steam under a child’s laughter.

Children, when you do see them, are not running loose in the canals. They’re taught early what the district tolerates. They climb because climbing keeps you out of the wet. They learn what to touch and what not to touch: the warm pipe that is safe, the warm pipe that means scald; the vine that is load-bearing, the vine that is only curtain; the glossy sheen that is harmless moisture, the glossy sheen that means oil where oil shouldn’t be. Their play imitates maintenance—braiding scraps of stem into cords, making little runnels in the spongy floor to see where water chooses to go. Even their games are training in the city’s laws.

The old people are not pushed out of work so much as shifted sideways into the roles that require memory. Someone has to remember how a wall used to sit before it thickened; someone has to recognize the early look of a fungal bloom that doesn’t yet smell; someone has to tell whether a canal is creeping inward because of root growth or because the whole basin has settled a fraction again. In a place that changes slowly, elders are not ornamental—they’re instruments.

And then, as you stay longer, you begin to feel that something else “lives” here besides human bodies.

The district’s true mass is **vegetation**, but not as scenery—more like a partner with its own appetites. The plants here are not generic; they are a curated population selected for obedience, repair, and tolerance: species that seal when cut, that thicken when braided, that accept grafting without sulk. Their growth is guided by tensioned lines and constant trimming, but it’s still growth: patient, opportunistic, always testing the boundaries. Left alone, the district would swallow its own machines. Under care, it becomes the machine’s housing and the machine’s product at once.

Beneath that, in the wet that never leaves, are the **microbial citizens**: the films on stone, the cultures in covered vats, the root-bed communities that keep channels from turning septic. They are the quiet reason the place can be so wet without becoming a fever swamp. People here talk about them indirectly, as if about weather: “the beds are tired,” “the filters are sweet today,” “that canal’s going wrong.” Maintenance includes feeding these invisible workers—introducing charcoal, adjusting flow, scraping without sterilizing. Total cleanliness would kill the district as surely as neglect.

The fauna, conspicuously absent, still exists—but mostly as **problem** rather than as neighbor.

A few lizards are tolerated because they are small, heat-loving, and eat what little insect life dares persist. The occasional heron-like bird is less a resident than a visitor making use of a quiet, managed canal. Rats and burrowers, if they appear, are treated like sabotage. The district has an entire minor class of people whose work is exclusion: tending nets, sealing gaps, setting quiet traps that don’t poison the water. When animals do manage to establish themselves, they do it in the margins—the less-managed edge where the district loosens into marsh, where production noise fades and the city’s rules are less strict. Out there live the things the district refuses: frogs, clouds of insects, the messy abundance that would thrive if allowed. The boundary is enforced not by a wall but by constant attention.

Because this place is settled and “maintained,” the society that lives here has the temperament of maintenance. It breeds a kind of person who is suspicious of sudden change, who trusts slow systems, who measures time in seasons of thickening and thinning rather than in new construction. Their culture—if you can call it that in a place so utilitarian—is built around routines that keep wetness from becoming disaster. There are small, practical rituals: sealing a fresh cut with sap and cloth the way another town might lay a cornerstone; listening for the exact pitch of a pump at dawn; doing the afternoon trim before tendrils can reach the night’s moving parts.

If there is faith here, it’s not in distant gods. It’s in **rules that work**: water belongs to channels, heat belongs where it prevents rot, plants can be persuaded, waste can be slowed until it becomes resource. The district’s people live like that—less like conquerors of a landscape than like negotiators who have learned which concessions keep the agreement stable.

So what lives in this basin is a dense, interlocked set of lives: human trades that have become instincts; plants shaped into architecture that still insist on being alive; microbial systems tended like livestock; and, in the quietest corners, a few tolerated scavengers that slip through the city’s netting because every system, no matter how maintained, leaves seams.

And the strangest resident, the one you feel more than see, is the district itself: a maintained equilibrium with momentum, a town that behaves like an organism—slow to change, hard to kill, always damp, always working.
