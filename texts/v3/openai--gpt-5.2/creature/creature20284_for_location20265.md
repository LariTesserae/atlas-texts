What lives in the basin is not one thing but a stacked citizenship—whole populations occupying different thicknesses of the same space, each one using the others’ wastes and noises as infrastructure.

At first it seems like an ecosystem built out of absence: no water you can see, no green architecture, no obvious “primary producers.” But when you watch the shed wall long enough, or lift a plate of crust and hold it at an angle, you realize the basin is thick with makers. They’re just makers that don’t advertise themselves with leaves.

## The first residents: the films that eat rock and remember metal

Every stable surface has a skin. It doesn’t look like growth until you learn its palette: milk-wash, bruise-gray, rust-bloom, the faint purple-black you get when iron has been persuaded into a different mood. In shade it gathers as a sheen; in sun it retreats into pores and seams.

These are not plants and they don’t behave like algae. They are **mineral-eaters** and **metal-breathers**: microbial mats and crusts that trade in oxidation states the way forests trade in sugar. They live where two things meet—iron and air, sulfide and salt, grease and dust—and they make calories by forcing those meetings to keep happening.

Some of them are so thin you only know they’re there when a skitter scrapes and the mark it leaves is darker than it should be, like graphite smeared on chalk. Some of them are thicker, almost leathery, and if you peel them you find they have layers: a sun-tolerant outer grit, then a moist (not wet—never wet) middle where bound water is hoarded molecule by molecule, then a living underside that stays pressed to the metal as if contact is a prayer.

They do a second job besides feeding the basin: **they mediate the machines**. Wherever current still leaks through corroded lines, the films arrange themselves along the easiest paths the way lichen follows a crack. In places they form faintly conductive bridges—bio-mineral lattices salted with ions—so an old control box that should have died years ago still gets enough continuity to hum. The basin doesn’t keep the machinery running out of nostalgia; it keeps it running because the chemistry likes the motion.

## The grazers: animals that harvest rust the way deer harvest grass

On top of the films move the **surface skitters**, and the basin makes them look like it made the ground: pale, matte, salt-dusted. Their shells aren’t glossy because gloss is a liability here—gloss holds heat, heat drives off the tiny bit of hydration they can afford to keep. Their bodies are compact, hinged tight as closed tools, with seams that overlap so nothing escapes.

Their feet are fringed and splayed, not sticky, not clawed: the geometry of snowshoes translated into dust. They don’t run so much as *launch*—a sudden burst of momentum, then a freeze so complete their own vibration disappears into the background hum. It isn’t camouflage by color; it’s camouflage by stillness.

Their mouths are where the strangeness concentrates. They don’t bite. They **file**. A skitter will anchor itself with those wide feet, lower its head, and rasp a pipe or a crust-plate with a mouthpart that sounds like two dry stones being tested against each other. They take the films, yes, but they also take what the films have loosened: rust-flour, sulfide crumbs, mineral salts the microbes have pre-digested into edible forms.

They do not waste liquid. Their waste falls as brittle pellets or thin flakes, already dry enough to grind into the dust stream. Even their eggs are packaged as **hard capsules** that can sit for months with nothing but temperature swing and vibration, waiting for the right tremor to tell them the surface is safe.

If you watch closely you’ll see skitters **farming by accident**: they prefer certain bolts, certain corners of certain panels where their scraping keeps the surface fresh. The film regrows there faster. Other skitters learn the routes. A bolt head becomes a pasture, then a nursery, then—if it gets hollowed enough—a tiny cave whose ceiling is metal and whose floor is powdered gypsum from their own traffic.

## The majority: the burrow-swarms that keep the ground “breathing”

The burrowers are not one species; they’re a function. The top handspan of mineral is a crowded apartment of things that survive by never committing to any tunnel for long. Their passageways are meant to collapse. Collapse is how they erase scent, conserve humidity, avoid predators, and keep the surface trying to smooth itself.

Some of them are **thread-thin**, moving like animated hair through powder, their bodies lined with micro-scales that ratchet dust backward so they can “swim” through dry fluid. Others are thicker, knuckled, built like short centipedes with wedge-shaped heads that pry open cracks and then let them close behind.

You rarely see their bodies cleanly. What you see are the signs:

- seams that widen as if exhaling,
- pinprick holes that appear, sip air, and seal,
- little collapses traveling in a line under the crust like a thought passing through a brain.

They are the basin’s **recyclers and hoarders**. When a skitter dies, the burrowers arrive not in a rush but in a certainty, because any disruption—any new source of bound water, any new protein, any new metal dust—propagates through the vibration-map they all share. They strip a carcass fast, and they do it with a grim precision: not just flesh, but salts, trace hydration, the chemical potential in every joint.

And they always, always take the last thing: the **water that isn’t water**, locked into minerals and tissues. They can’t afford to leave it behind as sentiment.

## The ambushers: predators that use the rules of the place as weapons

Predation here doesn’t look like chase. Chase is expensive; it makes heat, heat costs hydration, and running in powder is like running in smoke.

So the basin’s predators are engineers of surprise and stillness.

There are the **seam-jaws**—the ones that make a crack look ordinary until it isn’t. Their bodies are flat and folded, armored in plates dusted to match the crust. They lie just under the polygon seams, hinged into the geometry of the ground, and when a grazer lingers too long the seam doesn’t open so much as *unfold*. A mouth appears where there was only stress-line a second ago, and the prey is gone into powder with one dry snap of plates.

There are also **thread-predators**, thinner, quieter: they don’t erupt, they *thread*. They can slip between the grains as if the dust has gaps made just for them, and they strike by injecting digestive chemistry that turns solid nutrition into something they can absorb without ever drinking.

And then there are the **patient ones**—sleek, minimal-contact hunters that arrive late to events. When a conveyor drops a flake of scale and the grazers converge, these predators don’t charge in. They move through the crowd like a gloved hand through sand, touching as little as possible, harvesting bodies already distracted by food and vibration.

Even the fliers have predators: something small and darting that lives in the tower’s cages and launch-slits, snatching an air-threader in mid-drift and returning to a bolt-cup nest where the prey is dismantled into dry, sortable parts.

## The air-threaders: the basin’s moving punctuation

Above it all—never very high—drift the **air-threaders**, the chitin kites. They are so light they seem like a mistake the atmosphere is making: membranes stretched over sparse struts, joints designed for pivot rather than power. They don’t waste energy flapping unless they must. They ride the microdrafts that spill into the basin and die, and when the gust collapses they settle everywhere at once, turning pipes and shoulders and platforms into temporary roosts.

Their wings make that papery clatter not as an accident but as a signal. In a place where sound is a map, their noise is a kind of public statement: *wind is changing; the surface is moving; the dust-sheet is coming.* Burrowers respond by deepening. Grazers freeze or align. Predators choose their angles.

Some air-threaders carry hitchhikers—tiny mites that live in the joint folds and feed on the membrane’s shed skin. Some are themselves gardens: their undersides grow faint bacterial smears that feed on whatever metal dust the air brings, and the threaders harvest those smears with specialized comb-legs the way cats groom fur.

They are not “free” of the basin. They are part of its circulation system.

## The machine-dwellers: life that treats vibration as weather

Inside the still-running tower and the half-sunk shed, another population lives—more secretive, more tuned to enclosed acoustics.

There are **belt-nesters** that live where rubber meets pulley, feeding on the constant rain of abraded polymer and iron dust. Their bodies are heat-sinks, built to survive the small warmth that machinery still produces. They congregate in clusters that look like wads of lint until they shift and you see segmentation, eyes like dark pinholes, mouths made for scraping grease film into edible fractions.

There are **vent-whites**, pale things that cluster near the occasional exhalations of refrigerant-cold air. They don’t like the surface; it’s too variable. Down there the temperature swings are smaller, the dryness is absolute, and the chemical gradients are stable enough to build a life on. They are often translucent, their internal fluids bound into gels that don’t flow easily—liquid made reluctant.

Some of the machine-dwellers behave like maintenance. Not intelligent in a human way, but responsive: when grit builds in a joint, they gather to eat the oxidized edge; when a motor housing cracks and leaks a new kind of air, they relocate and seed it with film. Over long time, this looks like the place “taking care” of itself.

It’s not care. It’s appetite aligning with structure.

## If there is a single “presence,” it is the listening made collective

Stand still long enough and the basin’s crowdedness stops being merely biological and starts being *behavioral*. The animals here do not just hear vibration; they **think with it**, in the limited but powerful way swarm systems can think.

A dropped tool is not just a sound. It is an event that propagates through bodies:

- Burrow-swarms shear away from the tremor in coordinated waves, collapsing tunnels behind them like doors being shut.
- Surface skitters align, not toward the noise but along the vibration’s directionality, reading it as a line on a map.
- Predators adjust position without seeing prey, because the tremor tells them where the grazers will hesitate.

It can feel, from within, like the basin is one animal with many skins—one distributed reflex.

And in the deeper chambers, where the air is colder and the machines’ hum becomes a constant ground-tone, that reflex thickens. Life there is denser, paler, more synchronized. You get the sense that the basin has learned a simple law over its long rewriting:

**everything that moves announces itself, and everything that announces itself becomes food, shelter, or signal.**

So what lives here is abundance without softness: bodies designed to seal, mouths designed to file, senses tuned to tremor instead of scent, and metabolisms that treat rust as pasture and old grease as a seasonal bloom.

The basin is not haunted by emptiness. It is inhabited by an economy—dry, efficient, endlessly busy—where even the machines are no longer artifacts but organs, and the loud, tireless hum is just another kind of weather that the residents have evolved to harvest.
