What lives there is not *a* people or *an* animal so much as a residency status you can fall into: anything that can tolerate damp warmth, dimness, and crowding eventually finds a way to become part of the construction. The place is a city in the reef-sense—built by bodies, inhabited by bodies, and continuously re-inhabited as materials turn from “structure” back into “food” and then into “structure” again.

Still, there are makers. There are always makers.

## The primary residents: the builders as a guild, not a species
At the largest scale the “mountain range” is the fossilized habit of a set of eusocial or near-eusocial animals—several lineages braided together by long advantage. You can’t point to one and say *that* built it, because each does a kind of building that becomes the other’s substrate.

### The Mat-Workers (the ground that walks)
Deep in the composite’s thickness live the animals that manufacture “ground” in the most literal way: compacting, chewing, and excreting the fiber-mineral paste that becomes the load-bearing mat.

They are not elegant. They are numerous.

- **Form:** soft-bodied or armor-light, many-segmented, with broad grinding mouthparts and symbiotic gut flora. They move like slow ripples through the layers, never needing open air for long.
- **Product:** pellets and ribbons of processed plant fiber bound with microbial slime and fungal filaments; over time their output cures into the brick-hard terraces and springy hammocks you described.
- **Behavior:** they don’t “plan” terraces, but they follow pressure gradients—heat, humidity, carbon dioxide, vibration from traffic above. Where bodies pass, they thicken; where water wants to pool, they seal; where wind dries, they mineralize.

You rarely see them as individuals. You see their *work*: the consistent grain in the floor, the way a ramp has a direction like wood does, the way older strata show long, tidy lamination—as if the land were being 3D-printed by a million stomachs.

### The Weavers (the tensile skeleton)
If the Mat-Workers are the compressive mass, the Weavers provide tensile integrity: the ropes, curtains, and cables that let the whole thing hang and vault.

- **Form:** caterpillar- and spider-adjacent animals—silk glands, leg-hooks, an instinct for anchoring lines across voids.
- **Product:** silk blended with shredded hair, plant down, and fungal hyphae, then lacquered by other residents into something halfway between web and rebar.
- **Role:** they start the airy parts: underhangs, suspended corridors, the first scaffolds that later get plastered into tunnels. In wet districts their silk is a moisture trap; in windy districts it becomes a windbreak that allows other layers to persist.

Where you see a ceiling that looks “paper-laminated,” it’s often silk mat thickened by decades of re-layering and sealed with resin until it behaves like bark.

### The Plasterers and Resinators (the weather-skin)
Exposed surfaces don’t survive on fiber alone. The ridges that wind hardens and the lips rain polishes belong to animals that specialize in sealing.

- **Plasterers:** termite-like, using saliva and chewed pulp to smooth, curve, and patch. They are the reason edges round rather than shatter: they constantly erase beginnings of cracks.
- **Resinators:** beetle- or wasp-like, harvesting sap and exuding their own resins. Their contribution is the subtle shine on frequently wetted flow paths, the way water “chooses” the same gutters because the gutters are literally varnished into preference.

These groups are often most active at dusk and during fog: when the skin is damp enough to work but not so wet it slumps.

### The Gardeners (fungus and root as citizens)
The most important “animal” in the place may be fungal.

Fungus isn’t just rot; it’s mortar, digestion, and climate-control:

- **In the dark mass:** thick, ropey hyphae that stitch pellets into something that can bear weight without crumbling.
- **In the cistern chambers:** pale mats that act like living filters, cleaning captured rain and condensation; their metabolism adds gentle heat and steady humidity.
- **In old strata:** mineralizing species that turn droppings and dust into the claylike tang you smell—organic stone made slowly.

Plants, too, are not scenery. Moss skins, epiphytes, and vine-cables are treated by the builders the way bone is treated in a body: opportunistically incorporated. Roots become stitches. Vines become guy-wires. Moss becomes insulation.

In the oldest districts, the “canopy” isn’t above the city; it’s the city’s outer layer, alive and self-repairing.

## The city’s visible wildlife: commuters, tenants, grazers
Because the structure makes infinite niches, it supports an overwhelming amount of non-building life—species that live *in* the architecture the way reef fish live in coral.

### The Wing-Noise (pollinators, scavengers, messengers)
The air is busy because the city makes airspace safe: endless ledges, hollows, and escape routes.

- **Small fliers** (gnat clouds, moth-like pollinators, tiny wasps) treat the place as a continuous interior. They feed on fungus exudates, sap leaks, and the constant film of organic dust.
- **Larger fliers**—batlike mammals or birdlike things with broad wings—use the corridors as wind-sheltered highways. Their calls bounce and multiply in the curved walls, turning navigation into echolocation or call-and-response.

Some of these fliers are effectively *postal systems*: they carry spores, seeds, mites, and pheromone traces between basins. The city spreads information the way it spreads everything else—by hitchhiking.

### The Grazers (keepers of the plazas)
Those bowl-like openings don’t stay open by accident. They’re maintained—by teeth.

There are grazers adapted to living on structure-grown vegetation: animals that can digest moss, tender fern, lichen-cement, and the sugary films produced by the resinators’ cultivated microbes.

- In bright bowls: nimble, many-toed climbers and hopping herbivores keep growth cropped low.
- Along causeways: slower, heavier browsers scrape surfaces clean enough that traffic stays efficient.

Their droppings are not waste in this economy; they are aggregate. Pellets harden floors. Minerals concentrate. Seeds get planted in protected cracks.

### The Amphibian Chorus (cistern residents)
Where the city “holds” water, it holds bodies that require it.

Frogs and salamander-like animals live in the saturated chambers—places too wet for cured surfaces and too dark for much vegetation. Their eggs cling to wax-lined troughs; their skins are part of the humidity cycle. Many are ambush predators of the endless insect traffic; in turn they are prey for almost everything larger.

Their repetitive notes aren’t just mating calls—they’re spacing signals. In a city where visibility is poor, sound becomes territorial architecture.

### The Burrowers (the city’s immune system)
Not all residents build upward. Many live by *preventing* ruin:

- **Detritivores** shred dead plant skins and old fiber, feeding the Mat-Workers’ supply lines.
- **Mite and beetle swarms** patrol for mold blooms that would weaken load-bearing layers.
- **Wormlike recyclers** convert soft rot into stable humus-like binder that fungi can stitch.

These creatures are the reason collapse is “patient.” They eat failure before it becomes catastrophe, turning weakened material back into usable paste.

## The dangerous residents: hunters that learned the architecture
A structure this occupied grows predators the way a well-stocked sea grows sharks.

- **Mantid- and centipede-analogues** live in the ribbed stair-grooves and along drip-lines, using water traffic and insect traffic as the same thing: predictable movement.
- **Snake- or eel-bodied climbers** haunt the vine-cables and underhang gardens, moving through places too narrow for the big fliers to turn.
- **Gliding mammals or lizards** exploit the dim plazas—launching from cladding to cladding, taking advantage of the brief moments when the world opens into “outside.”

Predation here rarely looks like a chase. It looks like the architecture itself suddenly having a mouth.

## Social life, if you look long enough: the city as a set of moods
If there is intelligence here, it doesn’t sit behind eyes at the top of a head. It is distributed: pheromone gradients, vibration codes, airflow management, daily rhythms that behave like decisions.

Different districts feel like different “polities” without ever being nations:

- **Wind ridges:** austere, hard-skinned, constantly maintained; traffic is fast, paths narrow; builders favor resin and mineralization.
- **Fog basins:** lush, soft, sponge-thick; nurseries and fungus gardens dominate; the air is sweeter and warmer.
- **Old cores:** labyrinthine, quiet in a way that’s ominous rather than empty; here the mass is so thick the city’s heat and breath create their own microclimate.

Occasionally the place produces events you could mistake for intent: a corridor gradually abandoned and another reinforced; a cistern suddenly sealed and rerouted; a terrace thickened as if preparing for something. But it’s not prophecy. It’s feedback—millions of bodies obeying simple rules until the result looks like strategy.

## What the “citizens” ultimately are
Taken together, the residents form a living material cycle:

- plants and canopy skins capture light and moisture,
- fungi and gut-symbionts convert it to bindable matter,
- builders turn matter into terrain,
- tenants turn terrain into nutrient and pressure,
- predators prune the excess,
- decomposers prevent rot from becoming collapse.

So the truest inhabitant is the composite itself: a landform made of relationships. The animals are the hands, the fungus is the mortar, the plants are the cladding, and time is the only architect with a full map.

Stand still long enough and the city stops reading like a mountain range and starts reading like a single organism—one that doesn’t know you’re there, because it’s busy being a place.
