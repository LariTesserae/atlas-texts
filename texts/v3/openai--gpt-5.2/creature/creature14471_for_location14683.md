What lives here begins so small you could mistake it for chemistry.

On the exposed plazas—the white, bone-bright plates that crack into polygons—the first citizens are films: microbes and micro-lichens that don’t so much “grow” as insinuate themselves into the thin boundary where air becomes ground. In the day they look like nothing, a slight dulling of glare. At night, when the crust relaxes and the salts turn faintly tacky with stolen humidity, the films wake. They breathe in brief pulses. They make acids in quantities too small to feel, but enough to roughen a crystal edge, enough to turn mineral flour into a meal. They live on light and dust, and on the fact that even the driest air is never truly empty.

Those films are not alone. Threaded through the top centimeters is a mesh of hyphae—fungal and fungus-like things whose genius is patience. They are the accountants of the basin. They reach from root to root, from plant to plant, to trade what can be traded in a world where water cannot circulate openly: nitrates scavenged from ash; trace metals; sugars leaked at night from plant tissues; the briefest condensation captured in pores of salt-loving cells. They don’t make roads, but they make the agreements that roads depend on. Where they thicken, the crust holds together more like soil. Where they fail, the plates fracture and the city’s geometry shifts.

Then come the builders you can actually see.

The columnar “trees” are not trees in the familiar sense, but convergences: several lineages that have independently learned the same architecture because the basin rewards it. One common form is a ribbed pillar—photosynthetic cortex over a woody core—that rises straight, not from ambition but from crowding. Its skin is waxed and mineral-dusted, pale green in shade and almost silver where sun strikes. It has few broad leaves; instead it wears clusters of thick, spooned blades held tight to the trunk like shutters. During the day those shutters seal. At night they loosen, not opening wide but cracking just enough to drink the air’s private humidity.

Another foundational species grows as a lattice rather than a column. It sends up flexible rods that cross and fuse wherever they touch. The fusion is not incidental: the plant responds to pressure and shade by thickening contact points, laying down lignin-like tissue in rings, turning touch into structure. Over decades those fused intersections become arches; over centuries, vaults. This is how the “streets” get their interior sky: not by clearing, but by load-bearing growth that lifts the canopy and keeps it there.

The vines that wrap the columns are the basin’s rebar. They begin soft—green cords that spiral upward in the dimness, hunting for light and tension. As they age they harden into rope-wood, impregnated with resin and dust, their outer layers crystallized with a faint bloom of salts. In a humid forest, a vine competes with its host; here it becomes a brace. Some of them are obligate: they cannot stand alone, and the columns, paradoxically, stand better with them.

Not all plants are structural. The most socially important vegetation is small and discreet: “dew-well” thickets, the ones the browsers visit at dawn.

These are halophytic sponges—low shrubs and tufted rosettes whose tissues include deliquescent chambers. In the evening they taste the cooling air the way a tongue tastes food, pulling moisture not as droplets but as a softening of salts inside their cells. By midnight they are subtly swollen. By predawn they glisten in places you only see if you kneel: not free water, but a slickness, a chemical shine under leaf lips and along the edges of specialized pads. They cannot afford to give much away, so they give it in a form that evaporates slowly and can be licked faster than it can be stolen by sun.

Around the dew-wells grow sentinels: thorned, bitter plants that don’t collect much moisture but defend the collectors. They are the basin’s gates without hinges. Their spines are not only physical; they are mineralized, sheathed in silica and salts, hard enough to wear down teeth. Their leaves contain alkaloids that bind water in tissues in ways that make the leaf feel hydrating for an instant and then punishingly dry. The browsers learn the boundaries early.

The animals that move through this city are shaped by the same law as the plants: keep your water inside, keep your surfaces honest, waste nothing.

The low browsers—there are several kinds, filling the roles that antelope, goats, and rodents fill elsewhere—tend to be narrow-bodied and long-legged, built to thread between buttresses without brushing. Their hides carry a permanent dusting: not dirt, but salts and mineral flour worked into the outer hairs and skin oils. That coating is insulation and theft-deterrent; it reflects some sunlight, and it makes the animal taste like the ground. Their nostrils are complex, lined with folded turbinates that reclaim moisture from every exhale. Their droppings come out as dry pellets encased in a thin mineral glaze: waste made instantly less interesting to flies and fungi, nutrient locked until a rare humid night lets microbes crack it.

Their mouths are picky instruments. Many have split upper lips or comb-like incisors for shaving leaf margins—where the wax is thickest and the water cost to the plant is highest, but the bitter mineral load is lowest. They avoid cores and stems that store salts. When they do take stems, it is often from species that store salts in sacrificial tissues, essentially offering briny, low-value parts to be eaten so the rest can live.

You can follow the browsers without seeing them, because their movement maintains the city’s floors. The “streets” are tamped not by design but by centuries of hooves and feet seeking the same efficient corridors. In some of the oldest corridors, the crust has been polished to something like ceramic, and the fungal mesh beneath is thicker—fed by generations of manure dust, skin flakes, and dropped seeds.

Predators exist, but they are constrained by the same density that makes ambush possible. They tend to be slim, quiet, and precise—more like weasels, cats, and sighthounds compressed into one shape than like bears or lions. Their paws are broad and soft to distribute weight on brittle plates. Their fur is short and holds dust the way the browsers’ coats do. Their eyes are large, tuned to the green-gold dimness. Some hunt alone, ghosting through root-arches; others hunt in pairs with minimal signaling, using the city’s narrowings and thorn-gates as funnels. They do not chase far. A long chase is a water debt.

There are scavengers too, and they are some of the cleverest organisms here—not tool-users, but pattern-users. Small, sharp-faced omnivores with cheek pouches and desiccation-proof gut linings patrol the plazas at dawn, collecting fallen fruit, insect molts, bits of shed bark. They cache food in the coolest parts of the root-vaults where humidity lingers longest, and they mark those caches with musk that carries information in an air that doesn’t like to carry much.

In the canopy, the city becomes lighter and more communal.

The gliders are common: membrane-winged creatures and stiff-sailed ones, launching from high forks where the fused branches create platforms. Their bodies are flattened, their bones (or bone-analogues) lightened with hollow struts, their skin tight and waxed. They ride the basin’s slow thermals the way fish ride currents, circling without flapping much because flapping is heat and heat is water loss. They feed on canopy fruit, on sap-swellings that ooze resin rather than liquid, and on insects.

And there are many insects—though “insect” here covers a dizzying range of small arthropods, some familiar in outline and others not. Their wings make the sustained fizz you hear in shaded streets. Most are armored against desiccation: thick cuticles, spiracles that open in timed pulses, bodies that tolerate high salt loads. Some specialize in the city’s resins, drinking from hardened droplets and carrying fungal spores on their legs. Others are pollinators that work at night, when flowers open in brief, cool bursts and close again before sunrise can charge rent.

The nests in the upper canopy—the hanging neighborhoods—are not singular species’ projects so much as living composites. A common form begins with a plant: a bulb of interwoven twigs that grows as a response to repeated perching and pecking. The plant thickens where weight is applied, producing more side-branches, making a natural basket. Birds—or bird-analogues—bring in strips of bark and dried sap, not “building” so much as feeding the basket. Resin seeps and hardens, dust adheres, fungi colonize the seams, and over time the nest becomes a semi-permanent structure with its own microclimate: slightly cooler, slightly more humid, a pocket where eggs can develop without turning to jerky.

Some canopy dwellers never come down. They sleep in those bulbs, feed on canopy resources, drink by licking the same dawn slickness from leaves that the browsers use below. Their droppings, falling in fine dry scatter, fertilize the streets and vaults. The city is vertically integrated in the bluntest biological sense.

There are also the creatures you rarely notice because they are engineered to be overlooked: bark-skim lizards with salt-shedding scales; stick-thin ambush hunters that mimic vine-cords; burrowers that live in the mineral flour beneath plazas, where temperature swings are muted. The burrowers are especially important. They churn the soft ash, they create voids that let humidity persist a little longer, and they carry fungal threads and seeds from one neighborhood to another in their fur and droppings. Their burrows collapse and heal in days because the crust is alive with crystallization, but in those days they are oxygen channels, root invitation, microbial condos.

Even death is organized here.

A fallen column does not rot in the familiar wet way. It dries, slowly, becoming an archive of sugars locked behind resin and wax. Then the dry decomposers arrive: beetle-like larvae with gut symbionts that can digest lignin without much water; fungi that grow as powdery blooms, sporulating in clouds so fine they behave like dust; mites that graze those fungi and in turn feed larger predators. The carcasses of animals are treated similarly. Scavengers strip what is easily taken. Then the rest becomes a dry resource defended and parceled out by territorial insects and by the invisible chemistry of salts that deter some decomposers and invite others.

Under all of it, the deepest residents are the ones that keep the basin from collapsing into mere mineral: the mycorrhizal networks and root symbionts, the nitrogen-fixers tucked into nodules, the archaea that treat salt as normal and turn chemical gradients into food. They are why the city is stable without being static. They are why a place with no public water can still be green on a continental scale.

If you spend enough time here, you start to see that the “governance” of the metropolis is not a metaphor. It is literal feedback made flesh.

- Plants that are rubbed by passing bodies respond by thickening, turning traffic into walls.
- Animals that overbrowse trigger chemical shifts in leaf tissues, turning a neighborhood bitter until herds reroute.
- Salts that swell under a plaza push up plates, and those lifted edges become seed-traps, and those seed-traps become thickets, and the plaza shrinks—not by decree, but by physics recruited into ecology.

So what lives here is not one emblematic creature or one dominant tree. What lives here is a dense, negotiated machinery: organisms that treat moisture like currency, shade like shelter, touch like architecture, and dust like the medium everything else must travel through.

And if you listen closely—close enough to hear the soft tick of crystals and the paper-rub of leaves—you can hear the true majority: countless small lives doing their quiet work in the thin, precious interface between air and salt, making a continent-sized city out of nothing more than light, mineral, and the refusal to let water be wasted.
