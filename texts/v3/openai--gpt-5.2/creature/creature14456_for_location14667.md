At first you insist—because your own lungs insist—that nothing *could* live here. Not in the thin, abrasive air. Not in the drought that doesn’t feel like drought because it never changes. Not under a ceiling that denies weather and under a sound so constant it turns thought into something you do with your hands.

Then you notice that the place is not merely built; it is *kept*. And keeping is a kind of inhabitation.

### The first residents: the keepers you don’t see
The most obvious inhabitants are the ones whose presence is only legible as maintenance.

Handrails that have been re-smoothed where centuries of palms would have worried them down to splinters of geometry. Thin metal straps re-peened into old holes with a newer rhythm of hammering—newer meaning only that the oxidation is a different dullness. Hairline cracks stitched with inlays of the same pale composite, mixed fresh and tamped in so carefully the repair reads like a vow: *the seam will not be allowed to become a gap*.

In a few places, dust has been intentionally disturbed into flat, repeatable fields—cleaned not to remove it, but to make it readable. Not writing, exactly: more like a surface prepared to catch the next message of feet.

You learn to look for what’s been prevented. The blocked slot where a whistle would have gone sharp. The added baffle that turns a scream of wind into a note that fits the chord. The discreet buttress that denies a slow sag the right to become collapse.

Whoever lives here lives as a refusal of drift.

### When you finally meet them: a people shaped by constant sound
If there are bodies moving through this labyrinth, you rarely see them head-on. The architecture is too close, the light too sideways, the lanes too full of corners. You see them the way you see fish in deep water: by the change in what’s between you and the world.

A shoulder brushes past at an angle; a sleeve takes dust from a wall and leaves it on cloth. A shape pauses in a slot of light, and the dust around it changes how it falls.

They tend to be wrapped—layered textiles, not for warmth so much as for *boundary*. Cloth is the only soft thing that can exist here without advertising its own decay, and they treat it like skin you can choose. The folds are practical: pockets for tools, pads for knees, bands that can be tightened to turn a limb into a brace.

Their faces, when you catch one, aren’t dramatic. The drama is in what they ignore.

Many have their ears covered or filled, not because it makes the place quiet (it doesn’t), but because it makes the sound *consistent inside the skull*. They don’t seek silence; they seek a steadiness they can use. Some wear thin hammered plates at the throat or sternum—resonant surfaces tuned to particular bands of the place’s chord—so that information can be felt, not heard: a tiny change in vibration becomes a tactile syllable.

Their language is mostly not airborne. It’s tap and press: knuckles on stone, fingertips on the inside of a wrist, a short squeeze at the elbow that means *wait*, a long drag across a palm that means *danger in the next lane*. When they must “speak,” they do it cheek-to-cheek, mouth near cartilage, as if whispering into a drum.

And because the chord never stops, they are never startled by sound. What startles them is **variation**: a note that bends, a hum that roughens, a faint new overtone that doesn’t belong. Those are emergencies. Their whole culture has the sensitivity of a tuning fork.

They don’t walk loudly. They walk *informatively*: bare feet or thin soles on the pale composite, not to be quiet (quiet is impossible), but to keep their bodies in contact with the architecture’s truth. A keeper who can’t feel the wall’s tremor is half-blind.

### What they do: custodianship as livelihood, and livelihood as law
They live in cavities nested into the construction—little stacked hollows where the heat is most even and the vibration is cleanest. “Homes” aren’t marked by doors so much as by patterns of obstruction: a deliberately placed slab that turns a straight lane into a respectful detour; a curtain of overlapping metal strips that clatter in a private way; a resonant plate that changes the local chord slightly, so you can *feel* you’ve come to someone’s threshold.

They spend most of their lives doing three kinds of work:

1. **Tuning the breath of the place.**  
   Somewhere in the architecture, air is being driven—by altitude differential, by engineered vents, by a pressure that is never allowed to become weather. The keepers move through the throat-slots and chambers and adjust the apertures by fractions: sliding shutters, changing the angle of a baffle, swapping one worn bracket for another. Their tools are simple, but their *measurements* are embodied. They can tell a tenth of a turn by what it does to their teeth.

2. **Repairing tightness.**  
   Any gap is a future. Futures are what this place is designed to deny. The keepers pack seams, replace sagging blocks, re-seat arches, remove dust where it threatens to become grit that will grind something open over centuries. Their repairs are not attempts to return to an original state—there is no myth of pristine beginning here. There is only the state that is agreed upon now.

3. **Managing water as if it were contraband.**  
   There is almost none. So they make it, slowly, the way you make a fact into a tradition: by collecting breath, by condensing what little vapor can be coaxed from warm stone into cooler ceramic, by keeping minute reserves in sealed vessels that are handled with the seriousness other cultures reserve for relics. Water is not drunk casually; it is allocated. It is sometimes exchanged as pledge. To spill it is not sin but sabotage.

They do not farm. They do not hunt. They do not “go out” much, because outside is not home and not stable. When they must leave, they travel in pairs, tethered by cloth or cord, using touch to keep each other located in the noise.

### How many are there?
Fewer than the place implies.

The construction has cathedral ambition, but the population feels like a handful of hands maintaining an engine too large for them. You get the sense of lineages that don’t grow—families that keep their numbers within what water and equilibrium allow. Children, if any, are raised like apprentices to the chord: taught to place a palm on a vibrating wall and tell whether the note has “warmed” or “thinned.”

You also get the sense that the place *permits* them rather than depends on them. That their work matters, but not in the way a fire depends on fuel. More like a melody depends on musicians to keep it from becoming mere physics.

### The other inhabitants: the mineral skins that behave like decisions
The keepers share the labyrinth with something that doesn’t move through lanes.

In the seams and sheltered corners, the thin films you noticed—those mineral skins—are not just staining. They have edges. They have repeatable geometries. They “prefer” certain resonances: they appear where the chord’s pressure is clean, and they fail where vibrations are turbulent.

If you watch them as a keeper watches—over days rather than minutes—you see that they spread by rewriting surface: depositing micro-layers, dissolving others, pitting the pale composite into lace that is too patterned to be accident. They do not reach for light. They do not drink. They do not reproduce with exuberance.

They *persist*.

The keepers treat these skins the way sailors treat currents: not as enemies, not as gods, but as forces with habits. In some chambers, the skins are encouraged—protected by resonant plates, kept undisturbed—because they do useful work: they “heal” microfractures by filling them, they harden edges, they consume certain salts that would otherwise bloom into crust and push blocks apart. In other places, the skins are scraped away, because their lace-pitting can soften a bearing surface over centuries and change a tone.

This is the oldest partnership in the labyrinth: human-scale intention negotiating with stone-scale chemistry that has learned to keep its promises.

### The deepest resident: the place itself, if you allow it to count
Spend long enough here and you start to understand why “ground is an agreement.”

The architecture behaves less like inert matter and more like an instrument that has been taught to maintain its own note. Heat is stored and returned. Air is made to move and never gust. Dust is held in suspension as if settling would be a kind of surrender. The chord is not an accident; it is a condition.

Whether the builders intended it or discovered it, the complex has become a self-reinforcing equilibrium: sound feeding vibration, vibration feeding warmth, warmth driving airflow, airflow sustaining sound. The mineral skins are the slow immune system. The keepers are the quick one.

So what lives here?

A small, tight-limbed people whose entire social world is touch, habit, and tuning—custodians who can feel truth through stone.

A thin, patient near-life in the seams, crystallizing in patterns that look like ornament until you realize ornament is just a readable kind of persistence.

And an instrument so large it passes for terrain, whose held chord is as close to metabolism as anything in this altitude-sealed pocket will ever admit.

They all live by the same law: nothing is given; everything is kept.
