Life up here doesn’t present itself as individuals so much as *solutions*.

From below you might think the plateau is empty, and then you step onto it and realize the emptiness is only what the land shows outsiders. The residents—plant, animal, and the tool-using ones—are all built around the same premise: nothing gets to be casual about water, not even the ground.

## The thing the plateau is made of

The first inhabitant is also the oldest: the mat-network itself.

It isn’t “a plant” the way a tree is a plant. It’s an architecture that learned to sprawl without dissolving: braided rootcords, pressure chambers, heat-shedding skins, pores that behave like valves. In places it presents as pads and buttresses; elsewhere it throws up columns and arches and ribbed walls, but the real body is the connected understructure—ropes of living tissue that choose where to thicken, where to span, where to let stone stay bare.

It has a slow intelligence that doesn’t look like thought until you live beside it:

- It anticipates weather not by seeing clouds but by *feeling* the atmosphere. Pressure changes ripple through it like news.  
- It tolerates grazing the way a city tolerates foot traffic: up to a point, and then it changes the rules—hardens, slicks, closes, opens, redirects.  
- It treats carcasses and waste as delayed rainfall: not something to pounce on, something to *schedule*. Roots don’t rush. They arrive when the fast eaters have finished and the remaining value has stopped moving.

Even its “traps” don’t feel like predation; they feel like structural responses pushed into biological territory. A mat that softens under heat and load isn’t luring; it’s failing in a way it has learned to make useful. Immobilize the thrashing weight. Reduce tearing. Let the swarm-cleaners do what they do. Then drink the leftovers down to mineral.

This organism makes *habitat* the way coral does: by being simultaneously alive and load-bearing. Everything else on the plateau lives either on its surfaces, in its seams, or by timing their lives to its moods.

## The small, numerous citizens

Most of the plateau’s animal life is not charismatic from a distance. It’s busy.

### The runners and cutters
In the mineral basins where the heat scours everything, the dominant forms are low, armored, and quick—carapaced runners with jointed legs that keep their bodies off the frying grit. They move in clicking companies, always at the boundary where living mat meets bare mineral, because that edge is where food concentrates: shed skins, fungal films, windblown pollen-cakes, the occasional sweet seep from a pore that didn’t close in time.

Some specialize in the mat-surface itself. You can hear them more than you see them: wet clacking mouthparts rasping at the plant-skin, harvesting the thin microbial layer and the salt-sugars the plant exudes when it’s flushing heat. They are careful, not out of politeness but because the mat punishes careless feeding—tightening, slicking, exuding a resin that gums joints shut.

### The swarms that behave like weather
Above those, the plateau’s true biomass is in motion: winged swarms that migrate like fronts.

They are not one species so much as a seasonal consortium—some are pollinators, some are spore-carriers, some are living knives. A swarm’s sound is a pressure in the air, a constant thrumming that rises and falls as if the plateau is breathing through insect lungs.

They do three crucial jobs:

1. **They move fertility.** Pollen, spores, and the fine chaff of crushed blossoms ride their bodies and fall in streaks that become tomorrow’s green.  
2. **They enforce the plateau’s schedule.** When blossoms open after a storm band, the swarms arrive like debt collectors. The window is short. Anything that can’t bloom, mate, or molt quickly doesn’t persist here.  
3. **They keep death efficient.** The parasitic and scavenging swarms can reduce a wound to a clean white framework before the sun can pull the last dampness out of it.

The mat-network “likes” them in the only way it likes anything: it structures itself around their inevitability—growing trellises where swarms roost, releasing scents when it needs pollen moved, closing pores when the strip-feeders get too bold.

## The big bodies: grazers, gliders, and the obvious hunters

Large animals can’t afford to be delicate here. They’re built like traveling canteens.

### The herd-things
The grazers are tall, spare, and plated—overlapping scales that throw sunlight back in dull flashes. Their legs are made for crossing hot mineral without lingering; their feet are splayed or ridged to spread weight, because everyone learns quickly that the mat has thresholds. Step on the wrong vent field, press too hard, and the ground may change under you.

They don’t graze the plateau so much as *negotiate* with it. They take from pad edges and from the sparse, tough stalks that rise where dust has been allowed to become soil. They move constantly, not only following rain but avoiding provoking the mat into hardening or slicking. A herd that stays too long finds the surface turning uncooperative: pores closing, skin tightening, the taste of exudates changing from sweet-salt to bitter-resin.

Many carry symbionts: moss-films or microbial crusts along the ribs and shoulders that drink sweat and return moisture later as lickable beads. In the morning light you can sometimes see a herd shimmering, not from heat alone but from *dew they harvested on purpose*.

### The gliders
Above them, the thermals are crowded with wide-winged gliders: membrane-limbed, light-boned, patient. They don’t waste flaps. They circle on rising heat, folding and diving when they must—when they do, you hear the air tear, a clean ripping sound that makes smaller flyers scatter.

They eat many things—swarm-drifts, small runners, the vulnerable young of the herds—but more than that they are the plateau’s moving eyes. Where they congregate, something is opening: a fresh carcass, a new bloom-field, a moisture-release the mat couldn’t afford to keep private.

### Predators that don’t bother hiding
Secrecy is less useful than *control* here. The plateau is loud and open and full of movement. Predators make their living by reading patterns in that movement.

Some are vibration-hunters: heavy-bodied crouchers that settle onto the mat and listen through it. Their attention is downwards, not forwards. Footfalls, scraping claws, the drumming panic of a wounded grazer—each transmits as a different story through living tissue. When they strike, it’s sudden and economical, as if they’re not hunting the animal so much as interrupting a vibration.

Others hunt by thermal change rather than thermal presence: faces latticed with pits that register motion against a background that is *always* hot. Stillness is camouflage here; movement is a flare.

And then there are the true opportunists: bundles of legs with a central beak, scavengers that arrive so fast after a kill they feel like part of the killing. They work in frantic, efficient teams, stripping flesh, opening bones, leaving behind frameworks that the mat later incorporates—clean trellises, calcium stakes, pale architecture turning slowly green at the edges.

## The hidden communities: burrowers, renters, and things under the skin

Not all animals are built to cross the open. Many never do.

In the lee of living walls and under pad edges, burrowers make their homes in the cooler strata: blind or low-eyed diggers that tolerate resin-smell and damp heat, animals that eat fungal threads and root-sap and pay rent in nitrogen. Their tunnels lace the mat like capillaries. The mat allows them where they are useful and denies them where they are not; entire burrow-colonies have been found sealed behind a sudden thickening of root-mass, as if the plateau closed a door.

In the deepest hollows—where air is trapped cooler, where faint bioluminescent films flicker—there are soft-bodied things that would desiccate in minutes on the open flats. They come out only when the vent-pores close and the air calms, grazing on algae-glow and mold-sweet rot, leaving behind pellets that seed the next bloom of fungus.

If you want a sense of how the plateau thinks, watch what it allows to live under it. It does not merely *permit* shelter; it shapes the roster of who gets it.

## The tool-users: ridge people, mat-listeners, water-keepers

And then there are the ones who leave stone rings and fire-stains.

They are not numerous in the way the swarms are numerous, but their presence has weight because it’s deliberate. Their camps don’t scar the mat; they treat it like a host that can be offended, like a roof that can decide to shrug you off.

### What they are like
“People” is close enough to catch the shape of them, but up here bodies tend to converge on the same necessities:

- Skin that manages heat—either reflective coatings rubbed on from crushed mineral, or natural pigmentation that doesn’t burn easily.  
- Hair or cresting that can trap a little night moisture, combed in the morning into the mouth.  
- Lungs used to thin air, hearts used to long walking.  
- Hands made for cordage and for reading texture—because survival depends on knowing, by touch, whether a mat is compliant, stressed, or dangerous.

They move in small bands, not because they are timid but because big groups are expensive. Every extra mouth is extra water lost to breath.

### What they do all day
Their work is mostly about timing and listening.

They watch the plateau the way sailors watch sea-state:

- Vent pores opening in a wave means pressure is falling—storm might come.  
- A pad edge curling tighter means it’s hoarding—dry week ahead.  
- A sudden slicking of surface means a herd has overstayed—or a predator has been pressing the mat hard enough to trigger a response.

They harvest without “harvesting” in the agricultural sense. They take what the system sheds: hardened leaf-skins, resin nodules, dry stalk fiber, fungal cakes from shaded hollows, the mineral salts that crust around certain pores after a moisture release. They trap runoff in lined pits for the hour it exists, then seal it like treasure.

They hunt too, but their most important skill is not killing; it’s *arriving second*. The plateau already makes kills. Swarms already open bodies. The people’s expertise is stepping in at the safe moment—after the dangerous work has been done, before the value has evaporated.

### What they believe (without necessarily calling it belief)
They behave as if the mat can be angered, and it’s hard to tell where superstition ends and practical knowledge begins.

Certain actions are simply not done:

- Cutting into living cords.  
- Building heavy, permanent walls on active vent fields.  
- Bleeding an animal directly onto a pore cluster (blood draws swarms; swarms draw trouble; trouble makes the mat change).

In exchange, the plateau sometimes behaves like an ally. Camps placed in the lee of a living wall are cooler and less insect-thick. Burrows opened at the right edges are permitted to stay open. Some bands know places where a mat predictably “sweats” at dawn—a reliable, tiny generosity—and they treat those places like shrines without saying the word.

### Their tools and trade
Stone is common; metal is precious and social.

A dull green stone takes an edge well enough. Flint scrapes, thorn needles, bone awls—these are day-to-day. The rare copper ornaments and fishhook-shaped implements aren’t just useful; they are proofs of connection, evidence that someone walked far, found a vein, traded across multiple bands, and returned with a piece of the plateau’s hidden blood.

Their cordage is superb: vine-fiber and stalk-braid treated with resin, strong enough to hang ladders from pad rims, flexible enough to tie shelters onto stone without piercing the mat.

They mark routes with pale stone piles not as decoration but as an agreement with strangers: *this is where the ground behaves*. The plateau is too changeable to map in the usual way; you map it with consensus.

## How all these lives fit together

Nothing up here is self-contained. Every organism is a method of moving water, heat, and minerals from one place to another.

- The mat hoards moisture, then releases it in brief cooling breaths that create real dampness and false lakes.  
- The animals chase those releases, and their movement spreads seeds, crushes blossoms, fertilizes the seams.  
- Predators keep herds from lingering long enough to stress the mat into becoming inhospitable.  
- Swarms accelerate decay into usable nutrients before the sun can steal it.  
- The tool-users skim value from the edges: fiber, resin, bones cleaned by insects, water caught in hours, shade “rented” from living walls.

If you stay long enough, you stop asking what lives there and start asking what *phase* is happening.

Some weeks the plateau is all tight skins, bitter resins, long dry walks, and sharp sounds—the whole system braced against loss. Then a storm line arrives and, for a day, life becomes extravagant: blossoms over kilometers, swarms thick as smoke, herds bellowing, predators sleek and well-fed, people hanging dripping skins to catch runoff like a festival of restraint breaking.

And through it all, the plateau remains what it has always been: a living structure that treats every resident—gnawer, glider, hunter, and fire-maker—as another way to keep its water where it belongs.
