What lives in that pocket is not one kind of thing, but a way of being alive that has learned to treat dryness as a physical law—like gravity—and then to build a whole, noisy civilization inside it.

At first glance you’d think the dominant residents are the obvious ones: the swarms that turn the air into texture, the reptiles that own every warm surface, the clustered birds and bat-things that make the overhangs a breathing ceiling. But if you stand still long enough for your eyes to stop asking for sky, you realize the true majority is quieter and closer to the stone: skins that open only when the air allows it, crusts and mats that look inert until the light shifts and their chemistry tilts, roots that behave like fingers reading cracks for minerals rather than moisture.

Life here is built in layers, and each layer holds another.

### The people: a town made of collectors
The settlement’s humans (or humanlike folk—close enough in silhouette that you read them as “people” before you notice the differences) move like residents of a pressure system. They do not hurry unless something is evaporating.

They dress for abrasion and for thrift. Cloth is thick, patched, and oiled with resin so it doesn’t wick precious damp from skin. Hair is bound up not for fashion but because loose hair steals moisture and collects the wrong dust; the wrong dust can poison a store jar. Many wear mouth coverings when they work near storage—soft fiber masks that make speech a little more muffled, and therefore, in this echoing bowl, a little more polite. Children learn early the local manners of breath: turn your face aside before you laugh, don’t sing near the cool rooms, don’t weep where jars are open unless you want your tears counted against your household.

They are not “poor,” exactly—there is plenty, compared to the plateau above—yet they behave like heirs of scarcity, because they are. Moisture is not simply a resource; it’s a moral category. A person who wastes it isn’t just careless, they are indecent.

Daily work divides along the town’s vertical spine:

- **Screen-menders** climb before dawn to check the catch-screens—those angled curtains of hair and fiber stretched where night-coolness meets warm stone. They listen with their fingers: a screen with the right tension sings faintly when the updraft hits it.
- **Jar-keepers** guard the cool shadows. Their rooms are hush-temples of clay, cork, wax, and bone stoppers. They carry keys like status, because a seal is power.
- **Dust-millers** sweep gutters and bins, judging powders by taste (a dangerous expertise) and by the way they cling to a wetted thumb. They blend mineral meal with plant sap and insect protein into pastes that keep a long time and don’t demand water to swallow.
- **Cane-cutters** are licensed, named on knot-records, because cane isn’t just building material—it’s warmth-storage, resin-source, and sometimes medicine. Cutting the wrong stalk at the wrong time can collapse a whole microclimate on a ledge.
- **Roost-tenders** negotiate with the birds and batlike colonies. They don’t “farm” them so much as maintain treaties: keep nests safe from snakes, keep lighting low, harvest droppings on schedule, leave certain ledges untouched during molt.

Their governance is practical, and therefore sacred. The rules you saw posted in marks and knots are enforced by the town the way lungs enforce rhythm. There are watchers at the seam and there are watchers at the jars, but the most severe policing is communal—because everyone can smell a breach. If someone cooks too close to a collector, the air changes; the whole pocket knows.

### Their bodies, slightly rewritten by the pocket
Over generations, the place edits its inhabitants. You see it in small things that might just be habit until you’ve seen enough of them to call it adaptation:

- People here tend to have **weathered lips** and **toughened nasal tissue**—scarred by dry dust, protected by resin salves.
- Many have learned a **slow-breath discipline** that is half custom, half physiology; they speak in measured phrases because fast talk is fast water.
- Skin is often coated in a fine film of oil and dust that looks like grime but acts like armor. Cleanliness is redefined: not “free of dirt,” but “free of waste.”

They carry tools that look humble until you understand what they replace. A small metal hinge is treated like a jewel because it makes a lid reliably airtight. A knife is an heirloom because it makes cuts that don’t bruise plant flesh and waste sap. Fired clay is everywhere, and the best potters are half chemists—able to make glazes that don’t crack in the temperature swings between sun-squares and shadow.

### The nonhuman citizens: the loud majority
The “constant, crowded roar” is the town’s animal metabolism made audible by stone.

**Insects** are the mid-air bureaucracy: they manage decay, pollination, and the movement of protein. People don’t merely endure them; they work with them.

- Some families keep **resin-wasp combs** under their eaves and harvest the paper for wrapping and the larvae for food. The relationship is careful and negotiated: too much smoke and the wasps move; too much harvesting and the comb fails.
- There are **hard-bodied flyers** that rise on updrafts like living chaff. When a shadow crosses the slit above, they fold instantly into cracks. Children learn to read those movements the way sailors read wind: a sudden hush means a predator overhead, or simply a cloud passing high above the plateau that you cannot see from inside.

**Reptiles** are both vermin-control and neighborhood character. Semi-tame lizards are kept the way some places keep cats: they patrol granaries, take what they’re owed, vanish into warmth when displeased. Their claws across rooftops at dusk are part of the town’s sense of time. Certain ledges belong to certain big individuals, and you do not sit there unless you want to argue with a creature that has never in its life needed permission to exist.

**Birds and batlike creatures** make the settlement three-dimensional. Their droppings—guano, but here it has the weight of treasure—feeds terrace gardens, makes fuel-cakes when mixed with fiber, and becomes pigment for marking rules and debts. Their bodies also are moisture vessels: not water you can pour, but dampness that becomes fertilizer, warmth, and life. The town tolerates the smell because the smell is fertility. A ledge with no roost above it is a ledge that will never quite flourish.

**Small mammals** are the pocket’s thieves and pantries: they store seeds, steal dust-meal, and inadvertently plant half the town’s food in cracks. They are hunted with snares and with patient hands. Their pelts line jar rooms because fur holds temperature stable. Nothing that once held moisture is wasted.

And in the seams beneath all this—under rosette roots, inside cane hollows, behind smoke-black stones—there are **fungal threads** and **microbial films** doing the true alchemy: fixing nitrogen in a place that barely admits air, turning mineral powder into edible chemistry, making the lichen-mats pulse when the light changes. People don’t talk about them much, the way you don’t talk about the parts of your body that work without your permission. But some of the oldest households keep “starter stones”—chunks of living crust moved from terrace to terrace like inheritance, because the right film can turn a dead ledge into a garden in a few seasons.

### How everyone fits together: treaties, not domination
Nothing here is fully domesticated. Even the carved rooms feel less like conquest than like accommodation—stone that has been persuaded to hold a human-shaped hollow.

The town survives by maintaining a web of treaties:

- **Treaty with the stone:** don’t cut too deep, don’t destabilize a terrace, keep footpaths where the dust packs and doesn’t drift into collectors.
- **Treaty with the air:** smoke goes only where it will be pulled out; breath is moderated; loud celebrations happen on ledges where the pocket can “spill” sound upward toward the slit.
- **Treaty with the animals:** let the roosts be; accept that some losses are the price of guano and insect control; keep predators in balance so swarms don’t become famine.
- **Treaty with the plants:** harvest sap without killing the rosette; cut cane only when resin is high and the stalk can seal; rotate ledge-gardens so the living crust can recover.

When the treaties fail, the pocket punishes quickly. A screen left torn means a week of thin rations. A jar unsealed means sickness that spreads by shared dust. A roost disturbed means a season without fertilizer and a sudden explosion of insects that turns work into misery.

So the inhabitants become, by necessity, extremely good at small attentions. They are people of maintenance. Their heroism looks like checking knots. Their disasters begin with a missing lid.

### What they believe, when belief is practical
If there is religion here, it’s not a separate building. It’s folded into etiquette and infrastructure.

Breath is treated as both life and theft. The first lesson given to children is not “don’t fall,” though the heights demand it; it’s “know where your breath goes.” Certain ledges are considered “quiet” not because sound is forbidden, but because sound implies open mouths, and open mouths imply loss.

There are ceremonies around the first successful catch of a new screen, around a repaired hinge, around the seasonal shift when the updrafts change and the swarms begin rising in different columns. The most honored elders are not warriors but **keepers of timing**—those who know the pocket’s rhythms intimately enough to say when to harvest, when to seal, when to let the town go loud.

### The overall character of what lives there
If you could compress the entire pocket into one impression, it would be this: everything that lives there is an expert in the almost.

Almost-water. Almost-soil. Almost-sun. Almost-space.

The people are not merely surviving in spite of that; they’ve built an identity out of it. They live as if the world is a sealed vessel and every action changes the mixture. The animals, insects, and plants do the same, each in its own register—opening and closing, hoarding and spending, surging and folding back.

So what lives there is a kind of crowded intelligence, distributed across species and practices: a city that functions like an organism, and an ecosystem that has learned to sound like a marketplace—wings, claws, voices, ladders, and the constant, ceaseless commerce of dryness being turned, somehow, into life.
