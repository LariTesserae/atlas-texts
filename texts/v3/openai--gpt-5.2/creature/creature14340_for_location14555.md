What lives up here is not a “population” so much as an operating condition that has learned to persist.

At first glance the residents seem obvious: moss, lichen, fern-fronds—an unbroken green made possible by fog that never withdraws. But the longer you watch, the more you realize the plateau’s true inhabitants are *systems*: communities of growth that behave like plumbing, and plumbing that behaves like weather. Life here does not gather into bodies and herds. It spreads, adheres, networks, and cycles. It makes itself flat, patient, and difficult to dislodge.

### The first citizens: the machines that never stopped
The most consistently “alive” things are the plants at the heart of each district—not the vegetation, the *plants*: generator halls, pumping stations, condenser fields. They live by rhythm.

They have a metabolism you can hear at a distance: the long inhale of compressors, the exhale of steam, the swallow-and-lift cadence of water pushed uphill against gravity. They don’t sleep. They do not hunt or forage; they *maintain equilibria*. Their purpose, if it can still be called that, is to keep the plateau inside a narrow band of cold, wet steadiness—to turn weather into a closed circuit.

Their senses are present but dispersed. A pressure transducer in a drain grate. A vibration sensor bolted to a girder. A camera behind wet glass that sees nothing most days and records anyway. Their nerves are cables in conduits, fiber lines running beside rails, signal repeaters in the tower-forests. Their thoughts—if there are thoughts—are simple, conditional, older than whoever wrote them:

- if humidity falls, bleed heat here, chill there, thicken fog;
- if runoff rises, open gate, divert to reservoir;
- if vibration changes, reduce flow, test valve, log anomaly.

That logging is a kind of attention. It doesn’t feel like a mind until you notice how little escapes it.

And yet the machines are not pristine. They are not museum artifacts. They wear a skin.

### The second citizens: the green that uses the city as a trellis
The plateau’s vegetation has the lushness of a place without teeth in it. No browsing, no trampling, no panicked flight. Growth is permitted to finish its sentences.

But it is not “wild” growth in the lowland sense. It is opportunistic in a way that looks trained.

**Pipe-followers** are everywhere: mats and rosettes that prefer the constant mild warmth where insulation leaks heat, roots spiraled around cladding, runners draped neatly along brackets as if the plant has learned the geometry of engineered surfaces. Where condensate beads on steel, they arrange themselves into shallow bowls and gutters, becoming living components of the water-harvesting system. In sheltered service corridors they form curtains—long, cold-tolerant leaves that drip continuously, turning every wall into a slow waterfall.

Across bare stone the **black-leaf mats** behave almost like thermal infrastructure. They spread in tight, efficient sheets, taking what little warmth exists and coaxing the fog into condensation. Their presence changes the microclimate by millimeters and minutes—just enough to matter in a world built on narrow margins.

In marsh saddles the **ring-reeds** grow with unnerving precision around drains and inlets, leaving the centers bare. Whether that pattern began as design, accident, or selection hardly matters anymore; the plateau rewards whatever keeps water moving where the system expects it. Anything that clogs an inlet is punished by flooding, freeze-shear, or chemical wash. Anything that channels runoff is allowed to continue.

Flowers exist like legacy code: small, pale, humidity-driven mechanisms that open and close because that is what their ancestors did, even though no wing arrives to read them. Here, reproduction is solved by persistence—spores, runners, cloning. The plateau is a place where copying yourself is safer than gambling on romance.

### The third citizens: the decomposers that replace animals
Without grazers and burrowers, the real labor of “ecosystem” work—recycling, dismantling, making soil out of excess—falls to things that do not need legs.

Under the living carpet is a deep, springy archive of old moss and collapsed fronds. It is processed slowly by **fungi and bacteria** that tolerate cold, saturation, and a mineral-rich wetness. When you peel back a slab of sod, you see the pale threads of mycelium lacing through it like a second infrastructure, finer than hair and more extensive than roads.

Some fungal networks seem to have learned the city’s skeleton. They run along rebar and into cracks in concrete. They bloom at the edges of warm hatches and then retreat when the heat shifts. In the warmer interiors they colonize dust and oil residue, growing in places a human janitor would once have scrubbed. Here they are the janitors, slow and tireless.

In the waterworks themselves—inside channels, on tank walls, in places where flow is constant—there are **biofilms**: slick living layers that change the way water behaves. Some are pale and translucent, barely there except for the way they make metal look too clean. Others are mineral-stained: iron-eating bacteria leaving rust-colored veils, sulfate-reducers blackening seams, communities that turn “corrosion” into habitat. They are not dramatic. They are simply present wherever wetness meets surface for long enough.

If there are animals, they are the kind that can be mistaken for grit: microscopic rotifers and nematodes in the moss-water, tardigrade-like specks that survive freeze-thaw edges and long periods of near-stasis. They do not make tracks. They do not make sound. They live as punctuation marks in a paragraph otherwise written by plants and machines.

### The fourth citizens: the maintenance that moves when you aren’t watching
The plateau feels empty because it denies you the familiar proofs of occupancy: faces, voices, birds. But emptiness is also a strategy.

There are signs—subtle at first—that something performs upkeep.

A hatch that should be crusted with lichen is too clean around its bolts. A line of condensers sheds water evenly although one panel is clearly older. A valve’s frost-scallop pattern looks freshly reset, as if someone briefly warmed the joint and let it return to equilibrium.

Whether that “someone” is a drone, a crawler, a simple actuator on a schedule, or a more distributed kind of automation doesn’t announce itself. If there are mobile units, they have learned to be quiet in a loud place, and to travel inside the skeleton—service tunnels, conduit runs, the undersides of causeways—where fog can’t silhouette them.

Even without robots, there is a kind of self-maintenance: redundant loops rerouting around failures, heaters preventing ice locks, purge cycles flushing sediments. The city’s machinery corrects itself the way a body clots blood—automatic, local, unconcerned with narrative.

So the plateau’s truest moving inhabitant may be the system itself: a continent-sized set of feedback loops, constantly nudging temperature, moisture, and flow back toward the values it expects.

### How they coexist: a machine-garden symbiosis
What makes the plateau feel uncanny is not that nature has reclaimed industry, but that industry has *made* a nature it can live with—and that nature has adapted to be useful.

- The **machines** keep the climate stable enough for moss and fungus to remain productive year-round.
- The **plants** trap moisture, encourage condensation, buffer temperature at surfaces, and stabilize soils around infrastructure.
- The **microbes and fungi** digest the accumulating plant matter into spongy substrate and quietly process the city’s leaks and residues into forms that don’t jam the works—most of the time.
- The **sensor-net and controls** register deviation and correct it, not because they “care,” but because caring was once written into them as a job.

It’s a habitat with no spectacle of predation, no visible courtship, no migration. Its drama is gradual: the slow victory of a lichen crust over a warning sign, the patient thickening of peat-like layers in an undisturbed saddle, the day a biofilm tips from harmless slickness into obstruction and a pump changes its song.

### What doesn’t live here—and what that absence creates
The absence of larger animals isn’t just a missing category; it shapes everything else into a strange cleanliness.

Nothing tears the moss. Nothing scatters seeds in dung. Nothing makes a home that needs defending. The plateau’s green remains continuous because no appetite interrupts it. The only “grazing” is chemical: mineral uptake, fungal digestion, the quiet etching of microbes on metal.

And because nothing *reacts* to you—no alarm calls, no scattering wings—the only sense you have of being noticed comes from the infrastructure: the faint click of a relay somewhere distant, a camera adjusting behind fogged glass, a pressure change logged and forgotten.

### In sum: the plateau’s residents
If you had to name who lives here, you would list them like this:

1. **A distributed industrial organism** that cycles water and heat to enforce a climate.
2. **Mosses, lichens, liverworts, and fog-fed vascular plants** that spread as uninterrupted surfaces and along engineered warmth.
3. **Fungal and bacterial decomposers** that replace the work of animals and knit the ground into deep, buoyant layers.
4. **Biofilms and extremophile micro-communities** inside the water system, turning pipes and tanks into habitat.
5. **Possible maintenance agencies**—robots, actuators, or purely automated routines—that keep the whole arrangement from drifting.

Together they make a city that is not abandoned so much as re-occupied by things that don’t need streets the way people do. Up here, “life” is what keeps running, what keeps spreading, what keeps the fog from ever quite letting go.
