What lives here is what can flatten itself into a rule.

At first glance it seems like almost nothing—only the occasional darkening of a seam, the faint suggestion of green-black where two plates meet and the morning dew hesitates. But if you stop treating “life” as something that stands up, moves around, leaves tracks, and instead look for what *clings*, what *waits*, what *switches on and off*—the plain is quietly inhabited.

### The seam-skins (the first citizens)
In the joints between plates, the dark slots are not empty. They are lined—sometimes only in discontinuous patches, sometimes in continuous ribbons—with microbial films so thin they read as stain until the light hits at a low angle and you catch their sheen.

They live like varnish lives: spread, adhered, inseparable from what it coats.

Their bodies are communal rather than individual. A “colony” here is less a crowd of organisms and more a negotiated layer: cells embedded in their own secreted gel, dust grains captured and held, salts arranged like tools. When the air is dry (most of the time), the gel collapses into a glassy brittleness. Metabolism drops so far it’s almost a philosophical question whether anything is happening. Then dawn comes, and the seam breathes moisture—condensation beads, threads, briefly runs—and the film hydrates from the edges inward. For an hour it becomes active: repairing UV damage, trading ions, making pigments, fixing whatever scant carbon and nitrogen the rain or dust has managed to deliver.

In that active hour, the seams smell faintly of something alive—not rot, not leaf, but the clean, mineral-adjacent scent of wet stone that has decided to participate.

The seam-skins do not merely *tolerate* the crust’s insistence on seams and joins; they exploit it. Their gel is slightly hygroscopic. It holds water longer than bare ceramic does. It catches uniform grains and pins them. It turns the joint into a narrow ecological corridor, a capillary garden the width of a finger and the length of a horizon.

### The dew-threaders (the ones you only see once)
In certain seasons—defined not by temperature but by the rhythm of shy, localized rains—another lifeform appears like an apology and disappears like it never happened.

From the seam-skins’ hydrated patches, glass-clear stems rise. They are not plants in the familiar sense; they have no leaves and no ambition. Each stem is a thin tube stiffened by mineral deposits, so clear it’s easy to mistake it for a trick of light. At the tip it holds a bead of water, and the bead is not incidental: it is their whole strategy.

They harvest the morning by making the morning heavier. The bead grows by condensation, pulled from air that is already reluctant to give up moisture. In the bead, dissolved dust-nutrients concentrate. In the tube beneath, cells wake and feed and divide. By late morning, when the heat returns the world to its default hardness, the bead is gone—evaporated, spilled, or wicked back down—and the stem collapses into the seam, leaving behind a faint crust of minerals like a signature you can’t read.

If you find them twice, it is because you remembered the seam.

### The underhang fans (the slow architecture)
Where plates lift and form ridges, the undersides make small pockets of shade—cooler by only a few degrees, but here a few degrees is a continent’s worth of mercy. In those pockets cling the fibrous fans: parchment-colored growths with the texture of woven cartilage.

They do not photosynthesize much, if at all. They look like plants only because they are stationary and layered. They feed instead on chemistry and patience: on mineral leachate from the porous middle strata of the plates, on trace organics washed down seams, on the thin breath of moisture that repeatedly condenses and evaporates in the same sheltered air. Their bodies are laminated—each rain event lays down a new film, each drying cycle stiffens it, each mineral pulse cross-links it—so that over years they become thickened, ribbed structures that mimic the underside geometry of the plate itself.

They are not competing for space. There is so much empty crust, so few suitable underhangs, and so little energy to be gained that “competition” would be wasted motion. The fans simply persist where the physics already offers a niche, as if the landscape is growing its own felt lining.

### The endoliths (the hidden majority)
The surface is only the most visible habitat. The plates themselves—vitrified cap over porous middle over ringing base—are not inert, not to microbes.

In the porous layer, just beneath the glazed skin, live endolithic communities: organisms that inhabit microscopic voids and hairline cracks, safe from ultraviolet, buffered against temperature spikes, able to sip moisture that never becomes a rivulet. You never see them directly, but sometimes you notice their work: a faint discoloration that follows stress lines; a subtle weakening along a seam; a patch where the glaze crazes more readily because something beneath has been producing acids or binding salts.

Their lives are paced not by days but by events: a rain that happens twice in a month, a dust fall that delivers a new trace element, a fracture that opens a fresh, pale cross-section like newly exposed food. When a plate breaks, the clean chalky face is colonized first by endoliths creeping outward, not from above but from within, as if the plate is sprouting its own bruise.

### The throat-gardens (around the apertures)
The circular openings—those neat pipe-mouths rimmed with fused material—are the closest thing this world has to a “spring,” and life gathers there the way iron gathers to a magnet.

At dawn, when cooler air spills upward and moisture exhales, the rim darkens with hydrated biofilm. The seam-skins are thicker here, layered into leathery mats that can stay wet longer. The dew-threaders appear more often, and their stems are sturdier, sometimes tipped with beads large enough to wobble. The underhang fans creep closer to the lip, accepting the risk of daylight for the reward of regular breath.

And there are other presences here—still not animals, still nothing that moves with intent across the surface, but life that feels more *active* than elsewhere. Biofilms around the mouths sometimes organize into bands and rings, as if responding to gradients of temperature and chemistry. After a rain, these rings can shine, slick as oil, and for a few hours they look almost decorative—until the heat returns and they dry into cracked, matte paint.

If you put your hand near one of these mouths at dawn, you can feel the air’s damp coolness and imagine a deeper ecology below: chemotrophic communities living where minerals are reduced and oxidized, where water lingers longer, where the crust’s “finished” surface is only a lid. The apertures don’t prove an underworld full of creatures; they simply suggest that the most vigorous living might be happening where you cannot follow without becoming part of the dark.

### The rule they all obey: adhesion
Nothing here is allowed to be loose, and the living obey that as strictly as the dust does.

These organisms do not scatter seeds across open flats; they cling to edges, grooves, pores. Their dispersal happens in ways that match the planet’s preference for channels: spores and cells ride the dawn rivulets for a few meters and then lodge in a seam; fragments of dried film break off and slide until they fall into a joint; dust storms deliver both nutrients and dormant life, but the life survives only where the geometry catches it.

Even their bodies echo the place’s manufactured feel. Under magnification, the microbial mats do not spread in fuzzy circles like mold on bread. They form facets, boundaries, and branching angles that follow microfractures. They take the crack’s shape as law. A seam-skin is less a blob than a map of stress and joinery.

### What “absence of animals” does to them
Because nothing grazes, nothing burrows, nothing tramples, the living here doesn’t need defenses against mouths and feet. Its defenses are against *sun*, *dryness*, and *time*.

So the seam-skins become dark—not for camouflage, but for UV shielding. They make pigments that turn black-green, brown-purple, sometimes almost metallic. They build gels that vitrify when dry so that their own water loss becomes a kind of armor. The underhang fans become fibrous not to resist tearing by teeth, but to survive repeated swelling and shrinking without cracking apart. The dew-threaders collapse at noon not because something eats them, but because standing up past morning is a waste of material.

In places where the surface looks “clean,” it is often not lifeless; it is simply in its off-state, indistinguishable from the crust because that is the safest form to take.

### If you stay long enough, you start to notice “behavior”
Not movement across the landscape—nothing crosses the plates in tracks—but behavior at the scale of hours and microns.

At dawn, seams darken as films hydrate.
At mid-morning, the dew-threaders lift their beads.
At noon, everything tightens, pales, retreats.
At dusk, the plates tick as they cool and contract, and the seam-skins—re-wetted by the faintest returning condensation—briefly soften again, as if listening to the crust’s shifting and choosing where to thicken next.

Over months, you can see life drafting the landscape’s first softness. A seam that repeatedly runs with condensation gets darker, slicker, slightly wider—not because it erodes like a river, but because biofilm retains water, retained water mobilizes minerals, mobilized minerals change the glaze, changed glaze encourages more condensation. The living doesn’t conquer the built world. It *tunes* it, one joint at a time.

So what lives here is not a menagerie. It is a thin persistence that has learned the only viable posture on a planet that prefers assembly: stay attached, stay small, wake only when the world briefly allows it, and make the seams into home.
