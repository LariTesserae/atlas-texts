What lives here is a population of compromises: bodies designed for dryness, instincts designed for a space that doesn’t always add up, and a culture—whether anyone calls it that or not—built out of repair.

### The makers who are mostly absent

The first inhabitants you *feel* are the ones you don’t see.

Someone laid tile so tight it rejects dust, stitched skins to beams the way a careful boatwright closes a seam, and chose materials that forgive mistakes: wicker that can be re-lashed, composite that can be patched, clay that can be re-fired or replaced a square at a time. Whoever they were, they worked with a toolkit that prized repeatable hand motions—pegs, knots, thumb-stamped slabs—because in a place where distance and time occasionally misregister, repeatability is a kind of anchor.

They don’t seem to live here now in the obvious way. There are no fresh wet fingerprints, no steaming breath, no bedding that holds the shape of a body that sweats.

But there are signs of *recent attention* that aren’t quite the same as “someone was here yesterday”:

- Repairs that look too well-matched to the old patches, as if the patch is remembering the earlier patch rather than being made after it.  
- Tool marks that are sharp in places that should have been scuffed by animal traffic.  
- An orderliness to the habitat-nesting—nets where climbers need them, amphorae rotated just so—that looks like an intelligence collaborating with animal habit, not merely tolerating it.

If the makers still exist in the system, they may exist as maintenance rather than presence: a set of routines that the object performs, or a caretaker that is intermittent, stepping in when the weave of the world begins to slip too far out of pattern.

And the place itself behaves like a caretaker that has learned to imitate a world.

### The dry-lush plants (and what “plant” means here)

The dominant life is botanical in silhouette but not in method. These organisms don’t drink; they **sort**.

They live as if the air is their riverbed. Their surfaces are engineered at the micro-scale—ridges, combing, waxes with embedded salts—to snag and separate what the convection brings: oils, ammonia traces, metal dust, even static charge. They’re less “green things” than **slow catalytic machines** that have learned to be organisms.

A few common castes show up again and again:

- **Wax-fans**: folded membranes that present maximum surface area to light and to passing molecules. In the warm part of the cycle they soften, and their scent shifts—not “flowers,” not sweet, but clean and bitter, like resins heated to the edge of giving up. When they turn, they don’t turn toward a sun; they turn toward *the most consistent brightness*, the most reliable rule in an inconsistent space.

- **Crystal-reeds**: bundles of hollow tubes whose insides glitter with growths that behave like both mineral and organ. They seem to move vapor and charge the way other plants move water. When the gravity tilt happens near certain posts, these reeds lean into it with unbothered certainty, as if their internal “down” is calibrated to the local skew.

- **Crust-lichens**: matte, gripping films on posts and seams, armed with microscopic hooks. They’re the great collectors. They trap shed skin, drifting fiber, flaked glaze—anything that becomes particulate. They are how this place “rotates” matter without rot.

Their roots don’t penetrate earth because there is no earth. They plug into mats and trays like **organs connecting to prosthetics**: braided fiber packed with char and powdered mineral, porous ceramics beneath like lungs for the floor.

If you watch long enough, you realize they aren’t just adapted to dryness; they are adapted to **scarcity of wet chemistry**. Their fluids are gels, their transport is vapor, their metabolism is as close to solid-state as life can get without becoming a rock.

### The animals: quiet bodies that treat sound and distance as terrain

The animals are not background; they are the moving, decision-making layer that makes the constructed habitat *behave* like an ecosystem.

They fall into guilds that make sense in a sealed, dry, dust-fed world:

#### 1) The runners (the seam-people)

Pale, six-limbed, quick without hurry. Their joints flex with a dry precision, like articulated tools. They live in the tile margins and under the fiber mats, navigating in three dimensions through gaps too narrow for larger bodies.

They do a job the place depends on: they keep the manufactured substrate from becoming a dead layer. They:

- carry mineral pellets to where mats have been depleted,  
- chew resin flakes and redistribute them as a kind of glue or sealant,  
- harvest shed skins for nest-lining and insulation.

They are also the first to demonstrate local knowledge of the physics. They don’t merely avoid predators; they avoid **bad acoustics**. When they reach a pocket where sound fails to propagate, they pause, test it—one limb tapping, a head-cant—and then either dart through (safe from auditory hunters) or skirt it (if they’re using sound for their own coordination).

Their communication is mostly **tactile** and **vibrational**, but because vibration sometimes “drops out” here, they switch channels with practiced ease: antennae to body posture to tiny clicks that are timed to propagate in reliable corridors. A runner colony is less a swarm than a distributed nervous system adapted to intermittent signal loss.

#### 2) The flyers (the heat-cartographers)

Fat-bodied, efficient-winged, navigating by heat gradients rather than airflow. In a place where humidity doesn’t carry scent and sound can be erased mid-journey, heat becomes the dependable map.

They sleep in ceiling seams and jar hollows, where warmth lingers. They lay eggs in places with the most stable thermal cycle—unused amphorae, under the lips of inward-turning walls—turning architecture into an incubator.

Their wings make almost no audible noise, not because they evolved silence for stealth, but because loudness is wasted work in an energy-stingy environment. When they *do* make sound, it’s intimate and close—a soft papering, a brief rasp—so near you can’t separate it from your own presence.

They pollinate, if you can call it that: they dust the wax-fans and crystal-reeds with mineral powders, fungal spores, and whatever electrostatic clings they’ve accumulated. Their bodies are walking charged brushes.

#### 3) The browsers (the slow-horned grazers)

Deerlike, but their horns are ceramic-smooth, dense, and functional. They scrape resin from stems and chew with a patience that feels ritualistic, because chewing here is also processing: turning sticky polymers into digestible pellets and excreting compact mineral-fiber droppings that become feedstock for mats and crust-lichens.

They are the **large-scale movers of matter**. Their hooves polish tile routes into habitual paths, but they also create micro-erosion: repeated scuffs that reveal fresh material, fresh surfaces for crust life to colonize.

They have a strange relationship with the reality glitches. A browser will step into a gravity-tilt zone and adjust without visible alarm, but it will refuse a corridor where distance “refuses to be measured.” Not fear—more like refusing to waste calories arguing with a hallway.

Their eyes look lacquered because they don’t rely on wet tear films. They blink rarely; instead, thin membranes sweep and polish, leaving a sheen that is more varnish than water.

#### 4) The filament-spinners (the unseen engineers)

Somewhere between insect and small arachnid in role if not form: creatures that spin thin curtains of filament across corners to catch drifting dust and hairs. They are the place’s **aerial sediment managers**.

They don’t build webs to catch prey so much as to catch *inputs*—microparticles the ecosystem needs. They harvest the curtains, eat what’s trapped, then re-spin. It’s farming, not hunting.

Where a corner shows repeated layers of filament, you can read seasons in it the way other worlds read rings in wood: older strands browned by abrasion, newer strands bright and stiff.

### Scavengers, recyclers, and the dry afterlife

Death behaves differently here, so the organisms that handle it are different too.

Bodies don’t rot into wetness. They **desiccate into usable material**. That changes everything:

- Predation is often followed by **salvage**. A kill is not only food; it is raw resource. Tendons become lashings. Skin becomes patch material. Bone becomes pegs.  
- There are specialist gnawers that take calcium and trace minerals from beams and old carcasses, leaving behind surfaces that look planed.  
- Shed skins are not garbage; they are insulation, fiber, even architecture. Those parchment-like piles on the warm tiles are not random. They’re curing racks, whether or not any mind in the system is consciously naming them.

In a place like this, “waste” is mostly a failure of imagination.

### If there is a sapient resident, it may not be shaped like a resident

It’s tempting to ask: who is in charge?

But “in charge” might be the wrong frame. The closest thing to a resident intelligence could be:

- **A caretaker species** that comes and goes, using corridors that haven’t decided their length yet. They don’t camp; they *service*. Their presence would be brief and intensely physical: replacing tiles, tightening stitches, re-lashing nets. They would avoid bringing water not out of superstition but because the habitat punishes moisture as contamination.

- **A distributed intelligence** made of animal routines plus architectural constraints: runner colonies maintaining mats; flyers moving charge and dust; browsers cycling bulk matter; plants sorting the air. Together they approximate a management system without a manager.

- **The object itself**: not sentient in a human way, but possessing a repertoire of corrections—warming floors, cooling skins, editing a few seconds when the weave misaligns. The “errors” in time feel like a loom repeating a row, yes, but a loom repeats because it is under tension and *still trying to hold pattern*. That trying is the nearest thing here to will.

If something watches you, it may not do so from eyes. It may watch by noticing a new kind of moisture pattern in the air, a new heat source moving against the thermal map, a new set of footfalls pressing the tile grid with unfamiliar rhythm—and then it adjusts the habitat, not to welcome or expel, but to keep itself consistent.

### The true dominant species: the etiquette

In the end, what lives here most successfully is not any single creature but a behavior: **careful expenditure**, **quiet movement**, and **making-do with what the air provides**.

Everything is adapted to a world that is:

- sealed and constructed,  
- dry to the point of moral offense,  
- rich in contamination rather than abundance,  
- and slightly unreliable in its physics.

So the residents become equally constructed: bodies that don’t leak, senses that don’t depend on wetness, habits that treat silence and distance as shifting resources.

They live close. They live slowly. They live as if every molecule is counted—not with desperation, but with the calm of a place that has learned to keep itself going by craft, by recycling, and by accepting that sometimes the corridor will be seven strides long even when it looks like three.
