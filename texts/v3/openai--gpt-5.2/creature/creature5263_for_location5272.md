Life on the plain doesn’t announce itself as “wild” so much as it reveals itself as a workforce that learned to eat.

At first you notice only that nothing is empty. Every reflective film has a second texture moving through it, a faint rearrangement of gloss. Every dripline has a congregation beneath it, not clustered in panic but in positions that look pre-agreed upon. The creatures here aren’t posed against the engineered world; they’re posed *for* it, as if the facility grew an exterior skin and the skin grew an ecology the way a ship grows barnacles—except these barnacles have routes, schedules, and a sense of where not to be.

### The lowest residents: the films that are almost not life

The first “organisms” aren’t organisms the way your brain wants them to be. They’re layers.

Along seams, the clear pearly varnish is alive in the same way a reef is alive: a cooperative composite of microbes, exuded polymers, embedded mineral grains, and slow metabolic heat. It thickens only where the plain gives it permission—edges, rivulets, bolt heads, the lips of grates—because those are the places where feedstock concentrates: aerosolized oils caught by condensation, trace organics leached from gaskets, minute metal salts washed out of joints, deliberate nutrient dosing meant to keep corrosion predictable.

Some films are transparent and slick, built to seal and self-heal. Others are soot-dark and faintly conductive, tuned to wick charge away from exposed surfaces. In places you find milky blooms that look like spilled resin; if you watch long enough, you can see them “breathe” as pores open and close, taking in warm mist and releasing it cooler, like a living gasket.

They don’t climb. They don’t reach. They spread exactly to the hydrology the system maintains: the wet line, the drip shadow, the capillary fringe. Their borders are neat because the world’s borders are neat.

If there is a true primary producer here, it’s these films: chemo- and organotrophs that turn industrial trace inputs into biomass and into the more valuable thing—*surfaces that behave*. They are food, yes, but they are also maintenance: anti-fouling where it matters, deliberate fouling where filtration is needed, self-repair where erosion is allowed but not permitted to proceed.

### The grazers: quiet mouths made for metal, not leaves

The first animals you learn to recognize are the lickers.

They’re pale and many-legged, not because the designers (or evolution) had a fondness for centipedes, but because a wide footprint distributes weight and keeps the surface undisturbed. Each leg ends in a wet pad that holds to matte metal without clicking. Their bodies ride low, and their mouths are not delicate: a band of rasping plates, sandpaper-tough, set behind a curtain of flexible lips that can seal against a seam the way a suction cup seals to glass.

They graze the films the way cattle graze grass—slowly, with a kind of resigned appetite. You’ll see them parked at an expansion joint, heads down, tongues working in steady strokes. Their diet is half biofilm, half mineral: they need the trace metals and engineered salts embedded in the varnish, and their guts are built like settling tanks, with compartments that sort grit from digestible paste. Every so often one will lift slightly and express a ribbon of compacted pellets—already dried, already stabilized—which a smaller creature will claim and roll away as if it has been waiting.

Grazers don’t wander randomly. They travel in sheets that resemble fluid flow more than herds. They take the same “lanes” across plates, not because they remember but because the world cues them: subtle thermal gradients, vibration lines in the slab, perhaps even weak fields from the infrastructure. You can step aside and feel that you’ve stepped out of a current.

Their eyes are wet, compound, and unimpressed. They don’t look *at* you so much as they incorporate you as another obstacle that the schedule will route around.

### The canal bodies: things that swim through ankle water like it’s a deep sea

In the shallow channels, where the warm tea-colored water carries its manufactured silt, life changes shape. Everything becomes sleeker, more patient.

There are low-bodied swimmers, hand-length, with skins like oiled stone. They move with minimal splash, a rippling that barely disturbs the film on the surface. Their heads are blunt and their mouths open downward into bristled scoops. They don’t chase prey; they comb the canal like a conveyor belt, taking in water and silt and extracting the rich fraction: flocculated organics, microbial aggregates, the tiny fat droplets that ride the warmth.

Under grates, hanging in the slowest flow, are the filter-feeders. They don’t look like fish or worms so much as engineered curtains: frilled, layered tissues that spread into the current and pulse gently, keeping themselves from clogging. If you lift a grate (if anything here will let you), the underside is a city of these bodies, each one tethered, each one angled precisely. They are living strainers, but alive enough to adjust—closing when the grit load spikes, opening when the flow returns to normal.

Some species have translucent sacs filled with tiny reflective plates. When turbidity rises, the sacs flash faintly—an internal shimmer—perhaps a communication, perhaps a byproduct of sorting particles by charge. It’s the closest this place comes to “color,” and even then it’s a functional glint, like a warning label.

### The bead-colonies: animals that behave like spilled material

On the open plates you’ll see what at first looks like a scatter of fasteners—hundreds, thousands of small, dark ovals. Then they move.

They are colony-animals: each individual no bigger than a thumb joint, each with a hard shell and a soft underside. Alone, one is simple—six short legs, a mouth that can sip condensation and scrape biofilm. Together, they become a pattern that resembles computation. They flow around obstacles in arcs, they fill shallow depressions, they split and rejoin as if obeying traffic rules you cannot see.

They’re drawn to drips and to the undersides of ducts where water “weeps.” When a valve hood releases a steady bead-bead-bead, the colony positions itself to harvest. Individuals take their turn under the drip, swelling slightly as they store water in internal bladders, then shuffle outward to let another in. There is no jostling; there is an order that seems to emerge from touch and chemistry rather than aggression.

These colonies are also the plain’s janitors at the smallest scale. They pick at residues that would otherwise become unpredictable—oils, greases, stray polymers—and concentrate them into pellets or into waxy deposits that other creatures can manage. Their shells are subtly porous, hosting their own microflora; they are walking substrates, biofilm given legs.

If the place ever “thinks” through animals, these are candidates: distributed, numerous, responsive to vibration and chemical gradients, capable of becoming a moving layer of sensor and filter.

### The large ones: heat-eaters and wall-pressers

Every so often you encounter something big enough to make you mistake it for equipment.

A barrel-chested creature will be leaned against a heat-exchange wall, its flank pressed to the surface with the intimacy of a moth on a lamp. Its skin is thick, patterned with ports—round openings that dilate and seal in a slow rhythm. It doesn’t pant; it *valves*. When a port opens, you smell warm mineral air, faintly metallic, faintly like wet stone.

These animals feed on gradients. Not metaphorically—literally. They take in warm air, warm mist, warm runoff, and pass it through internal exchangers lined with symbiotic films. Their bodies are living heat sinks that harvest the chemical opportunities created when warm, moist air meets cooler surfaces. If the facility is designed to shed heat, these creatures are designed to drink the condensation that heat makes possible.

They move rarely, and when they do it’s not with haste but with inevitability. They lift from the wall, rotate, and settle again a few meters away where the temperature field is fractionally different. Their hooves—if you can call them that—are broad, soft, and leave no mark. Their eyes are small for their mass, because sight is less useful than touch and thermal sense in a world where the weather is scheduled.

Smaller animals attend them: lickers that clean the rims of their ports, bead-colonies that harvest the minerals their skin exudes, canal-sifters that gather beneath them when their bodies shed warm droplets.

In another landscape they would be megafauna. Here they are more like living radiators—part of the thermal management, whether deliberately introduced or simply too useful to be excluded.

### Predation: the procedural taking of bodies

There are predators, but they don’t perform drama.

The ribbon-like hunters are the most unsettling because of their civility. They are long—several meters, sometimes more—and their bodies have the flatness of something designed to slide through narrow spaces. Their heads are blunt, their mouths a simple opening that can expand and seal. They travel along the same lanes the grazers use, and when they enter a cluster of small bodies, the cluster parts with the ease of a zipper.

A fraction of the smaller animals vanish into the ribbon’s mouth. No chase, no struggle that you can hear. It’s as if the predator is harvesting a quota that the prey have already agreed to pay. The ribbon’s movement is continuous and unhurried, like a belt in a machine.

Other predation is parasitic and almost elegant. There are tiny attachers—disc-shaped, nearly transparent—that drift in the mist and settle on larger bodies where skin is thin. They feed briefly, then detach and float away on ventilation currents. The host doesn’t buck; it closes a port, shifts a flank, and continues. In a system where stability is prized, even harm is metered.

### The death crews: dissolution without rot

When something dies, it does not become a monument.

The first arrivals are the soft-bodied collectors—pale, sacklike animals that can extrude themselves into cracks and around contours. They secrete solvents and enzymes that break tissue down into a controllable slurry. Their work is quiet and almost sterile; there is no stench of decay, because decay is messy and mess is not tolerated here.

Then come the carriers: hard-shelled rollers that take the processed matter, now pelletized or packaged into waxy nodules, and move it toward the slots and grates that accept it. They travel in lines that match service paths. If you didn’t know better, you’d assume they were designed for this, that they’re mobile waste-handling components.

The ground accepts what they deliver. Somewhere below, in chambers that feel more like plumbing than soil, the nutrients are redistributed—back into the films, back into the water layer, back into the controlled effluent that sustains the rest. Death is not an interruption; it is a scheduled transfer.

### The mist layer: things that live in air that behaves like water

Just above the plates, in the persistent warm haze, there is another fauna that you don’t notice until it crosses the light.

They are not birds. They are not insects. They are floaters: light-bodied organisms with broad membranes that hold condensation like dew holds to spider silk. They “swim” by changing their wetness—opening textures that catch fog, closing them to shed weight, letting ventilation currents carry them along known routes.

Some cling to catwalk undersides in tidy rows, pulsing faintly as they wick moisture out of the air. Others drift in slow loops above vent fields where updrafts recur at the same hour each day. Their life cycle is probably synchronized to the facility’s breathing: they bloom where mist is predictable, they reproduce when airflow schedules align, they settle their eggs (or buds, or spores) in the sheltered damp of latticework where nothing ever truly dries.

If you listen closely during a dense fog, the finest patter you hear—so like a distant rain—may be these creatures landing and lifting, membrane edges tapping wet metal with the softness of cloth.

### The guardians that aren’t guards: animals as sensing and control

The most peculiar residents are the ones that behave less like animals and more like interfaces.

Along some hatches you’ll find ring-shaped congregations: small bodies arranged around a sweating seam, each one facing inward, each one taking tiny sips and then backing away in turn. Their patterning is too precise to be accidental. They aren’t just feeding; they’re regulating. By consuming certain minerals as they appear, they keep concentrations within bounds. They are living buffers, trained by selection (or design) to maintain chemistry.

In other places, you’ll notice “watchers” in the shadows of catwalk supports: motionless forms with large, damp eyes, bodies pressed into corners. They do not flee when approached. They do not threaten. But when you pass, a subtle change ripples outward—colonies shift, grazers pause and then resume, a ribbon predator reroutes by a meter. It’s hard to prove, impossible to unfeel: information moves through this fauna like pressure moves through a pipe.

Nothing here needs to be centralized to be controlled. Control can be an ecology.

### How they reproduce: emergence from infrastructure

There are no nests in trees, no burrows in soil. Reproduction here hides in engineered spaces.

Eggs—if they are eggs—are tucked into warm expansion joints where the damp never fails. Larvae—if they are larvae—hang beneath grates where flow is constant and food arrives pre-sorted. Some creatures bud pieces of themselves that detach and crawl away. Some may release spores into ventilation streams, trusting the facility’s airflow maps the way a forest trusts wind.

Birth is timed to schedules you can’t see: a slight change in water chemistry, a maintenance cycle that increases nutrient dosing, an annual recalibration that shifts temperature by half a degree. The young appear not as a surprise but as a new layer in an already-layered system.

### What lives there, finally: an ecosystem that functions like a subsystem

If you stand back far enough, the inhabitants resolve into roles the way components resolve into a machine—except each role is alive, adaptive, and opportunistic within narrow tolerances.

- Films convert trace industrial leak and dose into biomass and protective skins.
- Grazers turn skins into pellets and keep surfaces in their “finished” state.
- Filter-feeders keep canals from becoming unpredictable, taking richness out of flow.
- Colony-beads handle the small messes, the residues that would otherwise accumulate.
- Heat-eaters harvest gradients and translate waste heat into condensation into food.
- Predators trim populations without panic, keeping density high but stable.
- Death crews prevent rot, turning corpses into feedstock with procedural calm.
- Mist floaters occupy the air the way plankton occupy water, living in ventilation.
- Watcher-congregations sense and buffer, making biology part of regulation.

Nothing here is trying to reclaim the world from its making. The life has accepted that “environment” means “infrastructure,” that the sun is less relevant than a heat exchanger, that water comes from a schedule, that weather is behavior. The animals have become the plain’s moving softness: the part that can flex, clean, count, and heal without ever raising its voice.

And if there is an apex presence—if something truly “lives” here in the singular—it may not have a body you can point to. It may be the steady, planet-wide arrangement of these bodies into habits: a distributed metabolism laid over a manufactured surface, so consistent it feels enforced, so dense it feels like company, so tireless it begins to resemble intention.
