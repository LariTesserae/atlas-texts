At first you think the basin is inhabited the way any wetland is inhabited: by things that pass through, things that spawn and vanish, things that cling to the undersides of leaves and never need a name. Then you notice the *choices*—channels kept open against the wetland’s urge to stitch them shut, reed-fans trained into consistent arcs, the same three knots repeated like a signature. The place does not merely host life. It is *occupied*.

And not by a single kind of occupant.

### The first residents: the ones you don’t see until you stop being human about it
The basin is full of lives that don’t announce themselves as bodies.

There is the filament-work you mentioned—plant-made threads that harvest fog. Those threads are not incidental. They are tended, encouraged, *bred*. They appear most thickly where people move most often, strung like invisible lintels across narrow corridors. In still moments you can hear their function rather than see it: a faint, collective stippling as condensation gathers and releases, a damp, controlled rain that keeps certain mosses green even when the water level dips.

In the peat, fungi do what fungi always do—eat and connect—but here the mycelium is old enough, dense enough, and consistently fed enough that it behaves like infrastructure. It knits the floating mats back together. It colonizes patches of mud-plaster and turns them slightly more resilient. It “chooses” routes the way roots choose routes, except faster. People learn to read it by texture under the fingers: where it’s thick, the ground will hold; where it’s absent, the basin is about to re-arrange itself.

You can live in a place like this for a season and still believe it’s just ecology.

You can live there for a year and start speaking of the basin the way you speak of a neighbor: not sentient in a human sense, but responsive enough that ignoring it feels like rudeness.

### The visible residents: small nations of invertebrates
Insect life here is not background; it is governance.

There are beetles that are “allowed” and beetles that aren’t, and the difference is not species so much as behavior. The tolerated ones chew rot and fungus and old leaf—quiet work, useful work. The disfavored ones go for stored fiber, for woven seams, for the soft parts of sleeping-mats. People don’t wage war on them with poison. Poison is too blunt in a place where every death becomes a bloom of something else. Instead they alter routes, humidity, light.

A family will hang its netted storage slightly higher if the air is too still, because certain moths prefer the warm, unmoving pockets under roofs. They will deliberately let one patch of fungi bruise and glow near a walkway not for beauty but because some swarms avoid disturbance-lit places. They will keep a shallow canal seeded with a particular pale fish not primarily to eat it, but because it combs larvae from the water the way goats comb brush from a hillside.

The basin has “pests,” yes, but it also has employees: scavengers that keep waste from becoming sickness, spiders whose webs are tolerated because they thin the air’s constant pressure of wings, leeches that are a nuisance until you learn they can be bait, or medicine, or both.

### The mammals: the ghosts who share your streets
The sleek, watching mammals you glimpse at the edge of walkways don’t behave like intruders. They behave like parallel citizens.

They know the tether-creak of floating mats the way people know the sound of a friend’s step. They appear at the same hours, in the same corridors, with the same cautious confidence. Some are hunters that take what they can and vanish; others are opportunists that have learned the city’s rhythms—where scraps might fall, where fish are cleaned, where warmth gathers near rot pockets.

Nobody in the settlement expects to “own” these animals. Ownership would be a kind of joke in a place where your house is half vine and your street is half water. Instead there are understandings:

- Certain walkways are kept clear not for people, but because the ghosts use them, and when they have a clean route they hunt away from sleeping platforms.
- Certain nests are left alone even when they’re inconvenient, because disturbing them causes a week of predation-chaos.
- Certain eye-shines in certain places mean you turn back without argument.

Predation remains constant and efficient, but it becomes—strangely—predictable. The basin is too damp for grand violence. Everything here is close-quarters and practical.

### The people: not “marsh-dwellers,” but basin-dwellers
If you expect the inhabitants to look like a romance of swamp life—feral, mud-smeared, half-myth—you’ll miss them. They are not theatrical. They are *adapted*.

They dress in layers that don’t pretend to be dry: inner wraps that keep warmth even when wet, outer skins that shed water slowly, cords and ties instead of buttons (because fingers numb, and knots don’t care). Everything is designed for quiet repair. A torn sleeve becomes a patch in minutes, because no one walks around with a tear in this place unless they have chosen to.

Their hands tell you more than their faces. Fingers are toughened and stained; nails are kept short; palms are marked with a kind of polished roughness from pole-work, weaving, and constant contact with living surfaces. They move with the caution of people who have learned that the ground is not a guarantee.

They don’t stride. They *test*.

There is a small choreography everyone learns early:
- touch the rail-stem before you weight the mat (it tells you, in its tension, what the tether is doing);
- step where the moss is slightly darker (it means it’s fed, it means it’s anchored);
- keep a hand free, always, not for weapons but for balance, for reading, for apology to the place.

#### What they call themselves
They rarely use a single noun the way outsiders would. Names shift with context: “those of the mats,” “those under the low leaves,” “those who keep the seams open.” Identity is less tribe than function, because in a settlement this small and this precarious, function is survival.

When they speak of outsiders—if outsiders ever come—they don’t call them enemies. They call them “hard-steppers,” or “high-voiced,” or simply “dry-born,” as if dryness were a lineage.

#### How they live together: households as knots, not walls
A “home” is not one hut. It’s a cluster: sleeping-platform, storage loft, a small working deck, a shared cooking space where heat is managed like a resource too precious to flaunt. Families are less nuclear and more braided—people attached by skill and obligation as much as blood.

You can often tell what a cluster does by its textures:
- Fine, tight weave and clean mud-plaster: they make cordage, netting, careful repairs.
- Thick leaf-overlap and broader rails: they move loads, manage floating mats, handle the heavy work.
- A smell of tannin and mineral clay: they treat hides, seal seams, plaster and re-plaster against cold.

Children are not kept away from the dangerous parts. The basin is the dangerous part. Instead, children are trained early in *interpretation*: which reeds can bear weight, which vines are load-bearing, how to listen for the low *glug* that means the ground has shifted somewhere nearby.

Old age, here, is not simply survival; it’s authority. Not political authority, necessarily—practical authority. Elders remember which canal used to be a dead end before the slump, which rot pocket runs hot in late cold, which predator has cubs this season and therefore which walkway to avoid at dusk-dim.

### Their economy: not trade, but circulation
They don’t accumulate much, because dampness punishes hoarding. What they value is what can be maintained.

- **Fiber** is wealth: cordage, netting, lashings that don’t rot too fast.
- **Salt and ash** are rarities: precious for curing, cleaning, discouraging certain molds.
- **Metal** is almost myth, and when it appears it becomes communal by default—not because of ideology, but because losing it to the basin’s hungry mud would be unforgivable.

Food is a rotating agreement with the place: fish taken carefully so the canals stay clear, insects gathered in season, edible shoots and tubers cultivated on raised root-felt shelves. Meat from larger animals is rare and handled with ceremony—not solemnity for its own sake, but because a kill attracts attention, and attention can be expensive.

### Their religion (if you can call it that): etiquette elevated to cosmology
In a landscape that absorbs sound, religion doesn’t become song. It becomes procedure.

They treat the basin’s rules—don’t cut a load-bearing vine, don’t block drainage seams—as more than safety tips. These are moral statements, because they encode the truth that the settlement is not sovereign.

A person who carelessly severs tension, who makes a canal stagnant, who sleeps too low and then demands help when the cold takes them—such a person is not “sinful.” They are “unmannered.” They behave as if the place owes them stable ground.

Their rituals are small and constant:
- a smear of warm rot-mud placed on a rail-stem before a major repair, to “invite” regrowth;
- a pinch of crushed bitter leaf dropped into a canal when larvae surge, not as pesticide but as signal—fish learn, people learn, the basin learns a new pattern;
- a habit of touching the same marker-stalk when passing, leaving oils and pressure, reinforcing a path not just physically but communally.

If there is a god here, it is not a face in the sky. It is the basin’s ongoing decision about what it will become.

### How they defend themselves: by not being worth the trouble
They do have weapons—bone points, sharpened driftwood, stone blades kept absurdly honed—but defense is mostly architectural and behavioral.

The settlement is hard to find because it has agreed not to be obvious. No smoke. No shouting. No bright clearing. Paths that seem to invite entry turn slowly into constrictions where an outsider can’t swing an arm properly without snagging vine and making noise. Walkways are engineered so that those who don’t know them step wrong and get wet, and then cold, and then slower.

The basin itself is the best wall.

And if something truly dangerous comes—human raiders, a starving predator, a sickness-bloom of biting insects—the response is rarely “fight.” It’s *reroute*: close a corridor with a living bend, open a different canal, move sleeping platforms near a hot rot pocket for a few nights, starve the threat of access rather than try to overpower it.

### Their art: not decoration, but orientation
Because light is scarce, beauty becomes tactile.

They carve subtle ridges into handrails that tell you where you are by fingertip. They dye cords with clay-mineral and tannin not for color’s sake, but so you can distinguish load-bearing lashings from replaceable ones in dimness. They hang shells and polished bone not where they can be admired, but where they can *click* softly in a shift of air—tiny alarms, tiny music.

Stories are not told with grand gestures. They’re told close, under leaf-roofs, in a voice that doesn’t travel. A good story here has the same quality as a good path: it shows you what to avoid without making you feel afraid.

### The final resident: the settlement itself, as a living thing
After enough time, it becomes hard to separate “the inhabitants” from “what they inhabit.”

The city is not imposed on the basin; it is braided into it. Walkways are half-made of permission. Huts are agreements with vines. Canals are arguments with silt, renewed daily. Even the people’s bodies carry the place: skin that never quite dries, lungs accustomed to tannin air, ears trained for the difference between harmless drip and structural shift.

So what lives here?

A young, undecided landscape full of animal weather.

A fungal and root network that behaves like memory.

And a small human settlement that has learned the only workable form of urbanism in a world that won’t hold still: not conquest, not permanence—just continuous, intimate negotiation, conducted in whispers, touch, and the soft discipline of never asking the basin to be anything other than what it is in the process of becoming.
