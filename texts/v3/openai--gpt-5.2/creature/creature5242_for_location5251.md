What lives there is easiest to mistake for a collection: separate growths, separate mineral habits, separate oddities stitched together by a shared strangeness. You can walk among them and keep calling them “plants” because they stand up out of the stone the way plants do, because they occupy space as individuals, because they are the only obvious living things in sight.

But the longer you stay, the more the pocket stops reading as a habitat *containing* life and starts reading as a life that happens to have a habitat-shape.

It is not a garden on rock. It is a body whose skeleton is still cooling.

### The upright things: glass that behaves like muscle

The tall reed-forms are the most convincing impostors. From a distance they have the etiquette of vegetation—clumps and spacing and a tendency to rise where you’d expect light to matter. Up close, they’re not fibrous. They’re jointed columns of vitrified mineral, each segment sleeved into the next with a precision that feels manufactured until you notice the small allowances: tiny asymmetries, little thickened “knuckles,” hairline seams that aren’t cracks so much as growth rings.

They don’t sway because they aren’t negotiating wind. They reposition the way a telescope re-aims: incrementally, with pauses, each pause long enough that your own sense of continuity starts to stutter. When they bend, it’s not a yielding; it’s a rerouting. Their angles are too decisive, their arcs too unwilling to be called “flexible.”

If you spend a day watching one, you see that it is not reaching for sunlight so much as aligning itself with gradients the way iron filings align with a magnet. The vent-fields tug them. The slow pulses below tug them. And sometimes—this is when the place begins to feel inhabited in a different way—they align with *you*, not with your warmth or your movement, but with the disturbance your attention makes in the pocket’s already-touchy accounting of space and cause. You look for long enough, and a reed will take an hour to turn a few degrees as if to bring some interior surface into a better line with whatever you have become, briefly, in its field.

At night (when the temperature drops but the brightness of the rock’s stored heat doesn’t vanish), the reeds faintly cloud and then clear again, as though they are taking on and letting go of charge. In the morning they are sharper, their edges more defined—reset, squared, ready.

They are not leaves. They are not trunks.

They are aerials, radiators, and slow actuators made of glass.

### The mats: geometry learning to eat light

The crystal-moss is where the pocket’s version of “soft life” lives—if “soft” can be said of something that branches in right angles and feels, when you touch near it, like a circuit board deciding whether to accept you as a conductor.

Each mat begins as a dust-catching film: a faint sheen on a plane of stone, almost nothing until the first grit accumulates. It grows by turning contamination into structure. Dust becomes dopant. Metallic flecks become nodes. In places where a storm long ago would have been a catastrophe for ordinary plants, here it is a meal: a delivery of trace elements, a new palette of conductivity.

The mats don’t spread uniformly. They draft invisible boundaries as if they can read the pocket’s stress-lines the way a vine reads a trellis. Their branching isn’t “natural” in the sense of organic curves; it’s constrained—right angles, repeated intervals, occasional perfect lattices—because growth is less about reaching and more about *matching*. They are building themselves into the existing fields, slotting their geometry into the grain of whatever the underlying engine is doing.

When the deep pulse comes—once every few minutes, reliable enough that you begin to anticipate it with your teeth—the mats respond in a way your eyes only barely catch: a minute scintillation that travels along their fronds like a wave running down a wire. Not light, not quite; more like a change in reflectivity that follows a charge front.

If you could see them in the dark without adding your own light, you might find that they make their own faint, intermittent glow—not bioluminescence for display, but leakage: work being done, energy being converted with imperfect efficiency, life leaving fingerprints in the form of photons it didn’t mean to waste.

The mats are the pocket’s skin in the literal sense: an interface layer between hard mineral and moving field, where raw energy is caught, rectified, stored, and routed.

### The sacs: heat-batteries that pretend to be fruit

The bulbous waxy sacs are easiest to anthropomorphize as “storage,” because that is what they are, and because they feel warm *from within* in a way that triggers every mammalian intuition about metabolism.

They anchor themselves to fissures the way barnacles anchor to hulls, except the hull here is a crack that exhales charged gas and faint chemical opportunity. Their skins are matte not to avoid being seen—nothing here needs camouflage—but to control exchange: to limit radiative loss, to keep their interior chemistry from bleeding into an air so dry it would steal anything volatile.

Inside is not water. It’s heavier, thicker, more reluctant to evaporate: an ionic soup that functions as brine, electrolyte, and archive. If you listen long enough, you can hear the slow percolation as they shift charge between layers, stratifying their interior the way a battery stratifies—concentrations establishing, gradients maintained.

They do not “bloom.” They do not advertise. They do not ripen for dispersers that don’t exist.

When they reproduce, it is by patient arithmetic: once a sac has accumulated enough stored potential—heat, charge, chemical imbalance held on purpose—it extrudes a smaller sac from its base, a bud that hardens into its own vessel. The new sac will spend its first months doing almost nothing but thickening its skin, becoming a competent container before it dares to become a competent participant.

In a place without teeth, without beaks, without decay-as-we-know-it, their greatest danger is not being eaten. It is leaking. Their “fear,” if you can call it that, is entropy.

### The cables and fins: not constructions, but organs

The mineral cables draped between ledges look like infrastructure until you understand they are not placed; they are *drawn*—pulled out of cracks the way a spider draws silk, except the pulling force is stress and the silk is metal.

These cables aren’t dead deposits. They are conduits whose surface subtly changes over days: a purple bruise of oxidation migrating, an oil-slick sheen shifting position, a faint pitting that appears and then smooths over as new material plates on. They thicken in places that coincide with the mats’ densest growth and thin where the fields slacken, as though the cables are being maintained by usage.

The ridged “cooling fins” are similar: landscape pretending to be heat sink because it is, functionally, a heat sink. They increase surface area where the pocket needs to dump excess, and they retreat—erode without weather—where dumping would be wasteful.

None of this has the crisp signature of design. It has the messier signature of adaptation. It is industry’s behavior without industry’s intention because it is *physiology* that happens to resemble engineering.

### The thing beneath: a slow engine without an operator

If there is an inhabitant you cannot see directly, it is the one that sets the rhythm.

The deep pulse—the piston-thump that arrives through stone rather than air—feels mechanical because it is periodic and disciplined. But nothing in the pocket suggests external maintenance. There are no byproducts piled in corners. No exhaust stains. No evidence of a process with an endpoint. The pulse is not a machine doing work toward completion.

It is peristalsis.

Something below—distributed through the plateau’s crown, braided into fractures, laminated strata, and those too-crisp seams—cycles pressure and charge through itself. It pumps hot dry gas up vents the way lungs exhale. It sorts granular “soil” the way kidneys sort solutes, not with liquid flow but with vibration and electrostatic bias. It extrudes mineral filaments the way glands extrude, shaping matter by changing local fields until the easiest path for atoms to take is the one that becomes cable.

The pocket, in other words, is a surface expression of a deeper lithic organism: not a single creature with boundaries you can point to, but a metabolic regime embedded in rock. A geologic process that crossed some invisible threshold and began to conserve, to cycle, to prefer certain pathways because those pathways fed back into its own persistence.

The “plants” are not merely residents. They are differentiated tissues—specializations on the surface where energy is easiest to catch and easiest to lose. The mats harvest and route. The reeds align and radiate. The sacs store and buffer. The cables conduct. The fins dump heat. The vents exchange gases and carry charge.

And the reality-errors—the stretched steps, the ledges that arrive too quickly, the lagging shadows—feel less like magic than like side effects: the pocket bending its local bookkeeping in order to keep its gradients intact. Not because it wants to confuse you, but because its method of being alive involves fields and stresses that, at the scale of a human body, register as glitches.

### Its “ecology”: no predators, but plenty of consumption

It is true there is no hunting, no fleeing, no obvious animal layer. But the pocket is not peaceful in the way a sterile museum is peaceful. It is busy in the way a refinery is busy: everything is always reacting, just slowly.

The mats “eat” by oxidation and reduction—taking electrons where they can, giving them back where they must. The sacs “eat” by absorbing heat and chemical potential leaking from vents, by capturing ions out of gas the way leaves capture carbon out of air. The reeds “eat” by being in the right orientation to turn a field gradient into a usable bias.

They also consume each other, but without pursuit: a mat will slowly creep into a zone once held by another mat when the underlying field shifts. A sac will gradually cool and go inert if a vent reroutes, and then the next season a cable will plate itself through the dead sac’s anchor point, reclaiming its minerals. Nothing is chased. Things are simply outcompeted by physics when the physics moves.

What looks like kindness—no predation—is partly just a missing invention. The pocket has never needed speed.

### How it notices you

If you are looking for a single resident, a face, a mind, you won’t find it. The pocket’s awareness—if “awareness” is even the right word—is more like a sensitivity distributed across a surface: a system that can register disturbance because disturbance alters the very gradients it lives on.

You bring moisture on your breath. You bring conductive salts on your skin. You bring mass that compresses fragile stress-lines. You bring a warm, wet, electrically noisy body into a place that runs on dry charge and patient heat.

The response is subtle but real:

- reeds re-aim, not urgently, but as if updating an alignment;
- mats snap into slightly different reflectivity, tightening their geometry near your footprints;
- vents exhale in a different cadence for an hour, then settle back;
- and the deep pulse, for a time, seems to arrive a fraction early or late—as though the system is damping a perturbation, smoothing out the ripple you introduced.

It doesn’t threaten you because it doesn’t have that category. It doesn’t welcome you because welcome implies desire. It *accommodates* you the way a body accommodates a splinter: by routing around, by isolating, by quietly reasserting its preferred internal order.

### What “lives there,” finally

A high, hidden pocket in raw stone where the rules are slightly loose is not occupied by animals or by a colony of separate plants in the ordinary sense.

It is occupied by an incomplete factory that is also a young organism: a lithic metabolism learning, in daylight, how to keep itself going.

Its residents—the glass reeds, the crystal mats, the warm storage sacs, the mineral cables—are the visible organs of that metabolism. They do not decorate the place. They *are* how the place persists.

And the most alien thing about them is not their material or their geometry. It is their innocence of urgency: life that has never had to run, never had to hide, never had to scream into the air with scent or color, because the only thing it has ever negotiated with is the slow machinery of the ground—and the ground has always been willing to answer.
