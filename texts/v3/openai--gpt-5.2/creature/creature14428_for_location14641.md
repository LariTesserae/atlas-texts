They are not a people who “came up” so much as a people who learned to be *up*.

At first, from a distance, the inhabitants read as the simplest kind of life in a hard place: movement in lines, smoke held close to stone, herds folding and unfolding across ridges. The plateau seems to be doing what plateaus always do—making everything spare, practical, and quiet.

Then you get close enough to see how much is being carried, not on backs but in habits.

### The high-city folk: built for thin air, built for staying
Most are compact in the way cold climates tend to shape bodies: dense torsos, thick necks, wide ribcages, limbs that don’t waste heat. Their faces show the plateau’s two daily violences—light and wind. Cheeks are wind-burnished; eyes narrow easily; skin is treated with tallow and ash and powdered stone to keep it from cracking. Hair is kept braided or bound, not for fashion first but because loose hair is something the wind can use against you. Breath is always part of the scene: people speak on exhale and learn to hold words the way they hold heat.

They do not move like lowlanders. They climb without drama. They pause automatically at thresholds where the gusts change character. Their bodies have learned the plateau’s small taxes: don’t sprint unless you must; don’t waste sweat; don’t carry water where you can carry ice.

But what’s most distinctive is not physiology—it’s their relationship to *infrastructure* as if it were kin.

A child here learns “up” and “down” as moral categories before learning them as directions. Up is exposure, down is shelter; up is watchfulness, down is hoarding warmth. A child also learns the city’s second language early: the language of signals—cloth, whistle, gesture—because words are fragile in thin air and wind.

### They live in layers the way lichens do
Households are not simply families. They are heat-sharing units, work-sharing units, and—most importantly—water-accounting units. A “home” might include blood relatives, in-laws, apprentices, an elderly neighbor no one would allow to burn alone through winter, and a pair of teen herders who sleep there between drives. Privacy exists, but it’s negotiated like any scarce resource.

Rooms are half cut into stone. Doorways are dog-legged to trap warmth. Sleeping platforms are raised and ringed with low stone lips that keep blankets from wandering in the night drafts. On the inner walls you see the same thing again and again: ledges with smooth depressions where hands always touch, niches for measure-stones, pegs for tools whose shapes are standardized because you can’t afford bespoke nonsense when repairs are constant.

In the warmest corners, you find the city’s most telling luxury: not gold, not cloth, but *stillness*. A room that doesn’t whistle. A corridor where a candle flame stands straight. An alcove where your joints stop bracing.

### The plateau’s social order: elders of water, guilds of stone, and a quiet enforcement
No single crown makes sense here; wind and gravity are stronger than any ruler. Authority expresses itself as maintenance.

The most powerful figures are not generals but **keepers**:

- **Cistern-elders** (or whatever title the dialect gives them) who hold the measurements and the seasonal memory: how much ice was stored last winter, how quickly the shaded basins are thinning over decades, which ridge’s drip points have shifted since the last quake. They do not “own” water so much as embody the city’s pact with it. Their judgments are treated like weather forecasts: not personal, but final.
- **Stonewright guilds** who can read a crack’s intentions. They are part engineer, part diviner. They know which walls can be thickened and which must be abandoned; which talus slopes are merely noisy and which are hungry.
- **Wind-readers**—watchers trained to notice the plateau’s subtle pre-storm tells: the change in cloud stacking, the drop in scent, the pitch of rope-hum. They schedule markets and crossings the way sailors schedule by tide.

Enforcement is mostly communal and mostly preventative. You don’t steal water here because you can’t hide it. You don’t hoard heat because everyone can smell it. You don’t sabotage a parapet because the plateau will punish the whole tier, including you. Wrongdoing is treated less as sin than as *bad maintenance*—a failure to belong properly to the shared structure.

Punishments, when they happen, tend to be logistical: reassignment to windward work, loss of ration priority, exclusion from thaw-house access at peak times. The worst sentence is not death; it’s being made energetically expensive to keep alive.

### Their work is an ecology of trades
They have farmers’ patience with non-farming materials.

- **Herders** move shaggy grazers—animals bred to metabolize lichen and brittle grass—through a patchwork of high corrals and seasonal routes. Herding here is less “open range” than choreography through bottlenecks: ramps, narrow terraces, rope-net crossings. The herders are athletes of restraint, always preventing panic because panic kills animals on stone.
- **Masons** are everywhere, because stone is always trying to become rubble. A good mason is less admired for building new things than for making old things last another winter without looking patched.
- **Tanners and rope-makers** are a backbone trade. Hide becomes bridge, harness, bucket, gasket, wind-flap, net. Fiber is twisted the way lowland people might spin wool—except here it’s the difference between crossing a gully and dying in it.
- **Salters, curers, and grease-renderers** do the unglamorous alchemy that turns summer into winter food and turns animal fat into light, sealant, waterproofing, and medicine.
- **Tool-keepers**—not simply smiths, because metal is scarce—specialize in *preserving* iron and copper: straightening, re-peening, cold-hammering, scavenging rivets from dead hinges the way others salvage bone.

Markets, when you find them on the broad shelves, don’t shout. They click and murmur: stone bowls set down, measure-stones tapped, bundles of dried moss weighed, small metal items admired like relics. Trade is less about novelty than about balancing a ledger of survival—who has surplus dung-bricks, who has spare rope, who has a herd animal with a hoof crack that needs a certain resin.

### Their religion (if you can call it that) is mostly a calendar of respect
They do not worship the plateau like a god that can be appeased. They treat it like a law that can be understood.

You see it in small shrines at choke points: a cairn with a sliver of copper hammered into it, an old bone pin wedged into a crack, a strip of cloth faded to near-white. These are not promises to supernatural beings so much as acknowledgments of risk and gratitude for passage. People touch stone before crossing a bridge the way sailors touch a mast.

There are festivals, but they are not indulgent. The closest thing to revelry is **the first true wet spell**: when rain comes hard enough to fill gutters and every roof becomes a collector. Then the city’s mood loosens. People move faster. Children are allowed to shout for once. Bowls and skins appear everywhere. A good storm becomes communal wealth, and wealth makes room for singing.

Songs are short. Repetitive. Built for breath limits and for being remembered in cold. The best songs are also mnemonics: routes, repairs, warnings.

### The animals are not “around” them; they are *part of the city’s nervous system*
The plateau has its own fauna, but the settlement has shaped a specific menagerie.

- **Shaggy grazers** (yak-like, musk-ox-like, or uniquely local) are the city’s walking pantry and fuel source. Their dung is winter heat. Their hides are roofs and bridges. Their bones become pins, needles, and reinforcing pegs.
- **Thin-air dogs** are compact, broad-chested, with thick coats and a temperament bred for steadiness. They don’t chase; they hold lines, guard choke points, and warn of predators. They are trained not to bark much—sound is expensive—but to *signal*: a low chuff, a stiff posture, a run to a flag post.
- **Raptors** are the most striking partnership. They are not pets. They are trained collaborators—eyes that can ride thermals above the terraces, scanning for wolves, for rockfall dust, for a herd splitting wrong. Their nests are built into the architecture deliberately: ledges incorporated into walls, eave-hollows protected from gusts. The city accepts droppings and feathers as the price of shared surveillance.
- **Rodents and cliff-dwellers**—the pale, dense-furred small ones—are both nuisance and indicator species. If they move, it can mean a storm is coming; if they vanish, it can mean predators are near or a freeze is deepening. Children learn to read their absence the way lowland kids read dark clouds.

Predators exist at the edges and sometimes within. Wolves (or wolf-analogues), big cats, lean things with patience. The city’s approach is not extermination; it’s **channeling**. They build the landscape into funnels: safe corridors for herds, dead zones for ambush, watch posts where a predator is always visible long before it is close. When a predator takes an animal, the response is measured. Sometimes they track and drive it off; sometimes they let the loss stand, because chasing costs more calories than it saves.

### Their intelligence is visible in what they do *not* do
They do not waste heat on constant cooking. Food is often eaten cold or warmed in communal bursts. They do not light large fires for ambiance; flame is a tool, not a mood. They do not build tall for pride; they build tall because level ground is storage and passage and market.

They also do not pretend permanence means rigidity. Whole neighborhoods have procedures for “creep”: moving a wall outward a stone at a time, shifting a roofline, relocating a doorway before the crack reaches it. Old people can point to where their childhood stair used to be, and say it the way a coastal person talks about a beach that’s moved.

### What they are, ultimately: a collective organism in stone
If you stay long enough, you stop thinking of “the city” as separate from “the people.” The city is their external metabolism.

- Cisterns are kidneys.
- Thaw houses are stomachs.
- Rope bridges are tendons.
- Windbreak corridors are lungs trained to breathe a certain way.
- Watch posts and raptors are eyes.

And the inhabitants are, in turn, like blood: moving goods, warmth, warning, and labor through channels that keep the whole body alive.

They are not romantic mountaineers. They are not doomed survivors. They are simply a culture that has accepted the plateau’s terms so completely that those terms have become aesthetics, law, and intimacy.

On clear mornings, when the sun is honest and the air is sharpened, you can watch them become visible in small signs: flags flicking in code, a line of bodies leaning into a stair, a herder’s whistle too high to travel far, a raptor’s shadow sliding across a wall that has been repaired a hundred times. The plateau doesn’t soften them.

It *defines* them—until their density begins to define it back.
