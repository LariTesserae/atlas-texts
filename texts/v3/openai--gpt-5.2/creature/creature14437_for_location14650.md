What lives here, at first, looks like what *doesn’t*.

The district’s most successful resident is absence—not a supernatural void so much as a pressure kept in place, the way a low spot in land keeps a pond. Anything that has to keep its own rhythm—heartbeat, breath, wingbeat, nervous flicker—finds its timing bullied, then overwritten. Small life arrives the way it always does, on wind and shoes and rainwater, but it does not get to *continue* in the ordinary way. It becomes something else, or it leaves, or it goes quiet enough that it may as well be gone.

So the first inhabitants are the ones that do not need to argue with the place’s pulse.

### 1) The slow green: plants that learned the city’s grammar
The vegetation here isn’t a “jungle.” It’s a set of confident, spaced solutions—each plant staking out the kind of foothold that survives vibration, brackish seep, warm metal, and periodic drowning.

- **Angle-vines** (not a species so much as a habit) pick only what holds. They test with a tendril, abandon surfaces that sweat oil or shed rust, and commit to ladder rungs, corner braces, handrails—anything with regularity. Their spirals end up evenly pitched, as if they’re copying threads cut into bolts.

- **Gutter-root shrubs** colonize rooftops where silt collects. They don’t spread laterally; they send **one or two decisive roots** downward through drainpipes and seams, hunting constant moisture the way a miner follows a vein. Around those roots, the pipe interior becomes lined with pale root-hair felt, turning infrastructure into a living wick.

- **Canal-edge “mangroves”** (whatever their original name) space themselves like pylons—each tree a small engineering project. Buttress roots flare, then subdivide into finger-thick struts that brace against both mud and wave-slap from the canal walls. Their bark often carries a faint metallic sheen from aerosols, a varnish of the district.

- **Vent-near flora** grow low and thick, with leaf surfaces that crust with salt and then shed it in thin flakes. Their stems kink slightly where hot drafts hit them, as if they’ve learned to *lean away* from certain exhalations.

These plants aren’t “thriving” in the cheerful sense. They are persistent in the way rivets are persistent. They do not make the place lively; they make it *inhabited*.

### 2) The velvet underlayer: mosses, algae, and the real architects
Under catwalks, inside culverts, along the shadowed sides of pump housings—where heat and wetness don’t argue—there are living skins.

- **Algae films** lay themselves on metal like lacquer, sometimes iridescent with copper runoff. In places they form clear boundaries: a clean line where vibration shakes the surface too often for the film to hold, and a thick line where stillness allows it.

- **Moss mats** become actual terrain: springy fields that hold droplets like beads on fur. They are one of the few things here that look soft without being fragile.

- **Biofilms**—slimes so thin you only notice them when they smear—do the chemical work. They take in the district’s leavings (oil mist, mineral salts, old coolant) and turn them into layers: slick, then crust, then slick again. They are why some pipes look “healed” rather than merely corroded, wrapped in a living bandage that both eats and protects.

If you want to know what *really* lives here, it’s not the big rooted things above. It’s these membranes. They don’t mind being stepped on, peeled off, grown over, replaced. They’re built for a world that resets surfaces every day.

### 3) The pale shelves: fungi that treat buildings as fallen logs
The fungus doesn’t behave like rot in a normal abandoned place. It behaves like an industry of its own.

- **Shelf fungi** erupt from soaked timbers and from the paper-thin pockets between brick and plaster, flaring outward like frozen foam. They appear in clusters where condensation “dumps” in regular cycles—as if the fungus can feel timing and prefers to fruit on a schedule.

- **Thread-fine mycelium** runs through mortar seams and between riveted plates. In places it forms pale cords that look like cables—too purposeful to be accidental—bridging from one damp niche to another. Those cords bead water along their length and, at certain hours, seem to “sweat” in unison with nearby machinery.

Fungus is one of the few residents that can afford to be extravagant here, because it’s feeding on both sides of the compromise: the city’s cellulose and the swamp’s constant moisture.

### 4) The rust-skin: corrosion as an ecosystem, not a symptom
Rust in this district isn’t just decay; it’s habitat.

Iron surfaces blister into scales and honeycombs, and those cavities hold warm water. Inside them:

- **Iron-bacteria** (or the district’s equivalent) lay down slicks that shift from orange to purple-black. They make delicate patterns, contour maps of flow and vibration.

- **Salt crystals** and mineral blooms form on edges near vents and sluice spray. They grow like tiny reefs—then collapse, then regrow—each cycle leaving a different texture for films and spores to take hold.

This turns machines into porous, wet-bodied things. A pump housing becomes a kind of reef. A riveted canal wall becomes a cliff face with its own microclimate. Corrosion is not the end of function here; it’s one of the ways function continues.

### 5) The organs that never learned to stop: machines as living residents
If there’s a “fauna” in this basin, it’s mechanical—and it behaves, over time, like a species adapting.

Some pumps still show paint; others are softened into strange shapes by rust and mineral deposits, but they continue in their long labor. What makes them feel alive isn’t metaphor alone:

- They **respond** to rain with deeper cadence.
- Their belts and pistons **organize condensation**—bead-lines, release-lines, repeating like breath.
- Their warm surfaces create **local weather**: a fog bank under a walkway, a dry pocket behind a spinning flywheel, a steady drip that becomes a miniature waterfall that becomes a clean-washed wall.

Around the oldest stations there are often “nests” of infrastructure—redundant pipes, bypass lines, patched valves—added over decades by hands that kept trying to make the system more survivable. Those additions now read like scar tissue and callus. The district has, in effect, **a circulatory system**: intake grates, throats of pipe, ventricles of pump houses, return canals. It does not “want” anything in a human sense, but it persists with the stubborn coherence of a body.

### 6) The thing that rides the rhythm: an emergent inhabitant made of timing
Then there’s the resident you can’t point at.

You can see evidence of it in the way mist threads toward sound, the way droplets briefly hang near turning flywheels, the way the air seems to *take instruction* from machinery. It’s tempting to call it a spirit, but it’s more like an emergent physics—the district’s constant pulse producing a standing pattern in heat and moisture.

Over time, that pattern starts behaving like an ecology’s top predator, except it doesn’t eat flesh. It eats *coordination*.

Anything with a self-kept rhythm—an insect’s wingbeat, a frog’s throat pulse, a bird’s rapid heart—gets tugged off its timing. First it becomes frantic (if it can), then quiet, then absent. Seeds and spores don’t have that problem. They can be rearranged by the rhythm and still proceed. Plants can let their stomata open and close without panic. Fungi can fruit on schedule. Microbes don’t care who conducts the air.

So the district ends up populated by the kinds of life that can tolerate having their timing borrowed, and hollowed out of the kinds that can’t.

You could call that organizing force *the Choir*—not because it sings, but because it makes everything else either harmonize or leave.

### 7) If people still “live” here, they do it like divers
Human presence is mostly fossilized in signage, dates, numbered runs of pipe—intention turned into objects. But on the rare occasions someone enters, they don’t enter as residents. They enter as a task.

If there are pumpwrights or inspectors, they move in sealed gear, faces shielded not just from chemicals but from humidity that seems to cling and listen. They don’t linger. They don’t camp. They don’t bring animals. They do what must be done—swap a belt, clear a grate, reset a breaker—and leave, as if staying too long would let the district learn their pulse.

And when they’re gone, the place closes around their footprints quickly: a delayed wobble in the lid-like ground, a fresh sheen of condensation, a returning cadence.

### What you end up with
A basin-sized pocket of city-swamp where the residents are:

- **Rooted things** that can live without moving fast,
- **films and mats** that treat every surface as a shoreline,
- **fungi** that turn buildings into woodlands,
- **corrosion-reefs** that make metal porous and habitable,
- **machines** that function like organs,
- and an **unseen rhythmic field**—born from constant mechanical motion—that keeps animal life from ever becoming established.

It isn’t a dead district. It’s a district with its food web amputated at the level of breath and blood, leaving a world of skin, root, spore, rust, and endless industrious pulse—alive in layers, and strangely, meticulously unaccompanied.
