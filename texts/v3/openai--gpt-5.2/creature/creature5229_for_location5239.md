What lives in the pocket is not a single community so much as a stack of communities, each one keyed to a slightly different edge—hot-to-cool, sulfur-rich-to-sulfur-poor, breathable-to-deadly, fresh-crystal-to-spent-crust. If you try to picture “the inhabitants” all at once you get only motion: a workshop-full of small bodies, each using the vent the way coastal life uses a tide.

Start closer than your eyes want to start—at the scale of stains and films—because almost everything larger is feeding on, living inside, or renting space from something microbial.

## The first residents: the films that look like nothing
On the warm faces of the basalt ribs there are thin skins that you don’t notice until the ember-glow lifts and slides across them. They’re not wet. They’re not even obviously alive. They’re more like varnish laid down unevenly, a fog of biology trapped under mineral dust.

These are the pocket’s real “plants,” except they never see sun and they don’t make sugar from light. They make a living out of chemical bookkeeping:

- **Sulfur-oxidizers** that sit where hydrogen sulfide seeps and oxygen is scarce but not absent, building energy grain by grain from that gradient. In good spots they tint the rock **pale yellow-gray**, almost indistinguishable from the sulfur crust itself.
- **Iron-workers** that colonize fresh fracture edges where basalt has offered up reactive surfaces. They make **rust-ghost smears** that come and go as the gas chemistry shifts.
- **Purple bruise-films**—archaea-like things that tolerate heat and dry salt. The color isn’t for show; it’s a pigment that doubles as armor against reactive gases and stray electrical discharge. In the ember pulses, these patches look briefly bruised and then vanish back into darkness.

In places where gas condenses and immediately re-evaporates—too little water to bead, just enough to happen—microbes build **paper-thin mats** under the lip of salt shelves. These mats are the pocket’s pastureland. They are why the walls are crowded.

And they are not passive. They change the rock they live on. They roughen it, bind dust into crust, and exhale byproducts that become food for the next layer of life. If the vent is a factory, these are the slow machines that never shut off.

## The grazers: life made of joints and seals
Once you can see the films, you start seeing the eaters of films: the small, many-jointed fauna that makes the pocket sound busy.

They don’t resemble “cave insects” so much as a whole toolkit of body plans that converged on the same necessities: **seal the water in, keep the dust out, and treat heat like weather**.

### Crystal-mites (the living carpets)
On the warmest routes—where a hand held near the wall would feel the heat as texture—there are **mite-like swarms** that move as a unit. Individually they’re the size of grit; together they look like a shifting nap on the stone.

They have:
- **Ceramic-looking dorsal plates** that catch mineral dust and become, over time, almost indistinguishable from the wall. Some plates are literally mineralized: salts precipitate into their outer layers the way scale forms on a pipe.
- Mouthparts that don’t so much bite as **scrape**. They comb microbial varnish off the basalt and leave behind those dull traffic lanes you noticed—tracks polished not by feet but by millions of repeated, careful meals.

When the vent’s chemistry changes—more sulfur, less oxygen—the carpets thin or migrate, flowing like spilled grain toward a safer gradient.

### Salt-shelters (segmented crawlers that live upside-down)
Under curling shelves of brittle salt there are **pale segmented crawlers**, longer than a finger joint but thinner than your little finger, built like folded springs. They spend most of their time inverted, gripping the underside of overhangs where gas is slightly less caustic and where dust settles in edible layers.

Their trick is not speed but economy:
- They breathe through **oiled membranes** tucked into grooves along their sides; the membranes can close like shutters when a hot, bad breath of gas comes up.
- They can go still for hours, then suddenly move in a coordinated burst to harvest a fresh bloom of microbes after a cooler pulse.

Their cast skins—shed in tight rings—often remain hanging from salt whiskers like bits of pale wire insulation.

### The filament-pickers (tiny things that farm by accident)
In narrow constrictions where minerals grow into bristles—those whiskers and fins—there are very small arthropod analogues that seem to “pick” at the growths. What they’re doing is harvesting not the mineral but the **microbial fuzz** that lives in the mineral’s shadow.

They carry spores and cells on their bodies, and in doing so they seed new patches. No planning, no intention, just a behavior that makes the pocket more densely inhabited over time. A ledge they frequent becomes a ledge that feeds them.

## The engineers: animals that use the vent like architecture
Some inhabitants don’t just graze; they use the pocket’s mineral geometry as part of their survival.

### Static-runners (the spark-makers)
In the dustiest corridors—where the roar is highest and the air seems charged—there are quick, beetlelike bodies that run in short, decisive sprints and then freeze. They have stiff, **insulating shells** and brush-like appendages along their flanks.

When a swarm of them moves, you hear the ticking snaps you described because they’re perfect static generators: dust + dry air + insulating cuticle + tight passages.

They appear to use this:
- as a **deterrent** (predators don’t like to bite a thing that crackles),
- and as a way to **dislodge** the finest mineral powder and microbial flakes from whiskered surfaces, making food fall like invisible chaff.

Where static-runners are common, the walls look subtly different: cleaner in streaks, as if someone has been dry-brushing the stone.

### The lane-keepers (the ones that make roads)
The polished tracks aren’t only from grazing. In some places you can watch a constant two-way traffic of mid-sized, hard-shelled walkers—“kiln beetles” is the closest human shorthand. They’re not beetles, but they share the same compact insistence.

They travel fixed routes because routes are survival:
- the route passes close enough to the vent breath to keep microbes productive,
- but not so close that a sudden hot surge kills the travelers,
- and it crosses “settling tanks” where dust and condensed residue collect as edible powder.

They leave chemical markers that cling to mineral surfaces—waxy, persistent, hard to erase in dryness—so the route stays coherent even when the micro-topography changes.

If a passage clogs and the roar shifts, these lane-keepers are among the first to relocate, pouring into a new corridor in a matter of hours, as if the pocket has rerouted its traffic pattern.

## The predators and scavengers: things that wait for the air to do the killing
Where there is abundant small life, there are bodies that live by eating bodies. But predation here is less about pursuit than about positioning—finding the right place to let the environment do half the work.

### Heat-squatters (the vent-edge scavengers)
Near the hottest fissures—just outside the zones where a human would cough blood-thin air—there are squat, thick-bodied scavengers that look almost melted into their own armor. Their skin is leathery and dull, dusted permanently with mineral powder that sticks to oily surfaces.

They don’t chase. They **wait**.

When a surge of bad gas rolls up, it doesn’t just change the sound; it creates casualties. A crawler that can’t shutter its membranes fast enough, a glider that misjudges a CO₂ pool, a juvenile lane-keeper that strays too low—something will go still. The heat-squatters, tuned to vibration and chemical cues, will shuffle out and dismantle the loss with slow certainty.

They waste almost nothing:
- shell becomes calcium and salts,
- soft tissue becomes moisture reclaimed,
- microbial films recolonize the scraps.

### Wall-clingers (the small hunters)
On the upper ribs—where the air is marginally less lethal—there are quick, flat-bodied hunters that cling in the lee of fins and baffles. They feed on the grazers, striking in short bursts. Their mouths are adapted less for chewing than for **piercing and sucking**, minimizing water loss.

They are also some of the pocket’s most sensitive “weather vanes.” When the vent shifts chemistry, they are the first to disappear upward into cracks you cannot follow.

## The gliders: the pocket’s largest moving shadows
And then there are the batlike shapes you mentioned—except “bat” implies fur and wet breath and a mammal’s constant demand for oxygen. These are something else: **leathery gliders** that live where the ceiling ribs are broken into ledges and the air is just barely usable.

They don’t fill the pocket; they punctuate it—one on a ledge, two in a deeper chamber, a sudden flit across the ember pulse.

Their bodies are built around two problems: dust and dryness.

- Their wings are not thin skin but a **double membrane** with a dry lubricant between layers, allowing it to flex without tearing or drying into brittleness.
- Their breathing openings are shielded by **folded filters**—not hairs, but comb-like structures that trap abrasive particles.
- They roost in spots where warm updrafts rise steadily; when they launch, they don’t flap much. They let the vent do the work.

What do they eat? Mostly the mid-sized walkers and, when opportunity allows, each other’s dead. They aren’t above scavenging. In a place like this, moral categories don’t help; efficiency does.

Occasionally you see a glider with a pale crust on its muzzle and forelimbs—mineral deposit accumulated from roosting too close to a condenser-cold spot. That crust can become deadly if it thickens; older individuals often bear scars where they’ve torn deposits free.

## The truly intimate residents: symbionts inside bodies
The pocket is so chemically aggressive that many animals outsource their hardest chemistry.

Inside the grazers and lane-keepers are **gut symbionts** that do what the host cannot: break down sulfurous films, detoxify metals, turn mineral-laced dust into usable nutrients. Some of these symbionts likely began as free-living microbes on the walls and moved into bodies because bodies are steadier than stone—warmer, buffered, less exposed to the worst gas pulses.

Some animals may even “seed” their young by smearing egg cases with the right microbial paste. In this place, inheritance is not just genes; it’s the right internal passengers.

## How they all endure without water
Nothing here is hydrated in the way surface life is. The pocket’s inhabitants treat water as a rare reagent, not a background condition.

They survive by:
- **Sealing**: waxes, oils, mineralized cuticles, shuttered pores.
- **Recycling**: excreting dry pellets; reclaiming moisture from breath; producing small amounts of metabolic water and losing almost none of it.
- **Timing**: becoming still during harsh gas pulses; feeding during cooler, more chemically “gentle” breaths.
- **Living on interfaces**: staying exactly where condensation almost happens, where a molecule of water might briefly exist on a crystal edge before being taken back by heat.

Even death is adapted. A dead body dries fast and becomes a resource depot rather than a rot site. It feeds scavengers, then microbes, then the next layer of grazers.

## What the pocket feels like when you finally understand the crowd
After a while, the presences separate into roles you can sense even without seeing: the quiet chemistry of the films, the constant rasp of scraping mouths, the staccato tick of static, the rare heavy shift of a larger scavenger repositioning itself, the soft, almost reluctant launch of a glider from a high ledge.

Nothing here is delicate, but nothing is careless. Life has learned the pocket’s rules the way a machinist learns a dangerous tool: by repetition, by respect, by building habits around what cannot be changed.

If the barren massif above is a statement of absence, the slit is a rebuttal. It is a place where the planet’s interior leaks into the air, and the leak has been colonized so thoroughly that even the newest, sharpest fracture is already—quietly, loudly, patiently—occupied.
