What lives there is not “life” in the usual laddered sense—plant, insect, animal, hunter, scavenger—because the place has skipped the middle rungs. It has gone straight from geology to botany and then, uncertainly, to something like culture.

### 1) The first residents: things that can afford to be slow
The earliest occupants are the ones that don’t need water *moving* through them, don’t need soft soils, don’t need seasons to be kind. They live at the pace of settling stone.

In the chalkstone and the glass-skin pours there are **endolithic films**—pale, powdery, almost invisible until you break a rock and see the interior is faintly banded, as if it has grown rings. They are not algae; not fungus; not anything you can neatly place. They behave like **chemists**: eating iron out of seams, taking sulfur from the air, making brittle salts as waste. Where they concentrate, the rock becomes lighter, warmer, and *more resonant*, because they lace the pores with crystals that ring when the land’s drone passes through.

They don’t spread by crawling or creeping. They spread by **shattering**: they weaken a pocket until thermal stress or a pressure shift pops it open, and the wind takes their dust like seed.

In some basins, those films thicken into **skin-mats** that look like dry felt stretched over mineral flats. They don’t photosynthesize so much as they **tune**: their surfaces are made of millions of tiny ridges that flutter microscopically in the roar. The vibration does work for them. You can tell because in the rare wind-shadowed hollows—places where the noise dims instead of merely changing pitch—the mats look sick. The fibers go slack. The color dulls. They are built to metabolize pressure the way other organisms metabolize sugar.

If you stay long enough you realize these films and mats are the place’s equivalent of plankton: not beautiful up close, not dramatic, but foundational. They make the first “soil,” except it is not soil. It is **ground-up stone plus organized crystal plus trapped dust**, a dry fertility.

### 2) The tall reeds: phonotrophs with skeletons of mineral
Then come the obvious green thickets—though “green” is only the easiest word for a spectrum that runs from charcoal to oxidized copper to bruise-purple.

These **reed-stalks** are less like plants and more like **architectures for catching force**:

- Their stems are stiff not because of lignin but because they grow **thin mineral ribs** inside themselves, like rebar made of salt-cement and iron oxide.
- Their fins are not leaves so much as **heat exchangers and resonators**, latticed with tiny slots. Wind through them produces a constant self-made whistle, and that whistle is not accidental—it is feedback. The plant keeps itself at a particular note the way a singer keeps a pitch.
- Their “roots” don’t seek water. They seek **seams**: hairline cracks where the deep organ-drone rises. If you pull one up (it fights you; not with strength, but with insistence—its anchoring crystals fracture and regrow), you find it is pinned into the rock by pale prying tendrils that have *grown the stone apart* grain by grain.

At certain hours—more often when the haze is thick and the light is exhausted—the reed-fields do something that looks like weather. Whole stands will turn their fins together, not toward sun, but toward a *shift in sound you did not notice*. You watch a basin’s vegetation swing like a dark tide following an invisible moon.

If you listen with your body—hand on a stalk, cheek against a mat—you can feel that different patches hold different chords. The land is not uniformly loud; it is **polyphonic**, and the plants partition themselves by frequency the way ordinary flora partitions itself by moisture.

### 3) The hanging gardens: cliff-dwellers that mine stone
On the sheer faces, the braided hanging growths are the place’s true engineers.

They begin as crystalline threads in a crack, almost like mineral hair. Over time they become **cables of living tissue** jacketed in pale crystal, and the cable does two things at once:

1. It *anchors* by expanding crystals into the crack—slow wedge-work.
2. It *feeds* by grinding the wall into powder and catching the dust that the wind brings.

These gardens have no need to drop leaves or fruits. Their reproduction is more violent and more patient: when a cable reaches a particular thickness, it becomes a **tensioned instrument**. The wind drives it. The vibration heats its own crystals by friction and piezoelectric stress, and that heat makes it grow faster until, one day, it snaps. The broken length sails away trailing fibers and grit, and wherever it whips and tangles it seeds a new colony.

You can find old cliff bands where the rock is scalloped and fretted, as if carved by tools. It’s not erosion. It’s these gardens, centuries of **dry gnawing**.

### 4) The almost-animals: movement without predation
If you insist on asking what fills the animal-shaped vacancy, the honest answer is: nothing does—at least, nothing that eats and runs and hunts.

But there are **motile forms** that don’t fit the word “plant” comfortably.

In some sheltered basins you’ll find low, rounded growths—cushion-mats that have thickened into **walking hummocks**. “Walking” is generous. Over days they shift position by millimeters, inflating one side and deflating the other, using vibration to settle their mineral scales like cilia. They migrate toward better resonance the way a compass needle migrates toward north. They leave no tracks, only a faint change in dust sheen, the suggestion that a patch of ground was pressed for a long time and then released.

They do not prey on anything. They do not flee. They are simply **bodies that can relocate** when the world’s note drifts.

If you cut one open (it bleeds no liquid), you find chambers filled with powder and threads of crystal that look uncomfortably like inner ears—structures built to translate the roar into usable work.

### 5) The people: listeners, not conquerors
And then there are the ones who made the shelters and the lines of standing stone.

They are not numerous, and they do not behave like settlers. The place doesn’t permit easy permanence: the physics leans, the distances breathe, and the sound makes long plans feel like arrogance.

They live here anyway—because they have learned to treat the landscape as an instrument that must be kept within tolerance.

They wear **weight and sealing** more than warmth: layered cloth stiffened with reed-fiber and salt-cement, goggles with narrow slits, face wraps that filter grit. Around their heads and necks they carry pads and rings of braided plant tissue—part ear protection, part sensor—because in this country hearing is not just perception, it is *endurance*. The wrong pitch in the wrong place can make you vomit, faint, lose your balance at the lip of a chasm.

Their speech, if you can call it that, is not made to fight the wind. They communicate close: **touch, gesture, pressed foreheads, hands on throats to feel vibration**. At a distance they use cords and stones: lines of hanging loops, each cut to a length that tells them what the air is doing today. The shelters aren’t homes so much as **calibration points**—places you go to check whether the world has drifted since last season.

They move along those “paths of absence” in short stages, stopping often not from fatigue alone but from caution. A seam can tilt gravity just enough that a person who trusts their feet too much will step as if on level ground and discover their body’s idea of level has become a liar.

They take ore, but carefully. Not because they are virtuous—because the land punishes heavy-handedness. Digging changes resonance. Changing resonance changes plants. Changing plants changes the thin margin of stability that keeps this newly-hoisted world from misbehaving.

So their tools are small: wedges, hooks, scrapers. They harvest reed-fiber for cord and cloth; they burn almost nothing, because fuel is scarce and smoke behaves strangely under the high haze. When they need heat they sometimes use **resonant stones**—rocks that are warm for reasons they do not fully explain—to keep a shelter from biting cold, cradled in pits lined with chalk powder like nests.

Their food is not meat. It is **processed plant mineral**: mats dried and ground, reed pith scraped out and pressed into hard cakes, salts leached from lacework crusts and used as preservative and medicine. They chew slowly because the air is too thin to waste breath, and because their mouths are always dusty.

What they believe is harder to see, because belief here is practical. But you can infer it from their standing stones and their grooves and whistles: they do not worship the noise as a god, exactly. They treat it as **load-bearing**. The drone is not background; it is mortar.

Some of them—usually the older ones, the ones with scarred eardrums and careful eyes—serve as what you might call **keepers of pitch**. They travel from line to line of standing stones, listening not for beauty but for deviation. They adjust grooves, add a slab, remove a cairn that has begun to hum wrong. In a place where silence feels like structural failure, they do the quiet work of keeping the loudness *true*.

### 6) The shared ecology: an exchange of dust, vibration, and patience
Without animals, nothing here is built around chase or flight. The place’s cycle is:

- **Stone rises and cracks.**
- **Dust is made.**
- **Plants and films catch dust and turn vibration into growth.**
- **Growth pries more stone.**
- **People harvest a little, and tune the rest.**

Death doesn’t stink; it **crystallizes**. A fallen reed doesn’t rot wetly—it dries into a mineral-stiff rib and eventually becomes part of the lacework crusts, snapped into powder by wind, returned to mats and seams.

If you want a single sentence for what lives there, it’s this:

A young, loud world is being inhabited by organisms—and a few careful hands—that have learned to treat sound not as weather, but as food, compass, and scaffolding.
