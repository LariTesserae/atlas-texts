What lives here is not a population so much as a *permit list*.

At first, it seems simple: a garden under constraint. A single allowed kingdom spreading into every weld seam and bolt-hole, as if the chamber’s designers decided that if they couldn’t keep water out, they would turn the water into a tool. The inhabitants announce themselves the way ivy announces an abandoned wall—by softening it, claiming it, making the manufactured feel older than it is.

But the longer you look, the more the “plants” separate into roles, lineages, and temperaments, each bred or selected for a particular kind of obedience.

### The clingers (the ones that make metal feel like shore)
These are the first bodies you notice because they rewrite the architecture’s edges.

They live on the verticals and undersides: on pipe elbows, ladder rungs, the sharp corners of poured concrete where a current catches. Their anchoring pads are not roots in any terrestrial sense but gasket-flesh—broad, flared contact discs that conform to ridges in mill scale and the faint spiral of machining marks. If you pull one loose (and you can, with enough patience), it leaves behind a clean negative impression of the substrate, like a suction cup that learned to feed.

They “drink” from the chamber itself: not soil, not silt, but a thin halo of nutrients that always exists near surfaces—ions shedding from metal, trace dosing compounds, the slow exhalations of grout and sealant. Their leaves are small and tough, studded with micro-pores that open and close in coordinated patches, not for breathing air but for managing exchange in water the way a valve manages pressure.

They grow where the system’s fields are strongest, where animal cells would be discouraged into silence. They don’t mind the hum. In a way, they seem to recognize it as weather.

### The drapers (the ones that make corridors into hair and cloth)
In the calmer runs—between trusses, along catwalk rails, in the long slots between tanks—live the plants that don’t need to hold on so much as *hang*.

They make curtains: filamentous greens as fine as spun glass, gathered into thicker ropes where they’ve snagged on a bracket. They trail like slow banners and move just enough to reveal the current’s shape. If you watch long enough, you realize they are not passively drifting. They actively “choose” tension—tightening a thread here, releasing another there—so that their bodies settle into the cleanest lanes of flow.

These are the chamber’s strainers and scribes. Particulate that enters the water column ends up combed out by them, clinging in pale dustings until it is shed, eaten down to biofilm, or flushed toward an intake that will take it away. Their surfaces are slightly sticky, but not in a trap-like way; more like a deliberate invitation to adhesion, a way of turning mess into a resource.

Where the skylight’s lenses make bright columns, these drapers thicken until the beams look solid—green architecture inside industrial architecture.

### The floaters (the ones that live in the light’s budget)
There are plants here that never touch a wall at all. They exist only where the metered sunlight falls and where the nutrient manifolds breathe. They form hovering rafts—broad, thin blades and translucent fans that keep themselves in place with a network of threads so fine you don’t see them until you disturb them.

Their anatomy reads like a compromise between botany and plumbing: veins that pulse with slow peristaltic motion, not because they need speed but because they need *certainty* in a world that refuses turbulence. They are efficient, almost offensively healthy. Their greens are not wild greens; they’re the greens of an organism that has never had to fear a grazer.

If anything in this chamber could be accused of vanity, it would be these. They grow in shapes that look composed: arcs and spirals around specific pylons, repeated motifs across doorframes as if the infrastructure were a trellis designed by an architect rather than an engineer. That is where the place’s strangeness becomes less like accident and more like intention.

Because those patterns are not random. They align with invisible gradients—tiny leaks of current, subtle magnetic biases in rebar cages, minute temperature differentials from a buried heat exchanger. The floaters and drapers are sensitive enough to “feel” those gradients and arrange themselves along them, turning the chamber’s unseen forces into visible ornament.

### The skins (the ones you mistake for mere slime until you realize they’re doing most of the work)
Under everything—on tile, on paint, on the insides of intake bells—there is a thin living sheen: biofilm, velvet algae, microbial mats that survive because the sterilization regime is not meant to erase *all* life, only to prevent the wrong kind from assembling into motion.

This is an ecosystem of the tolerated small: bacteria that can weather the background field, cyanobacteria nested in plant surfaces, extremophile strains that treat trace biocides as a speed bump rather than a wall. They make the chamber feel quiet because they turn the water into a buffer. They eat compounds you would otherwise smell. They swallow the sharpness of chemistry the way carpets swallow footsteps.

If the visible plants are the chamber’s “forest,” these skins are its soil—manufactured, thin, everywhere, and constantly rebuilt.

### The chamber’s true resident: the management that behaves like a mind
And then there is the inhabitant that isn’t biomass at all.

It is present as constraint, as rhythm, as enforcement: the steady hum of panels, the periodic exhale of dosing ports, the way the skylight distributes light like an allocation rather than a gift. Something maintains the hostile hospitality of this place—keeps the water warm but not too warm, rich but not permissive, bright but not free.

You can call it an automated system, and that would be accurate in the way calling a reef “calcium” is accurate. The infrastructure is not just a setting. It behaves like a metabolism that has replaced animals with pumps and replaced predators with policies.

It does not “live” by moving through the chamber; it lives by *making the chamber predictable*.

- It lives in the intake screens that never quite clog because flow patterns are subtly adjusted.
- It lives in the slow seasonal cycling of pumps that you feel only as a change in how the curtains of green lean.
- It lives in the lens array overhead that rotates its prisms on schedules no one hears, producing latticed light as regular as breathing.

And it lives, most intimately, in the way the plants respond.

The vegetation here is not merely tolerated; it is *used*. You begin to suspect that certain growth forms are encouraged the way a maintenance crew might be directed: a spiral here to stiffen a conduit bundle, a thick mat there to quiet a vibrating panel, a deliberate clearing in a corridor where clarity matters. The plants are filtration media, heat exchangers, and living indicator lights. Their color shifts are not decorative; they are readouts. Their sudden dieback is an alarm. Their vigor is compliance.

If there is intelligence, it is distributed: not a face in a control room, but an old algorithm still running, still keeping its covenant—no animals, no chaos, no suddenness—while letting the garden do the slow, surface-bound labor that machines do poorly.

### What *doesn’t* live here, and how that absence becomes part of the population
The chamber’s most conspicuous resident is the emptiness where animals ought to be.

That absence isn’t just a lack of fish; it is an active shape, maintained the way temperature is maintained. Anything that tries to arrive with a nervous system—anything that wants to dart, hunt, breed, flee—meets a gentle, persistent refusal. Not a dramatic kill, but a failure to thrive so complete that the attempt leaves no story behind.

So the plants fill the niche that movement would have filled. They become the only visible change, the only “behavior.” They make the chamber feel inhabited because they are always, quietly, in the act of taking a little more surface, turning another bolt head into a foothold, converting another warning label into a faint fossil under green.

### Taken together
What lives here is a botanical city under a municipal ordinance: life is allowed if it stays put, if it works, if it can be metered.

The inhabitants are:
- engineered aquatic plants that cling, drape, float, and seal;
- microbial skins that turn chemistry into quiet;
- and the governing system—fields, lenses, dosing schedules, pump cycles—that functions like an invisible resident with a strict idea of peace.

If you want a single name for the whole thing, it isn’t “garden” and it isn’t “machine room.” It is a *living filtration cathedral*: a place where the only permitted creature is process, and where that process has learned to wear chlorophyll like a cloak.
