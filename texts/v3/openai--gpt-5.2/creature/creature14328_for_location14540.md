What lives here has learned two commandments so deeply they’ve become anatomy:

1) **The world pulses.**  
2) **Stone is food, but only where it is being changed.**

Nothing in the caldera is rooted, because nothing can afford to be. The rock is too young to trust, the currents too scheduled to ignore. Life doesn’t claim territory by holding still; it claims it by *keeping time*—by matching the hydraulic beat the way lungs match thin air.

### The first residents: the sheen
Before you notice animals, you notice the way the rock looks *slightly alive* wherever vent-water leaks. Not color—no green—just a change in texture, a dampened gloss, as if the basalt has been breathed on.

That is the foundational citizenry: films of chemo-feeders, not plants but chemists—bacteria and stranger things braided into a living frost. They grow in gradients: thickest where hot, reduced water meets the caldera’s colder, oxygenated layers. They do not spread like moss. They appear, vanish, reappear a handspan away, because their “sunlight” is chemistry and chemistry is fickle in moving water.

They make the first soft matter this place can keep. They trap silicate flour. They seed precipitate. They turn raw fracture into something other bodies can read with mouthparts and sensors. On a fresh landslide face, they arrive like weathering given hunger.

And they are not just food. They are *signal*. Their metabolic byproducts change local conductivity and pH, which matters here because so many creatures navigate by electrical and acoustic reflections. In a caldera where layers behave like floors, the sheen is the signage on the walls.

### The grazers: mouths built for glass
The “grazers” move like purposeful tools. They are broad, low-bodied swimmers—some almost disk-shaped—whose undersides are a patchwork of gripping pads and scraping plates. Their feeding is not browsing; it’s machining.

They press to stone, lock on, and rasp with keratinous ridges reinforced by embedded minerals. You can hear it up close: a faint, steady grind that becomes audible only because the whole caldera is quiet in its own way between pulses.

They eat biofilm, yes—but they also eat *the interface*: the thin zone where mineral and microbe interpenetrate. In their gut, the chemistry stays stratified, not fully mixed. They host internal symbionts that do what the films do on rock, but faster and protected: oxidize, reduce, harvest tiny energy differences the way surface creatures harvest photons. The grazers are walking (swimming) reaction chambers.

They travel in lanes along the walls and chimney-fields, because those are places where the caldera’s hydraulics keep refreshing the gradients. A stable patch is a dead patch. They prefer the fresh bruise of new seepage.

During a vent pulse, they flatten themselves and cling. Their bodies are tuned to avoid being torn away; their pads stiffen, their edges seal, and a pulse that would fling other animals becomes merely a heavier hand pressing them to their meal.

### The ribbon-swimmers: schooling as architecture
Near the rim, where spillover aerates the water and light is sharp enough to make clean silhouettes, the most conspicuous inhabitants are the ribbon-bodied swimmers—fast, bright, always in contact.

They look like fish only at a distance. Close, they are more like flexible blades with paired fins that can cup, scythe, or flatten into airfoil shapes depending on the current. Their skins carry mineralized patches—mica-like tiles that flash when they roll—not simply for display, but because mineral in skin makes a better resonator and a better pressure-sensor in thin, cold water.

Their constant brushing and tapping is not affection; it’s *coordination*. They share information through touch the way a swarm of machines might share a clock signal. When the caldera’s beat approaches, a school will compress, align, and then ride the pulse as a unit, slipping into an upper layer or diving into a safer band with a precision that makes “instinct” feel like an incomplete word.

They feed on everything smaller than themselves: drifting microbial clumps, mineral-floc seeded with life, the snow of shed scales and precipitate. They are the moving mesh that keeps the upper layers from becoming chemically stagnant. The rim-zone is their city: busy, reflective, and always one spill-rain away from a surge in oxygen that changes everyone’s mood.

### The bell-bodied hangers: the choir that eats with vibration
Deeper, where light still exists but starts to feel like it’s coming from far away, there are the bells—boat-sized bodies that hang with deliberate slowness, suspended in the densest bands.

They are not jellyfish, though they borrow the silhouette. Their bell walls are thick and layered, stiffened by mineral rods that run like rebar through translucent tissue. Inside are plates—calcified, ridged, tuned.

When a vent pulse hits, they flex and *ring*. The sound is literal, metallic and deep, and it travels along the density interfaces in ways ordinary noise does not. The ring is not an accident; it’s feeding.

The bells use vibration to do three things at once:

- **Stun or disorient** the smallest swimmers and drifting grazerspawn in their immediate volume.  
- **Shake loose** mineral-floc and biofilm fragments from nearby chimneys, creating a local snowfall.  
- **Call**—not romantically, but logistically—because sound is how you coordinate position in a layered world.

Their tendrils—cable bundles more than tentacles—are studded with sensory nodes that read pressure changes like fingertips reading Braille. They do not chase. They *collect*. After ringing, they draw inward slowly, concentrating drifting matter the way a filtration plant concentrates impurities—another reason the whole place feels industrial without any builder.

Around many bells live smaller hitchhikers: thin, eel-like opportunists that steal food from the bell’s capture currents, and tiny cleaners that nibble at precipitate before it can crust over the bell’s resonant plates. Even parasites here behave like maintenance crews, because any surface that becomes inert becomes useless.

### The chimney-fauna: living scaffolds on mineral towers
The chimneys themselves—those fluted carbonate and sulfide stacks—are not just substrate. They are neighborhood, nursery, and in some cases collaborator.

In the warmest seep mouths, where bubbles rise in chains and precipitation crackles, you find creatures that seem designed to endure constant abrasion: segmented clingers with hard mouth-rings and flexible bodies that can bend around vent lips. They wedge into crevices and feed directly on the densest microbial growth, the freshest chemical gradients.

Some of these chimney-dwellers secrete binding gels that trap precipitate; others bore tiny tunnels that change how fluid exits a crack. Over years, their collective presence *modulates* the vent geometry. It’s not conscious engineering, but it is selection made physical: vents that flow in ways that nourish the chimney community get reinforced; vents that starve them get clogged, diverted, abandoned.

This is one of the caldera’s quiet miracles: the hydraulics feel self-maintaining because life is part of the plumbing. Not as master, not as designer—more like lime-scale and cleaning agents acting at once, each generation pushing the flow a fraction toward “livable.”

### The wall-quilts: millions of grips making one fabric
Where overflow returns as submerged waterfalls—cold, aerated vertical rivers sliding down the crater walls—there are the quilts: flat-bodied clingers layered over one another in overlapping sheets.

Each individual is small, palm-sized, built around a suction disk and a crown of hooks. Together they become a living textile. When the internal currents surge, the entire quilt ripples, not randomly, but in traveling waves that distribute force so no single body takes the full shear. The sound they make—millions of tiny attachments flexing—is part roar, part purr.

They feed on what the vertical rivers bring: oxygen-rich microfloc, injured ribbon-swimmers, sloughed biofilm, and the constant rain of mineral dust that doesn’t quite fall straight. Many of them reproduce by releasing adhesive larvae that stick to whatever surface is currently being scoured clean—because scouring implies fresh chemistry and fresh chemistry implies food.

### The predators: absences with armor
Predators here don’t announce themselves with drama. They announce themselves with *silence*.

A dense school loosens. Certain clicks stop. A bell’s ringing shifts to a lower, more guarded tone. Then the predator passes: long as a canoe, sleek, plated, its fins folding and unfolding like shutters to manage drag in layered currents.

It doesn’t chase. It enters the swarm the way a hydraulic tool enters water—cleanly, efficiently—and opens a mouth that is less bite than vacuum. It takes a portion of the crowd, closes, and continues. In its wake hangs a brief glitter: shed mica-scales, a puff of disturbed silicate flour, a tremor of fear translated into altered swimming geometry.

These hunters are built for the caldera’s rhythm. During pulses they angle into the surge to gain speed “for free.” Between pulses they drift and listen, reading echoes from chimneys and walls to find where life is densest. Their senses privilege vibration and pressure over sight, because sound is more reliable than light in a layered, bubble-milked world.

### The small, the overlooked, the everywhere
Between the named forms are countless minor citizens that make the place feel crowded even when you try to focus on a single body:

- **Bubble-riders**: tiny, translucent drifters that attach to bubble chains, rising until the chain veers sideways along a density boundary—turning vertical lift into lateral travel.  
- **Dust-eaters**: threadlike swimmers that harvest the organic fraction of mineral floc, leaving behind cleaner, heavier particles that settle into those never-resting dunes.  
- **Echo-mites**: pinhead-sized clingers living on larger animals’ mineralized patches, using the host as both transport and resonant surface, feeding on micro-precipitate and the microbial skin that tries to colonize everything.  
- **Scavenger-scarfs**: ribbon-thin organisms that follow predators not for blood, but for the chemically rich cloud that a feeding event makes—panic, injury, and disturbed biofilm all at once.

Even decomposition is specialized. In cold, oxygen-variable water, bodies don’t simply rot. They become *reactors* as symbionts and scavengers race to exploit the last usable gradients inside a corpse. A dead grazer can feed a chimney-field for days.

### A place that behaves like one organism
If you stay long enough, the most unsettling resident is not any single animal but the coordination that emerges from all of them.

The caldera has a heartbeat—the vent cycle—and nearly everything here is entrained to it: feeding windows, migrations up and down a few meters, mating calls that propagate best along certain interfaces, predator patrol routes that assume predictable surges. The chimneys grow, clog, split, and reroute flows. The grazers keep the surfaces chemically active. The bells ring in ways that redistribute food and information. The quilts turn vertical rivers into living filters.

No one species is in charge. Yet the system has the feel of a body regulating itself: not intelligent in the human sense, but *coherent*, as if the crater and its inhabitants are a single high-altitude machine made of water, stone, chemistry, and sound.

What lives here, ultimately, is **an ecosystem that has replaced forests with plumbing**—a crowded, resonant city that eats gradients instead of sunlight, keeps time instead of seasons, and makes “industry” not a metaphor but the closest available description of how life can be abundant without ever turning green.
