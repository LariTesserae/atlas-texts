What lives here is not a single “people” plus their livestock, as if the mountain were a stage set and life the actors. The basin is more like a lung pocket in stone: everything inside it has learned to trade breaths, to keep dampness moving from body to body, to turn scarcity into circulation. The residents—human, plant, insect, bird, lichen—aren’t just adapted to the place; they *complete* one another’s missing functions.

### The humans: cliff-born, dew-counting, noise-stitched
They are smaller, on average, than valley folk; not frail, but tight-built—rope in the shoulders, stubborn calves, hands with thickened pads where stone and fiber have polished them. Their faces carry the place’s two permanent signatures: fine mineral dust in the pores, and sun-darkening where the overhang’s shade breaks and catches them by surprise. Hair is worn bound and oiled with resin-wax mixtures; loose strands drink moisture out of the air and then give it away to evaporation, so vanity and thrift point in the same direction.

Their eyes squint less than you’d expect. They live in a world without sharp contrast—amber haze, softened distances—so their gaze is trained for near detail: fissures in rock where a vine can be coaxed, the matte-versus-gloss shift that means a dew comb has “taken,” the faint blue-green seep of salt-glow that says a garden bed is alive rather than merely planted.

They move like people who have learned that falling is not an event but an ending. The settlement teaches them a special economy of motion: three points of contact without thinking, weight always centered, toes reading stone the way fingers read rope. Even children learn to carry loads close to the body, not because it’s efficient, but because it keeps their balance honest.

Their speech is shaped by the bowl. Sound bounces; privacy is a myth; a whispered argument can travel an alley and return like a rumor. So they develop two registers: the public voice—flat, practical, meant to be overheard without giving away more than necessary—and the intimate one, done with breath and fingertips and looks, with messages left in the way a jar is sealed or a reed mat is folded. The loudness of the place doesn’t make them boisterous so much as *precise*. You learn what not to say by hearing too much.

#### What they value
They do not hoard gold. They hoard *integrity*: intact jars, uncracked lime plaster, rope that hasn’t taken grit into its twist, a ceramic comb whose pores haven’t clogged. A house is judged less by size than by how well it holds coolth and dampness. A person’s reputation is judged less by generosity than by whether their generosity endangers the whole—whether they give from their own store or from tomorrow’s.

Water is not “holy” in the sentimental sense. It is *consequential*. It has lineage. People can tell you where a mouthful has been: “fog-caught at the lip,” “jar-rested three nights,” “lifted from compost,” “drawn from bulb flesh.” They can tell you who handled it last, the way other places track coins.

#### Roles you meet by walking the lanes
- **Dew-minders**: early-risers who climb roof edges in half-light to brush dew combs into collection troughs, watching for clogged weave, for dust that will steal yield. Their wrists are often wrapped—not for injury but to keep skin oils from contaminating porous ceramics.
- **Jarwrights**: potters and sealers whose craft is almost priestly. A good jar is a promise; a bad seal is a slow murder. They know clays the way sailors know currents, and they trade for ash and lime like other towns trade for spice.
- **Compost keepers**: not gardeners, but engineers of decay. They manage shaded vaults where chaff, skins, droppings, and scraps are layered to trap moisture, heat, and nitrogen. They are listened to in council because they can predict how “wet” the settlement will feel a month from now.
- **Ledge-herders**: people who can read a hoofprint on nearly bare rock and tell which animal is limping by the irregularity of dust scuffs. They spend days outside the bowl on knife-thin routes, returning with dung in sacks more carefully tied than food.
- **Resin-tenders**: cutters of wax-skinned shrubs who know how much you can take without killing the plant. Resin here is glue, sealant, medicine, lamp fuel extender, waterproofing, and sometimes—mixed with salts—a faintly glowing paint used for steps.
- **Shellsmiths**: not metalworkers so much as chitin-and-ceramic artisans. Beetle wing-cases become beads, clasps, scale-plates for forearm guards used when climbing through abrasive corridors. A good shellsmith can make something that looks decorative until you realize it’s functional armor against the mountain itself.
- **Wind-shapers**: masons who build walls that do not block air but *teach it where to go*—guiding currents to cool a granary, to keep dust from a cistern room, to make a certain alley sing as a warning when gusts rise.

Leadership is practical and rotating, but not democratic in the way outsiders might recognize. People who manage moisture and storage accrue a quiet authority: they know what can be spared, what cannot, and what will happen if a season turns thin. Councils are often held in granary shade, among jars, because nothing focuses the mind like being surrounded by the settlement’s true wealth.

### Kinship with the nonhuman: not pets, not pests—neighbors
Here, “wild” and “domestic” don’t split cleanly. Much of the fauna is half-tolerated, half-managed, because the settlement itself is an irresistible cliffside reef of warmth, scraps, ledges, and cavities.

#### The air-thin birds
The stiff-feathered updraft riders are everywhere—black and dust-gray silhouettes that seem to hang rather than fly. They are thieves, yes, but also sanitation, pollination, and early warning. People watch their flocking the way coastal towns watch tides. If the birds shift nesting pockets en masse, it means a wind change is coming; if they start raiding deeper into terraces, it means something higher up has failed—food, safety, or both.

Some families maintain **pocket-rights**: understood claims to certain honeycombed cavities where birds nest. It isn’t ownership in the legal sense—birds do not sign contracts—but a long tradition of toleration and exchange. People leave certain fibrous nest materials; birds, in return, leave droppings in predictable places or keep rival predators pressured away. When birds die, their stiff feathers are harvested for brushes used in dust-sweeping and dew-comb cleaning—an intimate loop of dependence.

#### The vertical “goats”
The sure-footed mammals are not fully domesticated, but they are not truly wild either. Think of them as cliff-kin: they breed in the ledges, but their routes and habits are shaped by the settlement’s presence. They come to known salt-lick stones—salt stones that sometimes faintly glow at night from mineral colonies—and in those places herders can count, cull, and collect.

People value their **fat** as much as their meat. Fat is stored water in another form: it extends lamp oil, enriches stews, and makes skin salves that slow evaporation. Hides become water-resistant wraps; sinew becomes thread that outlasts plant fiber in the abrasive dust.

A newborn kid is often carried into the basin briefly—not to tame it like a pet, but to imprint it on human smell so it will return near terraces, making future dung collection and occasional harvesting possible. It’s a delicate bargain: too much taming and the animal loses its cliff sense; too little and it vanishes into the spine’s endless ribs.

#### Insects: the true majority
The place’s density belongs to insects. Thick-bodied pollinators hum through shaded gardens, guided by faint bioluminescent salts smeared on trellis edges—subtle beacons that keep them working in dimness. Pale burrowers turn compost with an efficiency that makes human labor look ceremonial. Beetles with hard, varnished shells thrive in warm crevices; their larvae are sometimes farmed deliberately in boxes of chaff, because larvae are protein that requires almost no water input once established.

Insect life is so foundational that children learn to handle it gently the way other children learn to handle fire. A crushed pollinator is not a small tragedy; it is a future shortfall.

Predatory insects exist too—mantis-things, fast and angled—that are tolerated in gardens as living pest control. People do not romanticize them. They simply accept that in a room this crowded, you cannot remove teeth from the ecosystem without starving yourself.

#### The night-sneaks: fox-cats of the terraces
The catlike/foxlike predators are the settlement’s shadow-tax. They slip through at night, taking birds, rodents, sometimes a kid if a latch fails. They are hated when losses are high, respected when they keep certain vermin populations from exploding, and always watched.

Some households keep **scent-lines**—resin rubbed with bitter plant compounds—along favored entry points. Others leave decoy scraps in predictable places, bribing the predators away from granary doors. A few bold people learn individual animals by gait and ear-nick and treat them like dangerous neighbors: negotiated with rather than exterminated. Total removal would create a different disaster.

### The plants: built like containers, behaving like citizens
Vegetation here doesn’t read as “greenery” so much as infrastructure with a pulse.

- The wax-skinned shrubs are the settlement’s sealant and pharmacy. They are tapped carefully; their resin is warmed and worked like a craft material. The shrubs grow in microcolonies, each one a long-term investment. A family might inherit a particular crack-garden the way others inherit an orchard.
- The ropey air-drinking vines are literal *bridges*. People train them across gaps, braid them with fiber for strength, and use them to shade walls that would otherwise radiate too much heat. They are alive, but also treated as civic architecture—trimmed, redirected, and repaired after windstorms.
- Lichen and moss-like colonies are the settlement’s quiet alchemy: they take bare stone and, by slow dying and regrowth, begin the first thin manufacture of “soil.” In shaded alleys you can see where people have protected a patch for years, fencing it from foot traffic the way a town might fence a well.

In the gardens, succulents and grains are less “crops” than *storage devices*. Bulbs hold water behind waxy skins; grains hold calories without moisture. Harvest time looks strange to outsiders: people do not merely cut and carry—they *ration the plant’s own stored dampness*. A bulb may be eaten fresh when someone is ill or a jar has failed; otherwise it is sliced and dried into leathery crescents, because dried food is safer than wet food in a place where rot can become a thief of moisture.

### Micro-life and glow: the basin’s quiet lanterns
The faint blue-green bioluminescence is not decoration. It is a sign of mineral-loving colonies—salt-storing organisms that live on rock faces, in the weave of certain vines, in the porous surfaces of ceramic combs. People cultivate them the way other places cultivate yeast: scraping a little from a thriving patch and inoculating a new trellis or step-edge.

At night, the settlement reads itself by these dim marks. Safe routes are outlined in living glow; dangerous drop-edges are deliberately kept dark. Children learn early: glow means “touched often,” “kept,” “maintained.” Darkness means “don’t trust this.”

Some households keep a jar with a glowing colony inside—a living indicator. If it brightens, salts are high and food stores are stable; if it dims, something is off: too much dust, too little dew, an imbalance in compost. It’s not magic; it’s a household instrument.

### Domestic life: a culture of sealing, scraping, and sharing air
Homes are close, and so intimacy is communal whether desired or not. People sleep in layers—older family deeper in the cool, children nearer entrances where their heat is useful. Kitchens are more like workbenches than hearths; fire is controlled, fed small, and banked carefully because ash is precious and runaway flame would turn the basin into an oven.

Cleanliness is dry. Morning routines include:
- scraping dust from skin with dry grass bundles,
- rubbing hands with ash to lift oils,
- brushing textiles with stiff feathers,
- shaking sleeping mats over the dust-trough so the settlement’s “waste canal” can be swept and sorted.

The dust-trough is where you see how seriously they take cycles. It isn’t just swept; it’s *read*. Seeds that arrive on the wind are collected and tested. Shed skins and husks are separated for compost. The trough is a ledger of what the basin has been eating and what the mountain has been offering.

Meals are quiet for how crowded they are. Chewing is loud in dry air; people learn to eat with their mouths closed, to drink sparingly, to savor fat and salt because both feel like relief. Hospitality is real but measured: offering food is common; offering water is intimate, like lending someone your own future breath.

### Birth, aging, and the shape of time
Infants are born into a world that cannot afford wasted moisture. New mothers are given fat-rich broths, and older women—often the true managers of household storage—take over dew collection for a while, because a missed week can ripple through a season.

Children are raised in vertical freedom early, because competence is safety. A toddler learns ladders before they learn long flat runs. Play involves:
- tossing beetle shells to hear their click,
- racing along known safe ledges,
- daring one another to stand at the basin’s lip and name cloud-shapes in the haze.

Elders do not “retire” so much as shift from hauling to judging: they become the ones who know which plaster recipe keeps a jar-room cool, which vine braid holds best in a wind shift, which bird call means “predator on the terraces.” Their value is memory, and memory here is infrastructure.

Death is treated with the same blunt thrift as everything else, but not without tenderness. Bodies are not wasted. Depending on custom and necessity, they may be placed in high honeycomb cavities to dry to bone (preventing rot that would steal moisture into uselessness), then later their bones are ground for mineral amendment, or kept intact as ancestors in sealed niches. The line between reverence and practicality is thin because both serve continuity.

### Their temperament: neither desperate nor serene—competent
From below, outsiders might imagine them as grim ascetics clinging to rock. From within, they are busy, argumentative, inventive, and—when the fog comes—briefly exuberant in a way that looks almost childish.

They do not dream of turning the basin into a wetter place. That kind of dream would only teach disappointment. They dream of tighter seals, better combs, a new vine route, a compost vault that runs warmer, a bird pocket that can be coaxed into loyalty.

They are not an oasis people. They are a *hanging-world people*: experts in living where water only visits as a trace, where every material has two uses, where the mountain’s old, finished shape makes a room—and they have learned how to furnish it with life.

And if you stand in the amber dim and listen long enough, you start to hear what truly “lives” here: not just organisms, but an ongoing craft. A settlement that behaves like an organism itself—catching, storing, reusing, shedding—breathing against the void with thousands of small, practiced acts.
