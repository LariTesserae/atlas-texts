At first you don’t notice *who* lives here so much as you notice *how* life moves when the ground is always deciding where to be wet.

There’s a choreography to the terraces: bodies angled slightly forward because every surface is either slick stone or greedy grit; hands always busy with a strap or a knot or a basket rim; heads turning in quick checks because sound doesn’t travel far enough to warn you in time. Even the animals seem to have learned the local rule that falling is expensive. Hooves place themselves carefully. Wings lift without fuss. Small predators don’t sprint; they thread.

From the rim, the settlement reads as a compact crust—layered roofs and walkways pasted to the cirque wall like something that grew there rather than something built. But down among it, you start to see that nearly everything alive in the bowl belongs to one of three kinds of inhabitants:

1) those that turn seep-water into food,  
2) those that turn food into warmth and work,  
3) those that turn the whole arrangement back into opportunity.

And the people—because there are people, unmistakably—sit in the middle, acting less like owners than like careful conductors of an orchestra that never raises its voice.

## The people: terrace-dwellers, water-readers, knot-makers

They don’t look like a single “type” at first. The light is too harsh for that; it picks out differences the way it picks out frost on rope. Some are broad and heavily wrapped; others are leaner, bare-armed in the midday glare, skin mottled with old cold-burn. Hair is kept close or braided tight, not for fashion but because loose strands collect rime that later melts down the neck. The older ones often carry a permanent shine on their cheeks and brows where damp air has polished skin like stone.

Clothing is layered in a way that doesn’t billow. You see stitched hides, yes, but not the shaggy romantic kind—these are clipped, repaired, resewn until the nap is almost gone. Under that: reed-mat panels woven tight, treated with grease and mineral dust so they shed water. Over joints and shoulders: thin plates of slate or hardened leather as much for abrasion against wet rock as for cold. Everything fastens with toggles and bone pins that can be undone with numb fingers. Metal, when it appears, is never decorative; it’s a buckle that has lived three lives already.

They speak, but they don’t waste breath. Words are for arm’s-length work, for the inside of rooms, for ritual moments where everyone leans close. Out on the terraces they use a fluent economy of gesture:

- a whole hand tilted down means “moving ground,”
- two fingers tapped on the collarbone means “water is changing,”
- a circling wrist means “follow the channel, not me,”
- a flat palm held up between bodies means “don’t bring that problem here.”

Their city is built for eyes: sightlines down steps, across roofs, through latticed screens. People pause at predictable “look points” the way others might pause at street corners. It isn’t suspiciousness so much as mutual orientation—if sound won’t carry, you make yourself visible.

### What they do all day
Most work here is water-work, even when it doesn’t look like it.

There are the obvious jobs: cutting catch-steps into stone, clearing grit from channels, mending cistern linings with reed mats and pitch. But the subtler labors are what keep the place alive:

- **They curate wetness.** Not “drain it” or “avoid it”—curate it. The good wet is thin and moving; the bad wet is black, still, hungry. People learn to read it the way others read cloud shapes. They’ll touch a wall with the back of their fingers, smell a seep, watch how a droplet forms. The difference between safe ground and moving ground can be the thickness of a film.

- **They preserve warmth without fire.** Fires happen, but smoke is a resource you ration when it climbs and spreads into that flat shining layer. So they use animal heat, compost heat, sun-reflection, and bodies clustered in insulated rooms. Roofs are angled to bounce light into narrow courtyards. Storage niches are cut into stone where the temperature stays steady. Many tasks happen in bright cold rather than indoors, because indoors is where damp gathers.

- **They are obsessive about tying things together.** The paired-lashing taboo isn’t only superstition. Posts tied to posts make a net of tension; a net can redistribute a sudden softening of ground. The whole settlement behaves like a flexible raft pinned to rock. The people who are best at this—rope-makers, lashers, those who know which fiber swells and which shrinks—hold status that outsiders don’t expect. In a place where stone moves, a good knot is governance.

If you spend enough time watching, you see roles emerging, not as uniforms but as patterns of attention:

- **Water-readers** (often older, sometimes young with the right eye) move slowly, stooping often, checking seep lines and frost fans. They mark changes with mineral chalk on stone: a slash for “new wet,” a circle for “do not build,” a line of dots for “safe for now.” The marks are constantly overwritten; the city is a palimpsest of caution.

- **Keepers of the basins** manage the catch-steps like gardens. They skim insects and debris, tend little mats of moss that act as filters, and decide which pools belong to drinking, which to soaking hides, which to amphibians, which to the birds. Their hands always smell faintly of minerals.

- **Terrace-herders** run the shaggy grazers through narrow routes the way others run brooms. The animals crop sedge tufts and lichen and, just as importantly, stomp and compact certain soils while avoiding the black bands that would swallow hoof and leg. Herding here is as much geology management as it is animal care.

- **Dry-rack workers**—often adolescents, agile and fearless—live half their lives under eaves. They move tools, nets, hides, and reed mats through microclimates: this spot for fast drying in glare, this spot for slow curing in shade, this line for wind-shear that doesn’t whistle but will stiffen a hide beautifully if you time it right.

Children are everywhere and nowhere. They’re the quietest bodies in the streets because they’ve been trained into it early—no running on stone, no shouting across terraces, no stepping on a black band no matter how tempting. Their play is practice: balancing along lashed poles, counting drip rhythms, catching cold-water amphibians barehanded without startling them into hiding.

### What they believe (without needing it to be “religion”)
Their taboos read like engineering, but the feeling behind them is older than engineering.

They treat the mountain as an active participant—not a god exactly, not a monster, something closer to a neighbor with moods and habits. They don’t “conquer” seepage; they negotiate it. When a new wet line appears on a wall, someone will press a palm to the stone and leave it there for a long breath, as if acknowledging an arrival. When a channel goes dry, there’s a small, practical grief: *we planned around you.*

There are offerings, but they look like maintenance: a carefully cleaned runoff path left unobstructed; a scrap of fat rubbed into a post so it doesn’t crack; a first cup poured into a basin rather than drunk. The act says: *we’re not pretending we did this alone.*

## The grazers: heavy-shouldered, sure-footed, communal heat

The herd animals are the settlement’s moving infrastructure. Goat-like, yes, but with a compact mass that seems designed to retain heat and to resist being shoved by sudden gust-shears. Their hooves are broad and slightly splayed, gripping slick limestone the way a climber’s hand grips a hold.

They live close to people—not in a sentimental way, but in an intimate logistical way. Pens are woven into the architecture; some roofs are built to accommodate animal warmth rising into sleeping spaces above. In the coldest stretches, humans and animals share walls, and everyone pretends it’s only about efficiency.

They are not gentle creatures, but they are *used* to being touched. Herding involves contact: a hand on a shoulder to steer, fingers checking for ice packed between hoof plates, quick grooming to prevent rime clumping into painful mats. The animals know the route-logic of the terraces; they hesitate at black grit the way people do.

Their waste isn’t waste. It’s dried on racks, mixed with grit, used for slow heat and for sealing. In a bowl where wood is scarce, every burnable thing is treated like a stored season.

## The birds: ledge-nesters, insect-harvesters, bright-eyed neighbors

The long-necked marsh-birds are the basin’s sudden motion. They stand as if carved from pale weather—long lines, tucked patience—until some invisible signal passes and the whole ledge goes weightless with wings.

They nest where stone weeps because the insects come there, and because those wet ledges resist certain predators. Their nests are surprisingly tidy: woven with reed scraps stolen from human bundles, lined with down that holds heat even in damp.

The relationship between bird and person is not simple. People protect some ledges, raid others. Eggs are taken, but not carelessly; there are rules about how many, and from which nests, and in what weather. A bad raid year means fewer birds the next year, and fewer birds means more insects in the wrong places—biting, breeding, spreading into food stores. So the people talk about the birds the way farmers talk about rain: as something you can influence at the edges but never command.

Sometimes, when fog comes luminous and the silhouettes turn too crisp, you’ll see birds perched along rooflines as if the settlement is just another cliff. They watch people at work with an unnerving steadiness. No one throws stones at them. That would be like insulting the basin’s own balance.

## The amphibians: dark, slow, necessary

In the seep basins live the thick-skinned amphibians—creatures that look too ancient for such a bright place, as if they belong to the mountain’s memory rather than its present. They are sluggish until touched, and then they move with sudden, muscular certainty into cracks you didn’t know were there.

Children catch them in practiced ways. It isn’t sport; it’s harvesting, inspection, sometimes simple caretaking. Amphibians are food, yes—protein in a land where plant promises are small—but they are also indicators. If amphibians vanish from a basin, something has changed in the water: temperature, minerals, an underground reroute. The people notice. They adjust.

A few basins are left deliberately “wild,” unharvested, as reference points. That choice reads like wisdom: you keep one eye on what the mountain does when you aren’t meddling.

---

## The small predators: seam-walkers, thieves, cleanup crew

Between walls and pens live the sleek hunters—marten-like, weasel-quick, fur dark enough to look wet even when dry. They eat eggs, insects, scraps, the occasional careless chick, and they also keep worse infestations down. They are thieves and sanitation at once.

People tolerate them in the way you tolerate a sharp neighbor who steals your tools but also scares off bandits. There are traps, but not everywhere. Some households leave a deliberate scrap outside a storage niche as a bribe, a way of saying: *take this, not that.*

These predators move like the wind shears: present, absent, never loud. You’ll catch a flash of them on a roof-walkway and then nothing—only the awareness that you’ve been watched from close range.

## Insects: the bowl’s true abundance

If you want to know what *really* lives here, look at the insects.

They are the place’s pulse because they respond first to the micro-changes: a sunlit shelf warming by a few degrees, a new seep line altering mineral taste, a patch of moss turning from resilient to lush. They emerge in bursts—brief, synchronized, astonishing—and then vanish into seams.

People have built their routines around these bursts. Nets hang ready under eaves. Thin screens can be swung into place over channels to catch particular swarms without clogging waterflow. Some insects are dried for food, ground into flour-like meal, mixed into fats. Others are bait for birds or amphibians. Certain larvae are used as grease sources, certain wings as waterproofing additives in resin. In a place where nothing can be wasted, insects are not merely tolerated; they are incorporated.

Even the grazers rely on them indirectly: insect-rich basins mean bird activity; bird activity means droppings; droppings fertilize the reluctant sedge tufts and moss edges. The food web is a tight circle, and the circle’s smallest bodies are its fastest currency.

## The quiet collective: a city that behaves like a single organism

What emerges, the longer you watch, is that the “dense little city” is not just humans living among animals and water. It is a composite creature made of:

- stone cut into catch-steps (its ribs),
- channels and cisterns (its vessels),
- rope lashings and paired posts (its tendons),
- grazers moving like slow blood through terraces,
- birds lifting like a shared startle reflex,
- insects surging like nerves,
- people reading and responding like a distributed mind.

When the groundwater shifts and a channel goes dry, you can see the organism adapt in real time. No alarm bells, no shouted orders. Just a subtle redistribution: someone redirects a walkway; a herder changes route; a basin-keeper covers a pool; a ropeworker tightens a line; a child carries a message by running close, not by calling out.

The bowl’s strangest physical oddities—the droplets forming under shelves, frost pointing toward light—aren’t treated as miracles or omens so much as *features with consequences*. If condensation gathers where it “shouldn’t,” someone will put a bowl there. If frost fans toward the bright patch of sky, someone will read it as a map of invisible currents and choose that spot to dry a hide.

So what lives here, ultimately, is not one thing.

A human community lives here, yes—practical, interdependent, skilled in silent communication, culturally shaped by the need to see and tie and yield. Herd animals live here, giving heat and labor and steadiness. Birds live here, both harvested and heeded. Amphibians live here, both eaten and consulted. Predators live here, tolerated as the price of a functioning ecology. Insects live here in numbers that make the whole basin feel, at times, more alive than solid.

And beneath all of them, in the weeping stone and the wet grit, something else lives too: the mountain’s slow hydraulics, its cold breath, its habit of rearranging the rules without raising its voice.

The inhabitants—every species present—have become expert at one shared art:

Living as if the ground is listening, because here it probably is.
