What lives here is mostly not the sort of thing you can point to and say *there—an animal*. The drowned upland is a country of producers and arrangers: organisms that make their own body-matter out of water, minerals, and scant light, and organisms that ride the boundaries between sea and stone like a third element.

At first you think it’s only plants—an oceanic botany swollen to fill an empty world—but the longer you stay the more “plant” stops being a clean category. In this place, the living things borrow traits the way the currents borrow “up.”

### The tall frond-forests (the obvious citizens)

The dominant bodies are the pillar-kelps: long-corded, wrist-thick growers that rise from anchor stones and rubble fans. They don’t have trunks so much as *tethers*—braided tissues with an elastic patience. Their movement is slow enough to seem ceremonial, but it isn’t indecision. It’s governance: they flex to keep blades from tearing, to keep holds from popping loose, to keep the forest open enough that water can still move and bring the thin ration of dissolved minerals.

Without mouths to bite them back, they grow in architectures that look excessive until you realize the excess is doing work:

- **Overhang curtains** that hang into channels and comb out silt, turning current into food.
- **Vaulted domes** across gullies that create still pockets; the water inside goes quiet and “ages,” growing chemically different from the main flow.
- **Layered canopies** where old blades are not shed so much as repurposed into shade, baffles, and substrate for other life.

They reproduce by letting go. During the loud weather—the rain-walls, the churn—some pillar-kelps loosen their holds and tumble like slow stones until they find a new scar of rock. Others split, leaving a half behind like a promise kept to the old place.

In their tissues are mineral hoards: salts, rust-stains, pale crusts. Not decoration—banked chemistry. When the plateau’s water turns toward “sky” and the upper layers thin and rearrange, those hoards buffer the plants through abrupt shifts in density and direction.

### The encrusted vent-gardens (the tough ones)

Around the warm mineral mouths live the short, armored forms: squat fronds, crusted fans, scaled pads that look as if they’ve learned to wear rock.

These are not merely heat-tolerant; they’re *stone-literate*. Many exude mild acids, not to dissolve the world in anger but to edit it: to loosen trace metals and rare salts, to build hard tissue where being soft would be fatal. Some of them grow in spirals around vents, not because spirals are beautiful but because spirals keep each frond within reach of a particular gradient—warmth here, iron there, a trickle of carbonate at the edge.

In the vent zones you meet the first clear sign that “no animals” doesn’t mean “no appetites.” The vent-gardens host films—microbial, glossy, often colorless until they flash—whose metabolism is a kind of slow lightning. They don’t hunt; they *react*. When a vent exhales, when a mineral skin cracks and fresh surfaces meet seawater, these films drink the chemical difference and sometimes spill a brief, pallid glow as waste.

Those momentary lights aren’t for signaling. They’re the visible edge of a chemical feast.

### The filament mats and ruffles (the quiet occupiers)

On every broad blade and terrace, finer lives stack themselves:

- **Mossy filaments** that trap silt and turn it into a felted soil.
- **Lettuce-ruffles** whose frilled edges multiply surface area, drinking whatever scant nutrients the water carries.
- **Delicate fans** so thin they tremble at turbulence, and that trembling—strangely—seems to matter.

Because sound carries too well, vibration is a reliable way to know the world. Many of these fine forms have tissues that stiffen or relax in response to pressure waves. A far-off ice crack can tighten a whole patch of fan-growth the way a shadow makes land plants draw in. Over time, that becomes a kind of weather sense: not foresight, not thought, but an inherited attentiveness to the plateau’s loud habits.

The filaments also make the first real *boundaries* you can feel: places where the water slows, where fog-water thickens, where rain becomes ocean without a line. The mats turn that ambiguity into habitat, creating zones of different viscosity and chemistry—little rooms in the moving house.

### The drifters that “walk” (life as relocation)

Some species are built around the fact that “up” is negotiable here. They are buoyancy-agnostic: their internal bladders don’t insist on rising; they tune themselves to the current’s opinion. When flow changes, they detach, roll, hover, then root again.

Seen together, they make migration look like gardening. After a violent rain-wall passes, you can come back to a corridor and find that the whole arrangement has re-sorted: a grove moved fifty meters; a terrace acquired a new fringe; a gully that was open has been curtained.

Nothing chased anything else into those positions. The place simply *re-composed* itself using living pieces.

### The weavers (the ones that explain the tools without requiring “people”)

The built structures—cairn-pairs, hanging cords, nets that hum—don’t have to belong to hands in the human sense. There are organisms here that manipulate fibers because fiber is what they are.

Along ridges and in calmer bowls, you find a particular kind of growth that behaves less like a plant and more like a distributed craft:

- It sends out long, tough tendrils that seek tension the way roots seek moisture.
- It thickens where the current pulls, lays down braided strands where rubbing would abrade a single cord.
- It uses stones—picked up, dropped, repositioned not with fingers but with adhesive pads and slow constriction—as weights, anchors, and resonators.

Over years it can produce what looks like deliberate architecture: a current-tight shelter made of overlapping blades, a line of uprights stabilized by living lashings, a net strung between anchor stones. The “tools” inside those shelters—scrapers, wedges, a glossy-handled hammer-stone—can be understood as byproducts of this weaving life: stones worn smooth where the organism grips them repeatedly; hardened stem-sections shaped by abrasion and pressure into useful edges; mineral-cemented pieces that break along predictable lines.

If you watch long enough in a calm pocket, you may catch it in the act—not moving like an animal, not choosing like a person, but slowly tightening a loop, slowly shifting a weight, adjusting a whole structure until the local flow becomes gentler, and the organism—and all the smaller lives it hosts—can persist.

The nets that hum at the plateau edge are especially telling. They don’t catch fish, but they do catch *events*: debris, ice pellets, the increased push of spillway surges. Their vibration isn’t a song for an audience; it’s a way of turning force into information that spreads through the weavers’ tissues. The plateau is loud, and the weavers have made that loudness legible.

### The unseen majority (the chemistry-eaters)

In the deepest basins and along stained rock faces, the true biomass may be microscopic: biofilms that live where gradients are sharpest—warm to cold, iron-rich to iron-poor, oxygen-thin to oxygen-thicker. They form slicks, skins, dustings that look like mineral deposits until they bloom briefly into light or change the texture of stone.

They are the ones who make the place feel *established* despite its violence. They stabilize surfaces, trap sediments, and lay down coatings that become the next generation’s foothold. In a world without grazers to “reset” things, these films serve as both foundation and historian: each layer a record of a particular season of currents, a particular era of vent breath.

### What kind of “mind” lives here?

If there is intelligence here, it is not housed in a skull. It is not quick. It does not hunt. It is distributed through forests and nets and mats as responsiveness: an ability to read flow, to preserve calm pockets, to harvest sound as warning, to edit the plateau’s water into gradients that favor life.

The drowned upland’s inhabitants don’t compete by outrunning each other; they compete by *arranging conditions*. They alter current, they trap silt, they farm minerals, they tune themselves to the plateau’s loud, habitual violence. The closest thing to a predator is a chemistry that will strip you if you rest too long on the wrong vent-stained stone.

So the plateau is not empty. It is occupied—thickly—by lives that don’t need eyes, teeth, or blood to be decisive. The forests are their bodies. The nets are their long-term gestures. The brief cold glows are their meals made visible for a second.

And the old human word for such a thing—*ecosystem*—almost fits, except that here it behaves less like a web of mouths and more like a piece of engineering that learned how to grow.
