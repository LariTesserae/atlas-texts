The ground here is not ground so much as a lid—thin, flexing, saturated—over a shallow press of water. Every footfall, every vibration from the city’s machinery, makes the surface answer with a delayed wobble. The terrain is low and decided: a basin that has been itself for a long time, its slopes so slight you only notice them by how the wet chooses to settle. Channels remember old paths the way creases remember old folds. Some are straightened into canals with riveted sides; others are simply darker runs in the vegetation where water keeps insisting on passage.

Hot air hangs close to the skin. It doesn’t rise cleanly. It swells, thick with steam and plant breath, and then stalls under a ceiling of cloud and industrial haze. The light is always dim—not night-dim, but as if daylight has been filtered through wet cloth. The brightest thing in the place is not the sky but the occasional electrical glare: a lamp behind a grimy glass cage, a welding arc hidden somewhere down a corridor of warehouses, a row of indicator bulbs on a control panel half-swallowed by creepers.

Water is everywhere and never merely puddled. It beads along stone like sweat. It films metal like oil. It condenses in sheets on the undersides of walkways and then lets go in sudden, loud dumps. Even the old masonry seems to weep: brick walls darken in vertical streaks, mortar seams glisten, and from hairline cracks drip-lines form, patient as clocks. The tide—if it is a tide—moves without a visible body of sea. At intervals the water level in the canals lifts by a few centimeters, as though the whole district inhales; later it settles again, as though exhaling into the mud.

The city built here did not fight the wet; it collaborated with it. Streets are rare. Most routes are catwalks, narrow bridges, and plankways laid like sentences between structures. Buildings stand on short pylons or broad foundations that distribute weight into the saturated earth. Everywhere there are pumps: squat iron housings with bolted plates, flywheels turning slowly, intake grates furred with green. Some pumps are new enough to show paint; others are ancient, their labels worn blank, their casings scalloped by rust into soft, edible-looking shapes. Still, they thud and labor. They are part of the ecology now—organs that never learned to stop.

The loudness is constant and layered. Not one sound, but a stacked architecture of sound:

- the steady, chest-deep pulse of the big pump stations,
- the hiss of steam escaping from valves that no one has adjusted in years,
- the rattle of loose sheet metal in warm gusts,
- the slap of water against canal walls,
- the crack of settling timbers,
- and, threading through it all, the ceaseless dripping—fast as applause in some places, slow and ceremonial in others.

What’s missing makes the noise stranger. There are no calls, no wings, no insect whine. Even the water surfaces are too clean of disturbance—no rings of feeding, no darting shadows. The loudness has no living counterpoint. It is all process without witness.

Vegetation is lush, but it is not crowded. It grows in decisive, spaced presences, leaving broad patches of open water or bare, black mud between. The plants here have learned the geometry of industry: vines climb only the angles that hold, wrapping I-beams and ladder rungs with a careful spiral that looks engineered. Broad-leafed shrubs take root in the silt that gathers in corners of rooftops, their roots threading through gravel ballast and down into gutter pipes to drink. In the bright damp understructures—beneath walkways, inside culverts—mats of moss and algae form thick, velvety fields, punctuated by sudden, pale fungal shelves that flare out of wood like frozen foam.

The green is not purely green. It carries the stains of the place: coppery iridescence where runoff has pooled, a bruised purple sheen on leaves that catch certain vapors, and a dusting of pale salt crystals on stems nearest the vents. Some trees—mangrove-like, with buttressed roots—stand in the shallows by the canal edges, each one a small island of shade. They are spaced far enough apart that you can see between them into long, dim lanes of water.

There is an established balance here, not peaceful but stable: a long negotiation between rust, growth, and flow. Erosion has done its slow work. Brick corners are rounded. Stone steps have been hollowed into shallow bowls that always hold warm rainwater. Iron has blistered into layered scales. Yet nothing is collapsing all at once; the place has settled into a way of failing that can last.

The strangest rule of the district is how the air behaves around moving machinery. Wherever belts run and pistons cycle, condensation does not simply form—it organizes. Moisture gathers in the rhythm of the engine, bead-lines appearing at regular intervals along rails and cables, then releasing together. Stand near a turning flywheel and you can watch droplets lift from your skin and hang for a heartbeat, as if the machine has a brief claim on the water in the air. In the quietest pockets—under a collapsed awning, inside a stairwell choked with fern—you can sometimes see thin threads of mist flowing *toward* sound, not away from it, like smoke seeking a mouth.

This may be why there are no animals. It is not only the heat, not only the chemicals, not only the absence of clean niches. Something about the constant mechanized rhythm seems to pull small life out of its own patterns. There are no nests, no tracks, no gnawed edges. Seeds sprout readily; spores bloom; leaves unfurl as if nothing is wrong. But nothing with a pulse stays. If a bird ever crosses overhead, it does not do so low. If an insect ever hatches, it does not remain long enough to be heard.

People—if they are still part of this place—are present only through their design. Infrastructure persists as intention made solid: sluice gates, numbered pipe runs, inspection hatches stamped with dates, warning signs softened by mildew. A narrow-gauge track disappears into a warehouse whose doors have fused half-open with rust, inside which conveyor belts still crawl like slow tongues, moving nothing from one empty bay to another. There are reservoirs on rooftops where water is collected, warmed, and fed back into the system; their overflow spills in steady curtains that keep certain walls perpetually washed clean of dust. In the dim, those curtains catch stray light and turn it into thin, trembling silver.

The boundaries of the whole landscape are close. This is not a continent of swamp, not a sprawling metropolis. It is a district—a basin-sized pocket where the city decided to put its wet work: cooling, pumping, refining, draining. You can stand at one end and, through the gauze of steam, sense the other end by the slightly different pitch of its machines. The place feels like a room with many partitions: alleys of water, corridors of pipe, courtyards of mud, each separated by walls of vegetation that are thick but not sprawling, each opening into another with the logic of a plant that has learned how humans build.

And it is active, relentlessly. The weather is never finished here. Rain comes in short, heavy arguments, then stops, leaving the heat to lift steam off every surface. The pumps answer rain with a deeper cadence. The canals rise and fall. Vents cough. Leaves tilt, drip, dry, and drip again. Rust swells and flakes. Roots pry, slowly, at mortar. The city and the swamp keep remaking the same compromise—loudly, dimly, in the hot wet air—while the only truly quiet thing is the absence of any animal life to mark time with breath.
