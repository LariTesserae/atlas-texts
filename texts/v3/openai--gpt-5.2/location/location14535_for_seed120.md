It is easiest to call it a garden, because it is green and wet and alive, but the word *garden* implies ground, and there is no ground here—only surfaces that have been persuaded, pressed, fired, woven, stitched, mortared, pegged, and tied until they behave like terrain.

You find it in a place you would never name as a place: behind a blind panel; inside the throat of a pillar; under a stair that climbs too high for anyone to admit it does. A pocket in the construction. Small enough that a person could pace it out in a few dozen steps, and yet it sits at an elevation that makes your ears remember the thinness of air and your bones remember the drop. The pocket is hidden not by distance but by *fit*: it exists in the seam where two enormous things meet imperfectly, and the world politely ignores seams.

### Terrain (without earth)

The “land” is a series of close surfaces—packed, immediate, everywhere. A steep wedge of wall becomes a slope because mats of root and woven fiber cling to it. A “ravine” is a narrow slot between two ribs of fired clay. “Boulders” are bulges of resin-coated wicker, hard as horn, swollen by damp. There is no bare substrate anywhere. If something looks like stone, it is a stone someone made: kiln-brick blackened to basalt; slag-glass chips set in mortar; blocks of salt-cement that sweat.

Everything is vertical, but not in the simple way of a cliff. Angles change when you stop looking. A ledge is level until it remembers it was meant to be part of a curve, and then it becomes a curve. The pocket has the feeling of being *unfinished*: edges do not erode, they simply end in fresh break-lines, as if the material is still deciding what shape it wants. There is no patina of age—only the rawness of something that has just arrived.

And it keeps arriving.

A slow extrusion runs along one side like a time-lapse of masonry: wet clay being pushed through a lattice mold, swelling into a new buttress. It is not a landslide; it is construction behaving like geology. Where it touches older surfaces, it does not join cleanly. It hesitates, and in that hesitation you can see the rules of the world wobble—two textures occupying the same place for a heartbeat before one wins.

### Water (near-surface, everywhere, not quite obeying)

The wetness is not a single pool so much as a constant, intimate contact. The air condenses on you the way breath condenses on a cup. Every surface is either sweating, beading, or dripping.

Water comes from three places:

1. **Condensation**: The pocket is dim and hot, so moisture is always falling out of the air in tiny, relentless drops. The drops don’t always fall downward.
2. **Weeping seams**: Between made blocks there are hairline joints that “weep” not because anything is behind them but because the joint itself has decided to be a spring. You can hear them at close range—soft, persistent clicking as beads form and release.
3. **A small fall**: There is a thin sheet of water that slides over a rib and down into a trough—loud for its size, amplified by the hollow construction. The trough overflows into a mesh of channels made from split reed and baked-clay gutters, all hand-laid. The channels are so dense they look like veins.

The strangest part: sometimes the water behaves as if elevation is a suggestion. It will pour *up* a wall in a thin, determined ribbon until it reaches a corner, then surrender and drip normally. If you watch long enough, you realize the water is not reversing gravity—gravity is locally *tilting*, briefly, like a floor settling.

### Heat and light (hot, dim, close)

It is hot in the way enclosed spaces become hot: humid, thick, and breathing. The heat source is not a sunbeam—it’s a pulse coming through the structure, as though some enormous body is moving heat past the pocket in waves. Every few minutes the temperature rises half a step; the air shimmers; the plants sweat; then it eases back.

The light is dim and greenish, filtered through layers of leaves and through a ceiling of woven slats crusted with mineral deposits. What illumination there is comes from:

- **A narrow vent** high above that admits a diluted, cloud-bright glare.
- **Bioluminescent films**: not showy mushrooms, but thin algal skins on wet brick that glow faintly, like the memory of daylight.
- **Reflected wetness**: water holding and scattering what little light exists.

Shadows are crowded. There is almost no empty air to cast a clean one.

### Materials (total built, primitive hands)

The whole pocket reads as engineered—yet not by metal, not by precision machining. This is primitive fabrication scaled into a world that is anything but primitive.

You see:

- **Mudbrick and fired clay**, finger-smoothed, with straw impressions still visible.
- **Wickerwork and rope lashings** made from tough, glossy fiber (some plant, some maybe not).
- **Bone-like struts**—possibly carved wood polished by humidity, possibly something grown and hardened.
- **Tar and resin seams**, sticky in the heat, smelling sharp.
- **Stone tools** left in niches: chert scrapers, a basalt hammer, an adze handle wrapped in vine. They are used often; they shine where hands have held them.

The construction is dense because everything does double duty. A support rib is also a channel guide. A gutter is also a root trellis. A wall is also a sponge: packed with porous shards to hold water.

There is no “natural” here, but there is plenty of *wild*—what life does when given structure and moisture and time.

### Vegetation (lush, aggressive, opportunistic)

The plants are everywhere and insist on being everywhere. They don’t politely occupy planters; they colonize surfaces, penetrate lashings, stitch themselves through latticework.

- **Ferns** with broad, dark fronds stack over one another like layered hands, catching condensation.
- **Vines** as thick as wrists loop around supports, their tendrils gripping rope as if it were bark.
- **Epiphytes** cling to vertical brick, roots spread like netting, feeding on wet air and mineral sweat.
- **Mats of moss and algae** turn gutters slippery, glowing faintly in places.
- **Bulbous water-storing plants**—gourd-like sacs—hang from overhead trellises, heavy with captured condensation.

The greenery is not serene. It is in motion: leaves trembling from constant drafts; vines tightening around supports; root-mats creeping centimeter by centimeter across new extrusions of clay, as if the garden is racing to claim fresh surface before reality changes its mind.

### Fauna (rare, but present as absence)

There are surprisingly few animals. The place feels too young or too unstable to host a complex food web. What you do notice are signs of *attempts*:

- A few **gnat-like insects**, not in swarms but in lonely, wandering pairs, drawn to wet light.
- One **pale lizard** (or something that resembles one) that never fully commits to being seen; it holds to cracks, emerging only when the heat pulses.
- A **single bird sound** sometimes—close, sudden—yet you almost never see a bird. The sound might be air moving through a particular vent that happens to resonate like a call.

What thrives instead are **decomposers**: molds, bacterial films, the quiet machinery that turns dead leaf into slick black smear. They are the pocket’s true animals, doing their work with no bodies big enough to watch.

### Sound (loud, constant, layered)

For something small, it is loud enough to press on you. The pocket is an amplifier—hollow behind its walls, ribbed with ducts and cavities. Every drip becomes a knock; every leaf tremor becomes a rustle that multiplies.

You hear:

- The **continuous hiss** of warm air being pulled past vents.
- **Water percussion**: tapping beads, the thin slap of the little fall, the gurgle of overloaded gutters.
- **Creaking**: lashings tightening, woven supports flexing, clay ribs settling as new material extrudes.
- A deep, periodic **sub-bass thrum** that is not from within the pocket at all—something vast in the surrounding structure shifting weight or drawing breath.

Sometimes the thrum hits and the whole pocket answers: droplets fall at once, leaves shiver, and for a second you can tell the “world” outside is not land or sky but an enormous constructed thing that is still moving.

### Dynamics (active world, multiple processes)

Nothing here is static. The place is a convergence of cycles:

- **Heat pulses** drive condensation, which feeds the plants, which overloads the gutters, which changes the weight distribution, which makes the structure creak and settle, which opens new weeping seams, which adds more water.
- **Construction-as-geology** extrudes fresh clay forms, and the vegetation immediately colonizes them, as if life is part of the building schedule.
- **Reality misalignment**—small, frequent—rearranges tiny details: a channel now empties into a different trough; a ledge becomes slightly steeper; a moss patch finds itself on a surface that didn’t exist yesterday.

Because erosion is not yet a thing here, nothing wears down gently. Instead, things *switch*. A corner is sharp until the moment it isn’t there at all. A crack stays hairline until it suddenly becomes a finger-wide seam, clean as a cut.

### Rules of the world (as learned by staying too long)

You can live here by learning its local physics:

1. **Gravity is mostly reliable, but locally negotiable.** In the pocket, “down” is a consensus reached by the largest surfaces. Small streams and loose leaves sometimes dissent.
2. **New material appears with intention, not randomness.** The extrusions and seams behave like a building that is still being built, except no builders are visible and no plan is legible.
3. **The pocket resists emptiness.** Any cleared space fills—first with moisture, then with fungal film, then with green. Even sound fills it; silence doesn’t last.
4. **Scale is not stable.** You can reach the far wall in a dozen steps, but if you pace absentmindedly—if you stop *counting*—the path lengthens. Not infinitely. Just enough to make you doubt your body.

The longer you remain, the more the pocket feels like a compromise between incompatible truths: a tiny hidden place perched impossibly high; a primitive, hand-wrought environment embedded in something vast and unknowable; a garden that is also a construction site; a wet, lush ecology running full force with almost no animals to witness it.

And beneath all of it, the sense that this is not a finished landscape but an early sentence in a language still inventing its grammar—hot, dim, loud, crowded with growth—trying, over and over, to decide what “place” will mean.
