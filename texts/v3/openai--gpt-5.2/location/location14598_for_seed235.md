The first thing you understand is that the wetness here is not weather; it is a property of the place. Water is always near—beaded on every surface, sewn into the air, packed under the skin of the ground the way sap is packed under bark. Even when the sky is clear, the stone weeps. Even when nothing moves, droplets form, fall, and are replaced with the same patient insistence, as if condensation is the land’s chosen heartbeat.

It is hot in a way that has nothing to do with noon. Heat lives in the rock. You feel it radiate from cliff faces long after light has shifted; you feel it trapped in hollows where air should cool but does not. The humidity is thick enough to have boundaries—warm, wet layers you can step through like curtains. Breath feels shared, already used by leaves and stone and the green films that slick the shaded sides of things.

And all of it is high. Not merely “up on a mountain,” but a region built out of elevation, a world whose basic unit is the drop.

Across the open distance—wide enough that your eyes want to measure it in days rather than minutes—the terrain rises and falls in enormous, established forms. Not raw young peaks, but slopes that have had time to settle into their own geometry: huge ribs of basalt and pale limestone with softened edges, terraces worn smooth by centuries of water’s insistence. There are plateaus that look like they were once lakebeds lifted into the sky, and knife-thin ridgelines where the ground becomes a decision. The valleys are not gentle; they are vertical slots that you look into and cannot find the bottom of, because cloud and water and sound fill them.

The water does not come in a single river you can follow. It is everywhere, distributed. Sheets of seep run down walls. Thin falls thread themselves from ledge to ledge. In some places it hangs in the air as a standing mist, dense as breath in winter—except it’s warm, and it clings.

Marsh exists here at altitude, improbably: hanging wetlands that drape off the edges of terraces. Their soils are dark and spongy, built from slow-accumulated plant matter that never quite dries and never quite rots the way you expect. Pools sit in stone basins, brimming, their surfaces trembling not from wind but from the constant pressure of sound. Where you would expect streams to carve and rearrange, they have instead found equilibrium—channels lined with tufa and living root-mat, always flowing the same way, always full, always at the same level, as if the entire watershed has been tuned and fixed.

The lushness is not a riot; it is a saturation. Every available plane has been claimed. Leaves layer over leaves in patterns like shingles. Vines are not “climbing” so much as inhabiting space—braiding between columns, bridging gaps, suturing broken stone. Ferns pour out of cracks in the rock in thick green fans. Mosses and liverworts make the ground look upholstered. Even the sunlit faces of boulders carry a lacquer of algae that glows when the light hits it at an angle.

Yet there is no animal life.

That absence is not quiet. It is felt in the way nothing startles, nothing flees, nothing hunts; in the way puddles have no rings from insects, in the way flowers have no visitors, in the way no small movements stitch the scene together. Leaves do not have bites taken from them. Fallen fruit—if fruit exists here—does not get carried away. The green persists and accumulates. It makes the place feel too complete, too self-contained, like a system missing one of its traditional gears and still running, smoothly, unnervingly, without it.

The city is what surprises you next—because it is undeniably urban, dense with intention and repeating structure, and yet it carries no trace of machinery, no metal, no smoke, no wheel-ruts, no glass. It is a city made of arrangements rather than devices, built out of what the landscape already provides.

At first it presents as terraces: hundreds of steps of land cut into the mountain’s sides, each one faced with fitted stone blocks that have been rounded by time and water. The blocks are not sharp-edged; their corners have softened, and the seams between them have thickened with green. Above and between the terraces are vertical faces pierced with openings—doorways, windows, ventilation slots—dark mouths from which cool wet air exhales.

Then you realize the terraces are streets.

They connect not by gentle ramps but by stairways that climb like ladders, and by ramps that cling to walls at angles that would be impossible if the rules were consistent. In places, a walkway wraps around a pillar of rock, and when it passes behind the pillar it returns on the other side higher than it was, without having visibly ascended. The first time you try to track it with your eyes, your sense of direction slips, like a hand on wet stone.

The buildings are carved and grown. Their main bodies are stone chambers hollowed into cliffs and freestanding towers that look like they were cast from mineral-rich water: porous, honeycombed, their surfaces made of old flow. Over these mineral bones, living architecture has been encouraged—roots trained into arches, thick stems braided into railings, curtains of vines used as doors. Roofs are leaf-mats layered so densely that rain, when it falls, becomes a controlled seep rather than a drip. There are plazas that are not paved but carpeted in tight, springy moss, kept short by no grazing animal and yet somehow uniform, as if the moss itself has agreed to an ordinance.

Everything is close. The density is not merely visual; it presses into the body. Narrow alleys between wet walls. Overhead bridges, underfoot channels. Hanging gardens that intrude into thoroughfares. Drainage runs everywhere—gutters that are really troughs of living wood, channels lined with polished stone—so that water is always present as a surface, a sound, a reflective moving skin. The settlement is layered vertically like a reef, but made of rock and plant and water instead of coral and fish.

And the sound—always the sound.

It is deafening, a constant that never swells and never diminishes, as stable as gravity is elsewhere. It comes from the falls, yes: the innumerable threads of water dropping into unseen basins, the roar of larger cataracts in the distant slots between ridges. But there is more to it than water should produce. The city itself resonates.

Some walls hum. Not metaphorically: they carry a steady tone you feel in your teeth. Narrow corridors act like organ pipes, taking the pressure of moving air and turning it into a sustained note. Hollow towers throb with standing waves. In the open plazas, the noise is a broad white roar that fills every gap in thought; in the alleys it becomes a layered chord, more structured, like being inside a vast instrument that someone has left playing and cannot turn off.

Voices would be pointless here. If there were people—if there ever were—speech would have to be something other than air and throat. The urbanity feels like the afterimage of community: markets without vendors, doorways without footsteps, places built for gathering that gather only sound.

There is evidence that the city has been here a long time, yet it does not crumble. Erosion has done its work and then stopped, as if the place reached a stable shape and decided to keep it. Stone edges are weathered, but they do not keep retreating. Moss thickens, but it does not overtake everything. Sediment fills channels up to a certain line and no more. In the same way that water holds itself at the same levels, the city holds itself in the same state: old, used, settled—and fixed.

Reality breaks here in small, consistent ways.

Distances behave like suggestions. A tower that looks a kilometer away can be reached in a minute if you take the right sequence of bridges; or it can remain distant no matter how long you walk toward it, if you insist on a straight line. When you look down from a terrace into a vertical slot, you can see a lower street that should be directly beneath you—but when you descend, you arrive somewhere else, and the lower street remains visible above your shoulder as though it never belonged to your path.

Water sometimes falls upward in thin filaments near certain walls, not as an active reversal but as a loop—droplets completing a circuit you can watch repeat with hypnotic precision. Mist rolls into archways and comes out as a denser fog with the same curling edge each time, as if poured from a mold. In the hottest courtyards, sunlight seems to hold still on the same patch of stone, bright without shifting, even though the sky elsewhere makes sense. Shadows have sharp borders that do not tremble, no matter how the leaves above them tremble in the constant air.

If you pay attention, you can begin to infer the rules that keep the place in its never-changing state:

* Patterns repeat exactly, but only locally. A particular drip line will always bead and fall at the same interval; a particular vine will always sway through the same arc; a particular patch of fog will always curl the same way. The world is animated by loops.
* Change is permitted only if it can settle into a stable form. A rock may fracture, but the fracture will instantly become a channel for water and root, and then it will remain that way forever. A leaf may fall, but it will land in a place where it becomes part of the spongy soil, and the soil line will not rise beyond its allowed height.
* Life here is allowed to grow, but not to wander. Plants can proliferate and braid and thicken, but they do not spread into new territory beyond the city’s and mountain’s established boundaries. There is a sense of invisible zoning in the ecology itself.

This stability makes the absence of animals feel less like an accident and more like compliance. Nothing with hunger, nothing with migration, nothing with the urge to leave or to rearrange, can persist under rules like these. Even decay—the animal of time—has been kept out.

And still the place is not sterile. It is lush and living, but in a way that resembles architecture: everything interconnected, everything serving structure. Roots form buttresses. Mats of moss act as water filters, keeping channels clear. Certain broad-leaf plants grow only along the edges of terraces, their leaves angled to catch and redirect mist onto the soil behind them. In shaded cisterns, thin films of green bloom on the water’s surface, then stop at a perfect boundary, leaving a clean circle in the center like an intentional aperture.

In the highest parts of the city—where terraces give way to sheer walls and the air is bright with suspended moisture—the atmosphere feels close to boiling without ever quite becoming unbearable. Heat shimmers over wet stone. Condensation forms on your skin as fast as sweat. The sky is an ordinary color, but it carries a constant haze that makes the far ridges appear doubled, as if the landscape has been printed slightly out of register.

From a vantage point where the city’s uppermost structures meet the raw mountain, you can see the region’s scale: the city clinging for miles along the broken heights, then trailing off into other built clusters on other peaks—like a chain of cliff-cities, each connected by narrow, implausible spans that vanish into cloud. Between them lie vast green shelves and hanging marshes, and beyond those, the open expanse of ridges marching away, their faces wet and shining.

No smoke rises anywhere. No birds cross the gaps. No insects stitch through the light. There is only vegetation, water, stone, and the constant, world-filling roar—an unchanging anthem that makes your bones vibrate and your thoughts come in shorter lines.

It is a place that has made peace with itself so completely that it no longer needs a future. The city does not wait for anyone to return. The plants do not rush toward some climax. The water does not seek a new route. Everything that could happen has already happened once, found its shape, and set like cooled lava.

Standing in it, you begin to feel the strangest consequence of its stability: without animals, without voices, without the small negotiations of daily living, the urban landscape becomes pure form—streets as channels, homes as cavities, plazas as resonators. You are not so much a visitor as a disturbance, and even that disturbance will be absorbed into the endless wet heat and the deafening, unwavering sound, until you, too, feel like another element in a fixed design.
