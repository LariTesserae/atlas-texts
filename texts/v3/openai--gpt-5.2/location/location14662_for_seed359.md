You come to it by accident, because there isn’t a “landscape” in the usual sense—no exposed ground, no honest bedrock, no place where the world is allowed to be itself. The place is an object.

At first it reads as a shallow basin—low, warm air pooled in a concavity—but the basin is tiled. Every surface you can put weight on has been laid down by hands: pale fired-clay squares fitted so closely that dust can’t find a seam; ribs of wickerwork braced with bone pegs; slabs of chalky composite stamped with repeating fingerprints as if the maker wanted the world to remember the pressure of a thumb.

It’s cathedral-scale only in the way a grove can be: a bounded volume that makes you lower your voice. The ceiling is not sky but a lattice of beams and stretched skins. Light comes through it at a normal daytime strength, not glaring, not dim—sunlight without sun. The skins are patched and re-patched, their translucence uneven. In places, the light lands in crisp polygons, as if it has edges.

Water is almost absent. Not hidden in a streambed, not locked under a crust—absent in the chemistry of the air. There is no wet smell. Nothing ever drips. Breath does not feel like it’s adding to humidity; it feels like it’s being taken, the way a kiln steals moisture from clay.

And yet the vegetation is lush.

The “plants” here are built to do without water in the same way boats are built to do without roads. They are thick-bodied and glossy, full of resins and gels that never quite become liquid. Some grow as folded fans of wax-green membrane—leaf-shapes, but their surfaces are micro-ridged like pottery combed before firing. Others are bundles of hollow reeds whose inner walls are lined with crystals; they don’t transport sap, they transport vapor and charge. The stems click faintly when they flex, like dried cane, but they’re alive: they turn to the light with a slow insistence, hours-long movements that you only notice when you look away and look back.

Their nutrition is not “soil” (there is no soil, only manufactured substrate): they root into mats of braided fiber packed with powdered minerals and char. The mats are laid like carpets, and under them are shallow trays of porous ceramic. When the warm air moves—barely, a patient convection—the plants comb molecules out of it. Oils, ammonia traces, metallic dust. They feed on what most worlds call contamination. You can put your hand near a leaf and feel, not coolness of transpiration, but a faint static tug, as if the plant is sorting the air grain by grain.

Dense presences crowd the space. Not just greenery, but structures, tiers, habitats nested inside habitats: shelves of clay amphorae turned sideways to make burrows; hanging nets knotted from gut-fiber for climbing animals; upright posts carved with shallow spiral grooves where small creatures can grip. Paths exist, but they are narrow and deliberate, forcing your body close to everything—fronds brushing your shoulders, reed-bundles whispering against your sleeves, lichenlike crusts snagging hair with microscopic hooks.

The place is young. You see it in the edges. Many cuts are sharp. The corners of tiles have not been rounded by feet; the grooves in the posts still show tool chatter, small repetitive bites from a stone blade. Where walls meet floor, the seam is crisp and dry, no softened fillet of accumulated damp. The whole environment has the feeling of having been opened recently, or completed recently, or—more unsettling—remembered recently.

Sound is mostly quiet, but it is not the dead quiet of emptiness. It’s muffled, absorbed by the mats and skins and bodies. When something makes noise it feels intimate.

There are animals everywhere, but many of them move with the discretion of things that evolved in a place where loudness wastes energy.

You’ll see them if you stand still: pale, six-limbed runners that slip between tiles with joints that don’t seem to need lubrication; fat-bodied flyers that navigate by heat gradients rather than air currents; small, deerlike browsers with ceramic-smooth horns that scrape resin off stems and chew slowly as if the act is also a meditation. Their eyes often look lacquered, not wet. Their tongues are dry. Their droppings are pellets of compacted mineral and fiber, nearly odorless.

They’ve made their own layer of architecture on top of the human-made one. Nests tucked into beam joints. Thin curtains of spun filament stretched across corners to catch drifting dust. Piles of shed skins arranged along the warmest tiles as if someone is drying parchment.

Primitive technology is evident not as absence but as texture: nothing here is injection-molded or seamless. Everything is knotted, pegged, mortared, lashed. The ceiling lattice is stitched like a boat’s hull; the stitching is visible and proud. Repairs are layered repairs—new patches over old patches—so that the building reads like a history of hands returning with limited tools but abundant attention. There are no metals that shine. If there is metal at all, it’s dark and worked cold, bent rather than cast.

And then the weirdness asserts itself—not as a single miracle, but as a set of rules that don’t sit properly inside your skull.

Distances do not stay obedient. A walkway that looks like three strides may take seven. The far wall sometimes arrives sooner than it should, and sometimes refuses to approach, as if the space is negotiating with your intention. Shadows do not always attach to their owners; you may watch a browsing beast walk away while its shadow lingers a second longer, then slides after it like a reluctant blanket.

The air has pockets where sound fails to propagate. You can clap once and hear it; clap again in nearly the same place and get only the soft impact of skin on skin, no echo, as if the world forgot how to carry vibration for a moment. The animals know these pockets. They pause at their edges, cock their heads, and step through with the cautious confidence of locals crossing an invisible threshold.

Gravity is mostly normal—low elevation, low drama—but near certain posts it tilts by a few degrees. You feel it in your ankles first, a subtle insistence that “down” is slightly to the left. The plants there lean in impossible directions, luxuriant and calm, as if they have always grown into that skewed truth.

Time is slow here in the ordinary ways (growth measured in weeks, not minutes; dust settling in graceful patience), but it also slips. You may watch a reed-bundle unfurl and then, when you blink, find it folded again, not because it moved back but because your last few seconds were edited. The place doesn’t do this maliciously. It does it the way a poorly-tensioned loom repeats a row: an error in the weave, not a decision.

Because everything is made, even the “weather” is constructed. There is a cycle—so gradual you can live inside it and not notice—that warms the floor tiles at midday and cools the ceiling skins at night. The temperature stays warm overall, but the gradient breathes. When the floor warms, the resin plants soften and become slightly more fragrant (a clean, bitter scent like heated pine without the sweetness). When the ceiling cools, the skins tighten and the lattice creaks once or twice, a sound like someone quietly turning a page.

No water means no rain, no puddles, no mud. Erosion happens differently. It is not water wearing things down but abrasion and brittleness: dust polishing high spots; animals’ claws scratching repeated routes into the tile glaze; resin exudate catching grit and turning it into a slow sandpaper. Where surfaces do “erode,” they do so in crisp flake-offs, fresh scars exposing lighter material beneath. The place’s youth shows each scar plainly.

Ecology is a closed ledger. Nothing rots into wetness. Death does not bloom into smell. Bodies dry, then become structure. Bone pegs are sometimes literal. Fiber matting sometimes contains hair and tendon. The browsers gnaw old beams for minerals; the flyers lay eggs in the warm hollows of unused jars; the resin plants incorporate powdered shell into their skins, armoring themselves against the abrasive world.

The boundary of the landscape is clear: a ring-wall of stacked composite blocks, waist-high in some places, shoulder-high in others, topped with a lip that turns inward like the rim of a bowl. Beyond it is not “outside” so much as unfinished space: more lattice, more darkness, the suggestion of corridors that haven’t been laid with tile yet, or have been but are currently refusing to be measured.

If you stay long enough, you start to learn the local etiquette—less social than physical.

Don’t bring water. Even a wet cloth feels obscene here; moisture beads for a heartbeat and then vanishes with a faint tick, leaving the fabric dry and colder than it should be, as if the world has taxed you for the intrusion.

Don’t trust a straight line. Walk by landmarks: the post with the spiral grooves that lean wrong; the cluster of wax-fans that always face “east” even though there is no east; the beam joint where the sound dies.

And don’t mistake the quiet for emptiness. This place is dense with lives and with made-things pretending, patiently, to be a world. It doesn’t rush. It doesn’t shout. It simply continues—slow, warm, lush without water—holding itself together by craft, habit, and a handful of reality that sometimes comes unstitched and is stitched again.
