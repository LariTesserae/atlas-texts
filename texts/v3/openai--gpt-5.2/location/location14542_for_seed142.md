High ground doesn’t always mean air and sky.

Here the elevation announces itself first as pressure—or rather the lack of it. The “ground” is a plateau so old it has forgotten how to be jagged: broad, planed surfaces of pale mineral, ribs of basalt worn down to knuckles, escarpments rounded as if they were once soft. It feels like standing on the top of something immense that has been patiently sanded for an age, not by storms (there is hardly weather), but by time under a constant, faint abrasive: dust that moves the way cold honey moves, almost too slow to perceive.

Water is not part of the landscape. The word *dry* doesn’t fit; dry implies a missing thing. Here it’s simply *never been*. The salts on the flats are not the crust of a vanished sea; they are precipitates from non-aqueous chemistry, minerals that knit themselves directly from thin air and heat—chlorides, sulfides, chalky borates—forming matte sheets that crack into polygons. In places, the crust has a faint sheen like fired ceramic. When it breaks underfoot it doesn’t crumble like earth; it rings like thin stoneware.

Above, the sky is a lid.

Light is almost absent: not night, not cloud cover—more like a world whose star is weak in the visible and generous only in long, invisible wavelengths. The air, what little of it exists at this height, drinks visible light. Surfaces are discerned by contrast and edge, not color. The city makes its own illumination but does so reluctantly: a safety amber in deep hoods, narrow slits of sodium glow under eaves, the occasional cold line of arc-weld blue that flares and then is gone. Most structures prefer darkness because darkness is stable, and stability is the local religion.

The warmth is real, though. It comes from below and from machinery. Step into the lee of a slag berm and you can feel heat radiating out of the ground like a sleeping animal. There are no breezes to steal it away; convection is anemic in thin air, and the plateau sits in a kind of equilibrium where temperatures drift toward a long-held mean and stay there. If you set a metal tool down, it will eventually come to the same temperature as the stone, and it will stay that way, hour after hour, year after year, as if time has been slowed for everything except thought.

The open scale of the place is deceptive because the dark limits the eye. You can’t see far, yet you know it extends: the way the ground’s gentle curvature keeps going under the starless lid; the way rails vanish into black and return hours later from a direction that feels geometrically correct but emotionally wrong. On clear nights—*clear* meaning the dust-fall is minimal—you can just make out distant silhouettes: clusters of verticals like broken teeth, long low mounds that are spoil heaps or buried neighborhoods, and a few high stacks that exhale nothing visible but are always warmer than their surroundings, as if the act of being built still produces heat.

The city is industrial in the way a bone is structural: not decorative, not optional.

It’s sparse in footprint—much empty space between presences—but each presence is dense with intent. Factories sit apart like islands in an ocean of mineral flats, linked by straight infrastructure that ignores the old terrain rather than obeying it: elevated conveyors on latticed trusses; pipelines in insulated skins; double tracks that carry ore-cars silently on magnetic bearings. There are towers, but they are not for beauty or defense—they are heat exchangers, distillation columns for non-water solvents, stacks for gases that are reclaimed and recirculated because nothing here is casually vented. The built environment is layered: older foundations swallowed by later additions, catwalks bolted onto catwalks, entire streets roofed over to create controlled corridors where the air composition is maintained and the temperature never varies by more than a degree.

In the darkest stretches between worksites, the plateau feels almost untouched—until you realize the ground itself has been engineered. The flats are subtly ribbed, patterned to manage the slow migration of dust. Shallow channels guide granular flow the way canals guide water elsewhere, except these canals carry powdered salts and metallic fines, and they do it on timescales of decades. The city is not fighting erosion so much as participating in it, giving the ancient abrasion a shape.

Sound is mostly absent, too, but not because nothing is happening.

It’s because the atmosphere refuses to carry it. There’s a constant, low vibration you feel in your teeth rather than hear: the deep hum of turbines far underground; the rhythmic clack of a distant press that seems impossibly close and then, a hundred meters later, vanishes. Occasionally there’s a sharp ping when a stressed beam cools by a fraction and adjusts, or a cascading tinkle when someone steps onto a salt sheet and it fractures into overlapping plates. Otherwise, the loudest thing is your own motion—the rasp of fabric, the crunch of ceramic crust, the tiny static snaps where dust sticks and then lets go.

The ecology is almost a rumor.

No vegetation, nothing that claims sunlight or makes shade. But rare fauna exist in the margins where industry creates gradients—warmth, trace organics, minute leaks of reactive gas. In the sheltered hollows behind heat exchangers you might find a cluster of small, pale things like folded paper, motionless until you come close: scavengers that feed on polymer flakes and lubricant aerosols, their bodies more mineral than flesh. In the dark under rail beds, something wormlike may glide through powder, leaving a brief, precise furrow that doesn’t collapse for days because the dust grains interlock at a particular angle and hold. There are also the “listeners,” creatures (or machines that have become creaturelike) that cling to the undersides of pipes and harvest vibration. They are seen mostly as a faint, regular pattern of matte patches on metal, like lichen would be in a wetter world. They do not spread quickly. Nothing spreads quickly here.

Strangeness isn’t flamboyant; it’s structural.

There are rules the plateau follows that visitors learn without being taught. One: shadows can’t be trusted. In the near-dark, with only local light sources, you would think shadows would be straightforward—yet edges sometimes fall in the wrong direction, as if the geometry of illumination has been bent. The city’s lamps are shielded, yes, but there is also a persistent, dim glow from the sky-lid itself: a low, uniform radiance that seems to come from everywhere and nowhere, flattening depth. Two: corrosion behaves oddly. Metals tarnish to a stable patina and then stop; fasteners unearthed from older layers look new except for a thin, perfectly even film. Three: nothing truly *settles*. Dust piles form at angles that are too steep and remain that way, held by electrostatic forces in the thin air, as if the ground is lightly charged and prefers its grains to stand at attention.

Time, too, feels engineered. The city runs on cycles that barely drift. Shift bells (soft, not ringing—more like a pressure change in the air of corridors) have been sounding for so long that the structures themselves seem to anticipate them: lights brighten a fraction, gates unlatch, conveyors wake with a silent shiver. Even abandonment doesn’t look like ruin here. A factory that has been offline for centuries is simply *still*—no creeping vines, no puddles, no rot—just a dark hulk holding its shape, its surfaces dulled to the same quiet finish as the plateau around it.

From a height—say, from the top of an ore-silo reached by a spiral stair that barely echoes—you can sense the whole arrangement: islands of manufacture in a sea of ancient mineral, connected by straight lines of purpose, all under a sky that withholds light but not heat. The emptiness between buildings isn’t neglected; it’s part of the design, a buffer, a silence, a way of keeping everything in equilibrium.

It’s a place that has reached its oldest stable state and decided, long ago, not to move on.

Not dead—too warm, too active in its own restrained way—but settled into a final form: industry without growth, city without sprawl, landscape without weather, life without plants, darkness without cold. A plateau that doesn’t erode so much as slowly perfects its own stillness, while machines—patient as geology—continue doing what they were built to do, and the rare creatures in the shadows learn to live on the small, reliable leaks of an unchanging world.
