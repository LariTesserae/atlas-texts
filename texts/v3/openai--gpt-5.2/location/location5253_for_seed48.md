The first impression is not of buildings or water but of *air*: warm, heavy, and worked-over, as if it has been breathed and boiled and wrung out a thousand times. It hangs low across a flat country where the ground never commits to being solid. Everything sweats—stone, metal, even the undersides of bridges—and the sweat has nowhere to go but into shallow channels that braid and unbraid with the tide and with every passing storm.

Light arrives reluctantly. The sun is present only as a pale pressure behind a lid of cloud, turning the world the color of old tin. It is dim without being night. There are no long shadows to explain distances, only a gradual fading: closer things have edges, far things smear into haze. Lamps exist—industrial sodium and white strips under overhangs—but they don’t sharpen the scene so much as add small, local pools of certainty.

The terrain is low and wide. You can stand on a roof or a raised walkway and see an entire district—blocks of warehouses, tank farms, pumping stations, long sheds with louvered sides—then beyond that the open flats where concrete gives up and becomes cracked slabs and then becomes mud that has learned the shape of human grids. The whole place has been here long enough to settle into itself. Corners are rounded. Stair edges are worn soft by time and water and the steady abrasion of silt. Steel has a consistent skin of rust, layered and stable, the way a cliff face stabilizes: not pristine, not collapsing, just *aged*.

Water is everywhere but rarely deep. It lives near the surface, in the pores of the ground and in the seams of construction. After rain—frequent, warm, and persistent—the city doesn’t flood dramatically so much as it becomes a single connected wetness. You hear it in the patient tapping of drips into metal trays, the quiet runnels inside downspouts, the invisible trickle within walls where condensation has found a path. The rocks and concrete weep in slow motion. In places the ground is so saturated that a footstep makes a delayed sigh, as if the land is exhaling back into shape.

There is no plant life. No green softening a corner, no grass in cracks, no reeds claiming the canals. The absence is not a mystery so much as a defining fact, like gravity. The surfaces that would normally host moss or vines are instead bare mineral, bare polymer, bare paint flaking into colored dust. Where you expect roots to stitch soil together, there is only sediment and the engineering meant to replace them: retaining walls, gabions of stone wire, poured berms, and a vast system of drains that fight a constant, losing battle against the desire of the lowlands to become water.

And yet the place is not empty. It is *full*—overwhelmingly alive—just not with anything that grows upward toward light.

Fauna owns the city.

They come in layers and densities that make the wide spaces feel occupied even when no person is visible. On the mudflats beyond the last service road, pale crabs and dark, hard-backed scavengers move like spilled pepper, thousands at a time, their bodies clicking softly against grit. In the canals, fish with blunt heads roll near the surface, stirring oily rainbows that drift and break apart under the next drop. Under every overhang and inside every warm vent, there are nests: birds that have adapted to steel ledges and heat-exchange housings, bats that sleep in the lee of signage, colonies of small mammals that have learned the schedules of machinery the way forest animals learn seasons.

Without vegetation, the food web is built on what the city provides and what the water brings: algae-like films that are not quite plants, bacterial mats on warm concrete, insects feeding on those mats, larger things feeding on the insects, and so on up to sleek predators that patrol the canals and the empty streets. The city is a reef made of industry. Its infrastructure is habitat: pipe racks are cliffs; storm drains are caves; settling ponds are inland seas. The absence of trees means nothing interrupts movement, so flocks and swarms travel fast and low, streaming through corridors between buildings, turning corners as if the air itself has painted routes for them.

Their sounds are oddly restrained. For so much life, it is mostly quiet—quiet in the way a humid room muffles voices. There is constant soft motion: the papery rustle of wings, the faint clatter of claws on metal grating, a distant splash, the hush of bodies moving through shallow water. Even the industrial sounds are subdued. Somewhere a pump turns with a steady, almost comforting thrum, but it is far off and partly swallowed by the wet air. A conveyor starts up and then settles into a low, continuous note, like a throat clearing and deciding not to speak.

The city itself feels partly unoccupied, not ruined but *thinned out*. The roads are wide for the amount of traffic they carry. There are long stretches where nothing moves except animals and the slow mechanics of weather. A few district cores still function: you can tell by the clean straightness of certain lights, by the occasional pulse of a warning beacon atop a stack, by the way some doors have fresh seals and some inspection markings are new. But many buildings stand with their windows filmed over, their entrances blocked not by vines but by silt fans and drifts of salt that creep in like snow. Open plazas collect rainwater in shallow, reflective sheets; when the wind shifts, those sheets ripple, making the ground look briefly like skin.

Heat is constant. It radiates up from dark surfaces and down from the air itself. In the afternoons the entire district shimmers slightly, less from mirage than from the restless exchange between warm water and warm stone. Steam ghosts off grates. The underbellies of bridges drip steadily because the air condenses there, and the condensation is warm enough that it feels like a held breath. The hottest places are not the sunlit ones but the industrial hearts: around boiler houses and exhaust stacks where warm, wet air rises and then immediately meets a ceiling of cooler cloud and spreads out, adding to the dimness.

Erosion has done its work patiently. Water has found every weakness and made it a feature. Some streets have become shallow canals because the subgrade washed out long ago and was never fully replaced. Certain facades have a scalloped look where rainwater ran down the same path for decades, leaching minerals, leaving pale streaks like old tears. At the edge of the flats, the concrete has broken into plates and those plates tilt at gentle angles, forming small steps where animals gather to bask in heat that never quite leaves the material.

Despite all this, nothing about the place is uncanny. It follows rules that feel consistent and ordinary:

- Water collects in low places, and low places are most of the world here.
- Metal rusts; concrete spalls; paint peels.
- Warmth accelerates decay and life at the same time.
- Where plants are absent, other small, fast organisms take the role of turning waste into food.
- Animals go where shelter, heat, and reliable resources are—here, that means pipes, drains, tanks, and the steady outputs of industry.

The active processes are everywhere if you watch for a minute. Tides push brackish water up through storm gates and then retreat, leaving a lace of foam and a line of stranded shells against a curb. Rain begins as a soft, constant patter and becomes a curtain, then stops and immediately the city starts to evaporate, the air thickening as it takes the water back. Pumps cycle on and off, keeping certain basins from overflowing. Flocks lift and resettle in coordinated waves, responding to some predator’s pass. Scavengers converge on a single point—something dropped, something dead, something new—and then disperse again, leaving only a disturbed patch of mud and a few scattered fragments.

Stand still long enough and the emptiness reveals itself as a kind of spacing: vast intervals between human presences, wide roads without cars, whole blocks where the only regular traffic is the passage of animals and the slow migration of water. It is an urban landscape that has become a shoreline ecosystem, an industrial plain that behaves like an estuary. The city persists not as a monument to human density, but as a durable arrangement of materials that the wet heat and the overwhelming fauna have learned to use—quietly, continuously, and with the unromantic efficiency of life that has found a way to go on without leaves.
