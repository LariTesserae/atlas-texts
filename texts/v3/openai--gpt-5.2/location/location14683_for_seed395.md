At first it reads as a desert—flat, bright, and dry enough that your lips go papery in minutes. The ground is pale and granular, not quite sand and not quite stone: a crust of salts and soft mineral flour over a young bed of compacted ash. It has the wrong kind of whiteness for beach-sand—more chalk and bone, more chemical than coastal—and when you scuff it with your heel it breaks in plates, thin as pottery shards. Under the plates, the world is still deciding what it will be. New seams of crystal are pushing up like frost heave, except the “frost” is gypsum and halite, grown out of the air and out of whatever hidden brines the basin once had and has nearly finished losing.

There’s no open water. No streams, no puddles, no sheen on rock. The dryness isn’t just absence; it’s a rule. Anything that would pool either vanishes into the crust or is locked away immediately—absorbed, reacted, bound, or stolen by the first living thing that can grab it. The air itself feels conserved: it carries humidity only in small, private amounts, like money in a crowded market.

And it is crowded. “Vast” doesn’t mean empty here. It means the crowd continues past the horizon in every direction.

The lowlands stretch on a continental scale, a shallow basin so broad it has weather of its own. The sky is ordinary blue under ordinary sunlight, but the light has nowhere to fall unused: it hits mineral flats, then rebounds into a canopy; it threads down corridors between trunks; it breaks into green-gold under leaves, and into hard white on the salt plazas where the crust lies exposed. Even the wind, when it comes, doesn’t travel as a clean sheet. It is chopped and rerouted and filtered through structure.

Because the “city” is not an import laid on top of nature. It is what nature looks like when it is packed tight and organized by life.

The vegetation is lush, but not wet. It is lush the way a cactus garden can be lush, the way a greenhouse is lush when the panes are closed. Leaves are thick, waxed, and dusted with fine mineral bloom. Many plants hold their fluids deep and jealously, behind skins that don’t transpire much. Others are built like nets and sponges, but the sponges are made of fibrous tissue and salt-loving cells that pull moisture from night air by deliquescence: on evenings when the temperature eases, the crust sweats without ever becoming a surface of water. A faint dampness appears as a chemical softness, a brief yielding at the top millimeters of soil; beads form under leaves and are taken up before sunrise can spend them.

From above, the basin looks like an endless, interlocked roof of green and pale stone—an urban pattern without architects. The trees (if “tree” is the right word) grow in mutual constraint, each trunk rising straight because there is no room to sprawl. Branches meet and fuse. Roots braid with roots. In places, the living tissues have learned the same lessons as masonry: buttresses, arches, vaults. Where one species grows as thick, ribbed columns, another uses those columns as trellises, wrapping them in tight spirals that harden into load-bearing cords. The result is streets: long, shaded slots where the salt crust is tamped smooth by generations of feet, and where the canopy above is high enough to make an interior sky.

There are plazas too—openings where the mineral crust is exposed, ringed by vegetation that refuses to cross the bright, desiccating center. In the middle of some plazas, the ground has cracked into polygonal plates and lifted at the edges, not from drying—there was never water to dry—but from crystallization pressure underneath. The geology here is embryonic, still growing its first bones. It makes its own construction noises, but softly: a muffled tick as a salt seam expands, a faint grind when a crust sheet settles, the tiniest chime when crystals fracture against each other. You have to be close, and even then you wonder if you imagined it.

Sound is generally swallowed. The packed vegetation, the mineral dust, the maze of trunks and woven limbs—all of it dampens echoes. Even wind becomes a quiet thing, more breath than roar. The loudest regular noise is not weather but life: a dry, continuous whisper of leaves rubbing like paper; insect wings that make a soft, sustained fizz; the occasional hollow knock of a heavy fruit dropping onto crust and not quite breaking it.

Fauna is abundant because every niche is already occupied. The creatures are mostly small to mid-sized—not because larger bodies can’t exist, but because in a world that hoards moisture, efficiency is empire. There are herds of low browsers that move through the shaded streets in tight files, their hides powdered with salt, their mouths built to strip waxy leaf margins and avoid the bitter, mineral-rich cores. They pause at “dew-wells,” not pools but thickets of deliquescent plants whose tissues briefly swell at dawn; the animals lick the plants’ surfaces and move on, careful not to damage the living infrastructure that feeds them. Predators follow with equal discipline: silent-footed, narrow-bodied hunters that can thread through root-arches without brushing them, their eyes tuned to the green-gold dimness under canopy.

Above, the air is full without being noisy: gliders and short-winged fliers that launch from high forks and sail on the basin’s slow thermals. They nest in communal structures grown rather than built—bulbous clusters of woven branch and dried sap that hang like neighborhoods from the upper canopy. Some of these aerial “buildings” are centuries old, expanded each season by the same mindless, collective labor that a reef would use—except this reef is dry and warm, and its cement is resin and mineral dust.

Nothing here uses tools. There are no cut stones, no fired bricks, no metal, no glass. The infrastructure is biological and behavioral: paths kept clear by repeated passage; “walls” maintained by plants that respond to touch and shade, thickening where bodies rub against them; gates that are simply narrowings where thorny species dominate and only certain animals can pass. Even the sense of zoning—open plaza, shaded street, dense residential thicket—is an emergent treaty between species. Where one plant tries to colonize a plaza, another species’ root chemistry makes the crust too alkaline for it; where animals overbrowse, a bitter strain spreads and pushes them elsewhere. The city is governed by feedback, not councils.

The basin’s weather is active but restrained. Days are warm, the sun competent and steady. Nights cool just enough to trigger the brief, crucial exchange between air and life: salts that soften, pores that open, tissues that drink. Sometimes a front comes through and the whole continent seems to exhale. A dry storm rolls across the flats, not wetting anything but moving everything—lifting mineral flour into the air in a pale veil, scouring the exposed plazas, packing more dust into the crooks of bark. Under the canopy the storm becomes a moving pressure, a rush of filtered air that makes the fused branches flex together, entire neighborhoods swaying in slow synchrony. It looks dramatic and feels alive, but it sounds like almost nothing: a low, continuous hush, as if the world is trying not to disturb itself.

Erosion, in the familiar sense of running water carving valleys, is absent. Instead, the land is shaped by growth and crystallization. The crust expands, buckles, breaks, and heals. Dunes migrate, but they are dunes of powdered salts and ash. Roots split young rock before it has learned to be stone. Minerals precipitate upward into delicate spires in sheltered pockets, then collapse under their own weight and become part of the soil again. The landscape is under construction—geology learning its forms in the presence of an ecosystem that is faster, denser, and more opinionated than the rock.

If you stay long enough, you start to recognize the place’s basic laws:

- Nothing is allowed to be liquid in public for long. Moisture exists as a brief privilege—captured at night, stored inside skins, traded mouth-to-leaf at dawn.
- Space is always claimed. If there is a gap, something is already leaning into it, rooting into it, nesting in it, or passing through it in a pattern so regular it might as well be a road.
- Stability is not stasis. The city holds together by constant adjustment: plants thickening and shedding, animals pruning and fertilizing, salts swelling and settling, storms rearranging the dust and then being muffled back into quiet.

And that quiet is the strangest thing, given the scale and the density and the life. Standing in a shaded corridor beneath fused canopies, with bright mineral plazas flashing between trunks like courtyards of bone, you can feel the continent moving—breathing, growing, shifting its weight from season to season—without hearing more than the softest ongoing proof that it’s happening. The place doesn’t announce itself. It simply continues, vast and packed, a dry green metropolis made entirely of living agreements.
