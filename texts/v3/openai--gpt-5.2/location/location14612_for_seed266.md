The first thing the land gives you is its height.

You come up out of the low flats—if “flat” can mean a surface already higher than most worlds’ mountain ranges—and the horizon doesn’t broaden so much as it steps upward in tiers: escarpments stacked like pages of stone. Each rise is older than the last, each with its own shade of mineral, its own fracture pattern, its own particular way of catching light. The sky is a clean, ordinary blue at noon, but it feels nearer than it should, as if the planet’s bulk has been shaved away and you’re walking on the exposed ribs of it.

There is no water. Not “no rivers,” not “no rain,” but no wetness at all outside of machines and mouths. The word *dry* becomes inadequate; the air is a solvent for nothing. Whatever chemistry makes life here learned to do without the easy, flowing middle-state between solid and gas, so the world’s processes are mostly slow—grain by grain, flake by flake, molecule by molecule—across time scales that make you notice your own impatience.

### Terrain and materials

The ground is a vast catalogue of erosion that never got the courtesy of streams. Wind is the main sculptor, and temperature is its chisel’s edge.

- **Plateaus of black glass** spread for hundreds of kilometers, not smooth like ice but crazed with microscopic cracks. Under normal light they shine only when you catch the angle right; otherwise they drink brightness and return a dull sheen, like cooled slag.
- **Salt-fields** exist, but not the kind that once held a sea. These are salt as a *mineral habit*: crystals grown up from vapor deposition and slow chemical cycles. The flats are ridged with polygonal seams and razor efflorescences. Walking there is like walking over a bed of broken porcelain that somehow stayed arranged.
- **Needle ranges** rise abruptly—spires and fins of pale stone that look too thin to stand. They do, because the rock is dense and interlocked, and because erosion here is patient rather than violent. The pinnacles are wind-sung into shape over ages.
- **Canyon systems** cut down through layered deposits, but they are not river-carved. Their walls are scalloped by vortices and thermal pulses, their floors filled with talus that has sorted itself by vibration: heavy grains in one band, lighter dust in another, as if the planet is always gently sifting.

The dust is everywhere, but it never feels like soil. It’s mineral flour: silicates, oxidized metals, occasional glittering flecks of conductive crystal. It clings to edges and hides in seams. In the open, it moves in *slow dunes*, as if even wind has learned to conserve effort at this elevation.

### Atmosphere, light, and the quiet

It’s warm—warm enough that metal left in the sun is uncomfortable to touch, warm enough that shadows feel like relief—but the warmth is thin. At these heights, the air does not press on you; it only *exists around you*, a presence more than a weight. Sound doesn’t carry far. You can shout and feel as if your voice is being accepted politely and then set down, a few meters away, without commitment.

Most of the time the world is quiet in the way a workshop is quiet when all the machines are idling: not silence, but a low, distributed hush.

- A distant, intermittent **clink** as stone grains skitter down a slope.
- The **tick-tick** of thermal contraction in exposed girders.
- The far-off, almost imagined **harmonic** of cables under tension in the city.
- Occasionally, a broad **sigh** that isn’t wind so much as the landscape equalizing temperature—air sliding from sunlit faces into shaded clefts.

The light is normal, the kind you can read by without thinking, but the land changes its color under it. Minerals do the work that leaves do on wetter planets: surfaces flash greenish where copper salts bloom, go blood-brown where iron has breathed, turn violet where certain crystals catch the sun and return a thin, bruised sheen.

### The city in a sparse world

The urban presence doesn’t sprawl like moss. It perches, spans, drills.

From a distance it looks like a series of terraces on the cliff: stacks of platforms bolted into stone, connected by switchback roads and skeletal rail lines. Up close, it becomes an *infrastructure organism*—industrial, layered, lived-in. There are no parks, no street trees, no decorative water. Instead there are:

- **Condensers** that gather trace volatiles from the air, frosting internally at night and warming them into tanks by day.
- **Heat exchangers** like radiators the size of buildings, their fins whispering as they cycle.
- **Foundries** that smelt local rock directly, because shipping anything up and down these escarpments is a kind of pilgrimage.
- **Sky-bridges** that jump gaps no ordinary street could cross, cables singing so softly you feel the vibration more than you hear it.

The city is sparse in the way the landscape is sparse: big spaces between built things, long sightlines, functional emptiness. Habitation clusters around power and shelter and work, leaving stretches of bare platform and exposed conduit where only maintenance crews and animals go.

Industrial tech is not sleek here; it’s durable. Paint is rare and quickly sanded away. Surfaces are clad in sacrificial plates. Hinges are overbuilt. Everything assumes abrasion and time, not flood or rot.

And the city’s relationship to the land is not “on” it but “in argument with” it—anchored into it with rock bolts and deep pylons, forever compensating for slow creep, slow settling, slow drift of dust.

### Ecology without plants

There is almost no vegetation because “plant” is the wrong strategy here. No broad leaves to drink light, no roots to seek water that isn’t. But life is abundant anyway—life that eats gradients, metals, heat, and motion.

The base of the food web is subtle:

- **Mineral films** in sheltered cracks—thin, dull coatings of chemistry rather than greenery. They look like tarnish. They are alive in the way a battery is alive: exchanging ions, taking energy from temperature differences between day and night, from trace gases, from the contact potential of different rocks.
- **Spore-dust analogs**, not fungal in the familiar sense but particulate life that rides the wind and settles where surfaces are conductive enough, where a current can be teased out of the stone.

From that emerge the fauna—abundant, strange, and adapted to a world that offers no soft food.

You see the first ones near the outskirts: **long-legged grazers** that aren’t grazing so much as *scraping*. They move on jointed limbs with wide, compliant pads that don’t kick up much dust. Their mouths are grinding plates lined with crystalline teeth. They press their faces to sun-warmed rock and rasp off the living films, swallowing stone and life together and sorting what they need in a gut that works like a refinery.

Above them, in the thin air and the huge vertical spaces, drift **gliders**—not birds, because there are no feathers here, but creatures with broad, flexible membranes stiffened by mineral rods. They launch from thermal updrafts that rise along cliff faces. They make almost no sound, only a faint rustle when they bank close enough for you to hear it, and then they’re gone into the blue, riding a physics that feels too gentle for such a harsh land.

And everywhere, in the shade of infrastructure, there are **scavengers**. Some are clearly animals; others are harder to categorize. Small, armored things the color of the ground skitter along conduits, licking at the warm breath of machines, harvesting condensed salts, nibbling insulation to get at the interesting compounds inside. They’re drawn to the city because the city leaks: heat, trace organics, metallic dust, electromagnetic fields.

At night—when the temperature drops just enough to make the air behave differently—you can sometimes see **faint, crawling lights** on the cliff faces: colonies of surface-life discharging tiny sparks as they shuffle charge from one mineral boundary to another. It’s not bioluminescence in the wet-world sense; it’s electrochemistry made visible.

### Rules of the world (as you learn them by living here)

1. **Liquid is a luxury.** Anything that flows is either inside a sealed pipe, inside a body, or treated as precious beyond money. Outside, the world is solid and gas and dust, and it likes it that way.
2. **Erosion is wind and time, not storms.** Big changes happen as accumulation: a century of grit sanding an edge away; a thousand years of thermal cycling loosening a face; a million years of dust making a dune walk a kilometer.
3. **Height is a climate.** Step down a tier and the air thickens, sound carries farther, heat behaves differently. Step up and everything becomes more exposed, more quiet, more dependent on engineering and adaptation.
4. **Life feeds on chemistry, not greenery.** The land is not dead; it’s just not verdant. Abundance comes in hard forms: armor, grinding teeth, membranes, slow metabolisms tuned to thin resources.
5. **The city is an oasis of gradients.** Not water—gradients. Warmth against cold night. Metal against stone. Electricity against grounded earth. Where differences exist, life gathers.

### The slow dynamics that make it feel ancient

Nothing here changes quickly unless a machine fails or a cliff finally lets go.

The day has a rhythm: morning brings expansion and a faint chorus of metal settling; noon brings stillness and glare; evening brings contraction and a renewed drift of dust as air slides downhill. The animals time themselves to those pulses—scrapers moving when the rock is warm and active, gliders riding the afternoon thermals, scavengers coming out when the city’s lights and heat make islands in the cooling dark.

Once in a long while, you hear something like thunder. It is not rain. It is a **rockfall**, miles away, a slab finally conceding to centuries of tiny stresses. The sound arrives late and thin, and by the time you turn your head, dust is rising in a slow plume that looks almost polite against the enormous space.

Stand on a high terrace at dusk and you can see the continent’s scale in a single glance: tiered plateaus fading into haze, needle ranges cutting the horizon, the city’s infrastructure drawn across voids like pencil lines. The air is clear enough that distances feel dishonest. You can watch a glider become a speck, then nothing, without ever seeing it truly vanish into cloud.

And in the quiet, with no water and no leaves and no familiar softness, the place becomes itself: a warm, high, dry world where stone is food, industry is shelter, and life has learned to be abundant without ever becoming green.
