The first thing you register isn’t the green—though it’s everywhere, in every direction, in every layer like a second atmosphere—it’s the sound.

A constant pressure of noise sits on the skin. Not a single roar, but a stacked, braided deafening: the crackle of fibers tightening, the wet pop of cells swelling, the long groan of trunks flexing under their own fresh weight, and beneath it all a chorus of frictions—leaf against leaf, vine against bark, root-hairs rasping through new silt. In places it becomes rhythmic, almost municipal, as if the land has traffic and shift-changes. Then it breaks its own pattern and surges into a crescending snap-snap-snap, like a thousand cords being tuned too hard at once.

It’s warm—warm like a greenhouse that has forgotten it is contained. The air carries moisture irregularly: not rain so much as episodes. You move through pockets of damp, then through corridors where the warmth feels dry and breathy, then into sudden condensation zones where every surface sweats. Dew forms in seconds on any edge that’s thin enough; it pearls along veins, along stem-ridges, along the sharp angles of newly grown buttresses. Somewhere overhead—though “overhead” becomes an unsteady idea here—water gathers, releases, and gathers again in soft drops that never add up to a storm, only to a continual, uneven wetting.

The terrain is low and young. It has the look of land that recently arrived: flats that still don’t know if they’re plains or marsh, ridges that seem too crisp for their height, cuts that read like fresh wounds. The ground is not “soil” in the usual sense but a packed, living mat: root-woven substrate braided with pale fungal threads and fibrous peat, all of it in motion. When it erodes, it doesn’t crumble; it unknits. A footstep doesn’t leave a print so much as a temporary parting that closes behind you with a faint, irritated hiss.

And the city—because it is unavoidably a city—spreads across this low basin as a dense, organized proliferation. There is infrastructure, but nothing mechanical. Roads exist as flattened tendrils, wide bands of tough, waxed leaf-skin laid down in parallel lanes. Their surfaces are slightly resilient, giving back a fraction of your weight with each step, as if they are paying attention. Intersections happen at knots: thickened nodes where multiple vine-ways converge, reinforced with lattices of living xylem that arch overhead like colonnades.

Buildings are grown, not built, yet they obey an urban logic: blocks, courtyards, spines, plazas. Towers rise in clustered groves—trunks fused into single columns, their outer bark patterned in repeating scales like shingles. Some structures resemble apartments made of ribbed pods stacked around a central stalk; others are hollowed domes of interlaced branches, their ceilings stitched with translucent membranes that admit normal daylight in a softened, green-tinted wash.

There are no inhabitants you can see. No birds to claim the high perches, no insects to stitch the air, no mammals to make the alleys feel occupied. The absence doesn’t make the place quiet; it makes the noise feel unassigned, as if the city is operating without anyone to attribute it to. The ecology is plant-only, and because nothing eats and nothing flees, every surface is claimed. Every free centimeter has been taken up by some strategy of light, support, or water: epiphyte curtains draping from beam-vines, moss-slick carpets clinging to vertical faces, bulbous reservoirs tucked into crevices like municipal cisterns.

Water, when it appears, appears as design. Narrow canals run between districts, but they are not dug; they are grown as troughs—leaf-sheaths thickened into gutters that guide intermittent flow. After a brief rain, they fill quickly and then empty just as quickly into root-mouths that open and close like valves. In some plazas, shallow pools are held in living basins, their edges ringed with spongy tissue that drinks down the water once the air turns less humid, as if the pools are only temporary civic gestures: places for the city to show itself wet before returning to its default thirst.

The light is ordinary in brightness and angle, yet it behaves strangely. In the denser avenues, sunlight breaks into sheets, not beams—planes of illumination that slide along walls and then, without explanation, tilt and become staircases of brightness. Leaves don’t merely reflect; they seem to edit what passes through. You can watch shadows detach from their casters and drift a short distance before snapping back, like an elastic correction. Some corridors are brighter than the open squares beside them, lit by a kind of internal translucence in the plant-walls—as if photosynthesis has become not just a process, but a lantern.

Reality’s edges are soft here, and the city takes advantage.

A street can be the same street twice and still not lead to the same place. The grid holds in a general sense—there are districts, there are centers, there are peripheral thickets where the structures fray into wild mass—but locally it breaks down into contradictions that feel botanical rather than magical: growth-rules applied too literally. A building you pass may be taller behind you than it was ahead of you, as though it oriented itself to your attention. Courtyards sometimes open into courtyards again, nested like leaves in a bud, each one slightly warmer and louder than the last.

The turbulence is not weather alone, though weather contributes. The city is turbulent because growth is happening at street-speed.

You can stand at the edge of a plaza and watch an archway thicken. Fibers braid themselves into stronger angles, and the sound rises with it—wet tightening, creaking, then a sharp crack as some internal tension releases and the whole structure settles into a new geometry. Elsewhere, whole facades blister outward with fresh pods, then wither and slough off in sheets, revealing a different pattern underneath—maintenance by molting. The young land beneath participates: a new ridge lifts subtly over an hour, forcing the vine-roads to reroute, and the roads comply, sliding their lanes sideways like a living detour.

There are rules to it, even if they aren’t human rules.

1. **The city grows toward stress.** Where something strains—where wind funnels, where water collects then disappears, where light becomes contradictory—the plants thicken, reinforce, elaborate. Stress is the architect.
2. **Spaces remain navigable because navigation is a form of circulation.** Pathways persist not out of hospitality but because movement, even yours, seems to be treated as a nutrient: disturbance distributed through the grid.
3. **Nothing is allowed to be empty for long.** A gap is an injury; it will be sealed. If a wall collapses, vines pour into the vacancy like blood into a wound.
4. **Sound is structural.** The loudness isn’t incidental; it’s part of how the place holds shape. Resonance travels through trunks and lattices, and when the pitch changes, the architecture changes with it—adjusting, bracing, releasing.

In the densest quarter—where “urban” becomes undeniable—you find something like a civic center: a broad basin surrounded by tiered terraces of rootwood. The terraces are not seats, but they read like seating. The basin’s floor is a membrane that shivers, amplifying every vibration into the air. Here the noise is almost unbearable; it becomes a physical force, a hand on your chest. Above the basin, multiple canopy-layers interweave into a roof that pulses faintly with shifting green-gold light, and you realize the city may be listening to itself.

With no animals, no people, no tools, the place still feels busy—more than busy: preoccupied. It is a settlement with no settlers, an infrastructure with no commuters, an economy with no consumers. Its turbulence is its population. Its deafening is its speech.

At the outskirts, the organized forms blur into a continuous vegetative mass, but even there the “town” doesn’t stop so much as lose its angles. The last clear roads dissolve into braided mats. The last distinct towers become knuckled groves. The air gets a little wetter; condensation hangs in slow veils between stems. The ground softens into sponge. And the sound—still constant, still layered—begins to pick up new notes: a distant, ongoing thunder that is not weather at all, but the ongoing, unstoppable act of the city remaking itself, one fiber at a time.
