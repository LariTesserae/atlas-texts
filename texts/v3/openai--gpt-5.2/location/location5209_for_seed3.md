The first thing the place gives you is height, not as a view but as a condition. Everything is arranged around the fact that down is too far to be useful, and up is a ceiling you can’t quite see. The air is cool and thin enough that breath feels like a tool you’re using rather than something you’re doing unconsciously. The sky is there, but it’s dim—washed to pewter—because the light arrives after being filtered through permanent cloudbanks and the soot-haze of industry. When the sun does appear it doesn’t brighten so much as it *sketch* edges: cables, rails, rooflines, the pale lichen on stone. The whole region sits in the high reaches of something like a young mountain range, but “young” here doesn’t mean sharp and proud; it means unfinished, still deciding what it will be.

The terrain is a set of extreme escarpments, terraces, and vertical basins—like someone started carving a valley and then stopped halfway through, leaving half-formed amphitheaters and sheer faces with raw seams. Rock is exposed everywhere: newly lifted plates showing clean fractures, slate-dark walls with veins of bright mineral, aggregate that still looks freshly mixed. In places the stone hasn’t learned its final hardness. Footfalls can leave shallow impressions in what should be bedrock; rail spikes sometimes sink a millimeter more each season as if the mountain is still curing.

Erosion, in the familiar sense, hasn’t had time to do its patient work. There are no mature river canyons, no rounded boulders in orderly beds. Instead you get “proto-drainage”: tentative channels that begin in a crack, run for a few meters, then vanish into a face that hasn’t developed a path yet. Water behaves like a rumor—present in patches, absent where you expect it, always arriving indirectly.

Moisture is intermittent but persistent in its own way. Rain comes in brief, cold fits that seem to fall from a low ceiling, never committing to a storm, never fully stopping either. The more reliable water is the kind the place makes for itself: condensation pulled from fog by miles of meshwork and finned metal, dripping into gutters that thread the city like capillaries. Every industrial surface sweats. Under catwalks, beneath pipe racks, around the bases of turbines, there are pockets of wetness—darkened stone, slick moss, rivulets that form only where heat meets cold. There are basins where fog pools so densely it becomes almost a liquid you can lean against; walk into one and you come out damp, hair heavy, sleeves beaded, as if you’ve passed through a shallow river that forgot to be visible.

Because water is scarce in continuous bodies, vegetation has become opportunistic and lush in concentrated places, like bright statements in an otherwise restrained sentence. Wherever condensate gathers—on leeward faces, in the sheltered bowls of half-formed cirques, along the under-sides of bridges—plants crowd in with an intensity that feels deliberate. There are mats of fern and moss thick enough to swallow dropped tools. Vines lace the scaffolding of old construction projects and do not care that those projects were never finished. Small trees cling to ledges with roots splayed like hands, feeding on mist and mineral dust. The green is deep and wet-looking even in dim light, and it gives the rock a sense of being inhabited rather than merely present.

Yet overall the region reads as sparse because these oases are separated by distance and emptiness: long spans of exposed stone, wide wind-lanes, open voids between cliffs where you can look out and see nothing but fog and the occasional shadow of a bridge disappearing into it. Districts don’t blur together; they’re separated by gulfs, both literal and social. A neighborhood can be a shelf of land a kilometer long and fifty meters wide, densely worked, loud with machines—then suddenly it ends and there is only air, then another shelf, another hanging infrastructure, another pocket of life.

The city—if “city” is the right word for something distributed across verticality—has been built onto the mountains rather than *in* them. It’s urban in organization: grids, zoning, transport lines, stacked utilities. But it’s not compact; it’s strung out and layered, a network of platforms, suspended streets, elevators, and switchbacks. The oldest structures are heavy masonry and riveted iron—foundations that look like they were designed to convince the mountain to cooperate. Newer additions are industrial and pragmatic: corrugated walls, pipe forests, conveyor runs, gantries with warning paint fading in the mist. Where there is flat ground, it is precious and used for things that demand stability: foundries, generator halls, warehouses, civic buildings whose facades face not outward but inward toward the nearest reliable approach.

Infrastructure is everywhere and it makes the place loud even when it is “quiet.” You hear the clank and whine of cableways hauling freight between terraces. You hear the low, constant shudder of pumps moving condensate upward to reservoirs. You hear the cough of engines, the alternating thrum of dynamos, the impact rhythm of stamping presses echoing through stone that carries sound strangely well. Wind adds its own noise: it threads through trusses and pipes and turns whole districts into instruments—keening in gaps, drumming loose sheets of metal, making guy-wires hum in slow, shifting chords. Sometimes the loudest sound is not a machine but a mass: a flock of something winged passing close, a rush of air and thousands of small calls blending into one moving roar.

Fauna thrives here, not despite the industrial city but partly because of it. Heat leaks, food waste, sheltered cavities, and constant moisture pockets create microhabitats. The most obvious creatures are birds—too many kinds to count—using the vertical world like a forest turned on its side. Some nest in ventilation stacks warmed by exhaust; others make colonies under bridge decks, their droppings mineralizing into pale crusts that have to be chipped away from joints. There are small mammals adapted to ladders and cables: long-limbed, sure-footed, moving like they were built for catwalks. In the lush damp zones you find amphibians in improbable abundance, slick-bodied and unafraid, living in the drip-lines of condensers. Insect life is constant: clicking, buzzing, the faint rain of wings against lamps. At night—though “night” is mostly a deepening of the same dimness—bioluminescent things appear in the green pockets: fungi that glow along the underside of beams, larvae in moss that pulse like slow embers, making the vegetation look electrically alive.

And then there is the weirdness, the way reality here behaves as if it is still under construction too.

The first rule visitors learn is that distances do not always agree with one another. A bridge can look like it crosses a gap of two hundred meters, but halfway across you realize your steps have been too few or too many. Sometimes you arrive too quickly, as if the far end has been pulled toward you; sometimes you walk and walk, the latticework repeating, fog swallowing your progress, and only then does the landing appear—closer than it should have been, like it has been waiting just out of sight. Locals paint measurement marks on railings and still argue about them because the marks do not stay consistent from week to week.

The second rule is about gravity—not that it fails dramatically, but that it has moods. In certain vertical basins, falling objects hesitate for a fraction of a second longer than they should. In certain stairwells built into raw rock, your body feels lighter by a small, noticeable margin; workers take these routes when carrying heavy loads, and supervisors post signs warning against “unrated buoyancy corridors.” In a few places, rain does not fall straight down. It drifts sideways in thin sheets as if following invisible slopes in the air.

The third rule is sound. The stone here is young, full of stresses and hollow seams, and it conducts noise into places you wouldn’t expect. You can stand in a quiet alcove behind a warehouse and hear, perfectly clearly, the spoken argument of a foreman two terraces away, while the stamping press directly above you is muffled to a distant heartbeat. There are “listening walls” used by couriers and children alike: lean your ear to the right patch of rock and a different district’s life arrives—laughter, machinery, the sudden scream of a hawk—without attenuation, without delay, as if the mountain is piping the city through itself. Sometimes the piping is wrong and you hear things that have not happened yet, or echoes that never belonged to any source. The city has learned not to treat sound as evidence.

Light is unreliable too, but in a subtler way. In the dim, foggy air, lamps throw cones that should be straightforward. Here they sometimes don’t reach as far as they should, cut off abruptly by a plane of darkness like a wall; or they extend too far, illuminating a distant cliff face with the clarity of noon while the immediate walkway remains in shadow. People carry small calibrated lanterns not only to see but to check whether the world is agreeing to behave. A lantern that throws a normal pool of light is a comfort.

All of this makes the city feel provisional, like a settlement built on a moving idea. Yet the dynamics are slow. The mountain’s changes are not daily catastrophes; they are gradual shifts in how the place allows itself to be measured. A stair that takes thirty steps this year might take thirty-one next year. A drip line will move a few centimeters per season as condensation patterns migrate. Whole gardens relocate, not by being uprooted but by *fading* from one ledge and thickening on another as if the dampness has decided to favor a different architecture. The industrial apparatus responds with equal patience: crews re-level rails, re-tension cables, adjust pump timings. There’s a bureaucratic calm to it. Forms are filed. New maps are printed with dates, because maps here are always dated.

From a distance, the region looks open: a sequence of terraces and hanging works extending beyond sight, a broad highland with cities strung like beads along the mountain’s ribs. Up close, it feels like living inside a scaffolded geology lesson—stone that is still becoming stone, water that exists in strategies, light that has to be negotiated. The air smells of wet metal, moss, coal smoke, and the sharp mineral tang of freshly exposed rock. The ground is often slick. The wind has a chill that keeps you aware of your skin. Somewhere, always, there is the low thunder of industry and the busy, layered noise of too many animals making use of the same vertical world.

And if you stand at the edge of one of the great gaps—one of the unfinished valleys—and look down, you may not see the bottom. Not because it is too deep (though it is), but because the fog down there has weight and texture and seems to gather itself into shapes that the eye wants to interpret as streets, as rivers, as the continuation of the city in a lower register. Locals call it “the under-plan.” Some swear there are districts down there that can be reached on the right day by taking the wrong elevator. The city’s official stance is that there is no under-plan, only cloud.

The city’s unofficial stance is a network of hand-painted arrows on support beams pointing to ladders that aren’t on any map, and small shrines in sheltered drip-caves where condensation collects in bowls, where the lush green grows thickest, and where the mountain’s unfinished reality feels—if not safe—at least conversational.
