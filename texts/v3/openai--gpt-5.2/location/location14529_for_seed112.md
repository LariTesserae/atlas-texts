The first thing you meet is not a horizon but a glare: a sheet of brightness so clean it seems to erase distance. The air is thin enough that the sun feels close—white, unsentimental, with edges—yet the temperature stays cool, held down by altitude and by a constant, combing wind. You taste minerals before you taste anything else: chalk, salt, the faint metallic tang of ground stone kept airborne.

There is almost no water here. Not “little,” not “seasonal”—*structurally absent*. The sky makes no promises. Clouds, when they exist, are dry: high veils of dust and crystalline aerosols, glittering like ground glass. Moisture is a rumor; it comes only as brief hoarfrost on the shadow side of things, and even that is a different chemistry—condensed volatiles, pale films that vanish as soon as the sun swings around.

And still the place is vegetation.

Not in the way a forest is vegetation—thick, wet, generous. Here the plant-life is what the land is *made of*. It occupies the role that soil and stone usually play. It has become substrate, scaffolding, skin. The plateau—because it is a plateau, a vast high tableland ribbed by long ridges—wears a living crust that behaves like a material more than an organism: matte in the morning, glassy at noon, cracking open in slow, deliberate tessellations when the temperature shifts.

From far away the vegetation reads as sparse because it’s arranged in enormous spacing. There are miles of open emptiness between presences: broad salt pans where nothing rises above ankle height, wind-abraded corridors of bare mineral, basins full of nothing but light and sound. But whenever you look closely at “empty” ground, it resolves into plant—flat, adherent, stubborn plant, inlaid into the rock like lichen scaled up to a continent.

It grows without water by being almost unwilling to be soft. Its tissues are mineral-stiff, threaded with silica and salts, packed with waxes and ionic gels—fluids that don’t evaporate the way water does, that don’t freeze the way water freezes. Leaves here are not leaves so much as *vanes*: thin, angled plates that catch wind and sunlight. The plants drink air the way other plants drink rain, taking in trace molecules and dust, binding them, building their bodies out of scarcity.

The terrain has been shaped by ages of wind. Erosion is settled into a kind of equilibrium—everything weathered into forms that have learned how to persist: mesas with rounded edges, long dune-fields of pale grit anchored by living mesh, cliff faces fluted like organ pipes. The wind does the carving, but so do the plants. Whole ridges are stabilized by root-threads that behave like rebar, and then, after a season of growth, *released*—allowed to fail in controlled collapses that pour material downhill like slow avalanches. The land is old, but it is never at rest.

Sound is constant, and it is too loud.

It starts with the wind: a continuous roar that rises and falls like surf (except there is no surf, no sea to justify the mimicry). Then you hear the vegetation itself. The vane-leaves rattle against one another in sheets; the stiff stems knock and click; hollow stalks act as pipes. Across the flats, the living crust fractures and reseals with sharp, percussive reports—dry pops and snaps that travel far in the thin air. When gusts pour through the fluted cliffs, the rock *plays*, producing tones low enough to be felt in your chest. Add the animals and it becomes deafening in layers: drumming, stridulation, the grinding chorus of countless jaws.

Fauna are abundant, because the plants are everywhere and because “everywhere” includes the air.

Most creatures here are built for wind. There are glider-herds—light-bodied grazers with membrane sails that catch the updrafts along ridge lines. They don’t so much walk as tack, leaning into gusts, touching down only to rasp the living crust with mineral teeth. There are insect analogues by the billions: hard-shelled, reflective, tuned to vibration. Their wings are not delicate; they are corrugated and loud, turning flight into a kind of percussion. On calm days (rare), the sky is filled with drifting things: seed-kites, spore-bladders, tiny predators suspended on threads like airborne fishing lines.

The ecology runs on minerals and light, and on movement. With water missing, digestion and circulation are different: oils, waxes, salt-liquids; chemistry that tolerates desiccation as a default state. A dead organism does not rot so much as crumble and oxidize, becoming dust that is immediately someone else’s building material. Nothing is wasted because nothing can afford to be.

The weirdness is not that life exists without water—after a while that feels like a rule you stop questioning. The unusual thing is the industrialness of it, and how the land produces it without anything that looks built.

In certain bands—always aligned with prevailing winds—the vegetation organizes into repeating structures that behave like machinery. Not metal machinery, not human-made, but *industrial* in function and scale. Fields of tall stems grow in staggered ranks, each one a flexible column with a stiff vane at the top. When the wind hits, the vanes rotate, and the entire field becomes a continent-sized array of turbines. The spinning does work: it drives peristaltic pumping in the stems, pushing nutrient gels through underground capillary mats. It also generates electricity, or something like it—charge separation across salt membranes—stored in buried nodes that hum faintly until you get close, and then you feel your hair lift in the static.

Elsewhere, in hollows where dust collects, the plants build “conveyor” growths: slow-moving belts of interlinked segments that crawl centimeter by centimeter, dragging mineral grains toward processing mounds. These mounds look like termite hills at first, but they are plant tissue—porous, layered, full of internal channels. Inside them, heat is managed and gradients are maintained. Some vent warm air that smells of ozone and crushed stone. The vents whistle and moan, adding to the roar.

No one built any of it. There are no artifacts, no walls, no tools left behind. The industrial shapes are simply what this biosphere does when you give it bright light, high winds, and time: it becomes a planet-spanning factory whose product is itself, and whose fuel is weather.

And the weather is its own engine.

Turbulence is not episodic here; it is the normal condition. The plateau creates its own circulation: daytime heating over bright flats, rapid cooling over shaded escarpments, pressure gradients that set the air in motion. Dust storms don’t roll in as fronts; they *boil up* from the ground, drawn into towering columns that twist like living things. Dry lightning flickers inside them—blue-white, branchless, crackling across particles. When those storms pass over turbine-fields, the sound becomes unbearable: a scream of rotating vanes, a rattle of stems, thunder that has no rain attached to it.

In the storm’s wake the land is different, but not in a catastrophic way—more like an anthill after someone’s foot has nudged it. New channels have been cut in drifted grit. Whole sheets of living crust have peeled back and reattached somewhere else. Spore-kites have snagged on ridges and unfolded into new mats. Herds relocate, following fresh growth the way ocean animals follow currents.

At night the temperature drops sharply. The bright day gives way to a hard, clear darkness packed with stars, because there is no moisture to soften anything. The wind seldom stops, but it changes character: lower, steadier, a continuous bass note. The plants retract their vanes, reducing drag; the turbine-fields quiet into a hiss. In the coldest pockets, a thin glaze forms on the downwind side of rocks—condensed volatiles, not water, shimmering like a second skin. Small animals come to lick it, not for drink in the usual sense but for trace compounds, for salts, for whatever the night managed to precipitate out of emptiness.

If there is a single rule this place teaches, it is that absence can be a builder. With water removed, everything else has become exaggerated: wind as river, dust as soil, electricity as sap, sound as the constant reminder that the world is working. The landscape is vast enough to have its own moods—calm bands, violent bands, migrating belts of growth that take decades to traverse a continent—and yet it remains strangely open, spacious, full of bright emptiness between immense living mechanisms.

Stand anywhere long enough and you begin to feel it: not wilderness in the untouched sense, but a mature, weathered system that has found its balance inside turbulence. An old, high, dry world where the plants learned to become architecture, and the architecture learned to sing.
