Below the surface—well below the surface—the world is mostly one substance wearing different moods.

Water, everywhere, but not “clear.” It has weight and grain. It carries itself like a crowded room: packed with suspended silt, needle-fine ice crystals, and the pale dust of eroded stone. Visibility is a short conversation—ten, maybe twenty paces in the best moments—before the darkness takes the rest. Light exists only as a memory of direction, a faint thinning of black above, and then the patient, permanent dim of depth.

The temperature is steady in its coldness. Not the sharp cold of wind, but the slow, numbing cold that enters by contact and remains. Everything here is slightly slower because of it: reactions, decay, movement. Even turbulence feels measured, like a gigantic thing shifting in sleep rather than a quick violence. The water rolls and shears in layers. There are currents stacked on currents—one going east, another a hand’s-breadth below it going west, each with its own saltiness, its own density, its own stubbornness.

And the seafloor—if it deserves that name—is an old surface, worn down past drama. Erosion has had so long to work that sharp features are rare. Ridges are rounded into low backs. Cracks have been filled and refilled until they are seams instead of gaps. What would have once been cliffs are now slopes, their angles softened by ages of collapse and sediment settling like endless, fine snowfall. The ground is a mosaic of materials: broad swathes of dark basalt polished to a dull sheen, pale sediment plains that puff at a touch, and gravel beds where stones have been rolled so long their edges have forgotten how to cut.

Packed doesn’t mean crowded with buildings—there are none. No straight lines. No posts. No walls. The density is the density of *presence*: particulate clouds, drifting veils of organic debris, mats of microbial film clinging to rock in slick, almost invisible layers. If you put a hand down, it comes away coated—fine grit, cold slime, salt. Even “empty” water is full of small things moving independently: flecks rotating in tiny eddies, microbubbles caught and carried, threads of plant matter breaking down into softer and softer fragments. Space is occupied by the water’s contents as much as by the water itself.

Over a region’s span, the place changes by degrees rather than borders. A plain becomes a field of low hummocks. Hummocks become shallow channels. Channels become broad, scooped basins where heavier silt collects and the currents slow just enough for the water to clear—briefly—before another pulse comes through and stirs it all up again. Somewhere up above, storms and tides are constantly rewriting the pressure gradients, and that distant weather reaches down here as restlessness: periodic surges, sudden directional shifts, rolling turbidity currents that slide along the bottom like underwater sandstorms.

When those bottom-flows come, they don’t roar the way air-storms do. Water muffles. The soundscape stays restrained: a low, nearly sub-audible thrum in the bones; the faint hiss of grains skittering; occasional ticks when stones knock together and settle into new positions. Sometimes, if the current tears a sheet of frazil ice past a rock outcrop, you hear a thin, glassy rasp—quick, distant, gone. Mostly it is quiet enough that you notice your own disturbances: the swirl of a fin, the bubble of trapped gas released from mud, the soft *whump* of sediment collapsing into a footprint-like depression.

Vegetation is sparse, but not absent. It appears where the rules briefly allow it: in shallower reaches where a little more light leaks down, in places where currents bring nutrients without ripping everything loose. There, the “plants” are practical—short, tough fronds anchored to stone, low algae that spreads like a stain, kelp-like strands that never dare grow tall because anything tall becomes a sail, and sails get torn away. In deeper areas, “green” gives up entirely and the living films become colorless: bacteria and archaea making a thin, slick skin on rock, feeding on what drifts down or seeps up. Life is present, but it keeps a low profile, clinging rather than reaching.

Fauna is rarer than you expect for so much water. It’s not a barren sea—more a sea where animals have learned that being obvious is expensive. You might go a long time seeing nothing larger than a drifting comb-jelly pulse, a pale amphipod flicker over the silt, a worm retreating into a tube the same color as the mud. Then, in the middle distance, a single fish appears like an error in the darkness—one lantern-eye, a line of faint photophores, a body built to waste no motion. It hangs in the water as if suspended from an invisible thread, making the smallest corrections, reading the currents with its whole skin. If it moves, it moves once, decisively, and is gone.

The turbulence here is not a constant frenzy but a repeated breaking-and-settling, like breath. Long slow drift, then an abrupt rearrangement. Sediment lifted, carried, dropped. A gravel bed subtly migrates over years. A basin fills and empties. A low ridge is shaved a little flatter each century. In this way the landscape is ancient not because it is static, but because it has been changing in the same few ways for an unimaginably long time—changes so habitual they have become the place’s character.

There are no constructed things, but there are traces of primitive use—not permanent, not architectural, not even reliably human. A stone that has been struck and left, edges sharper than the sea normally permits. A line of shell fragments arranged by current and chance into something that *looks* intentional until the next surge scatters it. A bone wedge jammed under a rock, perhaps from an animal worrying at a carcass, perhaps from a hand that needed leverage and had nothing else. Nothing persists long. The water does not respect arrangements. It returns all careful placements back to the general drift.

Rules are simple and strict:

- Light dies quickly; color dies before light does. Red vanishes first, then orange and yellow, until everything is a blue-black and grey-brown world.
- Pressure is ordinary law. Hollow things collapse. Air cannot survive in pockets for long. Warmth rises and is stripped away.
- Sound travels far but arrives softened—information without volume.
- Anything not anchored will eventually be transported. Anything anchored will eventually be abraded.
- The bottom is not solid so much as *negotiated*: step wrong and it billows; move too fast and you blind yourself in your own stirred-up world.

What makes the place feel “full” is not bustle but layered persistence. Water pressing from every side. Cold holding everything in a long pause. Ancient stone made smooth by time. Darkness closing the distance. Currents constantly busy in their quiet way, packing the world with motion you can’t always see—until a veil of silt lifts like a curtain and you realize the landscape has been shifting the whole time, right under your eyes, in a silence dense enough to lean on.
