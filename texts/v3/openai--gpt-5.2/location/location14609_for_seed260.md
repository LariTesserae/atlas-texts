The first thing that makes sense is height.

Not the simple height of a mountain you can point to, but a height that feels like a rule imposed on your bones—air that is too thin to be generous, distances that seem to slope downward into nothing, horizons that sit *below* you like a dropped ribbon. The land is an open region of broken plateaus and newly-hoisted ridgelines, as if someone recently grabbed the crust and pulled it upward with impatient hands. Nothing here has had time to soften. Edges are still sharp enough to look freshly cut; slopes still carry the raw geometry of their making.

And yet it is not bright. The light is dim in a way that isn’t simply “cloudy day,” but “the world is backlit by an exhausted lantern.” Above, a sun exists—sometimes—but it is often behind a dense veil: a permanent high haze of mineral dust and thin, pale aerosols that turn even noon into a pewter twilight. Shadows are present, but shallow. The landscape doesn’t contrast; it broods.

Then the sound arrives.

It is not the occasional gust, not the dramatic roar of a storm. It is continuous, oppressive, deafening—a sustained pressure that turns the air into a physical thing. You feel it in teeth, in joints, in the soft tissues behind the eyes. The noise is layered: a deep, organ-like drone rising from the fissures; a harsh, constant scream where wind is forced through narrow slots in the rock; periodic *cracks* like distant artillery as new stone cools and splits. Sometimes the tone changes with no corresponding change in weather, as if the land itself is being played.

It takes a while to understand that the land is new enough to still be *settling into its own laws*.

### Terrain: unfinished high country
The region spreads valley-to-valley, plateau-to-plateau—mid-range in scale, open enough that you can walk for days and remain in the same “kind” of place, and yet always with multiple features in view.

- **Ridge spines** run like exposed ribs, knife-thin and oddly regular, too straight to be purely erosion. They are uplift seams—creases where the world has been folded.
- **Basins** lie between them, not filled with rivers or lakes but with pale mineral flats: salt, gypsum, chalky evaporites that never actually evaporated from water, but precipitated from airborne chemistry—dust reacting with cold and pressure.
- **Chasms** split the plateaus with vertical confidence. Their walls show strata that look mis-ordered. In places, layers repeat like a stutter: black glass, white brittle stone, black glass again, as if geology forgot what it already placed.
- **Scree fields** are small, sparse, and young—just a scattering of broken shards at the base of cliffs. There hasn’t been time for extensive breakdown. Erosion is *emerging*: the landscape is still more construction site than ruin.

If you pick up a rock, it often feels wrong in small ways. Some are too light. Some are faintly warm despite the cool air. Some emit a soft vibration that matches the background drone, like they are resonators rather than inert objects.

### Materials: dry chemistry that pretends to be fertile
There is almost no water in any familiar sense. No streams. No wet soil. No dew that beads and runs. Even “humidity” feels like a foreign concept here—air is dry enough to sting.

Instead, the ground is a conversation between minerals and cold gases:

- **Glass-skin basalt** in smooth pours, the edges still glossy. It suggests recent heat, but the ambient temperature is cool, so the heat must have been sudden, local, and gone quickly.
- **Porous chalkstone** that crumbles under finger pressure into fine powder, then clumps again as if magnetized by static.
- **Salt-cement** forming delicate bridges between stones, making lacework crusts that snap with a sharp report—one of many small contributions to the relentless noise.
- **Metallic seams**—simple, workable ores near the surface. Not abundant enough to be an industrial promise, but enough to invite primitive tools, enough to tempt hands.

The dust is everywhere. It hangs in the dim light and settles in thin drifts in the lee of any obstacle. When the wind rises, it doesn’t simply blow; it *sandblasts* softly, a constant dry whisper under the larger roar.

### Atmosphere: cool, thin, loud—and not entirely consistent
The temperature stays cool most of the time. It’s the cool of altitude and dim light, not the cool of shade beside water. When the wind slackens (rarely), you can feel radiative chill in the open, as if the sky is a vast cold sink.

Reality here does not hold perfectly. It frays.

- Sound sometimes arrives before its cause: a crack echoes, and only a heartbeat later does the cliff-face split.
- Shadows occasionally point the wrong way, briefly aligning with the drone rather than the sun.
- Gravity behaves normally until you approach certain uplift seams, where it tilts by a few degrees—subtle enough to make you nauseated rather than to make you float.
- Distances “breathe.” A far ridge seems to lean closer, then relax away, like a slow lung.

These breakdowns are not fast or theatrical. They are slow, patient errors—consistent with the whole place’s gradual tempo.

### Ecology: lush vegetation without animals, nourished by noise and stone
The surprise is how green—how *lush*—it can be, despite the dryness and the mineral harshness.

But the plants here are not fed by water the way you expect. They are built for scarcity, and they are opportunists of the unusual.

Across the basins and up the plateau edges grow **thickets of tall, reedlike stalks** with matte, dark surfaces. They do not transpire; they do not sweat water into the air. Their “leaves” are thin fins, ridged like radiators, designed to harvest trace volatiles and to shed dust in vibrating sheets.

In sheltered pockets—behind boulders, in the lee of standing stones—there are **cushion-mats** of low, dense growth: spongey-looking but dry to the touch, made of interlocking fibers and mineral scales. When the drone deepens, these mats ripple, as if the sound is combing them.

On some cliff faces, **hanging gardens** cling in impossible places: not vines, but braided cables of plant tissue laced with pale crystals. They anchor into hairline cracks and slowly pry them open, not by water-driven roots but by crystalline growth—expanding, contracting, grinding stone into nutrient powder.

There are no animals. No insects. No birds. No carrion, no tracks, no burrows. The silence of life is total—except that it is not *silent*, because the world itself is so loud.

What replaces animal activity is plant motion at a different timescale:

- Some stalks turn their fins across the wind not to catch light, but to catch *sound*. You can watch a stand of them orient to the loudest direction like a field of compasses seeking a magnetic pole.
- In the presence of certain resonant stones, growth is faster—still slow by human patience, but visibly favored. A patch near a humming seam will be denser, taller, greener (or the local equivalent of green: deep charcoal, coppery emerald, bruised violet).
- The plants exchange fine dust and spores on the wind, but the wind is violent enough that dispersal is not gentle; it is ballistic. Life here is built to survive impact.

If you kneel and listen closely—an absurd suggestion in a deafening place—you can sometimes detect a higher, thinner chorus within the roar: the faint rasp of plant tissues vibrating, millions of tiny structures tuned to the land’s constant note. It is as if the flora is not merely enduring the noise but feeding on it, using vibration as a metabolic input, converting mechanical energy into chemical work.

### Built traces: scattered, primitive, and careful
Human presence—if it is human—is sparse and scattered. A few structures appear like afterthoughts against the huge unfinished terrain.

- **Dry-stone shelters** tucked into rock folds: low, thick-walled, roofed with flat slabs. They offer protection from wind-driven grit more than from cold.
- **Standing stones** set in lines along plateau edges, each one slightly different in height, like a crude graph of the land’s shifting gravity. Some are carved with shallow grooves that whistle when the wind crosses them, adding a piercing overtone to the ambient roar.
- **Cairns** at chasm crossings, built not as memorials but as warnings: here the ground’s rules misbehave.
- **Tool marks** in soft chalkstone near ore seams: someone has chipped out nodules with stone hammers, perhaps with simple metal wedges where they could manage it. The work is cautious. Nothing suggests large-scale mining—just enough for blades, hooks, nails, small necessities.

There are no roads in the usual sense, but there are **paths of absence**: places where vegetation has been repeatedly pushed aside, where dust has been compacted into a darker sheen. The travelers—whoever they were—moved carefully, stopping often, likely because the sound and the shifting physics make long movement exhausting.

In a few shelters you find remnants that hint at a local rule: braided plant-fiber cords hung in loops from rafters, each loop a different length. When the wind comes, they vibrate at different pitches, producing a wavering chord. Not decoration—tuning. Calibration. An attempt to keep the world’s note from drifting too far.

### The world’s rules, as the place seems to enforce them
Spend time here and patterns emerge, less like natural laws and more like habits the landscape can’t stop doing.

1. **The land is still forming.** Uplift is ongoing but slow—measured in the patient creak of stone, in the occasional new fissure, in the way a cliff edge a year later looks subtly more strained.
2. **Erosion has not yet caught up.** There are few rounded forms. Talus is young. Gullies are rare, because there is almost no water to cut them, and because wind—though loud—works more by polishing than by carving, at least so far.
3. **Sound is structural.** The constant drone is not a side effect; it is a framework. It correlates with the seams, with the gravity-tilts, with where plants thrive. Silence—true silence—does not occur, and in the rare pockets where the roar is damped by terrain, things feel unstable, as if the place needs its noise to hold together.
4. **Life is botanical and mineral.** Without animals, the ecosystem is a slow exchange between dust, stone, and plant tissue. Death is not eaten; it is ground down and re-assimilated. Decay is dry, crystalline, and long.

### How it feels to stand there
You stand on a plateau rim and look out across a basin that might have been a lake in another world. Instead it is pale and dry, stitched with salt-cement veins and dotted—sparsely, but undeniably—with lush stands of vibrating reeds. Beyond, ridges rise in serrated ranks, each one catching the dim light differently, each one singing its own harsh register as the wind threads through it.

The air is cold enough to keep your skin tight. The grit finds every seam in clothing. The roar never lets up; it becomes a second heartbeat you don’t control.

And the strangest part is that, for all the reality-breakdown—shadows misbehaving, distances breathing, gravity leaning—nothing feels chaotic. It feels *in progress*. A place learning to be a place.

As if, given enough slow time, it might settle into a stable world.

Or as if the sound is the only thing preventing it from coming apart.
