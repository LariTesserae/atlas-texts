The first thing you learn here is that “ground” is a temporary courtesy.

It looks like land from a distance—low, wide, a basin spreading out to a hazy horizon—but up close everything is in the act of becoming something else. A skin has formed over water. A crust has formed over mud. A pavement has formed over a crust. None of it is old enough to have softened into familiar shapes. There are no rounded hills, no patient river-cut banks. The edges are sharp and young: broken plates of pale stone like fresh-fired pottery, seams of dark clay still wet in their own shine, ridges that look snapped rather than worn.

And everywhere, water.

Not dramatic water—no clear rivers with banks, no proud waterfalls—just water as a condition. It is in the air in a way you can taste, warm and metallic. It’s on every surface as a thin, persistent film. It beads on rock that should not be weeping, gathers under your fingernails, slicks every rope and rung. Step on a slab and the pressure answers with a slow bleed of brown; pause too long and condensation pearls on your hair as if you’ve walked into a very gentle rain that never finishes falling.

The terrain is low and open, but crowded. It is a flatland built out of layers: shallow pools stacked in terraces; lanes laid over lanes; platforms bridging platforms. The city does not sit *on* the marsh-edge so much as *thread through* it, like weaving done with stone and bone instead of reed. The horizon is a dim smear because the atmosphere is always thick—steam and mist lifting from warm water and cooling almost immediately into fog that refuses to clear. The light you get is not night-dark, but it is permanently subdued, as if the sun is behind a lid. Shadows exist only as soft bruises under eaves.

There are no plants. No grass, no mangrove, no moss to soften the edges or hold the soil. It changes how everything reads. Without green as a reference, the world is all mineral and animal: pale tufa and grey clay; slick black basalt; the dull ivory of bone; the waxy shine of shells. Even the city’s textures are not wooden but layered and striated—plaster over stone, hide over frame, shell-inlay over clay, all made by hand and repaired by hand. When something breaks, you see the fiber of sinew, the grain of fired mud, the laminated histories of re-used material. Nothing is hidden behind paint that can last.

The ground itself is still “under construction,” but the construction is natural and constant, like a factory that has found its steady rate and never varies.

Warm water rises everywhere—through cracks, through vents, through the seams between slabs—bringing dissolved minerals up from below. When that water meets air, it drops its load: limestone blooms into pale, porous terraces; salt crystals fur the edges of basins; thin sheets of mineral varnish creep over anything left still long enough. You can watch it if you’re patient: a white rim thickening along a pool’s lip over days, a bridge’s underside acquiring icicle-like drips of stone that never melt because they aren’t ice. It’s deposition so steady it feels like time has been replaced with a slow, audible process.

Erosion is almost absent—not because nothing moves, but because whatever water would normally carry away is replaced and re-made as fast as it can be lost. The city’s older surfaces aren’t worn down; they are grown over. Steps become shallower under a skin of new mineral. Drain channels tighten as their walls accrete. Even bones left in the open take on a chalky casing, as if the land is impatient to turn everything into the same pale stone.

This is why the place can feel embryonic and eternal at once: the forms are raw, but the pattern is stable. The basin keeps its shape. The terraces keep their edges. The fog keeps its depth. The water keeps rising, and the stone keeps forming, and the city keeps adapting—always busy, never *changing* in the way a traveler expects change.

The deafening sound is the other constant.

It isn’t one noise but a continuous stack of them, so dense you stop hearing the edges. There’s the rushing hiss of vents and seeps—thousands of little exhalations. There’s the steady gurgle of water traveling under thin crust, the hollow drum when it passes through cavities. There’s the slap and suck of feet on wet stone, the clack of shell and bone tools, the rasp of ropes sliding through mineral-furred pulleys.

And then there are the animals.

In a world without plants, everything that lives eats film, eats drift, eats each other, or eats what rises from beneath. The shallow pools are crowded with things that look like mollusks until they unfurl and you see jointed limbs; with eels that breathe air and water; with crustacean swarms that turn the water into a moving texture. Along the edges of terraces, colonies of small, slick-bodied grazers rasp bacterial mats off stone with teeth like files. They are not “plants,” not even close—more like animated mouths designed to harvest whatever chemistry coats the mineral.

Above the waterline, larger creatures own the city’s gaps. Amphibians the size of dogs cling to walls and gutters, their toes spread wide on wet mineral. They nest in warm vents where the steam keeps their eggs from cooling, and their calls—low, throaty, repeated with tireless sameness—add a pulsing bassline to the basin. Flocks of pale birds, more scavenger than songster, wheel through the fog and scream at one another over carcasses and offal, their cries sharp enough to cut through the hiss. In the alleys and under platforms, you hear the constant skitter of shell-backed things that live on scraps and shed mineral scales like dandruff.

The abundance is not lush; it is crowded. Every surface is occupied: by something crawling, something clinging, something feeding. The lack of plant life doesn’t create emptiness—it shifts the density into motion. If you stand still, you begin to feel it: little vibrations through the soles as creatures move under the thin crust; occasional taps as something bumps the underside of a bridge; the prickling sensation of insects testing the salt on your skin.

The city has learned to build with what the place offers, and what the place offers is mineral, water, and animals.

Structures rise from the terraces on stilt-like columns of stone and fired clay. Some are clearly placed—stacked blocks, mortared joints, walls patched with plaster made from precipitated lime. Others are more like collaborations with the landscape: a house built over a vent so its floor is always warm; a market platform that uses an accreting ridge as a natural curb; a drainage channel carved once and then maintained by scraping away new growth before it chokes. Roofs are hide stretched over bone frames, pitched steep to shed condensation that isn’t rain but behaves like it. Every roofline drips steadily. Gutters are everywhere, because without them the city would become a slow waterfall.

There’s no wood to be had. Rope is twisted from sinew and tough gut fibers, or braided from the long, tendon-like strands harvested from the larger swimmers. Baskets are woven from strips of hide cured until stiff, or from thin, flexible slats of porous stone cut from fresh tufa before it hardens too much. Tools are stone and bone and shell—scrapers, awls, chisels—and in some places simple metal, soft and precious, hammered from nodules traded in from elsewhere or collected where the vents bring up odd deposits. Fire exists, but it’s cherished: fuel is rendered fat, dried dung, sulfurous chunks skimmed from certain pools. Smoke doesn’t rise cleanly in the damp; it smears into the fog and makes the air taste like bitterness.

Light comes mostly from openings in the cloud cover that never widen, and from the city itself. Fat lamps glow behind translucent shells, turning streets into a dim constellation under eaves. In some canals, the water shimmers with bioluminescent swarms—animals that pulse when disturbed—so a passing boat leaves a wake of greenish light that fades slowly, like memory. But even that is muted by mist. The world insists on half-visibility. Faces are known by voice and silhouette, by the rhythm of footsteps on a particular bridge.

The infrastructure is dense because it has to be: the only reliable “dry” is what you make. Walkways overlap like ribs. Platforms are layered; the city has a second and third level in places, not for height but for separation—fresh water runnels above brackish pools, living quarters above animal pens, storage platforms above the loudest vents. There are channels everywhere, because if water is left to choose its own route it will undermine everything, and the city doesn’t have the luxury of letting erosion learn how to be erosion. So the people enforce drainage with constant labor: scraping mineral growth, unclogging sluices, resetting stones that have been lifted by buoyant mud. It is primitive work done at urban scale—thousands of small human actions adding up to a stable geometry.

That stability is one of the place’s rules: processes here run at fixed rates, like machines set and left alone. The vents do not surge. The fog does not thin. The waterline does not creep. If there is a tide, it is so slight and so regular it becomes another background rhythm, never a surprise. Seasons don’t announce themselves with color or bloom; temperature stays warm, and warmth stays damp. Weather happens as a constant state rather than events. The city’s calendar, if it has one, must be built from social cycles—market days, hatchings, migrations—because the sky won’t help.

Even the sound follows that rule. The deafening quality is not from occasional catastrophe but from perpetual activity: animals calling, water hissing, people shouting to be heard, tools striking, shells clattering, bridges humming under foot traffic. Your ears never get a clean silence to reset. At night, if “night” exists under the same dim ceiling, the sound barely changes—different calls, fewer human voices, more splashing in the canals—but the volume remains like a wall you live inside.

Smell is part of the density too. Wet stone has a sour, clean bite; vents add sulfur and iron; animal life adds musk and brine and the sweet rot of protein. Without plants, there’s no green sweetness, no resin, no floral note to soften the edges. Everything is salt, mineral, and warm flesh.

And yet it is not an alien place. The weirdness is low because the logic is consistent. Water rises, stone grows, animals feed, people build and mend. If you watch long enough, you can predict it all. You learn which terraces are safe to cross because their crust rings solid under a tap. You learn which alleys to avoid because the fog there is thicker—meaning a vent is close, meaning the ground is thin. You learn the local etiquette: announce yourself on bridges because no one can see far; keep your lamp low because bright light in fog blinds others; don’t spill blood near certain pools because it will bring the scavengers in a surge that blocks the lanes.

From a high platform—if you climb one of the taller watch-frames made of bone and lashed poles—you can see how far the city extends: a regional sprawl of terraces and canals, clustered dwellings and work yards, all threaded by water. Beyond it, the basin continues, open and low, a field of pale, wet stone broken by dark seams. The fog smooths the distance so it feels both close and unreachable. Somewhere out there, the same vents hiss, the same minerals precipitate, the same creatures swarm. The whole landscape is an equilibrium that never gets bored of itself.

And when you climb down, the city takes you back into its constant roar and damp dimness, its young stone and old routines—everything always being made, and everything always already made enough to last.
