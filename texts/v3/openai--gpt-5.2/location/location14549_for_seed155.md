The plateau looks as though someone once pressed a palm flat on the world and left it there for a million years.

Everything is rounded—ridges blunted into long backs, gullies widened into patient troughs, cliff faces softened into sloping ribs. Stone still exists, but it is not the dominant surface. The dominant surface is plant: a continuous, stubborn skin that has learned the plateau’s final shape and refuses to let it change quickly. Where the land should be bare, it is filmed in green-black lichen, in tight velvet moss, in mats of braided stems that read, from a distance, like weathered fabric stretched over bone. The high elevation keeps the air cool and thin; breath feels slightly sharpened, like drinking cold water too fast.

Open space is the plateau’s first impression. Not the emptiness of absence—more a feeling of far-apart presences. Features are widely spaced and large: a river-cut trench here, a shallow basin there, a district of the city sprawled across a saddle between two worn-down peaks. Between them are broad sweeps of living ground where the vegetation is total but not tall: a low, continuous cover, interrupted only by occasional upthrusts—knuckled root-hills, fungal mounds, the domes of seed houses, the tall chimneys of industry.

Water is not generous. It appears as events and pockets. A week can pass with only a faint dampness beading on cables and leaves, then a short rain comes through—cold, fine, almost metallic—followed by hours of condensation that crawls out of the air and collects in the city’s gutters as though the gutters were coaxing it. In the shadowed folds of ancient erosion, water lingers: narrow runnels that are more felt than seen, spongey flats that give underfoot, cistern-hollows where roots have lined the basin with living waterproof bark. The river, when you find it, is a dark ribbon at the bottom of a gorge, audible long before it is visible.

The city doesn’t sit on the vegetation. The city is negotiated with it.

Streets are not paved so much as trained. Broad avenues are strips where the plant-skin has been coaxed into a hard, corklike surface—springy, resilient, faintly warm even in the cool air, with embedded fibers that carry current the way copper once did. Edges of sidewalks are literal edges: braided hedgerows shaped into curbs, their leaves trimmed so close they present as matte planes. In older districts the buildings are grown frames stiffened with resin, then clad in panels cut from living bark that heals its own scratches slowly, like skin.

Industry is layered into this living architecture, and it is loud enough that you can navigate by sound.

There are pump-houses with piston hearts that thud at regular intervals, drawing moisture up from the gorge and sending it through translucent, vein-threaded conduits suspended over the streets. There are mills whose rotors are driven by wind funneled between ridges—huge blades sheathed in lacquered leaf-laminate, whining when gusts hit them at the wrong angle. There are “fogworks” along the plateau’s lip: banks of condenser towers that look like pale trees without leaves, fitted with ribbed metal collars. When the air turns right, they sweat. Droplets gather, tick, then become a steady clatter into collection trays. The sound is not gentle; it’s a constant bright percussion, like many small hands flicking glass.

And always, under the mechanical noise, the plateau’s own: wind scraping over low vegetation in a long, abrasive hush; the river’s distant, persistent roar; the city’s vents exhaling; the occasional crack of a root shifting in the ground as it thickens against stone.

Fauna are present mostly as absence. You notice how few things move on their own.

Sometimes a shape flits across an opening—thin, membranous, almost leaflike, riding the wind rather than beating wings. Sometimes at dawn you see the glint of eyes in a drainage slot and then nothing: a rare scavenger, small and patient, evolved to live off the city’s plant waste and the occasional fungus bloom. Insects exist but feel more like weather than wildlife: brief, localized eruptions of tiny bodies that appear around a compost stack or a sap spill and vanish again, leaving only a faint clicking residue in the ear.

Plants do the moving here. Not in quick ways—except when they do.

In the warmer hours of daylight (still cool, but less sharp), the city’s living surfaces flex microscopically as they regulate moisture. Entire walls can “breathe” if you watch long enough: pores opening, then sealing, as if the building is concentrating. Some roofs unfurl and furl like slow eyelids to shed condensation or catch it. In the industrial quarter, vines serve as belts, grown in loops and kept under tension; you can hear them creak and hum as they turn flywheels. When a belt frays, it doesn’t snap cleanly. It sheds fibers like a plant shedding hair, and the maintenance crews—more gardeners than mechanics—splice the living ends back together.

The strangest thing is that the city is sparse without being small. Its districts are separated by wide tracts of low living ground, and within districts there are plazas so open they feel like exposed anatomy. The openness comes from old planning and from old fear: density invites moisture loss and invites certain kinds of errors.

Because the plateau doesn’t just host weirdness. It manufactures it.

Reality breakdown here has the patient quality of erosion—slow, cumulative, treated as a known hazard. Most of it is localized, but not always where you expect. You learn rules the way you learn weather patterns:

- **Distances are honest only in straight lines.** If you walk a curving street where living walls lean inward, you may arrive too soon, or not at all, because the street remembers a shorter route from a year when it grew differently. People paint straight white “truth lines” down main avenues; you keep one foot on the line if you want to stay in the same world.

- **Condensation has memory.** Water that forms on certain surfaces can carry impressions—thin, film-like echoes of what the surface last “saw.” A gutter might briefly show you the sky from two days ago. A factory window might bead up into tiny lenses that replay a shift change, silent and inverted. Most citizens don’t stare into collected water unless they mean to. There are laws about it.

- **The vegetation is continuous, so the city is continuous.** Not physically, but in a subtler sense: information travels through the plant substrate. Sometimes it’s intentional—signal routed through treated fibers, power distributed through living cables. Sometimes it’s not. A rumor can spread as an actual pattern of growth: a slight twist in leaves, a recurring knot in bark. People say the city “hears” you if you speak too close to the walls.

- **High places are less stable at noon.** Not from heat, but from light. Normal daylight here has a way of becoming too perfectly even, as though the sky is a huge diffuser pressed close. When that flat clarity settles in, edges lose their authority. A chimney’s shadow might detach and drift. A staircase might extend one step longer than it did yesterday. Most heavy work pauses during those hours.

The ancientness of the landscape shows in how everyone accommodates rather than conquers. Foundations don’t cut deep; they anchor into the plant mat and spread load like snowshoes. Excavation is rare and regulated, because digging breaks the continuity and invites sinkholes—not just in the ground but in causality. Where the city needs depth, it uses the gorge walls, clinging to them with terraces and platforms that look like shelves of woven roots reinforced with riveted steel.

Those terraces are loud with life of a different kind: the life of processes.

Steam hisses from pressure relief valves. Conveyors clatter as they move bundles of dried stalk-fuel and resin bricks. There is the periodic, thunderous cough of a turbine starting up, followed by the steady chant of its rotation. In the fogworks, alarms are tuned not just to mechanical failure but to atmospheric thresholds—tones that rise and fall like a choir warming up, warning crews when the air is about to produce water, or about to produce something that only resembles water.

On the days when rain comes, it is brief and often slanted, chased by wind. The plant-skin takes it greedily. Streets darken. The city’s surfaces gloss over and the sound changes: footsteps become soft, machinery becomes brighter, the river’s roar seems closer. Then the rain stops, and for hours afterward the plateau exhales. Mist lifts from every surface and moves in low sheets across the open ground between districts, as if the city is smoldering.

If you stand in one of the wide, sparse plazas—an oval of hardened plant-cork bordered by low, sculpted hedges—you can watch the whole place be itself.

To the east, the plateau rolls away in worn swells, each swell flecked with tiny structures like seeds scattered on a table. To the north, the gorge cuts a dark, ancient incision, and you can see the river’s damp breath rising. Above, the light is ordinary but insistently present, revealing everything without warmth. Around you, the city’s living walls hold their shapes with quiet effort, and the industrial spine keeps beating: pumps, turbines, condensers, belts, whistles. Between those sounds there are gaps where you can hear plant matter working—minute crackles, faint sapsong, the dry brush of leaves against steel.

It feels like a place that has reached a late form and decided to keep going anyway: not growing new kinds of life so much as refining the ones it has, building an urban metabolism on a high, cool, damp-when-it-can-be plateau at the end of erosion’s long sentence.

Here, the world doesn’t end. It stabilizes—then learns how to move inside its stability.
