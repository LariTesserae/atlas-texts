The first thing the plateau does is make you look up.

Not because of mountains—those are there, but they sit back like old shoulders—rather because the air itself is busy. A warm, wet sky at high elevation has a way of showing every motion it contains. Clouds do not pass so much as *work*: condense, drag, tear on ridgelines, and then spill into the streets as mist, as patient drizzle, as sudden strings of droplets that hang from cables and gutters until they let go.

The light is ordinary—white enough at midday, honeyed toward evening—but it is filtered through a constant thickness. You rarely see a hard shadow. Everything looks slightly rinsed, as if the world has been recently wiped down and not quite dried.

### Terrain: a high country that sweats
This is a vast highland basin, big enough that storms can be born in it and die in it. The horizon is a long, shallow curve of stone: dark volcanic layers, pale sedimentary shelves, and bands of exposed aggregate that show where old waterways once chewed the land and then settled into quieter habits. Erosion here is not dramatic; it is *complete*. Edges have had time to decide what angle they want to be.

Water sits near the surface, not in bright rivers so much as in *pressure*. Step onto an unpaved patch and your boot leaves a slow, filling print. Along the older cuts—service trenches, abandoned foundations, the flanks of freight embankments—the rock weeps. It isn’t a metaphor: thin sheets of moisture bead and run from pores in the stone, collecting minerals as they go, leaving faint glossy trails like varnish.

Where the city ends (and it never truly ends, it only thins), the land is pocked with shallow basins that would be marshes if anything green grew here. Instead they are mudfields and salt-edges: flat, reflective pans with a skin that cracks into polygons when a dry week comes, then melts back into slurry under the next slow rain.

### Materials: stone, metal, and wet film
With vegetation absent, every surface shows what it is made of.

Stone is everywhere: basalt blocks old enough to have rounded corners; pale concrete that has leached until it looks like bone; brick glazed by decades of mist. Metal is everywhere too, but not the clean metal of new machines. It’s the metal of a working city in a damp climate: riveted, layered, patched; painted and repainted; sweating at seams; blooming with rust that forms delicate curtains beneath bolts.

And over everything—stone, metal, glass—there is a persistent *film*. Not plant-moss, not lichen in the familiar sense, but thin mineral and microbial sheens that come and go with humidity: translucent slicks that catch the light like oil, chalky efflorescences that taste metallic on the tongue, faint pearly skins that form on standing water and then tear apart in the wind.

### A dense urban highland
The built environment has the density of a place that grew up under constraint: limited flat ground, abundant moisture, and a population—human and otherwise—that never really left.

The city is layered. At ground level: narrow streets with drainage grooves worn so deep they look carved. Above: a web of catwalks, pipes, and conveyors strung between buildings to keep feet out of the wet when possible. Below: service tunnels where warm water runs in insulated channels, where the air is louder and closer and always smells faintly of iron.

Infrastructure is not hidden; it is civic architecture. Pump-houses sit on corners like public squares. Cooling towers (or heating towers, depending on the district’s needs) rise like blunt monuments. Rail lines braid through everything, elevated where the ground slumps, sunk where old cuts already existed. Because the plateau is high, moving mass is expensive; industry concentrates in thick knots—factory districts that never truly sleep, only shift their emphasis from one kind of clatter to another.

You can feel the city’s weight in the way water moves. Runoff is managed in broad, shallow canals that thread every neighborhood. They are not pretty, but they are essential: overflow paths for heavy rains, collection routes for condensation, and, in a world without plants to drink the wetness back, the only way to keep streets from becoming long-term ponds.

### Sound: loud, constant, and dampened
The loudness isn’t sharp; the wet air softens edges. But there is always *more than one* sound, stacked.

- Low industrial bass: turbines, compressors, distant stamping, the throaty exhale of pressure valves.
- Water-noise everywhere: trickles in gutters, a steady hiss where warm pipes meet cooler air and sweat, the slap of droplets on sheet metal roofs.
- Animal noise—so much of it that it becomes weather. Calls, scrapes, wingbeats, the collective rustle of bodies moving through tight spaces.

In open plazas you hear the city’s far-off roar, like surf. In alleys the sound is intimate: drips and breaths, claws on stone, the sudden percussion of something heavy landing above you and shifting its weight.

At night the loudness changes rather than fades. Machines drop to maintenance tempos; fauna takes the foreground. The city becomes a chorus of hunger, territory, and routine.

### Ecology without vegetation: life that feeds on stone, water, and waste
The absence of plant life is not an absence of biology; it is an absence of *green architecture*. There are no trees to define streets, no grasses to soften edges, no leaf-litter smell. The city has no seasonal color. Instead, life is animal-heavy and microbial-small, with an ecology that leans on chemistry, detritus, and the immense steady output of an industrial settlement.

Food begins in places that would be minor in another world:

- **Mineral films** on wet stone that draw energy from chemical gradients—iron, sulfur, ammonia from runoff and exhaust.
- **Warm outflows** from factories where temperature differences and dissolved compounds allow thick microbial blooms in channels and settling basins.
- **Urban detritus** itself: grease, scraps, dead skin, discarded fiber, the constant slow shedding of a dense city.

From those bases, the fauna builds upward—and it is overwhelming.

There are grazers that do not graze plants. Along the canal edges you’ll see broad-backed, many-limbed creatures (some as small as a hand, some the size of a dog) moving in slow, practiced lines, rasping at slick stone with mouthparts like files. On rooftops, colonies of light-bodied fliers cling to warm vents and pick at the thin pearly skins that form where steam condenses. In service tunnels, pale swimmers move through shallow flows, filtering suspended bloom with feathery gills and leaving the water clearer behind them.

Predators exist in tight proximity to prey because the city makes corridors and choke points. They nest in structural cavities: the gap behind cladding panels, the warm space above transformers, the sheltered underside of old bridges. You learn to recognize the signs: a particular scatter of cleaned bones near a drain, a silence in a normally noisy passage, the sharp ammonia note that means something has claimed a territory.

The fauna is not exotic in its *behavior*—it eats, mates, competes, migrates—but it is adapted to a world without leaves. Teeth are often ridged for scraping or crushing. Many bodies incorporate mineral: shells that look like river stone, scales that hold a metallic sheen from diet, bones dense enough to handle the plateau’s open exposures and hard landings.

### Atmosphere: warm wet high air
High elevation usually brings crispness; here it brings thinness and a mild dizziness under exertion, but the temperature stays warm because the plateau is a heat engine. Geothermal seams, industrial waste heat, and a basin-shaped topography keep the air from fully clearing. Moisture hangs and recycles. Rain feels close to the skin, not cold.

Fog is a daily event rather than a special one. Morning often arrives as a blankness that gradually reveals edges: first the nearest wall beaded with water, then the outlines of pipes and bridges, then, much later, the distant terraces of the city stepping away toward the basin rim.

When sunlight finally strengthens, it doesn’t burn the moisture off so much as turn it into motion. You see steam lifting from roofs, from animal bodies, from open grates. You hear it too: a soft, continuous sighing from millions of evaporating surfaces.

### Slow dynamics: a stable machine, a patient climate
For all the movement—water, animals, industry—the place changes slowly.

Storm tracks are habitual, arriving in familiar sequences. The basin’s canals and pumps have been tuned over generations, so flooding is rare and, when it happens, it happens in the same districts that have always taken the water first. Buildings lean but do not fall; corrosion is constant but anticipated. Repairs are a permanent layer of the architecture, as normal as doors.

The fauna migrates in long cycles tied to heat output and moisture patterns rather than seasons of growth. Certain neighborhoods are loud with breeding for a few weeks, then quiet for months. The biggest animals move like maintenance schedules: a slow procession from one warm district to another as temperatures shift across the city’s industrial grid.

Even the land’s erosion is steady-state. The plateau has already been carved into its preferred shapes; now it only refines them—rounding a corner, deepening a channel by a finger-width each year, polishing stone under endless wet contact.

### Rules of the world (as the place teaches them)
If you stay long enough, the landscape makes its laws clear:

1. **Water will be present.** If you remove it from a street, it will appear on the underside of a beam. If you seal a roof, it will condense inside the wall.
2. **Nothing green will come to claim the edges.** Every crack stays a crack until stone collapses or a builder fills it. Softness must be manufactured.
3. **Heat is currency.** Warmth concentrates life: animals gather around vents; microbes bloom in outflows; people build where the air is easiest to work in.
4. **Sound is a survival sense.** Quiet means either failure (a machine stopped, a pump dead) or danger (a predator nearby, a corridor claimed).
5. **The city and the fauna are not separate.** They are one system: industry feeds chemistry; chemistry feeds films; films feed grazers; grazers feed predators; predators and scavengers keep channels clear of carcass-clogs; and everything, in the end, returns to water.

Stand on one of the higher viaducts when the fog thins and you can finally see the basin’s scale: districts stacked like strata, canals catching light in long gray ribbons, the far rim of stone holding the weather in like a bowl. The whole place is damp, loud, and lived-in—an elevated world that has learned to run without leaves, and has had a long time to become very good at it.
