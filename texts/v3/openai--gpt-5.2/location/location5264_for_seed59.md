At first it reads as forest, because everything you can see is green and everything green is busy—leaf moving against leaf, frond sifting against frond, vines drawing tight and slack with the weight of water. But then the forest resolves into streets.

Not streets of stone or fired brick: lanes of packed fiber and root-mat, springy underfoot, stitched together with cordage that has the pale sheen of dried sap. The ground here is not really “ground” so much as a thick, living floor—an engineered braid of plants trained to hold, to drain, to give a little when the hill shudders with runoff. It smells like crushed fern, wet bark, and the metallic clean of rain.

The place sits high. The air is cool enough that breath turns visible when you stop moving, cool enough that the water doesn’t evaporate so much as *hang*—beads on every edge, droplets in midair, a constant soft perspiration from rock faces and leaf undersides. Clouds snag on the canopy and then dissolve into it; the whole town lives in the kind of light you get after rain even when it hasn’t rained for hours: ordinary daylight, but filtered through water and green, made gentle and persistent.

### Terrain and water: a city on a saturated slope
The settlement occupies a slope where the mountain has been worn into steadier shapes—rounded shoulders of rock and long, shallow steps that look natural until you notice their repetition. Erosion has done its slow work here for a very long time, leaving gullies that know exactly where they want to be and boulders that have stopped pretending they’ll roll again. The town has accepted those decisions and built into them.

Water is everywhere, but rarely as a wide river. It lives near the surface, in thin sheets and hidden channels.

- Along the uphill edge, seep-springs bead out of seams in the stone. The rocks *weep*—not dramatically, just constantly—feeding moss curtains and threadlike rivulets that vanish into the root-mat before you can follow them far.
- In the low places between terraces, shallow pools form, dark with tannins, rimmed with plants that drink with greedy delicacy. Some pools are permanent—marsh-eyes held open by the traffic of feet and the grazing of animals. Others appear overnight after a squall and are gone by midday, leaving only a ring of slick mud and the sharp scent of disturbed soil.
- Drainage is not a ditch so much as a living decision: certain plants are trained to open their roots into porous channels, others are trained to close and hold water like sponges. The town “plumbs” itself the way an orchard is pruned.

When rain arrives—and it arrives often—it does not fall into silence. It hits a thousand surfaces at once: leaf, reed, woven roof, stretched hide, running water, insect wing. The sound swells until it’s hard to speak. Then, when the rain eases, everything continues dripping as if unwilling to stop. The post-rain hours are their own weather.

### Vegetation as substrate, wall, and law
There’s almost no inert material exposed. Stone exists, yes, but it’s furred with life. Wood exists, but mostly as living trunks still rooted in place, bent into useful arcs.

The architecture is horticulture, and it shows its age—established, practiced, refined by generations.

- Houses are grown from clusters of thick-stemmed plants whose inner fibers are tough and water-resistant. People guide them while they’re young with woven braces and carved forms. When the stems harden, they hold the shape: rounded rooms, narrow windows, high lips to keep out wind-driven rain.
- Roofs are layered leaves, not thatched so much as *overlapped*—big, waxy blades that shed water fast. Between layers are mats of dry moss that swell when wet, sealing gaps.
- Bridges are braided from living vine-cables anchored to trunks. Under load they flex and hum, and you feel the vibration in your teeth when a herd crosses ahead of you.
- The “streets” are mostly open corridors under arching fronds, but the busiest ways are roofed with trained creepers that make a green tunnel. In the wet season those tunnels sweat steadily. People walk with hoods of leaf-fiber and collars of oiled cloth, always damp around the edges.

Plants are not decorative here; they are the literal foundation. The town’s borders are made of planted density rather than walls: thorny hedges, tall reeds, and barricades of interwoven branches that can be opened like gates if you know which bindings to loosen. The sense of boundary is strong even though nothing is straight.

And the plants are not passive. Not in any magical sense—just in the ordinary sense of a mature ecosystem that has been coaxed into doing work. You learn quickly where you can cut and where you can’t.

There are rules everyone seems to know:

1. **Never ring-bark a living support.** The walkway above your head may be depending on that trunk.
2. **Only take cuttings from marked donors.** Unmarked plants might be load-bearing, water-bearing, or someone’s livelihood.
3. **Keep the water-ways unblocked.** Anything that clogs a root-channel can flood a whole terrace by morning.

### The built world: primitive tools, urban density
For all the green, it is unmistakably urban—tight, layered, engineered. The density isn’t just physical; it’s procedural. Everything is used by someone, maintained by someone, argued over by someone.

Tools are simple but plentiful: stone blades with carefully ground edges, bone awls, wooden wedges, mallets wrapped in hide. You hear the steady *thok-thok* of splitting poles and the soft rasp of fiber being combed. There is little metal—perhaps a few traded iron nails or a precious knife passed from hand to hand—but most joinery is lash and peg, most fastenings are knots, and most durable surfaces are achieved by layering: resin, wax, tight weave, repeated oiling.

Infrastructure is everywhere in plant form:

- **Water-ladders:** stepped channels lined with smooth leaves that guide runoff down without tearing the soil free.
- **Silt traps:** shallow basins where the water slows, drops its load, and then filters through root curtains. People periodically shovel out the rich mud and carry it uphill in baskets.
- **Vent stacks:** hollow reeds or thick-stem plants deliberately kept open through rooms to move air and smoke upward—because cooking fires are small and frequent, and the air is already heavy with damp.

The town is compact—hamlet-sized, perhaps a few hundred people at most—but it is vertically complicated. Walkways overlap; platforms hang; storage lofts perch above water channels; small courtyards open suddenly like rooms cut into the greenery. You can stand in one spot and see five levels of life: feet passing above, hands working at waist height, water slipping beneath, birds hopping through the mid-canopy, and insects streaming in the humid air.

### Fauna: overwhelming, audible, unavoidable
The loudness of the place is not just weather and people. It’s animals. The ecosystem is crammed with bodies.

Insects are constant—winged, crawling, clicking. The air vibrates with them in warm hours even though the temperature stays on the cool side; in the morning they cluster under leaves, and by midday they lift in glittering clouds whenever someone disturbs the brush. Their chorus is a living electric line running through everything.

Birds make it worse, in the best way: sharp calls, liquid trills, harsh scolding rattles from unseen throats. Some birds nest in the woven eaves of houses and are tolerated because they eat biting insects. Others are chased off with thrown pods and shouted curses because they steal drying food from racks.

Mammals—small, quick, everywhere—move like a second economy. You glimpse them in root hollows and along railing vines: sleek, wet-furred things that look like rodents until they open their mouths and you see the odd, comb-like teeth for scraping moss and lichen. They have learned the town’s rhythms. They come when the market scraps are thrown out; they vanish when the dogs are loosed.

And there are larger presences too, because the town sits on a mountain corridor where animals pass between valleys:

- Herd beasts—goatlike, sure-footed—kept in pens of living thorn. Their hooves drum on the root-mats at dawn.
- Long-legged marsh birds that stalk the shallow pools, stabbing at larvae with patient violence.
- Occasionally, something heavy and unseen moving through the dense outer vegetation, making the whole hedge shiver as if a wind had passed through it. People pause then, listening, hands automatically checking the ties on gates.

None of this is “weird.” It is just what happens when food is abundant and water is reliable and shelter is thick. The town is not an intrusion into nature; it is one expression of it, and everything else has moved in.

### Atmosphere: cool humidity, normal light, perpetual motion
Despite the high elevation, the damp keeps the cold from becoming sharp. The chill is in your fingers when you handle wet cordage; it’s in the way cloth never fully dries; it’s in the ache of joints after a long day. But the air is rarely biting. It wraps you.

Light is normal daylight, but it arrives in layers:

1. A soft overhead brightness filtered by cloud.
2. A green middle light refracted through leaves and wet surfaces.
3. A low, reflected shimmer off water films and slick stone.

Shadows are present but never crisp. Everything is slightly blurred by moisture, as if the world refuses hard edges.

And always the place is moving. The turbulence is not a single storm, though storms come; it’s the constant churn of many systems overlapping.

- **Water surges** down channels when a cloud breaks, then slows to trickles that still insist on going somewhere.
- **Vegetation shifts** under weight: walkways flex, vines tighten, leaves sag with droplets and then spring back when they shed them.
- **People flow** through narrow passages, stopping suddenly to barter, to repair a tie, to lift a fallen basket from the water before it soaks through.
- **Animals intersect** it all—flocks exploding up from a courtyard, insects rising in a glittering panic, a tethered herd pressing against its living pen as if it remembers an older migration.

Even the smells are turbulent: sweet sap, sharp crushed leaf, smoke from cooking fires trapped under wet canopies, the animal musk of pens, the clean mineral smell where water has just found a new seam in the rock.

### Ecology and economy: what thrives here
Food grows almost without persuasion. The challenge is not coaxing life out of the ground; it is keeping it from taking everything.

Terraces hold beds of edible ferns, tubers that like wet soil, and climbing vines trained up frames of living wood. There are fruiting shrubs along the sunnier edges and fungi cultivated in shaded, humid alcoves where the air is cool enough to keep them from spoiling quickly. Drying racks are everywhere, hung where the wind can reach, often above the warm breath of kitchens to cheat the damp.

Fiber is as important as food. Whole sections of the town are devoted to plants grown for rope, matting, and cloth—stiff reeds for baskets, long bast fibers for cord. People sit in groups under roofed corridors, twisting and braiding, their hands moving with the speed of habit while their voices add to the general roar.

Waste is never just waste; it’s feedstock. Scraps go to animals, manure goes to silt traps, silt goes uphill, ash goes into soil. The town feels old in the way of a well-run garden: nothing dramatic, nothing sleek, just systems tuned to themselves.

### Boundaries and hazards
The settlement’s smallness makes its boundaries legible. You can walk from the busiest center—where platforms overlap like stacked shelves of commerce—to the outer edge in minutes. The edge is a thickened ring of vegetation and wet ground where the built structures become sparse and then stop.

Beyond, the mountain forest-marsh resumes its own priorities. The ground gets less stable. The water is less guided. The sounds change: fewer human voices, more uninterrupted animal chorus, more wind worrying at leaves.

Hazards are practical:

- Slipping from a wet walkway into a shallow channel and losing whatever you carried.
- Sudden runoff that turns a tame trickle into a calf-deep rush.
- Mold and rot if you misjudge what can be stored where.
- Bites and stings from the constant small fauna.
- The occasional larger animal pushing into the outer gardens when the weather shifts.

People respond with habit rather than fear. They tie everything down. They build redundancy into their paths. They keep spare cordage coiled on pegs. They talk loudly because the world is loud, because silence would be impractical.

### The feeling of standing in it
If you stop in one of the little courtyards—an open pocket where a pool reflects a patch of sky—you can feel how the place holds its contradictions.

High and wet. Cool and lush. Crowded with life yet orderly enough to be lived in. Primitive tools applied with sophisticated attention. An urban density made not of stone, but of trained, living matter.

A drop falls from a leaf onto the surface of the pool and the ripples carry reflected canopy light into motion. Above you, a bridge-vine thrums as a group passes. Somewhere close, a bird calls in a pattern that sounds like an argument. The water in the root-channel beneath your feet changes tone as it finds a faster path.

Nothing here is static, but nothing is new in a way that feels unstable. It’s turbulent like a market and a storm at once—an ongoing, practiced commotion—an established world that has learned how to stay saturated without dissolving.
