At first it reads as a mountain range—black ridges, buttresses, talus fans—until your eye begins to snag on repetition.

Lines are too straight. Switchbacks are too regular. Terraces step up the faces of cliffs with the patience of surveying. The high country is carved into districts: long shelves cut into stone, stacked like seating in a stadium that could hold a continent. What should be alpine wilderness is an urban plan without inhabitants, a city made from elevation itself.

The air is cool enough to sting your throat, thin enough that every breath feels like it has corners. Nothing here is softened by water. There are no streams, no varnish, no rounded pebbles singing in a current. Instead the land is all fracture and powder: shattered slate, pale salt-crust, wind-ground grit that behaves like flour until you step, and then it behaves like glass. Surfaces keep their edges. A cliff breaks; it does not slump. A wall chips; it does not swell.

Light arrives reluctantly. The sky is not dark so much as unwilling—dim like late afternoon that never decides to become evening. Sometimes it is the color of old paper; sometimes it is bruise-gray. The sun, when you can locate it, is a diffuse coin behind high haze. Shadows are present but indecisive, smudged along the ground as if the world can’t commit to where “down” is.

That uncertainty isn’t just weather.

### Terrain and “streets”
Everything is high. Not simply elevated—*extreme* in the way of places that make maps feel like lies. There are knife ridges where both sides drop away into distances that don’t resolve into bottoms. There are saddles wide as neighborhoods. There are chimneys of rock rising like towers, and between them—this is where the “urban” reveals itself—corridors.

They resemble streets.

Some are cut channels, smooth-sided, with steps carved directly into the bedrock: broad treads worn concave by something that climbed for centuries. Others are natural joints widened by frost-splitting and wind abrasion, but their intersections form grids that should not occur in geology. Avenues run along contour lines, then—without reason—tilt and continue as if the mountain rotated beneath them.

In places the “city” is vertical: façades with repeating niches; arch-like voids whose curves are too clean to be collapse. Doorways exist, but they don’t lead inside anything. They lead into the rock, and then—after a few meters—the passage becomes a seam of crystal or a plug of fused basalt, as though the mountain healed over whatever the corridor once accessed.

The materials are all mineral, all dry:

- **Basalt and obsidian** in dark flows and glassy plates, fractured into sharp tessellations.
- **Salt and pale evaporite minerals** in crusts that crack into polygonal tiles, lifting at the edges like old paint.
- **Metallic veins**—hematite, pyrite, things that flash dull gold in the dim—running through walls like wiring, though no current follows them in any ordinary way.
- **Dust** everywhere, not the soft dust of soil but the fined-down remainder of stone.

There is erosion, but it is the kind that takes its time and never uses water. Wind has sandblasted corners into bevels; temperature cycles have pried blocks apart. The whole place looks *settled*, long-in-balance: not freshly ruined, not actively collapsing, but continuously, patiently re-editing itself.

### Atmosphere and sound
It is quiet enough that you start to hear your own body as part of the landscape—your joints, your fabric, the small rasp of breath. Wind exists, but it does not roar. It slides along channels and makes only thin noises: a whisper through cracks, a faint reed-note where air is forced past a slit, a distant hiss of grit moving across stone.

Sometimes you hear a sound that is not wind at all: a single *tick* like cooling metal, or a soft *clink* as if two stones touched far away. You look for the source and find nothing moving.

The silence has a weight. In empty spaces between terraces—plazas the size of lakes, except there is no water—sound feels reluctant to travel. It dies quickly, not from muffling, but as if the air forgets to carry it.

### Ecology (rare, wrong, persistent)
There are no plants. No needles clinging to rock. No moss. No lichen in the ordinary sense. The surfaces are too dry, the chemistry too unforgiving, the light too meager.

And yet, in sheltered seams and on the undersides of overhangs, you can find *films*—not green, not brown, but faintly iridescent, like oil on stone. They are thin enough to be missed until the light catches them. They do not drink; they do not transpire. They seem to feed on gradients: on the difference between warm stone and cold air, on static charge, on the slow seep of minerals through microcracks. If you scrape a bit away, it doesn’t smell alive. It smells like crushed rock and a hint of something sharp, like ozone.

Fauna is rare, but not absent. You might see:

- **Small, pale arthropod shapes** that move only when your shadow passes, retreating into fissures with quick, quiet precision.
- **Long-bodied gliders**—more like folded membranes than birds—launching from ledges to cross impossible distances without flapping, riding thin currents you can’t feel.
- **Cliff-dwellers** that you notice only by the lines they leave: faint trails on dusted steps, or tiny piles of sorted grains outside a crack, as if someone has been mining the wind.

Nothing here calls. Nothing sings. Life is tuned to not announcing itself in a place this open and this empty.

### The city that has no technology
Despite the “urban” scale, there are no devices. No lamps. No wires. No engines. The infrastructure is all *form*: carving, stacking, aligning.

Some terraces are held by retaining walls made of blocks so large they feel like bedrock that decided to be rectangular. Mortar is absent; the fit is the thing. Steps, ramps, courtyards, and vertical shafts repeat across kilometers, suggesting a population that once moved primarily on foot, with time enough to make mountains into architecture.

In the broadest basin—a natural amphitheater half encircled by cliffs—there is something like a central district: concentric platforms descending toward a flat, circular floor. The floor is not paved; it is a single continuous sheet of stone, polished by wind to a soft sheen. The ring-walls around it are ribbed, each rib a buttress that could be climbed like a ladder if you trusted your grip and your courage.

Nothing occupies the center. That absence feels intentional, like a rule.

### Active processes (without water)
Even in stillness the place is busy.

- **Frost without melt:** At night (if “night” applies), a brittle rime forms out of the thin air—carbon dioxide or stranger volatiles—whitening ledges and stair edges. By “morning” it sublimates, leaving a faint pitted texture, like the stone has been gently bitten.
- **Dust weather:** Fine mineral powder behaves like a slow fluid. It gathers in corners, pours down steps when disturbed, avalanches in silent sheets off terraces when a gust finally finds an angle.
- **Electrostatic seasons:** In certain intervals the air prickles. Hair rises. Dust clings to vertical surfaces. You can hear a faint, near-imagined crackle if you press your ear to a wall veined with metal.
- **Rock creep:** Over decades, blocks shift imperceptibly. You can see the evidence: seams that have opened like lips, stair flights that no longer meet their landings cleanly, a straight corridor that now has a subtle bend.

The city is not crumbling quickly. It is being *rebalanced* continuously, the way dunes migrate without losing their identity as dunes.

### Weirdness: how reality breaks here
The first oddity is distance. The scale is open—vistas that should give you bearings—but the longer you look, the less reliable your sense of how far things are. A terrace across a chasm appears close enough to shout to, yet when you walk toward it for an hour it does not grow larger; it merely changes angle, like a drawing being rotated.

Then come the local failures of rule.

In some corridors, **gravity has preferences**. A stairway may feel normal going up, but on the way down your body grows light, your footfalls too springy, as if the world is reluctant to let you return. In certain plazas, dust falls upward in slow, lazy arcs and then settles in the corners anyway, as though “down” is negotiated case by case.

Sound behaves strangely too. Speak in one archway and your words arrive behind you, delayed. Strike two stones together in a narrow street and the click is swallowed, but somewhere higher—three terraces up, out of sight—you hear it repeated, softer, like an echo that chose the wrong origin.

The strangest breakdown is architectural: **perspective does not always agree with itself**. A row of niches can be counted from one end, and then from the other, and you will get different numbers without any niche being hidden. You can watch your own shadow cross a wall and see it detach slightly, lagging by a fraction of a second, then rejoin as if embarrassed.

These aren’t hallucinations in the common sense. They are properties of place, as consistent as the cool air, as repeatable as the frost.

Local travelers—if any exist—would have rules:

1. Don’t camp in a perfect circle of stones; circles here have opinions.
2. Don’t follow a straight street for too long; straightness is an invitation to loop.
3. If the wind stops completely, move to higher ground; stillness is when the city “adjusts.”

### What it feels like to be there
The emptiness is not loneliness so much as *space asserting itself*. There is so much air, so much drop, so much unoccupied architecture, that you become keenly aware of your own volume—a warm, wet thing in a world that does not admit wetness.

Your footprints last. Not because mud preserves them, but because nothing comes to erase them quickly. Later, dust will drift across and soften their edges, but the impression remains in the mineral flour like a fossil of a moment. You realize that most marks here are long-lived. The city is a ledger of contact: scraped corners, polished handholds, worn steps, a million minor proofs that something once moved through regularly—and then stopped.

And yet the place is not dead.

It is active in its own dry way, continually rewriting itself with wind and cold and electrostatic weather, continually flexing the rules of distance and direction. The mountains keep standing. The terraces keep stepping. The corridors keep offering you choices that don’t behave like choices anywhere else.

In the dim light, from a far ridge, the whole region can look like a skyline: spires, blocks, stacked levels—an urban silhouette drawn in stone.

But if you watch long enough, you’ll see a street subtly change its angle without any rock falling. You’ll see a shadow choose the wrong wall. You’ll feel your balance shift, as if the city took a breath.

And then the wind returns—thin, quiet—and the vast, high architecture continues pretending it is only geology.
