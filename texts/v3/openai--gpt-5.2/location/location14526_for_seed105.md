The first thing you learn here is that *high* doesn’t mean dry.

You are in a drowned upland: a plateau that used to be a roof of rock above the cloudline, now holding an ocean in its lap. The water is not a simple flood; it has weight and patience, as if it has always been meant to sit here. It fills basins, runs in broad channels across old ridges, and then—where the plateau breaks—spills over the edge in silent, continuous cataracts that drop into a deeper darkness below. From a distance the whole region reads like a set of lakes welded together, but when you move through it you realize it’s one body, threaded by long, cold currents.

Because it is high, the water is thin in a way your body can feel: less pressure than a true deep sea, yet the cold is sharper for it, a cold that slides into stone and stays there. The surface—when there is a surface—is a lid of slate: wind-sheared, flecked with frazil ice, and often covered by a skin of fog so thick it might as well be another layer of water. Sometimes there is no clear boundary at all. Rain doesn’t fall so much as *arrive as architecture*: a vertical wall you can swim into, where the ocean becomes a downpour and the downpour becomes ocean again. It is one of the local rules—one of the ways this place is strange—that water changes its direction of belonging without changing substance. Rivers don’t always run downhill; occasionally they decide to be weather.

Light is scarce. Even at “day,” the sky above the water is a dim bruise, and below it everything is filtered through green-black. At “night” the region becomes almost absolute. There are no fish eyes, no plankton sparks, no quick little movements to reassure you. The darkness is not full of watching—there is nothing here to watch—only full of depth.

And yet it is loud.

Sound behaves as if it has been given permission. It carries too well. The plateau’s bones are old and weathered, the erosion long-settled into stable forms—wide scoured bowls, smooth saddles between ridges, cliff faces rounded into immense steps. These shapes make amphitheaters for noise. You hear the grind of ice forming and breaking; you hear long, low booming as sheets of surface ice flex like drumheads. You hear currents rubbing sand and silt along the rock in steady, abrasive hiss. When the ocean spills over the plateau’s edge, it does not splash—there is no air to shatter into spray where it falls—but it roars anyway, a constant throat-sound transmitted through water and stone.

The terrain is a mix of basalt and pale granite, the kind of rock that keeps its structure even after centuries of abrasion. Here and there the plateau is capped with a softer sedimentary layer that has been carved into terraces. Those terraces are where the vegetation takes hold, and it is—by any normal measure—lush.

Not lush like a jungle. Lush like a library of fronds.

Forests of kelp-like pillars rise from anchor stones and old scree. Their “trunks” are rubbery cords, thick as a wrist or thicker, braided with fibrous strands. They sway slowly with the currents, each movement delayed and solemn, as if the plants are thinking before they bend. Broad blades ripple from them, and on those blades grow smaller things: mats of mossy filaments, ruffles that look like lettuce, delicate fans with edges so thin they tremble at any passing turbulence.

There are no animals to prune them, graze them, hide among them, or die in them. That absence changes the whole grammar of growth. Nothing here is shaped by teeth or hunger. The plants grow into forms that would be impossible in a browsed world: extravagant overhangs, curtains that hang down into channels, domes that almost close off gullies like vaulted ceilings. In the quiet spaces between currents, the vegetation can become so dense it turns the water inside it still, like a pocket of held breath.

The ecosystem’s motion comes from other engines. From weather. From chemistry. From the restless reshuffling of water between “sea” and “sky.” Periodically, cold fronts drive across the plateau and the ocean responds as if it is part of the atmosphere: the upper meters churn, fog thickens into actual liquid sheets, and rain-walls march over the region like moving cliffs. These events are violent in sound and gentle in touch, a contradiction you learn to accept. The water never strikes you like rain in air; it only changes its insistence, its direction, its density.

Down in the deeper basins there are vents—not hot, not the black smokers of deep oceans, but mineral mouths that exhale warmth just shy of comfort. Around them the stone is stained with rust-red and milk-white deposits. The plants here are different: shorter, tougher, encrusted with mineral scales. Some exude bubbles that don’t rise. The bubbles collect in wobbling beads along leaf edges and then drift sideways, as if buoyancy is a local opinion rather than a law. That is another rule: in certain currents, “up” is borrowed from whatever the water is doing. If you release something, it may not ascend; it may slide toward the nearest waterfall, or hover, or begin a slow pilgrimage along an invisible stream.

The openness of the region is not a wide plain you can walk; it is a wide *reach* you can traverse. Long corridors between ridges run for miles, floored with pale sand that lies in ripples and dunes as if shaped by wind. But there is no wind here, not in the way you expect. The dunes are made by currents that have been steady for so long they’ve become like seasons. The erosion is “done” in the sense that the plateau has found its stable shapes; it is still active in the sense that everything is always being re-sanded, re-polished, re-layered.

Structures appear sparsely, scattered like thoughts someone had and then abandoned.

On a ridge that would once have been a pass, you find a line of stone uprights—cairns, but taller, set in pairs. Between some pairs hang cords made from twisted plant fiber, weighted with smooth stones. They sway and tap together in current, producing a dull clacking that joins the larger roar. In a sheltered bowl there is a low hut-frame lashed from driftwood and kelp-stalk, roofed with overlapping blades that have dried into leathery shingles. It’s not watertight—nothing here is meant to keep water out—but it is *current-tight*, designed to create a pocket of calmer flow. Inside, there are tools: stone scrapers, bone-like wedges carved not from animal but from hardened plant-stem, and a simple hammering stone worn glossy at its grip points.

There are no people, and no animals, but the built things insist: something once worked here with hands.

In a few places the plateau’s edge has been marked. Not with walls—nothing is strong enough to argue with a waterfall—but with hanging nets of braided fiber, strung between anchor stones. These nets do not catch fish. They catch *debris*: drifting fronds, mineral nodules, ice pellets. They also catch sound. When the great spillways increase, the nets hum like massive instruments, turning the region’s constant motion into chord and vibration. It is primitive engineering in a world where the main resource is not meat or fire but *flow*.

The absence of fauna makes the loudness feel even stranger. There are no calls, no clicks, no feeding noises. The only rhythms are geophysical and botanical: the slow creak of kelp-forests flexing, the crack of ice, the long shudder of falling water, the scrape of stones as a current rearranges a gravel bar. Sometimes, when a rain-wall passes overhead, you hear a higher roar—a sheet-noise—like a thousand hands rubbing fabric. It is possible to stand in a calm pocket and listen to that wall approach across miles, a moving horizon of sound arriving before the water-change reaches you.

Ecologically, the plants have taken the roles animals usually play. They are not passive. Some species “walk” in their own way: they loosen their anchors during high-flow events and let themselves tumble to new ground, re-rooting when the current slackens. Others farm minerals by exuding mild acids, etching rock and collecting the resulting salts in their tissues. In the dimness these plants are almost black-green, but where minerals coat them they gleam faintly, as if dusted with stars that refuse to emit light.

Darkness does not mean blindness, not entirely. In the deepest basins there are occasional pulses—brief, cold glows not from living bodies but from chemistry. A vent exhales, a mineral film reacts, and for a few seconds a stretch of water holds a pallid luminosity, enough to reveal the size of a frond-forest, the curve of a cliff, the slow snowfall of silt. Then it’s gone, and the world returns to its main colors: slate, ink, moss, rust.

The place feels established—balanced—not because it is calm, but because its violence is habitual. The waterfalls have been falling for so long the rock beneath them has grown smooth. The currents have been combing the plateau for so long they’ve made patterns that repeat. The vegetation has learned how to be lush without the quickening pressure of predation, and so it is lavish in shape and stubborn in persistence.

If there is a single overarching rule the region teaches, it is this: here, life is not a chase. It is an arrangement.

A cold, roaring arrangement—high above whatever “sea level” used to mean—where an ocean lives on a mountain’s back, where rain behaves like terrain, where the forests are fronds and fibers, and where the only mouths are the mouths of stone.
