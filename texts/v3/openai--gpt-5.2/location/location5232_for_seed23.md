Up here the cold doesn’t feel like a single thing. It is a clean, bright pressure that holds the air still—yet everything is wet.

The place is a high bowl in the mountains, no wider than a long walk from rim to rim, a contained landscape with edges you can see: a cirque of pale stone and old ice, steep walls rubbed smooth by vanished glaciers. The rock has been eroded into familiar, settled forms—rounded shoulders, fluted gullies, stair-step shelves—like it has had millennia to decide where water should go. It isn’t dramatic in a fresh way; it’s dramatic in a practiced way. The land looks like it knows the weight of every season.

And the land is leaking.

Water sits close to the surface here. It doesn’t roar, it doesn’t race; it *appears*. Along the bowl’s inner slopes the stone “weeps” continuously—thin beads forming on mineral faces, joining into threads, then slipping into grooves that were worn long before any hand arrived. In places the rock is honeycombed, and each hole is a mouth that gathers condensation: the air, bright with sun, turns its own moisture into droplets as if it can’t hold itself together at this altitude.

Despite the brightness, snow lingers in shaded pockets and in cracks that never see the direct sun. The light is hard and clean, high-mountain light that shows everything—every wet sheen on stone, every hair on an animal, every frost-burned edge. Even the fog, when it comes, is luminous: a milky glare that makes silhouettes too crisp.

### Terrain and materials
The ground is a patchwork of:

- **Pale limestone and slate**, polished by old ice and scabbed with mineral crust where water has been seeping for ages. The stone breaks in plates and shallow bowls, perfect for catching runoff.
- **Black bands of wet grit**, where the water brings up fine sediment and lays it down in dark aprons. Step there and your boot sinks a thumb’s depth; lift it and it comes away heavy.
- **Rime and hoarfrost**, not as a blanket but as a skin—feathering the windward edges of posts and ridges, tracing every strand of rope and every wire of grass with glassy needles.

There are no deep forests to soften the shapes. Vegetation is present but reluctant: small, low, and widely spaced, like life has learned not to promise too much.

A few **alpine shrubs** cling in pockets where warmth collects, their branches red-brown and sparse. **Mosses and tight lichens** do most of the greening, thin as paint on rock. In the wettest places, **sedge tufts** rise like rough brushes, but they’re scattered—each one a little island.

### Atmosphere: bright, wet, cold
The air is cold enough that wetness feels sharper, almost metallic. Sometimes you can see moisture forming right in front of you, a sudden mist around a warm animal flank or a cooking fire, and then it’s gone—pulled into the broader, steady condensation that the bowl seems to breathe.

Wind funnels in over the rim, but it doesn’t arrive as a constant rush. It comes in **shears and eddies**, invisible turbulence you feel as shifting pressure against your face. The sun keeps the whole basin bright even when clouds pass overhead; the light reflects off wet stone and shallow water until the shadows look bleached.

### Quiet, but not calm
It’s a quiet place in a way that feels like a rule rather than an accident.

Even when water is moving, it doesn’t sound like water usually does. Rills slide over stone with only a faint, steady *tick* where drops fall into hollows. The wind, despite its frequent change, makes little noise; it threads through the structures and over the ice without producing a howl.

The loudest regular sounds are close-up:

- the soft **clack** of hooves on stone,
- the **scratch** of rope against a wet post,
- the **papery flutter** of wings,
- and, everywhere, the smallest sound of all: **dripping**, as if the mountain is counting.

People here speak with economy—not because they are solemn, but because the air eats speech. Words carry a few paces and then vanish, absorbed by the wet, thin atmosphere. The settlement has grown around that. Communication is done by **gesture, proximity, and sight**, and the place has been built for sight.

### The dense little city in the bowl
For a landscape that is only hamlet-sized, it is crowded with presences.

The settlement is packed onto the terraces where the cirque wall steps down—dense layers of construction pressed against stone, leaving little empty space between. Buildings are not tall; they are **stacked**, one roof serving as another’s walkway. Materials are local and hand-worked: slab-stone, bone pins, woven reed mats, stitched hides. Metal exists but is rare and reused; the technology is mostly leverage, rope, wood, stone, and patient hands.

Everything is arranged around water.

- **Catch-steps**: shallow basins cut into rock to slow seepage and collect it.
- **Channels** lined with flat stones, leading water from weeping walls into cistern bowls.
- **Raised walkways** of lashed poles and stone slabs that keep feet above the wet grit.
- **Hanging racks** under eaves where nets, hides, and tools dry in the bright cold.

The city feels urban not because it is large, but because it is **organized**: routes, terraces, pens, workspaces, storage niches, a logic of flow. The place is a machine built to live with constant damp.

And it is dense with life. Animals move through the streets the way wind moves through grass elsewhere.

### Fauna: abundance adapted to cold and wet
The bowl is an ecological trap and an ecological feast. Water is available year-round, and even sparse vegetation can support a surprising web when the base of that web is not plants alone.

There are:

- **Shaggy, compact grazers**—goat-like but heavier in the shoulder, with broad hooves that handle wet stone. They are kept in tight pens and moved across terraces like living brooms, cropping sedge tufts and lichen mats.
- **Long-necked marsh-birds**, pale and bright-eyed, nesting on ledges where the stone weeps. They feed on insects that breed in every shallow pool.
- **Cold-water amphibians** in the seep basins—dark, slow creatures with thick skins. Children catch them by hand, not as sport but as routine.
- **Insects in improbable numbers** for such a cold place, because the water never fully stops. They emerge in brief, synchronized bursts when the sun hits certain shelves; then they vanish into cracks and under stones when the shadow returns.

Predators exist too, quiet and close: small, sleek hunters that move in the narrow spaces between walls and pens, living off scraps, eggs, and the careless.

At times, the basin’s brightness fills with **wings**—not a loud migration, but a sudden density of motion: birds lifting all at once, circling in the glare, then settling again as if a single thought passed through them.

### The turbulence: change without noise
This is the strangest contradiction: the place is in constant, turbulent flux, yet it rarely becomes loud.

The turbulence isn’t mainly in wind or storms—though storms do come fast over the rim, and when they do, snow and rain can trade places within minutes. The deeper turbulence is in the *groundwater and the ice*.

Water here is restless under the rock. It undermines, redirects, resurfaces elsewhere. A channel that has flowed gently for years will, in a day, go dry—while a new seep begins weeping from a wall that was previously bare. Sometimes a terrace will soften overnight, turning from firm grit to sucking mud. Sometimes the ice in a shaded cleft will shift with a slow, silent shove and leave a new crack wide enough to swallow a hand.

The settlement lives with these changes as if they are weather. There are practices and taboos built around them:

- **No building directly on the wet-black bands**, no matter how convenient. Those are “moving ground,” and the ground will reclaim what you place there.
- **Structures must be tied in pairs**—each post lashed to another, each roofline anchored to a neighbor—because the earth occasionally rearranges its support without warning.
- **Water must be offered a path**. If you block a seep, the water does not stop; it simply finds a different place to appear, and the new place is usually worse for you.

The weirdness of the world shows in small, consistent ways. Droplets sometimes form **against gravity** on the undersides of stone shelves, gathering like hanging pearls before falling—condensation patterns that don’t match the wind you feel on your skin. Frost grows in **directional fans** that point not toward the wind but toward the brightest patch of sky, as if the light itself has a current. And there are days when footsteps leave no sound at all—not muffled, not softened—simply absent, as though the air refuses to carry that particular vibration.

### How it feels to stand there
Stand on the highest terrace and look down into the bowl: bright stone slick with moisture, thin threads of water joining and parting like hair combed and recombed. Low roofs crowded together, smoke from cooking fires rising straight up until it hits a colder layer and spreads into a flat, shining haze. Animals pressed shoulder to shoulder in pens, their breath making brief clouds that flash in sunlight and then condense into beads on their own fur.

There is little green, yet the place is not barren. Life is everywhere, not as a carpet but as a crowd: bodies, nests, nets, hanging stores, drying racks, tracks crossing tracks in wet grit.

Nothing here is grand in size. The basin is contained, almost intimate. But it is saturated—by water, by presence, by the sense that the mountain is actively participating in the settlement’s daily decisions.

In the bright cold, with the air swallowing distant sound, the whole city can feel like a diorama someone has leaned too close to—every detail sharp, every surface wet, every movement quick and silent. The turbulence doesn’t announce itself. It simply continues, patient and busy, rearranging the rules a little at a time while everyone learns to read the weeping stone.
