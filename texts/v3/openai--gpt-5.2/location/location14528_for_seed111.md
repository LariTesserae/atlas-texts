Up here the mountain doesn’t broaden into a friendly summit. It narrows.

A seam has opened in it—young, sharp, impatient rock—like the planet flexed once and forgot to relax. From a distance it looks like a black cut laid across pale stone. Closer, it’s a place you could step into and be swallowed without ever feeling buried. The fracture runs only a little way, a hamlet-sized gash, but it is steep to the point of vertigo and crowded with things that live by piling their lives into a narrow volume: bodies on ledges, wings in the air, legs and antennae in every crack, breath and heat held in the same few meters of space.

The elevation is extreme; you can feel it in the thinness, in how quickly your lungs notice themselves. Wind arrives already cold, having scraped ice and scree, and it doesn’t roll across the ground so much as fall into the cleft like water into a drain. The temperature stays cool even when the sun is out, because the sun rarely *gets in*. Light is a scarce currency here—spent in slivers and notches—so most of the ravine is a long dusk. The walls lean close enough in places to turn noon into something like late evening. Where the crack tightens, the sky becomes a blade.

The stone is fresh and undecided in color: raw gray with bruised blue, places where minerals have been torn open and haven’t yet weathered into softer tones. Edges have the brittle clarity of something recently made. There are flakes on ledges that will one day be rounded pebbles but aren’t yet—right now they’re knives. The floor, where there is a floor, is a clutter of angular blocks that look tossed down by a giant hand. This is young erosion: not the patient sculpture of rivers over ages, but the early aftermath of uplift and fracture, the first drafts of a landscape.

Water is not abundant, but it is persistent in a way that feels almost clever. It appears by surprise. The cleft collects moisture like a lung. Cloud and fog snag on the high rock, condense, and become beads that swell on the underside of overhangs. Every so often the air saturates—an intermittent rain that doesn’t fall from a sky so much as *sweats* from the walls. Drops gather, hesitate, and let go with a slow, metronomic patience: plink… plink… plink. In the darker pockets, the sound of dripping is constant enough to feel like a creature breathing.

There are pockets of water where the rock cups it: shallow basins on ledges, slick films along seams, a hidden thread of flow that runs for a few meters and then vanishes into a crack. Nothing here makes a broad, shining river. Instead the wetness is distributed—small reservoirs, damp skins, intermittent seeps—so life has learned to live on *occasions*.

Vegetation is sparse, but it’s not absent; it just refuses to waste effort on grandeur. No trees reach up here. Instead: lichens that look like old paint, crusting the stone in pale maps; moss that grows in thin, dark pads where the dripping keeps a corner reliably damp; a few wiry plants that anchor in dust-filled fissures and hold their leaves close as if to keep them warm. Where a ledge gathers enough grit, there are tiny, stubborn clumps of alpine grass that don’t sway so much as shiver. The green is concentrated, not spread—little congregations wherever water and shelter overlap.

And because the plant life is meager, the animals have become the main architecture of the place.

It’s loud here. Not with one sound, but with layers: wind roaring through narrowings; the slap and scrape of wings against air; the chatter of hundreds of throats; the constant rain of tiny impacts—pebbles dislodged by claws, insect bodies striking stone, droppings hitting ledges and breaking apart. Every surface seems to have an acoustic opinion. The cleft turns calls into echoes that bounce up and down like trapped birds, so even a single cry becomes a small flock of sound.

The fauna is overwhelming in the way a crowded market is overwhelming: too many presences to count, too close together, each with its own trajectory. Along the walls there are nesting colonies—dark birds clinging to near-vertical faces, their bodies packed shoulder-to-shoulder on ledges no wider than a handspan. In deeper shadow, bat-like things hang in mats that ripple when disturbed, and when they launch they do it in sudden coordinated surges, a living smoke that fills the narrow airspace. The insects are not background; they are a weather. They rise from the damp pockets in shimmering clouds, gather under overhangs in vibrating curtains, pour into the beam of any rare sun-slit as if drawn by it like iron filings to a magnet.

On the ground, what little “ground” exists, there are quick mammals—thin-bodied, pale-eyed climbers—threading through the rockfall. They make paths the way water does: always choosing the smallest gap that still allows passage. They have the look of creatures that learned early that falling is final. Higher up, on impossible points and spikes, larger shapes rest—goatlike silhouettes with split hooves adapted to edges you can’t quite believe will hold them. When they move, it’s slow and exact, every placement deliberate, as if they’re obeying a rule that says haste is a form of death.

The strange part is not that so much life fits here; it’s *how it stacks*.

The cleft has its own vertical ecology, layered like shelves in a pantry. The lowest, dampest pockets are dominated by soft-bodied things and scavengers: pale amphibians that cling to wet stone, translucent worms in the moss, beetles that live in the film of water and never venture into the dry. Above that, in the band of intermittent moisture, are the nesters and the grazers of lichen and moss. Above that—near the knife of sky—are the flyers and the watchers, creatures that use the updrafts and the echoing air to navigate.

And in the deepest slots where light nearly fails, there are animals that seem built for darkness not as an absence but as a substance. They don’t simply *see less*; they behave as if darkness has texture. Some feel their way with long facial whiskers that brush the air; some click and chirr and listen for the returning shape of the world. A few carry faint, reluctant glows—not lantern-bright, nothing that defeats the general dimness, just small points like fungus in rotwood, enough to outline a mouth or a lure. Here, “light” is not a broad condition; it is a private tool.

Because there are no built things—no cairns, no carved steps, no shelters—everything that looks like structure is made by living habits and the mountain’s young violence. A ledge whitened and slick is not a platform laid down; it’s years of droppings compacted into a mineral crust. A smooth chute is not a trail cut by hand; it’s where countless claws have chosen the same line, and the stone has started to polish under repetition. In one place you might find a scatter of cracked shells or bones—evidence of primitive tool-use by beaks and paws, stones dropped to break, teeth used as levers—but nothing is arranged for long. The mountain does not keep artifacts. Anything left unattended slides, tumbles, gets buried under the next small collapse.

The world’s rules feel a little different inside the fracture, as if the cleft is a separate climate with its own agreements:

- **Moisture belongs to surfaces.** Water doesn’t announce itself as streams; it clings, beads, hides. Life here reads wetness by sheen and smell.
- **Sound travels like a thing with weight.** A call can return from above when it was made below. Echoes arrive late enough to be mistaken for other animals. Some creatures seem to exploit this, calling in patterns that confuse predators about distance and number.
- **Gravity is absolute, but space is narrow.** Everything evolves toward clinging, hooking, gripping, balancing. Even the flyers land as if they expect the world to betray them.
- **Time is slow in the stone.** The cleft changes, but not with daily drama. It changes with freeze-thaw, with minute shifts, with small rockfalls that happen after long preparation. A ledge will exist for a lifetime of birds, then disappear overnight with a soft roar and a rush of dust.

Dust is part of the atmosphere too. Not the dry, desert kind—this dust is dampened, clumping, smelling faintly metallic and mineral. When something falls, it doesn’t plume into a bright cloud; it billows as a darker, heavier breath that settles onto feathers and fur, and the animals shake it off with practiced impatience.

At times the cleft fills with fog so completely that distances vanish. The walls become felted with moisture; every hair on your skin knows it. Sounds get closer, more intimate. The calls of birds and the flutter of wings seem to happen just behind your head even when they’re far above. You can’t see the sky-knife then; you can only guess it exists because the wind has a direction and the dripping has a rhythm.

When the fog thins, you catch the place in pieces: a sudden silver line of seepage; a ledge crowded with bodies, all facing outward like a congregation; a wet patch of moss so dark it’s almost black; a set of fresh gouges where something slipped and caught itself at the last possible moment.

It’s not a wide world. You could traverse its length in an hour if you weren’t careful about dying. But within its boundaries it contains an oppressive abundance—life pressed into a narrow, shadowed cathedral of new stone—sustained by intermittent water, by cold air, by the simple fact that the mountain has offered shelter from the broader winds and a thousand places to cling.

If you stand still long enough, you begin to hear the slow dynamics beneath the loudness: the faint ticking of ice forming in microcracks; the creak of stressed rock; the far-away, almost subsonic settling of the mountain’s new fracture as it learns its own shape. The animals keep shouting and flapping and scuttling, but the place itself is patient.

It will take its time rounding these edges. It will take its time growing soil. It will take its time deciding whether this cleft becomes a canyon, collapses shut, or opens further into the mountain’s dark. For now it is a fresh wound that has become a habitat—damp in the corners, cold everywhere, loud with bodies, dim with shadow, and strange in the way any new landscape is strange: not yet smoothed into something familiar, still full of sharp choices.
