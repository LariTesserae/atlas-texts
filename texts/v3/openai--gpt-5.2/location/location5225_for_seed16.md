From above it’s nothing: a low, heat-shimmering plain of dark stone, as flat as a cooling pan. The horizon is close because the air is heavy with dust and haze; the sun is there, but it feels muffled, reduced to a bruise of brightness behind a permanent veil. You could walk for hours and never suspect you’re passing over a city.

The entrance is not a gate. It’s a mistake in the rock—an old fracture widened by centuries of expansion and shrink, a seam that breathes out warmth. The wind that comes from it is hot and tastes of minerals: sharp, metallic, faintly sour, like struck flint and burned matches. When it gusts, it carries a dry, papery rustle that is too rhythmic to be sand.

Down the seam, the world opens into a pocket.

Not a canyon—more like a hollowed geode the size of a neighborhood, a contained void tucked under the plain. The ceiling is high enough to swallow sound; the floor is low and bowl-shaped, an ancient basin. Everything is rounded, settled into itself by long erosion: edges softened, surfaces glazed by a thousand seasons of grit and heat, the rock polished where dust has been driven across it in the same direction for a very long time.

Dim light rules here. It doesn’t come cleanly from a single source. It sifts through cracks in the roof as thin, slanted blades; it pools in places where pale minerals on the walls return a little glow; it reddens near vents where heat leaks up through pores in the stone. The overall color is dusk—even at noon—because the air is full of fine particles that never quite settle.

And yet the place is *lush*.

Not with anything that would recognize water. There is no wetness, no dew, no puddle hidden in shadow, no cool slick on stone. The dryness is absolute: lips crack fast, wood would split, skin would sting as if you’d been dusted with salt. Chemistry here learned other ways.

The vegetation—if that word still fits—is built on resins, waxes, and mineral gels. It grows in sheets and ropes and towers:

- **Saltwood stands** rise from cracks like petrified groves. Their “trunks” are not fibrous but laminated—thin plates of silicate and organic varnish stacked in slow spirals. When the heat pulses, the plates expand with tiny clicks, and the whole grove makes a faint, constant ticking like cooling pottery.
- **Amber vines** lace the walls, gripping with brittle tendrils. They exude a glossy film that traps dust and volatile compounds from the air. Over time the film thickens into translucent curtains—golden, smoky, sometimes almost black—hanging in folds. When the wind hits them, they flap with a sound like distant canvas sails.
- **Fan-palms**—flat, circular structures—open toward the vents. Their surfaces are matte and finely ridged, designed to comb the air. They don’t drink; they *harvest*: sulfurous gases, carbon compounds, traces of ammonia, anything that can be broken and reassembled. Their “sap” is a viscous ionic syrup stored in bladders that never leak because evaporation here is a law, not a tendency.

Nothing crawls through it. Nothing flutters. No insect click, no lizard skitter, no bird call. The absence is so complete that your mind keeps trying to invent life in the corners—until you realize the loudness you hear has other causes.

Because this pocket is *noisy*.

The primary voice is wind: not a single stream, but a set of circulating currents, driven by heat rising from vents and cooler air sinking along the basin walls. The air is always moving, and it has learned the architecture intimately. It funnels through streets, howls through stairwells, and makes different notes depending on which passages are open.

The second voice is the city itself.

Urban, but not crowded. Structures are spread out with a deliberate emptiness between them, as if the builders expected the air to need room to move. There are terraces carved into the basin sides; there are straight runs of steps and ramps; there are towers that look like stacked rings. Everything is built of what the place offers: saltstone blocks, fired clay, glassy slag, basalt cut with hand tools. Metal exists only as rare fittings—if it’s there at all—because the air here is aggressive in a way that makes certain alloys crumble, bloom, or turn to powder over years.

It’s primitive engineering executed at city scale: careful joinery, gravity-fed systems, rope-and-pulley lifts, wind-driven devices made of ceramic vanes and stone bearings. Many of them still move, not because anyone maintains them, but because the environment is so active that motion is inevitable. A tower’s slit windows become organ pipes; a row of hollow columns becomes a whistle array; suspended plaques of fired clay become percussion when gusts strike them. In some corridors the sound is so constant you can feel it in your teeth.

You can walk through a district where the buildings are intact but uninhabited, and it feels like arriving just after an evacuation—except no footprints ever appear, because dust skates over everything in minutes. Doorways are hung with resin curtains now. Courtyards are filled with fan-palms oriented like listeners. Some streets are roofed, turning them into long dim tunnels where the wind plays a steady low note and the walls sweat—not water, never water—just a slow bloom of crystalline efflorescence: salts migrating outward, growing into delicate white feathers that snap off and tumble away.

In the lowest part of the basin, where heat gathers, the ground has been shaped into channels: a canal system without water. Instead, dark, sluggish liquid sometimes moves there—molten salts or sulfur-rich fluids that remain stable at these temperatures, carrying heat and dissolved minerals from vent to vent. They glimmer faintly, not with reflection but with their own dull internal glow where they’re hottest. The plants lean toward these channels the way river plants would, extending heat-tolerant root-fans into the mineral flow to harvest energy and trace elements.

This is the place’s central rule: **nothing depends on water.** Not as solvent, not as structural component, not as seasonal gift. The air is a reactive soup of dry gases and dust; the plants are chemists and engineers; the city’s old infrastructure is an arrangement for guiding wind and heat the way other cities guide rivers.

Another rule follows from that: **water, if introduced, would be a catastrophe.** Not because it would “help,” but because it would *attack*. It would flash into steam, crack ceramics, spall stone, and in contact with certain minerals create corrosive acids. Even a small amount would leave scars: crazed surfaces, sudden pits, dead patches where the resin ecology can’t tolerate the reaction products. The pocket has stayed stable partly because it has stayed pure.

Despite the heat, the place doesn’t feel like a simple furnace. It cycles. Vents pulse—slowly, like breath. Dust storms sweep through the cavity and then settle into a lull. Resins harden, then soften. Crystals grow on the windward sides of structures and are sanded away on the leeward sides. Over decades, the city is being edited: not ruined exactly, but rewritten into something that fits the pocket’s physics better.

In some neighborhoods, the vegetation has become architectural. Amber vines seal cracked domes, making them airtight. Fan-palms form living awnings that shade walkways from the harshest shafts of light. Saltwood trunks grow in straight lines along old avenues, as if following buried foundations. Without animals to prune or graze or nest, the growth is unapologetic, unbalanced in beautiful ways—heavy where gases are rich, sparse where the air is too clean.

And always, there is space.

Above rooftops and between terraces the cavity holds a great hollow volume that feels almost empty—an indoor sky of dim haze. The buildings occupy it like islands. Sound crosses it easily. A single shift of wind can send a clay chime ringing in a tower on the far side, answered by a lower moan from a vent mouth near you, answered again by the papery thrash of resin curtains snapping shut somewhere you can’t see.

If you stand still long enough, you can feel the city’s age and steadiness. It has been hot for a long time. The erosion has done its patient work. The systems have found their equilibrium: wind to vent, dust to resin, mineral to crystal, crystal back to dust. It’s active without being frantic—busy the way a kiln is busy, the way a reef would be busy if reefs were made of stone and heat and varnish.

A hidden pocket under an unremarkable plain: empty of animals, full of growth; dim, loud, and continuously in motion; a dry urban basin where the air is the river and the plants drink the wind.
