At first it reads as a mountain range, because the air is thin and the sky sits close—hard, bright, rinsed of haze—and because everything is arranged in elevations: ridges, saddles, bowls, long high traverses where your lungs notice each step. But nothing here is stone.

The “peaks” are faceted buttresses of cast alloy and riveted plate, their edges still too crisp, their seams too clean. They look *new* in the way freshly broken rock looks new: sharp angles, unrounded corners, scars without lichen. Only the scale gives them away. A cliff is a single piece of infrastructure. A valley is an assembly gap between production blocks. From a distance the terrain resolves into an open region—town-size, basin-size—yet everywhere you stand you’re crowded by presences: pipes stacked like bundled roots, stairways layered over conveyor galleries, tension cables crossing above at different heights, gantries nested inside gantries. The density is not of people, exactly, but of *things that exist at arm’s reach*.

There is no ground in the usual sense. Underfoot is always a surface with intent: grating with a repeating pattern, poured composite with aggregate that’s been chosen, not found, plates with inspection hatches. Even the “talus” at the base of a slope is not gravel but a spill of identical fasteners, snapped bracketry, tiles that have sheared in a storm and piled up like slate. When you kneel and touch it, it doesn’t give you dirt; it gives you cold, a faint grease, and the soft static prickle of something energized nearby.

Despite the industrial build, the place is mostly quiet. It is the quiet of insulation and distance and design: engines boxed behind baffles, turbines buried in thick housings, air pushed through muffled plenums that swallow their own roar. What you hear most often is not the *work* but its aftereffects—metal contracting with a slow tick as the sun shifts, a cable singing once when wind brushes it, droplets pattering in a runnel somewhere inside a wall. Occasionally there’s a low, polite hum in your bones, like a power station heard through bedrock, except there is no bedrock and the hum seems to come from the geometry itself.

Moisture is intermittent and local, arriving in pockets the way secrets do.

A bright high-altitude day will still bead water on shaded undersides: condensation collecting where warm exhaust meets cold exterior skin. Along the northern faces of the tallest blocks, you find thin films of moisture that never quite become ice, protected from the sun by overhangs and by the constant faint warmth of circulating fluid. Rain happens, but rarely as a weatherfront you can watch approach. Instead it arrives as a brief, narrow shower—one corridor wet, the next dry—because much of it is not meteorology but release: a cooling tower venting, a cloud seeding itself off a thermal plume, a pressure differential bleeding into the open. You can follow water by the stains it leaves: faint mineral bloom on stainless, pale tracks where runoff has picked up dust and carried it to a seam.

Vegetation exists only where the built world accidentally becomes habitat. A few tough, sparse things cling to the margins of moisture—threadlike moss in gasket creases, a low mat of pale lichen on polymer where the sun has roughened it, a single wiry plant rooted in a pocket of windblown grit trapped behind a bolt flange. It’s never enough to make the place feel green. The dominant colors are the bleached glare of sun on metal, the dark of shadowed bays, and the occasional safety paint—faded stripes, hazard chevrons—worn down by weather as if the world has been scrubbing at its own warnings.

And yet the fauna are everywhere.

They fill the emptiness that vegetation doesn’t. On the updraft side of the ridges, flocks of thin-winged birds—some feathered, some membrane-sailed, some with glossy plates that click softly when they fold—ride the bright air and nest in the warmth of vent stacks. In the seams between panels, small mammals or mammal-analogs have made cities of insulation fibers, and when dusk cools the surfaces they emerge to forage for whatever passes as edible here: biofilm in condensation trays, insects around sodium lamps, the salt crust on evaporator fins. Insects are abundant in the way they are in warehouses: wherever there is heat, moisture, and shelter, they make a law of numbers. Some are ordinary-looking until you notice the metallic sheen of their abdomens—mineral uptake from a life spent licking oxidized surfaces. Others are clearly adapted to the architecture: flat bodies for living under plates, hook-feet for climbing smooth walls, antennae tuned to vibration rather than scent.

Predators follow. You’ll glimpse them in the bright distance as swift silhouettes crossing a gap between blocks, or you’ll find their leavings—pellets full of wire and chitin—beneath a warm grate. Their calls are rare; sound doesn’t carry cleanly here. It gets swallowed by the density of structure, broken into short corridors and dead spaces. A scream becomes a brief, contained event, like a door closing in another room.

The atmosphere is cool, not bitter, but constantly reminding you you’re high. The sun is brilliant and feels close, and the shadows are cold enough to make your fingers numb on a handrail. Wind is not a single river of air; it’s a set of behaviors imposed by the built forms: sudden jets through narrow passages, stillness in courtyards that should be exposed, an endless faint draft up the sides of tall towers where heat bleeds out and draws the world upward.

Over time you notice that the place is *still forming*, even though it is already complete in the sense that everything is made. Its “youngness” is in how unsoftened it is—how edges haven’t been rounded by decades, how surfaces haven’t yet learned their patina. There are fresh shear lines where a panel has popped under thermal stress. There are recently laid catwalks that still smell faintly of curing resin. There are expansions happening at a pace too slow to call active construction: a new segment of conduit appearing across a week, a blank wall one day punched with a door the next month, as if the infrastructure is growing by accretion and the schedule is measured in seasons.

This is where the world begins to behave strangely, not with a single dramatic tear but with a sustained, everyday unreliability.

Distances are not stable. A block that looks a hundred meters away can take you an hour to reach, because the corridors between you and it subdivide when you walk them, offering more turns than you remember. You can look down a straight run and see the end of it clearly—bright light, a painted bulkhead—and then start walking and never quite arrive, because the space elongates by the width of a footstep each time your heel lifts. The effect is gentle, not panicked; it feels like walking on a treadmill that pretends to be a floor.

Horizons fold oddly at high points. From a ridge you can see an open region of towers, stacks, and bridgework spreading out—and then, as you shift your weight, you’ll catch the same region again at a different angle, as if it has been mirrored and pinned beside itself. The sky is bright enough to make you squint, but sometimes the brightness is wrong: shadows fall a degree off from where they should, as if the sun has two positions and they’re negotiating. At rare moments you’ll see a hard-edged glint in the air like a pane of glass; birds avoid it without understanding why.

The rules, when you live with them, become practical:

- **Water collects where the structure “forgets” to be warm.** You learn to find damp by seeking thermal boundaries: behind insulated walls, under overhangs, near dormant machines.
- **Sound is absorbed by design and by distortion.** Shouting is useless over any distance; you communicate by sight, by vibration (a knock on a rail can travel far), or by leaving marks.
- **Straight lines are suggestions, not guarantees.** The safest routes are those with repeated landmarks—numbered hatches, painted icons—because geometry alone will lie to you.
- **Living things navigate the lies better than you do.** Animals seem to feel which corridor will actually lead somewhere, which stair will return you to your own back, which open door is a loop.

Ecology adapts to those rules. Nesting sites cluster around warmth and predictability: a vent that always vents, a lamp that always lights. Migration happens vertically as much as horizontally—creatures moving up the sunlit faces in the morning and down into heat-soaked cavities at night. Food webs are built from condensation and detritus: microbes in wet trays, insects eating microbes, small things eating insects, larger things eating those, all of it cycling through a place that produces heat and surface area the way a natural landscape produces soil.

And the landscape itself changes slowly, as if it is patient.

Oxidation blooms in thin halos around fasteners. Paint fades into paler versions of itself. Wind-polished corners brighten; sheltered corners darken with film. In cold weeks, moisture freezes in cracks and lifts a plate by a millimeter; in warm weeks it settles again with a barely audible sigh. The region doesn’t erode like rock, but it does abrade, fatigue, and shed. A “landslide” here is a cascade of identical panels slumping from a rack. A “river” is a perpetual drip line that has carved a clean track through accumulated dust, revealing the original surface beneath.

If you stand still long enough—long enough to let the bright air stop dazzling and the quiet stop feeling like absence—you can sense the place’s underlying intent: not wilderness, not city, but a high, open industrial plateau that has become its own weather system and its own biome, and whose newest feature is a gentle, ongoing failure of reality that everything living has decided to treat as just another condition of the climate.
