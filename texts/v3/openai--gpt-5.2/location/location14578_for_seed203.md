It is bright first, before it is anything else.

Not the bright of sun and sky—there is no sky, not in the way an aboveground mind expects—but a clean, spilling luminance that has nowhere to go but outward. It pours across a space so large your eyes can’t gather its edges. The light is cold-colored, almost blue, and it makes every surface look recently washed, freshly made, and slightly too honest. Shadows exist only as soft understatements; even recesses seem to hold a diluted glow.

You are below the surface. Not “in a cave” so much as in a continent-sized hollow—an underworld that has its own horizons, its own weather, its own long distances that take days even when you can see them. The roof is far overhead and not always there; sometimes it is a ceiling of pale stone and crystalline seams, sometimes it rises into a haze of brightness until it might as well be a second sky. The light comes from the roof’s mineral veins: lattices of ice-clear crystal and chalky salts that fluoresce when stressed by the slow flexing of the ground. The world is young, and it is still settling into itself; the ceiling groans imperceptibly, and the glow answers.

Cold rules the air. Not killing cold, but persistent cold—cold that keeps metal from ever feeling warm, cold that turns breath into a thin, floating mist that lingers longer than it should. The damp is intermittent: it arrives as brief rains that don’t fall from clouds, but from the ceiling’s condensation sheets. In some seasons, entire districts of the roof “sweat,” and a fine, glittering precipitation descends for hours, silent as flour. Elsewhere, everything is dry as paper, until you step into a pocket where the air is suddenly wet and tastes faintly of minerals, as if you’ve walked into the mouth of a hidden river.

Despite the emptiness—despite the vast stretches of open air and bare stone—vegetation is not merely present here. It is the substrate. It is the default. Where there is something that can be lived on, walked on, leaned against, climbed, or used, plant-life has claimed the job.

But it does not fill the volume. It fills the *meaning* of the space.

The ground is often a thin skin of plant over stone: a tough, fibrous mat that looks like dried grass until you touch it and feel it flex, alive, slightly warm compared to the air. In other places it becomes architecture—ribs and buttresses made of woody tissue, arches grown rather than built. Great “columns” descend from the ceiling: not stalactites, but thick, braided trunks that have learned to grow downward, feeding on the condensed moisture and the fluorescent light. They are far apart, leaving kilometer-wide chambers between them, like a forest designed by someone who values solitude.

The young geology shows through everything. Edges are sharp; slopes are raw. Whole walls look like they were broken yesterday, their faces fresh and pale, with the crystalline seams still exposed like bone. There are fields of angular debris where the roof has shed itself: newly fallen boulders, dusted with frost, already being netted over by quick-growing vines that stitch rock to rock. Erosion here is not the slow rounding of ages but the sudden edits of collapse—an underworld that keeps changing its mind.

And it is turbulent.

Not always loud—rarely loud—but always unstable. The air moves in vast convection loops: cold currents sliding along the floor, warmer mineral-lit currents rising, spiraling, returning. When a ceiling district begins to “rain,” the humidity front spreads like a weather system; fog banks form in minutes, rolling across the open chambers and dissolving just as fast when they meet colder stone. At times the plants themselves become weather. A grove of downward-trunks can decide, collectively, to release spores—fine, reflective dust that turns the bright air into a glittering storm. It looks beautiful from a distance, like slow snow; close up it coats your eyelashes, clings to skin, makes your lungs feel as if they’ve swallowed chalk.

The plant-life has adapted to the strange light and the cold by becoming patient and opportunistic. Many species are built around rapid response rather than constant growth. They hold themselves in taut readiness—buds armored in resin, leaves folded into insulated sheaths—waiting for a brief pulse of moisture or a week of stronger fluorescence when the stone flexes more than usual. Then, suddenly, the landscape changes. In a single day a bare wall can become a green-black tapestry of tendrils, each one seeking a crack, each one drinking the thin wetness from the air.

Some plants are glassy with ice in their tissues, using frozen water as structure. Their fronds ring faintly when tapped, not like chimes but like distant, muted porcelain. Others are dark and velvety, designed to drink light greedily. Here and there you see blossoms—but not soft, delicate things. The “flowers” are heat-traps and moisture-cups, tough-lipped funnels that condense the air’s damp into small, private pools.

Water collects in places that feel accidental, as if the underworld hasn’t finished deciding where rivers should be. Long trenches hold thin, black streams that move without sound. Shallow basins fill during ceiling-rains and then freeze into clear sheets, sealing trapped leaves and spores like insects in amber. Occasionally, in a low depression, you find a lake: perfectly still, with a skin of ice so clear the bright light passes through and illuminates the plant-reefs beneath—pale, waving fronds rooted in silt, feeding on dissolved minerals and whatever dim photons reach them.

Fauna is rare enough that the place can feel uninhabited for long stretches. When you do encounter life that moves, it feels like a secret. A small pale crawler, half crustacean and half root-knot, dragging itself between plant-mats. A batlike glider with translucent membrane-wings, drifting on the slow air currents, landing only to scrape resin from a trunk with a hooked beak. Once in a while, far overhead, you glimpse something larger passing between columns—silent, slow, maybe not an animal at all but a traveling sheet of plant-tissue that has learned to “sail” the air, migrating from moisture pocket to moisture pocket.

Most sound is missing. Your footsteps are swallowed by fibrous ground. Voices don’t carry as they should; the plants seem to drink vibration, turning noise into growth signals. What you do hear comes from the world itself: the far-off crack of settling stone; the soft, endless whisper of convection winds along a cliff; the occasional patter of condensation-rain starting up in one distant district like an audience beginning to clap.

And then, scattered through the immensity, signs of hands.

A platform grown from living branches has been cut and shaped with simple tools, the ends sealed with resin. A rope bridge spans a crevasse, woven from long vine-fibers, the knots thick and practical. In a sheltered alcove—where the ceiling-rain never reaches—you find a cluster of low walls made from stacked rock and packed plant-mat, roofed with layered leaves that have been dried and lacquered with sap. Smoke stains blacken the ceiling above it, though the air is too cold and thinly damp for smoke to linger long; it must have been lit often, and recently.

Nothing here suggests metalworking beyond a few hammered scraps—perhaps traded down from some other world. Most tools are bone, stone, wood, fiber. Scrapers made from sharp mineral flakes. Hooks carved from horn. Knives edged with crystal that holds a keen cut until it shatters. The built places are never large, never permanent; the underworld changes too quickly for that. A floor that is safe this season may be gone next season, swallowed by a new collapse or colonized by a plant that exudes sleep-inducing spores.

There are rules you learn by staying.

Light is not constant; it is responsive. When the ground tremors—when some slow, deep strain releases—the crystal-veins in the roof brighten for days, as if the whole ceiling has been struck like a bell. During those “bright tides,” plants wake. Vines uncoil, buds break, spore-storms become likely. Travelers time their movement to these tides: you can cross huge distances under the bright, when the plant-mats are firm and dry, or you can be trapped in fog and sudden growth when the moisture fronts roll in.

Sound matters too. Loud vibrations can provoke the vegetation. A shouted warning in the wrong chamber may bring a curtain of tendrils down from above, not aggressive but curious, testing the air with leaf-edges like fingers. Repeated hammering can cause a wall-mat to thicken and harden, turning soft climbable growth into a resinous shield. The place listens in its own way, and it answers by rearranging itself.

The strangest thing is how the vast emptiness and the total vegetation coexist without contradiction. You can stand in a chamber the size of a city and feel alone—nothing moving, nothing near—while every surface you can touch is alive or once was. Life here isn’t crowded; it’s infrastructural. The plants have become the stonework of the world, the insulation, the bridges, the nets that catch falling rock, the skins that make a habitable microclimate in a cold hollow.

And the turbulence never stops being present, even when it is subtle. You begin to notice the slow drift of fog, the way a distant trunk-grove leans a fraction as the air-current changes, the way the roof’s glow pulses faintly like breathing. This underworld is still forming. It is a newly opened space under a crust that hasn’t decided whether to close again. Collapse and growth are not opposites here; they are the same process seen from different distances.

In the bright cold, under a ceiling that shines because it hurts, a living architecture keeps rebuilding itself across an emptiness too large to fill—so it chooses instead to *span*, to *stitch*, to *claim the surfaces*, and to let the hollows remain vast enough for weather, silence, and the occasional, fragile mark of people trying to live where the world is still becoming.
