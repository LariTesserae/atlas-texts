You go down and down through a throat of young stone—rock that still looks undecided about being rock. It is not rounded by time. It is split, flaked, and pried apart as if it is still cooling, still learning what direction it wants to fracture in. The walls glitter with pale salts that have not yet dulled, and the floor is a kind of raw rubble, a new-made scree that shifts underfoot with the faintest vibration.

Then the vegetation begins—not as a fringe or a patch, but as the thing the place is made of.

It’s below the surface, yet it holds something like daylight. The “sky” here is a ceiling of living tissue: a continuous canopy of plant-mass spanning vault to vault, thick enough to be architecture and thin enough in places to glow. It emits a steady, broad-spectrum light the way a healthy overcast sky does—no dramatic shafts, no theatrical bioluminescent points, just a diffuse, dependable brightness that makes colors read true. The underside of the canopy is ribbed with veins as wide as tree trunks, and between them hangs a lace of finer stems and leaf-plates, overlapping until you can’t find bare stone unless you pry.

The warmth is constant, coming from everywhere: from the slow, exothermic chemistry of the plant-substrate, from the animals packed into it, from vents that breathe hot air up through cracks. The air is dry enough that your lips tighten, dry enough that dust doesn’t settle so much as remain suspended, caught in the churn. And the smell—sharp, resinous, spicy—clings to the back of your throat. This is not a wet green world. The plants don’t sweat. They lacquer.

Water is almost absent here. Not “no streams,” but *no give*—no dampness in the rock, no soft rot, no coolness in shadow. Any trace of moisture that arrives (carried in on breath, on skins, on rare seepage from deeper strata) gets snapped up immediately: bound into crystalline salts along the walls, locked into waxy tissues, or cracked apart by root-chemistry and repurposed. Life runs on other fluids: oils, saps, volatile solvents. When something bleeds, it bleeds clear resin that strings and hardens in seconds, turning wounds and battlefields into amber scars.

Underfoot, the ground is not soil; it is a mat. Layers of root, fiber, and husk pack together into a springy, creaking substrate that behaves like a floor only because so many living strands agree to hold tension at once. In places the mat thickens into terraces, stair-steps, and mounds that mimic hills; in other places it thins and you can feel the stone underneath—still sharp, still new, still actively being pried open by pale root-wedges that work like slow jacks. The “erosion” here isn’t wearing down; it’s *coming into being*—cavities enlarging because roots insist, domes forming because the canopy needs span, sinkholes appearing because a colony shifted its weight.

The scale is wide. You can walk for hours and still feel the system stretching beyond you: a regional underland of connected chambers, long galleries, and open basins where the canopy rises so high it becomes a second horizon. There are corridors like streets—natural tunnels widened by traffic and growth—where the plant-mass has been polished by passage into something like barkstone. There are “plazas” where multiple chambers meet, and the ceiling-lights brighten as if the canopy is paying attention.

And it is loud. Deafening, not with a single noise, but with layers that never stop.

A constant insect-thrum forms the base note: millions of small bodies vibrating membranes, rubbing legs, snapping mandibles. Over that: the paper-rattle of dry leaves shivering against one another, amplified by the vaulted spaces. Over that: the crack and pop of resin curing, like wood in a fire but everywhere at once. Deeper still: the low, physical groan of roots under tension, the occasional report like a distant gunshot when a stone rib finally gives and the chamber’s geometry changes by an inch. The place is a living drum, and everything in it is playing.

The fauna is overwhelming, not as “big predators” but as presence-per-volume. You are never alone in a cubic meter of air. There are gliding things that do not need open sky—membrane-winged animals that launch from rib-veins and coast on the warm updrafts. There are thick-bodied grazers that rasp the canopy’s undergrowth with ridged mouths, leaving pale tracks of exposed cambium that quickly lacquer over. There are burrowers that tunnel through the root-mat as if it were snow, collapsing and reknitting behind them. There are swarms that behave like weather: clouds of tiny, reflective-winged animals that turn the light into moving patterns, making the “day” flicker without changing brightness.

Predation here is fast because nothing can afford to leak. Teeth and claws are designed to clamp and seal; hunters bite and inject hardening enzymes, turning prey into a still-struggling bundle of resin before it can lose its precious chemistry. Scavengers come with chiseling mouthparts, able to mine hardened sap the way other worlds mine marrow. Even the parasites are built for dryness: they live in the wax layers, under lacquer skins, between fiber bundles, drinking oils and leaving behind crystalline waste that the plants incorporate into their own armored tissues.

Turbulence defines the ecology. The entire underland is in motion—not drifting, but actively reconfiguring.

At irregular intervals the canopy “breathes” in a way you can see. Whole spans tense, sag, and retighten as fluid moves through the great veins. When a vein shifts its pressure, the mat below responds: root-bundles contract, terraces lift, a corridor narrows until it becomes a crawlspace and, elsewhere, a new arch opens like a mouth. Animals treat these slow quakes as routine. They pause, brace, let the world pass through its flex, then resume as if nothing happened. The only consistent geography is the direction of growth and the memory of trails.

Sometimes the turbulence becomes meteorological. Not rain—never rain—but storms of seed-husk and spore. A hot wind surges down a gallery, carrying dry pollen like smoke. It scours the lacquered surfaces, whistles through hollow stems, and turns the air into a glittering haze. In those moments the deafening sound becomes almost violent: a shriek of airborne grit, the roar of millions of wings battered by the gust, the booming resonance of chambers acting like organ pipes. Visibility shortens. Animals cling to the underside of ribs. The plant-mass closes its pores, and the “sky” dims a shade—not dark, just as if the world squints.

Weirdness here is not decoration; it’s rule.

The plants behave less like passive organisms and more like infrastructure with intention. They reroute paths by thickening under popular trails, almost paving them with living fiber. They “mark” territories by exuding different scents—sharp citrus in one basin, peppery bitterness in the next—so that even without maps you can smell where you are. In places, the canopy grows reflective leaf-plates that angle themselves to spread light into newly opened chambers, as if the system refuses to leave any volume unused.

And there are things that look like mistakes that have been kept because they work: roots that form perfect hexagonal lattices, not because hexagons are pretty but because the tensile math is efficient; seedpods that ring like bells when struck by wind, turning the whole basin into a clattering instrument; colonies of small animals that coat themselves in salt crystals, becoming walking mineral mirrors that confuse predators and brighten the world in motion.

Scattered through it all are built marks—primitive, occasional, half-swallowed by growth.

A platform of woven stems raised above the mat where the ground is too restless. A line of stacked stones (salted white, carefully chosen) set into a corridor wall—cairns that remain only because the plants have grown around them, accepting them as anchors. A shelter like an upside-down basket of ribs, sealed with cured resin, its entrance a low slit that smells of old smoke and animal fat. The technology is hand-scale: blades of knapped stone sharp enough to shave lacquer, bone awls, fiber cord, simple fired clay that has been sintered by the ambient warmth. You find tools embedded in the plant-substrate the way you find fossils: a flint scraper halfway engulfed by a root-thickening, a length of braided cord turned brittle and ambered.

If there are people here, they live by listening, because you can’t see the turbulence coming. The sound tells you what is shifting: the pitch change that means a corridor is narrowing, the sudden hush that means the canopy has sealed its pores, the rising rattle that means a spore-squall is building. Silence, if it ever occurs, would be the true alarm.

And always, everywhere, the sensation that this is an ecosystem not merely thriving but *filling the available universe*. Below the surface, with almost no water, it has built itself out of dryness: out of oils and salts, out of fibers under tension, out of resins that harden into structure. Stone is young and still being pushed into shape. Plants are not a layer on the world; they are the world’s scaffolding. Animals are not inhabitants; they are weather.

You could stand in one of the open basins, look across a distance that feels like a valley, and watch the canopy ripple—slowly at first, then in a wave as something heavy moves along its upper surface, unseen. A moment later, the ripple becomes a change in sound, then a tremor underfoot, then a swarm response as thousands of bodies adjust their trajectories. The landscape is not a backdrop. It is a crowded, bright, roaring machine of living matter, continuously under construction, continuously deciding what shape “land” will mean next.
