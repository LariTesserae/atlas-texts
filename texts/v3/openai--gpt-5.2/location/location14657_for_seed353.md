It isn’t a desert in the way a map would mean it—sand and sky and nothing between. It’s a desert because there is no water anywhere that matters. No wetness. No dew. No seep. No hidden cistern. Even the word *moist* feels incorrect here, like a superstition. The air is thin with height and thin with humidity, a high place where your skin remembers oceans it has never had.

And yet the place is all vegetation.

Not “plants growing in” a landscape, but plants *being* the landscape, the infrastructure, the floorplan, the machinery housings, the walls that keep the heat in and the darkness thick. The base assumption here is reversed: mineral is incidental; plant is substrate.

From a distance—if distance exists in a region packed so full the horizon is always busy—the terrain reads like a single continuous mass, a plateau braided with ridges and troughs. Up close, it resolves into construction: a worked topography of ribs, vaults, buttresses, and interlocked beams, all grown to specification in the shape of architecture. You walk through corridors that were never quarried, never poured. They were cultivated.

Everything is made. Even the “rock” is composite: lignin-like struts mineralized with dust and slag, char-blackened and polished by ages of hot, dry wind. There is erosion, but it doesn’t expose geology; it exposes earlier *designs*. Old layers of structure, planed down and rounded over until the joints become gentle swells and the seams are soft as worn bone. The place has had time to settle into its final set of shapes—then be rebuilt on top of them, again and again, so the ancient and the engineered are not opposites but a single long process.

High elevation and heat combine into a particular pressure on the body: lungs working too hard in air that offers too little, sweat that should cool you but can’t because it vanishes before it has the courtesy to bead. The heat doesn’t come from sunlight—there is hardly any light worth naming. It comes from inside: from industry, from metabolism, from venting, from constant internal friction.

Darkness here isn’t an absence so much as a material.

Above you, the “canopy” is structural, a ceiling of braided plant-architecture so dense it turns the sky into a rumor. The little illumination that does exist is industrial: dull filaments behind translucent membranes; sodium-colored glows inside resin bladders; intermittent arcs in insulated chambers. Light leaks out like a controlled spill—only enough to read gauges or see the edge of a walkway—then is swallowed by matte surfaces that seem designed to refuse reflection. Even the air drinks it, thick with particulate: fine plant-spore dust, carbon soot, powdered mineral from old windwork.

Underfoot, the ground is not soil. It’s a tessellation of living plates—callused pads of tough, dry tissue—stitched together with seam-lines that flex. Occasionally the plates open with a slow, silent iris action to admit a passing cart or to breathe heat out of an internal conduit. When they close, they leave no crack. In places the “street” is ribbed like the inside of a giant stem, each ridge tuned to guide wheels, to keep movement ordered in a space that otherwise has no room to spare.

Packed is the correct word. There is no empty. Every volume is occupied: by load-bearing growth, by ducts, by cables, by vascular tubes that are not for water but for other fluids—oily saps, solvent resins, brines of molten salts, pressurized gases. The plants here run on chemistry that has learned to do without water, or to hide what little is needed inside locked microcells that never spill. Their “blood” is viscous and clear, smelling faintly of pine and hot plastic, and it hardens instantly when exposed—an evolutionary choice in a land where any leak is a death sentence.

There are no animals. Not even the small ones that normally arrive uninvited. No insects. No birds. No scavengers. The silence of that absence is part of the architecture. The only movement that has agency belongs to growth and to machines.

And it is turbulent—restlessly so—despite how old and worn everything looks.

The turbulence isn’t wind (though wind exists, high and abrasive, full of dry grit). It’s change inside the built-plant mass: pressure surges, growth corrections, pruning cycles, emergency reroutes when a conduit occludes, when a rib buckles, when heat spikes somewhere it shouldn’t. You can feel it in the way floors subtly rise and fall as internal bladders inflate; in the slow, unstoppable creep of a wall thickening overnight; in the sudden, disciplined violence of a cutting line.

Because there are machines. Industrial ones. They are embedded rather than installed: engines seated in living cradles; turbines whose housings are grown around them; stamping presses that descend into pads of sacrificial tissue and come up with neat, identical segments of structural “wood” already resin-cured. There are belts—literal belts—made from braided fiber, running through tunnels where they whisper rather than rattle. There are heat exchangers that look like gills. There are pumps that thud in a slow rhythm you feel in your teeth more than you hear.

Mostly quiet doesn’t mean peaceful. It means the materials absorb sound. Plant-architecture is generous with damping: spongy layers, fibrous composites, chambers designed to keep vibrations from propagating and shaking the whole. What noise remains is sparse and far away: a distant hiss as a vent purges hot gas; the occasional crack like a tree limb snapping as a load-bearing strut is replaced; a soft rainless patter of dust settling in a void space; the whisper of conveyor fibers sliding over polished ribs.

Sometimes—rarely—the whole region performs a larger adjustment.

A section of the plateau will “open,” not like a landslide, but like a mechanism: overlapping plates peel back, revealing a dark interior full of low red glow. Heat pours up in a slow wave. Inside, there are stacked bays of cultivation, long chambers where new beams are being grown in precise molds, where resin is being cooked, where older tissue is being rendered down into feedstock. The air that rises smells sharp and clean, like scorched citrus and hot tar. Then the plates interlock again, leaving the surface intact, as if nothing happened.

The ecology is an industry and the industry is an ecology.

The plants—if that word still fits—are not individual organisms but a continuous engineered continuum with specialized organs: structural trunks that are also columns; leaf-analogues that don’t seek light but radiate heat away; root-analogues that don’t hunt water but mine minerals, chelate metals, pull nitrogenous gases out of the thin air. They feed on dust, on carbon, on industrial byproducts, on heat gradients. Their “photosynthesis” is replaced by something stranger: thermosynthesis, chemosynthesis, the capture of waste energy and its conversion into growth and resin.

The “town-or-valley-size” openness is made of repeating districts, each with its own function. One area is a field of upright spines—antenna groves—tall enough that the thin air hisses around them. Another is a basin (it cannot be called a lakebed; lakes never happened here) where old structures have been worn down until only rounded stumps remain, and on those stumps new modules have been grafted: platforms, hatches, ribbed domes. Farther out, the plateau breaks into terraces like giant steps, each step crowded with ducts and buttresses, each ledge threaded with glowing lines where heated fluids run beneath translucent skin.

Even the sky participates in the strangeness. Above the ceiling-mass, storms may be happening—electrical, dry, full of static and grit—but you experience them as pressure changes and faint distant flickers through the thick canopy. Occasionally a tremor runs through the whole structure, as if the plateau is bracing. Dust drifts down in slow sheets. Somewhere, far away, a discharge pops—muted, swallowed, more felt than heard—and the darkness seems to tighten.

There are rules here, and they’re enforced without mercy:

1. **Nothing spills.** Any free liquid is a disaster. Every conduit is redundant; every reservoir self-sealing. When a line ruptures, tissue around it swells and vitrifies, locking the breach shut in seconds.
2. **Heat is currency.** Waste heat is captured, routed, traded between districts. Cold is not comfort; cold is failure.
3. **Growth is scheduled.** Left alone, the plant-mass will swell into every available void. So growth is constrained by cutting patterns, by chemical brakes, by engineered senescence. “Wild” growth is treated like a fire.
4. **Darkness is maintained.** Light is expensive here—not because it can’t be made, but because it disrupts. Some processes need shadow; some tissues harden incorrectly under illumination. Darkness keeps the system stable, keeps algae-like opportunists from arising (if they even could, with no water).
5. **No fauna is allowed to take hold.** Whether by accident or design, the environment is hostile to animals: too dry, too hot, too chemically wrong, too sealed. The only moving “life” is what the continuum permits—pollen-like spores that never become independent, microscopic workers that are more like enzymes than creatures.

If you stand still long enough, you can sense the age beneath the turbulence. The rounded edges. The smoothed-over catastrophes. The way old scaffolds have become indistinguishable from “natural” ridges because time has worn their sharpness into something geological. This place has been at the end of its own world for a long time—settled into a mature, stable ruin—and still it cannot stop making itself.

In the deep quiet between hiss and crack, the landscape feels like a single vast machine dreaming in plant tissue: hot, high, dry, crowded, dark—busy in ways that don’t require witnesses.
