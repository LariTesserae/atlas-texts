High up, where the air is thin enough to make every sound carry oddly far, the ground has not decided what shape it wants to keep.

It is a plateau, but a young one: a raw table of stone pushed up from whatever slept beneath, still warm in places, still seamed with stress-lines that have not finished becoming faults. The city sits on it like a second crust—dense, layered, and heavy with intention—yet even the city’s weight doesn’t make the plateau feel settled. Everything here is in the phase between “happened” and “happening.”

Cold dominates, but it isn’t the clean, dry cold of snowfields. The cold is wet. It clings.

Water is everywhere without ever committing to being a river.

It beads on the undersides of bridges. It sweats from rock like the plateau is exhaling. It pools in shallow basins that look accidental until you notice how often those basins repeat along old survey lines, as if the ground was encouraged to hold water in a pattern. After whatever passes for rain here—more often a thick fall of condensed mist than droplets—thin films of water run across surfaces and then stall, held by surface tension and grime, turning stair treads and railings into dark mirrors. In sheltered alcoves, it freezes into clear ribs that do not look like icicles so much as manufactured components, slotted into place by gravity.

There are no plants. No tufts, no moss, no lichens painting the stone. Even the usual stubborn pioneers are absent. The wetness has nothing green to feed, so it makes its own textures: varnish-darkened rock, slick concrete, swollen composite seams, salt blooms along drainage channels, and a perpetual sheen that makes every surface look freshly made and not yet used.

The light is nearly gone.

Not because the sky is cloudy—though it is, often, a lid of low, fast-moving vapor that scrapes the highest structures—but because the world’s illumination has been turned down at the source. The sky is a deep, inked violet-black even at what local clocks insist is midday. There are no stars most nights, either, as if the atmosphere holds an extra layer that eats distant light. The city does not answer with bright lamps. It answers with the minimum: thin indicator glows tucked into cracks, the occasional blush of status-light under a translucent panel, a faint moving thread of blue where an overhead line is actively carrying something.

You learn quickly that vision is not the primary sense this place expects you to use.

Sound is.

It is loud here in a way that isn’t a single roar but a crowded stack of noises, all occupying the same space. Some are infrastructural: a deep, sub-audible thrumming that you feel in your teeth, as if a slow engine is idling beneath the plateau; the periodic clack of mechanisms taking up slack; the distant, steady rush of water being moved through conduits. Some are environmental: wind hissing across wet edges; the crack of ice forming and unforming inside gutters; the soft, constant patter of condensation falling from cables like rain that never reaches the sky.

But most of it is alive.

Fauna is overwhelming—not as a wilderness outside the city, but as the city’s dominant population. The streets are not empty corridors between buildings. They are migration lanes, rookeries, feeding grounds, arenas, and nurseries. Movement is everywhere, often just beyond the reach of what little light exists: shapes passing under archways; clusters shifting like a single organism; sudden bursts of wingbeats in the dark.

The animals here are not “wildlife” visiting an urban edge. They are built into the urban ecology the way pigeons, rats, dogs, and insects are built into ours—except there is nothing green to anchor them, and nothing about them suggests they ever needed it.

Some of them are large enough to make you understand why the architecture is dense and reinforced. Long-backed quadrupeds—bone or chitin or something like ceramic—move in slow file under the elevated conduits, their hooves clicking on wet pavement with a sound like tools dropped on stone. Their flanks carry ridges that glisten as if coated in oil; when they breathe, you hear a bubbling through liquid. Smaller things cling to them and hop off at intervals, scavenging from the drips and crusts that form where the big bodies pass.

There are flocks too, but “flock” is the wrong word when the air is this thin and the darkness this complete. They navigate by echo and pressure: you hear them before you have any chance of seeing them, a rolling, synchronized chatter that rebounds off buildings and makes the whole street feel like it has become a resonant chamber. When they turn as one, the sound changes shape, and you can tell their direction the way you can tell the direction of an approaching train.

And there are the swarms close to the ground: dense carpets of small, jointed things that move like poured gravel until something disturbs them and they reconfigure, opening a path without panic. Their bodies are matte and wet-looking, as if designed not to reflect. They cluster around heat leaks and electrical junctions. They pour into cracks. They build up along curbs where mineral salts collect, and you hear their feeding as a soft, continuous rasping.

That is one of the rules of this place: the food chain is electrical and mineral, not botanical.

There are no leaves to harvest sunlight, but there is energy in abundance—bleeding from the city, stored in it, being moved through it. Some creatures lick condensation from charged rails and tremble with it. Others gnaw corrosion from old alloys, digesting metal oxides the way a goat digests grass. The wetness is not just water; it is a solvent carrying traces: dissolved salts, fine particulates, and whatever the city exhales. It makes a broth thin enough to drink and rich enough to live on.

The city itself feels like it was made by something that did not privilege human eyes.

Buildings are tall but not proud. They do not reach upward to be seen; they stack and interlock to create protected volumes. There are few broad facades. Instead, surfaces are broken into ribs, fins, and overlapping plates that catch condensation and direct it. Edges are rounded where animals pass and sharp where they shouldn’t. Openings are not doors so much as pressure-gates: membranes that flex; iris-like apertures that dilate when something approaches; slits that open only at certain sound signatures.

Materials are everywhere: basalt from the plateau, poured and re-poured into new foundations; pale concrete with a fine aggregate that looks like crushed glass; composites that feel almost soft under the hand until you press and realize they are simply yielding in a controlled way. Metals are present but rarely exposed in large sheets; when they are, they are patterned, etched with micro-grooves that hold water in place, preventing it from running freely. The city seems to prefer to keep water close.

At high points—rooftops, terraces, the tops of utility pylons—the wet air condenses into fog so dense it has texture. It moves slowly, not in gusty shoves but in patient, creeping shifts, like a tide deciding where to settle. You can stand and watch a streetlamp’s faint indicator glow vanish as a fog bank inches across it. The fog muffles some sounds and amplifies others: a distant call becomes intimate, a nearby scrape becomes a cavernous boom.

Erosion, here, has barely begun its work, and yet the landscape is already scarred—not by age, but by emergence.

The plateau’s edges are ragged, with fresh fractures that look like they happened yesterday. In some districts, the ground is still splitting: hairline cracks that widen over months, then suddenly drink a stream of runoff and turn into a channel overnight. Water finds these new weaknesses and inhabits them immediately, not carving deep canyons but making slick, shining seams that reflect faint city-glow like knife edges.

In other places, the city is actively shaping the terrain faster than wind and water can. There are retaining walls that seem to grow: bands of material extruded in slow increments, each layer bonding to the last as if the wall is being printed in place over years. You can tell because the layers are not uniform; they thicken near stress points, and in the dark you sometimes hear them, a quiet ticking as internal structures adjust.

That is another rule: construction here is not an event but a long-lived behavior.

Infrastructure behaves like a living thing that heals. A collapsed walkway is not cordoned off with bright tape; it is surrounded by a slow-moving assembly of scaffolds that arrive, lock into place, and begin to re-knit the gap. Over weeks they thicken, seal, and then retract, leaving behind a surface that matches nothing around it except in function. The city does not preserve aesthetics. It preserves flow.

Flow of water. Flow of bodies. Flow of energy.

Because the city is dense, those flows overlap. Underpasses run with shallow sheets of water and with herds at the same time. Elevated corridors carry both cables and roosting colonies. Drainage channels are also travel lanes for the small rasping swarms. Every volume is used; every hollow is occupied. Even the air has layers: the lower stratum thick with ground-swarms and the damp smell of minerals; the mid-level full of calls, wingbeats, and the metallic tang of electrical discharge; the upper stratum a cold fog threaded with faint, drifting particles that might be ice or might be something else shedding from the city.

The openness of the region reveals itself when you find a long, straight stretch—an arterial causeway running out of the dense core toward the plateau’s far side. Here the city thins, but it does not stop; it frays into infrastructure: towers spaced at regular intervals, low service blocks half-buried in wet stone, arrays of dark panels that do not reflect even starlight. Beyond them, the plateau continues, an expanse of slick rock and shallow water basins, and then—far off—ridges like frozen waves, higher elevations where the fog becomes ice crystals and the sounds become sharper.

There are places where water collects into something like marshland, except the “marsh” is mineral. Pools are rimmed not with reeds but with crystalline crusts, and the ground between pools is not soil but a spongy mat of porous rock that holds water in its structure. When you step on it, it gives slightly and then slowly rebounds, exhaling cold damp air through tiny pores. Animals gather here in numbers that make the dark feel crowded: long-necked drinkers lowering their heads to the pools; predatory shapes waiting at the edges, so still they could be statues until they move; countless small things skittering over the mineral sponge, leaving trails of displaced moisture that re-fill behind them.

Despite the loudness, the overall rhythm is slow.

Nothing here is frantic. Predation happens, but it is methodical. Repairs happen, but they take seasons. The plateau continues to rise or settle by amounts you would only notice if you lived here long enough to see a doorway become misaligned, a canal begin to run backwards, a building’s base develop a new tilt.

In the darkness, time is measured by cycles other than day and night: the deepening and thinning of fog; the periodic surge of some substructure’s hum; the seasonal shift in the animals’ calls. There is a particular moment—rare, but known—when the sky briefly lightens to a bruised gray and you realize the cloud ceiling has lifted. Not enough to reveal a sun. Just enough to show the city’s true scale: a regional sprawl of interlocked construction spread across a high, wet plateau, with no green anywhere, and with life—dense, loud, and everywhere—using every surface as if it was designed for them.

Maybe it was.

Maybe the reason the world is dark is that this place never needed light. Vision is optional. Water is plentiful. Energy is the true food. The city is not a human artifact surviving in spite of nature; it is a habitat-engine, and the fauna are not intruders but its intended citizens—living bodies tuned to wet stone, cold air, and the slow, patient building of a world still coming into being.
