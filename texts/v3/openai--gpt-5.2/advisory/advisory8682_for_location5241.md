## Core expectation: cold, damp, thin air, and a city that runs on gravity  
Prepare for three constant conditions:

1. **Altitude**: “Thin but not sharp” still means lower oxygen, faster fatigue, slower healing, and higher dehydration risk.  
2. **Cold + wet**: Not necessarily storms—persistent moisture, fog, seepage, and stone that never really warms. Hypothermia can happen above freezing.  
3. **Stone infrastructure everywhere**: Slopes, steps, channels, terraces, rooflines—beautiful, but hard on joints and unforgiving of mistakes.

If you arrive thinking “cool hiking weather,” you’ll under-pack and over-exert.

---

## Before you go: body prep (2–4 weeks out if possible)

### Altitude readiness
- **Cardio base**: 3–5 sessions/week of steady aerobic work (30–60 min). You’re training for *hours of stairs and grades*, not sprints.
- **Leg and ankle durability**: step-ups, calf raises, single-leg balance. The city is basically vertical mileage.
- **Acclimatization plan** (ideal):
  - Day 1–2: very light walking only; sleep low if you can (lower basins).
  - Day 3–4: moderate activity; avoid “big terrace days.”
  - Day 5+: longer traverses.
- **Know AMS signs** (acute mountain sickness): headache + nausea + unusual fatigue + poor sleep. If it escalates (confusion, unsteady gait, breathlessness at rest), your plan is simple: **descend and rest**.

### Cold-wet tolerance
- Practice a couple of **rainy-day long walks** with your intended clothing system. You’re testing: “Do I stay warm when damp?” not “Do I look dry?”

### Skill refreshers that matter here
- **Fog navigation**: comfort with map/compass and pacing. Fog will erase landmarks.
- **Slips/falls**: basic fall awareness, how to move on slick stone (short steps, weight over feet, slow turns).
- **Simple knots & carrying**: everything is carried, rolled, or tied. Bringing competence beats bringing gadgets.

## Packing: what actually works in this environment

### Clothing system (assume you will be damp daily)
**Goal: stay warm while wet, dry overnight, avoid cotton.**
- **Base layers**: merino or synthetic (2 sets).  
- **Mid-layer insulation**: fleece or light wool; add a **puffy jacket** (synthetic preferred—handles damp better than down).  
- **Shell**: waterproof breathable jacket with a real hood; rain pants if you’ll be out for hours.  
- **Legs**: softshell or quick-dry pants; avoid denim.  
- **Hands/head**:
  - thin liner gloves + warmer gloves (stone chills hands fast),
  - warm hat + a brimmed cap (fog drip + rain).  
- **Socks**: 3–5 pairs wool/synthetic. Rotate; your feet will thank you.

### Footwear: your #1 safety item
- **Boots/shoes**: grippy sole that handles wet stone and algae film (trail shoes can work if sturdy; boots add ankle protection on uneven steps).  
- **Gaiters** (optional but useful): keep grit and marsh-edge wet out.  
- **Foot care**: blister kit, antifungal cream (persistent damp), spare laces.

### Sleep & warmth
- **Sleeping bag/quilt** rated for cool nights, plus a **sleeping pad** (stone floors steal heat).  
- **Dry-sleep rule**: one dedicated dry base layer + socks that never leave your sleep kit.

### Navigation & lighting (assume electronics may be unreliable or socially unhelpful)
- **Paper map** (or locally acquired equivalent) + **compass**.  
- **Headlamp** + spare batteries (foggy evenings come early; stairwells can be dim).  
- A small **hand light** is useful in narrow lanes without blasting everyone’s eyes.

### Water and hygiene (critical, because cisterns are communal)
- **Water container(s)** you can fill without dipping into cistern mouths: narrow-mouth bottles + a small funnel if needed.  
- **Filter** (gravity or squeeze) + **backup purification** (tablets).  
- **Biodegradable soap** used away from channels/cistern mouths.  
- **Quick-dry towel** + a small scrub cloth: wet stone grime accumulates.

### Food and fueling (altitude + cold increases caloric needs)
- Bring or plan to acquire **dense snacks**: nuts, dried fruit, hard cheese, jerky, oat cakes—things you can eat while walking.  
- **Electrolytes**: foggy cold tricks you into under-drinking, but altitude still dehydrates you.

### Tools that match the city’s “mass + slope” logic
- **Light gloves** for gripping wet stone and cold railings.  
- **A few meters of cord** (repairs, bundling, carrying).  
- **Small repair kit**: needle/thread, tape, patch material. Damp environments punish gear.

## Arrival strategy: first 48 hours

### Where to base yourself
Choose lodging in a **mid-elevation district** if possible:
- Lower basins = more fog pooling, slick surfaces, soft ground.
- Upper terraces = more wind exposure and chill.
Mid-zones give you options to go up/down without being trapped by weather.

### First-day movement rules
- Walk like you’re on ice, even if it doesn’t look like ice.
- Treat **green-black staining** on steps as “slippery until proven otherwise.”
- Don’t commit to long traverses across multiple ridges until you know how quickly fog assembles.

### Hydration and breathing
- Drink on a schedule (small amounts often).  
- Expect mild breathlessness on stairs; pause early, not late.  
- If you get a headache, don’t “push through”—eat, drink, rest, reassess.

## Daily living in the wet-stone city

### How to move safely
- **Short stride, flat foot placement**, slow pivots.  
- Use walls for balance, but mind seep lines—some are slick as soap.  
- In narrow lanes, assume **runoff can appear suddenly** from a roof gutter above.

### Respect the water architecture (this is both etiquette and safety)
- Never wash directly in channels or at cistern mouths.  
- Don’t drop food scraps into drainage—blockages upstream are taken personally.  
- Step over rills and grooves; don’t stand in them to chat. They are “roads” for water.

### Drying routine (nightly)
This place rewards ritual:
1. Wipe boots and remove insoles.  
2. Hang socks/base layers where air moves (near a draft, not against cold stone).  
3. Keep one “sleep-dry” set sealed away from ambient damp.  
4. Check for skin breakdown: feet, between toes, and any friction points.

### Health issues you’re most likely to face
- **Low-grade hypothermia**: clumsy hands, irritability, bad decisions. Add layers *before* you feel cold.  
- **Trench foot / fungal problems**: rotate socks, dry feet daily, treat early.  
- **Respiratory irritation**: fog + cold + mineral air can inflame throats. A simple buff/scarf helps.  
- **Joint strain**: endless steps. Use trekking poles if you’re comfortable with them (they help a lot on slick descents).

## Weather and visibility: fog is not “just fog”
- Fog here **assembles locally**: you can walk from clear air into near-whiteout within minutes in courtyards, stairwells, and trough-like streets.
- Establish a rule: if visibility drops below “I can clearly identify the next junction,” **stop, mark your position, and wait or backtrack**. People get lost close to home in cities like this because everything is stone and everything looks plausible.

## Social and practical considerations (ordinary human, low-tech city)

### Expect a culture of maintenance
People will be moving stone, clearing channels, re-facing terraces. This isn’t scenery; it’s civic survival.
- Offer to help only if invited; otherwise you may interrupt a practiced workflow.
- Don’t assume “abandoned” terraces are unused—some are water buffers or seasonal catchments.

### Trade and value
In a city built without devices, the universal valuables tend to be:
- durable cloth, cordage, needles,
- salt, dried foods,
- waterproofing materials,
- skilled labor and reliability.
Bring small, useful items rather than flashy ones.

### Noise and conduct
Sound is naturally muffled; loud voices carry oddly in tight lanes. Move and speak a little softer than you think you need to, especially at night when drip-sounds act like an acoustic map for locals.

## Hazards you should actively plan for

### 1) Slips and falls (most common)
Mitigation:
- footwear traction,
- slow descents,
- keep hands free (use a pack, not armloads).

### 2) Structural edges and hidden drops
Terraces and stair seams can be unforgiving, especially in fog. Don’t sit on parapets unless you’ve checked for damp slime.

### 3) Contaminated water pockets
Most water is managed, but surface damp can carry algae/mineral salts.
- Drink only from trusted sources or treat everything.

### 4) Cold creep
Because the air isn’t “sharp,” you may not notice you’re cooling down until you’re already compromised. Make warmth deliberate: eat, layer, keep moving gently.

### 5) Getting “microclimate trapped”
A route that’s safe dry can become treacherous when fog condenses on shaded steps. Always keep a **secondary route** that stays on wider streets and gentler grades.

## A compact “do not arrive without” checklist
- Waterproof shell + warm mid-layer  
- 3+ pairs wool/synthetic socks  
- Grippy footwear suitable for wet stone  
- Headlamp + spare batteries  
- Paper map + compass  
- Water treatment (filter or tablets)  
- Basic first aid + blister care + antifungal  
- Sleeping insulation from stone (pad)  
- Cord + small repair kit
