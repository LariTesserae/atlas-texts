## What you’re walking into (so you pack for the right problems)
This basin isn’t “desert” so much as **a stable, warm, dim, mineral-dust chamber** with almost no natural cues—no wind voice, no sun rhythm, no water, no tracks, and very little biological activity to warn you when you’re doing something stupid.

The main risks for an ordinary human aren’t dramatic predators or storms; they’re:

- **Dehydration + heat stress** without the obvious “hot sun” feedback.
- **Respiratory and eye irritation** from fine, alkaline/mineral dust and efflorescent salts.
- **Atmospheric pooling**: a low basin that “holds its own weather” can also hold heavier gases or low-oxygen layers.
- **Navigation and time disorientation** in permanent twilight and near-silence.
- **Foot/hand injuries** from glassy nodules, sharp basalt fracture, and crust that can fail unpredictably.
- **Psychological effects** of sensory deprivation, monotony, and the uncanny “nothing circulates quickly” feeling.
- **Contamination risk** (both directions): extremophile films/mats you don’t want in your lungs—or back in your home ecosystem.

Treat it like entering a **quiet industrial mineral space** plus **a dark-room psychology experiment**.

## Pre-departure checklist (do this before you open a portal)
### 1) Know your limits and set a hard timeline
Because the place won’t push you out with weather, you need your own structure.

- Decide your **maximum on-site time** (e.g., 6–12 hours for a first entry).
- Set **turnaround triggers**:
  - You’ve used 50% of your water.
  - Your CO₂/O₂ monitor alarms or trends down.
  - You can’t clearly see the rim-shelves anymore (haze/dust increase).
  - You feel heat illness symptoms (headache, nausea, chills in warmth, confusion).
- File a simple plan: entry point, intended route, return time, and “if I miss check-in, send retrieval.”

### 2) Assume the air is “not automatically safe”
A basin that pools air can pool problems.

Bring:
- **4-gas monitor** (O₂, CO, H₂S, combustible/LEL) *plus* ideally a **CO₂ meter**.
- **Particulate protection**: P100/FFP3-level respirator. If you can tolerate it, a **half-face respirator with P100 filters** is better than disposable masks.
- **Spare filters**: the dust is fine and persistent.

If your monitor shows low O₂, rising CO₂, or anything you don’t understand: **leave immediately**. In places like this, “just a little dizzy” can become “lying down and not getting up.”

### 3) Power: don’t count on solar
The sky “drinks brightness.” Pack power as if it’s a cave.

- Headlamp + backup headlamp.
- Batteries for at least **2× your expected use**.
- A small area light for camp/work (optional, but morale-saving).
- Keep electronics in **dust-sealed bags/cases**; mineral dust can be abrasive and sometimes mildly conductive.

## Clothing and personal protective gear (dress for warm dust, not sun)
### Core clothing
- Lightweight, breathable long sleeves/pants (keeps dust off skin).
- **Neck gaiter** or buff (dust seal, sweat management).
- Wide-brim hat is optional; there’s no hard sun, but it helps keep dust off hair/ears.

### Footwear and hands
- Sturdy boots with a firm sole (crust plates + hidden sharp rock).
- Gaiters if you have them; dust finds its way into everything.
- Work gloves (basalt and glassy nodules cut; crust edges can be sharp).

### Eyes and breathing
- Sealed or semi-sealed goggles (not just sunglasses).
- Respirator as above. Practice wearing it before you go—panic from “can’t breathe right” is a real risk in a place that already feels air-thick.

## Water, food, and salts (the basin’s real tax)
Even without sun glare, **constant warmth + dry air + respirator use** will pull water out of you.

- Water: minimum **3–4 liters per person for a half-day**; more if you’re working, sampling, or anxious (anxiety dehydrates).
- Electrolytes: bring oral rehydration salts or electrolyte tabs.
- Food: compact, salty, easy to eat while masked (bars, nuts, jerky). You’ll burn more energy than you think because you’ll move slowly and tensely.

Do **not** rely on anything local. There’s essentially no moisture to harvest, and mineral crusts can be irritating or toxic.

## Navigation and orientation (because the basin won’t help you)
This place erases the normal “I know where I am” feeling.

Bring:
- A reliable compass (and know how to use it).
- A GPS if it works in your travel context—but assume it might not.
- **Physical breadcrumbing that leaves no trace**:
  - Small reflective markers on a spool line (retrievable).
  - Or a collapsible survey tape you can pick back up.
- A notebook: write down headings, landmarks, time stamps. Your memory gets weird in monotone environments.

Rule: **Always maintain a known line back to the exit** on first visits. Don’t “just wander the center.”

## On arrival: first 10 minutes should be procedure, not wonder
1) **Stop at the entry point**. Don’t walk in while your brain is still adjusting.
2) Check instruments: O₂/CO₂, particulates (if you have a meter), temperature.
3) Put on respirator/goggles *before* you feel you “need” them.
4) Pick a landmark on the rim-shelf directly opposite you.
5) Establish your return line (tape/line/markers) and start your timer.

This basin is inviting precisely because it’s not demanding—so you must be the demanding one.

## Moving through the basin (how not to get cut, stuck, or lost)
### Footing
- Treat polygon crust like **thin ice over firm ground**: mostly stable, occasionally treacherous.
- Step on **broader plates**, not seam-lines, when possible.
- Avoid hopping down into alcoves unless you can climb out easily—fine powder can behave like ball bearings.

### Hands-off rule for surfaces
- Don’t lean bare hands on “warm stone” without gloves; not because it’s scorching, but because mineral films and salts can irritate skin and then you’ll rub your eyes.
- Don’t break crust just to see inside. You’ll aerosolize dust and any microbial films.

### Watch for glass
Those glassy nodules that fracture sharp can slice you fast. A small cut in a dry, dusty place becomes a contamination and infection problem.

Carry:
- Sterile wound wipes, irrigation saline, butterfly closures, and a real bandage.
- A small splint wrap (even a sprained ankle is a serious logistical problem here).

## Air, dust, and the “mineral taste” problem
You described tasting bitterness after breathing. That suggests **fine particulates and/or alkaline salts**.

- Keep your respirator on whenever you’re moving.
- Avoid eating with dusty hands; use wipes.
- If you feel throat burn, gritty eyes, or a persistent cough: **leave, rinse, and reassess**. Don’t “push through” in a place with no urgent external threat—your body is the limiting factor.

Back at base, do a saline rinse for eyes/nose and shower as soon as practical.

## Heat management in a place with no “day/night”
Because the warmth is steady and the light never changes, you can accidentally cook yourself while feeling like you’re doing something gentle.

- Work/rest cycles: 25–45 minutes moving, then 10 minutes still.
- Unseal your jacket/vent layers during rests.
- If you start sweating heavily, you are spending water you can’t replace locally—slow down.
- Don’t camp directly on warm rock if you’re trying to sleep; insulate with a pad.

## Sleep and circadian sanity (if you stay longer than a few hours)
Permanent twilight and silence can scramble you.

Bring:
- A watch (or timer) you trust.
- A deliberate routine: “drink, note time, check instruments, eat, rest.”
- If sleeping: earplugs (optional—silence can be loud) and an eye mask (counterintuitive, but helpful if you use lamps).

The psychological hazard here is **time becoming syrup**. Structure is your lifeline.

## “Nothing circulates quickly” means your mistakes linger
- Dust you kick up will settle slowly.
- A poor navigation decision won’t be “fixed by following a stream” or “walking toward the sun.”
- A minor injury won’t be offset by finding water or help.

So:
- Make fewer actions, but make them deliberate.
- Keep your pack organized; don’t scatter items on the ground (they’ll vanish into tone-on-tone terrain).

## Biosecurity: treat the black-green smudges as alive (even if they barely are)
Those films could be lichen-like or microbial mats adapted to mineral dryness.

- Don’t touch them bare-handed.
- Don’t sample unless you have sterile containers and a plan.
- When you leave, **decontaminate**:
  - Bag outer layers.
  - Wipe down boots.
  - HEPA-vac or damp-wipe gear surfaces (dry brushing just re-aerosolizes).

This protects you and prevents you from exporting something extremely persistent into other worlds.

## Emergency planning (simple rules that keep you alive)
### If you feel unwell
- Stop, sit, drink.
- Check O₂/CO₂.
- If abnormal or symptoms persist: **follow your line out immediately**.

### If you get disoriented
- Don’t “try a new direction.”
- Freeze, breathe, instrument-check, then **return along the last known markers**.
- If you lose your markers: head for the rim-shelves—*but only if you can identify them with certainty*. Otherwise, stay put and signal.

### If a gas alarm triggers
Leave. Don’t investigate. Basins can become invisible traps.

### Signaling
Bring:
- A loud whistle (sound carries strangely in still air; sharp signals help).
- A beacon/strobe (visual reference matters in dim uniform light).
- A scheduled check-in system that does not rely on local infrastructure.

## How to mentally prepare (the part people skip)
Expect the basin to feel like it’s “finished”—as if it doesn’t want you, doesn’t notice you, and doesn’t change for you. That can trigger two bad impulses:

1) **Reverence that makes you passive** (“I’ll just stand here longer.”)  
2) **Boredom that makes you reckless** (“I’ll go see what’s over there.”)

Counter both with ritual:
- Every 30 minutes: drink, instrument-check, write one sentence, confirm exit heading.
- Keep your voice occasionally active (quiet self-talk) to anchor your sense of presence.
