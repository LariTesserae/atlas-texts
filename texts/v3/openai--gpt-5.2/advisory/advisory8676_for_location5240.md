## What you’re walking into (so you pack for the right problems)

This plateau is **warm, very humid, and permanently wet**, but not in the dramatic way of storms—more like everything is continuously **condensing, seeping, sweating**. The ground is **young volcanic rock**: sharp-edged basalt/andesite, clinker, fresh fractures. There are **many shallow waters**, slick algae films, saturated soils that cup your footprint, and **quiet** so deep you’ll hear your own movement as “loud.”

There are **no animals**, meaning:
- no bites, no swarms, no scavengers—good;
- but also no “natural” sound cues, no game trails, no insect activity to tell you where water is stagnant, and a different microbial/rot ecology—**mold and skin issues become your main biological enemy**.

The city is **stone-and-craft urban**, canal-heavy, gravity-driven, maintenance-centered. It will likely treat waterworks as sacred infrastructure. You won’t win arguments with weather here; you win by staying dry *inside your clothing system* while accepting that the outside world stays wet.

---

## Core hazards to prepare for

### 1) Constant damp + warm air = skin, gear, and morale failures
- **Trench foot / maceration** from constantly wet footwear.
- **Fungal rashes** (groin, toes, armpits), and slow-healing cuts.
- **Mold in bags**, leather, paper, food stores.
- **“Warm but clammy” hypothermia** is possible if you’re wet and winded at altitude (especially when you stop moving).

### 2) Footing and lacerations
- Basalt clinker and fresh fracture faces are **knife-sharp**.
- Algae + wet stone = **surprise skating rink**.
- Saturated flats hide **ankle-grabbing mud** and shallow channels.

### 3) Altitude without the usual sensory warning
Warm air can trick you into forgetting you may still be at **high elevation**:
- headaches, poor sleep, fatigue, bad decisions
- dehydration can still happen (you’ll sweat; it just won’t evaporate)

### 4) Navigation and “getting turned around”
Low cloud lid + featureless brightness + long terraces/canals can create:
- **flat-light disorientation**
- distances that look shorter than they are
- difficulty returning to a gate/anchor point in the open plateau

### 5) Social hazard: water infrastructure is the city’s bloodstream
You can offend people fast by:
- washing in a basin intended for settling/overflow
- stepping into canals
- contaminating runnels with soap, blood, food waste, or latrine runoff

## The clothing system that works here (don’t improvise this)

### Base layer (what touches skin)
- **Synthetic or merino** base layers that still insulate when damp.
- Bring **2–3 pairs** so you can rotate and keep one truly “sleep-dry.”

### Mid layer
- A **light fleece or wool** layer. You’ll want it when you stop moving or sit near stone that is constantly evaporatively cooling you.

### Shell
- Prioritize **breathability over absolute waterproofing**. In humidity, “waterproof” becomes “sweat-trap.”
- A good **wind shell** + a **light rain shell/poncho** (for drip zones and sustained condensation) is better than a heavy rain jacket you’ll stew in.

### Footwear (your single most important category)
Bring a **two-shoe strategy** if you can:
1) **Primary walkers:** sturdy boots or approach shoes with aggressive wet-rock grip.
2) **Camp/indoor shoes:** sandals or mesh shoes to let your feet dry.

Add:
- **Gaiters** (to keep saturated grit and algae off your socks)
- **6–8 pairs of socks** (thin liners + outer socks is ideal)
- **Foot powder + antifungal cream** (treat early, not after it blooms)

Rule on-site: **change socks midday** even if they don’t “feel” soaked. They are.

### Hands and head
- Thin **work gloves** (wet rope, stone edges, hauling, maintenance areas)
- A **brimmed cap** helps with constant drip and featureless glare.

## Shelter and sleep: you must manufacture “dry”

There are few trees; assume **ground sleep**.

Bring:
- A **bivy or small tent** with excellent ventilation (condensation is your enemy).
- A **groundsheet** plus a **thick sleeping pad** (keeps you off the wet-cold stone effect).
- A **dedicated dry-sleep bag liner** and a “sleep set” of clothing that never leaves the shelter.

Critical habit:  
**Never bring wet outer layers into your sleeping space.** Hang them in a vestibule or under an overhang where drip won’t land directly.

---

## Gear protection: treat humidity like sand

- Use **dry bags** inside your pack (double-bag anything critical).
- Pack **silica gel desiccant** or reusable desiccant packs for:
  - electronics
  - optics
  - medicines
  - paper / maps
- Prefer **sealed plastic or metal containers** over fabric pouches.
- If you carry batteries: humidity + corrosion are real; bring **contact cleaner** or at least dry wipes.

If you must bring a multiverse “anchor” device or return tool:  
Keep it in a **hard waterproof case** with desiccant and a tether—dropping something into a canal here is the kind of mistake that becomes permanent.

## Health kit tuned to this world

### Wound care (sharp rock world)
- Irrigation syringe + antiseptic
- Steri-strips, gauze, cohesive wrap
- A small tube of **skin barrier ointment** (prevents maceration turning into breaks)

### Antifungal + rash control (non-negotiable)
- Terbinafine or clotrimazole cream
- Zinc oxide barrier cream (groin/inner thigh, under pack straps)
- Hydrocortisone (sparingly, for irritation)

### Altitude and hydration
- Oral rehydration salts
- Ibuprofen/acetaminophen
- If you know you’re altitude-sensitive: consult beforehand for acetazolamide, and plan **2–3 easy days** on arrival.

### Respiratory
Warm damp air can still aggravate asthma/allergic responses:
- Inhaler if you use one
- Saline spray (constant moisture + microbes can irritate sinuses)

## Water, food, and fire: what changes here

### Water
There is water everywhere, but you should assume:
- algae mats may harbor irritants or microbial blooms
- canals may be “clean” culturally, but not necessarily potable

Bring:
- A **filter** capable of handling sediment/algae + a **chemical backup** (chlorine dioxide works well)
- A **wide-mouth bottle** for settling cloudy water before filtering

Cultural rule of thumb:  
**Never collect from a communal basin unless invited.** Better: ask for the designated draw point.

### Food
Humidity ruins staples.
- Pack foods in **sealed liners**.
- Prefer **oily, dense calories** (nuts, sealed bars, dried fish/meat if you can keep it sealed).
- Avoid paper packaging.

### Fire
Fire “has difficulty being itself” here.
- Bring a **reliable stove** if you’re self-sufficient.
- Use **waterproof fire starters** and assume local biomass is damp.
- If you’re trying to be low-tech: expect **smolder**, not flame, unless you have very dry resinous fuel.

## Movement and navigation in the plateau-city

### Footing discipline
- Walk like you’re on river rocks: **short steps, weight centered**.
- Use a **trekking pole** or staff; it’s as much for probing saturated ground as for balance.

### Route-finding tools
- **Compass** still works under cloud lid.
- **Bright survey tape** or chalk (sparingly and respectfully) for marking your own route outside the city.
- In the city: follow **canal hierarchies** (small runnels → broader channels → cistern courts). Learn their logic; it’s the local “street names.”

### Visibility and time
Featureless brightness erases shadows; you’ll lose the sense of hour.
- Keep a watch.
- Schedule check-ins by time, not “when the sun is overhead.”

## Social preparation: how to not become a problem

This city is built on maintenance and careful water direction. Present yourself as someone who understands systems.

### What to bring as gifts/trade (fits the tech level)
- Quality **steel needles**, awls, small cutting tools
- **Salt**, sealed spices, vinegar (fermentation-friendly world will value acidity)
- Tough **cordage** (hemp equivalents), waxed thread
- Waterproofing agents: **resin, pitch, oilcloth**, beeswax
- Durable cloth that handles damp better than plant fiber

### Etiquette assumptions that will keep you safe
- Ask before stepping onto **terrace edges, aqueducts, cistern rims**.
- Don’t wash wounds, clothes, or cookware in active channels.
- Keep latrine practices strict: **pack-out** if outside city; in-city, ask for the correct place. This is a water-respect culture.

### Communication reality
Sound doesn’t carry well; the world is quiet.
- Bring **visual signals** (small flag, reflective patch).
- When meeting people: speak calmly, don’t shout across wet plazas; approach close enough to be heard.

## Psychological prep (this matters more than people expect)

The absence of animals creates a subtle pressure:
- You will feel conspicuous—*everything you do is the loudest thing around*.
- The landscape can feel “complete but unwelcoming,” not hostile, just indifferent.

Countermeasures:
- Travel with a routine: foot care, dry-sleep ritual, daily gear airing.
- Build in “noise”: quiet music in earbuds if you use them, or deliberate human conversation if with a companion.
- Don’t wander far alone on day one; disorientation + eerie quiet is when people make mistakes.

## Arrival strategy (how an “ordinary” traveler should begin)

**First 24 hours**
1) Don’t push distance. Let your body register altitude.
2) Find where locals dry things (overhangs, ventilated racks, heated corners) and copy it.
3) Learn the water rules: where to draw, where to wash, where not to step.

**First week**
- Establish a foot-care schedule like it’s medicine.
- Choose a consistent route through the city using major basins/courts as landmarks.
- If you must explore the empty plateau: go out-and-back with time limits and visible markers; the landscape will not guide you home.
