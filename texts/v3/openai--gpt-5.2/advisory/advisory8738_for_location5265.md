## What you’re walking into (in practical terms)
This basin behaves less like “wetland with ruins” and more like a **humid, electrically chatty compost-machine** that happens to contain rails, vents, tanks, and a few still-working subsystems. The main risks aren’t dramatic predators; they’re:

- **Unreliable footing** (floating root-mat over water; sudden punch-throughs)
- **Constant wet + rot** (skin breakdown, trench foot, fungal exposure, infected cuts)
- **Spores and aerosols** (respiratory irritation; allergy/asthma triggers)
- **Localized electricity** (static discharge and low-current conduction through wet plants/metal)
- **Industrial leftovers** (sharp corroded edges; possible chemical residues; unknown gases from vents)
- **Navigation failure by sameness** (every corridor looks like the last; boundaries are “by feel”)

Preparation is mostly about **staying dry where it matters, insulating yourself, not getting lost, and not letting the place “claim” your gear**.

## Before you go (72–24 hours out)

### Medical and baseline readiness
- **Tetanus up to date** (non-negotiable: rust + punctures are likely).
- If you have asthma/allergies: bring your inhaler(s) and antihistamines; consider a steroid nasal spray if you know you react to mold.
- Cover existing cuts; trim nails; treat athlete’s foot beforehand. This place punishes small skin problems.

### Plan your mission like a dive, not a hike
Decide in advance:
- **Time in basin** (e.g., 2–4 hours) and a hard turnaround time.
- **Buddy system** (strongly recommended). Solo is how “knee-deep became chest-deep” turns fatal.
- **Extraction trigger conditions** (any one = leave): repeated mat punch-throughs, dizziness/headache near vents, uncontrolled shivering, gear soaked through, disorientation, or any electrical “bite” sensation.

### Choose the right day (if you can)
- Avoid **post-rain afternoons** if possible: steam/humidity peaks, mats get slicker, and spore load tends to climb.
- Early day can be better for visibility and cooler stress—though humid mornings are when conduction/static is most noticeable.

## Clothing: dress for wet contact and breathable protection

### On your body
- **Base layer:** synthetic or merino (no cotton). You will be wet; cotton will keep you cold and chafe you raw.
- **Legs:** quick-dry trousers plus **gaiters** (mud and spores love boot tops).
- **Outer:** lightweight shell or overshirt that resists snagging and dries fast. You want sleeves—leaves will constantly brush you.
- **Hands:** bring **two glove types**:
  - Thin nitrile gloves (under-layer for contamination/rot handling)
  - Insulating work gloves (over-layer) for grabbing metal, pushing vines, handling cables
- **Eyes:** clear safety glasses. Constant brushing + dripping + occasional crackle days = protect your eyes.
- **Respiratory:** **P100 (or FFP3) respirator**. This is the difference between “strange smell” and “two days of coughing up swamp.”
- **Head/neck:** brimmed cap + buff. Drips and plant oils will find your face.

### Feet: your most important system
Pick one approach and commit:

**Option A (recommended): knee-high rubber boots + neoprene socks**
- Best for constant wet and shallow water.
- Add a **thin liner sock** to reduce blistering.

**Option B: waterproof hiking boots + waterproof socks + gaiters**
- Better stability on root-mat, worse if water overtops your boot.
- If you go this route, assume you’ll eventually get wet anyway; bring spare socks in a dry bag.

Add:
- **Foot powder** and a small towel. Treat your feet immediately after exit.

## Core gear checklist (what actually matters here)

### Movement and safety
- **Two trekking poles** or one pole + one stout stick: probe every “dry-looking” patch.
- **15–30 m of bright, floating line** + a few carabiners:
  - Use as a buddy tether in sketchy zones
  - Use as a “handrail” back out when corridors start to look identical
- **Lightweight climbing sling/webbing**: quick anchors around tank legs, posts, or stout stems.
- **Compact inflatable (sit pad or small packraft/float pad)**:
  - Not for comfort—this is your “I fell through” flotation and a dry-ish platform for sorting gear.

### Atmosphere/industrial unknowns
- **4-gas detector** (O₂, CO, H₂S, LEL/CH₄).  
  The vents and under-mat pockets can hold unpleasant surprises; symptoms can be subtle until they aren’t.
- **Non-contact voltage tester** (optional but useful) if you expect to handle cables/metal.

### Navigation and marking
- **Primary navigation:** offline map + compass.
- **Secondary:** GPS/phone in a hard waterproof case (expect condensation; add silica gel).
- **Marking:** do *not* rely on biodegradable tape (it will sag, peel, and become part of the place). Use:
  - Bright synthetic cord loops you can retrieve
  - Chalk marker for metal/concrete (temporary, visible)
  - If you must flag, use **short, removable tags** and collect them on exit

### Lighting and electronics protection
- Headlamp (even in “normal day,” the green tunnels eat contrast).
- Dry bags inside your pack (double-bag critical items).
- Silica gel packets everywhere; wipe condensation often.
- Keep batteries warm-ish and dry; humidity kills contacts—bring a tiny contact-cleaning cloth.

### First aid tailored to this basin
- Wound wash + antiseptic
- Steri-strips / butterfly closures (cuts from hidden metal happen fast)
- Anti-fungal cream (clotrimazole) and zinc oxide (skin barrier)
- Blister kit
- Electrolytes (humidity dehydrates quietly)
- Emergency thermal layer (if you get soaked, you can still get chilled)

### Food and water
- Carry **all drinking water in**. Do not filter basin water unless you have a robust setup and no alternative; it’s a stew of rot, metal, and unknown residues.
- Bring salty snacks. Sweat is constant even when you don’t feel hot.

## Special handling rules for this place (learn these before entry)

### 1) The root-mat lies
- Step only where you’ve **probed**.
- Watch for “too perfect” vegetation carpets—often thin over open water.
- Treat every glossy patch as a potential punch-through.

**Technique:** pole-probe, then weight-shift gradually. If it gives, back off; don’t “test” with a full step.

### 2) Don’t complete circuits with your body
On crackle days, you can get unpleasant surprises by becoming the bridge between:
- wet leaf surface ↔ exposed metal
- cable/vine bundles ↔ grounded rail/concrete

**Rules:**
- One-hand contact when touching suspicious metal (reduces current path across chest).
- Gloves on; avoid bare forearms.
- Don’t lean your shoulder/hip on tank walls while grabbing a cable.

### 3) Assume every metal edge is a blade
Corroded sheet and rail can be razor-sharp under moss films.
- If you must climb over metal, do it slowly, with gloves, and with a spotter.

### 4) Treat vents as “small caves”
Even if they’re open air, they can exhale concentrated gases.
- Approach from the side, not directly above.
- If you smell “eggy,” solvent-like, or get a sudden headache: **leave that pocket immediately**.

### 5) Never leave gear un-tethered
“Rule of adhesion” in practice: straps snag, vines wrap, mats swallow.
- Anything you set down should be clipped to you or to a line.
- If you cache, cache **outside** the wet boundary on firmer ground.

## How to enter and move without getting trapped

### Establish a dry boundary base
At the point where your boots stop sinking:
- Drop a **base marker** (bright cord around a sapling/post, GPS point, compass bearing back).
- Pack stays closed; electronics stay sealed.
- Do a buddy gear check: respirators seated, gloves accessible, line ready.

### Use a “spoke and hub” exploration pattern
Because everything looks like green architecture:
1. From the base, run a **handline** inward (or mark every major turn).
2. Go in short legs (10–15 minutes), then return to the line.
3. Only push deeper after you’ve proven a reliable path.

### Keep your profile narrow
The basin is tactile; you’ll be brushed constantly.
- Pack should be slim; dangling straps will snag and pull you off balance.
- Consider taping loose strap ends.

## Camping is usually a bad idea here
If you *must* overnight:
- Do it **outside** the wet boundary on the first reliably firm rise.
- In the basin itself, you’re choosing between:
  - condensation soaking everything,
  - insects near pools,
  - and waking up colder than expected because you never truly dried.

If you insist on an internal bivy:
- Use a suspended hammock only if you find trustworthy anchors (rare and risky among fused stems and slick surfaces).
- Otherwise, bring an insulated pad and accept you’ll be damp.

## Interacting with the “core” (tanks/silos) without paying for it
The core is where risk stacks: slick verticals, cables, hum, vents, and the most aggressive plant-geometry behavior.

Protocol:
1. Gas-check before you commit.
2. Put on insulating gloves and eye protection.
3. No climbing higher than you can safely drop from into *known* footing (which is almost never).
4. Touch-test with the back of a gloved hand; if you feel buzzing/vibration, don’t grab harder—back off.
5. If you hear a change in rhythm (pump tempo shift, vent sigh pattern changes), treat it as “system responding”: step away and reassess.

## Decontamination and aftercare (do not skip)
This place follows you home via spores, damp fabric, and tiny skin breaks.

Immediately after exit:
- Remove boots last; keep “dirty” items separated.
- Wash hands/forearms; rinse eyes if they feel gritty.
- Dry feet completely; treat hot spots; change socks.
- Clean cuts aggressively.

Within 24 hours:
- Shower thoroughly; launder clothing hot if fabric allows.
- Clean and dry all gear; open cases and remove batteries.
- Watch for: persistent cough, fever, rash, red streaks from cuts, worsening foot pain—seek care early.

## Mindset: what makes people fail here
- **Rushing** (“it’s just a small basin”)—small places still kill.
- **Assuming dryness exists**—it’s always “about to be water.”
- **Letting the corridors hypnotize you**—green tunnels compress distance and scramble your sense of direction.
- **Handling metal casually**—slick + sharp + conductive is a nasty trio.
