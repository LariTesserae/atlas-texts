## What you’re walking into (in practical terms)
This plateau is **high-altitude, extremely arid, abrasive, and ecologically busy**—but quiet. Your main risks won’t be storms or getting wet; they’ll be:

- **Hypoxia + dehydration** (you won’t feel sweaty; you’ll still desiccate).
- **Abrasive dust/salts** (eyes, lungs, zippers, skin, electronics, joints).
- **Silent, densely inhabited terrain** (you can blunder into herds, nests, or predators without the usual warning cues).
- **Topological/metric weirdness** (“fold-grounds,” distance not closing, loops, duplication of landmarks, misaligned “down”).
- **Hard-to-audit navigation** (features remain crisp but refuse to get closer; sound cues are muted).

Plan as if you’re traveling in a cold high desert **plus** a landscape that occasionally cheats at geometry.

## Before you go: the non-negotiables

### 1) Get your body ready for thin air
If you can choose entry altitude/gradient, do.

- **Acclimatization plan:** 24–72 hours “low activity” on arrival if possible.
- **Monitor:** pulse oximeter + a written decision threshold (e.g., if SpO₂ stays <88% at rest, stop ascending / use O₂ / retreat).
- **Med kit additions (common high-altitude kit):**
  - Acetazolamide (if you tolerate it; trial it beforehand)
  - Ibuprofen/acetaminophen
  - Antiemetic
  - Dexamethasone (only if trained/comfortable; emergency AMS/HACE tool)
- **Oxygen strategy:** For an “ordinary human,” bring *some* oxygen redundancy:
  - Small bottle for emergencies **or** portable concentrator if power budget allows.
  - Assume exertion will feel “unfair” and errors compound fast.

### 2) Water: assume none is harvestable
Treat local “vegetation” and dust-hoarding columns as **not a water source**.

- **Carry all water** or bring a closed-loop recovery system:
  - Minimum planning number: **4–6 L/day** (cool temps reduce sweat, but thin air + dryness increases respiratory loss).
  - Add **electrolytes**; salt exposure and dry air can trick you into under-drinking.
- **Humidity care:** sterile-dry air leads to cracked lips, nosebleeds, cough.
  - Saline nasal gel/spray, lip balm, heavy moisturizer.

### 3) Dust management is life support
This place is basically a sandblaster running for millennia.

- **Respiratory:** P100/FFP3-class filtration (and spares). A half-face respirator is ideal.
- **Eyes:** sealed goggles (not just sunglasses).
- **Skin:** gaiters, tightly woven outer layer, gloves; nothing “breathable-mesh.”
- **Gear sealing:** cover zippers, tape seams, dry-lube for moving parts, spare zipper pulls.
- **Electronics:** hard cases + dust caps; clean with brush/air bulb, not wet wipes (salts smear).

## Packing list (optimized for this plateau)

### Clothing & personal protection
- Layering for **stable cool**: base + mid + wind shell (the wind is “persistent,” so windproof matters more than insulated bulk).
- Wide-brim hat **or** helmet + goggles (ravines + polished stone + falls).
- Durable gloves (abrasion) + thin liner gloves (dexterity in cold).
- Gaiters + dust-resistant boots; consider **wide-footprint overboots/sand-snow “shoes”** for dust drifts that behave solid until they don’t.

### Shelter & sleep
- Low-profile tent that tolerates constant wind; bring:
  - **Rock anchors** (webbing/cord for deadman anchors under stone plates)
  - Extra guylines
- Sleeping system for “cold but not plunging” nights:
  - A reliable pad (ground is hard, and heat loss to stone is real)
  - Bag/quilt rated below expected low + windproof bivy if needed

### Food & cooking
- High-calorie, low-prep, low-crumb foods (crumbs become dust-lures).
- Cooking: choose a stove that works in thin air and wind; bring windscreen.
- Don’t rely on open fires (fuel scarcity + wind + unwanted attention).

### Medical & hygiene (dry-world specific)
- Blister care (abrasive grit + long walking).
- Saline nasal gel, eye rinse ampoules (small), lip balm, heavy barrier cream.
- Waste plan: bags/containers. **Nothing decomposes here**; your traces persist.

### Tools
- Rope (static line) + harness or simple seat harness: ravines open “without warning.”
- Trekking pole(s): probe dust drifts and edges.
- Small hammer/steel rod: for testing rock resonance/firmness near voids (also useful for intentional vibration signals—see etiquette).
- Repair kit: needle + heavy thread, tape, zip ties, spare buckles.

### Power
Weather is stable, light is consistent: **solar is excellent**.
- Solar panel + battery, but seal connectors against dust.
- A tiny wind turbine can work too (wind persists), but it’s another moving part to clog—only if you can maintain it.

## Navigation: treat the map as adversarial

### Core rule: never trust a single navigation channel
Distance can lie; landmarks can duplicate; “down” can subtly change.

Use **three concurrent methods**, and require 2-of-3 agreement before committing:

1. **Dead reckoning:** pace count + time + heading
2. **Instrument:** GPS/IMU if it works in this universe (assume occasional nonsense)
3. **Physical control:** line-of-sight anchors, tethers, and “audit marks”

### Practical techniques that match local logic
- **Paired markers (local best practice):** build cairns in pairs *with a deliberate asymmetry*:
  - Example: one cairn has a flat “capstone,” the other has two thin plates.
  - If one becomes two, you can detect the liar.
- **Timestamped marks:** scratch a unique symbol + date/time on a hidden side of a marker stone. If it “reappears” ahead, you know you looped.
- **Tether discipline in fold-grounds:**
  - If you must cross a suspect basin, run a **continuous line** anchored to heavy stone every 20–50 m.
  - Keep the line under slight tension; if geometry tries to “add meters,” you’ll feel it as slack that shouldn’t exist.
- **No casual throwing. Ever.**
  - If thrown objects can return from impossible directions, treat every toss as a potential boomerang with malice.
  - Secure tools with lanyards. Don’t toss rocks to test depth—use rope/poles instead.

### How to identify “fold-ground” before it gets you
You likely can’t prove it, but you can screen for it:

- **Approach test:** walk toward a landmark for a fixed interval (e.g., 10 minutes), then stop and check:
  - Does angular size change appropriately?
  - Do parallax relationships between nearer stones shift?
  - If not, assume a metric anomaly and reroute.
- **Slope sanity check:** if “down” feels different across a crack, stop. Back up. Re-approach from another angle.
- **Duplication cues:** faint double ridgelines, extra standing stone, mirage-like “edited” edges without heat shimmer.

## Movement protocol (how to not get eaten, lost, or exhausted)

### Walk like you’re in a crowded city, not empty wilderness
It’s open—but occupied.

- Move **predictably**. Don’t sprint; don’t zigzag through herd corridors.
- Stop often, scan slowly. Silence will not warn you of nearby bodies.
- Assume ravine lips can be concealed by dust: probe with a pole before stepping onto pale crust.

### Don’t camp where the ecosystem concentrates
Avoid:
- Ravine walls (nest colonies; rockfall; you may be “tapping” your presence through the stone)
- Basin centers (fold-grounds more likely; mirage-editing)
- Animal routes (they move like “slow weather” and will not apologize)

Prefer:
- The lee of resistant domes (stable windbreak)
- Slight rises with long visibility
- Places with existing human traces **only if** you can interpret them (cairns/paired markers are meaningful here)

## Wildlife & “presence” safety

### Herd animals
- Give wide berth; don’t cut between juveniles and adults.
- Don’t approach from directly downwind if scent matters; but don’t assume wind direction is “felt”—it’s persistent and can mask your approach.

### Predators
Predation is quick; drama-free doesn’t mean safe.

- Keep food sealed; keep a clean camp.
- If you see scavenger “layers” forming, you’re near a kill or a habitual feeding zone—move on.

### Cliff-nesters / vibration communicators
They tap messages through rock. You don’t want to accidentally “say” something.

- Avoid hammering/tapping on ravine walls unless necessary.
- If you must signal a partner via stone taps, use a **consistent, simple pattern** and keep it localized (tap on a portable stone, not the cliff face).

## Camp discipline in a world that doesn’t erase
“Nothing rots here” means:

- Your waste, crumbs, and gear fragments become **long-term artifacts**.
- You can unintentionally create lure-sites or alter travel logic for locals.

Camp rules:
- Pack out everything.
- Don’t bury organics expecting disappearance.
- Don’t leave “helpful” new cairns unless you follow the local paired-marker convention; a single cairn can become a trap when duplication begins.

Night:
- Light fades without color; stars don’t twinkle; cold “sets” and holds.
- Set trip-lines/visual reflectors around camp because you won’t hear approach well.
- Eye moisture drops at night: keep goggles/eye drops accessible.

## Anomaly response (what to do when the plateau edits itself)

### If distance won’t close
- Stop chasing.
- Pick a nearer intermediate anchor (a distinctive ventifact, a dome, a crack system).
- Re-route in **angles**, not straight lines—triangulate rather than “beeline.”

### If you suspect a loop
- Don’t “push through.” That’s how you burn water and oxygen.
- Backtrack to last confirmed anchor (timestamp mark).
- Change a variable decisively: new heading by 30–60°, or travel along a hard boundary (escarpment edge) where geometry has fewer degrees of freedom.

### If a thrown/loose object returns or appears ahead
- Immediately tether all critical items.
- Treat the area as active fold-ground; withdraw on your tether/anchors.

### If “down” changes across a seam
- Sit. Hydrate. Check SpO₂. Fatigue mimics unreality.
- If you re-confirm it’s not just dizziness: mark the seam, do not cross again, and do not camp nearby.

## Interacting with locals (if you find them)
They will read you by whether you respect rules they consider obvious.

- Ask about:
  - Safe routes (often encoded in paired cairns)
  - Fold-ground boundaries (they may have names and taboos)
  - “Do not” behaviors (throwing stones is a big one here)
- Trade goods that matter in a waterless, abrasive world:
  - Cordage, needles, tough cloth, sealable containers, dust filters, spare blades
- Don’t treat their “superstitions” as optional; in this environment, taboo often equals field-tested survival protocol.

## A realistic minimum plan for an “ordinary human” visit (3–7 days)
1. **Arrive with oxygen assist available**, but try to acclimate rather than brute-force.
2. Carry **full water + electrolytes**, rationed with a hard rule (never “make up for it later”).
3. Use **paired cairn markers** for every major decision point you create.
4. Avoid basins with mirage-editing; travel by **domes, escarpments, and hard edges**.
5. Camp on a lee slope, off animal corridors, with reflectors and a clean perimeter.
6. If navigation starts feeling “argumentative,” retreat early—this is a place where the map wins by attrition.
