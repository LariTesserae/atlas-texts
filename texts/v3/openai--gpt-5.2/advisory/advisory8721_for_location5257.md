## What you’re walking into (so you pack the right “self”)
This basin-city is not hostile in the cinematic sense; it’s hostile in the *ergonomic* sense. It has been engineered to keep its own sound, moisture, and growth inside controlled “rooms.” Most of the risk comes from small mismatches between your instincts and the city’s rules:

- **Acoustics don’t behave:** sound is delayed, damped, and locally sculpted. You cannot rely on calling, echoes, traffic noise, or “I’ll follow the loud thing.”
- **Light is reluctant:** dim, pewter sky; surfaces that eat illumination; contrast is weird; distance reading is unreliable.
- **Weather is zoned:** you’ll step from dry heat to sweating humidity to localized warm rain with hard boundaries.
- **Ground and structures are semi-active:** surfaces tune grip, façades “breathe,” street furniture moves, materials heal.
- **Some phenomena are cognitively hazardous:** the “memory canal” reflection lag/overshoot is a classic nausea/disorientation trigger; the column-array seems to suppress perception/effort.
- **Life is curated:** fewer animals, fewer people in the open, more automation and indirect presence. You may be treated as a variable to be managed rather than a guest to be greeted.

Prepare like you’re visiting a quiet, humid, low-light, semi-living piece of infrastructure with pockets of mild physics weirdness.

## Pre-departure conditioning (do this before you pack)
### Train your navigation without sound
- Practice moving in **near-silence**: no audio cues, no “follow the crowd,” no audible intersections.
- Get comfortable with **hand signals**, **light signals**, and **haptic cues** (vibration prompts) for team communication.

### Acclimate to warm humidity + intermittent wetting
- If you’re prone to heat rash, fungal issues, or asthma, treat this as a **tropical city** with **industrial air**.
- Bring and pre-test an **anti-chafe system** (barrier cream + clothing that doesn’t hold wet seams).

### Motion-sickness + dissociation hardening
- The canal and certain “threshold” installations can provoke **vertigo, derealization, and nausea**.
- If you’re susceptible: start a **meclizine/scopolamine plan** (if medically appropriate) and rehearse grounding techniques (fixed-point gaze, breath counting, tactile anchor).

## Core hazards and how to beat them

### 1) “The air doesn’t carry” (acoustic isolation & delay)
**Problems:**
- You won’t hear vehicles/rigs until late (or at all).
- You can lose teammates at 30–50 m with no auditory feedback.
- Your own footsteps/voice won’t reassure you; that increases anxiety and bad decisions.

**Mitigations:**
- **Line-of-sight signaling kit:**  
  - Narrow-beam headlamp + secondary chest light (low lumen, wide flood for close work).  
  - Small IR strobe (for teammates with IR viewers).  
- **Mesh comms:** radios will likely work better than sound *if* EM isn’t being similarly sculpted. Bring:
  - 2-way radio + spare battery
  - phone-to-phone mesh app device (or dedicated mesh nodes)
  - a “dead simple” backup: laminated signal card + whistle (a whistle may still fail—but it’s tiny and worth the grams)
- **Personal locator beacon** (even for a day walk) and a habit: “stop, signal, wait” instead of “call and walk.”

### 2) Dim light + matte, light-eating materials (visual fatigue & missteps)
**Problems:**
- Depth perception degrades; matte surfaces erase cues.
- A wet film on bridges/handrails can make edges disappear.
- Long corridors + haze can cause distance compression (“that’s closer than it is”).

**Mitigations:**
- **Eye strategy:** clear lenses (not dark sunglasses) with hydrophobic coating. Consider *very* light amber/yellow only if it improves your contrast without dimming.
- **Lighting strategy:** avoid one powerful beam; use **two-layer light**:
  - low flood for ground texture
  - narrow spot for signage/edges at distance
- **Footwork:** treat every surface like it *might* be wet-polished. Slow down at transitions between districts (dry→humid→rain).

### 3) Zoned humidity, localized warm rain, oily-feeling droplets (skin & chemical unknowns)
**Problems:**
- You’ll be repeatedly wet/dry cycled, which destroys skin and feet.
- Warm “oily” rain may be benign condensation chemistry—or a contaminant.
- Condensation under bridges creates slick undersides and dripping surprises.

**Mitigations:**
- **Clothing:**  
  - Base: moisture-wicking, anti-odor (merino or treated synthetic)  
  - Mid: thin breathable layer you can vent quickly  
  - Shell: *not* a heavy raincoat—use a light, fast-drying wind shell + compact poncho for localized downpours  
- **Skin:** barrier cream (feet, inner thighs, under straps).  
- **Sampling & sanity:** bring pH strips + a tiny conductivity meter if you have one. If rain repeatedly tests “off,” treat it as **do-not-ingest** and minimize skin exposure (poncho up, sleeves down, rinse later).

### 4) Young printed/engineered terrain (crumbly seams, sharp edges, sudden traction changes)
**Problems:**
- “Printed soil” can crumble unexpectedly where water finds seams.
- Crisp terrace edges and channels mean more trip and fall lines.
- Adaptive microtextures may change under you (helpful, but startling).

**Mitigations:**
- **Footwear:** trail shoes or light boots with aggressive wet grip; avoid smooth urban soles.  
- **Trekking pole** (single is enough) for probing edges and stability on terraces.  
- **Gloves:** thin grippy gloves for handrails that may be wet-filmed.

### 5) Semi-living architecture (moving edges, responsive furniture, self-healing skins)
**Problems:**
- Things can “politely” reposition around you, including benches, bollards, planters.
- Doors may open for someone you never see; you may trigger systems unintentionally.
- Harvesting films on walls may be slippery, allergenic, or delicate infrastructure.

**Mitigations:**
- **Assume everything is sensorized.** Move deliberately; don’t sprint; don’t hide; don’t climb.
- **Don’t touch translucent wall films** or peel anything “skin-like.” If you must lean, use your own gear (e.g., lean on your pack, not the wall).
- **Give moving objects room**—treat street furniture like slow machinery.

### 6) The canal that shows “memory” (cognitive hazard)
**Problems:**
- Delayed reflections can induce motion sickness and fixation.
- Staring can create the urge to “solve” it, which is how people lose time and situational awareness.

**Mitigations:**
- Use the same rule divers use for disorienting visuals: **no long looks**.  
  - If you must cross near it, keep your gaze on fixed architecture, not the surface.  
- If you feel nausea: sit, hydrate, eyes on a matte stationary object, slow breathing.

### 7) The column array that deepens dimness / quiets perception (threshold effect)
**Problems:**
- It may damp sensory effort, attention, or even pressure regulation in the ear.
- The danger is not immediate harm—it’s **consent drift**: you wander through and forget why you’re here.

**Mitigations:**
- Before entering any “installation corridor,” do a **checkpoint ritual**:
  - confirm heading
  - confirm time
  - confirm next meet point
- Cross as a pair, spaced 2–3 m, maintaining visual on each other.  
- If ear pressure shifts sharply: stop, swallow/yawn, back out slowly.

### 8) Metallic/ozonic air, faint electrical taste (possible high fields or chemistry)
**Problems:**
- Could be harmless (ionization, materials) or could indicate EM activity in conduits.
- Can irritate lungs.

**Mitigations:**
- Bring **an N95/FFP2** mask (not for dust alone—also for irritation events).  
- If you have the kit: small **E-field/EMF meter** and a basic **ozone badge**.  
- Avoid lingering under elevated conduits where taste/tingle is strongest.

## Packing list (optimized for this city)

### Wear
- Breathable long pants (fast-dry) + long-sleeve top (for humidity + plant contact)
- Trail shoes/light boots with wet traction
- Thin liner socks + spare socks (2 pairs minimum; 3 if you’ll be out all day)
- Light gloves (grippy)
- Clear protective eyewear (hydrophobic coating recommended)
- Cap with brim (keeps warm rain off face; helps in dim glareless light)

### Carry (day pack)
**Navigation & comms**
- Map you can read offline + compass (even if you expect GPS)
- GPS device or phone with offline maps
- 2-way radio or mesh-capable communicator
- Small IR/visible strobe + spare batteries
- Chalk/marker *only if permitted*—better: removable reflective tape tabs for your own gear, not the city

**Light**
- Headlamp (flood + spot modes)
- Secondary clip light (backup; ideally warm tint for contrast)
- Spare batteries/power bank

**Weather & moisture**
- Compact poncho or ultralight rain shell
- Small pack towel
- Zip bags/dry bag for electronics (localized downpour means sudden saturation)

**Health**
- Water + electrolytes (humidity tricks you; you still dehydrate)
- Anti-nausea meds if prone
- Barrier cream / anti-chafe stick
- Blister kit (hydrocolloid + tape)
- N95/FFP2 mask
- Antihistamine (plants + wall films + humidity = surprise reactions)

**Sensing (optional but highly useful here)**
- Pocket thermometer/hygrometer (helps you predict zone changes)
- pH strips (rain/canal curiosity management)
- Small notebook: log “zone boundaries,” conduit hotspots, threshold arrays

**Emergency**
- Basic first aid
- Mylar blanket (warm place, but shock is shock)
- Small multi-tool (don’t use it on the city—use it on your gear)
- Locator beacon if you can

## Behavioral protocols (how to not become a problem the city must “solve”)

### Move like you are in a hospital crossed with a greenhouse
- Quiet is engineered; don’t “test” it by shouting or banging.
- Don’t force doors, panels, or skins. If it doesn’t open normally, assume it’s not for you.

### Treat vegetation as deliberate infrastructure
- Those lush corridors are not abandoned lots; they’re functioning environmental organs.
- Don’t cut through black-substrate root meshes or step into planted depressions (they may be drainage lungs, not parks).

### Don’t improvise with the anomalies
- No stones into the canal, no “let’s see what happens if…”
- If you find hovering canopy-trees: enjoy from the edge. The field/scaffold (if that’s what it is) may have exclusion zones.

### Expect indirect human presence
- You may never see residents, but you may be constantly monitored.
- If signage exists, follow it. If it doesn’t, follow **desire lines** only if they look intentionally maintained; otherwise stick to hard corridors.

## Timing, rest, and psychological hygiene
- The dimness and hush will make you underestimate time and overestimate safety. Use a **timer**:
  - every 30–45 min: drink, check bearings, check feet, check teammate
- Plan for **decision fatigue**. The city’s subtle motion and boundary-weather cause constant micro-decisions.
- Build in a “bright break”: a known higher-reflectance district or a sheltered spot where you can reset your eyes and vestibular system.

## Field rules of thumb (simple heuristics that work here)
1. **If it’s wetter, it’s slipperier and more sensorized.** Slow down.
2. **If you can’t hear it, it can still hit you.** Look farther ahead than you’re used to.
3. **If the environment starts feeling ‘too calm,’ checkpoint immediately.** That’s when you drift.
4. **If the city seems to anticipate you, let it.** Don’t fight alignments, reroutes, or silent cues.
5. **If running water sounds “illicit,” don’t chase it alone.** That’s exactly how people vanish into the thinning outskirts.

## Arrival plan (a practical first hour)
1. **Stand still for 2 minutes** at entry: note wind, humidity, any metallic taste, where condensation collects.
2. **Establish comms and visual IDs** with your party (lights, strobes, hand signals).
3. **Pick a high-contrast landmark** (a tower punctuation mark, a conduit junction, a vegetation swath edge).
4. **Walk one district boundary on purpose** (dry→humid or humid→rain) to learn the city’s transitions before you commit deeper.
5. **Set a hard turnaround time**. This place doesn’t “end,” it thins—and thinning is where orientation dies.
