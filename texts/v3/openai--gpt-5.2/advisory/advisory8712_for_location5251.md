## What this place is *to a human body*
Treat the pocket as three environments stacked together:

1. **High-altitude desert** (thin air, intense UV, rapid dehydration, big day–night swings).
2. **Electro-thermal industrial landscape** (charged dust, hot radiant stone, vents with reactive gases, conductive/semiconductive minerals, periodic vibration).
3. **Low-grade reality drift** (distance/effort mismatches, brief visual doubling, inconsistent sound propagation, “lagging” shadows).

None of these individually is exotic; together they make “ordinary hiking mistakes” much more expensive.

---

## Pre-trip preparation (before you even look for the seam)

### Medical / physiology
- **Acclimatize** as if you’re going above 3,500–4,500 m. Arrive a day or two early at a lower camp if you can.
- **Altitude meds** (if you tolerate them): acetazolamide; know your contraindications.
- **Hydration plan**: you will lose water faster than you think because the air is thin, hot, and *extremely* dry. Budget **1.5–2×** your normal desert carry.
- **Electrolytes**: the dryness + heat + exertion will salt-strip you even without sweat you can feel.
- **Eye/nose/lip protection** is not optional: the place “discourages” moisture.
  - Preservative-free eye drops, saline nasal gel, high-SPF lip balm, skin barrier ointment.

### Fitness and movement
- Train for **slow climbing while slightly hypoxic**. The pocket punishes rushing because distance and effort aren’t perfectly coupled.

### Mindset
- Expect mild perceptual unreliability. Agree with yourself *now*:
  - You will not “prove” anything by taking risks.
  - If something looks easy but feels wrong, you back off.
  - You will leave with the same number of holes you entered with.

## Core hazards and how to mitigate them

### 1) Thin air + “bright heat”
**Problem:** You can overheat in full sun while also drying out invisibly; shadows don’t cool the way your instincts expect.

**Mitigation:**
- Wear **high-albedo, vented, UPF-rated clothing**; wide-brim hat + neck protection.
- Bring a **reflective shade tarp** (mylar or silnylon with reflective face). Create your own “real shade.”
- Schedule movement in **short work blocks** with timer-enforced water breaks (don’t wait for thirst).
- Plan for **cold nights** anyway: the pocket is dry and high; radiative cooling after sunset can be brutal.

### 2) Charged dust, static, and field-aligned vents
**Problem:** The crystal mats and vents imply strong local electrostatic/EM gradients. That can:
- Zap sensitive electronics
- Increase ignition risk for solvents/resins
- Cause painful micro-discharges, tingling, headaches in susceptible people

**Mitigation (personal):**
- Wear **ESD-dissipative gloves** or thin liner gloves under work gloves.
- Footwear: **puncture-resistant sole + ESD-dissipative** if possible (or add an ESD heel strap when on flatter ground).
- Avoid kneeling/lying directly on crystal mats or near vent lips.

**Mitigation (gear):**
- Store electronics in a **Faraday pouch** when not actively used.
- Prefer **simple, rugged devices** over “smart” ones.
- Carry **analog backups** (paper notes, mechanical watch), but don’t rely on a compass—local fields may lie.

### 3) Hot, dry vents with “heated stone/metal” odor + ozone note
**Problem:** Could include ozone, nitrogen oxides, sulfur compounds, or things you don’t have names for.

**Mitigation:**
- Bring a **half-face respirator** with **P100 + acid gas/organic vapor** cartridges (or a powered respirator if you’re acclimatized to it). Use it near vents and in still air pockets.
- Carry a **multi-gas meter** (O₂, CO, H₂S, VOC) plus an **ozone indicator** if you can. Even if it can’t identify everything, it tells you when the air is changing.
- Rule: **never camp downwind of a vent** even if it “smells clean.” Thin air hides irritation until it’s serious.

### 4) “Young” sharp geology + ceramic plates + metal veins
**Problem:** Lacerations, shattered rock under load, unexpected conductivity, and edges that behave like broken glass.

**Mitigation:**
- **Approach like scree** even when it looks solid. Test every plate with a pole before committing weight.
- Wear **cut-resistant gloves** and long pants.
- Carry **serious wound care**: irrigation syringe, steri-strips, skin glue, hemostatic gauze, pressure wrap. Dry environments turn small cuts into persistent problems.

### 5) Space/cause “rounding errors”
**Problem:** You can misjudge distance, end up somewhere “too fast,” or take too long to reach a near object—classic setup for falls, getting cliffed out, or missing the exit seam.

**Mitigation (navigation):**
- Use a **physical guideline** system:
  - At the seam/entrance: place a **high-visibility marker** plus a **backup** (two different shapes/colors).
  - Run a **thin, bright line spool** (e.g., dyneema throwline) from the entrance into the pocket as your “truth cord.”
- Don’t trust GPS. If you use it, treat it as *one sensor*, not authority.
- Mark route with **reflective tape tabs** at eye level on stable features (but don’t put tape on living growths).
- Move on a **three-point rule** near edges: two feet + one hand/pole in contact, especially when perception feels “floaty.”

**Mitigation (time):**
- Set a **hard turnaround time** that’s earlier than you think you need. Reality drift often shows up most when you’re tired.

### 6) The “machine pulse” underfoot
**Problem:** Low-frequency vibration can loosen stacked plates, shift filaments, and fatigue you neurologically (sub-audible vibration can cause nausea/headache in some people).

**Mitigation:**
- Choose rest spots on **monolithic blocks** rather than laminated “sheet” strata.
- If you notice headache/irritability rising, relocate—don’t argue with it.

## Packing list (human-scale, practical)

### Clothing / personal
- UPF shirt + long pants, wide-brim hat, neck gaiter
- Glacier-style sunglasses + **sealed goggles** (fine mineral flour is abrasive)
- Lip balm SPF, skin barrier ointment, eye drops, nasal gel
- Thin liner gloves (ESD/dexterity) + durable over-gloves
- Boots: puncture-resistant soles; optional ESD heel strap
- Lightweight insulated jacket + warm hat for night

### Life support
- Water capacity for **at least 24 hours longer** than planned (bladder + hard bottles)
- Electrolytes + high-calorie dry food (nothing here is edible)
- Emergency shelter + reflective shade tarp
- If you’re pushing altitude: **portable oxygen** or an emergency canister

### Navigation / comms
- Paper map sheet you make yourself (hand sketch as you go) + pencil
- Bright line spool (route cord) + reflective tabs
- Headlamp with extra batteries (even in “normal daylight,” you may need hands-free light in cracks)
- If comms matter: bring two systems (e.g., satellite messenger + VHF). Assume one will misbehave.

### Tools / safety
- Trekking pole(s) for probing plates and judging “false near/far”
- Rope + a few slings/carabiners if there are ledges (keep metal minimal but reliable)
- First aid kit emphasized for:
  - dehydration/heat illness
  - altitude sickness
  - lacerations/abrasions
  - eye irrigation
- Respirator + spare cartridges; multi-gas meter if available
- Faraday pouch for electronics; zip bags for dust isolation

### “Do not bring if avoidable”
- Loose metallic jewelry (conductive, catches on sharp rock)
- Highly spark-prone tools near vents (unshielded steel-on-stone striking)
- Perfumes/volatile solvents (the place already has reactive chemistry)

## On-site protocol (how to behave once you find the seam)

### 1) Set an external cache *before* entering
- Leave extra water, food, warm layer, and a note at the approach side.
- Photograph/sketch the seam area from multiple angles.
- Tie your guideline to something that will not move (a deep crack anchor or massive block).

### 2) Enter slowly and “calibrate”
Spend the first 20–30 minutes doing nothing ambitious:
- Walk 20 steps out, 20 back. Check if return distance “matches.”
- Listen for sound oddities (your own steps, your voice).
- Test your electronics: turn one device on briefly, then off. Don’t leave everything powered.

### 3) Establish a safe rest zone
Pick a spot that is:
- away from vents (no odor, no warmth plume, no hair-raising static)
- not on crystal mats
- not on laminated plates that ring or flex
- visible from the entrance marker if possible

Erect shade. Drink. Log baseline symptoms (headache, nausea, tingling, unusual calm/agitation).

### 4) Movement rules inside the pocket
- **One new variable at a time**: don’t combine climbing + vent exploration + fatigue.
- If you see **double-exposure motion** in reeds or your own hands, treat it like “slippery rock”: slow down, widen stance, shorten steps.
- Avoid stepping over/under mineral “cables.” Assume they can be under tension or conductive.

### 5) Interacting with the life-forms (minimize consequences)
- Don’t puncture sacs. That internal warmth/pressure suggests slow chemistry you don’t want on skin or in lungs.
- Don’t trample the crystalline mats; you don’t know if they’re structural to the whole charge-sorting ecology.
- If you must sample, do *tiny*, sterile, sealed samples; label time/location; store away from water and electronics.

## Camping guidance (if you must stay)
- Sleep on an **insulating pad** (reduces vibration conduction and heat exchange).
- Keep water containers inside your shelter to reduce loss and contamination by mineral flour.
- Put a **lightweight groundsheet** down: the dust is fine, dry, and will invade everything.
- At night, watch for:
  - increased venting
  - stronger “transformer hum”
  - subtle shifting of ridges (mark a reference line in dust and check it)

If the pocket feels “more active” (stronger pulses, more static, more perceptual glitches), treat it like weather moving in: you leave early.

## Emergency triggers (leave immediately if…)
- You cannot re-locate the entrance marker within a pre-set time (e.g., 10 minutes).
- Persistent shortness of breath at rest, ataxia, confusion (altitude cerebral edema risk).
- New chest tightness, burning eyes/throat that doesn’t improve away from vents (gas exposure).
- Repeated “distance doesn’t add up” episodes near ledges (fall risk spikes).
- Electronics all fail at once *and* your perception feels “laggy” (you’ve lost too many reference points).

## A simple “ordinary traveler” plan that works
1. **Day trip only** on first visit. No heroics.
2. Carry **more water than pride allows**.
3. Use a **guideline to the seam** and never unhook from your way home.
4. Stay **away from vents**, **off crystal mats**, and **off ringing plates**.
5. Move as if you’re on a bright, dry glacier: slow, deliberate, redundant safety.
