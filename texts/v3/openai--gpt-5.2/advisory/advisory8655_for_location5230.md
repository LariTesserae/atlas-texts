## Core read of the place (what your body will be arguing with)
You’re visiting a **small, high-elevation terrace** that behaves like a **self-maintaining wetland microclimate**—hot sun, thin air, and persistent surface moisture that keeps “coming back” even when physics says it should flash off.

Assume four simultaneous stressors:

1. **Altitude physiology**: thin air → faster fatigue, dizziness, poor judgment.
2. **Solar/thermal load**: near, insistent sun → sunburn + dehydration even when you feel “cool enough.”
3. **Slip + cut terrain**: young, sharp rock + invisible water films → falls, lacerations, ankle injuries.
4. **Rule-bending microforces near artifacts**: condensation “chooses,” ripples bias, warmth pulses in plates → your instruments and instincts can be subtly wrong.

Treat it like stepping into a **working environmental machine** that happens to look natural.

## Before you go (48 hours to 2 hours prior)

### Medical / physical readiness
- **Altitude**: If you’re not acclimated, plan for **short exposure** the first day (30–90 minutes on-site) and scale up only if you remain symptom-free.
  - Red flags to abort: persistent headache not relieved by water/food, nausea, confusion, unsteady gait, worsening shortness of breath at rest.
- **Hydration**: Begin well-hydrated *before* entry. This place tricks people into under-drinking because “everything is wet.”
- **Tetanus up to date**, and if you have them: **steri-strips** and a way to irrigate cuts (young rock is basically a blade field).

### Multiverse-travel-specific planning
- **Anchor your return** outside the terrace proper if you can. You want your exit/portal/marker in a **drier, less “preferential” moisture zone** so you’re not fighting condensation, mist, or subtle forces that may bias directionality.
- Bring **two independent ways to locate your exit**:
  - one that’s purely mechanical/visual (high-contrast flagging, reflective marker),
  - one instrument-based (beacon/GPS/field compass equivalent).
- Plan for **instrument drift**. If water and plants are leaning “as if listening,” your magnetometer/compass equivalents might lie, and even inertial navigation may accumulate small errors if local gradients are strange.

## What to wear (this is not a rainforest kit; it’s a hot, thin, wet, sharp kit)

### Clothing system
- **UPF sun protection** is non-negotiable: hooded sun shirt or light jacket, long pants, neck coverage.
- **Breathable but not flimsy** fabric: you’ll brush serrated leaves and abrasive stone.
- **Wide-brim hat** + **glacier-style sunglasses** (high altitude glare + mirror-pools).
- **Light insulating layer** packed anyway. Even if nights don’t drop much, a brief cloud veil plus wet skin can chill you faster than expected.

### Footwear
- Prioritize **rock grip on wet stone**:
  - approach shoes or boots with sticky rubber and sharp edging,
  - **ankle support** if you’re not a strong scrambler.
- Consider **microspike-like traction** only if you’ve tested it on slick rock; some traction devices skate on wet smooth mineral films.

### Hands
- **Thin, high-dexterity gloves**. You’ll want to steady yourself on rock without shredding skin, and you should avoid bare-hand contact with warm plates/fins until you know what they do.

## What to carry (minimum kit that keeps you alive and keeps the system unbroken)

### Water & electrolytes
- Carry drinking water as if you’re in a dry alpine zone:
  - **1–2 liters for a short visit**, more if you’ll be there hours.
- **Electrolytes** (tabs or powder). Heat + altitude + constant low-level sweating will flatten you.

### First aid (focused)
- Irrigation syringe or squeeze bottle, antiseptic, steri-strips, gauze, blister care.
- Elastic wrap (ankle sprain is the most likely “I can’t get back” injury here).
- A small roll of athletic tape (wet rock falls often mean hand/knuckle splits).

### Safety & movement
- **Trekking pole** (single) or compact pole: helps on slick benches without overloading your hands.
- **Headlamp** even if you plan a day visit—mist and sudden veils can dim contrast quickly.
- **Emergency thermal sheet**: thin air + wet clothing can chill you if you’re immobilized.

### Observation without contamination
- A small **notebook and pencil** (pens fail in wet).
- **Sample containers** only if you are authorized and disciplined; otherwise treat it as a closed system.
- If you must sample: take **photos first**, then minimal samples from already-disturbed gravel fans, not from living mats.

### Tools for “quiet engineering” artifacts
- **Non-contact thermometer** (IR): map warm plates without touching.
- **Simple string + small weight** (plumb line): a low-tech check for subtle bias that isn’t fooled by magnetism. (If the plumb behaves oddly near fins/plates, note distance and retreat.)
- **Smoke source is a bad idea** (humidity curtain + unknown sensors). Use **soap bubbles** or a feather on a thread if you need airflow clues without contaminating.

## Entry protocol (how not to get hurt in the first 10 minutes)

### 1) Stop at the threshold and do a body check
- Breathing rate, headache, balance.
- Take a drink *before* you feel thirsty.
- Apply sunscreen even if it feels pointless because everything is wet.

### 2) Walk like it’s black ice
- Assume every dark stone has a **clear film**.
- Step on **textured rock** and avoid the temptation to stride across mirror-smooth “dry-looking” panes—those are often the slickest.
- Test footholds with the pole or toe before committing weight.

### 3) Don’t “improve” the paths
- Those flat tiles and grippy textures exist for a reason. Use them if present.
- Do not stack stones, dig drainage, or “clear” vegetation to make footing. The terrace may require emptiness and open stone to keep its cycle stable.

## On-site behavioral rules (this place is running)

### Treat the built fragments as active, even if silent
- **Do not touch embedded warm plates** with bare skin.
- Do not insert anything into sockets/posts.
- Keep metallic tools and electronics **a short distance away** from fins/rings until you’ve confirmed they don’t attract condensation or heat unexpectedly.
- If you feel a hum through your feet/hands: step back. Don’t “ground” yourself on unknown conductive wet stone.

### Keep your presence low-impact
- Stay on rock and tiles where possible; the vegetation islands may be fragile *and* functionally important (water capture and distribution).
- Avoid stepping in shallow panes/pools: you can’t see depth, the bottom may be glass-slick, and you may introduce oils/chemistry.

### Watch for the subtle hazard: confident heat with hidden hypoxia
People make bad decisions here because the environment feels small, quiet, and contained—so you overestimate your control.
- Set a timer: **every 20 minutes** do a water sip + symptom scan.
- If you become irritable, sloppy, or euphoric: treat that as hypoxia until proven otherwise and leave.

## Environmental hazards you should specifically anticipate

### 1) Skin-level wetness that doesn’t evaporate right
- Expect **maceration** (softened skin) on feet/hands over time.
- Change socks after the visit if possible. Keep a dry pair in a sealed bag.

### 2) Young rock = sharp injuries
- Even a “minor” fall can become a serious laceration because edges haven’t rounded.
- If cut: irrigate immediately; don’t just wipe. The grit here is angular and likes to stay.

### 3) Biological exposure (quiet but real)
- Lush mats + warm wet surfaces can host **fungal spores, bacteria films**, and irritant saps.
- Don’t rub your eyes. Wash hands before eating.
- If you have asthma/allergies, consider a mask that can handle humidity without collapsing (a simple cloth will become a wet rag).

### 4) “Preference” gradients (the nonstandard part)
You described directionality in ripples, plant lean, uneven drying. That suggests a gentle field or gradient.
Practical implications:
- Lightweight items (paper, thin plastic, foil) may drift or settle “wrong.”
- Drones may have stability issues near fins/plates because humidity density shifts can fool sensors.
- Your sense of “downhill” might be locally unreliable if your vestibular system is subtly stressed by heat + altitude + microgradients.

Use low-tech checks (plumb line; water in a clear bottle) before you trust your intuition.

## How to observe safely (and get meaningful data)

### Map the terrace in layers
1. **Dry rock zones** (likely “lungs” of the system)
2. **Mirror panes/pools** (water storage/heat exchange surfaces)
3. **Vegetation islands** (capture points / filtration)
4. **Artifact nodes** (rings, fins, warm plates, sockets)

Record:
- cloud veil timing,
- where condensation forms first,
- whether droplets slide toward etched lines,
- whether warmth pulses correlate with shade/sun transitions.

### The one experiment that’s usually safe
- Place a **single droplet** (from your own clean water) on a dry rock patch *away from vegetation* and watch how it moves compared to a natural droplet nearby.
  - If your droplet “chooses” the same paths, the preference isn’t purely mineral salts in the local water; it’s the field/texture/temperature gradients.

Do not pour water; do not add dyes; do not add salts. Small, reversible, documented.

## Exit and decontamination (don’t take the terrace home with you)

### On departure
- Before you step out, do a final scan:
  - Did you drop anything metallic near an artifact?
  - Did you leave footprints through a mat that will channel water differently?
- Backtrack on the same route. Don’t “shortcut” across wet stone at the end when you’re tired.

### Decon
- Rinse hands/forearms; clean under nails.
- Change socks; dry feet.
- Bag wet clothing separately. The biological films here may be benign, but multiverse travel has a way of turning “benign” into “invasive” elsewhere.

## If something goes wrong (simple responses that work here)

### Altitude symptoms begin
- Stop moving. Sit on dry rock.
- Drink, eat a quick carbohydrate.
- If not improving in 10–15 minutes: **leave**. Don’t debate it.

### You slip and get cut
- Irrigate thoroughly, close with steri-strips if needed, cover.
- If bleeding won’t stop or the wound is deep: evacuate immediately. Wet heat and unknown microbes are bad companions for open injuries.

### You feel the artifacts “notice” you (mist shifts, droplets re-route, hum intensifies)
- Increase distance from the nearest node.
- Avoid standing with both hands/feet on wet stone near a warm plate (don’t become a conductive bridge).
- Do not attempt to “test” the response. The system may be benign, but it is clearly responsive.
