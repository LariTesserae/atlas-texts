## What you’re actually visiting (so you pack for the right world)

This is a **high-altitude basin that is completely flooded**, with a **dense, lived-in underwater city** built from wood, cordage, living plants, floats, and anchors—because **air is scarce and managed**, and because the basin floor and currents are **still “settling into existence.”**

Key consequences for an ordinary human body:

- **You cannot breathe here without equipment** (and locals treat breathable air as a stored resource, not an ambient right).
- **Light is dim and filtered**; vision is never your primary sense.
- **Movement is mostly buoyant travel** (handholds, guide ropes, drift-lanes), not walking.
- There are **geothermal/mineral vents** (quiet but chemically present).
- The water has “layers” where **buoyancy and drift behave oddly**—as if objects “prefer” certain depths.
- **Processes are slow**: currents reveal themselves over days, structural failures settle over hours, and small mistakes can become big problems later.

You should prepare like you’re going to a **submerged, low-visibility, low-fauna, plant-dense, chemically active highland sea**—and like you’re entering a city whose infrastructure assumes everyone respects currents and ties.

## Before you go: the three trainings that matter most

### 1) Closed‑water breathing competence
If you aren’t already comfortable underwater, get *real* practice before this trip:

- Clearing a flooded mask calmly
- Switching to backup gas without panic
- Managing buoyancy with minimal finning (you will be near plants, ropes, and fragile structures)
- Zero‑visibility navigation on a line

If you only learn one thing: **how to stop, stabilize, and think while suspended**. Panic is more dangerous than almost anything in this basin.

### 2) Line discipline and entanglement survival
This city is ropes, kelp, curtains, lashings, and tethered gardens.

Practice:

- Moving without snagging hoses/straps
- Maintaining one “clean” profile (nothing dangling)
- Cutting yourself free *without* cutting the city (see tools below)
- Tether management (you will be expected to clip in often)

### 3) “Slow hazard” thinking
Most threats here are not sudden bites or storms; they’re **creeping**:

- CO₂ buildup in enclosed chambers
- Minor chemical irritation becoming lung trouble
- A redirected current undermining an anchor weeks later
- Mild hypothermia or overheating from warm-but-wicking water

Train yourself to do routine checks on a schedule, not “when it feels urgent.”

## Core life-support: how to breathe without becoming a burden

### Bring a closed‑circuit rebreather (CCR) if you can; otherwise plan heavy redundancy
**Why CCR is ideal here:**
- Locals value air; open-circuit bubbles are wasteful, conspicuous, and sometimes socially frowned upon.
- CCR drastically extends duration and reduces bubble disruption in plant corridors and silt pockets.

**Minimum workable setup (if you’re ordinary but serious):**
- **CCR with dual oxygen sensors**, reliable handset, and a manual add option
- **Redundant bailout gas** (enough to reach a known safe-air chamber or the surface *plus* a margin)
- **CO₂ scrubber canisters** appropriate for warm water duration (warmth can increase metabolic CO₂ output)
- **Spare mouthpiece / DSV parts** and basic field service kit

**If you must use open‑circuit:**
- Bring more gas than you think; then add another margin.
- Use **low-bubble techniques** near silt and membranes (slow exhale, careful trim).
- Be prepared for social friction; mitigate by offering trade goods (see “Trade” below).

### Air etiquette you should assume
- Air is **stored, traded, rationed**, and sometimes *communal*.
- Do not assume you can “just borrow a breath” in someone’s chamber.
- Never open an air bladder or sealed room casually—treat it like opening someone’s pantry in a famine zone.

## Pressure, altitude, and the trip home (this is where people get hurt)

Because the basin is **high**, the surface pressure (if you ever surface) can be **lower than sea level**. Combine that with underwater pressure exposure and you get:

- Increased decompression complexity
- Higher risk if you “pop” out of the water and then ascend further (ridge, platform towers, etc.)
- A strong reason to **plan decompression as if you’re diving at altitude**

**Preparation:**
- Use an altitude-capable dive computer and know how to set it.
- Build conservative ascent profiles and include safety stops even when “not deep.”
- If you plan to exit the basin to higher elevations, treat it like post-dive flight: **delay ascent**.

If your multiverse travel method “teleports” you: do **not** portal from depth to a low-pressure environment. Transition through a pressure-stable habitat or make your portal endpoint **at equivalent pressure**.

## Mobility and buoyancy in “water with opinions”

You described bands where objects drift until they “choose” a level. Whether that’s unusual density stratification, microcurrents, bioelectric gradients, or stranger physics: plan for your gear to behave inconsistently.

### What to bring
- **A buoyancy system with fine control** (wing/BCD or rebreather wing)
- **Trim weights in small increments** (so you can “dial in” to stubborn layers)
- **Two independent tethers**:
  - a short working tether (1–2 m)
  - a longer guideline tether (5–15 m) for crossings
- **A line reel/spool** and non-snag line
- **Carabiners that work with gloves** (avoid complex gates)

### What to do on arrival
- Spend your first hour doing nothing but:
  - finding your neutral buoyancy at 2–3 depths
  - observing what your loose items do
  - learning the local “comfortable” travel layer (usually the middle busy band)

Assume the locals tie everything twice because **one tie resists current; the second resists the basin’s “preference.”** Copy them.

## Exposure protection: warm water still steals heat (and can overheat you)

Warm water feels forgiving until you stop moving. It also conducts heat away far faster than air.

Bring:
- A **full wetsuit** (5–7 mm) *or* a **light drysuit** if you’ll spend long periods in still districts
- **Gloves** with good dexterity (you’ll be handling rope constantly)
- **Hood** (even in warm water, it stabilizes comfort and reduces fatigue)
- Anti-fog that won’t poison gardens (use mild solutions)

Also pack:
- **Hydration salts**: you’ll dehydrate without noticing, especially on CCR (dry mouth + exertion).

## Visibility, navigation, and communication in a dim three-dimensional city

### Lighting
Bring at least two:
- Primary light with a **wide, soft beam** (narrow beams backscatter in silt)
- Backup light that you can operate one-handed

Avoid blasting bright light into windows/gardens; dimness is part of the city’s “privacy architecture.”

### Navigation (how you avoid getting lost)
This place is **depth-defined**, not compass-defined.

Bring:
- Depth gauge/computer you trust
- A simple wrist compass anyway (helpful in open basin)
- Waterproof slate + grease pencil for mapping “lanes” and anchor landmarks
- A small set of tactile markers (knotted tags) you can place *only where permitted*

Practice:
- “Follow the rope, not the view.”
- Count junctions; note current direction; note the feel of vegetation (some districts will be identifiable by texture alone).

### Communication
Underwater speech is limited.

Prepare:
- Basic hand signals + light signals
- Tactile signals on a tether (1 pull = stop, 2 = come, etc.)
- A writing slate for negotiations and directions

## Water chemistry, vents, and the “metallic taste” problem

Those pale chimneys are telling you: **minerals, heat, and dissolved gases are part of daily life**.

Risks to plan for:
- Irritation to eyes/skin from mineral-rich water
- Localized low oxygen or weird gas pockets near vents (even if they “breathe” gently)
- Heavy-metal exposure (the metallic taste is a warning sign, not flavor)

Bring:
- A mask that seals well; consider a spare
- Rinse solution for eyes (sterile saline)
- Post-exposure wash (mild soap, not harsh solvents)
- If you have a rebreather, keep sensors/calibration disciplined—chemical environments punish sloppy oxygen management

Rule of thumb:
- **Do not linger in vent-fields**, and do not “rest” on the pale quilted ground. It’s warm, soft, and not fully land.

## Biological risks (even with few animals)

Low fauna doesn’t mean low hazard.

Likely issues:
- Microbial films on warm wood and living walls
- Fungal/skin infections from constant wetness
- Cuts that heal poorly in mineral-rich water

Bring:
- Antiseptic suitable for aquatic environments (and bandaging that stays put)
- A small antibiotic course if medically appropriate for you (discuss beforehand if you can)
- A razor/skin-care routine to prevent breakdown under straps (seriously—strap rash becomes an infection vector)

Get up to date on:
- Tetanus
- Hep A / typhoid if you’ll eat local food (not guaranteed necessary, but prudent)
- Any region-analog travel vaccines your multiverse service recommends

## Clothing and personal gear: design for snag-free living

Everything you wear should be:
- close-fitting
- minimal dangling straps
- easy to clip, stow, and retrieve

Bring:
- A compact waterproof pouch for documents, trade tokens, and emergency meds
- Soft-soled booties (you’ll push off wood, not rock)
- A small personal net bag (locals use nets; it reads as “I understand how to live here”)

Avoid:
- Long scarves, loose belts, ornamental cords
- Hard protruding gear that can puncture membranes and curtains

## Social preparation: how not to be the traveler who ruins a neighborhood

This city survives by **negotiation with currents** and by **not pretending anything is permanent**.

### Etiquette you should adopt immediately
- **Tie everything twice.** Even if you don’t believe the water “decides,” the locals do, and it’s good practice.
- **Don’t open large membranes or curtains** unless invited; you can redirect flow, silt, warmth.
- **Move slowly through narrow lanes**; let residents pass—shoulder-brushing is normal, but snagging is a sin.
- **Keep your bubbles and thrashing minimal.** It’s like shouting indoors.
- **Don’t step on new ground.** The pale pillows are not sidewalks.

### Conversation norms
- Voices are private and close. Expect people to come near your mask/ear area.
- Silence is not awkward; it’s the default.

### Trade (what to bring that will matter)
Metal is scarce. So are certain resins and durable fibers.

Good trade items:
- Stainless fasteners, needles, fishhooks (small, repairable)
- High-quality cordage that swells tight when wet
- Resin, sealants, patch materials
- CO₂ scrubber material (if compatible) or airtight valves/bladders
- Simple optical items (small magnifiers) can be surprisingly valuable in dim places

Bad trade items:
- Loud, power-hungry tech with no way to maintain it
- Anything that corrodes quickly in warm mineral water

## Where you sleep and how you don’t suffocate

Most households seal themselves into warmer chambers. That’s great for comfort, risky for air.

If you’re offered lodging:
- Ask (politely) how air is managed: **exchange bladders, plant filtration, scrubbers, vent warmth circulation**
- Watch for signs of CO₂ buildup: headache, confusion, breathlessness, unusual sleepiness

Bring:
- A compact **CO₂ indicator** if you can (or at least be disciplined about symptoms)
- Your own small air reserve if culturally acceptable (and offer something in exchange)

Never assume you can run a flame. Fire is difficult and expensive here.

## Daily routine that will keep you healthy (and welcomed)

1. **Morning:** equipment check + re-tie your tether points (the city moves)
2. **Travel:** clip in when entering rope-guided lanes; keep hands free for gentle pushes
3. **Midday:** hydration + warm rest (fatigue accumulates faster in water)
4. **Evening:** rinse skin, check for small cuts, dry critical seals, store gear where it won’t drift to a “preferred layer”

In this basin, “maintenance” isn’t chores; it’s citizenship.

## Emergency planning (assume rescue is slow and calm)

### The most likely emergencies
- Entanglement in kelp/cordage
- Running low on breathable gas/air access
- Disorientation in silt or dusk
- Mild chemical exposure near vents
- Gear drifting away to a different depth band

### Your emergency kit should include
- Two cutting tools (as above)
- A small DSMB or marker float *only if it won’t offend local norms* (useful in open basin)
- Backup light
- Audible/tactile alarm device (underwater rattles can be a last resort)
- A written card (waterproof) explaining:
  - your breathing system basics
  - how to help you (e.g., “do not remove mouthpiece; close valve; bring to air chamber”)
  - allergies/medical conditions

### The rule that saves lives here
If something goes wrong: **stop, clip in, stabilize buoyancy, then act.**  
Thrashing turns plants into nets and silt into night.

Expect:
- A persistent pressure-like quiet that can feel intimate or oppressive

- Keep a routine.
- Keep a log (written, not voice).
- Give yourself deliberate “surface-garden time” near brighter layers if possible.
