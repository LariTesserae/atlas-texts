## What you’re walking into (in human terms)
This pocket is a high-altitude, sealed-ish micro-ecology driven by **dry gas flow, trace chemicals, salts, and persistent machinery**. It’s not “dangerous” in a predatory way; it’s dangerous in the way a running industrial system and an alien atmosphere are dangerous: **lungs, eyes, skin, balance, hearing, and judgment** are the failure points.

Assume five primary hazards:

1. **Breathable-but-wrong air**: low O₂ partial pressure + ammonia/solvent/VOC traces + ozone/ionization near electrified panels.
2. **Extreme dryness**: rapid dehydration of mucosa/eyes/skin; contact lenses become a liability.
3. **Continuous vibration + high noise floor**: fatigue, disorientation, communication failure, sinus/inner-ear stress with pressure pulses.
4. **Fall/entanglement/industrial injury**: open drop, loose grates/catwalk remnants, ducts that can pull or pinch, energized metal.
5. **Chemical/biological unknowns**: resin films that polymerize fast; salt crusts; black lichens that may be caustic or conductive.

Your goal is to visit like a careful technician, not like a hiker.

## Before you go: health and tolerance checks
### Medical reality check (do this even if you’re fit)
- If you’ve had **asthma, reactive airways, chronic sinus issues, panic attacks, or arrhythmia**, treat this location as high-risk.
- Get comfortable using a respirator **before** the jump. Don’t “learn it there.”
- If you’re prone to nosebleeds or dry-eye, pre-stage mitigation (see kit list).

### Acclimatization isn’t reliable here
Even if you tolerate altitude on Earth, the description implies the air is **chemically tailored for machines/plants**. Plan as though you’ll be on **supplemental oxygen and filtration** from arrival.

## Core packing list (don’t negotiate these)
### Breathing / atmosphere
- **Full-face respirator** (preferred) or half-mask respirator + sealed goggles.
- Filter cartridges rated for:
  - **Acid gases + ammonia/methylamine**
  - **Organic vapors (VOC/solvents)**
  - **Particulates (P100)**
  - If you can’t source combo cartridges, bring separate units and swap—don’t gamble.
- **Portable oxygen**:
  - Minimum: small rescue cylinder.
  - Better: compact continuous-flow system.
- **Portable gas meter**, ideally with: O₂, CO, CO₂, VOC (PID if available), and **NH₃**; ozone sensor if you can get it.
- **Nasal moisture strategy**: saline spray + petroleum-free gel (some gels react poorly with unknown solvents).

### Eyes / skin / dryness
- **Non-vented goggles** (if not full-face).
- **Skin barrier**: nitrile under-gloves + tougher over-gloves (mechanic’s gloves).
- **Disposable sleeve covers / oversuit** if you plan to touch surfaces.
- **Eye drops** (preservative-free), and **do not wear contact lenses**.

### Hearing / communication
- **Hearing protection** (earplugs + earmuffs). The constant fan tone and vibrating metal can do real damage quickly.
- **Non-verbal comms**: printed hand-signal card or pre-agreed signals. Voices won’t carry cleanly.

### Mobility / fall safety
- **Climbing harness + two lanyards + locking carabiners** (via ferrata style works well).
- **Static line / anchor kit** if you’re going near the open drop or old catwalk segments.
- Footwear: **stiff-soled boots** with good edge control. The living mat “rebounds” and can trick your ankles.

### Light / power
- **Headlamp** with a wide flood mode (dimness + overhang).
- Spare batteries in sealed bag. Fine dust + salts + vibration ruin battery contacts.

### Hydration / calories
- Bring **more water than you think**; the dryness is aggressive.
- Add **electrolytes**. Dry cold plus respirator use dehydrates fast without you noticing.

## Strongly recommended “this place specifically” items
- **Anti-static strap or anti-static spray** for outer layers (use cautiously): ozone/arc micro-events plus polymer surfaces can build charge.

- **Printed checklist**. The hum + pressure pulses can make you forget steps you’d never forget elsewhere.

## Arrival protocol (first 3 minutes matter)
1. **Do not take a deep “test breath.”** Mask on first, then assess.
2. Step in and **stand still**:
   - Let your vestibular system adjust to vibration.
   - Watch for any immediate eye/throat sting (ozone/ammonia sign).
3. Check instruments:
   - **O₂ reading**
   - VOC/NH₃ trend
   - CO/CO₂
4. Identify:
   - The **nearest stable anchor point**
   - The **edge line** (mark it mentally; depth perception in dim + monotone surfaces lies)
   - Any **energized metal** (look for faint glow, buzzing, micro-arcing)

If your eyes water, throat tightens, or you taste “clean sharp” chemicals through the mask, you either need better cartridges or you leave. Don’t “push through”—chemical exposure here is chronic-like and cumulative even when it feels mild.

## How to move without getting hurt (or stuck)
### Walking on the living laminated mat
- Treat it like **rubberized flooring over grit**:
  - It can be slightly springy.
  - It can hide hard edges (bolt heads, grating fragments).
- Shuffle steps in uncertain areas; don’t pivot sharply—ankle rolls are common on surfaces that rebound.

### Touch discipline
- Avoid grabbing conduits or grates with bare gloves unless necessary.
- Assume **any metal could be vibrating because it’s part of a running system**, not because it’s loose.
- If you touch resin threads or glossy black lichens:
  - Stop.
  - Don’t yank away.
  - Roll fabric off slowly; cut free if needed.
  - Plan to discard contaminated outer layers later.

### Old catwalk rule
If it doesn’t have modern-rated supports, it’s decoration. Don’t step on it. Plants may be using it as a template; your weight is a different kind of load.

## What not to do (this environment punishes “normal” instincts)
- **Don’t look for water.** There won’t be any safe liquid sources.
- **Don’t open sealed hatches.** The interior may be a different atmosphere/pressure regime, and the system may respond mechanically.
- **Don’t cut vegetation casually.** The quick-setting film is great for them and terrible for your tools (and may off-gas as it cures).
- **Don’t assume “no animals” means “no pathogens.”** There can be spores, corrosive biochemistry, or catalytic dusts.

## Time-on-site planning (how long is safe?)
Without hard sensor data, keep it conservative:
- **First visit: 20–40 minutes max**, then exit, evaluate symptoms, and decontaminate.
- If O₂ is low or VOC/NH₃ is elevated, reduce to **10–15 minutes**.
- The constant hum and pressure rhythm can create a subtle trance—set a **timer you can feel** (vibration alarm) rather than one you must hear.

## Decontamination and exit (don’t bring the pocket home)
This place is built on slow cycles and persistent films. You don’t want its chemistry on your gear—or in your lungs later.

1. **Before you leave the pocket**, brush off loose dust with a dedicated brush you can discard.
2. At your transition point:
   - Remove outer gloves/oversuit first, bag them.
   - Wipe hard gear (carabiners, meters) with solvent-compatible wipes (isopropyl may not be enough; but don’t use anything that reacts with unknown resins—test on a tiny spot).
3. Once in clean air:
   - Keep the respirator on until you’re sure you’re not off-gassing something from your clothes.
4. After:
   - Rinse eyes with saline if irritated.
   - Monitor for delayed symptoms: cough, wheeze, headache, “chemical” taste, unusual fatigue (signs of ozone/solvent exposure).

## Psychological preparation (it sounds trivial; it isn’t)
Expect:
- **No biological “feedback”** (no birds, insects, movement by hunger).
- **A loud monotone** that never resolves.
- **Slow, synchronized plant motion** that can feel like the place is breathing *you*.

This can produce unease, derealization, or time distortion. The fix is practical:
- Work in pairs if possible.
- Use a checklist.
- Set a hard exit time.
- Keep one person on “environment watch” while the other observes.

## If you intend to study or take a sample (minimal harm approach)
- Prefer **non-destructive sampling**: collect shed desiccated layers, salt crust flakes already flex-cracked, or dust from inert surfaces.
- Use solvent-rated containers, label immediately.
- Never sample from anything that appears to be part of the pocket’s “lung” function (tube-leaves near ducts, membranes, vibrating conduit junctions). Small damage could matter a lot in a stable closed loop.

## Emergency triggers (leave immediately if…)
- Gas meter shows:
  - O₂ trending downward,
  - VOC/NH₃ spiking,
  - CO₂ elevated (even if O₂ seems “ok”).
- You feel:
  - Chest tightness, wheeze, burning eyes/throat,
  - Sudden headache or confusion (hypoxia/chemical exposure),
  - Metallic taste intensifying despite filtration.
- You observe:
  - Increased micro-arcing,
  - A change in fan tone/compressor rhythm,
  - Any shift in airflow direction or strength (the “held” stability changing is your warning).
