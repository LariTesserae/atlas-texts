## What you’re walking into (so you pack the right self)

This basin-city isn’t dangerous in the dramatic way (no constant storms, no active war, no obvious monsters). It’s dangerous in the quiet way: **cold that doesn’t fluctuate**, **light that doesn’t let your eyes rest**, **sound that doesn’t travel normally**, **space that’s tighter than your body expects**, and a community whose survival depends on **not wasting heat, moisture, or calm**.

Prepare for a place that will feel like:
- a terrarium built of stone and moss,
- a bright overcast that never becomes warm,
- a town where the “streets” are seams, roofs, ladders, and consensus,
- and where your biggest risk is being the loud, leaky, disruptive variable.

---

## 1) Pre-trip body prep (do this before you cross universes)

### Cold tolerance and circulation
This is not “I might get chilled,” it’s “cold is the default background of life.”
- **Train hands/feet**: get used to working in gloves; practice knot-tying, opening clasps, and handling small items while insulated.
- **Check circulation issues** (Raynaud’s, asthma, anemia). Bring meds; don’t assume resupply.

### Eyes and sleep
The brightness is constant and reflective.
- Pack **high-quality tinted goggles** (category 3–4 sunglasses) plus **clear protective glasses** for overcast glare.
- Bring a **sleep mask** or blackout scarf. Even if they have night, the place is built to hold light.

### Respiratory tolerance
Peat smoke, damp wool, animal warmth, compost—none of it is “filthy,” but it’s dense and communal.
- If you’re sensitive, bring a **small reusable respirator** (or at least a few masks) for workshops and smoke-heavy halls.

## 2) Biosecurity: you are the invasive species

This place has had “long uninterrupted centuries” to negotiate every niche. Don’t be the new fungus.

### Before entry
- **Clean and disinfect**: boots (treads), tent stakes, clothing cuffs, pack seams.
- Avoid bringing **seeds, fresh soil, unwashed produce**, or anything that can shed spores.
- If you can, do a **24–48 hour clean quarantine** of your kit (dry, brushed, inspected).

### In the city
- Don’t dump rinse water wherever. **Ask where graywater goes.**
- Treat their cisterns and mist-catching stones as **infrastructure + sacred habit**. Don’t “improve” them.

### Health precautions
- Assume **zoonotic risk** (dense animals, close living). Bring:
  - hand sanitizer / soap flakes,
  - a compact wound kit,
  - oral rehydration salts,
  - broad-spectrum antiparasitic *only if you know how to use it responsibly* (and local norms allow it).

## 3) Clothing and personal gear (optimize for quiet, grip, and layers)

### Footwear (most important)
You’ll walk on mossy seams, rime-slick planks, angled rooflines.
- **Soft-soled boots** with aggressive tread and good ankle support.
- Add **removable traction** (microspikes or strap-on cleats) that you can take off when entering interiors (you don’t want to chew up their planks).
- Bring **extra socks** (wool/synthetic blend) and a way to dry them (small line + clips).

### Layering system
You want warmth without bulk: the paths are narrow and people live close.
- Base: moisture-wicking
- Mid: wool or fleece
- Outer: wind-resistant shell (even though wind dies in the basin, you’ll cross the plain to reach it)
- **Insulated vest** is ideal: core warmth, arm mobility in tight corridors.

### Hands
Gestural communication is common; you’ll need dexterity.
- Two-layer glove strategy:
  - thin liner gloves (dexterity),
  - warm over-mittens (heat).
- Bring a **spare pair**. Wet gloves become a slow emergency.

### Light/eye protection
- Sunglasses/goggles (glare)
- Brimmed cap or hood
- A little **skin barrier** (cold-dry air + bright light can chap faster than you expect): balm, hand cream.

### Pack choice
Leave the expedition pack behind.
- Use a **small, narrow profile pack**. No external dangling gear; it will snag constantly.
- Nothing wheeled. Nothing wide. Assume ladders, roof-walks, and shoulder turns.

## 4) Tools and supplies that won’t make you a problem

### Water and food
Water exists as “pockets and films.” Don’t assume you can just scoop it.
- Bring a **collapsible bottle** and a **filter** (or purification tabs).
- Carry **dense calories**: nuts, fats, hard biscuits, jerky. Their food economy is carefully balanced; arriving hungry makes you an unplanned load.

### Fire/heat
Heat is communal and social.
- Don’t bring an attitude of “I’ll just make my own fire.” Fuel is slow-earned.
- A small **chemical hand warmer** stash can keep you functional without needing their peat.

### Navigation and orientation
The basin is easy to miss; inside, it’s easy to get turned around because sound cues are damped.
- Bring:
  - a **compass**,
  - a simple **paper sketch map notebook**,
  - chalk or removable wax marker *only if locals allow marking* (better: tie a small thread marker on your own pack and memorize landmarks).
- Don’t rely solely on GPS-style tech; interworld conditions can make electronics unreliable.

### Timekeeping
They say “time doesn’t do much here” environmentally. That can mess with you psychologically: your sense of day, urgency, and pace.
- Bring a **reliable watch** (ideally analog or mechanical backup).
- Keep a **written log** of sleep/food/temp so you don’t drift.

### Sound discipline
In a place where loudness is treated like a spill:
- Silence anything that beeps (timers, phone alarms).
- Avoid Velcro (it’s loud), hard plastic clacks, jangling metal.
- Wrap tools in cloth. Tie down buckles.

## 5) Social preparation: how not to be “that traveler”

### Learn the local logic
Their rules are built around conservation and density:
- Don’t waste warmth (doors close fully; fires are shared).
- Don’t waste moisture (every drip is guided).
- Don’t waste space (paths are negotiated commons).
- Don’t disturb the rim (treat it like a living boundary).

Internalize this: **your convenience is never a sufficient reason**.

### Communication
Because gestural language is common:
- Practice **clear pointing with whole hand**, not finger-jabbing.
- Bring a **small slate/notebook** for drawing (silent, fast, respectful).
- Speak softly; pause often. In tight quiet spaces, uninterrupted talking reads as pushing.

### Gifts / trade that make sense here
Metal isn’t absent, it’s scarce and cherished. So are salt and durable fibers.
Good, non-insulting offerings:
- sewing needles, awls, fishhooks,
- thin wire, small hinge pins, spring steel scraps,
- salt, hard spices, dye pigments,
- waterproof thread, strong cordage,
- a small sharpening stone.

Bad offerings:
- flashy junk, loud devices, anything that implies “modern superiority,”
- food that introduces seeds/pests,
- anything that forces them to reorganize space to store it.

### Ask before you photograph/scan/measure
Even if nobody says “taboo,” in a place built on careful equilibrium, obsessive measurement can look like predation.

## 6) Approach and entry: don’t miss it, don’t stumble into it

### Finding the basin
From above, it looks like “a slightly greener depression.” Your best cues:
- the **wind drop** (you’ll feel it),
- the **sound drop** (your footsteps suddenly seem too intimate),
- ground becoming **springy** with moisture habit,
- increased **diffuse brightness** reflecting up from pale stones.

### Transition protocol
At the rim:
- Stop, tighten gear (nothing dangling).
- Switch to quiet mode: no clacking straps, no loud chatter.
- Put on glare protection if the light ricochets.
- If you have a tradition in your own travel practice: do it *quietly* here. The rim matters.

### First contact
Don’t wander in like you’re exploring ruins. It’s inhabited, dense, and watchful.
- Wait at a visible edge point.
- Make yourself small: hands visible, pack off one shoulder, posture non-urgent.
- Offer a gift **before** asking for lodging.

## 7) On-the-ground hazards (the ones that will actually get you)

### Slips and falls
Roof-walk streets + rime + moss = sudden injury.
- Move with “three points of contact” thinking.
- Don’t jump gaps. Don’t assume a plank is structural just because it’s there.
- If offered local overshoes or bindings, accept them; they’re adapted to the surfaces.

### Cold + damp creep
You can feel “fine” and then realize you’ve lost hand function.
- Eat and drink on schedule, not by appetite.
- Change socks early, not late.
- If you start making clumsy mistakes, treat it as hypothermia’s early bureaucratic form.

### Quiet disorientation
Because sound doesn’t travel, you may not notice:
- someone approaching,
- animals moving behind you,
- your own noise level rising.

Adopt a habit: **turn and visually check** before backing up, opening a door, or stepping out into a seam-path.

### Path etiquette (space conflicts)
Narrow passages mean right-of-way matters.
- Pause at pinch points.
- Let locals pass; you’re the bulky unfamiliar object.
- Don’t set your pack down in a corridor.

### Smoke and indoor air
Communal workshops conserve heat; they also concentrate particulates.
- Take outside breaks.
- Hydrate more than you think.
- If your eyes sting constantly, use clear goggles indoors rather than rubbing (dirty hands + shared surfaces).

## 8) Lodging and daily life: how to fit without strain

### Sleeping
Expect:
- shared air,
- quiet so deep your own breathing feels loud,
- possible persistent brightness.

Bring:
- sleep mask,
- earplugs (not because it’s loud, but because *your brain may hunt for noise* and keep you awake),
- a compact sleeping liner (hygiene + warmth).

### Hygiene
Water is careful; bathing norms will reflect that.
- Use **wipe-down hygiene** (cloth + minimal water).
- Pack **biodegradable soap** but use it only where they say runoff is safe.

### Work participation
If you stay more than a day, you should contribute.
Skills that translate well without disrupting:
- sharpening, mending, cordage repair,
- helping with roof-mat maintenance,
- compost turning (if they permit),
- carrying loads carefully through tight routes.

Avoid trying to “optimize” their systems unless asked. Their systems are not naive—they’re the outcome of centuries of non-dramatic survival.

## 9) Multiverse contingencies (assume something will be slightly off)

### Light anomaly precautions
If light “prefers the basin,” you might experience:
- eye strain that feels like mild headache,
- colors slightly washed or overly crisp,
- difficulty judging distance on pale surfaces.

Mitigation:
- alternate tinted/clear lenses,
- take “dark rests” (hood over eyes) briefly during the day,
- don’t drive yourself to keep moving just because it’s always bright.

### Tech reliability
Even if nothing overtly magical is happening, stable inversion pockets and unusual reflectivity can make signals weird.
- Keep critical info on **paper**.
- Have a **non-powered** way to navigate and keep time.

### Psychological effect of steadiness
A place without seasonal drama can make you feel unanchored—like you’re waiting for something that never arrives.
- Keep routine: meals, sleep, a daily walk, journaling.
- Plan a clear exit day before you get “stuck” in the calm.

---

## 10) Leaving without damage (to them or you)

### Exit care
When you climb back over the rim:
- the wind and noise will feel aggressive; pause and reorient.
- check for frostbite hotspots (ears, fingers) you didn’t notice in the still air.

### Don’t export their ecology
Before you go:
- brush/clean boots and clothing again,
- shake out pack seams,
- don’t carry living moss/lichen “souvenirs.”

### Social closure
In a consensus-based dense place, leaving abruptly can read as distrust.
- Say goodbye formally (even if it’s gestural).
- Offer a final practical gift (thread, salt, needle) rather than something sentimental they must store.
