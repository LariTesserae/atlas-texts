## What you’re walking into (in human terms)

This plateau is “high mountain” plus “dry geothermal field” plus “permanent ash dusk” plus “a crowded reef, but made of chitin instead of coral.” It isn’t overtly hostile in a cinematic way; it’s hostile the way a conveyor belt is hostile: it keeps moving according to its own rules, and it doesn’t slow down to notice you.

The core hazards are:

- **Heat from below (contact heat + pulsed warm ground)**: burns, boot failure, dehydration acceleration.
- **Thin air / altitude physiology**: hypoxia, impaired judgment, fast fatigue.
- **Toxic/asphyxiant gases**: sulfur compounds and other volcanic gasses, plus oxygen displacement near fissures.
- **Near-total dimness**: navigation errors, missteps into fissures, stepping on the wrong living thing.
- **A living ground layer**: you are constantly “in the way,” and some things will treat you as terrain; others will treat you as food or heat.
- **A mineral chemistry environment**: abrasive glass patches, caustic dusts/crusts, metallic salts, unknown irritants.
- **Low-water ecology**: you cannot resupply water locally, and “wet solutions” (sweat, tears, open wounds) become magnets for chemistry and organisms.

If you prepare for **(1) heat + (2) low oxygen + (3) gas + (4) darkness + (5) biofilm/arthropod analogs**, you’ll be fine. If you prepare for only one or two, you’ll leave early or not at all.

## Pre-trip planning (the stuff that saves you before you arrive)

### 1) Set your exit conditions before you go
This place is designed to make you lose track of time and distance. Decide in advance:

- **Maximum exposure window**: e.g., “90 minutes on the plateau, then retreat to the rim,” or “one migration cycle only.”
- **Turnaround triggers** (non-negotiable):
  - Headache + nausea (hypoxia) that doesn’t improve with oxygen.
  - Any smell shift from “old matches/struck stone” to “sweet/solvent/chlorine/metallic biting” (gas composition change).
  - Any sign of confusion, clumsiness, or irrational calm.
  - Boot soles softening, delaminating, or picking up tacky residue.
  - Two unexplained “attention events” from fauna (swarm convergence, repeated following, coordinated blocking).

Write these on a physical card. You will not think clearly in the dim + thin air.

### 2) Quarantine mindset
Assume you will bring something back on your boots, clothing, filters, or in your lungs if you do it wrong.

Plan:
- A **dirty bag** (sealed) for everything worn on-site.
- A **decon protocol** you can do blind and tired: brush → wipe → bag → wash hands/face → change filters.
- No eating or touching your face on the plateau. Not once.

## Core gear (what an “ordinary human” should actually carry)

### A) Breathing & atmosphere
This is not optional.

- **Full-face respirator** with combination cartridges:
  - Acid gas/organic vapor + particulate (P100 or equivalent).
  - Bring **spares** in sealed packaging; ash/mineral haze clogs faster than you expect.
- **Portable oxygen** (small bottle) or a lightweight oxygen concentrator rated for altitude (bottles are more reliable).
- **Personal gas monitor** (multi-gas): O₂, H₂S, SO₂, CO₂ at minimum.
- **Eye-safe anti-fog** measures (the dim + mask fogging is a real failure mode).

Why: fissures that “breathe” can be oxygen-poor without smelling dramatic, and volcanic-style gases can overwhelm you before you feel panic.

### B) Footing & heat isolation
You are walking on warm stone, glassy patches, and living mats.

- **Mountaineering boots** with:
  - High-temp resistant outsole (or add a sacrificial heat-resistant over-sole).
  - Puncture-resistant midsole (glass + crystal collars).
- **Gaiters** to keep skittering dust and small crawlers out of cuffs.
- **Trekking pole** (or two) to probe ahead in the dark and test crust strength near fissures.
- **Spare laces / boot repair tape** (heat and salts destroy adhesives; bring mechanical fasteners if possible).

Rule: if you can feel warmth clearly through your boot, you’re under-insulated for long exposure.

### C) Light that won’t ruin your night vision
You need light, but you also need to preserve your ability to see subtle texture and movement.

- **Two headlamps** (redundant), both with:
  - Very low “moonlight” mode.
  - **Red/amber mode** (less disruptive, less attractive to some life).
- **One handheld throw light** for reading terrain at distance (briefly, not continuously).
- **Chem-light sticks** (non-toxic) for marking your own route without electronics.

Avoid high-frequency strobing or loud buzzing electronics; if anything here responds to vibration, you don’t want to advertise.

### D) Skin protection & abrasion
Everything is dry, sharp, and chemically opinionated.

- **Durable outer layer** (tight weave), preferably something that sheds dust.
- **Hard-shell gloves** (not just fabric) for rock contact.
- **Neck gaiter / hood** even with a respirator (dust gets everywhere).
- **Barrier ointment** for exposed skin (nose bridge, wrists) to reduce irritation.

### E) Navigation & anchoring
Distances collapse in the murk. You need a way to “un-collapse” them.

- **Route-marking kit**:
  - Chalk that contrasts on basalt (or reflective tape tabs).
  - Small cairn markers (but don’t rely on stacked stones—fauna traffic and vibration will disturb them).
- **Inert line spool** (thin cord) to “breadcrumb” a return path in the worst visibility.
- **Altimeter + compass** (simple, non-electronic backup).
- **Return beacon/anchor** for your multiverse travel method, placed on the rim where gas is less intense.

If you can’t explain to yourself, out loud, how you will get back to your entry point in total darkness with one headlamp dead, you’re not ready.

### F) Water & heat management
You will not find water. Also, the air and heat will strip you faster than you feel it happening.

- **More water than you think** (minimum 1.5–2 L for a short recon; 3–4 L if you’re staying for a full cycle).
- **Electrolytes** (salts) and easy calories.
- **A way to drink without unmasking** (hydration adapter or step-off protocol in a safer zone).

Do not plan to “sweat it out.” Sweat is lost water + sticky chemistry + scent cue.

### G) First aid tuned for this place
- **Burn dressings** + blister care (contact heat and friction).
- **Eye wash** (sterile) — but use only in a controlled way; don’t dribble water onto contaminated skin and spread irritants.
- **Antihistamine** and topical steroid (unknown irritant exposure).
- **Wound sealant** / steri-strips (keep wounds closed; this ecology loves openings).
- **Portable pulse oximeter** (altitude reality check).

## Clothing choices (counterintuitive but important)

- Go for **covered, sealed, and boring**.
- Avoid:
  - Dangling straps (they become tow lines for curious crawlers).
  - Soft mesh fabric (hooks and claws love it).
  - Perfumes, scented detergents, minty lip balm—anything volatile becomes a trail.

Aim for: smooth surfaces, tight cuffs, minimal loose ends.

## Behavioral protocol on-site (how to move without becoming “part of the mosaic”)

### 1) Walk like you’re in a crowded subway, not a wilderness
Assume the ground is occupied even when it looks empty.

- Step on **bare rock** whenever possible, not on mats, films, or pooled “sediment.”
- Move **slowly and predictably**; sudden movement triggers collective shifts.
- Keep your feet close to the ground—high stepping catches on crystal collars and fissure lips.

### 2) Treat fissures like open water
They aren’t just cracks; they’re breathing organs.

- Probe ahead with a pole.
- Don’t kneel near them.
- Don’t hover your face over them “to smell” (this is how people get dosed).

If your gas monitor alarms: you leave. No debate.

### 3) Don’t “assert dominance” with sound
This world prices vibration. Loudness is not safety.

- Speak softly.
- Don’t stomp to scare things off; it may instead call them in.
- If you must signal a partner, use **light taps on your own gear** or hand signals.

### 4) Respect the “rules zones” you don’t understand
You described at least one: the basalt arch corridor where predators don’t strike.

Treat such places as **wildlife treaties**:
- Don’t camp there.
- Don’t spill food, water, or chemicals there.
- Don’t block the passage.
- Don’t interfere with interactions near it.

If you violate a local “don’t hunt here” pattern, you may become the exception that proves the rule.

### 5) Keep your body heat and scent down
You are a warm, moist, CO₂-breathing anomaly.

- Minimize exposed skin.
- Keep movements steady to reduce sweating.
- Don’t eat openly on the plateau; food odors are a beacon.

### 6) Never put a bare hand on the ground
If you need to steady yourself, use a gloved hand on a known clean rock face.

## Timing: when to arrive, when to leave

Because migration is tied to vent cycles, you want to avoid being present during the “whole system shifts” window until you’ve learned the rhythm.

**First visit strategy**
- Enter, observe, map, and retreat **before** the first major migration surge you notice.
- Watch from a higher, less “occupied” shoulder if possible.

**If you choose to witness a migration**
- Pick a place with:
  - Multiple exits,
  - Good footing,
  - A solid backstop (rock wall),
  - No fissures upwind/downwind of you.
- Do not stand in a “saddle,” narrow passage, or funnel. Those become rivers.

## Interactions with fauna (what not to do)

### Don’t:
- Step into a depression full of “living sediment.”
- Pick up shells/carapaces bare-handed (they may be alive, molting, or chemically defended).
- Assume small = harmless. Chemical defenses scale well.
- Shine strong light directly into the densest aggregations for long periods.
- Leave gear on the ground unattended. It will be climbed, sampled, or moved.

### If something approaches:
- Freeze first. Let it decide you’re not food.
- Slowly back away onto clean rock.
- Do not swat. Swatting is how you get punctured and dosed.

### If you’re surrounded by small crawlers:
- Don’t run (you’ll fall or step into a fissure).
- Move to a **warmer bare-rock zone** near (not on top of) a vent collar if safe—many organisms will avoid unstable heat gradients.
- Use gentle vibration on your own boots (toe tapping) rather than sweeping motions.

## Camps and rests (if you must)

Ideally, you **don’t camp** on the plateau. If you must rest:

- Do it on a **raised slab of bare basalt**, away from vents, away from funnels, away from structures that appear “socially important.”
- Sit on an **insulating pad**; do not sit directly on warm stone.
- Keep all gear **on you** or on a clipped tether.
- Keep the rest brief; lingering makes you “part of the pattern,” and patterns draw attention.

## Contamination & decon (how to leave without importing the place)

### On exit, before you cross back out:
1. Brush down boots and gaiters (dry brush).
2. Wipe hard surfaces with a damp (not wet-dripping) cloth.
3. Seal used wipes + brush in a waste bag.
4. Remove outer layer into a **sealed dirty bag**.
5. Change respirator cartridges if you had any alarms or strong odor events.

### At your safe point:
- Shower (or full wipe-down).
- Inspect skin for tiny punctures or embedded crystals.
- Monitor respiratory symptoms for 24–48 hours.

If you develop persistent cough, metallic taste, burning eyes, or delayed rash: treat it as an exposure, not “just dust.”

## Psychological preparation (the underrated part)

This place can make you feel:
- **Claustrophobic in open space** (because distance collapses),
- **Uneasy without clear threat** (because life is everywhere but quiet),
- **Tempted to test boundaries** (because the danger is subtle).

Countermeasures:
- Use checklists and time boxes.
- Talk through actions with a partner if you have one.
- If alone: narrate aloud occasionally (“marking route; turning left at ribbed wall; gas reading normal”). It keeps you anchored.

## Practical “first recon” checklist

**If you do only one careful visit, do it like this:**
- Arrive with full-face respirator on.
- Place return anchor/beacon at the rim.
- Mark a straight, simple route in (tape/chalk + chem-lights low to ground).
- Spend 10 minutes doing nothing but: gas readings, footing tests, listening.
- Move only on bare rock, avoiding films/mats.
- Identify:
  - One safe retreat line,
  - One “no-go” zone (active fissure field),
  - One observation perch.
- Leave before you feel “used to it.” Familiarity here is how mistakes happen.
