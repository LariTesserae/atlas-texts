## What you’re walking into (so you pack like you mean it)

This place is not “underwater” in the way a sea is underwater. It’s a **flooded industrial volume**—a high, engineered reservoir/cathedral of machinery and plant growth—where:

- **Pressure is “wrong” for depth** (your ears don’t pop), so you can’t use depth-feel for orientation or risk sense.
- **Everything is structure**: sharp edges, grating, valves, catwalks, ducts, intake bells, shafts.
- **Plants are encouraged; animals are discouraged** by continuous sterilization fields and trace biocides.
- **Sound is swallowed**; distance and urgency don’t behave the way your brain expects.
- **There is no open “surface”** to bolt for. The “ceiling” is far above and solid.

So you prepare as if you’re visiting a **flooded, powered facility with active anti-fauna measures**, not a dive site.

## 1) Pre-trip decisions (these matter more than your gear)

### Decide your exposure philosophy
You have three viable approaches:

1. **No-contact approach (recommended):** you never expose skin, eyes, mouth, or suit interior air to local water. You treat the entire chamber as chemically and biologically hostile.
2. **Limited-contact approach:** you accept exterior suit exposure but keep breathing loop and inner layers isolated. You can work longer, but you still plan decon and time limits.
3. **Wet-diving approach (not recommended here):** open-circuit scuba, exposed wetsuit/skin. This is how you get quietly injured by low-level biocides/fields and don’t notice until you’re back through the gate.

Given the site’s “nothing that moves is allowed to persist” rule, assume **human tissue counts as “moving animal life.”** Plan accordingly.

### Set a hard maximum time-in-chamber
Even if you feel fine, treat the sterilization environment like low-dose radiation: **time is exposure**. Pick a limit (e.g., 20–40 minutes for first entry), and enforce it with alarms you can’t ignore.

### Choose your entry/exit method
Do not enter unless you have **a guaranteed, retraceable exit**:

- Best: **tethered to your portal anchor** (physical line or powered tether) + redundant beacon.
- Next best: mapped corridor from a dry access lock.
- Worst: “I’ll just navigate back visually.” In a place like this, you won’t.

## 2) Core life-support kit (assume the water is not for you)

### Breathing
- **Closed-circuit rebreather (CCR)** or **surface-supplied breathing** from your anchor point.
  - You want *zero dependence* on local dissolved gases.
  - Bring redundant O₂ supply and a bailout bottle.
- **Full-face mask inside a sealed helmet** if possible. Eyes and mucosa are the easiest places to get chemically irritated.

### Suit
- **Dry suit** with chemical-resistant outer layer (or a light hard-suit if you have it).
- **Cut-resistant gloves** (industrial, not reef gloves). Everything is bolts, burrs, grating.
- **Redundant seals**: neck/wrist rings you can re-seat, patch kit that works underwater.

### Shielding against the “anti-animal” measures
You don’t know whether the discouragement system is primarily:
- electric fields,
- ultrasonic,
- UV sterilization,
- reactive oxidizers,
- trace heavy-metal dosing,
- or something stranger-but-physical.

Pack for the boring versions first:

- **Faraday-mesh layer** around torso/helmet electronics (and ideally your whole suit) to reduce field coupling.
- **EM/ultrasound dosimeter** if you have access to one; otherwise, bring simple indicators:
  - a wideband EM sniffer,
  - a contact thermometer (some sterilization systems correlate with heat cycling),
  - and a chemical indicator strip kit for oxidizers (chlorine/peroxide/ozone families).

If you can’t measure exposure, you can’t manage it—so manage it by **short time, sealed suit, and predictable retreat.**

## 3) Mobility and navigation (you will get disoriented if you don’t plan for it)

### Buoyancy and propulsion
- Aim for **slightly negative buoyancy**. The environment is cluttered; uncontrolled ascent into trusses/ducting is a real hazard.
- Bring a **small DPV (underwater scooter)** only if it has:
  - a shrouded prop,
  - a dead-man switch,
  - and you’re disciplined about not stirring particulates near intake zones.

Otherwise: **frog kicks / careful finning** and handholds (with gloves).

### Light
The ambient light is warm and clear, but it’s *architectural*—shafts, bands, and “pretty” areas that can trick you into thinking you’ve got visibility everywhere.

Bring:
- **Primary light** with a tight beam (for seeing edges and seams).
- **Secondary flood light** (for reading spaces, not objects).
- **Two backups**. If your light goes, the place becomes a maze of soft green clutter fast.

### Wayfinding in a 3D machine garden
Do at least two of these, not one:

- **Guideline reel** (primary) + safety spool (backup).
- **Tactile markers** you can clip onto grating/bolts (non-corroding, non-magnetic if possible).
- **Inertial nav puck** or breadcrumb beacons (but don’t trust radio to get through water and metal).
- **Handheld sonar** can help, but dense structure creates confusing returns. Use it for *void detection* (shafts, bays), not mapping.

Rule: if you enter a corridor/slot where you cannot turn your body around easily, you are in a **one-mistake environment**. Lay line before you commit.

## 4) Comms (assume you can’t shout, and acoustics won’t save you)

- **Tether comms** (cable) are best.
- Through-water acoustic comms may work but will be muffled/short-range in clutter.
- Have **a fully rehearsed set of light signals** (flashes) and touch signals if paired.
- If solo: your suit should run an **automatic return timer** (vibration alarm) so you don’t “just check one more bay” until you’re low on bailout gas.

## 5) Environmental hazards specific to this chamber

### A) The “nothing that moves persists” layer
Expect subtle human effects even if the system was designed for small fauna:

- skin tingling or numbness,
- eye irritation,
- metallic taste (if any leaks into breathing loop—bad sign),
- unusual fatigue,
- nausea or vertigo (fields + disorientation),
- headache after exit.

Mitigations:
- Keep **all water outside the suit**.
- Don’t loiter near **flush wall panels that hum** (those are almost certainly part of the deterrent system).
- If you begin to feel “odd,” do not analyze it in place. **Leave.** This site rewards early exits.

### B) Industrial geometry hazards
- **Intake bells / pump inlets:** the flow may be gentle until it isn’t. Keep distance; avoid dangling lines.
- **Vertical shafts and settling bays:** they look inviting and open compared to the clutter; they’re also where you can lose reference and line-of-sight instantly.
- **Pressure doors and hatches:** do not trust them to stay static. Automated cycles can still exist.

Practical rule: if you see a structure that looks like it was built to move (doors, valves, gates), assume it **can** move.

### C) Plant entanglement and “engineered clinging”
Many plants here sound designed to grip metal (micro-suction pores, gasket-like pads). Treat them like living cable ties.

Pack:
- **Two cutters**: a line cutter + a serrated blade/shears capable of slicing fibrous plant matter.
- **Non-snag profile**: minimize dangling straps, hoses, accessories.
- If something grabs you, do not thrash; you’ll drive yourself deeper into the mesh. Stop, back out a centimeter at a time, cut only what you must.

### D) Slip and puncture risks
Biofilm turns every surface into a quiet hazard.

- Assume every handhold is slick.
- Move like you’re in a machine room wearing thick gloves—slow, deliberate, three points of contact when possible.
- Bring **underwater epoxy/patches** sized for small punctures and seam leaks.

## 6) Entry protocol (how to not bring trouble in, and not bring it back out)

### Before you cross
- **Sterilize your exterior** as much as is safe for your suit materials. You don’t want to introduce animal microbes that trigger stronger sterilization response—or clog filtration screens with unexpected biology.
- Confirm:
  - rebreather scrubber duration,
  - bailout pressure,
  - line kit attached and accessible,
  - exit beacon active,
  - timer set.

### On arrival
Pause for 60–90 seconds and do nothing but observe:
- Where are the **bright lens-shafts**? (likely growth-dense, snag-prone)
- Where are the **clear tunnels**? (possibly treated corridors—also possibly “keep-clear” maintenance paths)
- Where do you see **fine particulates tilting**? (that reveals flow direction—important near intakes)

Then choose a route that gives you at least one “hard reference” surface (wall or floor grid) the whole way.

### Behavior inside
- Don’t touch panels that are flush, smooth, and “too deliberate.” Assume they’re part of sterilization/monitoring.
- Avoid breathing-loop or suit maintenance underwater. If you must fix something, retreat to your nearest known-safe pocket (wider bay with minimal hum/panel density).

## 7) Sampling and interaction (if you’re here to learn, not just sightsee)

If you plan to collect anything (plant tissue, biofilm, water, residues):

- Use **sealed, double-contained sample tubes** that never open again until you’re back in a controlled environment.
- Label with:
  - location (use your own grid reference from line markers),
  - proximity to “humming panels,”
  - light level (in-lens shaft vs diffuse),
  - water temp.
- Do not scrape large areas. In a managed system, disturbing growth can change local filtration and may trigger maintenance cycles you’re not ready for.

Photography/video:
- Bring a **color reference slate**. The warm filtered light will lie to your camera and your later memory.
- Expect reflections from layered glazing to produce “impossible” images. Document angles and distances so you can later separate optical tricks from anomalies.

## 8) Exit and decontamination (do not skip this)

### Immediate exit
Once you decide to leave, **leave directly**. This place is engineered to make everything feel calm and incremental; that’s how you overstay.

### Decon priorities
You are carrying two classes of risk out:

1. **Engineered plant spores/biofilms** adapted to metal and controlled nutrient flows (they may colonize your other gear or other worlds).
2. **Residual biocides/sterilants** on suit exterior that can hurt you later in a confined air environment.

Minimum decon:
- Exterior rinse in a neutral medium (if available) + wipe-down.
- Remove and bag outer layers before entering any dry habitat.
- Filter/replace breathing loop filters if there’s any chance of chemical ingress.
- Shower/eye rinse as soon as practical, even if you “never got wet.” Vapors and residues still transfer.

Medical self-check for 24 hours:
- skin irritation, unusual fatigue, headache, chest tightness, palpitations, numbness.
If any appear, assume exposure and treat it seriously.

## 9) What to mentally rehearse (this place messes with human instincts)

- **Altitude feeling while submerged:** your body will insist “I’m high up,” not “I’m deep.” That can make you careless about ascent/descent, or overly confident about “finding the way out.”
- **The cathedral hush:** silence feels like safety. Here, silence is just damping.
- **No animals:** humans are wired to use animal presence as “environmental normal.” The emptiness will put you slightly on edge; don’t let that push you into rushing.
- **Soft edges and crowded surfaces:** you will underestimate how close things are and snag gear.

Rehearse: stop → breathe → check line → check timer → check exit direction.
