## What you’re actually visiting
This pocket is not “a cave you swim into.” It’s an *overhead, fully submerged room* with three compounding hazards:

1. **Physical confinement**: narrow entrance, dense vegetation, sharp young rock, heavy silt.
2. **Ecological grip**: the place actively “packs itself”—clearings close, pathways vanish, vegetation re-occupies space.
3. **Spatial misbehavior centered on boundaries**: edges attract matter and sometimes “crease” space (doubles, wrong distances, brief gel-like layers).

Prepare as if for a **cold, silty, plant-choked cave dive** *plus* a small reality anomaly where **navigation by sight and intuition becomes unreliable**.

If you are not already trained for overhead diving, the honest preparation is: **don’t go in bodily**—send a tethered camera or a sacrificial probe first.

---

## Before you go: prerequisites and planning

### Training you should have (minimum)
- **Overhead environment / cave-diving fundamentals**: guideline use, zero-visibility exit, anti-silt propulsion, lost-line and lost-buddy drills.
- **Cold-water exposure management** (drysuit or thick wetsuit proficiency).
- **Entanglement response**: controlled cutting, not thrashing.

If you don’t have these, the pocket’s “dislikes open water” rule turns ordinary mistakes (a fin kick, a panicked turn) into a slow, quiet trap.

### Timing and external conditions
Because entry is “beneath a seam” and exchange is slow:
- **Plan for tide/level changes** (even subtle ones). The entrance being “brushed shut by growth” means small changes can make it *much* harder to find from the inside.
- Go when outside water is **calmest** (least surge), because surge will pump silt and push vegetation into the throat.
- Assume the pocket has **worse visibility the moment you touch anything**.

### Team and surface support
- **Do not solo.** Go as a buddy pair at minimum.
- Have a **surface/shore tender** whose only job is to monitor time and manage a tether spool (and call for help).
- Agree on a **hard turn time** and a **hard exit pressure** (gas management). In a place that changes distances, “just one more minute” is a classic failure mode.

## Equipment: choose tools that don’t create “strong edges”

The pocket responds to boundaries. You can’t avoid edges entirely, but you can avoid *sharp, high-contrast, rigid* ones that act like new “tuning forks.”

### Breathing system
- **Redundant gas** is non-negotiable (overhead environment).
  - Best: sidemount doubles or backmount doubles with isolation manifold.
  - Acceptable: single tank *only* with a true redundant bailout (pony) sized for a calm exit.
- Prefer a **rebreather** only if you’re already qualified; bubbles can:
  - dislodge silt,
  - stir peat gas,
  - and in weird geometry, bubbles may not reliably indicate “up.”

### Exposure protection
- **Drysuit** strongly recommended (persistent cool + slow movement + stress = hypothermia risk).
- Hood + gloves. You will be in contact with stone and plants; you want protection without losing tactile sense.

### Lighting (make it diffuse)
Light is “used up” here and hard beams create harsh boundaries.
- Primary light with a **wide, diffused beam** (add a diffuser if needed).
- Two backups.
- Avoid strobe/flash unless necessary for documentation; sudden hard-edged illumination can worsen disorientation and silt-out.

### Navigation: don’t rely on compass or vision
- Bring a **primary guideline reel** and a **safety spool**.
- But: a taut, thin line is an edge. So use:
  - **Thicker, softer, matte line** (less “knife-line” boundary),
  - kept **slightly slack** rather than piano-wire tight,
  - routed to avoid sharp corners and stacked-stone features.
- Use **tactile markers** (line cookies/knots you can feel with gloves).
- Expect electronics (compass, IMU, even dive computer) to be *less trustworthy* near the built edges. Bring analog backups if you can.

### Entanglement tools (carry two, placed where you can reach them blind)
Vegetation is ribboning and the chamber closes gaps.
- **Trauma shears** (preferred: cuts plants without wild stabbing).
- **Line cutter** (hook style).
- A knife is last resort—sharp edges + frantic motions are exactly what this pocket punishes.

### Buoyancy and propulsion
- Use fins that support **frog kick** and **modified flutter** (low silt).
- Consider slightly more **neutral buoyancy** than usual; you may not have a consistent “up/down,” so you want to hover without fighting.

### Minimal-contact probing
For “doubles” and gel-layers:
- A **short, blunt probe** (rounded plastic or rubber tip) to test before you put a hand into a suspicious “reflection.”
- Avoid metal if you can—not because it’s forbidden, but because cold metal encourages you to grip hard and it makes crisp edges.

### Emergency and aftercare
- Thermal blanket, warm drink, dry clothes at exit.
- Oxygen kit if you’re doing anything that could accidentally become a decompression event.
- First aid for cuts (young basalt edges are sharp).

## Personal preparation: how to move and think in there

### Mindset: slow is safety
This is not a place to “swim.” It’s a place to **inchworm**.
- Maintain **three-point contact** whenever possible: one hand on line, one hand on environment, fins controlled.
- Move as if every motion writes something into the room that the room will try to erase—because it will.

### Buoyancy discipline
- If you touch the bottom, you create a silt cloud that becomes your new weather system.
- If you touch the vegetation, you may get gently but persistently wrapped.
- Practice hovering with **micro-adjustments**. No big inflator bursts.

### Communication
The pocket muffles sound and “seems to lower its volume further” when you stop.
- Use **touch signals** and **light signals** close-range.
- Stay close enough to maintain contact in one kick. If you lose your buddy in silt here, the pocket will happily close the gap you need to search.

## Entry protocol (the part most likely to go wrong)

### 1) Establish an outside reference that cannot be “packed over”
At the seam/roots/shoreline break:
- Secure your line to a **large, stable anchor** outside the pocket (rock/roots you trust).
- Put a **secondary marker** outside that you can find even in bad viz (a weighted tag line, a textured float, something tactile).

### 2) Don’t “force” the curtain
That ribboning leaf curtain is both a filter and a trap.
- Part it with **forearms** and the **rounded probe**, not fins.
- If it resists, back out and re-approach; don’t thrash. Thrashing creates open water briefly, and the pocket will close it at the worst moment.

### 3) Tie in early, then commit
Place your first tie-off *before* you’re fully in the chamber so you can always retreat to the seam by touch.

## Inside behavior: the rules that keep you alive

### Treat built edges like active anomalies
Those stacked stones, steps-to-nowhere, and lintel slabs are where:
- currents kink,
- silt spirals strangely,
- distance lies,
- and doubles appear.

Protocol:
- **Do not thread your guideline through** the built structures.
- **Do not place yourself between** a strong edge and a closing curtain of plants.
- Observe from a little distance, then back away along your line.

### How to handle “doubles” safely
If you see an offset “second” version of a step/lintel:
- Stop.
- Stabilize buoyancy.
- Test with the **rounded probe** first.
- If it feels like “gel then release,” do not push through. Mark that zone mentally as a *soft boundary* and route around it.

A useful rule: **if it looks like a clean shortcut, it’s probably the pocket folding space along an edge.**

### Distance lies: switch to counted movement
When an arm’s-length becomes three strokes:
- Don’t chase it.
- Use your guideline and **measured pulls** (hand-over-hand).
- If you must approach an object, do it by **incremental tethering**: advance the line, check tension, advance again.

### Assume your path is being erased behind you
Because the pocket “dislikes open water,” your exit corridor may narrow while you’re inside.
- Turn early.
- Keep your line route simple and retraceable.
- Avoid making a “tour loop.” Do in-and-out.

## Time, gas, and thermal limits (be conservative)
This chamber is small, but it is not consistent, and you may end up spending time just re-finding the obvious.

- Use the **rule of thirds** for gas (or more conservative):  
  1/3 in, 1/3 out, 1/3 reserve.
- Set a **cold limit**: if you start shivering or losing hand dexterity, you leave immediately. This place rewards slowness; hypothermia removes your ability to be slow and precise.

## What not to bring (or how to bring it safely)

### Avoid
- Long dangling straps, snorkels, loose hoses, jewelry: entanglement bait.
- Hard-edged frames and crates: they create strong boundaries and snag points.
- Nets and anything that can “bag” water and plants.

### If you must bring instruments/cameras
- Use **rounded housings**, matte tape over sharp logos/edges, minimal protrusions.
- Mount on a **short lanyard** you can cut instantly.

## Emergency procedures specific to this pocket

### If the entrance “vanishes” in vegetation
- Do not search by sight. Search by **line**.
- If you somehow lost the line:
  - Stop, breathe, stabilize.
  - Perform a **systematic touch search** (cave “lost line” drill) while staying in one place—do not wander into built-edge zones.
  - Your buddy should do the same; reunite by protocol, not by calling.

### If you become entangled
- Freeze.
- Identify whether it’s plant wrap or guideline wrap.
- Cut **plants first with shears**, deliberately. Avoid sawing motions that stir silt.
- If it’s the guideline and you must cut it, do so only after you’ve:
  - secured yourself to a secondary spool,
  - marked direction,
  - and ensured you can still find the exit reference.

### If you feel the “push off floor/ceiling at once” disorientation
- Close your eyes.
- Find the guideline with both hands.
- Make buoyancy neutral.
- Move only by **line pulls**, not by “swimming.”

## After you exit: decontam, decompress, debrief
This is a contained ecology and you’re a vector.
- Rinse and decontam gear (especially anything that touched moss/peat).
- Check for cuts (basalt can slice through gloves).
- Write down observations immediately. Places with inconsistent distance and visibility have a way of rewriting your memory of them.

---

## A practical first-visit plan (recommended)
1. **Scout without entry**: map the seam/roots area outside; identify stable anchors.
2. **Tethered probe pass**: send a small rounded camera on a soft line through the curtain, *without a diver committing overhead*.
3. **Short penetration dive**: one objective only (e.g., confirm chamber size + locate built stones), then exit.
4. **Only then** attempt close observation of edge-structures, and only with strict turn limits.
