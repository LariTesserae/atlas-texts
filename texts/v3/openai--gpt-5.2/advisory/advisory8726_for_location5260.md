## What you’re walking into (in practical terms)

This is a high-altitude, cold-wet, low-light megastructure on an actively “young” plateau: unstable ground, constant condensation, pervasive conductive moisture, and a dense animal population that treats the city as its habitat and its food web. The place “expects” navigation and access by **sound/pressure signatures**, not by sight. Energy and minerals are the base of the ecology; surfaces are slick, electrically interesting, and used as corridors by large moving bodies.

Your preparation should assume:
- **Hypoxia** (thin air) + **cold damp** (fast heat loss)
- **Near-dark** with fog that can erase visibility completely
- **Slippery everything**, with hidden ice ribs and thin films
- **Constant noise** + important acoustic cues you can’t afford to miss
- **Fauna-as-traffic**: you’re entering migration lanes, feeding grounds, and rookeries, not “streets”
- **Access systems keyed to sound** (gates/slits/iris apertures) that may ignore you—or react to you

## Pre-departure: medical and body prep

### 1) Acclimation plan (don’t improvise altitude)
If you can’t pre-acclimate, bring support that makes you functional while mildly hypoxic.
- **Portable oxygen** (not just a canister—an actual regulated system) sized for exertion.
- **Pulse oximeter** and a rule: if your SpO₂ drops and stays low even at rest, you stop moving.
- **Acetazolamide** (if medically appropriate) for altitude adaptation—decide before you go.
- Train for “slow work”: you’ll move like it’s underwater. If you push, you’ll gas out.

### 2) Cold-wet management (the dangerous combo)
This isn’t dry alpine cold; it’s *soaking, conductive* cold.
- Bring a clothing system that works when wet: **synthetic or wool base**, **insulating mid**, **waterproof breathable shell**.
- Avoid cotton entirely.
- Plan for **hands failing first**: you need dexterity gloves + over-mitts.

### 3) Respiratory exposure
Fog and “city exhalations” imply aerosols and fine particulates.
- Bring a **P100 / FFP3-class respirator** (or powered respirator if you can manage it).
- Eye protection that seals (fog will find gaps).

## Core kit (optimized for this specific place)

### A) Vision is secondary: build for audio-first navigation
You still need vision, but you must *not* depend on it.
- **Directional microphone + bone-conduction headset** so you can listen while your ears stay protected.
- **Variable hearing protection** (filtered earplugs, not full muffs). You need to reduce fatigue without losing directional cues.
- **Audio recorder** (seriously): being able to replay a call pattern or a “safe corridor sound” can save you.
- Optional but powerful: a **handheld ultrasonic rangefinder / LIDAR** for fog. (A flashlight won’t cut it.)

### B) Light discipline
The city doesn’t answer with bright lamps; bright light may also attract or agitate.
- **Low-lumen, red/amber headlamp** for close work.
- **IR illuminator + thermal monocular** if you have it (thermal will read bodies through darkness/fog better than visible light).
- Avoid strobe/scan lights.

### C) Footing and fall prevention (this will be your #1 daily hazard)
Everything is wet, mirror-slick, or secretly iced.
- Boots with **aggressive compound** plus **microspikes** you can put on fast.
- **Trekking pole** with carbide tip (also doubles as a “probe” for spongy mineral mats).
- **Climbing-rated harness + short lanyard** if you expect elevated walkways/bridges; a slip on wet infrastructure can be unrecoverable.

### D) Electrical + corrosion awareness
Your environment is a conductive film over grooved metals, rails, and energized lines. Some creatures feed from charge.
- **Insulated gloves** for any hand contact with rails/panels.
- **Non-conductive walking pole** if you’re probing near cables.
- Keep electronics in **sealed cases with desiccant**; use **conformal-coated** gear if possible.
- Pack a small **multimeter + non-contact voltage detector**. The place “bleeds energy.”

### E) Water: abundant but not automatically safe
Condensation is a solvent full of dissolved salts and whatever the city sheds.
- Bring your own water, and a **filter + chemical backup**.
- Do **not** drink local condensate unless you can test conductivity/heavy metals and you have a plan for dissolved contaminants.
- If you must use local water: prefer pooled areas away from active conduits, then filter + treat—still risky.

### F) Shelter and rest
You need a dry microclimate to recover; otherwise you’ll spiral into fatigue/hypothermia.
- **Bivy or small tent** that can handle constant wet and high wind.
- **Insulated pad** (ground will wick heat relentlessly).
- **Heat source** only if you can vent safely—also consider that heat may attract ground swarms that cluster at leaks.

## Behavioral prep: how not to get “read” as a problem

### 1) You are entering traffic, not wilderness
Those “streets” are migration lanes and feeding grounds. Your best survival strategy is to behave like a non-entity:
- Move **predictably** and **slowly**.
- Don’t block narrow corridors.
- Don’t stand in underpasses where water + herds share the same volume.
- When a mass of bodies is coming, **yield early** and get to a vertical edge/alcove.

### 2) Sound etiquette (this place has acoustic rules)
If apertures respond to “sound signatures,” your noises matter.
- Eliminate loose gear clatter: tape buckles, secure metal.
- Avoid shouting. Use **short, low voice** or text comms.
- Don’t mimic local calls unless you know what you’re doing; it may be territorial, predatory, or a “gate key.”
- Use a **metronome-like soft foot pace**—erratic footfalls can read as distress/prey.

### 3) Smell and residue
Even without plants, chemistry matters.
- Avoid leaving food smells; pack waste airtight.
- Assume local swarms can track mineral salts: your sweat and battery residue may be interesting. Keep gear clean, wipe spills.

## On arrival: the first 30 minutes checklist

1) **Stop moving**. Let your hearing map the area.
2) Check **SpO₂, temperature**, hand function.
3) Note the **dominant hum** (baseline). If it changes sharply, you’re near an active trunk line or stress adjustment.
4) Identify **three escape niches**: an alcove above ground level, a recessed doorway (even if closed), and an elevated structure you can clip into.
5) Decide travel rules immediately:
   - Never cross a corridor you can’t hear through.
   - Never step onto a mirror-dark surface without a test step/probe.
   - Never touch patterned metal without insulated gloves.

## Moving through the city: practical route strategy

### Use “acoustic topography”
Because fog and darkness collapse distance, you’ll navigate by sound layers:
- **Sub-audible tooth-hum** = big infrastructure beneath; don’t camp over it.
- **Periodic clack** = tensioning/adjustment cycles; don’t be under suspended loads during a clack period.
- **Rushing conduit-water** = reliable landmark; also a high-traffic animal lane.

### Avoid the “three bad overlaps”
The most dangerous places are where these overlap:
1) Water sheet + narrow passage  
2) Herd corridor + low ceiling (limited vertical escape)  
3) Active charge line + condensation drip (shock risk + creature congregation)

### Step selection in wet-dark
- Favor surfaces with **texture**: ribs, seams, grit.
- Treat perfectly smooth dark patches as either **thin ice** or **biofilm** until proven otherwise.
- Probe the spongy mineral mats: they rebound, but edges can conceal sudden holes where new cracks drank runoff.

## Interfacing with gates and sound-keyed apertures

Don’t assume “door = entry.”
- Approach slowly, stand off to the side, and **wait**—many systems “listen” first.
- If nothing happens: do not escalate by banging or forcing. That’s how you get classified as a threat.
- If you must attempt a “signature,” use a **controlled, simple tone** (single frequency sweep at low volume) rather than complex noises.
- Be ready for the opening to be temporary and narrow—have your pack streamlined; no dangling items.

If you can bring one specialized tool: a **programmable tone emitter** with precise volume control and the ability to record-and-replay ambient “access chirps” you hear locally. Use sparingly; replaying the wrong sound could summon something that thinks you’re food or competition.

## Fauna hazards (and how to not become part of the ecology)

### Large file-moving quadrupeds
- Don’t cut between individuals; they move like a system.
- Their “oil-gloss ridges” and bubbling breath suggest wet respiration—give them space; aerosols may be biologically active.
- If one stops: you stop. A stopped line often precedes a surge.

### Flocks navigating by echo/pressure
- Your light is less relevant than your silhouette and turbulence. Sudden arm-waves can trigger collision.
- If the chatter tightens and “rolls” toward you: crouch, go still, protect eyes, let them pass.

### Ground carpets/swarms
They open paths without panic—take the hint.
- Don’t stomp into dense clusters; go around. They may be feeding on salts/oxides, and your boots are “new mineral.”
- Keep heat sources insulated; they cluster around heat leaks.
- If they begin climbing your gear: increase smoothness (cover fabric with slick shell), and move to a colder, drier patch.

### Predators at mineral pools
Assume ambush at edges.
- Don’t kneel to sample water.
- Don’t linger at rims. Observe from elevation if possible.

## Camp discipline (if you must sleep there)

- Camp **above** the lowest drainage line and **away** from the loudest hum.
- Pick a site with:
  - minimal drip points overhead (bridges/cables are constant rain)
  - textured ground (less ice sheen)
  - at least two exits
- Drying protocol:
  - Strip inner layers, wipe down, change base layer
  - Get hands/feet warm and dry before sleep—this is where you lose the expedition

Keep lights minimal; rely on thermal for checks.

## Emergency planning (this place punishes “winging it”)

### 1) Slip/fall injury
- Have a plan to self-extract without needing speed.
- Carry a **compact splint**, pain control, and a way to **clip in** and rest off your feet.

### 2) Hypoxia spiral
Symptoms: irrational decisions, clumsiness, sudden “I’m fine” feeling.
- Hard rule: if you feel euphoric or impulsive, you stop and oxygenate.
- Don’t “push to reach shelter.” You’ll fall first.

### 3) Electrical incident
- Don’t grab a shocked person directly in a wet environment.
- Break contact with non-conductive tools; move them to a dry-ish surface; treat burns as contaminated.

### 4) Becoming acoustically “targeted”
If you notice the environment responding to you (calls aligning, swarms tracking, gates “following” your approach):
- Reduce sound output: stop, secure gear, lower voice to zero.
- Move to a boundary zone (edge of corridor, up onto ribbed structure).
- Wait for the system to “forget” you, then change route.
