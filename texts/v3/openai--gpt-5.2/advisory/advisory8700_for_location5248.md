## What you’re walking into (so you pack for the right problem)
This is not “underground” the way a cave is underground. It’s a constructed void: tiled, plastered, bridged, netted, and continuously inhabited by *systems*—water management, light management, traffic patterns, and an ecology that has learned the architecture as if it were geology.

For a traveler, that means:

- **Everything is hard, slick, and echoing.** Falls, dropped gear, and noise mistakes are the most likely first-day hazards.
- **Humidity and condensation are intermittent and localized.** You’ll be alternately dry-dusty and film-wet, sometimes within a few steps.
- **Verticality is normal.** You are moving through a 3D lattice of ladders, platforms, ropes, and bridges.
- **Animals are not background.** Bats, birds, insects, rodents, reptiles, and amphibians are active “traffic,” and you will cross their routes.
- **There are unwritten rules encoded into the build.** If you violate water flow, light-well openness, or established pathways, the place “pushes back” quickly.

Prepare like you’re visiting an ancient working aqueduct + attic roost + ship’s rigging, all at once.

## Physical conditioning & mindset
**Train before you go** (even lightly):

- **Ankles/knees:** step-downs, balance work, and slow stair descents. Tile + damp + worn edges = ankle traps.
- **Grip and shoulders:** you’ll use rails, ropes, and ladders more than you expect.
- **Heat tolerance:** it’s warm without sun—more like a crowded workshop than a forest. If you overheat easily, plan accordingly.

**Mindset:** act like a guest inside a living machine. Don’t “explore” by improvising; learn the flows first.

## Clothing: what works in warm damp with abrasive hard surfaces
Aim for layers that handle moisture swings and constant brushing against rope, plaster, and masonry.

- **Base layer:** synthetic or merino that dries fast. Avoid cotton; it stays wet and chills when drafts “breathe” down.
- **Mid layer:** thin fleece or grid layer you can put on when you’re stationary in the cool exhale periods.
- **Outer:** light, abrasion-resistant overshirt or softshell. You want something that won’t snag constantly on hooks and lashings.
- **Pants:** tough fabric with stretch; knee-friendly. Consider **knee pads** if you expect to crouch on tile or beams.
- **Gloves:** one dexterous pair (rope handling) + optional rubberized thin pair for wet, algae-slick surfaces.
- **Footwear (most important):**
  - sticky rubber sole (approach shoes or light boots),
  - good drainage/quick-dry,
  - toe protection (tiles and jar edges bite),
  - bring **two pairs of socks per day** if you can—your feet will cycle between clammy and dry-dusty.

**Head/eyes:**
- **Clear protective glasses** (insects, dust, droppings, stray fibers, sudden drafts).
- **Light cap or buff** to keep bat/bird fallout out of hair.

## Respiratory and infection prep (this place has “attic biology”)
Because of bats + birds + warm damp + rodent presence, treat air and surfaces as potentially contaminated.

**Before travel (if you can):**
- Make sure your **tetanus** is current.
- If your baseline universe has it and you can access it, consider **rabies pre-exposure vaccination** (bats are unpredictable in roost-dense spaces).
- If you’re immunocompromised, reconsider the trip or bring higher-grade respiratory protection.

**Bring:**
- **Respirator or high-filtration mask** (N95/FFP2 minimum; P100/FFP3 if you have it), especially if you’ll go near upper haze, roost beams, or dusty repair areas.
- **Hand hygiene:** alcohol gel plus a small soap sheet pack; you will touch rails/ropes constantly.
- **Wound care:** disinfectant, steri-strips, blister care. Small cuts in warm damp environments turn into big problems.
- **Antifungal cream/powder** (feet, skin folds).
- **Electrolytes** (warm, damp air + exertion drains you even if you don’t “feel sweaty”).
- If you have allergies/asthma: your meds plus a spare inhaler; expect insect particulate, dander, droppings dust.

## Gear that makes this place safer (and helps you not break its rules)
### Light & navigation (even though “light is normal”)
The ambient light can be readable, but **you still need personal light** because occlusions, platforms under platforms, and ladder wells create sudden dim pockets.

- **Headlamp** (wide flood + red mode). Red mode matters because you *will* be near birds/bats; you don’t want to panic the ceiling.
- **Spare battery** sealed in a bag with desiccant.
- **Small directional flashlight** for looking up shafts without staring into glare.

**Navigation aids:**
- A **compact notebook + pencil** (works when damp).
- **Non-residue marking method**: removable tape tabs or a small clip you can reclaim—*avoid chalking walls/floors* if locals interpret markings as maintenance signals.
- If you must mark: mark *your gear*, not their infrastructure.

### Vertical safety & handling the “traffic”
- **6–8 m of thin static cord** + a few **carabiners**: for tethering your pack, stabilizing yourself at a platform edge, or creating a handline without tying into questionable old lashings.
- **A belay/descender isn’t mandatory** for a careful visitor, but a simple **friction device** can save you if you misjudge a ladder pitch.
- **Compact first-aid shears/knife** (rope entanglements happen in netted spaces).

### Water discipline kit (because “don’t fight it; guide it”)
Assume locals have strict water hygiene norms.

- Bring a **sealed bottle** and a **filter**; do not drink from cistern basins unless explicitly invited and you understand their upstream.
- Bring a **small collapsible cup** rather than dipping your bottle into shared water.
- Avoid soaps or chemicals near channels. If you must clean, do it over your own container.

### Noise control (sound here is “information”)
- **Soft gear management:** cloth wraps, rubber bands, or Velcro straps to stop clatter.
- **Earplugs** for rest (bat colonies + crickets + constant drip will erode your sleep).

## Behavioral etiquette: how to not become “the problem”
Even if you never see a caretaker, this place behaves like one is always present.

### 1) Respect the water paths
- Never block a gutter, trough, or drip bowl—*even temporarily*.
- Don’t set packs where water is pitched to flow. The slope is subtle on purpose.
- If you spill, wipe it and then *move*—standing water migrates into rope and reed.

### 2) Keep light-wells and reflectors unimpeded
- Don’t toss anything into shafts “to see what happens.”
- Don’t climb in a way that sheds debris into a light throat.
- If you carry loose fiber (bandages, frayed cord), secure it—birds will steal it, and that can change nest distribution.

### 3) Don’t create panic currents
The wildlife uses airflow and echo the way city dwellers use streets.

- Avoid sudden shouting; speak steadily.
- Don’t run under roost zones; it triggers cascades of flight.
- If a daily cool “exhale” begins, **pause** on bridges and ladders; swaying racks and airflow shifts can unbalance you.

### 4) Treat ropes and ladders as load-bearing history
A ladder “older than straight” might still be trustworthy—or it might be ceremonial, obsolete, or half-rotten under a hardened skin.

- Test every rung with *progressive weight*.
- Prefer **multiple points of contact**.
- Don’t cut or “fix” lashings unless asked; repairs here are part of a living maintenance language.

## Managing the wildlife (practical, not heroic)
- **Bats:** don’t handle; don’t corner; don’t shine directly into dense roosts. If one collides with you, wash/clean immediately and treat any bite/scratch seriously.
- **Bird shafts:** expect fast fly-throughs. Keep goggles on when looking up; don’t stand directly under active corridors for long.
- **Insects:** repellent helps, but mechanical control is better—tight cuffs, tucked shirt, sealed snacks. Avoid strong fragrances; they can draw swarms.
- **Rodents:** never leave food open; don’t sleep on the floor if you can avoid it.
- **Snakes/lizards/amphibians:** watch warm conduits and cistern edges. Don’t put hands into dark crevices—use your light first.

## Food, sleep, and recovery
### Food
Bring foods that don’t crumble into the ecosystem.

- Prefer **dense, low-crumb** items (bars, dried fruit, jerky, rice balls wrapped, hard cheese).
- Pack in **hard containers** where possible; rodents can chew soft bags quickly.
- Carry out all scraps. In a closed ecology, “a little food” is a major intervention.

### Sleep
If you can choose, **sleep elevated** and away from obvious drip-lines.

- A **hammock** works if there are trustworthy anchors (but don’t assume historic beams are OK to load).
- Otherwise, a **compact pad** plus a **groundsheet** that won’t trap water underneath.
- Use earplugs; consider an eye mask only if you’re comfortable waking in a bright, echoing place.

## What to do on arrival (first hour protocol)
1. **Stand still and listen.** Identify:
   - active water channels,
   - the main bird/bat corridors,
   - where the drafts come down,
   - where the loudest echo points are (they’re often crossroads).
2. **Pick a “home base” spot** that is:
   - not under a drip,
   - not in the middle of a traffic bridge,
   - not adjacent to stored materials (jars, reeds, skins).
3. **Secure your gear against clatter** immediately.
4. **Hydrate** and set an internal schedule—warm damp spaces trick you into under-drinking.
5. **Watch how locals/animals move** (if people are present, follow their routes; if not, follow the *worn routes* and avoid pristine edges).

## Emergency planning (don’t skip this)
This is a place where you can be uninjured and still get stranded.

**Primary risks:** slip/fall, entanglement, respiratory irritation, infection from minor cuts, disorientation in a multi-level lattice.

Bring and pre-decide:
- A **rally point** (a distinctive platform, a particular reflector bank, a unique tiled repair patch).
- A **buddy rule** if you’re not alone: no solo ladder/bridge crossings when tired.
- A **rapid-exit kit** kept on your body: light, mask, water, small first aid, cord—because your pack can end up on the wrong side of a net or bridge.

If you become lost: go *toward* the strongest organized sound (wheels, steady drip arrays, main drafts), not toward silence. Silence here often means dead-end storage, roost haze, or maintenance voids.

## The one preparation most travelers miss: your impact
Because this chamber’s “normal daylight” and livability are maintained by balance, a visitor can disrupt it without noticing:

- leaving food changes rodent routes,
- blocking one gutter changes moisture maps,
- stirring roost dust changes air quality,
- taking “souvenirs” (a tile, a reed braid, a hook) removes load-bearing redundancy.

Prepare to be the kind of traveler who leaves **no new gradients** behind: no new wet spots, no new smells, no new food sources, no new obstructions, no new noise habits.

If you do that—and you move like someone walking through a working organ rather than a ruin—you’ll find the place surprisingly accommodating: bright, legible, and stable, with its own schedules that you can learn in a day.
