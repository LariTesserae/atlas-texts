## What you’re walking into (so you pack for the right problems)

This district is not “a cave” and not “a normal facility.” It behaves like a **newly-cut, heavily-engineered underlayer**: close ceiling, dense pipework, controlled light, managed sound, constant heat load, intermittent damp, and lots of ways for an untrained visitor to get hurt without anything dramatic happening.

Your main risks, in plain terms:

- **Heat stress + dehydration** (sweat doesn’t work well here)
- **Respiratory exposure** (dust, mineral fines, insulation fibers, cleaning chemicals, bioaerosols)
- **Low light + trip/impact hazards** (catwalks, ladders, grates, jagged exposed stone)
- **Industrial hazards** (hot pipes, steam lines, rotating equipment, electrical cabinets, pressure doors)
- **Atmosphere risk pockets** (oxygen displacement, CO/CO₂, solvent fumes near sump/“SCRUB” areas)
- **Skin/foot issues** (constant warm damp → blisters, fungal infections, rashes)
- **Claustrophobia / cognitive load** (no horizons, many similar corridors, muffled audio)

The place’s “rules” are real: doors, leaks, lighting, and pathways are part of safety and resource discipline. If you violate them, you’ll stand out fast.

## Pre-trip preparation (do this before you cross)

### 1) Get your body ready for the climate
- **Heat acclimation**: for 7–14 days prior, do light exercise in warm conditions (sauna or warm room) to improve heat tolerance. You’re training your sweating/cardiovascular response.
- **Hydration habits**: practice drinking on a schedule, not “when thirsty.” In this environment thirst lags behind need.
- **Claustrophobia check**: if you’re prone, do controlled exposure (tight stairwells, industrial basements) and learn a breathing pattern you can use while moving (slow inhale, longer exhale). Panic plus ladders is how people die.

### 2) Medical and hygiene prep
- **Tetanus** up to date (cuts are likely; edges are young and sharp).
- If you have **asthma/allergies**, bring:
  - rescue inhaler
  - antihistamine you tolerate well
  - saline eye wash
- Assume **warm damp skin problems**:
  - start using foot powder/antifungal prophylactically a few days before
  - trim nails; treat any existing blisters/cuts; seal small cracks in skin

### 3) Plan your “cover” like a normal person would
This looks like a place that tolerates visitors mainly as **workers**. Arriving as a tourist is socially and operationally suspicious.

Good plausible roles:
- contractor/inspector (water discipline, leak tagging)
- instrument tech (gauges, relay panels)
- ventilation maintenance (filters, duct wraps)
- safety auditor (PPE compliance)

Have a reason to be there that explains:
- your PPE
- your note-taking
- your desire to look at signage and junction numbers

## Core packing list (what an ordinary human should bring)

### A) PPE that matches *this* district
**Head**
- Low-profile **hard hat** (catwalk edges, low ceilings, overhead drips)
- **Eye protection**: sealed goggles preferred (dust strata + chemical mist near sumps)

**Respiratory**
- Half-face respirator with:
  - **P100** particulate filters (dust/mineral fines/mold)
  - plus **OV/AG** (organic vapor/acid gas) cartridges if you might pass “SCRUB / RECLAIM” or solvent-cleaned areas  
- If you can only bring one setup: bring P100 + combo OV/AG in your kit and swap as needed.

**Hands**
- Thin **cut-resistant work gloves** (dexterity + sharp edges)
- A second pair that tolerates getting wet/dirty

**Feet**
- **Safety boots** with:
  - slip-resistant soles (wet metal + condensation)
  - puncture-resistant midsole (jagged stone, metal scraps)
- Bring **two extra sock changes** in sealed bags.

**Hearing**
- Earplugs are still useful even in the muffled soundscape—some zones will spike (fans spooling, valve releases).

### B) Environmental awareness tools (these keep you alive quietly)
- **4-gas meter** (O₂, CO, H₂S, LEL) and ideally CO₂ if available  
  Rock + dense infrastructure + sumps = occasional “bad pocket” risk. You won’t smell oxygen loss.
- **Non-contact IR thermometer** (optional, but excellent): tells you which “innocent-looking” pipe will burn you.
- **Headlamp** with multiple modes:
  - low white
  - red/amber
  - no strobe
  - a diffuser if possible  
  Bright light is socially disruptive and ruins your night vision; also it makes you look naïve in a place that *chooses* dimness.

### C) Hydration and heat management
- Water capacity for at least **2–3 liters** on your person (bladder + bottle).
- **Electrolytes** (oral rehydration salts; avoid only-sugar mixes).
- A small **cooling towel** helps, but note: evaporation is limited—use it mainly as a comfort/skin-cleaning tool, not a miracle cooler.
- **Skin barrier**: zinc oxide or anti-chafe balm (inner thighs, shoulders under straps, boot tops).

### D) Clothing: what works in “hot + intermittently damp + dirty”
- Base layer: **light synthetic or merino** that handles sweat (cotton becomes a wet abrasive rag here)
- Outer: breathable work shirt + durable pants
- Bring one **dry “clean layer”** sealed for the exit, so you don’t transport the district back out with you.

### E) Navigation and documentation (no horizons = easy to get lost)
- A small **notebook** + pencil (ink runs in humidity)
- A roll of **high-temp tape** or removable markers *only if it’s culturally acceptable*  
  Better: take photos of junction stencils and arrows instead of marking their walls.
- Offline map tool if your device works: but expect **poor radio/GPS**. Rock kills signals.

### F) Decon / aftercare kit (don’t skip)
- Antibacterial wipes + plain water wipes
- Antifungal cream/powder
- Sterile saline eye wash
- Small first-aid kit focused on:
  - cuts (sharp edges)
  - blisters
  - mild burns (hot surfaces)

## Arrival behavior: how not to get hurt or noticed

### 1) Respect “pressure logic”
Bulkheads and doors aren’t privacy; they’re **climate and pressure control**.
- Don’t prop doors.
- Don’t “just peek” into restricted corridors.
- Move through airlocks quickly and decisively; don’t linger.

### 2) Touch nothing that has a label, wheel, lever, or gauge
In a district like this, “a random valve” is never random.
- Do not spin valve wheels “to see what happens.”
- Don’t lean on pipes or use them as handrails.
- Don’t step over low pipe runs; use intended crossings.

### 3) Walk like you’re on a ship
- One hand free for balance on ladders.
- Three points of contact when climbing.
- Treat every wet-looking patch as slippery and every dry-looking patch as possibly dusty.

### 4) Light discipline
- Use low modes.
- Avoid shining lights into control panels if people are working.
- If you must do close inspection: shield your lamp with your hand.

### 5) Don’t stir the “dust strata”
You described particulate layers that behave almost like curtains. Whether it’s ordinary airflow stratification or something stranger, treat it as:
- a respiratory irritant
- a visibility hazard (sudden glare)
- a contamination carrier  
Move smoothly; don’t run; don’t fan things with your hands.

## Zone-by-zone cautions (based on the signage you’ll see)

### **INTAKE**
- Higher chance of pressure differentials, colder surfaces (more condensation), and unexpected drafts.
- Watch for slick grates and sudden temperature changes (fogging goggles).

### **POWER**
- Electrical cabinets, transformer hum, hot metal, restricted access.
- Keep metal jewelry off (rings, necklaces).
- Don’t take photos if it’s security-sensitive.

### **SCRUB**
- Air treatment and chemical processes: acids/alkalis/solvents are plausible.
- Use the respirator with appropriate cartridges.
- Avoid sumps and any “clean smell” that’s too clean (often means chemical).

### **BLEED**
- Pressure release zones: valves can vent hot air/steam with little warning.
- Don’t stand in front of flanges, vents, or “open-ended” pipes.
- Eye protection is non-negotiable here.

### **RECLAIM**
- Water discipline area: leaks are tracked; people will notice your footsteps.
- Expect wet floors, biofilms, mineral crust edges (sharp).
- Around reservoirs: treat the pale crust like broken ceramic—**it will cut you**.

## Heat + damp management while inside (staying functional)

### Hydration rule of thumb
- Drink small amounts often: **200–300 ml every 20–30 minutes** in sustained heat.
- Add electrolytes if you’re sweating continuously or your clothes remain damp.
- If you stop sweating, get dizzy, or become clumsy: that’s not “getting used to it.” That’s a problem.

### Clothing discipline
- Change socks proactively, not when your feet hurt.
- If you feel a hotspot/blister forming, stop and address it immediately—walking one more kilometer can turn it into a wound that won’t heal well in this environment.

### Breathing discipline
- If the air feels “thick,” that’s your cue to:
  - check your gas meter
  - mask up
  - slow down  
Your lungs are not the instrument you use to test industrial atmospheres.

## Emergency planning (assume you’ll need it once)

### Know your exits in the first 10 minutes
Because there are no horizons, you can get disoriented fast. On arrival:
- Identify the nearest **bulkhead**, **ladder to upper catwalk**, and **main corridor width** path.
- Note the stencil numbers at your entry junction and one junction away.

### If something alarms (sirens, lights, pressure events)
- Stop moving for 3 seconds and listen. In muffled environments, panic-motion is worse than delay.
- Follow **established flow** (people who belong there will move with purpose).
- Do not run onto catwalks unless directed—crowding and slips happen there.

### If your gas meter alarms
- Mask up immediately.
- Move to a **ventilated main corridor** (often closer to INTAKE/vent trunks).
- Go *up* if possible (many heavy gases settle), but don’t assume—trust the meter and posted protocols.

### If you’re alone
You shouldn’t be, but if you are:
- stay on the widest corridor
- do not enter narrow service gaps between pipe bundles
- do not climb ladders you can’t safely descend while tired and sweaty

## Exit strategy (what you do when you leave matters)

This district will cling to you: dust, salts, microbial films, chemical residues.
- Wipe down boots and gloves before crossing back into any “clean” world.
- Bag contaminated clothing.
- Shower as soon as possible; treat feet proactively (dry thoroughly, powder, inspect cuts).
- If you develop persistent cough, eye irritation, or skin rash: treat it as exposure, not “a cold.”
