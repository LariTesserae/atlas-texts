## What you’re walking into (so you pack the right problems)
This isn’t “a wet plain.” It’s a planet-scale **operating surface**: warm, saturated air; constant condensation; shallow moving water; grates/voids; self-repairing materials; and an ecology that functions like an extension of maintenance. The stability is the hazard: conditions don’t drift to warn you—if something is dangerous, it will stay dangerous at the same intensity forever.

Key implications for an ordinary human:
- **High humidity + constant wet surfaces** → hypothermia is unlikely, but **skin maceration, trench foot, fungal rashes, and slips** are constant risks.
- **Industrial aerosols + biofilms + fine grit** → likely **respiratory irritants**, contact dermatitis, and contamination risk.
- **Hidden infrastructure (grates, expansion joints, access mounds, ducts)** → fall-through and entrapment hazards.
- **Dense, quiet fauna** → you can be surrounded without realizing it; many organisms will treat you as terrain, waste, or a heat source.
- **Monotony + repeating rhythms** → navigation errors and “looping” are common; psychologically it can feel coerced and claustrophobic even in open space.
- **Possible supervisory system** (implicit in scheduling, self-repair, cleanliness) → assume you are being *sensed*, even if you never see cameras.

## Baseline rules that keep you alive here
1. **Never assume a flat surface is load-bearing.** Treat plates as strong and grates as traps until tested.
2. **Avoid becoming a heat exchanger.** Don’t lie on the ground; don’t lean on warm walls for long; don’t camp near vents.
3. **Don’t interrupt “lanes.”** Fauna flows like scheduled traffic. Standing in a path invites being climbed over, sampled, parasitized, or simply knocked down.
4. **No bare-skin contact with films or water.** This world’s “food web” is chemistry and microbes. Your skin is an unapproved substrate.
5. **Do not leave organics behind.** Hair, food crumbs, blood, paper—anything can become feedstock or signal “leak/contamination.”
6. **If something arrives after you stop moving, move.** In this ecosystem, stillness reads like “material for processing.”

## Health prep (before you jump)
### Medical
- **Skin protection plan:** barrier cream (zinc oxide or silicone-based), antifungal powder, hydrocolloid blister dressings, spare socks/liners.
- **Respiratory plan:** if you have asthma/allergies, bring rescue meds; humidity + aerosols can trigger bronchospasm even without obvious “smoke.”
- **Vaccines won’t map well**, but you want broad coverage: tetanus, hepatitis A/B, and a current flu/COVID baseline (not because they exist there—because you don’t want *your* world’s infections to complicate an injury).
- **Quarantine mindset:** plan to treat this as a biohazard exposure on return.

### Fitness / tolerance
- Practice walking long distances on **slick, uneven surfaces** with a loaded pack.
- If you get motion sickness, train for **low-frequency vibration** exposure (vehicles/ships). The constant sub-audible hum can cause nausea and fatigue.

## Gear: what matters most in a warm, wet, made world

### Clothing (assume constant damp)
- **Base layer:** synthetic or merino that stays functional wet.
- **Mid layer:** thin insulation (it’s warm, but evaporative cooling and constant wet contact can chill you when you stop moving).
- **Shell:** *breathable waterproof* is less useful than you think (humidity is near-saturation). Prioritize **mechanical protection** and fast-drying.
- **Gloves:** nitrile inner + abrasion-resistant outer. You’ll touch railings/catwalks; assume they’re chemically and biologically active.
- **Eye protection:** sealed or semi-sealed glasses. Aerosolized oils + fine grit + mist is a miserable combo.

### Foot system (your single most important category)
- **Primary boots:** waterproof, chemical-resistant, aggressive siped soles (boat-deck style grip helps on wet plates).
- **Over-boot/wader option:** knee-high if you expect canal edges or frequent puddles.
- **Sock system:** liner + main sock; change often; carry a way to dry (body heat + mesh drying on pack).
- **Foot hygiene kit:** antifungal, alcohol wipes, blister care. Treat hot spots immediately—this place punishes neglect.

### Breathing protection
- **Half-face respirator** with combination cartridges (P100/organic vapor/acid gas) is ideal.
- Minimum: well-fitting N95 + eye protection, but expect irritation from hydrocarbons and unknown volatiles.

### Navigation (monotony is a real threat)
- **Inertial navigation** (IMU-based) or a dead-reckoning app/device that doesn’t rely on GPS.
- **Physical markers:** high-visibility tape/flags *that you retrieve*. Better: **UV-reactive chalk** or removable magnetic markers for metal surfaces.
- **Compass** may be unreliable if there’s pervasive metal and active infrastructure.
- **“Return line” discipline:** always maintain a known route back (or staged caches/beacons).

### Shelter (you need separation from the floor)
- **Raised sleep system:** hammock with stand, or ultralight cot. Do not sleep directly on plates.
- **Groundsheet is not enough**—water films and crawling fauna will defeat it.
- **Bug netting** with a fine mesh (for tiny crawling colonies) plus a sealable bivy.
- **Earplugs + vibration-damping pad** (not for loudness—just to get real rest).

### Tools and safety
- **Trekking pole / probe staff:** test grates, seams, and slickness; check canal edges.
- **Traction aids:** microspikes don’t help on smooth wet metal; bring **sticky sole patches** or deck traction tape you can apply to your own gear.
- **Headlamp + area light:** the light is diffuse; shadows under catwalks can hide openings and fauna.
- **Thermal scanner** (small IR): helps spot warm vents, active ducts, and “feeding walls” that attract large fauna.
- **Compact first aid + irrigation syringe:** cuts will happen; you’ll want to flush grit out immediately.
- **Dry bags inside pack:** assume everything external will be damp within hours.

### Food and water
- **Do not drink local water untreated.** It’s warm, gritty, chemically “busy,” and biologically optimized for *their* system.
- Bring your own water or a **two-stage approach**:
  1) prefilter for grit (cloth/ceramic prefilter)  
  2) activated carbon + robust purifier (UV alone may fail in turbid tea-colored water)  
- **Calorie-dense food** in sealed packaging. Avoid crumbs. Eat over a catch cloth, pack all residues.

## How to move through the landscape without becoming “a problem”
### Read the flow like traffic engineering
- Watch a canal edge or catwalk for 10–15 minutes before crossing. You’re looking for:
  - **Periodic sheets/migrations** (the “same geometry” events)
  - **No-go seams** where things avoid (often chemical leaks, micro-currents, or sensor zones)
  - **Maintenance-creature corridors** (steady, quiet streams)

### Step choices
- Prefer **catwalks and lattices** over plates when available: they’re designed for loads and reduce random fauna contact.
- Avoid standing water that has a **rainbow sheen** or persistent foam: likely oils/surfactants.
- Don’t sit on rails or ledges—those are condensation collection points and often “licked” by film-feeders.

### Interaction with fauna (best practice)
- Assume most organisms are:
  - indifferent unless you block them,
  - curious about heat/salts,
  - tasked (ecologically) with cleanup and regulation.
- **No sudden stomping, no chasing, no feeding.**
- If small colony-beads flow around your boots: stop moving, let them pass, then relocate off their route.
- If something attaches: don’t rip. **Flood with clean water**, peel slowly with a tool, disinfect, and cover. Tearing can leave hooks/parts behind.

## What not to do (these trigger attention or injury)
- **Don’t open hatches.** They “sweat” minerals for a reason; opening them may be read as a fault condition.
- **Don’t throw anything into slots/grates.** You may be feeding the system, signaling, or contaminating a regulated layer.
- **Don’t camp near “quiet warmth.”** The most comfortable spots are often biologically active feeding zones.
- **Don’t follow the herd.** Their schedule is not yours; you’ll eventually be in the wrong place when a procedural predator or maintenance wave passes through.

## Contamination control (both directions)
### Before entry
- Arrive **clean and sealed**:
  - freshly laundered clothing
  - minimal loose fibers
  - gear wiped down
- If you can, use **non-shedding fabrics** and cover hair.

### During
- Treat your body like a sterile instrument:
  - gloves for any contact
  - no kneeling on surfaces
  - manage sweat (salt is attractive; you don’t want to become a mineral lick)

### Exit / return
- Plan a **full decon**:
  - outer gear wash
  - clothing sealed and laundered hot
  - shower with attention to feet, nails, and hair
  - 48–72 hour symptom watch (skin lesions, cough, unusual fatigue)
- Quarantine any samples. Better: **don’t take samples** unless you have proper containment.

## A practical “first day” plan (recommended)
1. **Establish a raised safe point** (cot/hammock stand) away from vents, away from heavy fauna routes, near an obvious landmark structure.
2. Spend **one full cycle** observing: when mist peaks, when migrations pass, when flow rates shift.
3. Make a **short out-and-back** route (30–60 minutes) with removable markers and tested footing.
4. Only then extend range; maintain a hard turnaround time so you never navigate in fatigue.

## Emergency cues and responses
### You feel the ground “change”
- Clicking engagement beneath you, subtle plate movement, a new vibration mode: **back off**. You may be on an access plate entering a cycle.

### The fauna gets out of your way
- If dense life suddenly parts or goes still, assume **a purge, vent, or predator/procedure** is imminent. Get to a catwalk or higher structure.

### You notice your gear getting “filmed”
- If clear/pearly varnish accumulates on boots or tools, treat it as reactive coating or biofilm:
  - wipe with disposable cloth
  - avoid solvents unless necessary (unknown reactions)
  - don’t let it build up around buckles/seams (it can lock gear shut)

### Injury management priorities
- Flush cuts immediately (grit + wet surfaces = infection and embedded particles).
- Cover wounds completely. This world has dedicated recyclers; blood is an attractant and a signal.

## Mindset: how to stay functional in enforced equilibrium
- Expect a constant feeling of being *in the way*. Counter it by acting like a respectful temporary worker:
  - predictable movements
  - minimal disruption
  - careful waste handling
- Use **time anchors** (scheduled meals, logs, route rules). The repeating environment can dissolve your sense of progress.
- Don’t trust “no change” as safety. The system can remain stable while still being hostile to a foreign organism.
