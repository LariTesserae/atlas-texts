## What you’re going to (in practical terms)
This is a high-altitude, newly-fractured alpine cleft containing an anomalous “pocket” of violently circulating water that **adheres to specific rock** and **does not behave as a normal lake**. The environment is:

- **Cold + wet + thin air** (hypothermia and exhaustion come early)
- **Extremely loud** (hearing damage, communication failure, disorientation)
- **Geologically young/unstable** (rockfall, sharp edges, brittle mineral crusts)
- **Hydrodynamically hostile** (turbulence that can grab, spin, and hold you)
- **Reactive to rhythm** (certain disturbances can amplify turbulence)

Preparation is mostly about: *staying attached to the mountain, staying warm, protecting hearing/eyes, minimizing “interesting” disturbances, and planning for a fast retreat.*

---

## 1) Before you go: physical and logistical preparation

### Acclimatization and fitness
- Plan **24–72 hours** of altitude acclimatization if you’re not already adapted. The approach sounds above tree line; assume exertion costs more than you expect.
- Train for **slippery scrambling while wearing a pack**. Wet stone + thin air is where people make “small” mistakes that become falls.

### Team size and roles
Go with **2–3 people**, not solo.
- One person is always the **primary tether/anchor handler**.
- One person is the **observer/recorder** (notes, sketches, time stamps).
- If three: the third is **safety/medical** and watches for hypothermia, panic breathing, and bad footing.

### Timing and weather window
- Choose a window with **stable wind**. Wind isn’t just comfort here; it can change resonance and spray reach.
- Plan a **hard turnaround time** (example: “If core temp drops / gloves soak through / we lose comms, we leave immediately.”)

## 2) Core hazards and how to blunt them

### A) Noise (hearing damage + cognitive overload)
The roar isn’t just unpleasant—it will **fatigue you, shorten attention span, and wreck coordination**.
- Bring **proper hearing protection**:
  - Foam earplugs **plus** over-ear defenders (double protection).
  - If you must talk: **bone-conduction comms** or simple **hand signals**; assume voices won’t work.
- Establish pre-agreed signals: *stop, retreat, slack, tension, emergency, OK, anchor set*.

### B) Cold wet exposure (hypothermia + numb hands)
- Wear layers that still work when wet:
  - **Synthetic or wool base**, insulating mid-layer, **hard shell**.
  - **Waterproof gloves** plus a spare dry pair in a sealed bag.
  - **Goggles** (spray + wind + salt/mineral grit = eye injury).
- Pack a **small thermos** of hot drink. It’s not luxury; it’s decision-making fuel.

### C) Falls and rock instability
“Young rock” means sharp, untrustworthy edges and surprise fractures.
- Helmet is non-negotiable.
- Move as if every ledge could shear: **three points of contact**, test holds, avoid glassy-looking surfaces.
- Treat pale granular salt/mineral dust like ball bearings.

### D) Water hazard (drowning/entrapment)
This pocket isn’t “swimmable water.” It’s closer to a **contained rapid** with weird adhesion rules.
- Do not enter the water unless you have:
  - A **tethered harness system**, a competent belayer, and a pre-rigged extraction plan.
  - Thermal protection that still functions when fully soaked (drysuit if you’re doing anything close).
- Assume that if you lose footing within spray reach, the next problem is being **pushed, spun, and unable to stand**.

### E) “Water remembers motion” (turbulence amplification)
Treat the cleft like an acoustic instrument and the water like a feedback loop.
- Avoid:
  - **Rhythmic banging**, repeated tapping, metal-on-stone clinks
  - Chanting/counting out loud near the mouth of the cleft
  - Repetitive movements at a steady tempo close to the water
- Prefer:
  - Slow, irregular movement
  - Soft contact points (rubber, webbing, cloth)
  - Quiet anchors (no ringing pitons if you can avoid it)

## 3) Gear: what an ordinary traveler should pack

### Clothing & personal protection
- Mountaineering layers (base/mid/shell), warm hat/buff
- **Goggles** + clear backup lenses
- **Helmet**
- **Double hearing protection**
- 2× gloves (one kept dry), warm socks, spare dry layer in dry bag

### Footing and attachment
- Sticky-sole approach shoes or light boots with good edging
- **Climbing harness**
- 1–2 dynamic ropes (or a single rope + tagline depending on approach)
- **Static line** for fixed safety near the platform (static is easier for tether work)
- Slings, locking carabiners, cordelette
- Removable protection if cracks allow (nuts/cams). Avoid anything that “sings” when struck.
- **Soft anchor material** (webbing) to reduce ringing/vibration.

### Survival/medical
- Hypothermia-focused kit: heat packs, emergency bivy, dry spare base layer
- Standard first aid plus:
  - blister care (wet feet),
  - eye rinse (mineral spray),
  - pain relief (cold-induced headaches are common at altitude)
- High-calorie food that can be eaten with gloves (bars/chews)

### Tools and recording (low-tech bias)
Electronics may fail from wetness; also, you don’t want to be troubleshooting a device in a deafening spray zone.
- **Waterproof notebook + pencil** (not pen)
- Chalk-free marking tape (minimal use; remove it)
- Camera in a waterproof housing if you must, but expect spray to defeat lenses
- A simple **analog compass** and a physical map of your exit route

### “Don’t excite the pocket” loadout choices
- Wrap metal gear so it can’t clatter.
- Tape down loose buckles.
- Choose **rubberized** or fabric contact points wherever possible.

## 4) Approach protocol (how to arrive without getting hurt)

### Step 1: Establish a staging zone
Stop **well outside** the spray reach and noise peak.
- Eat, drink, layer up, insert earplugs here—**not** at the edge.
- Do a buddy check: harness doubled back, gloves on, goggles accessible, comms plan reviewed.

### Step 2: Rig a retreat line before you “need” it
- Fix a line from a solid anchor back to your staging zone.
- Keep the rope path simple—no places where it can saw across sharp young stone.

### Step 3: Move one at a time in the final approach
- One person advances while others keep tension management clean.
- Maintain irregular pacing; avoid any repeated “step—tap—step—tap” pattern right at the cleft.

### Step 4: Treat the existing ropes/platform as *evidence*, not assurance
Those anchors show prior knowledge, but you don’t know their age or integrity.
- Inspect any existing rope: if it’s weathered or stiff or frayed, **do not weight it**.
- If you use the slate platform, assume it can shift—stay tethered.

## 5) On-site behavior (how to “bargain with the edges”)

### Communication
- Expect **no voice comms** near the pocket.
- Use hand signals close-in; use prewritten cue cards if needed (laminated).

### Movement discipline
- Keep your center of gravity low; avoid standing tall near gust funnels.
- Don’t linger in the heaviest spray: it soaks gloves, numbs fingers, and increases fall risk fast.

### Observation without escalation
If the place “catches” rhythms:
- Avoid tossing stones, repeated knocks, or any test that creates a clean, repeatable pulse.
- Prefer passive observation:
  - timed notes of surge intervals,
  - sketches of flow directions,
  - single, non-repeated disturbances (if you must) with long pauses between.

### Respect the marks and shelter
- Do not deepen scratches or add symbols. If those marks are a safety record, altering them is actively harmful.
- If you use the lean-to: don’t light a fire (it likely fails, and smoke/soot adds residue). Use it only as a wind break.

## 6) If you intend to touch the water (minimum safe method)
If your mission requires contact, do it like a hazardous confined-space procedure:

1. **Full tether**: harness + belay + redundant attachment (two points).
2. **Hard limit line**: a knot/stopper so you physically cannot be pulled past a set point.
3. **Thermal plan**: time limit measured in minutes; immediate dry-change ready.
4. **No breath-holding bravado**: cold shock + noise + turbulence = panic spiral.
5. **One-touch rule**: one controlled contact, then back out. Do not repeat rhythmically.

Do **not** free-swim. Do **not** enter alone. Do **not** enter to “see what it feels like.”

---

## 7) Emergency triggers (leave immediately if…)
- Anyone’s hands stop working well enough to clip gear (numbness)
- Shivering becomes violent or stops suddenly (both bad signs)
- You drop an essential item into the pocket (don’t attempt a “quick” retrieval)
- Rock begins popping or shedding near your anchor stance
- The pocket surges outward enough to change the spray boundary you thought you had
- Anyone feels vertigo, dissociation, or “tunnel attention” from the roar

Your exit plan should be **simpler than your entry**: follow the fixed line back, regroup at staging, then descend.

---

## 8) Multiverse-traveler specific cautions
If your travel method involves a portal/gate/anchor point:
- Place it **well away** from the cleft mouth and spray zone. Constant aerosolized mineral water will foul mechanisms and optics.
- Avoid anchoring your return point to the “special” rock that binds the water. If that stone participates in the anomaly, you don’t want your exit hardware inheriting its rules.
- Bring an **analog fallback** for orientation and timing (watch, compass, written coordinates). Assume electronics may die wet.
