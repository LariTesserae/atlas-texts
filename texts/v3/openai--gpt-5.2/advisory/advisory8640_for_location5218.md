## What you’re walking into (so you pack the right mindset)
This is not “high altitude wilderness.” It’s a warm, humid *operating machine* that happens to be walkable and overgrown. Most hazards come from:

- **Wet metal + engineered slopes** (slip and fall are the default failure mode).
- **Fog-as-fluid dynamics** (visibility, condensation, and sudden “breath” from vents).
- **Hidden voids** (grates, access gaps, understructure drops).
- **Quiet automation** (panels tilt, louvers open, screens shake water off—without warning).
- **Bioindustrial ecology** (plants are infrastructure; infrastructure is alive enough to bite back via irritants, spores, and biofilms).

Plan like you’re visiting a greenhouse built inside a ship’s superstructure, above the clouds.

## Clothing & personal protection (optimize for wet warmth, not cold)
**Base layer**
- Lightweight, sweat-wicking synthetics or merino. Humidity will saturate cotton and keep you clammy.
- Bring **one dry spare base layer in a sealed bag**. You’ll want it.

**Outer layer**
- **Breathable waterproof shell** (real breathability matters here; you’ll sweat under a non-breathable rain jacket).
- Hood with a brim. The main “rain” is drip and dump: droplets from leaves, screens, and rails.

**Hands**
- Thin **grippy gloves** (nitrile-dipped work gloves or mechanic gloves). You’ll grab wet rails and need friction.
- Avoid bare-hand contact with biofilm-coated metal (slippery) and unknown plant secretions (irritants).

**Feet (most important)**
- **Non-marking, oil/wet-rated slip-resistant soles** (deck-shoe grip, industrial anti-slip, or approach shoes with sticky rubber).
- Prefer closed-toe, puncture-resistant. This place has new edges, seams, fasteners, and sharp corners.
- Optional: **lightweight over-boot traction straps** if you’re not confident in your sole compound.

**Eyes / breathing**
- Clear anti-fog glasses or goggles if you expect to be in dense fog under active vents.
- A **compact respirator (P100 or equivalent)** is wise if you’re sensitive to spores, aerosolized nutrient mist, or ozone/cleaning volatiles. You may not need it constantly; you’ll be glad to have it when you do.

**Skin / hygiene**
- Humidity + constant wet contact = rash/fungal risk. Pack:
  - Travel antifungal powder/cream
  - Barrier cream for hands
  - Small towel + alcohol wipes

## Gear you’ll actually use (keep it simple, water-safe)
**Carry system**
- Waterproof daypack or pack cover + internal dry bags. Assume everything will get wet eventually.

**Lighting**
- Headlamp with a wide flood mode. Fog and under-ribs eat contrast.
- Spare battery sealed in a dry pouch.

**Navigation (don’t trust compasses)**
- Magnetic compasses can be unreliable around steel and running machinery.
- Use:
  - **Inertial/visual nav** (phone + offline maps, but protect it)
  - **Physical breadcrumbing**: small **magnetic markers** or bright zip ties (placed sparingly, retrieved on exit)

**Electronics protection**
- IP67-rated phone case, or at least a sealed pouch.
- Silica gel packets in your electronics bag.
- Avoid exposing charging ports; condensation will creep in.

**Stability / fall prevention**
- A **short walking pole** with a rubber tip helps test slick panels and probe fog-hidden edges.
- If you’re going near grates, catwalks, or rib-walls with drop exposure: **light harness + 5–10 m tether** (even a simple lanyard to a solid rail can save you).

**Field tools**
- Non-marring multitool (plastic pry tools are better than metal prying here; you don’t want to gouge seals).
- A small roll of high-visibility tape (use only for emergencies; adhesives fail when constantly wet).

**Water & food**
- You *can* collect condensed water, but treat it as **chemically blank + easily contaminated**:
  - Carry your own drinking water.
  - If you must drink local: filter + activated carbon (it’ll pick up metal tangs and organics).
- High-calorie snacks you can eat one-handed.

**Medical**
- Basic kit: blister care (you will slip-microstep), antiseptic, bandage wraps, antihistamine, electrolyte packets.

## Approach protocol: how to move without getting hurt
**1) Walk like you’re on a ship deck**
- Keep one hand available for rails.
- Short steps. Weight centered. No rushing.
- Expect *surprise slickness*: biofilm looks like a faint sheen, not slime.

**2) Treat seams as both clues and traps**
- Seams indicate plate boundaries, gaskets, tilt-panels, service hatches.
- Don’t step on **swollen dark gasket lines** if you can avoid it—they can be soft, springy, and slick.
- Use seams to read drainage direction; they often “tell you” where the system wants you to walk.

**3) Don’t stand under “self-shaking” mesh or canopy edges**
- A screen that sheds mist can dump a sheet of water without warning.
- Listen for subtle mechanical pre-sounds: a relay click, a change in duct whisper, a rising hum.

**4) Fog is traffic**
- Fog thickening/streaming in a direction often marks:
  - vent outflow
  - pressure changes
  - door cycling
- If fog suddenly “pulls” past you, brace—airflow shifts can nudge you on a slick surface.

**5) Avoid the edges until you’ve learned the grammar**
- Grates and “cliff-face doors” can look decorative. Assume every architectural flourish is functional and may move.

## Social/automation etiquette (so the system doesn’t treat you as debris)
You described maintenance drones and a place that’s “polite” about boundaries. That usually means sensors and rules.

- **Look non-threatening to automation**:
  - No flailing lights directly at sensors.
  - Don’t cover cameras/markers you find.
  - Move predictably; pause if a drone approaches and let it pass.
- **Do not interfere with plants that look “trained”**:
  - Vines paired with filaments are not decorative—cutting or even pulling can trigger alarms or destabilize a subsystem.
- If you need to signal peaceful intent: open hands, step aside, keep your light angled down, and don’t chase drones.

If there are posted icons, color codes, or repeated panel markings, photograph them early—they’ll matter later.

## What not to touch (high-probability regret list)
- **Moist cable runs and junction boxes** (condensation + power is a bad mix; even “safe” systems can bite).
- **Warm vents** (they’re tempting to warm hands on, but they can cycle hotter or blast humid air).
- **Microporous “sweating” sleeves** on pipes (clogging them changes humidity/temperature control).
- **Biofilm-green corners** (slippery + microbial load + chemical residues).
- **Any door disguised as strata** unless you’re prepared for pressure differentials and moving hardware.

## Health risks specific to this pocket
- **Skin maceration** from constant damp contact → blisters, cracks, infections.
- **Respiratory irritation** from spores/aerosols and faint ozone.
- **Dehydration** despite humidity (you sweat continuously and don’t notice).
- **Minor metal exposure** (zinc/other tangs): wash hands before eating, don’t lick condensation off rails.

## Practical staging plan (before you step through)
**24 hours before**
- Waterproof your kit (dry bags).
- Pre-load offline notes/maps; assume poor signal.
- Pack spare socks and base layer sealed.

**At entry**
- Spend 5 minutes stationary: listen to the system cycle.
- Note: closest stable rail line, nearest “door-cliff,” and any repeating safe-walk texture (grit strips, ribbed plates).

**First 15 minutes**
- Slow loop close to your entry point.
- Test surfaces: stamped dimples, gaskets, moss mats (some are surprisingly slick).
- Identify one “high ground” (least wet) where you could sit and sort gear without dripping onto sensitive equipment.

**Exit strategy**
- Keep a hard turnaround time. Fog and monotony can make distance feel shorter than it is.
- Retrieve any markers you placed.
