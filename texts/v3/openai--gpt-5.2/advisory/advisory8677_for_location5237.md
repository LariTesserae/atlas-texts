## What you’re walking into (mental model)

Treat this shelf as a **live calibration platform**: a young landscape plus an embedded network (the hum) that is actively negotiating local constants—gravity vectors, timing, adhesion, maybe even the way “distance” works. It is **not a wilderness with hazards you can learn once**; it’s a testbed where the rules drift in pulses.

Two implications to hold onto:

1. **Edges are real.** “Step out of the experiment” may mean stepping into unrendered space, a null volume, or a region where your body’s assumptions (pressure, causality, inertia) stop matching the environment.
2. **Built objects are not ruins.** The pylons, filaments, and the doorframe read like *active instrumentation*—they may be sensing you, shaping the shelf, or providing gateways into slightly dephased versions of the same place.

Your goal as an ordinary human traveler is to **stay reference-locked**, **stay tethered**, **minimize interaction**, and **leave with your baseline physiology intact**.

## Primary risks (ranked)

### 1) Boundary failure / “edge step”
- You keep walking and suddenly the shelf isn’t there in the way you expect.
- Air and pressure change at the boundary; sound changes; ears pop.
- Visual “blankness” beyond ledges may be literal non-geometry.

**Mitigation:** strict perimeter protocol, tethering, and a hard rule: *never move toward an edge during or immediately after a pulse event*.

### 2) Metric drift (space has grain)
- Distances stretch in one direction.
- Your own actions feel delayed (“the splash takes longer to exist”).
- A short hop becomes a long fall, or a safe step becomes a slide.

**Mitigation:** move slower than feels necessary; three-point contact on wet rock; no jumping; keep a constant physical reference line (tether or tape) when doing any traversal.

### 3) Pulse events (rule changes)
- Puddles tremble; pylons “sing”; fauna freezes then resumes in a new pattern.
- Local gravity/down-direction briefly tilts; water shifts sideways; surface tension changes.

**Mitigation:** learn pre-signals; adopt a “freeze posture” protocol; keep your center of mass low; never stand in ankle-deep water during high-activity periods if you can avoid it.

### 4) Water that behaves like skin
- Pools are shallow but “feel deep.”
- Surface tension may support or trap; dimpling upward implies pressure from below.
- Predators strike when the surface “forgets” cohesion.

**Mitigation:** assume every pool edge is active habitat; do not kneel at pool margins; do not wade unless you must; do not drink or splash.

### 5) Aerosols, minerals, and warm wet air
- Metallic taste; constant condensate; mineral tang.
- Likely fine particulates, dissolved metals, unusual salts, possibly bioactive spores.

**Mitigation:** respiratory protection, eye protection, skin barrier, rigorous decon.

### 6) Built-field interactions (pylons / filaments / doorframe)
- Filaments can startle fauna; likely field lines (electrostatic, inertial, or temporal).
- Pylons distort air and pause droplets; could create shear zones or gravity wells.
- Doorframe shows a phase-shifted version of the shelf; stepping through is an uncontrolled variable.

**Mitigation:** maintain standoff distances; never cross filaments; never pass between pylons; do not step through the doorframe unless your entire mission is “controlled portal traversal” with an anchored return plan.

## Pre-departure preparation (what to do before you go)

### A) Physical conditioning (realistic)
You need:
- **Ankles and knees** ready for uneven, slick rock and sudden micro-tilts.
- **Balance under load** (pack + tether).
- **Heat tolerance** (warm, wet air) and **constant damp** exposure.

If you can do only one thing: practice moving slowly over wet rock with a pack, focusing on *never committing weight until the foot is proven*.

### B) Medical & personal baseline
Because the place messes with timing and pressure, establish your “normal”:
- Resting HR, BP, SpO₂, temperature.
- Ear health (equalization ability).
- Any vestibular issues (vertigo will be amplified).

Bring:
- Antinausea meds (metric drift can feel like motion sickness).
- Sterile saline eye wash.
- Skin barrier ointment (petrolatum or similar) to reduce mineral irritation.
- A short course of broad-spectrum topical antiseptic (constant wet = maceration risk).

### C) Instruments that are robust to weirdness
Assume GPS/compass may lie. Bring tools that anchor to **physical reality**:
- **50–100 m nonconductive tether line** (Dyneema is strong but can be slippery; consider a sheath for grip).
- **Climbing harness + two lanyards** (so you’re never unclipped).
- **Mechanical measuring tape** and **chalk/paint marker** (physical marks on rock).
- **Simple analog watch** + a second time source (even a cheap quartz) to detect drift; don’t rely only on phones.
- **Thermometer + barometer** (pressure changes at edges matter).
- **pH strips + conductivity meter** for water/condensate (fast hazard read).
- **Audio recorder** (the hum’s harmonics may correlate with pulses; also useful as an early warning).

## Packing list (optimized for this shelf)

### Clothing (assume perpetual wet + warm + abrasive mineral film)
- Waterproof-breathable shell (hooded), but prioritize **venting**; you’ll sweat.
- Base layers that still insulate when wet (synthetic or wool).
- **Waterproof gloves** with tactile grip (you’ll be touching rock, not just air).
- **Gaiters** (wet grit will chew your ankles).
- Spare socks x2–3 (maceration is a real injury here).

### Footwear (non-negotiable)
- Approach shoes or boots with:
  - Sticky rubber (wet rock traction),
  - A stiff-ish sole (sharp facets),
  - Excellent drainage (water is everywhere).
- Add **microspikes only if** the rock has algae-like slickness; otherwise they may reduce stability on bare facets.

### Breathing & eye protection
- P100 or equivalent respirator (high humidity makes filters harder to breathe through; bring spares).
- Sealed goggles (mist + mineral aerosols + sudden steam vents).

### Safety & navigation
- Harness + helmet (edges + gullies + sudden tilt).
- Two locking carabiners per lanyard; extra cordage.
- 3–6 rock anchors or removable protection if you know how to place it; otherwise bring **portable deadman anchors** for slick surfaces.
- High-visibility ribbon/flagging tape (to mark “safe corridors” without relying on distance intuition).
- Headlamp (light collapses fast; fog acts like a dimmer switch).

### Environmental sampling / contamination control
- Nitrile gloves (under work gloves when handling filaments or built objects *if you must*).
- Sealable sample bags (for rocks/lichen only if your mission requires; otherwise don’t collect).
- Decon kit: fresh water (from home), mild detergent, wipes, trash bags, and a way to isolate wet gear.

### Emergency
- Thermal blanket (warm wind is not a guarantee; wet + wind can chill you fast).
- Tourniquet + basic trauma kit.
- Whistle (sound layers are loud; your voice may not carry reliably).
- A **hard rule card** (laminated): “No doorframe, no pylons, no edge during pulses.”

## Arrival protocol (first 15 minutes on the shelf)

### 1) Stop and listen before you move
You’re looking for:
- The baseline hum (single tone or braided tones).
- Wind intensity and direction.
- Drip cadence (steady vs “hesitating” droplets).
- Any pattern of fog “venting” (clean lift/drop suggests scheduled cycles).

Record 2 minutes of audio. This becomes your “calm baseline.”

### 2) Establish a home point
Pick a spot:
- Away from pools and away from filament arcs.
- Not in line with the pylon half-circle.
- Not near any edge.

Mark it physically (paint/chalk + a small cairn if permitted). This is your anchor for orientation when distance starts lying.

### 3) Tether rules begin immediately
- One person tethered at all times if solo; if two people, at least one line between you.
- No one approaches a gully lip or an edge without a secured point.

## Movement protocol (how to walk where the ground “decides”)

### Foot placement
- Test with toe first, then commit weight.
- Avoid stepping on the thinnest water films on smooth stone—they’re the most likely to become frictionless during a pulse.
- Use the rock’s **facets and seams** like stair edges; avoid the glossy planes.

### Speed
Move at “survey pace,” not hiking pace. This isn’t about fitness; it’s about giving your brain time to detect metric weirdness.

### Route selection
Prefer:
- Slightly rougher rock textures (better traction),
- Areas with sparse vegetation (plants may indicate micro-stability),
- Avoid hex-grid lichen patches if they look too “designed”—they might map to the underlying field.

Avoid:
- Pool margins,
- Overhang drip lines (constant splash + slick),
- Any place where sediment collects “wrong” (often a sign the down-direction recently changed).

## Reading pulse events (and what to do)

### Common pre-signals
- The hum resolves into clearer multiple tones.
- Mist droplets hesitate or bead in strange arcs.
- Fauna “stutters” (freeze/continue).
- Puddles show tight ripples without wind.

### Your response: the Freeze Posture
When a pulse starts:
1. Stop walking.
2. Lower your center of gravity (half crouch).
3. Put one hand on stable rock (gloved), one hand on tether.
4. Turn your face away from steam vents if nearby.
5. Wait 10–30 seconds *after* the environment seems normal before moving again.

Do not try to “beat” a pulse by hurrying across something. That’s how you end up stepping during a gravity-tilt or adhesion-change.

## Fauna etiquette (staying alive without starting a local incident)

### Assume the ecosystem uses the pulses
Many animals here are timing-adapted. If you move erratically or interfere with filaments, you may:
- Trigger swarms (scavengers following lines),
- Disrupt predators at pools (they may redirect),
- Become the tallest warm object during a pulse (insects/birds may investigate).

Rules:
- Don’t chase or “shoo” gliders—if they circle an invisible updraft column, that column may form during pulses.
- Give pool edges a wide berth (at least 2–3 meters).
- If thin-legged hoof/pad creatures cross your path, stop and let them pass; they’re better at reading traction than you are.

If a creature focuses on you:
- Go still.
- Make yourself boring.
- Back away diagonally, not straight-line retreat (straight lines may cross a filament you didn’t see).

## Built objects: strict interaction boundaries

### The pylons (half-circle)
Treat the space between/near them as **an active field gate**.
- Maintain at least 20–30 meters distance unless you have dedicated instrumentation and a belayed approach.
- Never stand where you can see “cold heat haze” distortion centered on your torso—move out of it.
- If droplets pause midair near you, you’re too close.

### Filaments (nearly invisible arcs)
Assume:
- They carry charge or encode measurement paths.
- Crossing them might not hurt immediately but could “tag” you (pressure-web sensation, delayed effects).

Protocol:
- Do not step over a filament.
- Mark them with flagging tape at a distance (do not attach tape to the filament).
- If you accidentally contact one: stop, note time, back away the way you came, and monitor for symptoms (ear pressure change, metallic taste spike, skin prickling, time-perception distortion).

### The doorframe
This is the most dangerous “simple” object on the shelf.

Rules:
- Do not put any part of your body through the plane “to test it.”
- Do not throw objects through (retrieval temptation is how you escalate).
- If you must examine: view from the side, not head-on, and keep a tethered partner behind you.

If you step through unintentionally:
- Do not run.
- Immediately check: color shift, sound delay, your watch vs backup watch.
- Return through the frame at once **unless** it visually desyncs (creatures “fraction out of sync” can mean the doorway is not phase-stable). If it desyncs, prioritize tether contact with your original side; do not wander looking for a “better angle.”

## Food, water, and hygiene (how people get quietly wrecked)

- **Do not drink local water.** Not even boiled. The hazard is not only biological; it may be mineral/chemical/field-structured.
- Bring sealed hydration. Wipe the bottle mouth before drinking—mist carries residue.
- Keep snacks in sealed bags. Anything exposed will get a mineral film fast.

Skin care:
- The constant wet will macerate feet and hands. Change socks early, not late.
- After leaving, treat your boots as contaminated until washed; don’t wear them into your sleeping area.

## Setting up a safe “camp” (if you must stay)

Pick a site that is:
- On textured rock,
- Above any obvious flow channels,
- Far from pylons, filaments, and the doorframe,
- Not in a fog “vent line” (watch one full fog cycle before committing).

Sleep is risky here because pulses can shift your environment while you’re inert. If you must sleep:
- Tether yourself to a solid anchor.
- Keep helmet within reach.
- Keep respirator and goggles immediately accessible (steam vent + mineral mist can spike fast).

## Emergency triggers (leave immediately if…)

- You experience **persistent ear pressure** that doesn’t equalize within a minute.
- You see **droplets pausing** around you away from the pylons (field zone drifted onto you).
- Your two time sources diverge by more than a few seconds over short intervals.
- You notice **“down” changing** (sediment hopping sideways, puddles deepening without inflow) in repeated pulses near your route.
- Fog cycles accelerate beyond what you observed on arrival (calibration ramping up).
- You glimpse “no-data” blankness expanding or moving closer to the shelf.

Exit strategy should always be: backtrack along marked physical points, not “shortcuts.”

## Post-visit decontamination and aftercare

1. Bag outer layers before entering any clean space.
2. Rinse gear with brought water + mild detergent.
3. Wash exposed skin; flush eyes if irritated.
4. Monitor for 24–72 hours:
   - Dizziness, time-perception anomalies, headache,
   - Skin rash (mineral/contact),
   - Respiratory irritation.

If you brought recordings: keep them. Changes in hum harmonics might correlate with any delayed symptoms.
