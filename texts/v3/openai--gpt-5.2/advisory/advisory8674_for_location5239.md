## What you’re going into (in human terms)
This pocket is essentially a newborn volcanic vent/cave at high altitude: **thin outside air + hot, chemically nasty, layered inside air + abrasive dust + sharp basalt + darkness + constant loud roar + static**. It behaves like a “natural industrial plant” that can change its flow paths without warning. The two main killers for an ordinary human are:

1. **Breathing the wrong air** (low O₂, high CO₂ pooling, acid gases like SO₂/H₂S, possibly HF/HCl, fine particulates).
2. **Heat + confinement + disorientation** (you cannot “tough it out” if you get turned around or if your mask/air supply fails).

Everything else—cuts, burns, falls, contaminated gear, altitude illness—are the secondary ways you lose time until #1 or #2 finishes the job.

---

## Pre-trip preparation (before you even pack)

### Medical and physical readiness
- **Altitude competence:** if you’re not acclimatized, arrive and spend *at least* 24–72 hours at elevation before doing any work near the slit. Have a plan for AMS/HACE/HAPE.
  - Consider **acetazolamide** (if you know you tolerate it) and bring a **pulse oximeter**.
- **Confined-space tolerance:** practice moving while wearing:
  - a harness + helmet + gloves,
  - a full-face mask,
  - and a pack or air cylinder.
  This place punishes first-timers because you will snag on ribs and crystals and instinctively lift your mask/visor when you panic.
- **Heat stress conditioning:** you will be in protective gear near radiant heat. If you’re prone to overheating, shorten your allowable exposure dramatically.

### Mindset (this is not a cave hike)
Adopt these rules before you arrive:
- **If you smell anything, you are already unprotected.** Your goal is to smell *nothing* because you’re on supplied air.
- **Never go lower than your air-monitor probe.** CO₂ and heavier gases can form invisible “floors.”
- **Never enter without a tether and an outside watcher.** If you go down alone, you’re gambling on a medical emergency in toxic air.

## Core hazards and what they imply for gear

### 1) Atmosphere: unknown mix, layered, hot, acidic, suffocating
**Assume**: low O₂, high CO₂ in depressions, SO₂/H₂S/CO, abrasive mineral dust, maybe halogen acids (HF/HCl) depending on geology.

**Implication:** a normal respirator is not enough.
- Use **supplied air**: **SCBA** (self-contained breathing apparatus) or a **surface-supplied airline with escape bottle** if you can rig it safely.
- Bring a **multi-gas meter** that reads at minimum: **O₂, CO₂, H₂S, SO₂, CO** (+ PID/VOC if you have it).
- Bring **detector tubes** (colorimetric) as a sanity check for weird chemistry; meters lie when sensors get poisoned.

### 2) Heat and radiant hotspots
- Wear **heat-resistant base layer** (Nomex or equivalent) with an **acid-resistant outer shell** where you’ll brush deposits.
- Bring a **thermal camera** or at least an IR thermometer. Your eyes won’t judge radiant heat well in darkness.

### 3) Dust + crystals + sharp fresh basalt
- **Sealed goggles or full-face mask** (not safety glasses).
- **Cut-resistant gloves** over chemical gloves (dexterity + protection).
- **Boots** with aggressive tread and a puncture-resistant midsole.
- Expect surfaces that crumble into powder but also hide **razor edges**.

### 4) Noise and constant vibration-like roar
- Wear **hearing protection** (earplugs + earmuffs if you can fit them with your helmet/mask).
- Plan for impaired communication: hand signals and tether signals matter.

### 5) Static discharge / ignition risk
Even if it’s “mostly mineral,” treat it like a dust-and-gas industrial space:
- Prefer **intrinsically safe** lights and meters.
- Avoid sparking tools.
- Keep fabrics **anti-static** if possible. Don’t wear fleece that turns you into a charge generator.

### 6) Biological contamination (both directions)
Life here is surface-based and chemically tuned. You can kill patches by stepping on them—or introduce spores/oils from your world.
- Treat it like a clean/dirty environment: **bagged suit protocols**, **gear decon**, and **no bare-skin contact**.

## Packing list (practical, human-scale)

### Life support and PPE (non-negotiable)
- **SCBA** (full-face) with **spare cylinder(s)** *or* surface-supplied air with escape bottle.
- **Multi-gas meter** (O₂/CO₂/H₂S/SO₂/CO) + calibration kit/bump-test gas.
- **Helmet** with chin strap + mounted intrinsically safe headlamp.
- **Full-face protection** (ideally integrated with SCBA).
- **Hearing protection.**
- **Heat-resistant, acid-resistant clothing system:**
  - Nomex-like underlayer
  - acid-resistant oversuit or apron-style outer
- **Gloves:** chemical inner + cut/heat outer.
- **Boots:** ankle support, puncture-resistant.
- **Harness** + cow’s tails/lanyards (via ferrata style) for clipped movement on ribs.
- **Rope kit:** static rope, anchors, quick links, edge protection, and a compact mechanical ascender/descender system.

### Navigation, light, and marking (because the place is geometry + darkness)
- **Two independent light sources** on your person (headlamp + chest/hand).
- **Reflective markers** or IR markers (chalk will vanish into dust).
- **Tether line** with distance marks every meter.
- **Thermal camera** (helps you “see” airflow paths and hot zones in darkness).

### Sampling / interaction (optional, but if you’re curious)
- Sterile swabs, vials, forceps; **sealed sample bags**.
- A small **scraper** and **non-sparking** tools.
- **Field microscope** or lens for microbial films.
- **Do not** collect with bare gloves you’ll later use for climbing; cross-contamination becomes a slip hazard and a biology hazard.

### Medical and emergency
- **Altitude meds** you know you tolerate + pulse oximeter.
- **Burn kit** + sterile saline + eye wash.
- **Calcium gluconate gel** (specifically if there’s any chance of HF exposure; many volcanic vents can include it).
- **Hydration + electrolytes**, but plan to drink *outside* the pocket; you don’t want to remove a mask inside.
- **Rescue knife/line cutter** accessible with either hand.
- **Emergency shelter** and insulation for the barren cold outside (the exit world is still a high massif).

## Team structure and protocols (how ordinary humans survive this)

### Minimum team
- **Rule of 3:** one inside, one at the lip managing tether/air line, one free to rescue/call for help.
- If you insist on solo: don’t. This is one of the worst environments for it.

### Entry checklist (do this every time)
1. **Bump-test** gas meter.
2. Confirm **SCBA pressure**, seal check, comms check.
3. Confirm **tether attached** to harness, anchor rated, edge protected.
4. Lights: primary + backup on your body, not in your pack.
5. Agree on **turnaround pressure/time** (example: turn at 2/3 cylinder, exit by 1/2 if exerting).
6. Establish **tether signals** (e.g., 1 tug = stop, 2 = take up slack, 3 = emergency haul).

### Air management (the rule that keeps you alive)
- Treat your breathing gas like you’re diving.
- Set a **hard turnaround** well before you “need” it; climbing out of a slit in heat while stressed burns air fast.
- Never rely on “I’ll just hold my breath and dash out” as a backup. In layered CO₂/SO₂ air, that’s how people die one meter from safety.

## How to move and behave inside the pocket

### Approach at the mouth
- Don’t kneel and “peer in” with your face near the seam. **CO₂ can pour out like an invisible spill**.
- Probe with:
  - a **gas meter on a pole** (sample low, mid, high),
  - a **thermal camera** (hot updrafts, cold sinks),
  - and observe dust movement (airflow direction changes are warnings).

### Descent and positioning
- Move as if it’s a fragile factory:
  - **Stay high** when possible; heavier gases pool low.
  - Avoid “comfortable ledges” if they are in dead-air pockets.
- Clip in frequently; the basalt ribs are great handholds until they aren’t.
- Keep your **center of gravity** tight; crystals will crumble under sideways load.

### Touch discipline
- Assume every pale shelf and yellow/gray crust is **chemically reactive and brittle**.
- Touch only what you must, with the outer glove, and keep one “clean hand” for critical operations (rope, valves, mask).

### Noise and communication
- You will not hear normal speech reliably. Plan:
  - hand signs in headlamp beam,
  - tether tugs,
  - or throat-mic / bone-conduction comms if you have them.

## Contamination and decontamination (don’t bring the pocket home with you)
This place coats you in fine reactive powder.

### On exit (at the lip, before you remove anything)
- **Dry brush-down** with a dedicated brush *away from the opening* (don’t dump dust back into the vent).
- Bag outer layers in sealed bags.
- Do not eat/drink until hands/face are cleaned.

### Back at camp
- Rinse eyes and exposed skin.
- Wash gear with appropriate neutral protocols (water may be scarce; bring wipes and a controlled rinse system).
- If you took biological samples, keep them sealed and segregated from your clothing/air gear.

---

## Ethical and practical “don’ts” that matter here
- **Don’t step on microbial mats** unless you accept that you’re bulldozing the foundation of the ecosystem.
- **Don’t leave tape, markers, or fibers**; this is a place where surfaces are “everything,” and tiny foreign materials can become persistent.
- **Don’t chase fauna** in confined space. Startling a swarm in dusty, staticky air is how you get disoriented and snagged.

---

## A conservative first-visit plan (what I’d recommend)
1. **Day 1:** altitude acclimation + surface mapping + anchor assessment around the slit.
2. **Day 2:** instrument-only survey: gas profiling (top/mid/low), thermal survey, airflow mapping, sound/vibration logging. No entry beyond arm’s length.
3. **Day 3:** short tethered entry (10–20 minutes) on SCBA, staying near the mouth, focusing on route proofing and identifying safe “high shelves.”
4. Only after you’ve repeated stable readings: deeper exploration, and only with staged markers and a pre-rigged extraction plan.

---

## Final reality check
You can be an excellent climber and still die here from **one bad breath** or **one wrong kneel into a CO₂ layer**. Prepare like you’re entering a confined industrial space with volcanic chemistry: supplied air, tethered movement, redundant lights, continuous monitoring, and strict turnaround rules. If you do that, the pocket stops being “alien” and becomes what it really is: a harsh, loud, beautiful system you can visit briefly—on its terms.
