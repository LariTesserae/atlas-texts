## What you’re walking into (so you pack like you mean it)
This district is effectively a **warm, saturated, industrial wetland** that has been **stabilized by living architecture and constant maintenance**. The main stresses on a visitor are:

- **Moisture that never lets up** (skin, lungs, gear, paper, electronics, adhesives).
- **Yielding ground** with hidden water and uneven load-bearing mats.
- **Constant machinery** (noise, moving belts, exposed wet metal, hot pipes, pressurized lines).
- **Biological management** (fungal control, pest exclusion, strict waste handling).
- **Dense sightlines and narrow corridors** that make it easy to get turned around or accidentally trespass into work zones.

Prepare as if you’re visiting **a greenhouse + ship engine room + marsh boardwalk**, all at once, and it’s someone’s home and workplace.

## Health preparation (do this before you step through)
### 1) Lungs / spores / mold tolerance
Even if the town is well-managed, a warm wet environment means **microbial load is real**.

- If you have asthma, COPD, mold allergy, or immunosuppression: bring a **tight-seal respirator (P100/FFP3)** and plan limited exposure until you know how your body reacts.
- Pack **saline rinse** (nasal), and consider a **non-drowsy antihistamine** if you’re prone to reactions.
- Have **antifungal cream/powder** on you (feet, groin, under straps). This place is “athlete’s foot country” even when it’s clean.

### 2) Skin maceration and trench-foot prevention
Your feet will be the first failure point.

- Pre-trip: toughen with short wet-wear practice walks in your actual boots/socks.
- Bring **barrier ointment** (zinc oxide or similar) for hotspots.
- Plan a daily “drying interval” even if you never get truly dry.

### 3) Vaccinations / wound infection assumptions
Cuts in warm wet environments go bad fast.

- Have current **tetanus** coverage.
- Carry **wound wash**, sterile strips, and an **occlusive dressing** option (you’ll snag yourself on vine-fiber, metal edges, or braid-joins).
- Any puncture + swampy floor contact: treat as contaminated.

## Clothing and personal gear (what actually works here)
### Footwear (most important)
You need traction, water management, and puncture resistance.

- **Boots:** mid or high ankle, **aggressive siped sole**, puncture-resistant midsole if possible. Waterproof is helpful but don’t rely on it; wet will get in from above.
- **Socks:** bring many. Best combo is **thin wicking liner + thicker wool/synthetic outer**. Change often.
- Consider **gaiters** to keep debris and canal splash out.

Avoid smooth-soled “city” shoes. The plant-woven walkways will be slick and the root faces can be like wet stairs.

### Clothing
- **Light, fast-drying, long sleeves/pants**. You want abrasion protection without overheating.
- A **vented rain shell** is still useful—not for rain, but for brushing through wet foliage and protecting inner layers.
- Bring **one set of “clean dry” clothes** sealed in a waterproof bag; treat it like emergency reserves.

### Hands and eye protection
- **Work gloves** (thin grippy nitrile-coated or similar) for railings, ladders, and anywhere near woven stems/metal.
- **Safety glasses** if you plan to enter workshop areas (sap spray, fiber dust, belt snap, press splatter).

### Hearing
Noise is layered and constant; it will fatigue you.

- Pack **earplugs** or **filtered ear protection**. You want to hear warnings, but not absorb pump-bass all day.

## Electronics, documents, and tools: assume everything will get damp
### Electronics
- Use a **waterproof pouch** or dry bag. Keep devices on lanyards; canals and narrow walkways don’t forgive drops.
- Bring **desiccant packs** and swap them daily.
- Any charging: use **GFCI-protected** sources if available; otherwise keep it minimal and supervised.

### Paper is a liability
If you must carry paper: laminate it or keep it sealed. Prefer **etched/plastic notebook** or a device in a sealed case.

### Light
You’ll hit shaded corridors where daylight doesn’t reach well.

- A **headlamp** with a strap that won’t soak-stretch, plus a backup micro-light.

## Navigation and behavior in a dense, living industrial maze
### Getting around without causing trouble
This place runs on rules. Visitors who behave like tourists become hazards.

- **Stay on trained walkways.** “Looks walkable” is not the same as load-bearing here.
- **Don’t step onto braided mats that are actively being trained** (tension lines, fresh cuts, glossy sap, new bindings).
- **Do not touch valves, belts, or drip-lines.** Small adjustments are part of moisture control; you can cause a cascade (condensation → rot → sag → failure).
- If you need to stop: choose a **platform edge with rail/root-shoulder support**, not mid-corridor.

### Wayfinding tricks that work
- Follow **sound gradients**: big pump rooms are anchors; textile/press areas have distinct rhythms.
- Track **canal orientation**: canals are “streets” here. Learn which ones are main lines vs service seams.
- Note **heat corridors**: warm, slightly drier passages are often primary routes.

Bring or improvise a simple **breadcrumb method** that doesn’t damage anything: chalk is messy in wet; better is a **temporary clip marker** on your own strap, or notes on your map keyed to sound/odor landmarks.

## Environmental hazards you should plan around
### 1) Slips, sags, and “polite water”
The water isn’t turbulent, but it’s everywhere.

- Expect **unexpected shin-deep holes** where a mat ends or a basin begins.
- Root-lined canal edges can be **near-vertical and slick**—don’t assume you can climb out where you fell in.
- Practice a “test step”: put weight in slowly; if it yields, back off.

### 2) Machinery in damp conditions
Wet metal + moving parts + tight corridors = unforgiving.

- Stay well clear of **rotating shafts, belts, rollers, looms**.
- Loose clothing, dangling straps, long hair: secure them.
- Don’t lean on “harmless” housings—they may be hot, vibrating, or slick with oil.

### 3) Electrical zones
You’ll smell ozone near distribution rooms. Treat those like restricted areas.

- Keep distance; don’t pass unless guided.
- Never handle overhead bundles that “look like vines.” They may be insulated lines.

### 4) Heat stress
It’s warm, humid, and you may not feel sweat evaporating.

- Hydrate more than you think; include **electrolytes**.
- Watch for headache, lethargy, nausea—classic heat strain in humid shade.

## Biosecurity and etiquette (this place will care what you bring in)
Because fauna are actively discouraged, locals will be sensitive about contamination.

### Don’t introduce pests, seeds, or pathogens
Before entry:
- Clean mud off footwear and gear.
- Remove hitchhiker seeds/plant bits from clothing seams.
- If you traveled from somewhere insect-heavy, expect (or request) a **decon rinse**.

Inside:
- Don’t discard organic waste casually.
- Don’t feed tolerated scavengers (if you see any); it changes their behavior and invites more.

### Don’t cut or harvest anything casually
Here, plants are **infrastructure**.

- If you need clearance, ask. Even “one vine” may be tension-bearing or part of a grafted arch.
- Don’t carve initials, strip bark, or test “how strong” a braid is.

### Respect water routing
Spilling is normal; blocking flow is not.

- Don’t pile bags where they dam a groove or runnel.
- Don’t reroute drip-lines “to stop the drip.” That drip is probably deliberate.

## What to pack (practical list)
**Carry-on / day kit**
- Dry bag or waterproof pouch
- 2–3 sock changes
- Antifungal powder/cream + blister care
- Small first aid (wound wash, dressings, tape)
- Ear protection
- Headlamp + backup light
- 1–2 L water capacity + electrolytes
- Gloves + safety glasses (especially if touring workshops)
- Compact towel or absorbent cloth
- Desiccants for electronics

**If staying overnight**
- A reliable “dry capsule”: sealed bag with sleep clothes + spare base layer
- Sandals or camp shoes (for giving feet air)
- Clothesline + clips (things will need hanging constantly)
- Mild detergent/soap that won’t disrupt local water systems (ask what’s acceptable)

## Arrival checklist (first hour in-country)
1. **Assess your feet:** tighten laces, set gaiters, decide how often you’ll change socks.
2. **Identify a safe rest platform** near your entry point—somewhere you can regroup without blocking corridors.
3. **Locate the main canals** and one major pump anchor by sound.
4. **Ask about restricted zones** (electrical rooms, press lines, biofilter beds).
5. **Match local pace:** the safest way to move here is unhurried, eyes down for footing, ears up for machinery cues.

---

## How to conduct yourself day-to-day (to avoid becoming the problem)
- Walk like you’re on a wet ship deck: **one hand free**, short steps, deliberate weight transfer.
- Assume every surface is either **load-bearing** or **alive and being trained**; treat both gently.
- If you drop something into a canal: don’t wade blindly. Ask—there may be intake grates, filters, or contamination rules.
- If you feel chilled despite warmth: you’re likely **wet-cold** from constant damp; change layers early.
