## What you’re walking into (so you pack for the right problems)

This pocket-chamber is not “hazardous” in the dramatic way (no predators, no collapse vibe). It’s hazardous in the *physiology* way:

- **Desiccation:** air that steals moisture fast; dust that behaves like ash; salts that will happily take water from you.
- **Acoustic load:** a constant, high-energy drone with harmonics—fatiguing, disorienting, and potentially hearing-damaging even if it doesn’t “sound” painfully loud at first.
- **Optical overload:** bright, source-less white light with fixed shadows; lots of specular scatter; little visual rest.
- **Mechanical abrasion:** fine mineral dust + steady wind = sandblasting over time; unpleasant for eyes, lungs, joints, and delicate gear.
- **Crowding/geometry:** small volume, many protrusions, niches, baffles—easy to bump, snag, or lose the exit slit if you stop thinking.

Treat it like a **dry, resonant, reflective machine-space** that’s been running for ages.

## Pre-trip preparation (24–48 hours out)

### Medical / body prep
- **Hydrate aggressively in advance.** The place will pull water from your breath and skin; you want to start topped off.
- **Nasal and lip protection plan:** saline gel (not spray-mist), lip balm, and a small tube of skin barrier cream. Use *before* entry.
- **If you have tinnitus, vestibular issues, migraines, or panic/claustrophobia:** strongly consider not going alone. This environment is tailor-made to amplify those.

### Mental prep
- Decide *beforehand*:
- **Maximum time inside** (e.g., 10–20 minutes for a first entry).
- **Hard turn-back triggers:** headache, ear pain/pressure, “floating” sensation, rising panic, confusion, or if you stop being able to judge direction to the slit.

The chamber’s constancy encourages “just a little longer.” Don’t negotiate with it.

## Core kit (what an ordinary human should bring)

### Breathing / eyes / ears (non-negotiable)
- **P100 (or FFP3) respirator** with a good face seal. This is mineral dust country; don’t rely on a cloth mask.
- **Sealed goggles** (indirect vented at most). The dust is fine and persistent; regular glasses won’t cut it.
- **Hearing protection, doubled:** foam plugs *plus* over-ear muffs (high NRR/SNR).  
  - The danger isn’t only volume; it’s duration and low-frequency vibration fatigue.

### Clothing / impact protection
- **Long sleeves, long pants**, tightly woven fabric.
- **Thin gloves** (nitrile under light work gloves is ideal): keeps salts/dust off skin and prevents you from unconsciously licking or rubbing your mouth/eyes.
- **Knee pads** if you expect to crouch or pass tight spots.
- **Bump cap or climbing helmet:** the architecture is “close-set” and you’ll misjudge distance under glare.

### Navigation / “do not lose the seam” tools
- **A tether line** (thin static cord) with a solid anchor outside the slit, plus a simple reel or figure-eight wrap so it doesn’t snag constantly.
- **High-contrast markers** that work in white glare:
  - matte black tape strips, or charcoal/chalk *in a sealed applicator* (don’t create a dust plume).
- **A small, directional flashlight** (even though it’s bright). You need it for *contrast and shadow control*, not illumination. A narrow beam helps you read edges.

### Water (yes, but managed)
- **Water in a bite-valve bladder** or a bottle you can open without spilling.  
- Plan to **drink outside** unless you must. If “nothing becomes wet” holds true literally, a spill may:
  - vanish into salts immediately (leaving slippery crystals or crust),
  - or aerosolize/flash off oddly, putting more irritant into the air.
Either way: avoid introducing free water.

### Basic instrumentation (optional but very useful)
- **Decibel meter app + external mic** (rough info is still info).
- **Pocket hygrometer/thermometer** (expect “very low” humidity; confirm).
- **A small notepad + pencil** (speech may feel swallowed by the drone; writing stays reliable).

## Finding and securing the entry (outside protocol)

This place is found by a “seam,” and seams get lost.

1. **Mark the exterior approach from far away.**  
   Use a tall visible marker (bright cloth on a pole, rock cairn, whatever fits your ethics) that you can see across the flats.
2. **Photograph the seam area from multiple angles** before entering (wide + close). In a featureless basin, photos are your map.
3. **Anchor the tether outside** to something you trust (a buried deadman anchor if needed). Don’t rely on a loose rock in brittle crust.
4. **Assume the slit is tighter on exit** than it looked on entry—because your brain will be tired, your pack will snag, and you’ll hurry.

Pack discipline: go in with a **low-profile setup**; nothing dangling.

## Entry procedure (how to avoid immediate overload)

- **Put on respirator + goggles + earplugs before the slit.**  
  Don’t wait until you “feel” the dust or sound; by then you’ve already taken a dose.
- **Add ear muffs immediately after entry** when you can do it without snagging.
- **Pause just inside** for 30–60 seconds:
  - check breathing seal,
  - check tether tension,
  - do a slow head turn to see how glare affects depth perception.

If you get instant vertigo: leave. That’s not weakness; it’s a predictable sensory mismatch.

## Moving inside (this chamber punishes casual motion)

### General movement rules
- **One hand stays “navigation-ready.”** Either on tether or on a consistent wall feature. The space is visually crowded; you want a physical reference.
- **Step lightly.** The dust is ultrafine; you can create your own visibility problem.
- **Avoid brushing walls/screens.** Salt blooms and mineral films may be fragile, and you don’t want residue on gloves that later touches your face.

### Managing the sound
- The constant chord will start to feel like pressure. Expect:
  - jaw tension,
  - tooth ache sensations,
  - fatigue/irritability,
  - time distortion (minutes feel long or vanish).
- **Do timed check-ins** (watch/vibration timer). Don’t rely on “feel.”

If you must communicate with a companion:
- use **hand signals** or **written notes**. Talking over the drone encourages shouting, which increases dehydration and fatigue.

### Managing the light
- Use **tinted or polarized eye protection** if your goggles allow inserts; otherwise, take micro-breaks: close eyes for 10 seconds while holding the tether.
- Depth perception will lie. Treat every edge like it’s closer than it looks.

## What not to do (specific to this place)

- **Do not lick lips, touch face, or eat inside.** Dust + salts + dryness becomes a throat problem fast.
- **Do not introduce moisture “to test the rule.”** Apart from ethics, it can create unpredictable salt reactions and make the surface nastier for the next traveler (including you on exit).
- **Do not remove structural material.** Those fitted slabs and voids are what make the stable light and stable wind; disturbing them may change airflow, sound pressure, or the slit’s behavior.
- **Do not chase the sound.** People instinctively move toward resonant “sweet spots.” In a crowded chamber, that’s how you get turned around.

## Exit protocol (leave like you might need to come back fast)

- **Leave on a schedule, not on a feeling.**
- Follow the **tether out** even if you “see” the seam—your eyes will be tired and overconfident.
- Once outside:
  - keep respirator on until you’re upwind and away from disturbed dust,
  - drink water,
  - check for nosebleed, headache, ear ringing, or eye irritation.

If you plan a second entry that day: don’t. Give your ears and mucosa recovery time; the damage here is cumulative and sneaky.

---

## Emergency considerations

- **Hearing pain or sudden ringing spike:** exit immediately; keep protection on. Low-frequency exposure can injure without “loudness.”
- **Panic/claustrophobia:** stop, hand on tether, slow exhale through respirator, then move out without rushing (rushing snags you).
- **Respirator failure (strap breaks, seal lost):** don’t “just finish the look.” Leave. The dust is the long-term hazard you won’t feel until later.
