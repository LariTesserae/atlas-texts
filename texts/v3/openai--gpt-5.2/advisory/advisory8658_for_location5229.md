## What you’re walking into (in plain hazard terms)

This basin is not “a dive site.” It’s **warm, pressurized, high-energy moving water in a hard stone bowl**, with:

- **Constant extreme broadband noise** that transmits through water and into your body (bone conduction). Expect pain, disorientation, fatigue, and potential tissue/inner-ear injury even if you “can’t hear” it normally.
- **Violent, changeable currents**: pinning flows, suction, vortex formation, sudden slackening, and a central column that **periodically reverses** and then drops everything it lifted.
- **Sharp, young basalt**: knife-edges, spines, fresh fractures, unstable talus.
- **Localized physics “glitches” triggered by resonance**:  
  - current sheets acting like solid planes  
  - slight gravity/“down” tilts  
  - bubbles and silt behaving non-intuitively  
  - brief “memory cracks” showing air/sky (high distraction risk)
- **Low visibility** from fines + constant turbulence; light will backscatter.
- Sparse but persistent life; nothing here will save you, but some things may react to resonance in ways you don’t predict.

If you prepare like it’s a normal ocean dive, this place will hurt you quickly. Prepare like it’s a **high-flow cave + industrial vibration exposure + anomalous acoustics**.

## Core strategy: don’t “swim” it—**anchor, tether, and stage**

An ordinary human survives this basin by treating it as a **tethered excursion from a stable refuge**, not as free-roaming exploration.

1. **Stage a stable entry/refuge point** (behind a fin, in an eddy, or—best—near that arch where the roar changes key).
2. **Run a hard guideline and a comms tether** from refuge to any exploration point.
3. **Move only when currents slacken**; otherwise you clip in and wait.
4. **Never be in the center column during a resonance swell** unless that’s the explicit plan and you’re rigged for it.

## Physical preparation (your body)

### Medical/fitness prerequisites
- **Cardio and CO₂ tolerance**: the stress response + work against current will spike breathing. If you’re prone to panic, CO₂ headaches, or asthma, fix that before you go.
- **Vestibular resilience**: constant vibration + shifting “down” will scramble balance. If you get motion-sick easily, train and medicate accordingly.
- **Ear health**: any prior inner-ear injury is a major risk. Underwater vibration can do damage without “loud sound” in the usual sense.

### Conditioning that actually helps
- High-flow finning and “pull-and-glide” techniques (think: river rescue / cave diving).
- Rope/line discipline with thick gloves (clipping, re-clipping, anti-entanglement habits).
- Task performance while body is buzzing (practice with vibration platforms / loud sub-bass).

### Drugs/meds (practical, not heroic)
- **Anti-nausea** (meclizine or similar) is worth it.
- **Anxiety/panic management** only if you already know how you react (this is not the place to experiment).
- Avoid anything that impairs judgment or slows reaction time—this environment punishes hesitation.

## Life support: choose gear that makes the place less “in you”

### Best option: an atmospheric diving suit (ADS) / hard suit
If you can access one, use it. It solves multiple problems at once:
- isolates you from most pressure variation
- reduces direct body exposure to turbulence
- gives you armor against basalt
- allows long dwell without decompression complexity

**But**: it does *not* eliminate vibration; you still need damping and limits on exposure.

### If you must use conventional dive gear
- **Closed-circuit rebreather (CCR)** preferred:
  - fewer bubbles (bubbles behave weirdly here and can impair visibility/“up” cues)
  - better gas efficiency if you get pinned and can’t surface/exit quickly
- Redundant bailout gas and a plan to reach it while pinned.

### Absolute requirements (either way)
- **Full-face mask** (protects face from impact; supports comms; reduces jaw fatigue).
- **Physical tether** rated for shock loads (think “tow line,” not thin cave line).
- **Cutting tools x2** (line cutter + knife), mounted where either hand can reach.
- **Drysuit or thermal layer** even in warm water: vibration and stress burn heat, and geothermal seams can scald locally.
- **Impact/abrasion protection**: hard knee/elbow, thick gloves, hood.

## Surviving the noise (this is the first law here)

You can’t rely on “earplugs.” Underwater, the roar is in your bones.

### What to do instead
- **Vibration isolation**:
  - padding at contact points (shoulders/hips/helmet suspension)
  - avoid bracing your chest or jaw against rock/gear
- **Limit exposure time**:
  - set strict “buzz thresholds” (when teeth ache, ribcage resonates, or you feel breath/heart rhythm disruption: you retreat to refuge)
- **Communication that doesn’t require hearing**:
  - tethered fiber-optic or wired comms
  - haptic signals (vibration taps on line, pulse codes)
  - light codes as backup (wide-beam, low-scatter)

### Cognitive effects to expect
- impaired time sense
- irrational urgency (“move now!”)
- difficulty forming short-term memory (“what was I doing?”)

Countermeasure: **ritualize your procedure**. Everything is checklists and clipped steps.

## Current management: you don’t fight it—you “clip and breathe”

### Movement rules
- Never cross open water without a plan to **arrest** (clip point, hook, or piton) within a few seconds.
- Approach ledges diagonally, not straight-on; straight approaches get you pinned.
- Use **handholds sparingly**: fresh basalt cuts gloves and hands fast.

### Anchoring kit that matters here
- Removable rock anchors/pitons suitable for basalt fractures
- A short **cow’s tail lanyard** for quick clipping
- A **grapnel/hook** you can sacrifice (this place eats equipment)

### Safe micro-habit
Any time you stop to look at something: **one hand on tether, one hand on anchor**. Curiosity kills quickly in moving water.

## Resonance/glitch protocol (how not to get surprised)

You described repeatable patterns: chords, peaks, and then rule breaks.

### Instrumentation that helps
- A **hydrophone + spectral analyzer** (even a ruggedized simple unit) to detect the recurring “bell” notes amid the roar.
- A simple **traffic-light alarm**: green (stable), amber (approaching known chord), red (imminent reversal/solid-sheet events).

### Behavioral rules
- When you detect a build toward the known chord:
  - **get off the basin floor** (falling stones + silt rain are real)
  - **avoid the center ring** unless you’re intentionally riding the reversal
  - clip in with slack that won’t whip you into rock

### The “solid current wall” hazard
If a flow becomes a pressure-plane, you can get pinned in a way that feels like invisible architecture.
- Don’t push; you waste oxygen and time.
- Move **laterally along the plane** to find its edge while maintaining tether contact.
- If gravity tilts, assume your sense of “sideways escape” is wrong—follow the tether, not your gut.

## The center column reversal: treat it like an elevator that drops its passengers

It’s tempting to use the reversal to gain height or cross gaps. You can—if you rig for it.

### If you want to observe it safely
- Watch from a wall anchor point with overhead clearance.
- Keep your body streamlined; anything loose becomes a sail.

### If you plan to “ride” it (high risk)
- You need:
  - a **secondary belay line** above/behind you so you can arrest when the chord breaks
  - a plan for the “rain” phase (helmet, face protection, retreat vector)
- Never ride it when you’re fatigued. Timing errors here are expensive.

## Entry checklist (what you do at the threshold)

1. **Confirm exit is real and reachable** (portal/route stable, tether secured).
2. Establish **refuge anchor** (two independent anchors; equalized).
3. Run a **primary line** with tactile direction markers.
4. Do a **comms check using haptics**, not audio.
5. Set a **hard turn time** (example: 20 minutes in basin, 10 minutes in refuge, reassess).
6. Agree on **two abort triggers**:
   - physiological (rib buzz/teeth ache/vertigo spike)
   - environmental (approaching chord + inability to reach refuge within X seconds)

## Emergency playbook (simple, because you won’t think clearly)

### If you get pinned to rock by current
- Stop fighting.
- Clip in.
- Slow breathing (CO₂ control).
- Signal via tether.
- Wait for slackening; move only on slack.

### If visibility goes to zero
- Face into the line.
- Hand-over-hand to refuge. No free swimming.

### If your “down” tilts and you panic
- Close eyes.
- Find tether with both hands.
- Follow it. Do not negotiate with your inner ear.

### If the chord hits while you’re in open water
- Get to wall/anchor point fast.
- Protect head/face; expect falling debris when the column drops.

## Aftercare (don’t skip this)

Exposure here is like a full-body concert inside a washing machine.

- Hydrate, warm down, and monitor for:
  - persistent vertigo/tinnitus
  - unusual bruising pain (vibration injury)
  - numbness/tingling
  - decompression symptoms (treat aggressively—don’t “wait and see”)
- Sleep may be disturbed; your nervous system will keep “hearing” the basin. Plan a quiet recovery environment.
