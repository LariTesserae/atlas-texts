## What you’re walking into (in human terms)

This is an underground, geode-like basin-city running on **heat + wind + dry reactive chemistry**. To you it will feel like:

- **Desert-dry to the point of injury** (skin stinging, lips cracking fast, eyes drying out).
- **Hot, but unevenly**: comfortable-to-dangerous pockets depending on vents, airflow corridors, and exposed mineral surfaces.
- **Dusty and gaseous**: fine particulates that don’t settle + mineral/acid-forming vapors in some zones.
- **Very loud in a structural way**: wind-driven resonance, constant whistles/booms/chimes that can fatigue you and interfere with comms.
- **Biologically “empty” but chemically alive**: resin/wax/mineral-gel “plants” that can be brittle, sticky, or fume-trapping.

The single biggest rule for a human traveler:

### Do not introduce water into the pocket.
Not as a “helpful” resource, not as cleaning, not as spilled drinking water, not as sweat-soaked rags you wring out. Water here is a **reactant**. It can:
- flash to steam and fracture ceramics/stone
- mobilize salts and form corrosives
- kill or scar the resin ecology
- create slicks or spalling surfaces that become falling hazards

You *can* bring water for yourself, but it must remain **contained and controlled**.

## Core hazards to prepare for

### 1) Dehydration + desiccation injury
You will lose moisture through breathing and skin faster than you expect, even if ambient temperatures aren’t extreme everywhere.

**Human failure mode here**: you “feel fine,” then suddenly you’re dizzy, cognitively slow, and your eyes/airways are burning.

### 2) Heat stress in pulses
Vents “breathe” and wind channels move heat around. A corridor can turn from tolerable to dangerous as airflow shifts.

**Human failure mode**: you pick a route that becomes a heat funnel and you can’t cool down because sweat evaporates instantly and your respirator air is warm.

### 3) Respiratory exposure (dust + reactive gases)
The described “burned matches/struck flint” taste suggests sulfur compounds, metal oxides, and other irritants. Dust is persistent and fine enough to get everywhere.

**Human failure mode**: cough/bronchospasm, eye irritation, sinus bleeding, reduced visibility, then panic because the environment is noisy and disorienting.

### 4) Chemical contact hazards (salts/resins/mineral gels)
Some deposits will be hygroscopic (they steal moisture), some resins may off-gas or be solvent-like, and mineral efflorescence can be sharp and fragile.

**Human failure mode**: you touch a surface, your skin dries/whitens/burns, you rub your eyes, now you have a compounded problem.

### 5) Structural + terrain hazards
- Polished stone + dust = low traction.
- Efflorescent “feathers” break into needles.
- Old wind devices and hanging ceramic plaques can move violently in gusts.
- Canal-like channels may contain **hot molten salts/sulfur-rich fluids** that look dull but can cook you instantly.

**Human failure mode**: a simple slip becomes a serious injury because you can’t safely “wash” wounds and you’re far from an exit.

### 6) Navigation + comms degradation
Haze, dim light, repetitive architecture, and constant wind-noise make humans lose orientation quickly.

**Human failure mode**: you think you’re circling back and you’re not; your voice/radio is masked by resonance.

## Personal protective approach (what to wear)

You want to be a sealed, shaded, dust-proof, heat-managed “capsule” that does **not shed water** and does not absorb local chemistry.

### Minimum viable kit (if you’re trying to survive, not just sightsee)
**Respiratory**
- Full-face respirator (not a simple mask), with **P100/HEPA particulate** plus **multi-gas cartridges** (acid gas/organic vapor).  
- If you can: a **powered air-purifying respirator (PAPR)** to reduce breathing effort and keep positive pressure.
- If you can’t verify gas composition: bring a **small SCBA / escape bottle** for emergencies (5–15 minutes can save your life).

**Eye/face**
- Full-face is best. Otherwise sealed goggles + half-mask, but this place will punish any gaps.

**Skin**
- Covered head-to-toe: non-wicking outer layer that doesn’t trap dust (smooth technical fabric or coated textile).
- Gloves: inner nitrile + outer heat-resistant work gloves.
- Boots: heat-resistant sole, aggressive tread, ankle support, gaiters to keep dust out.

**Hearing**
- High-NRR earplugs *plus* earmuffs (layering). This isn’t comfort—it’s fatigue and safety. Wind-driven resonance can be teeth-buzzing.

**Heat + dryness management**
- A **sun/heat hood** even underground: radiant heat and hot gusts matter.
- Cooling strategy that does *not* vent moisture: phase-change packs or insulated layers. Avoid evaporative cooling methods.

### “Nice to have but strongly recommended”
- Lightweight over-suit you can remove at exit to avoid exporting dust/resin.
- Anti-fog treatment for visor/goggles (dry air still fogs from your breath).
- Lip barrier ointment and nasal saline gel (used sparingly and kept internal—don’t smear it on local surfaces).

## Instruments you should bring (don’t go in blind)

If you can carry only a few sensors, prioritize these:

1. **4-gas meter** (O₂, CO, H₂S, LEL)  
   - Even if the exact gases differ, it’s a sanity check for “bad air.”
2. **Particulate meter** (PM2.5/PM10) or at least filter-loading awareness (spare filters).
3. **Temperature gun** (IR thermometer)  
   - For checking canal fluids, vent lips, sunlit mineral patches, “safe-looking” stone.
4. **Humidity + dewpoint meter** (mostly to confirm how aggressively dry it is).
5. **Navigation**: inertial + mapping (GPS may be useless).  
   - Chalk won’t work (too dusty), tape may fail (resins). Use **high-temp ceramic markers** or reflective tags.

Also bring:
- Headlamp with wide flood + a tight spot beam. Dust makes tight beams “glow”; flood helps with footing.
- Spare batteries kept warm/insulated (heat can also cook them).

## Water, food, and the “don’t contaminate the pocket” rule

### How to carry water without harming the environment
- Use **double-contained** bladders/bottles: inner vessel + hard outer shell.
- Drink through a bite valve you can close positively; no open-mouth pouring.
- Bring absorbent pads *inside your suit system* for accidental leaks—do not discard pads in the pocket.
- If you must rinse something (ideally you don’t): do it in a sealed bag and pack it out.

### Hydration strategy
- You will need **more** than you think, but you should sip steadily.
- Add electrolytes. In ultra-dry environments you can dehydrate and cramp even without heavy sweat.

### Food
- Dry, crumb-free, non-greasy foods (crumbs become “foreign bodies” in a dry chemical ecosystem and attract dust to your gear).
- Avoid anything that requires water prep or creates trash you can’t seal.

## Entry protocol (the seam is an active hazard)

That “breathing” fracture is effectively a **hot lung** exchanging air with the pocket.

Before you descend:
1. **Mask on before you approach the seam.**
2. **Secure everything** (no loose straps, no dangling fabric): wind gusts will snatch them.
3. **Thermal check** surfaces as you go. Some rock will be deceptively hot.
4. **Mark the entrance** in a way you can see from inside haze (reflector + line).

Consider running a **guideline** (heat-resistant cord) from outside anchor to your first internal “safe node.” In low visibility and noise, a line beats memory.

## Movement and behavior inside (how not to get hurt)

### Walking rules
- Assume anything polished + dusty is slippery.
- Never step on efflorescent crusts without testing; they can collapse into sharp fragments.
- Give moving wind devices a wide berth—gusts can turn them into swinging hazards.

### Canal/vent rules
- Treat canals as **open molten material** even if they look slow and dull.
- Check with IR thermometer before approaching edges.
- Do not cross canals unless there is a clearly engineered bridge that you’ve verified is stable and not resin-sealed (resin seals can be brittle).

### Touch rules
- Touch nothing bare-handed.
- Don’t lean on walls: salts can wick moisture through fabric and irritate skin.
- Avoid brushing against amber “curtains”—they may trap concentrated dust/volatiles and shed brittle shards.

### Sound + comms rules
- Use **pre-arranged hand signals** and vibration taps. Wind will steal speech.
- Keep radio transmissions short; use throat mic if available.
- Plan quiet “checkpoints” (alcoves out of main wind paths) for longer talk and sensor checks.

## Light, orientation, and not getting lost

This place is built to make humans disoriented: dim, hazy, echoing, repetitive.

Do this:
- Build a **breadcrumb trail** that doesn’t rely on dust-free floors:
  - reflective markers on vertical surfaces
  - numbered tags on doorframes
  - periodic photos with orientation notes (“marker 12 facing vent-glow red”)
- Always know your nearest “return vector” to the seam or to an alternate crack/light blade.
- Watch airflow: dust streams often indicate main corridors back toward the seam’s pressure exchange.

If you start feeling “turned around,” stop immediately, go to one knee, and **rebuild your map**. Wandering burns water and time.

## Managing your body (the ordinary human limits)

### Heat illness prevention
- Work/rest cycles. You don’t “push through” here because there’s no easy rescue.
- If you feel chills, nausea, headache, or irrational anger: treat it as heat stress and retreat to a cooler airflow boundary.

### Desiccation prevention
- Protect eyes and lips early, not after damage.
- Keep your mouth closed when possible; breathe through the respirator properly.
- If you wear contacts: don’t. Use glasses inserts.

### Wound care
- Carry dry wound dressings, antiseptic wipes (minimal liquid), and occlusive coverings.
- If you get dust in a wound, don’t try to “air it out.” Seal it and get out.

## Interaction with the ecology and city (how to be a good visitor)

- Don’t cut or harvest resin/gel structures unless you have containment and a reason. They may be load-bearing seals for domes or vents.
- Don’t disturb fan-palms near vents: they’re likely part of the basin’s chemical equilibrium and may also be fragile.
- Never dump anything—no water, no food, no filters, no wipes. Pack out everything.

If you must sample:
- Use **dry, inert containers** (glass/ceramic-lined if possible).
- Label externally; don’t open containers inside the pocket if you can avoid it.

## Timing and “weather” inside the pocket

You’re dealing with internal “meteorology”:
- Vent pulse cycles
- Dust storm surges
- Wind direction reversals along terraces

Practical planning:
- Spend the first hour doing nothing heroic: measure cycles and identify safe alcoves.
- Do not commit to deep routes until you know where the air becomes unbreathable or the heat spikes.

## Emergency plan (assume you will need it)

### Your three most likely emergencies
1. **Respirator failure / filter overload**
2. **Heat injury**
3. **Slip/fall near hot channel or from terraces**

### Mandatory contingencies
- Carry an **escape air source** (even small).
- Establish a **hard turnaround time** (e.g., “exit when water hits 60%” or “after 2 filter changes”).
- Keep a **tether/guideline** to the seam for at least the first expedition.
- Identify **two exits** if possible: the seam you came in plus at least one secondary crack/light blade.

If visibility collapses in a dust surge:
- Get low (often slightly clearer).
- Follow your guideline or wall-markers out.
- Don’t sprint; falls are worse than slow retreat.

## Mental preparation (it matters here)

Expect **uncanny emptiness** (no animals, no familiar cues) paired with **constant mechanical sound** and **alive-looking plants** that aren’t “soft” the way Earth life is. People commonly make two mistakes in places like this:

- treating it like a ruin (it’s an active system)
- treating dryness like comfort (it’s an ongoing injury)

Go slow, keep everything contained, and treat airflow the way you’d treat water in a river city: it’s the thing that moves everything, including you.
