## What you’re walking into (so you pack the right self)
This is not “a cave.” It’s a maintained, inhabited hydraulic machine built into damp stone, where **water routing, sound behavior, and humidity control are civic law**. You’re visiting a place that survives by keeping equilibrium. The main way an outsider harms it is not violence—it’s **introducing disorder**: contaminants, heat, loudness, metal oxidation, or unplanned water diversion.

Prepare as if you’re entering:
- a **humid cold room** that never dries,
- a **highly reverberant indoor city** where privacy is acoustic, and
- a **waterworks** where every channel is assigned and protected.

## Pre-trip preparation (do this before you even look for the seam)

### 1) Health & body readiness
- **Claustrophobia / sensory fatigue**: the constant drip + resonance + dimness wears people down. If you’re prone to panic, practice slow breathing with earplugs beforehand. Plan shorter stays.
- **Skin care**: constant wet film + mineral residues can macerate skin.
  - Bring a **barrier ointment** (petrolatum or zinc oxide) for hands/feet/inner thighs.
  - Pack **two pairs** of thin liner socks + one thicker pair; rotate to reduce trench-foot-like problems.
- **Respiratory**: air is damp, smoky near lamps/hearths, and biofilm is present.
  - A **P100 / FFP3** respirator is overkill socially, but a **low-profile N95/FFP2** and spare filters are sensible if you’ll be near soot/hearths.
- **Vaccination/antibiotics**: you’re in tight quarters with recycled water. Bring a small course of broad-spectrum antibiotics only if you know how to use them responsibly; otherwise bring wound care and antiseptic.

### 2) Decontamination (you are an invasive species)
The ecology is sparse and stable. Don’t bring a surface biome.
- Arrive with **freshly washed clothing** (no plant debris, no soil).
- Avoid fuzzy fabrics that shed fibers.
- Consider a **wipe-down** before entry (unscented wipes) and bag your outer layer.

### 3) What not to bring
These aren’t just “bad ideas”—they clash with local survival logic.
- **Iron/steel items** if you can avoid them. If you must carry a blade/tool:
  - Choose **stainless**, **bronze**, **titanium**, or **ceramic**.
  - Seal it: **oil + waxed cloth + clay/putty wrap**, kept inside a dry pouch.
- **Glitter, powders, dyes, chalk**: they contaminate channels and cistern edges.
- **Strong perfumes/solvents**: they ride humidity and spread everywhere.
- **Anything that leaks** (batteries, fuel, oil bottles without secondary containment).

## Gear: what actually works down there

### Clothing (humidity-cold + slick stone)
- **Base layer**: synthetic or merino (but merino can stay clammy); bring a spare.
- **Mid layer**: thin fleece or wool that still insulates when damp.
- **Outer**: light waterproof shell *that doesn’t loudly crinkle* (sound matters).
- **Hands**: grippy gloves (rubberized palms). You will touch slick parapets.
- **Feet**: approach shoes or boots with **very high wet traction**; consider microspike-like grip only if it won’t damage stone or offend locals. Expect polished steps.

### Lighting (dim, reflective, and socially sensitive)
Locals use smoky lamps and heated stones; your light can disrupt mood, etiquette, and even humidity patterns if it’s hot.
- Best: **cool LED headlamp** with a **diffuser** and **low mode**.
- Add: a small **red/amber mode** for courtesy in crowded walkways.
- Avoid: high-lumen “searchlight” behavior—glare off wet stone can blind people and feel like aggression.
- Bring **spare batteries in sealed pouches**.

### Navigation & marking (without contaminating)
- Do not chalk walls.
- Use **removable, non-shedding markers**:
  - tied **waxed cord** loops,
  - small **ceramic beads** you can retrieve,
  - or a **paper/cloth map** you update as you go.
- Bring **10–20 m of static cord** and a couple of soft anchors/slings for the entry chute and any steep slick traverse.

### Hygiene & water
Assume water is managed and socially policed.
- Bring your own **collapsible bottle** and **filter**.
- But do not casually filter from “any channel.” Ask which flow is for drinking.
- Bring **unscented soap** only if you can use it where washing is designated; otherwise use wipes and pack them out.

### Tools that won’t start a rust disaster
- Ceramic or polymer **knife** (or stainless sealed).
- **Ceramic scissors** or a small stainless multitool kept oiled and wrapped.
- A few **zip ties** and **waxed twine** (quiet, useful).
- A small **silicone mat** to set items down without touching wet stone or parapets.

### Medical kit (tuned to this place)
- Blister care (hydrocolloid), antiseptic, bandages that stick to damp skin.
- Eye rinse (mist + soot + mineral spray can irritate).
- Electrolytes (you’ll sweat subtly even when cold).
- Something for **hypothermia management**: emergency foil blanket + a dry beanie.

## Entry: finding and using the seam without making enemies
The entrance is a damp throat behind ruin-stone. Treat it like someone’s door.

1) **Listen before you enter.** If you hear tuned knocks, work-clacks, or crowded voices, you’re approaching active hours.
2) **Secure the chute.** Stone worn smooth by bodies and water is a slip hazard.
   - Tie in your cord before committing your weight.
3) **Control your first breath.** People will hear you arrive. Don’t cough if you can help it; swallow, sip water, breathe through your nose.
4) **Light discipline.** Low mode, downward angle. Let your eyes adapt.

## First-contact etiquette (this city runs on behavior)
In a place where sound carries, etiquette is architecture.

- **Speak softly by default.** Never shout across channels.
- **Don’t touch water infrastructure** unless invited:
  - no stepping over channels where there are bridges,
  - no dipping hands into basins,
  - no “just rinsing” gear.
- **Never spit.** In a closed humidity loop it’s not rude—it’s contamination.
- **Metal discretion**: if locals notice exposed iron/steel, expect alarm. Keep metal wrapped; don’t set it on wet stone.
- **Don’t “improve” anything**: moving a stone, clearing a drip, redirecting a rivulet—those are system-level changes.

### Gifts that make sense here
Bring something that matches their economy: **heat, dryness, clean containment, quiet utility**.
Good options:
- sealed **wax candles** (low smoke) if culturally acceptable,
- **ceramic needles/hooks**, cordage, waxed thread,
- **water-safe containers** with good seals,
- **salt** in a tight vial (but offer carefully—salts may be sensitive to their chemistry),
- a **small, efficient, low-smoke wick** or lamp parts.
Avoid gifting:
- raw metal,
- perfumes,
- seeds/plant matter,
- anything that flakes.

## Moving inside: how not to fall, offend, or get lost

### 1) Walk like it’s always wet (because it is)
- Keep one hand free.
- Test slick steps with the side of your boot before committing.
- Don’t lean on parapets; they’re damp and often part of channel boundaries.

### 2) Respect acoustic zones
They have “whisper wires” and “dead pockets.” Those are used for governance, intimacy, and security.
- If you notice a corner where sound drops unnaturally: **do not hold private conversations there unless invited.**
- If voices carry strangely well: assume you are being overheard even if nobody is visible.

### 3) Don’t be a humidity event
Crowds + your body heat + strong lighting can push the air toward condensation.
- Keep your lamp low.
- Remove layers before you sweat.
- If you’re in a dense area, step aside to a wider platform to cool down rather than steaming in a narrow walkway.

## Food, sleep, and staying longer than a visit

### Eating
- Eat your own rations unless offered local food.
- Keep crumbs contained—pest ecology is minimal; you don’t want to introduce a new one.

### Sleeping
If you must sleep inside:
- Choose a spot **away from active channels** and **downwind of hearth smoke**.
- Use a **sleeping pad**; stone will drain heat from you all night.
- Expect constant sound—bring earplugs, but keep one ear available if you’re not alone (situational awareness matters).

## Hazards to plan for (the real ones)

1) **Slip-and-fall injuries**: polished, wet stone + narrow walkways.
2) **Hypothermia by damp**: you can get chilled without realizing it.
3) **Respiratory irritation**: soot + humidity + biofilm aerosols near drips.
4) **Social hazard**: being seen as a contamination risk (especially via metal, waste, or water misuse).
5) **Disorientation**: “distance” is corners + echoes. You can’t rely on horizon sense.

---

## Exit strategy (leave clean, leave quiet, leave nothing)
- Pack out *everything*, including wipes and wrappers.
- Wipe down boots if you picked up biofilm slick (for your own world’s sake, too).
- Turn lights down on the way out—don’t flash the chamber.
- If you used any cord markers, retrieve them all.
