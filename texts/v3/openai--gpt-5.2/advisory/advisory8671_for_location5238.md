## Preparation guide for visiting the Folded Basin City (the wind-roar, water-forbidden terraces)

You are going to a place that behaves like a *dry instrument*: it amplifies airflow into continuous, body-level sound; it reflects light with punishing efficiency; it supports “lush” biology on mineral vapor and non-aqueous solvents; and it treats free water as a structural and biochemical hazard. Preparing well is mostly about **protecting your ears, eyes, airway, skin, and assumptions**, and about carrying the *right kinds of consumables*.

---

# 1) What to expect (so you don’t pack wrong)

### The governing conditions
- **Brightness is constant and weaponized**: glare erases depth cues and cooks exposed skin fast.
- **Humidity is near-zero**: you will lose water through breath and skin without noticing; fabrics, lips, eyes, and mucosa degrade quickly.
- **Noise is continuous and physically felt**: a resonant roar that can fatigue you, disturb sleep, and interfere with balance and cognition.
- **Wind is structured**: not “breezy,” but *channeled*, with sudden gusts and standing flows that can pull at clothing and throw dust and salt.
- **Free water is taboo and potentially destructive**: it can fizz/pit stone, blister local plants, and crack masonry by waking salts. Socially, spilling water reads like negligence with fire.

### The city is built around void
You will spend time on **exposed terraces, bridges, and ribs over an open cavity**, with lots of stairs/ramps and fewer enclosed rooms. If you have vertigo, plan for it.

---

# 2) Body prep (start before you arrive)

### Train for dryness and exertion
- **Cardio + stair endurance**: terraces and long ramps, often in heat.
- **Salt/dust breathing tolerance**: practice with a respirator during light workouts (do not overdo it). The goal is comfort with resistance, not hypoxia.
- **Heat acclimation**: short, controlled sessions in warm environments help.

### Ears and sleep resilience
Constant low-frequency roar is more exhausting than “loud music.”
- If you can, **habituate with brown noise / low-frequency noise** during sleep in the weeks before travel.
- Learn to sleep with **in-ear plugs + over-ear muffs** without pressure sores.

### Medical considerations
- If you have **dry-eye syndrome, asthma, eczema, migraines, tinnitus, sensory processing issues**, this environment will amplify them. Bring extra meds and redundancy.

---

# 3) Core gear (non-negotiable)

## A. Hearing protection (bring two layers)
The roar is not optional; you need sustained protection.
- **Primary**: high-NRR foam or molded earplugs (bring many pairs).
- **Secondary**: comfortable **over-ear defenders** (industrial earmuffs).
- **Sleep set**: soft silicone plugs + a headband-style muff to reduce jaw/ear soreness.
- **Communication aid**: a small **vibration pager** or wrist-tap protocol with your companions (see §8).

*Why two layers:* you’ll want to “dial” your exposure depending on where you are (wind niches vs central cavity).

## B. Eye and face protection
- **Wraparound glacier-grade sunglasses** (Category 4 if possible) + a backup pair.
- **Clear sealed goggles** for dust storms and high-velocity salt grit.
- **A scarf or soft jaw wrap**: locals wear mouth coverings partly to damp vibration; it also reduces mouth drying.

## C. Respiratory and mucosa protection
- **P100 or FFP3/N99 respirator** with spare filters (salt and gypsum dust are harsh).
- **Sterile saline eye drops** *for your eyes only* (do not spill broadly; dispense carefully).
- **Nasal gel** (not spray) to reduce nosebleeds.
- **Lip barrier** (lanolin or heavy wax).

## D. Skin and clothing system (abrasion + UV + desiccation)
- **Full-coverage, breathable but tight-weave clothing** (think desert technical fabric).
- **UV-rated hood or brim + neck drape**.
- **Light gloves** for gripping saltstone and rope fibers.
- **Footwear**: grippy soles + closed uppers (dust infiltration is constant). Bring blister care.

## E. Hydration strategy (your body still needs water)
This is the trick: you must hydrate **without becoming the person who spills “free water.”**
- Use **leakproof bite-valve bladders** inside a secondary sealed pouch.
- Consider **semi-solid hydration**: water bound in gels/chews (less spill risk).
- Bring **electrolytes**; you’ll lose water invisibly and cramp easily.
- Pack a **dripless dosing syringe** for mouth wetting (tiny controlled amounts).

**Do not** carry open bottles. **Do not** pour. **Do not** rinse anything in public.

## F. Light control and navigation
Glare flattens distance; the city is terraced and repetitive.
- **Polarized lens option** (in addition to high UV) helps with mineral sheen.
- **Chalk/grease pencil** for marking *your own* gear (do not mark their stone).
- **Tactile map** or simple cord-and-knot route system if you’re traveling independently.

# 4) Consumables you should *not* assume exist there

### Things that may be scarce or socially complex
- Metals, batteries, and adhesives not based on local resin.
- Soft plastics (dryness makes some polymers brittle).
- Conventional soap (washing is largely dry/oily).
- Quiet, enclosed sleeping rooms.

### Bring your own
- **Resin-compatible repair kit**: sewing kit, dental floss line, flexible UV-resistant tape, a small tube of neutral silicone sealant (keep it sealed; use sparingly).
- **Spare straps and cordage**: wind and vibration loosen everything.
- **Eye/skin barriers**: petroleum jelly or waxes last longer than lotions in this climate.

---

# 5) Chemical / biological safety (this place’s “wet” is not water)

### The plants vent solvents
The air includes resinous/metallic volatiles and occasional sweet cooling releases.
- Treat it like a place with **chronic low-level VOC exposure**.
- If you’re sensitive: bring a **respirator you can tolerate for hours**, and schedule breaks in sheltered niches.

### Do not taste-test the environment
- Mineral crusts and plant exudates can be **reactive**, caustic, or simply not metabolizable.
- “Cool mint and copper” roots may be safe for locals but not for you.

### Cross-contamination etiquette
- Keep your offworld oils/chemicals from touching their root mats and terraces.
- Brush your boots before stepping onto dense root-textiles; those mats are infrastructure.

---

# 6) Water taboo: how to behave without causing harm

### Think of water as open flame
Rules of thumb:
- **Never uncap water in wind-exposed areas.**
- **Never discard rinse water.** (Ideally: do not rinse at all.)
- If you must clean: use **dry grit + oil**, then wipe cloth and pack it out sealed.

### If you spill accidentally
- Stop moving (don’t spread it).
- Signal for help; locals likely have absorbent salts, resins, or sacrificial media.
- Offer restitution; expect social consequences similar to starting a small fire.

### Personal hygiene plan
- Pack **dry wipes**, oil cleanser, and a sealable waste bag.
- Plan for **“desert hygiene”**: wipe sweat-salt, oil lightly, protect skin folds, treat hot spots early.

---

# 7) Wind and structure hazards (falls, cords, resonance)

### Wind can move you
- Secure loose fabric; avoid capes/ponchos.
- Use **carabiner tethers** on exposed bridges if you’re not sure-footed (but don’t clip to living plant structures unless invited—some “ropes” are grown).

### Resonant fatigue is real
Continuous vibration can cause:
- Headaches, irritability, poor judgment
- Jaw pain, tooth soreness
- Balance issues

Mitigations:
- Wear the jaw wrap.
- Take scheduled “quiet-niche breaks.”
- Magnesium supplementation can help some travelers with muscle tension (test beforehand).

---

# 8) Communication: plan for gesture-first

The roar makes normal conversation unreliable.
Bring or establish:
- **Hand-sign lexicon** among your group (basic: stop/go/danger/need break/where).
- **Touch signals**: shoulder taps with meaning (one tap = attention; two = follow; long press = stop).
- **Written slate**: small notebook + grease pencil works even in dryness.
- If you have tech: **bone-conduction headsets** can work better than speakers, but dust protection is crucial.

Watch locals: they “strike the city” (taps, stomps, shutter flicks). Don’t imitate randomly—those can be messages, alarms, or ritual.

---

# 9) Finding the place and entering it safely

### Locating the seam
From the plain, it’s intentionally missable:
- Look for a **crease between salt-ridged slabs** where the sound becomes *felt* before it’s obvious.
- Don’t approach from downwind during high glare; you can walk past it because depth cues fail.

### Descent
- Assume **crumbly gypsum** and polished saltstone = slip risk.
- Gloves on; goggles ready; respirator accessible.
- Move slowly at first: the sound pressure and glare together can cause disorientation.

---

# 10) Social and practical exchange (how to be welcomed)

### Bring trade goods that won’t insult their constraints
Good:
- **Precision small metal items** (needles, fine wire, tiny springs) kept minimal and well-protected.
- **High-quality cutting edges** (ceramic blades) that don’t rust.
- **Pigments** that won’t react with salts (keep sealed).
- **Optics** (spare lenses, mirrors) can be valued in a light-managed city.
- **Quiet materials**: soft felt, dense foams—anything that can help make micro-silence in niches.

Bad:
- Open water, watery alcohol, perfumes, volatile solvents, soaps.
- Anything that sheds microplastics or flakes under UV/dryness.
- Loudness tech (speakers, fireworks equivalents). Sound is infrastructure; uncontrolled sound is rude.

### Conduct
- Walk on designated stone where possible; **root-mats are “living floors.”**
- Ask before touching wind instruments (bridges, tension cords, slats).
- Don’t demand quiet; request a *windless niche* if you need relief.

# 12) A simple arrival routine (keeps you functional)

1. **Before descent**: goggles around neck, plugs in, water sealed, check straps.
2. **At the first terrace**: switch to two-layer hearing protection until you learn the acoustics.
3. **After 30 minutes**: drink *small amounts* from a bite valve; apply lip/nostril barrier early (don’t wait).
4. **Every 2–3 hours**: take a break in a sheltered niche; remove earmuffs (keep plugs) to reduce pressure sores.
5. **Before sleep**: full skin check; oil/wax hotspots; set up wind-baffle; commit to ear protection even if it feels “not that bad” yet—fatigue accumulates.

---

# 13) Emergency planning (assume something will go wrong)

### Dust storm protocol
- Goggles on, respirator on, secure all loose items.
- Get off bridges; move to a **leeward pocket**.
- Do not open water or electronics; grit intrusion is brutal.

### Heat illness without sweat feedback
Because sweat evaporates instantly, you may not realize you’re overheating.
- Watch for headache, nausea, confusion, clumsiness.
- Stop, shade, cool the neck with *sealed* cool packs if you brought them (do not use wet cloths openly).

### If your ears start “ringing” or you feel nauseated from resonance
- Move toward a quiet niche (often indicated by sudden drop in roar near certain cut rock hollows).
- Double up hearing protection.
- Reduce caffeine and stimulants; they worsen vibration stress for many people.

---

## Final mindset
Don’t treat this as a desert with a hidden oasis. It’s a **dry, mineral-breathing conservatory** where life runs on vapor, salt creep, and controlled solvent exchange—and where architecture is half instrument, half lung.

If you arrive thinking “I must bring water and make comfort,” you’ll be dangerous. If you arrive thinking “I must bring containment, protection, and respect for the void,” you’ll do fine—and you’ll start to hear the city’s timing in the roar the way residents do: not as noise, but as weather, clock, and speech.
