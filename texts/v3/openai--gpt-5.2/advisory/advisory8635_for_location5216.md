## What you’re actually visiting
You’re not going to a “site.” You’re going into a young basin that is still settling, where **ground behaves like wet fabric** and the “city” is **woven into living structure**. The dominant risks are not dramatic ones (storms, cliffs, obvious predators) but **quiet ones**: hypothermia, infection, getting lost in ten-minute distances, stepping onto something that *looks* like ground and is actually floating rot.

Prepare like you’re going somewhere that is:
- **Cold + wet + dim** all the time  
- **Soft underfoot** (load-bearing is a skill, not a property)
- **Biologically crowded** (everything wants your warmth, salt, food scraps, or skin)
- **Architecturally alive** (cutting or blocking the wrong thing is vandalism *and* structural failure)

## Before you go (body, paperwork, and biosecurity)

### Medical prep (do this even if you’re “tough”)
- **Tetanus up to date** (non-negotiable; everything is a puncture hazard).
- **Hep A / Typhoid** (water + close quarters + shared canals = risk).
- Consider **leptospirosis precautions** (common in wet environments with mammal urine; ask your clinician about region-agnostic risk planning).
- Pack a field-ready kit for:
  - **Skin infections**: mupirocin or similar topical antibiotic; oral antibiotics only if you’re trained/approved to use them.
  - **Fungal issues** (feet/groin/skin folds): terbinafine/clotrimazole.
  - **Allergic reactions**: antihistamines; if you have any history, carry an epinephrine auto-injector.
  - **GI upset**: oral rehydration salts, loperamide (use judiciously).
  - **Wound care**: irrigating syringe, steri-strips, hydrocolloid/blister care.

### Conditioning (2–3 weeks helps)
- Train **ankle stability + balance** (you’ll be walking on shifting mats).
- Practice moving quietly with a loaded pack.
- Do a few long sessions in **cold-wet clothing management**: staying warm without ever becoming fully dry.

### Biosecurity (multiverse travelers cause problems)
You can accidentally introduce molds, insects, seeds, microbes.
- Arrive with **cleaned gear** (hot wash + dry, wipe-down hard surfaces).
- Avoid bringing natural fibers that shed seeds (hay-stuffed items, untreated burlap).
- Plan to **pack out** anything non-biodegradable, and don’t dump soaps/chemicals into canals.

## Core gear: what keeps you alive here

### Clothing (assume you will be damp 100% of the time)
Aim for **warm-when-wet** and **fast-drying**, not “waterproof everything.”

**On body**
- Base: synthetic or merino (merino stays warmer, synthetic dries faster).
- Midlayer: fleece or active insulation.
- Shell: breathable rain shell (pit zips matter; you’ll sweat even in cold).
- Legs: quick-dry softshell or synthetic hiking pants.

**Feet (most important system)**
- Two-layer strategy:
  - Thin liner sock (synthetic)
  - Warm sock (wool blend)
- Bring either:
  - **Neoprene socks** *or* a **vapor barrier liner** (VBL) system if you know how to use it.
- Footwear:
  - Lightweight boots with drainage can work, but constant peat can eat them.
  - Many do better with **trail runners + neoprene socks**, accepting wetness and focusing on warmth.
- Absolute essentials:
  - **Foot powder**, blister kit, and a daily foot check routine.
  - A way to dry feet briefly: small pack towel + “dry sleep socks” reserved only for sleeping.

**Hands**
- Thin dexterity gloves + over-mittens or waterproof shell mitts.
- Hands go numb slowly here; you won’t notice damage until you’re clumsy.

**Head/neck**
- Warm hat + buff/neck gaiter.
- Insect head net (even in cold wetlands, you’ll meet biting swarms at the wrong time).

### Pack protection (everything rots)
- Dry bags (multiple small ones > one large).
- Desiccant packets for electronics.
- Spare cord/straps; wet environments loosen everything.

### Light (you need it, but it changes your relationship with the place)
- Headlamp with:
  - **Very low “moonlight” mode**
  - **Red mode** (reduces insect interest and preserves your night vision)
- A secondary micro-light (backup).
- Avoid bright flood lighting unless it’s an emergency; it draws attention (animal and human) and ruins your ability to read the dim.

### Navigation (maps won’t help much)
This place is “not on anything.” Navigate like a caver in vegetation.
- Waterproof watch (timekeeping matters when daylight doesn’t).
- Simple compass is still useful for **consistent headings**, even in canopy.
- Marking method that does *not* become trash:
  - Reusable ties/clips you retrieve on the way out, **or**
  - Learn local marker conventions (preferred if people live here).
- A **probing staff** (trekking pole with a blunt tip) is survival gear, not trekking gear.

### Water and food
**Water**
- Expect tannins and silt. Filters clog.
- Bring:
  - A filter (hollow fiber) **plus**
  - Chemical backup (chlorine dioxide works better than iodine for many pathogens)
  - Pre-filter cloth / coffee filters
- Don’t rely on boiling alone unless you have a stove system that can function in constant damp.

**Cooking**
- Open fire is socially and practically wrong here.
- Use a contained stove:
  - Alcohol stove, solid-fuel stove, or a very reliable canister stove in a sheltered cook pouch.
- Hot drinks are morale and thermoregulation.

**Food storage**
- Anything unattended will be negotiated by the ecosystem.
- Bring line/cord and hang food **overhead** in sealed bags, even if you’re only stopping briefly.

### Sleeping (follow the local rule: never on the ground)
- Hammock system or raised cot-style solution:
  - Hammock + bug net + underquilt (or insulated pad)
- A sleeping bag that tolerates humidity:
  - Synthetic insulation is safer than down here unless you are extremely disciplined with moisture management.
- Groundsheet is not for sleeping; it’s for staging gear on platforms.

## Arrival strategy: how not to get swallowed in the first hour

### 1) Stop immediately and listen
In this basin, movement is loud *to the environment* even when it’s quiet to you.  
Before you push deeper:
- Stand still for several minutes.
- Note:
  - Where drip rhythms are regular (often indicates stable canopy/platform zones)
  - Where insects suddenly quiet (predator movement corridor)
  - Where fog thickens (low pockets, colder air, sometimes deeper water)

### 2) Assume every “dry” surface is a test
- Probe before each step when off known mats.
- Step with **flat feet** and distribute weight.
- Keep knees soft; sudden weight shifts break floating structure.

### 3) Pick a “home tether” early
Choose one reliable landmark near entry:
- A distinctive scar of pale clay
- A line of marker-stalks
- A particular knot pattern in trained reeds

In a place with no skyline, you need a psychologically reliable “north.”

## Movement: the etiquette that is also physics

### Don’t fight the basin’s logic
- Follow vine lines and reed rails like you’d follow a hallway.
- Straight lines are expensive here; they cost you time, noise, and stability.

### Three rules that prevent most accidents
1. **One hand free** whenever possible (for grabbing stems/rails).  
2. **Don’t jump** between mats. Step, test, commit.  
3. **Don’t step where you can’t see the surface texture.** Fog hides holes; leaf-shadow hides water.

### If you break through
- Don’t thrash downward. You’re not “sinking,” you’re deforming a mat.
- Lean back, spread weight, get your chest onto the nearest stable vegetation.
- Roll onto a known mat rather than trying to stand immediately.

## Dealing with fauna (you are made of warm salt and soft skin)

### Insects
- Treat clothing with permethrin beforehand if your ethics/biology-security rules allow it; otherwise rely on physical barriers.
- Use head net at dusk-equivalent periods (you’ll learn them by insect behavior, not sun).
- Don’t swat wildly—swatting breaks calm and can tear load-bearing stems.

### Leeches and aquatic hitchhikers
- Expect them. Build removal into routine.
- Removal:
  - Don’t yank. Use salt, heat, or a careful slide with a flat tool.
- Aftercare:
  - Clean bite sites; monitor for infection (wet environments make “minor” become “serious”).

### Larger animals
They’re ghosts until they’re close. Your best defense is:
- Move predictably.
- Keep food sealed.
- Don’t corner anything on a narrow mat corridor.

## Thermal management: how people get hypothermic without noticing
Cold here is *quiet*. You won’t feel “freezing”; you’ll feel slowly worse at tasks.

**Daily minimum routine**
- Change into dedicated dry(ish) sleep layers every night.
- Hot drink before sleep if possible.
- Ventilate just enough to reduce condensation inside your shelter; a wet sleeping bag is a countdown.

**Warning signs**
- Clumsiness with knots
- “Mild” confusion or irrational irritation
- Stopping to rest too often because everything feels heavy

When that starts: stop, insulate, sugar + warmth, and reduce exposure.

## Social preparation: how to enter a woven settlement without becoming a problem
If you meet inhabitants, treat the place like a shared vessel, not a wilderness.

### First contact behavior
- Approach slowly on established mats.
- Keep your light low and angled down.
- Don’t reach out and grab supports casually; in their architecture, “handhold” may be “roof tension member.”

### Learn and follow local constraints (they’re survival laws)
- Never cut vines or reeds without explicit permission.
- Never dam or block seep lines.
- Never sleep on ground level inside their territory.
- Keep voices low; use hand signals when possible.

### Gifts/barter that actually matter here
Small, durable, repairable items:
- Needles, strong thread, fishing hooks
- Salt (if they don’t have easy access)
- Durable cordage
- A tiny amount of metal stock (sheet fragments, wire)
- Medical supplies in small sealed units (bandages, antiseptic wipes)

Avoid gifting things that create dependence or waste (disposable batteries, plastics they can’t repair/reuse).

## Timekeeping and sanity in perpetual dusk
This basin erodes your sense of duration. Plan for it.

- Use watch alarms as “bells” for:
  - hydration
  - foot checks
  - food hangs
  - light discipline (to keep your night vision and reduce attention)
- Keep a simple log: *entered canal X, passed three marker-stalks, reached clay scar, turned east*.
- If you’re solo, narrate actions quietly to yourself; it reduces dumb mistakes in low stimulus environments.

## Emergency planning (because extraction is harder than entry)

### Signaling
Sound is absorbed; shouting is pointless.
- Bring a whistle, but don’t expect miracles.
- Better: a **sharp clicking device** or wooden clapper—short, repeated patterns can cut through dampened acoustics better than voice.
- Light signals work, but attract insects and attention; keep them directional.

### Injury contingencies
- A sprained ankle here can become immobilizing fast.
- Bring:
  - Elastic wrap
  - Pain control you can function on
  - The ability to make a simple splint from local materials (practice beforehand)

### Exit discipline
- Reserve energy for leaving. The basin is easy to enter and hard to depart when tired.
- Retrieve your markers (don’t salt the place with foreign material).
- Decontam gear on the way out (mud carries life).

## The one mindset that keeps you safe
Treat the basin as **a living building**. Floors are plants. Walls are tension. Roofs are leaves. Plumbing is seepage. Wildlife is both pest and maintenance crew.

If you move like a guest in someone’s damp, breathing house—slow hands, tested steps, minimal light, everything hung up and sealed—you’ll last long enough to learn its routes. If you move like you’re “outdoors,” it will politely, quietly, and continuously take pieces of you: heat, footing, tools, certainty.
