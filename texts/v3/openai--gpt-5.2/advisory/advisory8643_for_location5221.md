## Working assumption about this place
You’re visiting a **high-altitude basin city** engineered around **fog/condensation water capture** and terrace agriculture, with a pervasive anomaly: **local systems strongly resist change**. Weather, moisture, growth, wear, and even “messy decay” behave like they’re being continuously corrected back to a stable pattern. Things still *happen*, but the *net state* tends to snap back into a fixed “daily song.”

Prepare as if you’re entering:
- a **thin-air, cold-sun highland** environment,
- a **resource-precise, low-waste preindustrial city**,
- and a **pattern-sensitive zone** where disturbances may persist or recur.

## 1) Before you go: medical + physical preparation

### Altitude readiness (primary risk for ordinary humans)
Even if you’re fit, the first danger is altitude illness.

**Do in advance (1–3 weeks):**
- If possible, **acclimatize** in steps: spend a few nights at moderate altitude before arrival.
- Consider a consult for **acetazolamide** (if you can safely take it), and bring it with clear dosing instructions.
- Train with light cardio, but don’t over-index on fitness—altitude is not a willpower contest.

**Bring and use:**
- A **pulse oximeter** (cheap, small, extremely useful).
- A written “if-then” plan:
  - If persistent headache + nausea + poor sleep + low SpO₂ → **rest, hydrate, stop ascending**.
  - If confusion, ataxia, severe breathlessness at rest → **descend immediately** (or exit the world).

**On arrival rules:**
- First 24–48 hours: move slowly, eat lightly, sleep more, **no sprinting up terraces**.

### Cold, dryness, and “wet that isn’t rain”
You’ll face: cool air, high UV, low humidity—*plus* constant small-contact damp (mist beads, weeping stone, wet moss).

Prepare for:
- **Dehydration** (dry air) despite feeling “damp.”
- **Chill** from intermittent wetness + wind.
- **Sunburn** (highland sun, clean shadows).

## 2) Clothing and personal kit (optimized for this specific city)

### Clothing system
- **Base layer:** moisture-wicking (merino or synthetic). Avoid cotton (it will stay cold once mist-wet).
- **Mid layer:** fleece/light puffy.
- **Shell:** windproof jacket; waterproofing matters less than wind resistance and quick-dry.
- **Pants:** durable, quick-dry; you’ll brush mossy stone constantly.
- **Footwear:** grippy approach shoes/boots with a quiet sole. (Noise matters culturally and practically.)
- **Socks:** bring extras; expect damp cuffs from mist and weeping walls.

### Must-have small items
- **Sunscreen + lip balm (SPF)** and **sunglasses**.
- **Light gloves** (cold stone + wet moss + altitude).
- **Headlamp** (no electric glow assumed; interior corridors and stairwells can be dim).
- **Microfiber cloth** (you’ll be wiping lenses, hands, and condensation constantly).
- **Earplugs** (not for loudness—rather for sleep if you end up in a corridor where one repeating sound “locks in.”)

### Water and food
Even if cisterns look pristine, don’t assume your biology matches local microbes.

- **Filter + purification backup** (filter + tablets).
- **Electrolytes** (altitude + dry air).
- **Calorie-dense snacks** you can eat when appetite drops: nuts, bars, dried fruit.

## 3) Gifts, trade, and what *not* to bring

### Understand local value
Metal is present but scarce and repaired endlessly. The city is engineered; they’ll value **reliability** and **materials that don’t fail**.

Good trade items:
- **High-quality steel needles/awls/fishhooks**, small knife blanks, sharpening stones.
- **Pitch/resin**, waterproofing compounds, durable cordage.
- **Salt**, if it’s not locally abundant.
- **Antibiotic ointment / sterile bandages** (if you can ethically share; more on this below).

Things to avoid:
- **Single-use plastics** and anything you’d be tempted to discard. Waste doesn’t “fade out” here the way you expect.
- **Anything loud or mechanically complex** that will break and become a permanent problem.
- **Firearms / explosive tools**: fire and irreversible damage are treated as near-sacrilege for a reason.

## 4) Behavioral etiquette: how to not become the daily disturbance

This city’s social norms are tuned to a world where:
- stains don’t fade,
- damage doesn’t “weather away,”
- and patterns repeat.

Act like you’re in a museum where the exhibits are also load-bearing infrastructure.

### Sound discipline
- Keep your voice low; avoid shouting across terraces.
- Secure dangling gear. A single clatter in a stone corridor may become “that sound that happens every day” if the anomaly reinforces it.
- Choose soft footwear; don’t drag pack buckles along walls.

### Fire discipline (extremely important)
- Don’t light open flame without explicit local permission and a controlled hearth.
- Don’t cook with soot-producing fuels near stonework.
- Treat smoke, char, and scorch marks as effectively permanent.

### Water discipline
- Don’t contaminate cisterns or channels (soap, food scraps, bodily fluids).
- Don’t “improve” their waterworks. Their moisture system is tuned; small changes may cascade or be socially unacceptable.

### Waste discipline
- Pack out everything you bring unless locals tell you exactly how they handle refuse.
- If you must dispose of something, do it **their way**, in their designated place—this is a “nothing truly disappears” culture.

## 5) Navigation and movement in the terraced stone city

### Your body will misjudge distances
Altitude + thin air + stairs cut into bedrock = deceptively taxing travel. Plan by **vertical gain**, not map distance.

Practical approach:
- Break movement into short segments between rest points (basins, public steps, gardens).
- Always know where the nearest **flat terrace** is in case you get dizzy.

### Respect edges
Ecology thrives on boundaries (wet/dry seams, garden/stone, canal margins). Don’t trample:
- moss seams along aqueducts,
- fog-catch screens,
- terrace lips (they’re structural).

Bring:
- A small **walking stick** (also a social signal of careful movement).
- A **basic topographic compass** even if you have other navigation—electronics may be unwanted or unreliable, and the city’s geometry can create confusing sightlines.

## 6) Biological hazards: animals, insects, and the unseen predator

### Grazers and birds
They’re integrated into city life. Don’t startle them on stairs—an alarmed animal on stone terraces becomes a chain reaction.

### Insects
“Shimmering congregations” near waterworks suggest:
- potential biting/irritant species,
- or simply dense swarms in still air pockets.

Bring:
- A **non-perfumed insect repellent** (perfumes may attract attention or be culturally jarring).
- **Antihistamines** for bites or plant reactions.

### Predators (the “effect before the sight” problem)
You may never see it, but you can become the odd prey-shaped variable in an otherwise stable web.

Do:
- Travel with a local guide if possible.
- Avoid walking alone at dawn/dusk near green pockets and canal seams.
- Don’t follow “too-clean” tracks into mossy corridors or basins.

## 7) The anomaly: how to think and plan in a world that resists change

### Expect “equilibrium enforcement”
Common traveler mistakes here:
- Assuming you can “wait for it to dry out” or “let the weather pass.”
- Assuming damage will slowly become less noticeable.
- Assuming you can create a new routine without consequences.

Operate with these rules:
1. **Small actions may become permanent features.**  
   A stain, a scratch, a dropped object—treat them as long-lived.
2. **Time may be locally cyclic even if you aren’t.**  
   Keep a log with timestamps; verify whether *you* are looping or only the environment is.
3. **Don’t try to prove the anomaly** by doing something harmful “just to see.”  
   If the place “holds” changes in a stable way, your experiment may become a lasting scar.

### Personal note-keeping
- Carry a pocket notebook and write down:
  - sleep quality,
  - symptoms,
  - weather observed,
  - any repeating events with precise location.
This helps you stay oriented if days feel identical.

### Digital devices
If you bring them:
- Keep them off in public unless you’re certain it won’t violate norms.
- Don’t rely on them for survival functions you can’t replace (charging may be impossible, and breaking something may create a permanent social problem).

## 8) Lodging, food, and social integration

### Lodging
Choose a place:
- away from main stair arteries (less footfall noise),
- near but not directly adjacent to water channels (humidity pockets can chill you),
- with good ventilation (indoor condensation can happen).

Bring a **sleeping liner**—cleanliness standards may be strict, and you don’t want to introduce pests.

### Food expectations
Likely diet:
- terrace-grown greens and shrubs,
- preserved items (dry air + stable conditions favor curing),
- dairy or meat from goatlike grazers,
- minimal waste cooking.

Be polite about portions: in a low-waste culture, leaving food is discourteous.

### Communication
If you don’t share language:
- Bring a **small sketchbook**. This city is geometry, terraces, water paths—drawings will communicate intent better than pantomime.
- Learn a few gestures: thanks, permission to pass, water request, apology.

## 9) Safety, first aid, and when to leave

### First aid tailored to this environment
- Blister care (stone stairs + long descents).
- Elastic wrap for ankle/knee (uneven rock).
- Wound cleaning: minor cuts on raw stone can become serious.
- Heat is less the issue; **hypothermia from damp + wind** is more plausible than people expect.

### Red lines (leave immediately)
- Signs of severe altitude illness (ataxia, confusion, breathlessness at rest).
- You notice **your own behavior repeating** without choosing it (possible personal-loop involvement).
- Locals become unusually concerned about your presence near waterworks or fog collectors—take it seriously; you may be about to violate a “pattern boundary.”

### Exit strategy
Plan an exit that does not depend on:
- a single path (terraces can be maze-like),
- a single person (guides can be unavailable),
- or a single time window (if time behaves oddly).

Have:
- a pre-committed “I leave if X happens” checklist,
- your gear always packable in minutes,
- and a way to signal your departure without creating drama.
