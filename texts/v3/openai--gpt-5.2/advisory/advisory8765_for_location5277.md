## Preparation guide for visiting the High Table (the thinning plateau with patient cities)

This is not a “hostile” world in the usual way—there’s no obvious predation, no swarming pests, no constant storms. The danger is subtler: altitude, cold-damp exposure, navigation that can stop meaning what you think it means, and a built environment that behaves like infrastructure for a kind of life you may not recognize.

Think of it as a place where **biology is simple, materials are honest, and geometry is negotiable**.

---

# 1) Before you go: decide what kind of trip this is

### If you are doing a short visit (hours to 2 days)
Your main risks are:
- **hypoxia** (high elevation),
- **getting “turned around”** by elastic distance,
- **cold-damp** (hands/feet especially),
- **psychological load** from silence and absence.

Plan to *not* rely on a map, and to return by the same “logic” you used to arrive (same weather window, same landmarks, same route discipline).

### If you are doing an extended stay (3+ days)
Add:
- **fungal/spore exposure**, skin maceration, trench-foot style problems,
- **looping routes** that waste calories and time,
- **slow dehydration** (air can feel dry even when surfaces are wet),
- **loss of schedule** (because water and weather don’t keep your timing).

Extended stays should be treated like a high-altitude expedition *plus* a mild reality-anomaly environment.

---

# 2) Body prep: altitude, cold, and quiet

### Acclimatization (seriously)
The plateau reads “above the weather” and behaves like it: you can arrive already compromised.

- If you can, spend **24–72 hours** in a moderately high world beforehand.
- Consider **acetazolamide** (if you normally tolerate it) starting the day before.
- Bring a **pulse oximeter** and know your normal baseline.
- Have a hard rule: if you develop **ataxia, confusion, persistent headache + nausea**, you descend/exit. Don’t negotiate with hypoxia in a place where distance may stretch.

### Cold here is “in the material”
You won’t always feel a dramatic drop in air temperature, but stone and wind will keep draining you.

- Pack for **cold-soaked contact**: gloves that stay warm when damp, insulated kneepads if you’ll be examining canals/terraces, and a sit-pad.
- Expect your fingers to lose fine dexterity during fog tides.

### The silence can change your judgment
No birds, no insects, no animal punctuation means your brain starts generating pattern and “meaning” aggressively.

- If you travel with a partner, establish **check-in prompts** (“Describe the last 5 minutes of terrain without metaphor.”)
- If solo, keep a **spoken log** (even whispered) to maintain linear time and reduce dissociation.

# 3) Clothing & personal kit (optimized for cold-damp and spore-rich air)

### Footwear: you are walking on living sponge and slick stone
- Waterproof-breathable boots are not enough; bring **gaiters** and **two systems**:
  - a “dry” sock system for sleep,
  - a “travel” sock system that you accept will get damp.
- Add **wide-footing traction**: the moss mats and canal stones will be slick with condensation.
- Bring foot care: **zinc oxide tape, antifungal cream, drying powder**, and a small towel you keep sacredly dry.

### Respiratory protection (non-negotiable on long stays)
Spores are everywhere even if you don’t see them.

- Pack an **N95/P100-style mask** for:
  - fog-heavy days,
  - canal-grid districts,
  - any time you’re disturbing mats, silt, or old woven screens.
- Bring saline rinse / eye drops if you’re sensitive.

### Layers
- Base: moisture-wicking (avoid cotton).
- Mid: fleece/wool.
- Outer: wind shell + waterproof layer.
- Hands: a **dexterity pair** + a **warm pair**.
- Head: windproof hat + buff.

---

# 4) Shelter & heat: treat damp as the primary enemy

The plateau’s damp comes sideways and from below. You need insulation from *ground cold* more than you need stormproofing.

- Shelter: a **low-profile tent/bivy** that can handle wind and persistent condensation.
- Sleep system:
  - high R-value sleeping pad (consider **two pads**: foam + inflatable),
  - sleeping bag rated colder than you think you need (humidity reduces perceived warmth),
  - vapor barrier liner if you know how to use one.
- Heat: bring a **reliable stove** plus redundancy (firewood is not guaranteed, and “plants” here are often structure/ecology, not fuel).
- Drying: a small **clothesline + clips**. In the cities, sheltered windbreak lattices are tempting—don’t assume you’re welcome to use them.

---

# 5) Water & food: water exists, but not on your terms

### Water handling
Water appears/disappears and can sit in thin sheets under vegetation.

- Bring **two methods**:
  - filtration for silt/microbes,
  - chemical treatment as backup.
- Carry **extra capacity**. The mistake here is assuming you can “top up later.”
- Don’t drink from basins that “rise on schedule” without treating; those cycles often correlate with heavy microbial life.

### Food
There’s lush biomass but uncertain edibility.

- Bring calorie-dense rations for your full planned time plus at least **50% contingency**.
- If you experiment with local moss/fungal products, do it like a field biologist:
  - tiny quantities,
  - one variable at a time,
  - keep antihistamines available,
  - and never do first trials when you’re already cold/hypoxic.

# 6) Navigation in elastic distance (how not to lose days walking a mile)

Assume that:
- straight lines can “agree” to curve,
- following a canal can return you without turning,
- towers can be nearer/farther than they look.

### The core rule: navigate by *relations*, not by absolute distance
Use triangulation and state changes rather than “we walked east for 2 km.”

**Bring:**
- a physical notebook + pencil (not just digital),
- survey tape or fiber cord (biodegradable if possible),
- a compass/GPS *but don’t trust them as primary*,
- an altimeter can be useful, but expect occasional nonsense.

### Establish anchors immediately on arrival
Pick a “home anchor” that is hard to misidentify:
- an unusual basalt exposure,
- a specific tower with a distinctive ring pattern,
- an escarpment lip with a unique lichen gradient.

At the anchor:
- record time, weather, fog presence, wind direction,
- take photos in all directions,
- leave a minimal marker *that doesn’t damage moss* (e.g., a small stacked stone on bare basalt, not in vegetation).

### Use “edge discipline”
Edges are powerful; treat them like charged surfaces.

- When you must pass corners, tower rims, terrace lips: **pause, observe, then cross cleanly**.
- Avoid loitering at edges when fatigued or emotionally “thin.” That’s when people report the most route weirdness in edge-active worlds.

### Test for a “trustworthy day”
Before committing to a long traverse, run a 20–40 minute loop near your anchor and check:

- Do echoes return normally?
- Do your own footsteps sound delayed/advanced?
- Do you arrive back exactly when expected?

If the place feels “misaligned,” stay local, shelter, do short forays, and don’t try to force a cross-plateau march.

### Don’t breadcrumb with sound
Sound is unreliable here. Whistles, claps, and call-and-response can mislead you. Use **visual and tactile** systems.

# 7) Moving through the cities: etiquette and survival

These settlements feel “unoccupied” but maintained. Treat them like someone’s workshop.

### Assumptions that keep you safe
- You are likely being *noticed* even if you don’t see anyone.
- Maintenance is a language. Damage is a broadcast.

### Do not:
- cut windbreak lattices for material,
- peel moss mats, pry lichens, or “sample” structural vegetation from terraces,
- move stacked stones from walls/causeways,
- sleep inside towers (they “drink” mist; they are condensation engines and edge-magnets).

### Do:
- walk on causeways and established paths when possible,
- leave gates/screens exactly as you found them,
- if you must make camp in a district, choose **bare stone** over living mat,
- if you take water from a canal grid, do it downstream and leave no contamination (soap/food waste away from waterways).

### Gifts/trade items that fit the world
Metal is rare and therefore socially loud. Offer small, high-value, non-invasive items:
- durable cordage,
- needles, simple fasteners,
- salt,
- waterproof fabric patches,
- clean cutting tools *only if you’re prepared for the social weight of that gift*.

Avoid gifting anything that could be interpreted as an attempt to “upgrade” the system (complex electronics, bright lights, loud devices).

# 8) Reality-safety practices (practical, not mystical)

You need habits that keep your timeline and identity coherent when the environment is permissive about bending.

### Keep a “continuity kit”
- a physical token you always carry (coin, stone, ring),
- a written card with:
  - your name,
  - origin coordinates,
  - mission intent,
  - exit method,
  - emergency instructions to yourself.

When you feel turned around, you use the card to reassert the sequence: *who/where/why/what next*.

### Maintain strict, simple routines
- Eat on schedule even if you don’t feel hungry.
- Log every route change.
- Don’t change more than one variable at a time (route + weather + fatigue is how people vanish into “it didn’t take long” stories).

### Avoid brinkmanship with paradox
If you notice:
- sound answering early,
- water running “wrong,”
- a familiar structure presenting as unfamiliar,
then you stop, return to last known stable point, and reassess. Curiosity is expensive at altitude.

---

# 9) Hazards checklist (what actually gets travelers)

### Medical
- Altitude sickness (fast onset if you portal in)
- Hypothermia from condensation + wind
- Foot maceration/trench foot
- Respiratory irritation from spores/silt
- Eye irritation (fog + fine particulates)

### Environmental
- Hidden water lenses under vegetation (sudden cold soaking)
- Slick canal stone, especially when “empty” but condensed
- Exhaustion from distance elasticity (you plan 3 hours, you spend 9)

### Cognitive
- Overconfidence because it looks gentle
- Decision fatigue from silence
- “One more landmark” thinking (classic way to get caught out when geometry stretches)

---

# 10) A good arrival plan (template)

1. Arrive with **daylight** and at least 6 hours before your intended sleep.
2. Identify a **primary anchor** + two secondary anchors.
3. Do a **short calibration loop** and return to anchor.
4. Make camp early, dry feet, eat, log.
5. Next day: travel only between anchors you can verify visually; avoid tower rims and long canal-following until you’ve seen how the day behaves.

---

# 11) Emergency exit strategy

Because the world can “fold” routes, your exit plan must not depend on a single path.

- Have **two independent exit triggers**:
  1) location-based (return to anchor),
  2) condition-based (oxygen saturation below X for Y minutes; or temperature/wetness thresholds; or time elapsed).
- If you can deploy a multiverse beacon, place it on **bare basalt** near your anchor, not on moss, not on a tower lip, not inside a canal district.
- If you suspect you’re looping: stop moving, shelter, warm up, and reattempt exit during a different weather state (fog tide vs clear, dawn vs midday). In many elastic-distance environments, *phase of day* matters more than direction.

## What it will feel like (so you’re not surprised)

- Your senses will insist the land is simple; your itinerary will discover it isn’t.
- The cities will feel like they’re waiting without waiting.
- You’ll start respecting corners, lips, rims, and thresholds the way sailors respect shoals.
- You may come to believe the maintenance is the civilization—whether or not bodies are present.
