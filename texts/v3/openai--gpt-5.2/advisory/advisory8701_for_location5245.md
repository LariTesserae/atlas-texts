## What you’re walking into (and why it’s not “just a lake”)

This is an **underwater city in a high-altitude basin**: cold, clear, bright-shafted light, lots of open “streets” between living structures, and a **root-knit rim** that keeps the water from draining. It’s inhabited—by animals for sure, and very likely by **people/keepers** whose engineering is horticulture and whose “public sound” is current-driven instruments.

For an ordinary human, the trip is mostly about: **(1) breathing, (2) cold, (3) altitude + diving physiology, (4) entanglement / navigation in living architecture, (5) not being mistaken for a threat or a vandal.**

---

## 1) The non‑negotiables: breathing + altitude + decompression

### Breathing options (pick one; don’t improvise on site)
1. **Closed-circuit rebreather (CCR)** with redundant bailout (best if you’re trained).
2. **Open-circuit scuba** (simpler, louder, more disruptive, heavier logistics).
3. **Local/para-tech adaptation** (temporary gills / oxygenation charm / symbiont)—only if you’ve used it before in another world and trust it.

**Do not** rely on “I’ll just surface to breathe.” The surface is high altitude (thin air, cold wind), and much of what you’ll want to see is in chambers, tunnels, under canopies, and along terraces where surfacing may be awkward or socially unsafe.

### Altitude + diving: treat this as a high-risk profile
A “sea at altitude” creates a classic trap:

- At high elevation, **your decompression limits get worse** even at modest depths.
- If you arrived from a lower elevation quickly (portal drop-in), you’re effectively doing **altitude exposure + a dive** back-to-back.
- If you have to climb out over the rim or hike after diving, you stack more risk.

**Preparation rule of thumb:**
- **Acclimatize** if possible: spend **12–24 hours** at the basin altitude *without diving* (even if you feel fine).
- Use **very conservative depth/time** plans (assume altitude-adjusted tables; if you don’t have them, treat any meaningful depth as requiring staged caution).
- Bring a way to deliver **100% oxygen** at the surface in an emergency (portable O₂ kit).
- Have a **clear no-questions-asked abort trigger**: headache, unusual fatigue, tingling, joint pain, confusion, chest tightness, or “I just feel wrong.”

If you are not trained in diving physiology, do not make this your first “real dive.” Hire a competent diver-guide or choose a breathing adaptation that avoids inert gas loading.

## 2) Cold management: the cold here is structural

This place reads like **glacial reservoir cold**: the kind that gets into joints and decision-making. Plan for cold even if the light looks “near-sun bright.”

### Wear
- **Drysuit** (preferred) with thick undergarments; or a serious wetsuit if you must.
- **Two-layer gloves** (dexterity matters for lashings/lines; cold hands lead to panic mistakes).
- **Hood** that seals well—most heat loss is head/neck.
- **Chemical heat packs** for surface intervals (not for underwater use unless rated safe).

### Plan around warm seams
There are **spring-warm “hearth” zones**. They are tempting, but also:
- likely **socially important** (like communal fires),
- ecologically delicate (animals gather there),
- and possibly hazardous (mineral flux, low oxygen pockets under canopies).

Use them for brief recovery, not as your base camp unless invited.

## 3) Mobility in a city that is alive (and grabs)

You’re moving through **rootmats, kelp cables, bladder-canopies, and lashings** designed to flex. That means snagging risks are real and constant.

### Carry (and practice using)
- **Two cutting tools**: one line cutter + one small knife or shears.
- **Reel + guideline spool** (for tunnels and big chambers).
- **Low-profile kit**: minimize dangling straps, hoses, accessories.
- **Buoyancy control you can manage precisely** (you want to hover in “streets” without finning into curtains).

### Movement style
- Move **slow and vertical-aware**: ceilings may be bladdered mats; floors may be soft living substrate.
- Use **frog kick / modified flutter** to avoid silting mineral blooms and spooking fauna.
- Treat every curtain of fine-leaf plants like a doorway with tripwires—**probe gently** before pushing through.

## 4) Navigation: it’s legible, but disorienting

It’s “roomlike,” bounded, with open lanes—but underwater cities mess with the brain. The music doesn’t help; you’ll think sounds are “over there” when they’re “everywhere.”

### Bring
- **Underwater compass** (simple, reliable).
- **Writable slate** with a sketch map as you go.
- **Light** even in clear water: one primary + one backup. Shafts of light create harsh contrast; inside chambers it’ll drop fast.
- **Marker protocol**: biodegradable ribbon is tempting but disrespectful. Use **removable clips** on *your own guideline* only.

### Landmarks that will actually work
- Terrace steps along basin wall (consistent contour)
- Causeway lines of basalt blocks (straight geometry)
- Columned “avenues”
- Tunnel mouths (dark ovals; treat as decision points)

## 5) Sound etiquette: you are entering a musical civic space

Those shell racks and bone tubes are not décor; they’re **public instruments** played by currents. Loud bubble exhaust, banging tanks, prop-wash, and careless finning can read as vandalism or aggression.

### If you can
- Prefer **rebreather** or a quiet breathing adaptation.
- Stow hard gear so it doesn’t clack.
- Don’t touch or “test” instruments.
- In big resonant chambers, **slow down**—your movement changes local flow and can change the sound.

If you must use open-circuit scuba, treat it like entering a cathedral with a leaf blower: keep your visit short, purposeful, and away from ceremonial spaces.

## 6) Biological and environmental hazards (most are subtle)

### Fauna
- Long-finned predators patrol edges. They sound like they’re part of the city’s normal ecology, but you’re an odd-shaped animal with shiny gear.
  - Avoid thrashing, avoid spearfishing, don’t corner yourself near terrace drops.
- Docile filter-feeders tethered to stakes: **do not disturb**. Those are livestock/infrastructure.
- Large snails “sweeping” algae: also likely managed.

### Water quality
High basin + living dam implies **limited turnover**. Expect microzones:
- pockets of low oxygen under dense mats,
- mineral seep zones (skin/eye irritation),
- algal bloom patches (reduced visibility, clogging).

Bring:
- **Mask defog + spare mask**, because you’ll be tempted to clear often in cold water.
- **A small water sampler strip** (pH/irritant check) if you have it.
- **Eye rinse** for after.

### Disease / biosecurity
A multiverse traveler is the worst invasive species.
- Decontaminate gear before entry (bleach solutions are effective but ecologically harsh—use controlled methods and rinse *away* from the basin if possible).
- Avoid introducing oils, fuels, soaps.
- Do not bleed in the water if you can help it; cover cuts with waterproof dressings.

## 7) Light, visibility, and psychological effects

The light is *clean and high*, with **white shafts** and big open volumes. That combination can cause:
- time dilation feelings (“I’ve been here ten minutes”—it’s been an hour),
- vertigo in open lanes,
- tunnel fixation when you see a dark mouth at the end of a bright avenue.

Countermeasures:
- Set **timers** (turnaround time, warmth check, gas check).
- Make every transition (open lane → tangle-building → chamber) a deliberate pause point: stop, breathe, check.

## 8) What to bring that will actually matter (and what not to)

### Core kit (minimum sensible loadout)
- Breathing system + redundancy
- Drysuit + hood + gloves
- Primary + backup light
- Compass + slate
- Reel/line + 1–2 spools
- Two cutting tools
- Small first aid + O₂ on surface
- Thermal shelter for surface (bivy + windproof layer)

### Food & heat reality
There is no easy fire. Bring:
- **Ready-to-eat high-calorie rations** you can eat cold.
- Warm drink in a thermos (surface morale saver).
- If you must cook, use a sealed chemical heater; do not expect local fire.

### Trade / gifts (if you expect contact)
Offer things that fit their world:
- **Charcoal**, soot pigment, or stable dyes (fire is “a story” here; these may be precious).
- High-quality **fiber cordage** (even if they lash expertly, good fiber is always valuable).
- Small quantities of **bronze/copper** in usable form, not flashy.
- Fine **needles, awls, cutting edges** (simple tools with long lifetimes).
Avoid:
- Batteries you can’t replace,
- Plastics that shed,
- Anything that implies you expect ownership (flags, stakes, permanent markers).

## 9) Social approach: you’re a diver in someone’s living levee

The city is built *with* the basin’s seal. Maintenance of the root-knit rim is likely sacred civic work. Assume:
- Certain corridors are “kept” (pruned, trained, protected).
- Warm spring seams may be communal.
- Instrument chambers may be ritual or civic messaging (the current “plays announcements”).

### Conduct
- Keep hands off lashings, tensioned kelp cables, and canopy seams.
- If you must grab something for stability, choose **bare rock**, not living braid.
- Don’t swim through a curtain like it’s a beaded doorway in a shop—pause, look for a clear lane.
- If approached by locals: stop moving, reduce noise, open palms, let them circle and assess.

If you have a translator, tune it for **low speech underwater**; otherwise plan for gestures and patience.

## 10) Entry, exit, and contingency planning (assume you might need to leave fast)

### Identify before you go in
- **Two exits**: your entry point and a secondary route (even if longer).
- A **surface rendezvous** spot that is sheltered from wind.
- A “lost diver” protocol: if separated, meet at a specific landmark (e.g., the basalt causeway midpoint or the largest chime chamber threshold—*outside* it, not inside).

### Emergency scenarios to rehearse mentally
1. **Entanglement in rootmat**  
   Stop, don’t fight. Inflate slightly, create slack, cut only what you must, and avoid cutting load-bearing lashings (they may snap back).
2. **Sudden cold incapacitation**  
   Abort early. Cold errors cascade.
3. **DCS / altitude sickness overlap**  
   Oxygen, warmth, hydration, no further ascent if possible, and seek medical-grade help via your multiverse network immediately.
4. **Aggressive animal interest**  
   Stay vertical, make yourself “boring,” back toward structure, avoid flashing lights directly at eyes.

## 11) Mindset adjustment: the city isn’t ruined—it’s maintained

If you go in thinking “flooded ruins,” you’ll touch things, pull on things, pry at things. Here, **compliance is the engineering**—a lashing you tug might be holding a whole terrace’s shape. A canopy you duck under might be managing flow, sediment, and oxygen for an entire courtyard.

Treat every “plant wall” as:
- architecture,
- agriculture,
- and civic infrastructure all at once.

Move like you’re in someone’s greenhouse, someone’s cathedral, and someone’s dam—simultaneously.
