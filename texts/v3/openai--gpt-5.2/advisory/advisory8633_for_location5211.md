## What you’re walking into (in human terms)

This basin is **cold, wet, electrically “alive,” and narratively slippery**.

- **Cold + saturation**: You’ll be wet almost immediately (fog, beading, sheet-water). Hypothermia and trench foot are the default failure modes.
- **Unstable ground**: Thin ice skins, pudding-soft clays, sudden subsidence during “Drain Shift.”
- **Embedded electricity**: Natural battery flats, piezo seams, small arcs. Not a lightning show—more like constant low-grade hazard and interference.
- **Animals as infrastructure**: Herds literally make safe crossings; predators exploit the places where distance and footing lie.
- **Reality edits**: Distances can “skip,” reflections can lead reality by a beat, and repetition makes routes easier (the place reinforces patterns). The fog is not just obscuring; it’s selectively revealing.
- **No plants**: No familiar forage, no wood, no dry kindling, no easy “natural” shelter materials.

Preparation is mostly about **staying warm/dry, not getting electrically or biologically messed up, and navigating without trusting your senses**.

## Pre-departure: training and mindset (do this before gear)

### 1) Condition your “I am lost” response
You will experience:
- Walking toward something that never grows larger until you “arrive” abruptly.
- Seeing a pool show a sky that doesn’t match overhead.
- Hearing calls twice (air, then “printed” off wet stone).

**Practice a rule**: *When the world disagrees with itself, you slow down and default to instruments + buddy confirmation.*  
Do not “push through” confusion; that’s how this place feeds predators and accidents.

### 2) Travel as a two-person minimum (three is better)
Solo travel is a bad idea here because:
- The fog edits lines-of-sight.
- Ground collapse can swallow a person quietly.
- “Distance deletion” makes it easy to lose your own track.

Pick roles:
- **Lead** probes footing and watches wildlife.
- **Navigator** runs instruments, logs time/direction/events.
- **Rear** watches for separation, marks, and changing ground.

### 3) Adopt a “non-interaction” protocol with the basin’s feedback
The basin “remembers repetitions more than facts.” That’s useful, but it can also trap you in loops.

Before entry, agree:
- You will **not** mimic calls.
- You will **not** repeatedly stare into the same pool.
- You will **not** repeatedly test “what happens if I step there.”
- You will **not** do “rituals” unless they’re planned (because repetition is a kind of engineering here).

## Timing: plan around the basin’s “shifts”

You described four coupled cycles. Treat them like weather + factory shift change.

### Condensation Shift (slick, visibility worst)
- Pros: fewer freeze-cracks, sometimes calmer animals.
- Cons: maximum slipperiness, maximum wetting, visibility collapses, microbial films most active.
- Recommendation: **Avoid long moves.** Do short hops between known stable points.

### Fracture Shift (ice skins form/crack; loud reports)
- Pros: some ground firms temporarily.
- Cons: brittle ice over water, sudden cracks, unpredictable load-bearing.
- Recommendation: **Move only with probing poles and spacing.** Avoid crossing thin ice unless you can distribute weight (see gear).

### Vent Shift (warm mist ropes; airborne swarms; predators follow)
- Pros: brief warmth helps hypothermia risk; upwellings can clear corridors.
- Cons: wildlife activity spikes; predators coordinate with swarms/herds.
- Recommendation: **Good for drying and short instrument checks**, but keep a tight formation and don’t chase “clear corridors.”

### Drain Shift (the killer)
- Pros: sometimes exposes firmer mineral bands.
- Cons: “safe” ground can liquefy or subside in minutes.
- Recommendation: **Do not camp. Do not cross unknown flats.** Use elevated routes and tested paths only.

If you can’t predict shifts, assume you’ll experience at least one Drain event per day and plan conservatively.

## Clothing & personal protective system (you’re building a wearable shelter)

### Core principles
- You need **immersion-grade lower-body protection**, not “hiking rain gear.”
- You need **insulation that still insulates when damp**, and a way to manage sweat (because sweat + cold fog = fast hypothermia).
- You need **skin barriers** against microbial/fungal films and mineral salts.

### Recommended layering
1. **Base layer**: synthetic or merino blend + **vapor barrier socks** (seriously).  
2. **Mid layer**: high-loft fleece or active insulation (synthetic fill beats down here).
3. **Shell**: breathable waterproof is not enough; consider **waders or drysuit-style lower body** if you’ll be in flats frequently.
4. **Hands**: thin liner gloves + waterproof insulated over-mitts. You’ll be handling wet tools constantly.
5. **Head/face**: sealed goggles + brim/hood. Fog + arcs + mineral spray makes eye irritation common.

### Foot system (critical)
- **Primary**: insulated muck boots *or* drysuit booties inside rugged overboots.
- **Add-ons**: gaiters, replaceable felted insoles, foot powder, blister kit.
- **Rule**: if your feet are wet for more than ~2 hours, treat it as an injury-in-progress. Rotate socks, warm feet, and dry aggressively whenever vents permit.

### Respiratory/eye protection
- Bring a **P100-class respirator** (or equivalent) with spare filters stored dry.  
Why: fungal lattices, mineral aerosols, salt blooms, and the general “industrial hiss” environment.
- **Anti-fog strategy**: sealed goggles + anti-fog wipes + a routine. If you can’t see, you will fall.

## Navigation: assume compasses and intuition will fail

### What interferes here
- Magnetite-rich bands can tug compasses.
- Battery flats and arcs can produce RF noise and sensor weirdness.
- Fog editing makes visual landmarks unreliable.
- Distance “skips” break dead reckoning if you don’t log carefully.

### Your navigation stack (redundant on purpose)
1. **Inertial + time-based logging**: a simple wrist compass may lie; a **timed pace + heading + timestamp** log helps detect “missing segments.”
2. **Tether discipline**: in low visibility, use a **physical line** between travelers (short, quick-release to avoid entanglement in mud).
3. **Beacons you can retrieve**: place **weighted marker stakes with reflective + audible ping** (but don’t overuse repetition—see below).
4. **Return anchor**: multiverse travel demands a reliable extraction reference—carry a **dedicated, sealed “home beacon”** and do not power it on/off repeatedly (repetition matters here).

### Marking without feeding the basin
Because the land “accommodates repetition,” you want markers that are:
- **Sparse** (don’t create a “ritual trail” that becomes the basin’s preferred loop),
- **Distinct** (unique shapes/codes each time),
- **Non-ecological** (don’t smear films or carve grooves).

A good pattern: mark only decision points, and each marker includes **date/time + direction-of-travel**.

## Ground travel technique (how not to get eaten by mud or geometry)

### Default posture
- Move slower than you want to.
- Keep spacing so one collapse doesn’t take everyone.
- Probe continuously.

### Tools
- **Two probing poles** (not one). One for testing depth, one for balance.
- **Snowshoe-style flotation** or wide mud plates for boots when crossing clays.
- **Throw line + harness**: if someone starts to sink, you do not run to them; you anchor and haul.

### “Do not step on” cues
- Pearly clays that look smooth and “clean.”
- Flats where droplets align into migrating lines (strong ground currents nearby).
- Ice skins that flex like metal—treat as a lid over water.
- Any place where sound changes suddenly (hollow thunking = cavities, drum basins).

### Use animals carefully
Herd paths can be the safest route, but:
- Don’t cut into a moving formation (they’re using each other as bridges).
- Don’t get between herd and a vibrating trough “highway.”
- Predators exploit panic; your job is to not become a new variable.

## Electrical hazards & electronics

### Personal safety
- Avoid kneeling/lying on “battery flats.”
- Don’t bridge two stones across a seam with wet hands (you can become the conductor).
- Keep metal tools tethered and insulated-handled.

### Gear protection
- Store sensitive electronics in **dry bags inside a Faraday pouch** when not actively used.
- Expect **static-like audio interference**; don’t assume “radio hiss” is merely a weak signal.
- Bring **analog backups**: whistle, mirror/LED strobe, paper map, grease pencil.

### A simple detector helps
A compact **non-contact voltage detector** or multimeter with long leads can warn you before you step into a lively seam. It won’t explain the weirdness, but it can prevent dumb shocks.

## Water and food: assume nothing is drinkable

### Water
Even if it looks clear:
- dissolved salts,
- microbial sheens,
- mineral contamination (iron, sulfur compounds),
- and unknown biology.

Bring:
- Your own water for at least the first phase,
- A **distillation option** (chemical tabs won’t remove salts; filters clog fast in silts),
- A silt pre-filter (cloth + settling container).

### Food
There’s no plant forage; animal products are high-risk (unknown pathogens + mineral accumulation).  
Bring dense calories: fats, carbohydrate bricks, warm drink mixes. Plan for cold-wet energy drain: you will burn more than you expect.

## Medical & contamination protocols (treat this like a bioindustrial wetland)

### Likely injuries
- Hypothermia, immersion foot, frostbite.
- Slips, sprains, lacerations (slick varnish + hidden edges).
- Eye/skin irritation from salts/films.
- Electrical shock (mild but destabilizing).
- Respiratory irritation.

### Med kit additions
- Rewarming supplies: chemical heat packs, emergency bivy, insulated pad.
- Antifungal + broad-spectrum topical antibiotic.
- Sterile saline for eye wash (you will want this).
- Compression wraps + ankle support.
- Electrolyte replacement (you’ll sweat under shells even in cold).

### Decontamination
On exit (or at camp):
- Rinse outer gear with clean water you brought.
- Bag contaminated layers separately.
- Don’t bring “basin mud” into your sleeping system.
- Quarantine any cuts; infections in cold wet environments can turn fast.

## Camping: if you must, camp like you’re on floating machinery

### Site selection
- Avoid depressions (everything is a depression waiting to fill).
- Avoid humming troughs (they’re highways—for animals and for whatever follows them).
- Avoid battery flats (arcs + interference + unpredictable ground behavior).
- Prefer slightly raised shoulders with proven firmness—ideally mineral crust that doesn’t deform under probe pressure.

### Shelter strategy
- Use a **groundsheet + insulated pad + vapor barrier**; the ground will suck heat endlessly.
- Ventilate your shelter even in cold—condensation will soak you from the inside otherwise.
- Keep a “dry core” bag that never touches outside surfaces: sleep base layer, socks, insulation.

### Fire/heat
Assume you cannot make a reliable fire (no dry biomass). Bring:
- stove fuel sufficient for hot water and rewarming,
- ignition redundancy,
- windscreen.

## Behavioral rules inside the basin (these matter as much as equipment)

1. **Never chase clarity.** Those brief fog corridors are tempting; treat them as opportunistic glimpses, not invitations.
2. **Don’t repeat actions casually.** Repetition is a kind of handshake here. If you must repeat (route-finding), do it deliberately and log it.
3. **No call-and-response.** Mimicry gets “refined” and returned; you don’t want to become part of the soundscape.
4. **Log everything.** Time, heading, terrain underfoot, sound changes, animal movements. Your log is an anchor when geometry edits.
5. **If you suspect a loop, stop.** Eat, warm up, compare notes, and change one variable (direction, formation order, marker type). Don’t “power through.”
