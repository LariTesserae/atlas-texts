## What you’re walking into (in human terms)

This is not “the ocean.” It’s an underwater room that behaves like a hydraulic instrument: shallow, warm, very bright, and constantly moving. Its hazards come less from depth and more from **mechanism**—currents, valves, chutes, edges, ropes, and the way sound and pressure never stop.

The two defining traits you should prepare around:

1. **No natural bottom / no natural cues.** The floor is tiled; the walls meet at deliberate angles; you can lose your sense of “terrain” because nothing slopes the way seafloors do.
2. **Water is doing work.** There are mouths, grates, flaps, and narrowing passages that create jets, suction, backflow, and pulsed turbulence.

Treat it like entering an operating machine that happens to be made of brick, rope, and sea.

## Before you go: decide how you’ll breathe

Because it’s only a few body-lengths down, you *can* free-dive it—but the chamber’s constant motion, noise, and snag-points make “quick dips” riskier than they sound.

### Option A: Snorkel + free-dive (simplest, but time-limited)
**Recommended only if:** you’re comfortable doing repeated dives with good breath-hold discipline and you have a spotter at the surface.

- Pros: minimal gear, easy in/out.
- Cons: short bottom time encourages rushing; rushing + machinery-like currents = mistakes.

### Option B: Scuba (safest for observation, highest entanglement risk)
**Recommended if:** you want to map it, watch flow patterns, examine valves/slots, or linger near the chute.

- Pros: time, calm, better decision-making.
- Cons: hoses/console can snag on lattice/rope/posts; bubbles may collect under the overhead grid and disturb flows.

### Option C: Supplied air from surface (best compromise if you have support)
If you can rig a surface line and keep a tender above, a hookah/surface-supplied setup reduces tank bulk while giving time.

**Bottom line:** If you’re “ordinary human” without strong freediving habits, use scuba or surface-supplied air and plan like a confined-space dive even though it’s shallow.

## Timing: enter on the chamber’s “quietest” breath

This place changes its mood with swell and tide. Your goal is to minimize flow reversals and sudden jets.

- **Aim for slack tide** if the chamber connects to open water through tuned mouths. That’s when valves and one-way flaps tend to cycle less violently.
- Avoid storm chop or heavy swell days. The overhead lattice turns surface violence into constant vibration; in rough conditions the whole chamber will feel like it’s being shaken.
- If you can, do a **surface recon first**: watch the waterline above the pocket for unusual pulsing, foam lines, or periodic “gulping” (air being pulled and spilled).

## Core hazards (and what to do about them)

### 1. Entrapment and pinning
The mouths, chutes, and grates can create **localized force**—enough to pin a swimmer against masonry or pull a fin into a slot.

**Rules:**
- Do not approach any opening head-on. Approach from the side and keep an exit angle.
- Stay out of any place where your body could bridge between a current source and a hard surface (classic pin scenario).
- Assume any flap/valve can move unexpectedly with a surge. Do not put hands under/behind them.

### 2. Snag/entanglement (rope, pegs, lattices, overhead grid)
This environment is full of “hand-built” catch points.

**Bring:**
- A **cutting tool** you can use one-handed (line cutter or small shears beats a big knife).
- Streamlined configuration (secure hoses, clip everything down).
- Gloves are useful, but don’t let gloves make you careless around slots.

**Practice:**
- If something catches, don’t thrash. Stop, exhale, back out along the path you came, then cut only if needed.

### 3. Abrasion and sharp edges
Young masonry is sharp. Tile edges and mortar lines can take skin off fast.

**Wear:**
- Thin wetsuit or rashguard + leggings.
- Booties with real sole (tiles + terraces + unseen edges).
- Gloves if you’ll touch anything (but touching less is better).

### 4. Chemical/irritant risk (anti-fouling washes)
You noted a faint metallic tang—very plausibly copper salts or similar. In some universes it may be stronger or paired with other biocides.

**Precautions:**
- Avoid rubbing your eyes underwater.
- Minimize contact with joints, pegs, washed wood, and “young” mortar seams.
- Rinse thoroughly afterward; if you get a rash/tingle, treat it as chemical irritation, not “jellyfish.”

### 5. Sensory overload: continuous loudness + vibration
Underwater noise here is physical. People can get anxious or fatigued without realizing why.

**Do:**
- Plan shorter passes with surface breaks even if your air allows longer.
- Use deliberate, slow finning; fast motion + loud chamber can spike panic.
- Agree on simple signals with your buddy: “too loud / overwhelmed / exit now.”

### 6. Disorientation (because it’s a “room,” not a reef)
You can lose “up/down/where’s the exit” when every surface is built and light is shifting.

**Use:**
- A **primary reference line** from the entry mouth to a safe central point (even a thin guideline).
- A compass is less useful than you think; a line and landmarks (chute, settling basin corner, brick berm) work better.
- A small torch helps you read edges and openings despite brightness.

## Skills to brush up before attempting

Even shallow, this is not a casual swim.

- **Buoyancy control / hovering:** You want to observe currents without touching tile or drifting into a valve.
- **Current management:** Sideways crabbing, backing out, controlled turns.
- **One-handed problem-solving:** clearing a snag, cutting a line, sharing air (if scuba), all without stirring silt traps.
- **Stress protocol:** stop → breathe → think → move. Loud chambers make people skip the “think.”

If you don’t have these, your safest preparation is: **bring a buddy who does**, and keep your role observational.

## What to pack (practical checklist)

### Minimum safe kit (snorkel/free-dive)
- Mask + snorkel + fins
- Exposure protection (rashguard/wetsuit, booties)
- Cutting tool (line cutter/shears)
- Surface marker (bright float) and a surface spotter
- Waterproof whistle on the float (for surface coordination)
- Water + electrolytes (warm water still dehydrates)

### Preferred kit (scuba / extended observation)
- Streamlined BCD/wing; secure all dangling gear
- Redundant cutting tool (two is not excessive here)
- SMB + spool (mark exit / call surface help)
- Primary light (small) + backup (even though it’s bright)
- Slate or wetnotes (you’ll want to sketch; cameras miss flow logic)
- Thin gloves + booties + full coverage suit
- Optional: small non-permanent markers (chalk/grease pencil) for temporary notes on your slate, not on the structure

### Multiverse-traveler additions (the stuff you’ll regret not having)
- A way to **anchor your exit**: a literal tether to your entry point, plus a written description of where/how you entered (people forget under sensory load).
- A small **sample vial** only if you have a strong reason (and only from loose sediment traps, never scraped from joints).
- Translation/etiquette kit if builders are present nearby (offerings, tokens, non-threatening posture cues). This feels maintained.

## Entry protocol (how to do it without becoming part of the mechanism)

1. **Observe from outside first.** Watch how water moves at each mouth. Identify the “loud” chute and decide to treat it as a boundary, not a destination.
2. **Choose a single entry/exit mouth.** Don’t improvise exits in a chamber with one-way behaviors.
3. **Run a line** from the entry to a central “decision point.” In a featureless pocket, that line is your calm.
4. **First circuit = perimeter only.** Stay off the floor, 1–2 meters above it if depth allows. You’re mapping hazards, not investigating details.
5. **Second circuit = read the flows.** Drop neutrally and watch suspended specks: where do they shear, where do they settle, where do they accelerate?
6. **Only then approach features** (brick berm, settling basin, lattices), and never while fighting a current.

Exit early if:
- you feel yourself “working” to stay in place,
- you start touching things to steady yourself,
- the sound shifts suddenly (often a swell set or valve cycle),
- your buddy’s eyes get wide (seriously—people telegraph stress underwater).

## Behavior inside: how not to break it (or be blamed for breaking it)

This place reads like it expects maintenance. Someone may return. Treat it as owned.

- Don’t pull pegs, test flaps, or “see what happens” with a gate.
- Don’t wedge yourself into terraces or corners; they may be flow-calibrated.
- Don’t stir the settling basin unless you want the whole chamber to go opaque.
- If you must touch: touch **brick**, not mortar seams or anything washed/painted.

## Health and aftercare

- Rinse skin, ears, and gear thoroughly after. If copper/biocide is present, residue matters.
- Watch for delayed irritation: red eyes, metallic taste that lingers, skin itching.
- If you were on scuba: it’s shallow, but don’t let that make you sloppy about ascent control—overhead lattice + distraction can lead to rapid ascents.
