## What you’re walking into (in human terms)

This is **very high, very cold, very wet terrain** with **young, unstable rock**, **patchy but dangerous snow**, **persistent fog/low cloud**, and a landscape that behaves like it’s still settling into itself. The big risks aren’t exotic—they’re the classic ones, stacked together:

- **Hypothermia** (because you’ll be wet *all the time* and wind will arrive in pulses)
- **Altitude illness** (because the whole country sits high, not just a few peaks)
- **Falls** (slick glassy grit, loose talus, steep chutes, hidden ice)
- **Avalanche/cornice failure** (because snow is “trapped reserves under tension,” not a stable blanket)
- **Rockfall** (freshly shattered slopes + freeze–thaw = constant projectile generation)
- **Navigation failure** (long sightlines that suddenly erase into mist; few landmarks; no trails)
- **Lightning/storm electricity** (St. Elmo’s fire conditions = you’re in the charge zone)

Prepare like you’re going into a **cold maritime alpine plateau** with **active freeze–thaw**, **poor visibility**, and **no infrastructure**.

## 1) Pre-trip preparation (don’t skip this)

### Medical and acclimatization
- **Get altitude-ready**: if you can’t pre-acclimatize in-world, plan to **spend 2–4 days ramping up** your sleeping elevation after arrival.
  - Rule of thumb: once above ~2500 m, increase sleeping altitude gradually and take rest days.
- **Know the altitude red flags**:
  - AMS: headache + nausea + poor sleep + unusual fatigue.
  - HACE: confusion, stumbling, severe lethargy (evacuate *down* immediately).
  - HAPE: breathlessness at rest, wet cough, chest tightness (evacuate down).
- Consider bringing **acetazolamide** (if you tolerate it and your physician agrees). Bring **ibuprofen**; altitude headaches are common.

### Conditioning and skills
If you’re “ordinary,” your biggest advantage is **competence, not toughness**.
- Be comfortable with:
  - **Layering for cold-wet wind**
  - **Talus travel** (ankle/ knee stability)
  - **Whiteout navigation** (map + compass + pacing + bearing discipline)
  - **Basic avalanche awareness** if you’ll touch snow slopes at all
- Practice: setting up shelter and stove **with gloves on** in wind.

### Time budgeting (the “space between things” problem)
Distances will **feel engineered**: long empty spans with few intermediate safe spots.
- Plan travel days assuming **slow pace**:
  - Loose scree + boggy plateaus + route-finding + visibility delays.
- Build in **forced weather days**. Expect to lose movement days to fog/wind.

## 2) Core gear: built for cold-wet, not just cold

### Clothing system (prioritize drying and wind control)
You’ll be damp from drizzle/mist, ground saturation, and sweat trapped by shells.

**Bring:**
- Base layers: **synthetic or merino** (no cotton, ever)
- Midlayer: **fleece** (keeps working while wet)
- Insulation: **synthetic puffy** (safer than down in persistent wet; down only if you can guarantee dryness)
- Shell: truly **waterproof + windproof** jacket and pants (not “water resistant”)
- Gloves: **two systems**
  - thin liner gloves (dexterity)
  - waterproof insulated over-gloves/mitts (survival)
- Hat + neck gaiter/buff
- **Gaiters** (bog + scree + snow patches)
- Spare socks (rotate; sleep in dry pair)

**Footwear:**
- **Stiff, supportive boots** with aggressive tread (talus + wet rock)
- Consider **microspikes** as baseline; **crampons + ice axe** if you will cross steep firm snow or glacier ice.
- Insoles that dry fast; blister care kit.

### Shelter and sleep (assume wind + wet ground)
- **4-season or very solid 3+ season tent** that pitches tight and handles gusts
- Ground is often saturated: bring a **tough footprint** and expect pooling
- Sleeping bag: warmer than you think (cold + damp feels colder)
  - Prefer **synthetic** or treated down + strict moisture management
- Sleeping pads: **two-pad system** (closed-cell + inflatable) for insulation and puncture resilience on sharp gravel

### Cooking and water
Water is everywhere, but safe water isn’t guaranteed.
- Stove: reliable in wind and cold (integrated canister systems work; liquid fuel is robust in deep cold)
- Fuel: extra—storms trap you in place
- Water treatment: **filter + chemical backup**
  - Filters can clog with glacial flour/silt; chemical alone is slow in cold
- Containers: 2–3 L capacity; include a wide-mouth bottle you can manage with gloves

### Navigation and comms (fog will eat your confidence)
- Map + compass (non-negotiable)
- GPS device/phone with offline maps + **spare power**
- **Altimeter watch** helps when the horizon disappears
- Satellite messenger or PLB (because there are no roads, no cairns, no fences, no help)

### Protection from rock/ice and “industrial” hazards
- **Climbing helmet**: worth the weight if you’re anywhere near chutes, cliffs, or under cornices
- Eye protection: clear/amber lenses for dim glare and wind grit
- Ear protection optional but useful in sustained wind (fatigue management)
- Trekking poles: stability on scree and in edge-marsh ground

## 3) Movement strategy: how not to get “processed” by the landscape

### Route choices
- Prefer **ridges and broad shoulders** *when lightning risk is low* and wind is manageable—better visibility and less bog.
- Prefer **benches/shelves** for travel efficiency, but watch for:
  - sudden drop-offs into chutes
  - invisible slick rock under mist
- Avoid **traveling under corniced ridgelines**. Cornices fail backward and outward—give them a wide berth.

### Scree and talus
Young terrain means **ankle breakers** and “marbles on slab.”
- Step on **bigger, more embedded** rocks when possible.
- Test each boulder before committing weight.
- Keep spacing between travelers (rockfall you start can hit your partner).

### Wet plateaus (“edge marshes”)
This ground will steal energy and soak you.
- Skirt saturated basins when possible.
- Use poles to probe depth; what looks firm can be floating moss over water.
- Keep feet warm: cold-wet foot problems escalate fast.

### Snow patches and ice
The snow here isn’t friendly. Treat every patch as if it could slide.
- Early day: snow may be firm/icy—slips become long falls.
- Midday: thin surface melt can turn slopes into lubricated slabs.
- Avoid crossing below hanging drifts and cornices, especially during warming or wind events.

### Stream crossings (because streams “start and stop”)
- Don’t trust a channel that disappears into gravel—subsurface flow can undermine your footing.
- Cross early in the day when melt is lower.
- Unbuckle pack straps for quick release if you fall.

## 4) Weather and electricity: practical storm behavior

### What to expect
- “Daytime without light”: low cloud, drizzle, and constant condensation.
- Wind arrives **in pulses**, accelerated through passes.

### Storm protocol (simple and strict)
- If visibility collapses: stop and **anchor your position** (bearing, waypoint, notes). Wandering in fog is how people walk into chutes.
- If wind spikes: get off exposed ridges; secure loose items; protect hands first (cold injury happens when you’re trying to fix gear barehanded).
- If you feel hair lift / hear buzzing / see faint corona:
  - You’re in extreme electrical conditions.
  - Get off high points and away from isolated rock spires.
  - Crouch on insulating material (pack/foam pad), feet together, minimize contact with ground. Spread group out.

## 5) Wildlife and bio-precautions (life is “punctuated” here)

You’re not the apex predator just because you’re clever.
- Keep food sealed; use **odor-proof bags** and a hard container if you have one.
- Predators are quiet here. Don’t rely on hearing them.
- Give ungulates space—on narrow ledges, spooking them can trigger rockfall onto you.

**Insects and illness:**
- There may be brief swarms near warm seeps. Bring head net if you hate misery.
- Treat water; high animal density near melt lines means higher fecal contamination risk.

## 6) Camps: where to sleep without waking up inside a problem

### Site selection rules
Pick places that are boring in the best way:
- Not under cliffs (rockfall)
- Not below cornices or steep snow faces (avalanche)
- Not in the lowest basin (cold sink + saturation + flooding)
- Not on obvious animal highways near seeps (nighttime visitors)

Look for:
- Slightly raised gravel benches with drainage
- Natural windbreaks that won’t become a rockfall funnel
- Enough distance from steep chutes that a rolling stone can’t reach you

### Moisture management at camp
- Your primary job each evening is **get dry enough not to hypothermia overnight**.
- Strip wet layers immediately; put on your dedicated dry sleep layer.
- Ventilate the tent even in bad weather—condensation will soak your insulation otherwise.

## 7) Emergency planning (assume self-rescue)

### Carry an “unplanned night out” kit even on day walks
- Bivy or emergency shelter
- Fire kit (even if firewood is nonexistent, flame is a tool)
- Extra insulation (puffy, spare gloves)
- Calories you can eat without cooking
- Headlamp + spare battery
- First aid emphasizing:
  - blister care
  - sprains (wrap/tape)
  - hypothermia (heat + shelter)
  - altitude symptoms (descent plan)

### Evacuation logic
Because the land is vast and unfinished, the safest exit is often:
- **Down** (altitude illness + weather)
- **Away from chutes and undercuts**
- **Toward consistent terrain** (broad shelves over fractured gullies)

Pre-plan “decision points”:
- If fog drops below X visibility → stop.
- If wind exceeds your ability to stand safely → camp.
- If anyone shows AMS symptoms that worsen → descend immediately.

## 8) Mindset: how to travel so the place doesn’t wear you down

This landscape runs on cycles: wetting/drying, freezing/thawing, fog/clearing. You should too.

- Move in **short, efficient blocks**, then stop before you’re exhausted.
- Eat and drink continuously; cold damp suppresses thirst but accelerates dehydration.
- Treat every “easy” open span as a trap for time loss: featureless distance + fog = slow.

If you prepare for **wet cold**, **low visibility**, **unstable ground**, and **long stretches without bailout**, you can travel here safely—and even enjoy the strange “factory-floor quiet” without becoming one more object the mountain sorts downhill.
