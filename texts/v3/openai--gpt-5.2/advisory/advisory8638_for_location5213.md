## What you’re walking into (in practical terms)
This chamber behaves like a **warm, humid, partially powered industrial cave** with **young, sharp geology** and **slow, chemistry-driven “plant” growth** that likely runs on heat/mineral gradients rather than sunlight. Hazards come less from monsters and more from **air quality, electrics, footing, and time** (getting turned around, overexposure, unnoticed hypoxia, a single bad slip onto knife-edge rock).

Assume it is a **confined space** until proven otherwise.

---

## 1) Pre-visit decisions (the things that prevent most bad outcomes)

### Go/no-go criteria
Do not enter if you can’t meet all of these:
- **Two independent light sources per person** (plus spare batteries).
- **A way to monitor air** (at minimum O₂; ideally multi-gas).
- **A guaranteed exit plan** that does not depend on the chamber’s machinery (doors, fans, pumps, lights).
- **A buddy or tether plan**. Solo is how people die quietly in places like this.

### Time budget
Plan on **short, purposeful sorties**:
- First entry: **15–30 minutes** max, focused on route/air/electrical survey.
- Subsequent entries: **60–120 minutes** with timed turn-around.
Warmth + humidity + low light quietly accelerates fatigue and mistakes.

### Contamination mindset (both directions)
Treat the lush patches as **biologically and chemically unfamiliar**:
- You don’t want their films/spores in your lungs.
- You don’t want your surface microbes introduced into a closed habitat.
So you need **basic quarantine discipline** (covered later).

---

## 2) Core kit (what an “ordinary human” should realistically bring)

### Personal protective equipment (PPE)
- **Helmet** with chin strap (low ceiling + sharp fins + unseen metal edges).
- **Eye protection** (clear goggles). Condensation drips + mineral dust + rust flakes.
- **Gloves**: one dexterous pair + one tougher over-pair (wet metal and sharp rock shred skin fast).
- **Boots**: aggressive tread, ankle support, puncture-resistant sole. Expect slick basalt and hidden metal shards.
- **Knee protection** optional but helpful (you’ll kneel to inspect grooves, drains, cracks).

### Lighting & navigation
- **Primary headlamp** (high CRI helps differentiate wet rock vs plant membrane vs corroded metal).
- **Backup headlamp** on-body.
- **Handheld flashlight** for distance throws (spotting walkway ends, dangling cables).
- **Route marking**: high-visibility flagging tape + chalk/grease pencil (chalk fails on wet surfaces; bring both).
- **A “breadcrumb” beacon option**: small magnetic/adhesive LED markers or reflective tabs placed at decision points.
- **Compass is unreliable** around ore/steel; favor **tape-marked route + distance counting + sketches**.

### Air safety (non-negotiable in this place)
Minimum:
- **O₂ meter** (continuous).
Better:
- **4-gas meter** (O₂, CO₂, CO, H₂S) because:
  - CO₂ can pool low in warm, still chambers
  - CO can appear from failing generators/compressors
  - H₂S can appear in mineral/anaerobic seep zones
Add:
- **Respirator** (half-face) with **P100 + multi-gas/organic vapor cartridges**.
  - The “warm tea / wet metal” smell suggests volatile chemistry; also unknown aerosols from membranes/films.
- If you can’t source cartridges across worlds, at least bring a **P100** and accept you’re still gambling on gases.

### Clothing & thermal management
- Lightweight, quick-dry base layer (you’ll sweat).
- Outer layer that tolerates wet abrasion (humidity + rock = constant rubbing).
- **Spare dry layer** in a sealed bag (for after).
- **Electrolytes + water**. The heat is “breathy” and deceptive; dehydration creeps up.

### Tools that matter here
- **Non-contact voltage tester** + small **multimeter** (old lamps/boxes can be live).
- **Insulated pliers** / cable cutter (for emergencies only; don’t “fix” the place casually).
- **20–30 m of static line** + a few locking carabiners (for the broken walkway/crevice edges).
- **Walking pole** or short probe (tests slickness and depth of runnels).
- **First aid**: include irrigation saline (eyes), blister care, antiseptic, compression wrap.
- **Emergency**: small chemical lightsticks (work when everything electronic fails).

### Quarantine / decon kit
- Nitrile gloves (disposable)
- Sealable bags for contaminated items
- Wipes (70% isopropyl) + mild soap
- If you’ll revisit other habitats: dedicate one set of boots/gloves to this chamber only.

## 3) Arrival protocol (the first 10 minutes inside)

### Stop at the threshold and do a “systems check”
1. **Air meter on**, wait for stable readings.
2. **Listen**: locate the pump “sigh,” note drip rhythms, identify any arcing/buzzing.
3. **Light discipline**: use low mode while you orient; save high mode for hazards.
4. **Scan high and low**:
   - High: hanging vines/strands, low beams, dangling cables, condensation zones
   - Low: slick patches, metal basins, grooves that catch a boot, sudden drop-offs

### Establish a “hard line” for retreat
Put a visible marker at the entry and agree:
- Turn around if **O₂ drops**, **CO/CO₂ rises**, you smell sharp “electrical/ozone,” or you feel **headache/pressure**.
- If the chamber makes you feel “out of breath but not panicked,” suspect CO₂/hypoxia—leave first, analyze later.

---

## 4) Moving through the chamber safely (how to not get cut, shocked, or lost)

### Footing rules
- Assume **every dark sheen is slippery** and every dry matte face may be **razor-sharp**.
- Step on **textured stone**, not on plant membranes (they may be slick, fragile, or reactive).
- Avoid the temptation to “shortcut” across warm wet rock—warmth often means **condensation film**.

### Hand placement
- Do not palm overhead ledges; feel with the **back of a gloved hand** first.
- Watch for:
  - **Knife-edge fins**
  - **fresh shear faces** (sharp like broken ceramic)
  - **rusted metal flanges** (serrated by corrosion)

### The broken walkway / industrial remnants
Treat all built elements as **structural lies**:
- Walkways may be **delaminated** by corrosion.
- Pipes can be **hot** (heat-sinking) or unexpectedly **cold and wet** (condensation traps).
- Control boxes can be **live** even if lights are dim; humidity-triggered strips imply automated switching.

Rule: **No touching metal** until you’ve tested it for voltage if there’s any chance it’s tied to power.

### Route marking that works in darkness + humidity
- Mark **at every decision point**, not every 10 steps.
- Use **paired markers**: one at knee height, one at eye height, because vegetation can obscure one level.
- Add directional cues (arrow + distance to exit).

## 5) The vegetation: how to coexist with it without getting hurt

### Assume it’s doing chemistry, not photosynthesis
Those slick films and mineral-grit surfaces suggest it’s **harvesting water + minerals + heat**. That can mean:
- Mildly acidic/alkaline secretions
- Metal-binding compounds
- Irritant aerosols when disturbed

Practical rules:
- **Don’t brush through** hanging strands. Detour.
- **Don’t kneel on mats** that “open and close pores.”
- If you must sample: do it with tools, not fingers, and bag immediately.

### Exposure management
- Keep respirator on when you’re close enough to smell it clearly.
- If your skin tingles/itches after contact, treat it like chemical exposure: rinse with water, then mild soap; don’t scrub hard (you’ll drive irritants into microcuts).

---

## 6) Air and heat: the quiet threat

### What to watch for (symptom-based)
Leave if you notice:
- Unusual fatigue, heavy legs, yawning (CO₂)
- Headache, nausea, confusion (CO/CO₂/low O₂)
- Burning eyes/throat (chemical vapors)
- “Can’t get a full breath” feeling near lush patches (local gas pocket or aerosol)

### Positioning matters
- CO₂ and some heavier gases pool low; avoid lingering in **low basins, crevice bottoms, and metal sumps**.
- Warmer air rises; if you need a better air mix, sometimes **a slightly higher stance** helps.

---

## 7) Electrical and mechanical etiquette (don’t become the ground path)
- Assume humidity-triggered lights mean **active sensing circuitry**.
- Never bridge two metal objects with your hands.
- Avoid stepping on cables/straps hidden under growth.
- If you hear a relay click or a light changes state when you approach: stop, reassess route, and avoid “investigating” by touch.

---

## 8) Communications and emergency planning
This chamber is small enough to learn, but dark enough to lose orientation.

Minimum:
- Buddy system with **voice check every 2–3 minutes**.
- If solo is unavoidable: use a **tether line** from the entry and keep it in hand when you leave the main route.

Emergency triggers (agree in advance):
- Any fall with possible head impact
- Any shock/tingle from metal
- Any abnormal air reading
- Any sudden increase in drip/rain (condensation shift can turn surfaces to ice-slick equivalents quickly)

Have a simple evac method:
- “Drop what you’re carrying, follow markers, hand on line, lights to medium, no running.”

---

## 9) After you exit (this is where cross-world travelers often mess up)

### Decontamination
- Remove outer layer/boots onto a groundsheet.
- Bag anything damp with plant film or mineral grit.
- Wash hands/face/neck thoroughly.
- If you wore a respirator: wipe down seal and replace filters if they got wet (wet filters can become useless or hazardous).

### Health monitoring
For 24 hours, watch for:
- Persistent cough, wheeze, chest tightness
- Feverish feeling (could be infection or reaction)
- Skin rash at contact points
Seek help early; cave exposures can have delayed effects.

---

## 10) Small, smart “quality of visit” choices
- Bring a **small thermocouple/IR thermometer**: mapping heat gradients will explain the room better than light.
- Keep a **field notebook**: sketch where lush patches cluster relative to pipes and vents; this place is about gradients.
- Sit still for 5 minutes (when safe): your ears will start separating pump-cycle pressure changes from drip notes, and you’ll predict where wet zones will appear.

---

## Summary: the three priorities
1. **Air safety** (monitor + respirator + exit discipline)
2. **Light + navigation** (redundancy + marking)
3. **Contact discipline** (assume sharp rock, live metal, reactive films)

If you prepare like it’s a living, humid machine-room disguised as a cave, you’ll have the right instincts: slow movement, clean routes, minimal touching, and a constant awareness that the chamber’s “stillness” is not the same thing as safety.
