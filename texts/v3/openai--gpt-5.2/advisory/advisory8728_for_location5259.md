## What you’re actually visiting (operational summary)

This is not “an ocean with a bottom.” It’s a planet-scale, cold, dim, high-pressure water column over an artificial substrate—endless terraced masonry, canals, embankments, timber districts—now functioning as seabed. Visibility is short (meters on good days), silt hangs, currents are slow but route-like, and there is essentially **no animal life** to cue danger or orientation. The primary risks are **pressure, cold, disorientation, entanglement/silt-out, and structural collapse**—not predators.

Assume: **no breathable atmosphere anywhere you can reach**, no reliable “surface,” and no quick rescue unless you bring it with you.

---

## The big hazards (what injures or kills travelers here)

1. **Pressure**
   - If there are kilometers of water overhead, pressure is lethal without a rigid pressure-rated system.
   - Even moderate depths + long durations make decompression and gas management a major failure point if you dive “wet.”

2. **Cold + conductive heat loss**
   - Water will strip heat fast. Hypothermia and loss of dexterity are the earliest performance failures.

3. **Navigation failure (no horizon, low contrast, few landmarks at distance)**
   - You can get lost within minutes in silt, vegetation, and repeating architecture.
   - A “return to the surface” plan may not exist.

4. **Silt-out and zero-vis events**
   - Your own propulsion can erase visibility. In enclosed spaces, one fin-kick can turn the world into ink.

5. **Entrapment**
   - Kelp-like ribbons, draping mats, cracks between paving stones, culverts, and collapsed arches can snag hoses, limbs, and tethers.

6. **Structural movement**
   - “Old, pressure-loaded architecture” means occasional collapses. The danger is less “falling rocks” and more **shockwaves, sudden silt clouds, and shifting debris** in confined areas.

7. **Chemical/biological unknowns**
   - No animals doesn’t mean sterile. Biofilms, mineral gradients, and plant analogues may produce irritants or toxins; water chemistry may be corrosive.

8. **Psychological strain**
   - Constant twilight, silence, slow time, and featureless repetition can cause time distortion, panic spirals, or reckless decisions.

## The single most important prep decision: **don’t visit “wet” unless you must**

### Recommended: a **one-atmosphere system**
Pick one of these as your “ordinary human” baseline:

- **Small pressure-rated submersible (1–2 person)**
  - Best safety margin: stable warmth, power, sonar, manipulator arms, sample containment, and you avoid decompression entirely.
  - Downsides: can’t enter tight interiors; can snag on vegetation if poorly piloted.

- **Atmospheric diving suit (ADS) / hard suit**
  - You keep 1 atm inside; no decompression requirements.
  - Better for close inspection and limited entry into openings.
  - Downsides: mobility limits; joint snag risk; requires training to avoid falls/gets stuck.

### Only if you have strong reasons: **saturation or mixed-gas diving**
If you go “wet,” you inherit:
- decompression obligations,
- narcosis/HPNS risk,
- gas logistics,
- heightened entanglement danger,
- drastically higher fatality odds in silted interiors.

For a first visit: bring a rigid system.

## Pre-departure checklist (do this before you cross universes)

### 1) Baseline environmental probes (minimum viable science kit)
You want a 10-minute “is this survivable?” survey before committing people:

- CTD sensor: **conductivity/salinity, temperature, depth/pressure**
- pH / ORP (oxidation-reduction potential)
- Dissolved oxygen (mostly for corrosion/biology expectations)
- Turbidity meter (visibility forecasting)
- Water sample bottles (sealed) + filter papers (for biofilm/microbe capture)
- Corrosion coupons (simple metal strips) to see if the water aggressively eats alloys

If the water is unusually corrosive, choose titanium/ceramic/exotic polymer where possible and avoid exposed electrical contacts.

### 2) Physical/skills training (what matters here)
- **Line handling and guideline discipline** (cave-diving mindset even if you’re in a sub/ADS)
- **Zero-visibility navigation drills** (touch contact, instrument-only return)
- **Cold-stress performance** (fine motor tasks with thick gloves, emergency procedures while chilled)
- **Entanglement escapes** (cutting tool use, tether management)
- **Acoustic comms protocols** (short, structured messages; silence discipline)

### 3) Mission rules (write them down; follow them)
- No interior penetrations (stairs, chambers, culverts) on the first sortie.
- No un-tethered excursions beyond a set radius from your entry beacon.
- Abort triggers: visibility below X, current above Y, suit/sub fault, comm degradation, unexplained structural noises, rising turbidity.

## Core equipment (assume you must be self-rescuing)

### A) Life support & thermal
- One-atmosphere habitat (sub or ADS) with:
  - Redundant CO₂ scrubbing
  - Redundant power buses
  - Emergency O₂ reserves sized for **2× planned duration**
  - Active heating + insulation
- External temperature sensor and internal core-temperature alarm (hypothermia sneaks up)

### B) Navigation & “don’t get lost” stack (this is non-negotiable)
Because the landscape repeats and distance vanishes:

- **Inertial navigation unit (INS)** + dead-reckoning display
- **Doppler Velocity Log (DVL)** (for precise movement over ground)
- **Forward-looking sonar** (your substitute for vision)
- **Downward imaging sonar** (reads the “paving fields” and channels like a map)
- **Deployable acoustic beacons** (breadcrumb trail you can home to)
- **Physical guideline spool** (even in open areas, for the last 50–200 m near openings/structures)
- **High-contrast markers** that clamp to masonry without damaging it (magnetic won’t work on stone; use clamp/expanding anchors)

Rule of thumb: if you can’t find your way back **with lights off and visibility at zero**, you’re overextended.

### C) Lighting (done for silt, not for beauty)
- Primary: wide, low-backscatter flood
- Secondary: narrow spot for reading inscriptions/edges
- Emergency: chemical or passive strobes for “find me” (placed on beacons)
- Use **low angle** lighting; avoid blasting straight ahead into suspended silt.

### D) Propulsion & mobility
- Thrusters/propulsors with **fine control** (the goal is not speed; it’s not stirring silt)
- If in a suit: avoid finning; use controlled thrusters or careful handholds.
- Carry a compact “pusher pole”/standoff rod for gentle contact with stone (keeps you off fragile growth and reduces silt disturbance).

### E) Entanglement & extraction tools
- Two cutting tools in different locations (e.g., line cutter + shears)
- Small grapnel hook only for emergencies (risk of snagging architecture/vegetation)
- Inflatable lift bag (if “wet”) or micro-ballast control (if ADS/sub) to free from cracks
- For a sub: external manipulator with a guarded blade attachment (vegetation can wrap thrusters)

### F) Communications
- Acoustic modem for text pings (better than voice in many conditions)
- Short-range ultrasonic “buddy pinger”
- Prearranged codebook for failures (e.g., “TURB-3” = visibility collapse, returning to beacon)

### G) Medical & contamination control
- Sealed sample containers; never bring raw water into your cabin without containment.
- Rinse/decon protocol: external rinse, then suit/sub interface wipe-down.
- Eye/skin irritant kit; antihistamines; topical steroid; broad-spectrum antimicrobial for minor cuts (unknown biofilms).
- If you *must* breach into an air pocket (rare): treat it as contaminated until proven otherwise (spores, gases).

## How to move without getting killed (local technique)

### Buoyancy/trim discipline (even in a sub)
- Maintain standoff distance above the paving (1–2 m). The “floor” is a silt trap; turbulence will blind you.
- Do not land unless necessary. If you must, choose **bare stone** over vegetation mats (mats can hide voids).

### Read the currents like roads
- Channels and causeways will “aim” the flow. Use them for predictable return travel, but beware:
  - faster flow = more suspended grit = more abrasion and less visibility
  - lee-side eddies = silt drifts (easy to stir)

### Treat openings like caves
Any stairwell, arch, culvert, or chamber:
- Run a **guideline** from outside to your turn point.
- Keep one hand/tool dedicated to line management.
- Enforce a hard turn time, not “until we feel done.”
- Expect colder, stiller water and instant silt-out.

### Respect structural sound
Those groans and distant thuds are real information:
- If you hear a new sequence of creaks *nearby*, back off from walls, ceilings, timber frameworks.
- Don’t shelter under arches “because it feels protected.” Pressure-aged arches fail without drama.

- Soft brushes and a suction sampler for biofilms (don’t scrape stone; you’re sampling, not excavating)

Assume the vegetation may:
- exude slippery polysaccharides (traction hazard),
- stain optics,

## Psychological preparation (the part people skip)

This place is *quietly* hostile because it doesn’t react.

- Train for **sensory deprivation**: low light, monotone color, low sound.
- Expect **time dilation**: minutes feel like hours; hours feel like minutes.
- Build a routine: callouts every 2–5 minutes (depth, heading, remaining power, turbidity, next beacon).
- Bring something that anchors judgment: a strict checklist culture and a partner who will say “abort” early.

If you’re prone to panic in confined, dark environments: do not do interior penetrations here, full stop.

## A safe first-visit mission profile (suggested)

1. **Insert and anchor**
   - Deploy a primary entry beacon + strobe.
   - Take baseline water chemistry and turbidity.
   - Run a short guideline “home lane” (even if in a sub, it helps in sensor failures).

2. **Map before you roam**
   - Do a slow sonar “lawnmower” pattern at a fixed altitude.
   - Identify: major channels, terraces, buttresses, any large voids or unstable timber districts.

3. **Pick one feature**
   - Example: a causeway intersection or concentric ring edge.
   - Approach from down-current to minimize silt wake.
   - Photograph/sonar-scan; take *minimal* samples.

4. **Return early**
   - Return with at least 50% reserves on a first sortie. This world punishes “one more look.”

5. **Debrief and revise**
   - Update local rules: where silt is worst, where currents concentrate, what growth snags you, any corrosion seen.

## Emergency doctrine (what you do when it goes wrong)

- **Visibility collapse (silt-out):**
  - Stop. Hold position. Reduce propulsion to near-zero.
  - Switch to sonar/INS return.
  - If in an opening: follow guideline out—do not “feel around” without line.

- **Entanglement:**
  - Freeze propulsion (thrusters worsen wrapping).
  - Locate the snag point visually/sonar if possible.
  - Cut *the entangling material*, not your tether, unless you have a second guaranteed way home.

- **Power degradation:**
  - Abort immediately. Darkness + no horizon is how you die here.
  - Drop a beacon where you are so the route is recoverable later.

- **Structural event nearby (thud/settling):**
  - Back away from vertical faces and ceilings.
  - Expect delayed silt wave; prepare for sudden zero-vis.

- **Life support anomaly:**
  - Immediate return to entry point.
  - If return is impossible: go stationary, minimize exertion, start emergency comms pings, prepare for long wait (this is why you overpack reserves).

## Cultural/ethical handling (because the “seafloor” is made)

You’re walking on an artifact the size of a continent.

- Don’t pry stones, don’t widen openings, don’t “test” timber strength.
- Mark with removable clamps, not paint or etched lines.
- Favor non-contact mapping: sonar, photogrammetry, passive sampling.
- Disturbance lasts a long time here; your silt plume can remain like a scar.
