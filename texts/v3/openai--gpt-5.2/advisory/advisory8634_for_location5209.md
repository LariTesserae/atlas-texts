## What you’re walking into (mentally, before you pack anything)

Treat this place less like a “high-altitude industrial city” and more like **a worksite built on a mountain that hasn’t finished compiling**. Most dangers aren’t cinematic; they’re cumulative: hypoxia, slick stone, cableways, mis-measured distances, a stairwell that’s subtly buoyant, a fog basin that turns navigation into improvisation.

Your main preparation is not bravery, it’s **systems**:
- redundant navigation (because geometry lies),
- redundant illumination (because light lies),
- physical tethering (because “down” is final),
- hydration and respiration discipline (because air is thin and dirty),
- and social integration (because locals survive by procedure, signs, and dated maps).

If you arrive expecting consistency, you’ll make fast, dumb decisions.

---

## 1) Body prep (2–4 weeks out if you can)

### Acclimatization
The air is “tool-like” to breathe: cool, thin, and soot-streaked. Plan for:
- **48–72 hours of reduced exertion on arrival**.
- If you’re prone to altitude issues: talk to a clinician in your origin world about **acetazolamide** or your setting’s equivalent, and do a test run (some people hate the side effects).
- Train stairs/hills with a pack. This city is stairs, ladders, switchbacks, and catwalks.

### Strength you actually need
- **Grip + forearms** (railings, ladders, “don’t drop that tool”).
- **Ankles + balance** (slick rock, grated walkways, wind gusts).
- **Core stability** (sudden buoyancy corridors make you step “wrong” unless you’re stable).

### Vaccines / prophylaxis (practical, not exotic)
Given dense birds, damp moss, industrial grime:
- keep tetanus up to date,
- plan for respiratory irritation (soot + fog),
- assume cuts get infected fast if you don’t clean them.

## 2) Paperwork and social access (this place runs on dated maps and forms)

The city’s “bureaucratic calm” is a survival adaptation. Lean into it.

Bring:
- **multiple IDs** (some districts may not recognize others),
- a way to print or stamp **date + time on documents**,
- a **travel letter** stating where you’re staying and who can vouch for you,
- cash/credits in **small denominations** (fees change; arguing wastes oxygen).

Do not treat unofficial arrows and ladders as “secrets to exploit.” Treat them as **relationships made visible**. If you didn’t earn the arrow, don’t follow it.

## 3) Essential gear (what keeps an ordinary human alive here)

### A) Fall prevention and movement
This is non-negotiable.
- **Climbing-style harness** (comfortable enough to wear all day).
- **2 lanyards** (so you can remain clipped while re-clipping).
- **Carabiners rated for industrial loads** (not decorative).
- **Work gloves**: one dexterous pair + one warm waterproof pair.
- **Footwear**: aggressive tread, waterproof, ankle support.
- Add-ons depending on season: **microspikes** (slick mineral faces + mossy stone can be ice-like even when not frozen).

Also:
- a **small tool tether kit** (anything dropped may be unrecoverable; worse, it may hit someone a terrace below).

### B) Breathing and air quality
The smell profile you described—wet metal, coal smoke, mineral tang—means particulates.
- **P100/FFP3-class respirator** (or your world’s equivalent).
- Spare filters in sealed bags (fog saturates everything).
- Optional but wise: a **small emergency oxygen canister** for exertion spikes or rescue situations. Not for comfort—**for “I must climb 200 steps right now”**.

### C) Wet + cold management
You’re going to get damp repeatedly; staying damp is how hypothermia sneaks in.
- Layering system: **wicking base**, **insulating mid**, **waterproof shell**.
- Pack **dry bags** inside your pack.
- Two pairs of socks per day if you’re moving a lot.
- A compact **synthetic insulating layer** (synthetic still insulates when wet; down often doesn’t).

### D) Lighting that doubles as a reality-check
Locals use calibrated lanterns because light is diagnostic.
Bring:
- **Primary headlamp** (hands-free) with a known beam pattern.
- **Secondary handheld light** with a different optics design.
- **Chem lights / glow markers** (useful when beams behave strangely).
- A small **lux meter** (even a simple one) if you’re methodical; it’s a way to detect “my light is being cut by a plane” vs. “the bulb is failing.”

### E) Navigation that assumes geometry can drift
Do not rely on one reference.
- **Altimeter** (barometric) + a second alt reference (GPS-like if it works; if not, a calibrated pressure log).
- **Inertial/step counter** (simple pedometer works; the point is to notice discontinuities).
- **Chalk + reflective tape** for temporary marking (but don’t expect marks to remain spatially consistent—use them as *local breadcrumbs*, not universal truth).
- **Paper maps with dates**, stored flat and dry. Assume digital maps can become socially “wrong” even when technically correct.

### F) Water and food
Condensate is abundant but not automatically safe.
- **Water filter** capable of particulates + a chemical treatment (fog and industrial runoff can carry metals and microbes).
- High-calorie, low-bulk food. You’ll burn more just breathing and staying warm.

### G) Hearing protection
Not optional in an always-industrial soundscape.
- **Earplugs** + **over-ear protection** if you’ll be near presses, dynamos, cable stations.

### H) Medical and field repair
- Basic first aid + wound wash (fog + soot makes abrasions nasty).
- Blister care (constant damp).
- Anti-inflammatory, antihistamine (moss/fungal zones can be reactive).
- A small **repair kit**: zip ties, wire, tape, spare buckles, sewing needle—this city eats straps.

## 4) On arrival: your first 24 hours should be boring on purpose

### Do these immediately
1. **Acquire a dated local map** and learn how locals talk about space (terrace names, lift lines, “safe” stairwells).
2. Identify:
   - the nearest **clinic**,
   - the nearest **official transit spine** (elevators, main cableway platforms),
   - two **shelter points** (indoors, heated, staffed).
3. Buy/borrow a local **calibrated lantern** if your own lights behave “wrong” in testing.

### Don’t do these yet
- Don’t go exploring bridges into fog.
- Don’t chase “listening wall” information as if it’s actionable.
- Don’t go looking for under-plan access “just to see.” People disappear doing that casually.

## 5) Daily operating rules (how to not become a cautionary tale)

### Rule A: Clip in where locals clip in
If you see anchor points and people using them, use them. Falls here aren’t heroic; they’re administrative.

### Rule B: Treat fog basins like water hazards
If fog “has weight,” it will:
- soak you,
- hide drop-offs,
- swallow visual references,
- and make distance anomalies harder to detect.
If you must enter: go in tethered, timed, and with a buddy.

### Rule C: Don’t use sound as proof
Because sound transmits unpredictably and sometimes “wrong,” adopt this discipline:
- If you hear a person, confirm visually or by agreed signal.
- Don’t act on shouted warnings unless they match your immediate context.
- If you use radio: confirm messages with **call-and-response protocols** and location checks.

### Rule D: When gravity has “moods,” test before committing
Before you carry something heavy down a stairwell or cross a suspicious corridor:
- drop a small, safe test object on a line (or toss a bit of grit) and watch the fall,
- notice your own step feel: do you feel subtly lighter/heavier?
Locals routing heavy loads through buoyancy corridors is a hint: **those corridors exist**, and they’re useful until they aren’t.

### Rule E: Bridges are negotiations, not facts
Distance disagreement is a mind-trap. Use:
- time-to-cross + step count + visual checks.
If any two disagree strongly, assume the bridge is “active” today and pick an alternate route unless you have a local guide who says it’s fine *now*.

### Rule F: Respect the machine ecology
Bird colonies under bridges and in vents aren’t just gross; they change traction and corrode joints.
- Don’t grab handholds blindly.
- Don’t camp under warm exhaust outlets (animals and people both treat those as resources).

## 6) Where to sleep (and where not to)

Pick lodging that is:
- on a **stable shelf** with civic infrastructure nearby,
- inside a structure with **thick walls** (masonry older districts are often more stable and less drafty),
- not directly over/under major cable lines (constant vibration + noise + occasional “events”).

Avoid:
- edges of vertical basins,
- “convenient” warm caverns near turbines (great until maintenance purge cycles),
- anywhere a local calls “a little flexible” with a shrug.

---

## 7) How to interact with locals (this is safety equipment)

Locals live with slow, bureaucratic instability. They will value:
- **predictability**, **procedures**, and **dated information**.

Do:
- ask what map edition they trust this week,
- ask which stairwells are “rated” and which are “buoyant,”
- learn the local signage for: load limits, re-tension schedules, fog hazard, and corridor closures.

Don’t:
- mock shrines in drip-caves; those are often located where conditions are *consistently survivable* (stable moisture, less wind shear, good anchors). Even if you don’t share the belief, you can respect the siting intelligence.

## 8) Under-plan guidance (the thing that will tempt you)

Assume the under-plan is **a real hazard whether or not it is “real” geographically**.

If you insist on approaching it:
- only with a vetted local guide,
- on a pre-agreed route with a fixed turnaround time,
- tethered, with redundant lights,
- with someone topside tracking your departure and expected return.

Never take “the wrong elevator” on purpose without a recovery plan. In a city where measurement drifts, **“wrong” is not a cute adjective**—it’s a category of unrecoverable.

---

## 9) Emergency planning (simple, workable)

Have three thresholds:

1) **Stop-work threshold** (turn around):
- persistent dizziness, clumsy hands, unusual breathlessness,
- lights behaving discontinuously,
- sudden traction loss combined with wind.

2) **Shelter threshold** (get indoors now):
- you’re wet + cold and can’t get dry within 30 minutes,
- fog thickening to “liquid” with no clear edge,
- you lose two navigation references at once.

3) **Call-help threshold**:
- any fall arrest event,
- chest pain / confusion,
- you hear/see structural distress (new cracks, cable vibration out of pattern).

Carry a written card (waterproof) with:
- your lodging,
- emergency contacts,
- medical issues,
- and a simple “if found, do X” instruction set (locals may not share your assumptions).

## 10) The one piece of “weirdness hygiene” that matters most

In places where reality is “unfinished,” your biggest vulnerability is **letting curiosity override procedure**.

So adopt a rule:
- **If a phenomenon offers you a shortcut (shorter distance, easier climb, a magically convenient elevator), treat it like an uninspected machine.**  
Shortcuts here are often just inconsistencies wearing a helpful mask.
