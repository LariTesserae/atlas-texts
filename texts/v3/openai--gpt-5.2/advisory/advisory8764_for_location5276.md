## What you’re visiting (in practical terms)
This undercity district is not “a cave.” It’s an active, wet industrial organ-space: hot, saturated air; constant machine noise; intermittent fog; slick surfaces; open channels and sumps; overhead beams and catwalks; and dense, fearless fauna that treat light, airflow cycles, and condensation as their calendar.

Your main risks are mundane and cumulative rather than dramatic: heat stress, dehydration, slips/falls, electrical hazards around water, respiratory irritation (bioaerosols, dust, possible ammonia/ozone/oil mist), hearing damage, bites/contamination, and getting disoriented in pooled light and repeating geometry.

Treat it like entering a working utility plant that has partially rewilded.

## Preparation timeline

### 1–2 weeks before: health and baseline readiness
- **Vaccinations / prophylaxis (real-world analogs):**
  - Tetanus up to date.
  - Hepatitis A (and B if you expect contact with contaminated water or bites).
  - Consider rabies pre-exposure *if* bat contact is plausible (it often is in low, cluttered overhead spaces).
- **Medical reality check:** If you have asthma, heat intolerance, kidney issues, migraines triggered by noise, or skin conditions aggravated by humidity, plan accommodations or don’t go.
- **Fitness:** You don’t need “combat fitness,” you need *heat + humidity endurance* while wearing PPE. Practice walking 60–90 minutes in a warm environment with a pack, helmet, and respirator.
- **Allergy plan:** Insect fragments, molds, and biofilms can irritate. Bring antihistamines you’ve used before; don’t test new meds down there.

### 48 hours before: logistics and failure planning
- **Define a hard turn-back rule** (examples):
  - If you lose forced ventilation noise and can’t confirm airflow within 2 minutes, you leave.
  - If your primary light fails and backup light isn’t immediately available, you leave.
  - If you feel dizzy/nauseated or stop sweating in the heat, you leave.
- **Tell someone outside the universe/zone** your entry time, expected return, and a “no contact by X = initiate extraction” rule.
- **Prepare a decontamination plan** for returning: sealed bag for outerwear, wipe-down protocol, shower, laundering.

## Core hazards and how to counter them

### Heat + humidity (the slow killer)
**What it does:** You overheat without noticing; sweat doesn’t evaporate; electrolytes crash; judgment degrades.

**Countermeasures**
- Hydration: plan **0.5–1.0 L/hour** depending on exertion; bring **electrolytes**, not just water.
- Clothing: **synthetic wicking base layer**, quick-dry pants; avoid cotton (stays wet, chafes).
- Pace: “industrial pace,” not “explorer pace.” Move, stop, evaluate, move.
- Recognize heat illness early: headache, irritability, gooseflesh in heat, cramps, confusion, nausea.

### Water everywhere (slips + electricity + contamination)
**What it does:** Slick concrete, hidden holes, shallow trays that are deeper than they look, and water near cable runs/control boxes.

**Countermeasures**
- Footwear: **ankle-support boots** with **oil/chemical-resistant, slip-rated soles** (industrial standard). No hiking trail runners.
- Trekking pole or short staff: helps probe depth and slickness without committing your weight.
- Never step over a grate or puddle you haven’t visually cleared (edges hide drop-offs).
- Treat all pooled water as **contaminated** (oil sheen, biofilm, rodent urine risk).

### Noise (permanent damage + missed cues)
**What it does:** Pumps and fans will fatigue you and mask warning sounds; you won’t notice changes unless you deliberately listen for them.

**Countermeasures**
- **Hearing protection:** bring **earplugs + earmuffs** (double protection). Use at least plugs at all times.
- Build “listening stops” into your route: pause, reduce movement, and listen specifically for **changes** (a fan lagging, a belt whining up, a new hiss).

### Air quality (steam, aerosols, fumes, oxygen uncertainty)
**What it does:** Irritation at best; at worst, low oxygen or buildup of industrial gases in pockets.

**Countermeasures**
- **Gas monitor:** at minimum O₂ + CO + H₂S; ideally add VOC sensor. Clip it where it samples your breathing zone.
- **Respiratory protection:** a **half-face respirator** with combination cartridges (P100 + organic vapor/acid gas) is a good general solution for “hot wet industrial + biological” air. If you cannot tolerate a respirator in humidity, you should reconsider the visit.
- **Do not trust your nose** in constant humidity and machine smell; use instruments.

### Machinery + infrastructure (pinch points, moving parts, energized panels)
**What it does:** You’ll be tempted to step where crews step. But you’re not authorized maintenance.

**Countermeasures**
- **Do not touch** valves, breakers, control boxes, or tagged cable trays.
- Keep clear of belts, couplings, and any rotating shafts (even if guarded).
- Assume catwalk rails are degraded: test with body weight gradually.
- Avoid standing under the gantry crane path or any suspended load/hook.

### Fauna (not “monsters,” but vectors and startle hazards)
**What it does:** Bats can startle and collide; insects will swarm around light; rats will investigate food; amphibians/fish imply warm water ecology that can host pathogens.

**Countermeasures**
- **Light discipline:** avoid blasting bright light into rafters; use angled light and red mode when possible.
- **Insect strategy:** use **head net** if you can tolerate it with a helmet; otherwise keep skin covered and use repellent on clothing (permethrin-treated garments are effective; apply well before travel).
- **Food discipline:** sealed containers only; never eat with contaminated gloves; no loose snacks that attract rodents.
- **Bite protocol:** any bite/scratch is a “leave and treat” event.

## Essential kit (practical, not fantasy)

### Wearable PPE (don’t compromise here)
- Climbing/industrial **helmet** with chin strap (things drip; you’ll look up; overhead hazards exist).
- **Eye protection**: sealed or semi-sealed goggles (fog-resistant) > open glasses.
- **Respirator** (half-face) + spare cartridges in a sealed bag.
- **Hearing protection** (plugs + muffs).
- **Gloves**: nitrile inner + durable outer work gloves.
- **Boots**: slip-rated, chemical-resistant soles, good ankle support.
- **Knee pads** (optional but valuable; wet concrete is unforgiving).

### Lighting (assume shadows are physical)
- Primary **headlamp** (high CRI helps read wet surfaces).
- Backup headlamp.
- Handheld flashlight for “look around corners / down sumps.”
- Spare batteries in waterproof pouch.
- Small **glow marker / chem light** for emergency “this way back” marking (use sparingly; don’t litter).

### Navigation + marking (geometry repeats; light pools mislead)
- A simple **route line**: bright cord on a spool can be lifesaving in fog + repeating corridors (tie-offs at turns).
- Chalk or wax marker for **temporary** markings (avoid permanent paint).
- A basic compass is less useful underground; instead bring a **notebook** and map as you go: “Pump gallery → 3 pillars → cable tray junction → service plaza.”

### Monitoring + comms
- **Gas monitor** (O₂/CO/H₂S minimum).
- Waterproof **two-way radio** if you have a partner; otherwise a locator/beacon appropriate to your multiverse ruleset.
- A loud **whistle** (works when your voice doesn’t).

### Medical + decon
- Electrolyte packets; blister care; antiseptic; sterile dressings.
- Anti-diarrheal + oral rehydration salts (contamination happens).
- Tweezers, tick/insect removal tools (even underground, you can pick up hitchhikers).
- Heavy-duty trash bags or dry bags for contaminated clothing on exit.
- Alcohol wipes / chlorhexidine wipes for hands and small gear.

### Tools (keep it minimal; you’re not maintenance)
- Multi-tool, duct tape, zip ties (humidity-resistant if possible).
- Small pry bar only if you *expect* jammed doors—otherwise it tempts bad decisions.
- Rope/cord (already mentioned) is more useful than most tools.

## Arrival protocol (first 10 minutes determine whether you stay)

1. **Stop immediately on entry** and let your senses calibrate.
2. **Check instruments:** gas monitor stable? O₂ normal? Any alarms = leave.
3. **Set your “baseline sound.”** Listen carefully to the pumps/fans and mentally note the cadence. Later, changes are warnings.
4. **Confirm traction:** test the floor with a deliberate short shuffle; wet concrete varies wildly.
5. **Pick your first anchor point** for navigation (a unique pillar, a tagged panel, a distinctive pipe junction).
6. **Lock in your exit plan:** you should be able to point (not vaguely) to “the way back” at all times for the first segment.

## Movement and behavior inside

### How to walk this place
- Move **slowly enough to notice** condensation patterns. A surface that looks merely damp can be biofilm-slick.
- Keep **one hand free** (don’t carry everything in your hands; use a pack).
- Avoid stepping where the **shine** is strongest; that’s often the slickest film.
- When crossing grates or bridges: test with weight gradually, then cross without stopping.

### How to use light without becoming the ecosystem’s event
- Use the lowest effective brightness.
- Angle beams at the ground ahead; avoid sweeping the upper dark (you’ll provoke bats).
- Insect clouds concentrate near weak lamps—walk the edges of light pools, not through the center if you can.

### What not to do
- Don’t drink any water there, even if it looks “clean condensate.”
- Don’t sit on concrete (contamination + heat loss weirdness + you won’t want to stand again).
- Don’t open doors/panels “to see what’s inside.”
- Don’t follow animals. They know routes you cannot survive (tight ducts, electrified voids, sumps).

## Partnering and team rules (strongly recommended)
If you can, go as **two people**:
- One person watches footing; the other watches overhead and instruments.
- Agree on hand signals beforehand (noise makes speech unreliable).
- If one person removes respirator due to discomfort, both leave. Heat + humidity makes people do stupid, stubborn things.

## Contingencies you should rehearse mentally

### If the ventilation noise changes or stops
- Freeze, check gas monitor, feel for airflow (a ribbon/tape on your pack strap helps).
- If uncertain: **turn back immediately**. Stagnant pockets can become hazardous fast.

### If you lose light
- Don’t walk. Stop, deploy backup headlamp, then orient.
- If you drop a light into water: let it go. Don’t reach into unknown depth near cables.

### If you fall into a sump/channel
- Treat it as contamination + injury risk. Exit as soon as you can safely.
- Remove wet contaminated clothing only when you can bag it; avoid hypothermia-by-evaporation on exit routes with drafts.

### If you feel “sudden quiet”
- Quiet is information here. Assume something changed that every animal noticed first.
- Treat it like a fire alarm: regroup, check instruments, and leave unless you have a compelling, measured reason not to.

## Exit and aftercare (don’t bring the undercity home)

1. **Bag outer layers** before entering clean spaces.
2. **Wipe down hard gear** (helmet, lights, respirator exterior, boots).
3. Shower promptly; clean any abrasions.
4. Launder clothing hot; disinfect boots.
5. Monitor for 48–72 hours: fever, rash, respiratory symptoms, unusual fatigue, wound redness. Seek care early if anything develops.
