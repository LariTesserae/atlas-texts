## What you’re walking into (in human terms)

This is a **high-altitude continent** where the main hazards are not predators or temperature so much as **physics that stops cooperating with your habits**:

- **Altitude/thin air**: low oxygen, low pressure, low sound transmission, fast dehydration, rapid fatigue, impaired judgment.
- **Water is effectively “chemically unwelcome”**: moisture you bring is precious; moisture you spill may vanish into salts/stone rather than “stay wet.”
- **Dust is a climate system**: persistent abrasive particulate, migratory dust fronts, occasional large vortices, and static buildup.
- **Electrostatics/lightning without rain**: charge storms, creeping discharges along ridges and structures, ESD risk to you and gear.
- **Terrain is deceptively old and smooth—yet hollow**: collapse zones, vesicular rock, thin crust over voids, wide canyon “slots.”
- **Artifacts still doing something**: fields that bias your balance, levitate dust, bend wind fronts; unknown EM effects.

Your preparation should assume: **you can’t rely on water, you can’t rely on sound, and you can’t rely on “normal” weather intuition.** Treat it like a high-altitude polar desert crossed with an electrostatic industrial site.

## 1) Medical and physiological prep (do this before you pack)

### Altitude readiness
Even if you’re fit, thin air changes everything.

- **Get a medical clearance** if you have any history of: asthma/COPD, sleep apnea, pulmonary hypertension, arrhythmia, clotting disorders, migraines with aura, seizure disorders.
- **Acclimatization plan** (if your multiverse jump can stage):  
  - Spend 24–72 hours at “moderate high” altitude equivalent before full exposure, or  
  - Bring oxygen and treat the first 48 hours as “work capacity reduced by half.”

### Vaccines/pathogens
This environment sounds **low-biomass**, but not sterile. Your risk is more **microabrasion + strange dust + sealed cavities** than contagious disease.

- Be current on **tetanus**.
- Consider **pneumococcal** protection if you’re high-risk.
- Carry **broad-spectrum topical antibiotic** and proper wound irrigation supplies (you may not want to waste clean water on wounds unless you must—plan accordingly).

### Medications to pack (practical, not exotic)
- **Acetazolamide** (Diamox) if you tolerate it (altitude sickness prevention).
- **Dexamethasone** as emergency backup for severe AMS/HACE (with instructions).
- **Nifedipine** (or appropriate alternative) for HAPE risk only if you’ve been instructed—don’t improvise.
- **Saline nasal gel**, lip balm, eye lubricants: the dryness will punish mucous membranes.
- Pain control that won’t ruin judgment (avoid heavy sedatives).

## 2) Core survival principle: bring your own “sea level”

You want a personal microclimate: **pressure, oxygen, humidity, filtration, static control**.

### Respiratory system (non-negotiable)
You have three workable tiers:

1. **Best**: a **positive-pressure respirator/helmet** with:
   - O₂ supply (compressed or chemical),
   - CO₂ scrubbing (replaceable cartridges),
   - particulate filtration (P3/P100 equivalent),
   - **humidity recovery** (exhalation condenser).
2. **Acceptable**: tight-seal mask + O₂ + good filtration + goggles, but you will dry out fast and you’ll fight dust ingress.
3. **Risky**: no O₂, just filter. Only attempt if you’ve confirmed local partial pressure is safe and you can retreat rapidly.

Plan oxygen like you plan fuel: **assume double your estimate**. Thin air mistakes happen when you’re already impaired.

### Hydration strategy (the place will steal it)
- **Carry water in sealed bladders/bottles with dust-proof valves.**
- Use a **humidity-capture system** if you have one (even a simple heat-exchanger mask helps).
- Bring **oral rehydration salts**; dry air accelerates electrolyte loss.
- Do not rely on “finding water” or “melting something.” If any moisture exists, it’s likely chemically trapped or transient.

**Rule:** never open your main water reservoir in the open wind. Decant inside shelter or behind a windbreak.

## 3) Clothing and personal protection: abrasion + UV + static

### Base layer
- Moisture-wicking, but not “breathable mesh.” You want **controlled vapor loss**, not maximum ventilation.
- Choose fabrics that **don’t hold static** aggressively (some synthetics will make you a walking capacitor).

### Outer layer
- **Hard-shell, dust-resistant, abrasion-resistant** outer suit.
- Sealed cuffs, sealed zippers, gasketed closures.
- A hood/helmet interface that can handle **gust-driven grit**.

### Eyes/skin
- **Full goggles** with replaceable tear-offs or sacrificial lenses.
- **High-UV protection**: at altitude with clean air, UV is harsh even when it feels cold.
- Face and hand protection against both **sunburn** and **wind burn**.

### Footwear
- Aggressive sole with **ankle support**—but the real feature is puncture resistance and dust sealing.
- Bring **gaiters**; dust will migrate into everything.

## 4) Dust and electrostatics: treat it like an ESD lab in a sandstorm

### Dust management kit
- Soft brush, microfiber wipes, lens cleaning system.
- Sealable “clean bag” for filters, optics, connectors.
- Spare respirator filters; expect faster clogging than you think.

### Static control
- **Grounding strap** options when touching structures (even a simple resistive strap can prevent unpleasant surprises).
- **ESD-safe bags** for electronics.
- Avoid loose dangling metal that can become a discharge point on ridgelines.

### Lightning-without-rain behavior
When charge storms build:
- Get off high points and ridge crests.
- Avoid being the tallest isolated object.
- Avoid proximity to the artifact pylons/needles during active discharge periods—those look like intentional charge-handling infrastructure.

If you see faint crawling filaments along ridges, treat it as “already lightning-adjacent.”

## 5) Navigation and comms: don’t trust your senses

### Why normal navigation fails here
- **Sound doesn’t carry**, so you won’t hear danger.
- **Visibility can be enormous**, so distances look “walkable” when they’re not.
- Dust fronts can erase contrast and horizon cues.

### What to bring
- **Redundant positioning**: inertial nav + GNSS equivalent (if it works cross-reality) + physical dead reckoning.
- **Line-of-sight signaling**: strobes, laser beacon, reflective panels (sound signals are weak).
- **Short-range team comms** with throat mics (wind and masks ruin normal microphones).
- Markers that survive dust migration: **weighted flags, anchored reflectors, or rock cairns** (but cairns may be swallowed by migrating sheets).

### Simple rule
If you can see a feature “clearly,” assume it is **farther than it feels** and plan water/oxygen accordingly.

## 6) Shelter: you need a “still air island”

Wind here is less about noise and more about **continuous force + abrasive grit + dehydration**.

### Minimal viable shelter
- A **low-profile, bombproof tent** that can be anchored to rock (drill anchors / rock bolts / deadman anchors won’t work well in hollow crust).
- Double-entry dust lock (even improvised: vestibule + inner door).
- Groundsheet that can handle sharp mineral surfaces and won’t become a static generator.

### Best shelter option
A **portable microhab** (small pressure-capable bivy or hard shell) with:
- filtered intake,
- humidity management,
- the ability to close entirely during dust fronts.

### Where to camp (and where not to)
Camp:
- In the lee of large rock masses, but not directly beneath overhangs that may shed.
- On **solid-sounding rock** (test carefully) rather than crusty, vesicular surfaces.

Avoid:
- Ridge crests (charge + wind acceleration).
- Canyon lips (gust shear + misstep risk).
- Areas where dust will not settle (likely field effects or weird airflow).

## 7) Terrain hazards: “old” does not mean stable

### Hollow ground protocol
Because the ground can ring and hide voids:

- Use a **trekking pole or probe** constantly.
- Distribute weight; move like you’re on thin ice when the substrate sounds different.
- If a step produces a distinct resonance: back up, mark it, route around.

### Canyon and “slot” safety
- Treat canyon edges as **actively unstable** even if rounded.
- Wind shear near edges can be invisible and strong—clip into a line if you must work close.

### Falling is worse here than it looks
Thin air reduces drag; you don’t get the “slowing” you subconsciously expect from denser atmospheres in some kinds of falls, and rescue is harder because sound and signal don’t travel well.

## 8) Food and energy: your body will burn fuel faster

Cold + altitude + constant micro-effort against wind = higher caloric burn.

- Bring **dense calories**: fats, nut butters, hard cheeses (if stable), high-calorie bars that don’t crumble into dust.
- Pack food in **dustproof wrappers** you can open with gloves.
- If you’re using a stove: combustion may be unreliable in thin air—prefer **chemical heaters** or pressure-optimized burners.

---

## 9) Interacting with the artifacts: assume they’re active

Those towers/arrays are not “ruins” in the ordinary sense; they sound like **still-operating infrastructure**.

### Safety rules around structures
- Don’t touch anything bare-handed.
- Approach slowly; watch for:
  - hair/cloth rising (static),
  - dust levitating,
  - subtle balance bias (field gradients),
  - electronics behaving oddly (compass drift, sensor spikes).
- Keep a **minimum standoff distance** during charge activity.
- Never shelter *under* or *inside* something that looks like a conductor designed to bleed charge.

### Electronics discipline
- Carry at least one **fully non-electronic backup** for navigation and timekeeping.
- Keep critical electronics in ESD-safe storage when not in use.
- Expect intermittent sensor nonsense near arrays.

### If you feel “gravity has a preference”
That’s your warning sign. Leave the area and reassess from a distance. Field effects that merely feel odd can become dangerous fast when combined with fatigue and low oxygen.

## 10) Wildlife and biohazards: low frequency, high surprise

Most danger isn’t attack; it’s contamination or complacency.

- Don’t handle lichens/reeds with bare hands—unknown chemistry, potential irritants.
- Keep food sealed; “microthings” may be sparse but opportunistic.
- If you see ceramic-shard crawlers: observe, don’t chase. They’re likely tied to microhabitats near structures, and you don’t want to learn why.

## 11) Daily operating procedure (what a smart day looks like)

1. **Morning check** (before leaving shelter):
   - O₂ level, filter status, water count, comms test, GPS/INS check, storm/charge assessment.
2. **Travel in short blocks**:
   - 20–40 minutes movement, 10 minutes rest (altitude discipline).
3. **Hard turnaround time**:
   - Set it early. The landscape tricks you into overextending.
4. **Dust front protocol**:
   - If you see a migrating sheet approaching: stop early, shelter, seal up. Don’t “race it.”
5. **Evening maintenance**:
   - Brush dust from seals, clean optics, dry out inner layers inside controlled humidity, recharge power.

## 12) Emergency planning (assume rescue is not coming quickly)

### The big three emergencies here
1. **Hypoxia/altitude illness**
2. **Dust/charge storm exposure**
3. **Fall/collapse injury**

### Emergency kit essentials
- Compact **oxygen reserve** you never touch except emergencies.
- **Satellite/relay beacon** that can punch through (and a multiverse-compatible return anchor if your method supports it).
- **Splinting** supplies and wound closure.
- Eye irrigation ampoules (sealed) and a method to rinse grit without pouring your drinking water away.

### Decision rules (simple, lifesaving)
- If you develop confusion, ataxia, severe headache, or breathlessness at rest: **stop, oxygen, descend/leave**. Don’t debate it.
- If electrical activity becomes visible: **get low, get away from structures, shelter**.
- If your route crosses “hollow-sounding” ground repeatedly: **reroute**, even if it adds hours.

## 13) Mental preparation: the emptiness is part of the hazard

People make mistakes here because the environment is **quiet, clean-edged, and vast**, which produces a false sense of control. You’ll also lose the normal emotional feedback of weather (no roar, no rain smell, no thunder).

- Expect a kind of **sensory starvation plus constant low-level stress** (wind pressure, mask noise, breathing).
- Build routine to prevent drift: timed breaks, checklists, spoken logs.
- Travel with a buddy if at all possible; thin-air judgment failure is subtle.

## 14) Packing list (a practical baseline)

### Life support
- Positive-pressure respirator/helmet (preferred) + spare seals
- Oxygen supply + regulator + emergency reserve
- CO₂ scrubbers if using closed/assisted system
- Humidity recovery or at least nasal/airway moisture mitigation

### Protection
- Dustproof hard shell outer layer + goggles + tear-offs
- UV protection: sunscreen, lip balm, neck coverage
- Gloves (dexterous + insulated), gaiters, sturdy boots

### Shelter & tools
- Low-profile tent or microhab + rock anchoring kit
- Sleeping system for cold, dry conditions
- Probing pole, climbing line/harness if near canyons
- Dust cleaning kit, ESD-safe bags, grounding strap

### Navigation & comms
- Redundant nav (INS + whatever positioning works)
- Line-of-sight beacon, strobes/laser, reflective panels
- Throat mic comms, spare batteries/power bank

### Medical
- Altitude meds (as appropriate), wound care, eye irrigation
- Electrolytes, high-calorie rations

---

## 15) Final advice for an “ordinary human traveler”

If you prepare for **altitude + dryness + dust + static**, you’ll be ahead of most outcomes.

The failure mode here is not dramatic. It’s the slow, accumulating kind:
- you breathe a little faster,
- you drink a little less,
- you walk a little farther because it “looks close,”
- you touch a structure because it “seems inert,”
- you camp on ground that “feels solid enough.”

Build your trip around humility: **short ranges, strong shelter, redundant life support, and cautious distance from anything that shapes wind or charge.** This continent isn’t trying to kill you; it simply won’t adjust itself to include you.
