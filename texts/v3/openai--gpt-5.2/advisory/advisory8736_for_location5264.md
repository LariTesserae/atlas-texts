## Core reality check: what will get you in trouble here
This town is **cool, saturated, and alive**, built out of **load‑bearing plants** and **water-routing rootworks**. Most “surfaces” are either (a) slippery, (b) structural, (c) both, or (d) somebody’s food/fiber stock.

Your two biggest risks as an ordinary human visitor are:

1. **Injury + infection** from slips, cuts, punctures, bites, and constant wet exposure.
2. **Accidentally damaging infrastructure** (cutting/stripping the wrong plant, clogging a root-channel, stressing a living bridge) and becoming *the problem* everyone has to fix.

Prepare like you’re visiting a **high-elevation wet greenhouse city** with **primitive tooling, sophisticated horticulture, and zero tolerance for careless cutting**.

---

## 1) Body prep (before you cross)
### Vaccines / meds (bring if you can)
- **Tetanus** coverage is non-negotiable.
- Broad **antihistamine** (bites/stings are constant).
- **Antifungal cream/powder** (feet, groin, armpits).
- **Oral rehydration salts** (cool air hides dehydration; you’ll still lose water).
- **Anti-diarrheal + antibiotic backup** (new microbes + wet food handling is a classic travel failure).
- If you’re prone to asthma: humidity + smoke trapped under canopy can be rough—bring your inhaler.

### Conditioning
- Do at least a week of practice walking with a pack on **wet, uneven surfaces** if possible.
- Train your ankles: this place is springy, shifting, and full of surprise slopes.

---

## 2) Clothing: dress for cold damp, not rain
You’re not trying to stay perfectly dry (you won’t). You’re trying to stay **warm, unrotted, and unblistered**.

### What works
- **Base layer:** wool or high-quality synthetic that still insulates damp. Avoid cotton.
- **Mid layer:** light fleece or wool; easy to vent.
- **Shell:** a breathable rain layer *or* a waxed/oiled outer (locals use oiled cloth; it’s culturally “normal”).
- **Head/neck:** hood + a brim (drip management matters more than “rainproofness”).
- **Hands:** thin grippy gloves (wet cordage + wet vines will shred skin).
- **Legs:** tough quick-dry trousers; add **gaiters** to keep muck and small biters off ankles.

### Footwear (the most important choice)
You want:
- **Aggressive wet-grip soles** (think approach shoes / trail runners with sticky rubber).
- **Fast drainage** or fast drying; waterproof boots can turn into cold swamps.
- **Spare insoles and spare laces** (everything stays damp; rot happens).
- **Foot care kit** (see below).

### Foot care kit (don’t skip)
- Antifungal powder/cream
- Zinc oxide or anti-chafe balm
- Leukotape / blister pads
- A “dry set” of sleep socks stored in a sealed bag like it’s treasure

---

## 3) Pack and gear: assume everything wants to get wet and grow things
### Waterproofing strategy
Use layers:
- One **pack liner** (trash-compactor bag works in many realities).
- Multiple **drybags** inside (one for sleep, one for clothes, one for medical/electronics).
- A few **silica gel packs** for anything you truly need to keep dry.

### Tools that fit the local tech level (and won’t alarm people)
- Small **stainless needle + strong thread** (repairs; barter potential).
- **Cordage** you trust (but don’t tie into their load-bearing systems without permission).
- Compact **headlamp** (if you have it) but use discreetly; bright white light at night can spook animals and feel uncanny. A low-lumen warm mode is ideal.
- **Water container** that seals well; a wide-mouth bottle you can clean.
- **Filter + chemical backup** (tannins and organic load are high; filters clog—bring back-up tabs/drops).

### Sleep
Humidity will defeat “minimalist” sleep plans.
- **Hammock with bug net** is ideal *if* you can hang it without damaging living supports. Otherwise:
- **Ground mat** + a way to elevate your bedding from the floor.
- **Synthetic quilt/bag** performs better than down here (down can become a sad wet clump).

### Fire/cooking
Open flame inside a living, resinous, leaf-layered town is politically sensitive.
- Bring a **small contained stove** only if you’re confident you can use it without scorching plant material.
- Otherwise plan to **eat what they eat**, and contribute labor or trade instead of insisting on cooking privately.

---

## 4) Medical and hygiene: you’re managing wet skin, small wounds, and mold
### Minimum med kit for this environment
- Antiseptic (iodine/chlorhexidine)
- Sterile dressings + gauze + tape that sticks when damp (not all tapes do)
- Tweezers (splinters, thorns, insect parts)
- Small scissors
- Anti-itch/sting treatment
- Antifungal
- Pain relief
- Rehydration salts

### Hygiene realities
- Your clothes will never “fully dry.” Plan for **rotation**, not dryness.
- Wash feet daily. Dry them aggressively. Powder them.
- Keep nails trimmed: wet grime + scratches = infections.

### “Don’t import a plague” protocol (multiverse common sense)
Before entry and on departure:
- Clean soles, rinse gear, and **quarantine wet textiles** if you can.
- Avoid dumping foreign food scraps or plant matter; this place is an engineered ecosystem and can be destabilized by new seeds/spores.

## 5) Social preparation: learn the three plant-laws and act like a guest
These are not vibes; they are structural safety rules.

1. **Never ring-bark, strip, or “tidy up” a living support.**  
   If you need a handhold, use it gently; don’t carve it.
2. **Only take cuttings from marked donors.**  
   If it isn’t clearly offered, assume it’s load-bearing, water-bearing, or owned.
3. **Keep water-ways unblocked.**  
   Don’t dump grit, food, ash, or fibers into channels. Don’t wash greasy items into them.

### How to behave so people relax around you
- **Ask before tying** anything to vines/rails/bridges. Your “quick knot” might cinch a living cable the wrong way.
- Walk with **short steps** and keep weight centered—locals will read reckless footwork as a threat to the walkway.
- Speak a little louder than you’re used to; the environment is loud and everyone already does.
- If you’re offered a hood/collar of oiled cloth, take it. That’s local competence, not charity.

### What to bring as gifts/barter (high value, low disruption)
- **Salt** (often valuable in upland wet settlements)
- **Quality needles, fishhooks, awls**, small metal blades
- **Good cordage** or a demonstration of a useful knot that doesn’t bite living fiber
- **Waterproof oil/wax** or resin-compatible sealant
- **Bright dye**, if it doesn’t run in damp conditions

Avoid showing off high-tech unless you want to become a problem people gather around.

## 6) Navigation and safety in the streets
### Movement
- Expect **5-level vertical complexity**: something can pass above you while water moves below you.
- Keep one hand free. You’ll need it for rails, balance, or sudden courtesy gestures.
- Don’t run unless it’s an emergency; running on springy root-mat is how ankles and social trust get broken.

### Slips and falls: assume they will happen once
Pack so a fall doesn’t destroy you:
- Phone/critical items in a waterproof inner pocket.
- Knife/tools secured; dropped metal is hard to find in green wet density.
- Use tether cords for anything you cannot lose.

### Rain protocol
When the rain hits hard enough that conversation fails:
- Stop trying to “push through.” Find cover and let runoff settle.
- Watch the water: trickles can become calf-deep rushes fast on a saturated slope.
- After rain, assume **post-rain drip hazards** for hours (cold water down the neck drains warmth quickly).

---

## 7) Food and water: plentiful, but don’t get overconfident
### Water
Even if it looks clean:
- Springs and seep-lines pick up microbes from the root-mat.
- Tannin-dark pools hide what’s in them.
Filter + treat if you’re not drinking the exact same sources locals drink *with local guidance*.

### Food
This place grows abundance; the danger is unfamiliarity.
- Start with small portions.
- Favor cooked foods early on; raw wet greens can be rough on an unadapted gut.
- Watch where drying racks are—food theft by birds/rodents is a real issue, and being blamed for it is easy if you’re careless.

---

## 8) Wildlife and insects: you’re not the main character here
### Insect defense that actually works in cool humidity
- Physical barriers beat chemicals: **netting, long sleeves, gaiters**.
- Repellent helps, but sweat/wet dilutes it. Reapply on a schedule.
- Check for leech-like or tick-like hitchhikers if the local ecology supports them (it often does in saturated uplands).

### Around larger animals
- Don’t approach herd pens without permission. Living thorn enclosures have “gates” you can break by pulling the wrong binding.
- If the outer hedge shivers and locals pause: **you pause too**. Copy their stillness and wait for cues.

---

## 9) What to practice before you arrive (skills > gear here)
- **Basic knots** that hold without biting into wet fiber (and how to untie them fast).
- **Blister management** and damp-clothing rotation.
- **Quiet observation**: learn which paths are “main arteries” vs. delicate service ways.
- **Non-damaging load carrying**: basket etiquette, shoulder yokes, or how to keep your pack from snagging vines.

If you can do one thing well, learn to **repair cordage and weave/patch matting**. It’s universally useful, socially legible, and a good way to “pay rent.”

---

## 10) Arrival plan (first 2 hours) for a smooth integration
1. **Stop at the edge** and wait to be noticed rather than pushing in.
2. Ask for:
   - where to walk,
   - where *not* to walk,
   - where to stow wet gear,
   - and which water is acceptable for visitors.
3. Offer a small gift (salt/needle/cord) and a willingness to do a simple task.
4. Change into your “town footwear” if you have one—clean soles signal respect for their living floors and water systems.
5. Learn the nearest:
   - dry(ish) communal spot,
   - healer,
   - and a covered route that stays passable during hard rain.

---

## 11) Emergency thresholds (know when to stop being brave)
Leave the streets and get help if you have:
- A deep puncture (thorn/bite) in constantly wet conditions
- Fever, spreading redness, or red streaks (infection moves fast when you can’t keep wounds dry)
- Numb, pale fingers/toes (cold + wet + immobility)
- Persistent diarrhea (dehydration sneaks up in cool air)

In this environment, “I’ll walk it off” is a bad philosophy.

---

## 12) The mindset that makes the trip work
Treat the town as a **living machine**: elegant, maintained, and easy to damage if you assume it’s inert. Move slowly, ask before you cut or tie, keep water paths clean, and manage your own wet-body problems proactively.

If you do that, you’ll read to locals as competent rather than fragile—and competence is the real passport in a place like this.
