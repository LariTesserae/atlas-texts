## What this place is like (from a human-body perspective)
You’re entering a *maintained* subterranean pocket: warm, extremely dry, electrically charged dust, constant machinery vibration, dim sodium/green light, and an ecology that treats moisture as a disruptive chemical event. Expect:

- Rapid dehydration of eyes/airways/skin (sweat vanishes; lips crack fast).
- Fine dust + metal/salt particulates + solvent/rubber tang (respiratory/chemical irritation).
- High static buildup and unexpected discharges (you, your gear, and the dust all want to spark).
- Continuous noise and vibration (fatigue, poor communication, impaired judgment).
- Sharp, young mineral edges overhead and underfoot.
- Dense small fauna that treat surfaces (including you) as just more terrain.

Your goal is to **stay sealed, stay grounded, stay dry externally, and don’t perturb the chamber’s equilibrium**.

---

## Primary hazards to plan for
### 1) Desiccation (fast, sneaky)
- Eyes: gritty pain, contact lenses become a liability.
- Airways: dry cough, nosebleeds, headache.
- Skin: cracking, electrostatic “sting,” microtears.
- Performance: dehydration without feeling sweaty.

**Countermeasure:** sealed eye/respiratory protection + disciplined hydration inside the mask/suit.

---

### 2) Dust + salts + metal fines (lungs, electronics, moving parts)
That “static gravity” dust will cling to fabric, seals, fans, optics, and connectors. Salt plates mean any moisture becomes crust and glue.

**Countermeasure:** particulate filtration, smooth outer layers, protective caps over ports, no exposed lubricated mechanisms.

---

### 3) Electrostatic discharge (ESD) and electrical-field–sensing fauna
Static buildup is not just uncomfortable; it can:
- kill unprotected electronics,
- ignite volatile contaminants if present,
- attract/interest ribbon-thin cable-tray predators that “taste” fields.

**Countermeasure:** conductive outer layer + grounding discipline + intrinsically safe electronics choices.

---

### 4) Noise/vibration load (cognitive risk)
Factory-steady bass + fan rasp + biological clicking = poor situational awareness and faster fatigue.

**Countermeasure:** hearing protection that still allows comms + time-boxed excursions.

---

### 5) Sharp geology + overhead “teeth”
Fresh fracture faces, knifed stalactites/pipes, glassy breaks. Cuts will dry fast and crack open again.

**Countermeasure:** hard hat, face shield or goggles, puncture-resistant gloves, high-ankle boots.

---

### 6) Atmospheric unknowns (even if “equilibrium held”)
Scrubbers keep it stable, not necessarily human-optimal. Expect possible:
- lowered humidity (certain),
- odd VOCs/solvent-like compounds,
- unusual O₂/CO₂ balance,
- fine aerosols.

**Countermeasure:** bring your own breathable air (best) or at minimum a serious respirator + gas monitor.

## Best-practice kit (ordinary human, serious environment)

### A) Breathing and eye protection (do not compromise here)
**Preferred:** light sealed suit or coverall + **PAPR** (powered air-purifying respirator) with:
- P100/HEPA particulate filters
- combination cartridge for organic vapors/acid gases if available  
Plus **sealed goggles** (or full-face PAPR).

**Better still (if you can):** a **small SCBA/closed-circuit rebreather** for short excursions. This avoids depending on the chamber’s composition and keeps your exhaled moisture contained.

**Avoid:**  
- contact lenses  
- cloth masks  
- anything that vents moist breath freely into the chamber (it will crust nearby surfaces and may draw grazers)

---

### B) Clothing (outer layer should be smooth, conductive, and cleanable)
- Base layer: moisture-wicking, long-sleeve, full length.
- Mid layer: light insulation (it’s warm, but constant airflow can chill).
- Outer: **anti-static/conductive coverall** (ESD-rated) with tight cuffs.
- Boots: puncture-resistant sole + high ankle; prefer smooth uppers (dust sheds better).
- Gloves: nitrile inner + cut-resistant outer.

**Add:** barrier cream for hands/face (non-water-based if possible), and **saline-free lubricating eye gel** (lipid-based if you can find it).

---

### C) Hydration without “introducing water”
You still need water; you just can’t *spill* it.

- Use a **hydration bladder with bite valve** routed inside your respirator/suit.
- Pack **electrolyte tablets** (dry) to prevent cramps/headache.
- Bring **water gel packs** or thickened water (less splash risk) if your setup allows.

**Rule:** no open cups, no pouring, no “quick rinse.” Treat water like a reactive chemical.

---

### D) Lighting and navigation
Light “pools” and colors lie. Bring your own reference illumination.

- Headlamp with **neutral white** + **red mode** (doesn’t blow out vision).
- Secondary handheld light.
- **Reflective markers** or IR beacons for return route through the drilled throat.
- Chalk is a bad idea here (it becomes food/dust). Use **tape tabs** or clip-on markers.

---

### E) Noise and comms
- High-NRR ear protection **plus** in-ear comms or throat mic.
- Agree on hand signals (noise may drown voice).
- Set timers: e.g., 20–30 minute working blocks, then a quiet check-in.

---

### F) Tools and electronics (ESD + dust discipline)
- Put sensitive electronics in a **Faraday pouch** when not in use.
- Use sealed connectors; keep caps on.
- Avoid spark-producing tools.
- Prefer manual tools with dry bearings (dust will kill lubricated hinges fast).
- Bring a small **soft brush** and dry wipes for seals/visors.

---

### G) Medical and contingency
- Wound closure strips + antiseptic wipes (minimal liquid) + occlusive dressing.
- Nasal gel (non-aqueous if possible).
- Pain relief, antihistamine (unknown biofilms).
- **Gas meter** (O₂/CO₂/VOCs) if you’re not on independent air.
- Emergency bailout: compact escape respirator or spare cartridges.

## Approach protocol (what to do, in order)

### 1) Stage at the tunnel mouth
Before you enter the “service throat,” do a full check:
- Filters seated, seals good, hydration connected, lights tested.
- All ports closed; nothing dangling that can snag.
- Establish a **hard turn-around time** based on air supply + fatigue.

### 2) Enter grounded and slow
Static rises with friction and speed.
- Walk, don’t shuffle.
- Touch a grounded metal point *with a gloved hand* before you handle sensitive gear.
- Keep one hand free for balance; the floor mosaic will include slick salt and sharp basalt.

### 3) Treat every surface as “active”
- Don’t lean on pillars: scrubber boxes and cables may be part of a controlled system.
- Don’t touch fluorescing veins/films/lichen: could be chemically active, could be food-web base, could be mildly caustic.

### 4) Move like you’re in someone else’s factory
Because you are.
- Stay out of cable trays and junction clusters (predators + electrical anomalies).
- Avoid stepping onto salt plates that sound hollow; something moves beneath.
- Watch overhead: droppings are dry, but impacts can startle flyers into swarms.

### 5) Control your moisture output
- Keep suit/respirator sealed.
- Don’t remove mask to speak.
- If you must open anything wet (medical, hydration repair), do it inside a containment bag.

### 6) Don’t “improve” anything
This chamber feels stable because it is regulated.
- Don’t turn knobs, toggle breakers, or “test” indicator lights.
- Don’t block airflow from fans/ducts.
- Don’t relocate crates/nesting shelves; they may be load-bearing ecologically (and you don’t want the replacements—animals—to relocate onto you).

## Wildlife etiquette (how not to become the event of the day)
- Grazers scraping films: generally ignore you unless you leave salts/oils they like.
- Hanging membrane-winged creatures: disturbance = mass movement overhead.
- Under-plate movers: don’t stomp; don’t pry plates; vibration is their map.
- Ribbon-thin cable predators: minimize EM novelty; keep radios low power when possible, avoid draping powered cables.

**If surrounded by grazers:** freeze, then back out slowly. Sudden motion triggers ripple-predator behavior.

**If something investigates your gear:** resist the urge to swat. Brush it off gently with a tool; you don’t want chitin fragments in your seals.

## What not to bring
- Open water containers, spray bottles, humidifiers.
- Cotton clothing (dust retention, static, poor seal behavior).
- Duct tape on primary seals (adhesive becomes dust-magnet).
- Unsealed food (crumbs become persistent contamination).
- Spark tools, disposable lighters, anything with exposed heating coils.

## Emergency responses (simple rules)
### If you feel “air hunger,” dizziness, or confusion
1) Stop moving.  
2) Check gauge/flow and buddy status.  
3) Switch to bailout/escape air if available.  
4) Leave immediately—don’t “finish the task.”

### If you breach a seal (tear, cracked visor, filter failure)
- Clamp/patch *dry* (tape/patch kit), then exit.
- Don’t attempt a careful repair inside the chamber unless you have containment and spare seals.

### If you spill water
- Do not smear it.  
- Cover with absorbent pad (inside a bag if possible), remove the pad sealed.  
- Expect grazers to arrive; withdraw and reroute.

### If electronics begin to flicker near cable runs
- Power down nonessential devices.
- Increase distance from junction boxes and trays.
- Ground yourself before touching anything metal.

## Decontamination and exit discipline
You don’t want to export this ecology (or import your moisture and microbes).

At the tunnel mouth (or a designated transition area):
- Dry-brush dust off suit seams, boots, and packs.
- Bag outer layer if possible.
- Wipe seals and visor edges with dry wipes.
- Keep any “wet waste” sealed until you’re fully out.

Once back in a normal environment:
- Rehydrate slowly; your thirst may lag behind your deficit.
- Rinse eyes (then), treat cracked skin, and monitor for respiratory irritation over 24–48 hours.

## Mental preparation (this matters here)
This chamber loops—sound, motion, feeding rings, repeating routes. Humans can get complacent fast in “stable” spaces.

- Use checklists.
- Rotate tasks to prevent attention drift.
- Maintain an explicit navigation anchor (markers + timed return).
- Assume the system notices disturbances even if it doesn’t react immediately.
