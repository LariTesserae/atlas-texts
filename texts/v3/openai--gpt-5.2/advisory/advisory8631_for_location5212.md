## What you’re walking into (so your prep makes sense)

This is not “an ocean with weird architecture.” It’s an industrial plant the size of a sea, flooded on purpose (or flooded and still being run), with **managed currents, active intakes/outflows, persistent vibration, high clarity, and hard edges everywhere**. The biggest dangers to an ordinary human aren’t monsters; they’re:

- **Getting pulled into flow** (intakes, gates, spillways, duct-throats, current accelerators)
- **Disorientation in vast open volume** (no shoreline, few landmarks, deceptively clear long sightlines)
- **Mechanical hazards** (moving parts, swinging debris, pressure doors, trapped corridors)
- **Physiology** (temperature over time, CO₂ buildup, pressure/ear issues, fatigue)
- **Contamination** (industrial fines, metal salts, lubricants, microbial films)
- **Noise/vibration stress** (not ear-splitting—bone-deep, constant, fatiguing)

Prepare like you’re doing a long-duration dive inside a working hydroelectric/water-treatment megastructure, not like you’re visiting a reef.

## 1) Before you go: information you want in hand

### A. Find the “operating schedule” equivalent
You described procedural tides and shift-changes. You want, at minimum:

- **Flow direction timetable** (when it reverses, when it strengthens)
- **Known high-risk intervals** (valve actuations, flush cycles, aerator ramp-ups)
- **Restricted zones** (intakes, turbine halls, pressure gates, service corridors)
- **Emergency dump behavior** (what happens if the system “breathes hard”)

If you can’t obtain it, assume there *is* a schedule and treat any unexplained change as a precursor to a larger one.

### B. Entry/exit plan that doesn’t rely on “surface”
Do not assume you can simply ascend to air. This place reads like it may have a “ceiling of water” with structures above it.

Have at least two independent exits:
- **Primary insertion/extraction point** (beaconed)
- **Secondary extraction** (a different district, different current basin)

### C. Map style that matches the environment
A normal seafloor map won’t help much. You want an **infrastructure map**:
- towers/pylons as “city blocks”
- conduits as “roads”
- basins as “sinkholes”
- gates as “chokepoints”

If you can’t get a map, bring tools to build one as you go (see navigation gear below).

## 2) Physical prep (ordinary human constraints)

### A. Dive fitness + panic-proofing
This environment is psychologically tricky: huge empty volume + constant industrial hum + no natural up/down cues near big walls.

Train/practice:
- **mask-off drills**, regulator recovery, buoyancy control while distracted
- **low-visibility/tactile navigation** (even if the water is clear, shadows behind bulkheads won’t be)
- **current crossing technique** (ferry angles, grabbing points, avoiding exhaust jets)

### B. Ears/sinuses and pressure strategy
Even if overall depth isn’t extreme, you may pass through **pressure gradients** near gates, ducts, spillways. If you can’t equalize reliably, don’t go.

### C. Exposure tolerance
“Cool basement cool” becomes hypothermia if you’re in it long enough. Plan for **hours**, not minutes.

## 3) Life support: choose a configuration that survives a machine-sea

### A. Breathing system: prioritize reliability + low bubble signature
- **Closed-circuit rebreather (CCR)** is ideal if you’re trained—quiet, efficient, less likely to attract animals and less likely to get noticed by any automated monitoring.
- If you’re not CCR-trained, use **redundant open-circuit** (twinset or sidemount) with **at least one independent bailout**. Don’t go single-tank.

Bring:
- CO₂ monitoring (if CCR)
- redundant pressure gauges/computer
- a conservative plan for exertion (currents can force heavy work)

### B. Buoyancy and propulsion
- **Wing BCD** or equivalent with redundant bladder if possible
- **DPV (underwater scooter)** with a tow leash and hard kill switch; but treat it as optional—currents + intakes can turn a scooter into a bad decision fast.
- Gloves and sturdy boots: almost everything is sharp, abrasive, or fouled.

### C. Comms
Sound carries “too well,” but that doesn’t mean intelligible voice comms will work.

Best practical stack:
- **through-water comms** (if available) for team coordination
- **line-pull / light-signal protocols** as fallback
- **buddy tether** only in specific scenarios (see below); in strong currents a tether can become a hazard

## 4) Navigation: treat it like a three-dimensional industrial maze

### A. Don’t trust “clarity” to keep you oriented
Clear water can trick you into thinking you can always see your way back. Then you pass a tower, a shadow zone, a flow line, and everything looks the same.

Bring:
- **inertial/compass navigation** (digital compass + heading discipline)
- **distance logging** (DPV odometer or swim-time estimates)
- **deployable breadcrumb system**:
  - primary reel/spool with high-visibility line for entering any corridor-like space
  - line arrows/markers (directional)
- **acoustic transponder beacon** at your base/exit (homing capability is a major safety multiplier)

### B. Rules for entering “corridors”
Anything that looks like a canyon may be a service corridor that can become a tunnel of accelerating flow.

Hard rules:
- Never enter a corridor/gate without a **continuous guideline**
- Never pass a point where you can’t turn around against the current with your remaining gas
- If you feel flow increasing, assume it can increase more—retreat early

## 5) Mechanical and current hazards: how not to become feedstock

### A. Intakes/outflows are the apex predators here
They may be silent until you’re close enough that it’s too late.

Watch for:
- unnatural straight “rivers” in the water (fast laminar sheets)
- particulate being pulled in like smoke into a vent
- vibration increasing as you approach
- schooling fish “pinning” to bars or suddenly avoiding a zone

Carry:
- **current meter** or at least streamers you can drop to visualize flow
- **two cutting tools** (line cutter + shears/knife). You want redundancy because entanglement here can be metal straps, cables, nets, peeling paint sheets, biofilm ropes—things that laugh at one blade.

### B. Cavitation zones
That “gravel in a blender” sound is a warning. Cavitation can:
- shred soft tissue
- disorient you
- destroy small equipment

If you hear/feel it intensifying, angle away immediately—don’t “go look.”

### C. Swinging debris and “alive” structures
Chains, loose panels, ladder sections: treat them like moving animals. Keep distance; don’t rely on them as handholds.

## 6) Light and visibility: you still bring your own

Even if it’s noon-forever, shadows behind bulkheads are real.

Bring:
- **primary dive light** (wide beam for situational awareness)
- **backup light**
- **marker strobes** for rendezvous points
- avoid laser pointers unless you know there’s no sensor/security that keys off them

## 7) Environmental health: contamination and biohazard precautions

This place has “industrial dust” in the water column and settled fines.

Minimum precautions:
- **full-face mask** or at least good mouth/eye protection (keeps fines out, reduces infection risk)
- **post-dive rinse/decon plan** (fresh water wash, mild surfactant; don’t grind grit into seals/zippers)
- treat any cut as contaminated: **immediate cleaning + antiseptic + seal**
- be wary of **heavy-metal exposure** if you’ll be there repeatedly (bloodwork is a real-world-style precaution)

Don’t eat anything you catch here. Don’t assume “clear water = clean water.”

## 8) Interacting with fauna (practically)

Most animals here are habituated to structure and flow. Your risk profile is more “collision and curiosity” than “attack,” but there are exceptions.

- Avoid wearing dangling gear; it invites investigation.
- If large predators key on choke points, **do not linger at gates/narrow passages**.
- If seals/analogues are present, expect close passes; keep your hands in, don’t chase, don’t corner them against walls.

## 9) Human factors: the place is designed to fatigue you

### A. The hum is stress
That constant low-frequency vibration can elevate anxiety and exhaustion. Plan for:
- shorter work periods
- explicit check-ins with your buddy
- a hard “turn time” even if everything seems fine

### B. The scale causes bad decisions
You’ll think, “It’s just over there,” and then you’ll spend 40 minutes crossing empty water against a schedule-shift current.

Use disciplined navigation:
- objective waypoints
- time boxed legs
- turn pressures/turn times that you do not negotiate with yourself

## 11) Operating procedure once you arrive (a safe default)

1. **Do a 10–15 minute “listening hover”** near your insertion point: learn the baseline hum, identify dominant flow direction, watch particulate drift.
2. **Drop a beacon** (or establish a hard reference line) immediately.
3. Travel **structure-to-structure**, not through open water, unless you have a clear reason and a timed leg.
4. Treat every narrowing as a potential gate: slow down, check flow, check exit.
5. When you detect a schedule shift (flow tightening, tone change), **stop exploring and move to a safe lee** (behind a large pylon, away from corridors) until it stabilizes.
6. Leave with generous margins. Your biggest enemy here is “one more look.”

---

## 12) Ethical/low-impact note (also practical)

Don’t unbolt, pry open, or “see what’s inside” sealed hatches. In a managed sea, a curious human can become the cause of:
- a pressure event
- a contamination event
- an automated response (lockdowns, flush cycles)

Assume the system is still operating, even if it looks damaged.
