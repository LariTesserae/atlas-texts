## What you’re walking into (so you pack the right instincts)

This district is a **working city made of trained living systems**: load-bearing groves, root-walkways, valve-like facades, and “quiet machines” cushioned inside plant casings. The main risks aren’t dramatic predators or cliffs; they’re **misread geometry, damp-cold exposure, hidden moving machinery, slow biological “responses,” and getting disoriented in open-but-interrupted sightlines**.

Treat it like entering an **industrial garden with municipal rules**—rules that are enforced by growth patterns rather than signs.

## Pre-departure prep (do before you jump)

### 1) Biosecurity: arrive clean, leave cleaner
This place sounds stable and curated. The fastest way to become a problem is to introduce outside fungi, seeds, mites, or bacteria.

- **Decontaminate footwear**: scrub treads; wipe with 70% isopropyl or a quaternary disinfectant; let dry.
- **Launder outer layers** and remove burrs/seedheads from seams and Velcro.
- **Pack a “clean kit”**: nitrile gloves, alcohol wipes, small trash bags, a sealable “dirty gear” sack.
- **Do not bring living plants/soil**, herbal teas with whole seeds, compostable items, or untreated wood products.

### 2) Health: assume spores, damp cold, and mild hypoxia-by-stillness
Even if air is normal, the combination of shade + constant evaporation + fungal management strips can bother lungs/skin.

- If you have asthma/allergies: **bring your inhaler and antihistamines**.
- Pack a **P100 (or FFP3) respirator** for basins/compost courts/foggy mornings.
- Bring **antifungal cream** (clotrimazole) and **blister care** (moleskin/hydrocolloid).
- Make sure tetanus is current.

### 3) Navigation: “flat” doesn’t mean “straight”
Your eyes will keep expecting a horizon; growth keeps interrupting it. Fog and repeating courtyards can make you loop.

- Bring **a compass** and **a notebook map** you can update by hand.
- Bring **two independent lights** (headlamp + handheld). Dew makes everything go matte; contrast drops.
- Don’t rely on GPS unless you know it works cross-reality. If you use electronic nav, treat it as “bonus.”

## Clothing & personal gear (optimized for cool damp + living surfaces)

### On-body
- **Boots**: waterproof or highly water-resistant, good tread, ankle support. Root decks can be springy and slick with dew.
- **Socks**: wool/synthetic, plus one spare pair in a dry bag.
- **Pants**: abrasion-resistant, quick-dry (roots, woven mats, mineral plates will scuff).
- **Layers**: breathable base + midlayer fleece; **a shell** for intermittent rain/fog drift.
- **Gloves**: thin work gloves. You’ll want hand protection without losing dexterity around bark slits, metal edges, and vine fibers.
- **Eye protection**: clear glasses if you’re near filter-plants or soot-scrape areas.

### In pack
- **Respirator** (P100/FFP3) + spare filters if you’ll be there more than a day.
- **First aid**: include antiseptic, bandage wrap (for ankle tweaks), tweezers, antihistamine, oral rehydration salts.
- **Water kit**: 1–2L capacity + **filter + chemical backup** (some cisterns may be wax-lined and “clean,” but don’t assume potability).
- **Food**: bring your own. Don’t forage; engineered guild-plants can be chemically weird, and “industrial compost courts” are not picnics.
- **Dry bags**: dew and fog will damp everything eventually.

## Tools: what to bring—and what *not* to

### Bring (non-destructive, low-impact)
- **Soft measuring tape** (for clearances if you’re moving gear).
- **Small brush** (to remove mineral dust/soot gently).
- **Mechanical pencil + waterproof notebook** (ink can run in constant damp).
- **A few inert wedges/shims** (rubber/wood-free synthetic) if you must stabilize something briefly.

### Avoid
- **Open flames** (even banked furnaces exist; traditions around firebreaks suggest fire risk is taken seriously).
- **Cutting tools used casually** (machetes, hatchets). In a city like this, cutting a “beam” might be cutting a load path.
- **Sprays** (bug spray, perfumes, solvents) near living facades; volatile compounds may trigger closure/response or contaminate filters/cisterns.
- **Chalk marking** on living surfaces. If you must mark, do it on your own tape/flagging—and remove it when you leave.

## Arrival protocol (first 30 minutes matter)

### 1) Pause at the boundary
The edge is “a change in habit.” Use it like an airlock.

- Check boots and pack for burrs/seeds again.
- Put on your **baseline layer** (shell accessible, respirator accessible).
- Do a quick listen: note where pumps, presses, or furnaces are operating. Sound is muted; your first map is auditory.

### 2) Let the city “read you” before you push into it
This place seems to regulate clearance and support. Act like you’re entering an occupied workshop.

- Walk **slower than you think you need to**.
- Don’t lean your full weight on living railings until you’ve tested them.
- If a route subtly narrows or arches, **accept the hint** and choose another line rather than forcing through.

### 3) Keep your gear off the walls
You noted: if you leave tools against living walls, they won’t be swallowed—but they’ll be **contoured and stabilized**. That sounds convenient until it isn’t.

- Use your own stand, hook, or ground cloth.
- If you must set something down: choose **mineral plate** areas, not living mat.

## Moving safely through the district

### Footing rules
- **Living mat**: springy, reliable—until saturated with dew. Expect slip risk in the morning.
- **Mineral plates**: quiet, stable, but edges are softened; you can catch a toe where rootwork has lifted a lip.
- **Root decks**: treat like suspended walkways. They can flex; don’t run; keep loads centered.

### Fog and basin behavior
Basins are colder, heavier air, different plants, and likely more fungal management.

- In basins: consider respirator; keep skin covered; avoid sitting directly on growth.
- If fog thickens: stop, take a bearing, and **don’t chase “the straight way.”** Straight lines here are a trap.

### Machinery etiquette (this is an industrial site)
- Assume pumps/presses can start without warning.
- Don’t put fingers into bark slits or viewing apertures.
- Give flywheels and counterweights wide clearance; damped sound makes danger feel far away when it’s not.

## Social/behavioral prep (even if no one is visibly there)

Even if humans aren’t present, the place reads like it has **maintenance protocols**. Act as though you’re being observed by a slow, nonverbal city authority.

- **Don’t break living surfaces** unless it’s an emergency.
- **Don’t block airflow lanes**: those “gaps of air” feel intentional, possibly part of pollination/venting.
- If something seems to be “asking” you to go around (vines not reaching across a path; a facade tightening), comply. Fighting it is how you get snagged, misrouted, or marked as a problem.

A useful mindset: you’re not in wilderness; you’re in **a cathedral workshop** where the walls breathe.

## Water & food: safe habits

### Water
- Condensation ribs and pocket cisterns may be part of a closed civic cycle. Treat them as infrastructure, not freebies.
- If you take water:
  - Prefer **flowing thread-streams right after rain** (less stagnation) and filter anyway.
  - Avoid cisterns that smell sweet/mineral unless you can verify they’re meant for drinking.
- Don’t contaminate: never wash hands/gear in cisterns.

### Food
- Bring shelf-stable food. If you must eat onsite:
  - Eat on mineral plates or your ground cloth.
  - Pack out all crumbs and wrappers; decomposition is “managed,” and you don’t want to introduce new nutrient patterns.

## What can hurt you here (the realistic hazard list)

1. **Hypothermia-by-damp**: cool shade + sweat + fog = slow chilling. Layer early, not late.
2. **Slips/ankle turns**: dew on living mat/root decks, softened plate edges.
3. **Hidden pinch points**: machinery in plant housings; motion is quiet.
4. **Respiratory irritation**: fungal strips, filter-plant soot deposits, mineral dust.
5. **Getting routed**: repeating courtyards, interrupted sightlines, fog.
6. **Accidental “entanglement”**: not malicious—just the city stabilizing objects (including straps, ropes, loose fabric) that rest where they “shouldn’t.”

## Emergency planning (what to decide before you need it)

### Establish a “clean exit point”
Pick a specific spot at the boundary as your return anchor. In a place with subtle repetition, “the way back” can feel different.

- Mark it with something you **carry back out** (a tripod, a bright cloth on your own pole), not something you tie to the city.

### If you’re injured
- Move to mineral plates or compacted loam (stable).
- Get out of basins (cold sink + spores).
- Treat for cold early; wrap and insulate from below.

### If the city starts “closing” around you (clearances narrowing, facades tightening)
Assume it’s a safety protocol, not hostility.

- Stop pushing forward.
- Back out along the last route that felt “allowed.”
- Reduce contact: tuck straps, remove snag points, carry gear rather than dragging.
