## What you’re walking into (so you pack for the *right* dangers)
This place looks like high alpine desert plus cyclopean urban planning, but it behaves less like a mountain range and more like a **dry, cold, low-pressure architectural field with intermittent rule-changes**:

- **Physiology hazard:** thin air, cold that steals breath, dehydration without noticing, UV/IR exposure under haze, and dust that is sharper than it looks.
- **Mechanical hazard:** knife ridges, glassy talus, dust that flows like flour then cuts like glass, vertical “street” shafts, and terraces with long fall-lines.
- **Environmental hazard:** no water, no biomass, no easy fuel, silence that hides moving grit, frost that forms without melt, static seasons.
- **Reality hazard:** unreliable distance, gravity “preferences,” sound routing wrong, perspective miscounts, straight corridors that loop, stillness events where the “city adjusts.”

Prepare like you’re doing a **high-altitude, cold-desert traverse inside a mildly adversarial non-Euclidean ruin**.

## Before you go: body, paperwork, and training
### Physical conditioning (minimum)
- Be able to hike **6–8 hours/day** at altitude carrying **15–22 kg (33–48 lb)**.
- Be comfortable with **Class 3 scrambling** and exposure (hands needed, big drops).
- Practice moving on **loose scree + sharp rock** and downclimbing with a pack.

### Altitude + cold readiness
- If you can, acclimatize in-world first (sleep high, climb higher).
- Learn symptoms and self-treatment for:
  - **AMS/HACE/HAPE** (altitude illness)
  - **hypothermia** (including “I feel fine” stage)
  - **frostnip** (on fingers, toes, nose)
- If you have asthma or panic history: bring your usual meds and a plan. Thin air here “has corners”—breathing *feels* sharper even when you’re technically okay.

### Psychological prep
You will have long stretches with:
- no living soundscape,
- no reliable sense of “that’s a mile away,”
- architecture that looks intentional but refuses to explain itself.

Practice staying calm when your brain’s mapping tools fail. The big risk is not fear—it’s **making confident decisions with bad spatial information**.

## Timing your visit (the place has “seasons,” even if it doesn’t have weather)
You don’t get normal day/night cues. Plan around *conditions*:

- **Avoid “stillness” periods** if possible. Local rule-of-thumb matches your best survival instinct: if wind stops completely, relocate to a **higher, simpler geometry** (ridge, open shelf) and avoid shafts/courtyards.
- **Static intervals:** if hair rises, dust clings vertically, or metal veins “tick,” treat it like a dry lightning storm. Get off prominent metal-veined walls and ridges.

If you can choose, enter during a period of **gentle, steady wind**: it gives you a consistent external reference and reduces the city’s “holding its breath” feeling.

## Core equipment: the non-negotiables
### Breathing + altitude
- **Portable oxygen** (small bottle) for emergencies and for the “gravity preference” corridors that suddenly make exertion feel wrong.
- **Pulse oximeter** (simple, rugged). Your perception will be untrustworthy; numbers help.
- **Acetazolamide** (if you tolerate it) and a clear dosing plan; **dexamethasone** only if you know what you’re doing and accept the risks.

### Water and hydration (you cannot “find some later”)
There is no stream logic here. Assume **zero liquid water**.
- Carry **at least 4–6 L/day** baseline, more if you’re moving hard in dust.
- Use **hard bottles + a bladder**, because puncture risk is real.
- Add **electrolytes**; the cold hides sweat loss.
- Don’t rely on melting frost. It sublimates; it’s not your friend.

### Cold + dryness + light
- Layering suitable for **cold, windy high country**: base + mid + insulated + shell.
- **Balaclava or buff**: dust and cold air both punish throats.
- **Goggles** (not just sunglasses). Wind-ground grit can abrade eyes fast.
- **High-SPF sunscreen + lip balm**. Haze does not mean safe UV.
- **Skin barrier cream** for knuckles/cheeks; the dryness cracks you open.

### Foot and hand protection (this terrain eats people)
- Boots with **stiff soles** and aggressive edges; bring **gaiters** (dust infiltration is constant).
- Consider **approach shoes** as a secondary pair only if weight allows.
- **Cut-resistant gloves** for obsidian/basalt edges and for “polished” handholds that still bite.

### Movement safety
- **Helmet** (falling isn’t always “down,” but your skull still loses arguments).
- **Climbing harness + 30–40 m rope** (light alpine line) + a small rack:
  - slings, locking carabiners, a couple of nuts/cams if you know how
  - a **descender** and prusiks/ascenders (a “street” can become a shaft)
- **Trekking poles** help on dust-stairs and for probing “down” when down is negotiable.

### Navigation that survives bad geometry
Do not trust a single system.
- **Paper map + compass** (but treat compass as *advisory*; local mineralization may lie).
- **Inertial/step counter** (watch/phone) for relative movement tracking.
- **Rugged GPS** can help, but expect multipath or nonsense if space is misbehaving. Log tracks anyway; patterns matter.
- **Physical breadcrumbing:** bright chalk, reflective tape, or removable tags.
  - Make marks **non-circular**. Avoid rings. Use asymmetrical symbols (e.g., a long slash + short slash).
- **Surveyor’s line / thin cord** for “don’t follow straight too long” corridors: you can literally pay out line and retrieve it, preventing silent loops.

### Comms and power
- Radios may suffer from weird propagation; still worth having:
  - **handheld radio** + spare battery
  - **satellite messenger** if you have it (expect intermittent)
- Power:
  - Cold kills batteries. Carry them close to body.
  - Bring **two independent power banks** and a simple charging strategy.
- Light:
  - **Two headlamps**. Shadows here are indecisive; you may need your own certainty.

### Shelter and sleep (camping rules matter here)
- **Four-season tent** or a bivy that seals against grit.
- **Sleeping pad with high R-value**; stone sucks heat relentlessly.
- **Warm bag/quilt** rated well below your expectation.
- **Earplugs** can help you sleep, but don’t fully deafen yourself; subtle grit-slides are an early warning.

## Field practices: how to move without getting “edited” by the city
### 1) Treat straightness as a hazard
Locals would say: straight streets invite loops. Your rule:
- If you’ve been moving in a **single straight corridor** for more than ~10–15 minutes without a landmark change, **force a deviation**:
  - take a side stair,
  - climb one terrace level,
  - cross an open shelf.
Then re-orient from a new vantage.

### 2) Test gravity before you commit
Carry a tiny “gravity kit” in an easy pocket:
- a **small plumb bob** (or weighted string),
- a **bubble level**,
- a pinch of **fine dust/sand** in a vial.

Before descending a long stair or crossing a plaza that feels “floaty”:
- drop the plumb; watch for sideways bias or lag.
- sprinkle dust; see if it arcs strangely.
- take one step and stop; notice if your balance “returns late.”

If something is off: clip into a rope, shorten your stride, avoid edges, and don’t jump.

### 3) Sound lies; sight lies; touch is more honest
Because echoes route wrong and distance won’t resolve:
- Navigate by **contact and sequence**: “three broad steps, ribbed buttress, warm wall, left niche with metallic vein.”
- Photograph key junctions with a time stamp and a note card in frame (analog truth anchor).

### 4) Don’t camp in perfect circles (seriously—don’t)
Even if you think it’s superstition, it’s easy to comply:
- Don’t arrange stones in a ring.
- Don’t pitch your tent centered inside a naturally perfect circle of blocks/tiles.
- If you discover your site is circular after setting up: move.

Choose camps that are:
- **asymmetrical**, slightly broken geometry,
- on **open shelves** with multiple exits,
- away from the bottoms of terraces (dust avalanches) and away from the mouths of shafts (air behavior changes).

### 5) “Stillness protocol”
If wind drops to absolute zero and the silence feels *thicker*:
1. Stop traveling through corridors/streets.
2. Move toward **higher, simpler topography** (ridge, open terrace).
3. Avoid metallic-veined walls and tight arches.
4. Clip loose items; dust can move in sheets when it resumes.
5. Wait it out with shelter ready, like you would for an electrical storm.

## Environmental hazards you’ll underestimate on day one
### Dust that cuts
This is not soil. It’s pulverized mineral edge.
- Keep skin covered.
- Seal tent seams.
- Clean your nose and eyes with saline; don’t rub them with dusty gloves.
- Use a **real particulate mask/respirator** when wind picks up.

### Obsidian/basalt edges
Assume every dark plate can slice:
- Don’t “hand on wall” casually.
- Patch small cuts early; in this dryness they crack wider.

### Frost without melt
Rime on steps makes them deceptively slick.
- Treat dawn-like periods as ice time.
- Don’t assume traction improves when it “warms”—it may sublimate and leave ball bearings of grit.

### Static seasons + metal veins
When the air prickles:
- Avoid leaning on metallic seams.
- Don’t string your rope along shiny veins.
- Consider a simple **grounding strap** (even a deliberate touch to a broad, non-metal rock before handling sensitive electronics helps discharge you).

## Food, fuel, and “nothing burns”
Plan for **no biomass** and wind that steals heat.
- Bring a stove that works in cold, thin air (liquid fuel is safer than canisters).
- Bring food you can eat **without cooking** in an emergency.
- Budget extra calories; cold + altitude + stress is expensive.

## Life you might meet: what not to do
### Iridescent films
Treat them like a delicate, possibly reactive ecology.
- Don’t scrape them unless you must for science/survival.
- Don’t eat near them; don’t let them contaminate water or wounds.
- If you touch them: wash hands; avoid eyes.

### Pale arthropods, gliders, cliff-dwellers
They’re unlikely to attack, but they are tuned to shadows and vibration.
- Don’t chase; don’t corner in cracks.
- Store food sealed; “nothing lives here” is how you get surprised.

## Getting un-lost: protocols for when the city starts looping you
If you realize you’re walking “toward” something and it isn’t getting closer:

1. **Stop and time-box the problem** (10 minutes stationary).
2. Take out your last **three** navigation anchors (photo, physical tag, map note).
3. Ascend to the nearest vantage that is:
   - open to sky,
   - not inside an arch,
   - not at the end of a long straight.
4. Reacquire a *new* target that is large-scale (a ribbed amphitheater, a distinct chimney tower).
5. Move in **short legs** (5–10 minutes) with hard confirmation at each leg end.

If you suspect a corridor is looping:
- Do not “prove it” by continuing. That’s how it eats your daylight and oxygen.
- Lay line, backtrack to the last unquestioned junction, and change elevation.

## Emergency planning (what actually kills visitors here)
### The big four
1) **Falls** (exposure + gravity quirks)  
2) **Altitude illness** (thin air with exertion)  
3) **Cold injury** (dry cold creeps in silently)  
4) **Disorientation/looping** (you spend resources to go nowhere)

### What to carry on your body at all times
If you get separated from your pack, you still need to survive:
- headlamp
- lighter/fire starter
- emergency bivy
- 500–1000 ml water
- radio/locator
- small med kit (bleeding control + blister + altitude meds)
- a few high-calorie bars
- navigation crumbs (compass, small notebook, marker)

### Extraction mindset
Pick a “home ridge” or “safe terrace band” early—a band of elevation you can always retreat to. In a place where streets rotate under your assumptions, **retreat needs to be simpler than advance**.

## A practical packing checklist (compressed)
**Wear/carry:** helmet, goggles, buff, gloves, boots + gaiters, shell, insulation.  
**Health:** water 4–6 L/day, electrolytes, oximeter, oxygen bottle, altitude meds, robust first aid.  
**Shelter:** sealed tent/bivy, warm sleep system, high-R pad.  
**Move:** rope 30–40 m, harness, basic hardware, poles.  
**Nav:** map/compass, GPS logging, chalk/tags (non-circular marks), line cord, camera notes.  
**Respiratory:** respirator/filters, saline rinse.  
**Power/comms:** two lights, radios, spare batteries warm-carried.

## How to behave once you’re there (the “local etiquette” that doubles as safety)
- Leave **asymmetrical** traces; avoid circles.
- Change direction and elevation regularly; don’t marry a straight street.
- When the world gets quiet in a way that feels intentional, **get higher and simpler**.
- Don’t argue with the place about distance. Treat “an hour toward it” as data, not failure.
- Respect the emptiness: it’s not absence, it’s a different kind of occupancy.
