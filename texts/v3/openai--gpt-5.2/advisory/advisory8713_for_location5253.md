## Core idea of the place
Prepare as if you’re visiting a *hot, brackish industrial estuary* where the “city” is mostly low infrastructure, constant condensation, shallow water everywhere, unstable ground, subdued visibility, and an unusually dense animal ecosystem using pipes/ducts/drains as habitat. The main threats aren’t dramatic—they’re cumulative: heat stress, skin breakdown, infections, footing failures, corrosion, contamination, and getting quietly boxed in by tides, storms, or territorial fauna.

---

## Before you go: what to assume (so you don’t pack wrong)

### Environment assumptions
- **Wet heat, always:** You will sweat constantly and also be rained on / condensed on. Drying out is hard.
- **Dim, low-contrast light:** Distance judgement is poor; everything beyond mid-range blurs.
- **Ground that lies:** “Solid-looking” slabs may be mud-supported, undermined, or floating on saturated silt.
- **Brackish, oily water:** Likely polluted; treat all water contact as contamination.
- **No plants:** No easy natural fibers, no edible greens, no shade from trees, no wood to burn. Biomass is mostly animal/microbial.
- **Fauna density is the norm:** Expect nests in vents, flocks using corridors, scavenger surges, and canal predators.

### Human factors
- **Sparse people, selective activity:** Some zones are maintained and may have functioning security, protocols, and “do not enter” assumptions even if the city feels empty.
- **Industrial hazards persist:** Pumps cycle, tanks exist, vents vent. Treat it like an operating facility with wildlife on top.

## Physical preparation (your body is the first piece of gear)

### Heat + humidity conditioning
If you’re not acclimated, do 7–14 days of heat training before departure:
- Daily 30–60 min mild exertion in warm conditions
- Practice drinking to thirst *plus* replacing electrolytes
- Learn your personal early signs: irritability, clumsy steps, headache, “chills” in heat (danger sign)

### Skin, feet, and infection prevention (critical here)
Most travelers fail via macerated skin → blisters → infection.
- Pre-treat feet: trim nails, toughen gradually, bring blister strategy (see gear)
- If you have eczema, chronic athlete’s foot, or slow-healing wounds: stabilize them first. This environment punishes open skin.

### Vaccines/meds (general)
You can’t know the local microbiome, but plan like warm-brackish + industrial waste:
- Tetanus up to date
- Broad “traveler’s” coverage if available to you (hepatitis, etc.)
- Bring: oral rehydration salts, anti-diarrheal (with caution), topical antifungal, topical antibiotic, antiseptic wash, wound closure strips, a full course of an antibiotic your clinician would approve for skin/soft-tissue infections (multiverse travel often means “no pharmacy”)

## The right gear: what actually works in warm, wet, corrosive dimness

### Clothing system (counterintuitive: you need both ventilation and barriers)
- **Base layer:** synthetic or merino that tolerates constant damp; avoid cotton.
- **Outer:** lightweight rain shell *with pit vents* (you’ll be wet either way; the goal is controlled wet).
- **Legs:** quick-dry pants or durable work pants; consider **lightweight waders** only if you must enter flats/canals (waders increase risk if you fall).
- **Hands:** thin work gloves + nitrile disposables for contamination tasks.
- **Head/neck:** brimmed hat (rain + drip lines), neck gaiter to reduce aerosol inhalation in tight spaces.

### Footwear (do not improvise)
You need three properties: grip on slime, puncture resistance, and fast drainage.
- **Primary:** high-traction, lugged boots rated for wet industrial surfaces (anti-slip compound); ideally puncture-resistant midsole.
- **Secondary:** neoprene/water shoe for controlled wading (kept separate so you don’t sleep in canal-shoes).
- **Socks:** two systems  
  - thin liner + thicker outer (reduces friction)  
  - bring more socks than you think; rotate and rinse; dry inside your shelter if possible
- **Foot care kit:** leukotape or equivalent, blister pads, zinc oxide barrier cream, antifungal powder.

### Breathing/eye protection (humidity-friendly)
- **Respirator:** at least a compact half-mask with filters suitable for particulates and nuisance organic vapors; at minimum, high-quality N95/FFP2 equivalents, but humidity degrades them faster—bring spares in sealed bags.
- **Eye protection:** sealed or semi-sealed safety glasses; drizzle + aerosolized grime is constant near vents and canals.

### Waterproofing electronics (assume everything will get wet and salty)
- IP-rated cases, dry bags, and desiccant packs
- Conformal-coated backup flashlight/headlamp if possible
- Avoid exposed metal tools unless stainless/coated; wipe down nightly

### Light and navigation (because the world won’t give you shadows)
- Headlamp + handheld flashlight (two independent sources)
- A low-power area lantern for your safe room
- Physical marking: bright survey tape / chalk marker that adheres to damp surfaces
- Offline maps if you have them, but assume signal/GPS may be unreliable; bring a simple compass anyway
- A small thermal viewer can be extremely useful for spotting animals in vents/overhangs (optional, but it fits the ecology)

### Sleeping and shelter (you must create “dry enough”)
- Hammock or cot system if floors are wet; otherwise an insulated pad with high R-value (concrete steals heat even in warm air)
- Bug netting even if you don’t see insects at first (they’ll find you)
- A small fan helps prevent skin rot and improves sleep
- Chemical dehumidifiers help only in enclosed spaces; don’t expect miracles

### Water and food
- Bring your own calories. There is no plant foraging, and animal harvesting is risky (bioaccumulated pollutants).
- Water: treat local sources as last resort. If you must process:
  - Pre-filter (sediment)
  - Activated carbon (for some organics; not a guarantee)
  - Then disinfection (boil if you can, or chemical + time)
  - In brackish water, salinity is a problem—basic filters won’t desalinate. Plan to carry water or have a reliable resupply.

## Arrival protocol (first hour rules your whole trip)

### 1) Pick a “known-safe” staging point
You want:
- Elevation (roof, raised walkway) **above** the obvious tide line
- Structural soundness (newer markings, less spall, intact railings)
- Visibility of at least two routes out
- Not directly downwind of vents/stacks
- Not under roosting overhangs (droppings = pathogens)

### 2) Establish your dry core immediately
- Change into your “camp dry” socks/shirt and keep them sacred.
- Set up a small clean zone in your pack (zip bag) where nothing contaminated goes.

### 3) Run a quick environmental check
- Wind direction (vents + smells + birds will tell you)
- Tide state if you’re near channels (look for foam lines, stranded shells, wet sheen on walls)
- Sound check: pump cycles, conveyor hum—these can indicate “active zone” boundaries and sometimes security presence

## How to move without getting hurt (this place injures the impatient)

### Footing rules
- **Probe before stepping** on any surface that looks like a “tile” of concrete in mud—those plates can tip.
- Avoid the tempting straight lines; old roads may be undercut into shallow canals.
- Treat grates and catwalks as slippery even when they look dry (condensation makes them treacherous).

### Tides and rain: the silent trap
- A “single connected wetness” becomes connectivity you can’t walk through.
- Pick routes that remain viable in **two conditions**: heavy rain and high tide.
- Mark your exit path physically; haze makes “obvious landmarks” vanish.

### Don’t fight the ecology; route around it
- Overhangs, vents, and warm housings are *occupied*. If you disturb a nest, you may trigger swarm/defense behavior.
- Corridors between buildings can become animal highways—if a flock uses it, don’t stand in it.

## Wildlife safety (practical, not dramatic)

### General principles
- You are not the apex presence here; you are a warm, sweaty mammal walking through established systems.
- Most risk is from **startle** + **close quarters** + **contamination**, not cinematic attacks.

### Canal and drain hazards
- Assume anything that looks like calm shallow water may have:
  - sudden depth at a culvert mouth
  - submerged debris
  - aggressive/territorial predators
  - contaminated sediment that will infect cuts

### Deterrence and food discipline
- Store food sealed and odor-minimized.
- Don’t eat where you sleep; scavengers learn.
- A loud whistle/air horn can clear space briefly; use sparingly (it also advertises you).

---

## Industrial and chemical hazards (treat “quiet” as deceptive)

### Active infrastructure behavior
- Pumps can reverse flows, open gates, or change water levels fast.
- Settling ponds and channels can contain low-oxygen pockets or toxic films; don’t wade unless necessary.

### What to avoid touching
- Oily sheens, unknown foams, bright salt crusts near tank farms (could be chemical residues)
- Warm wet walls in enclosed spaces (microbial mats; aerosol risk)
- Any powdery paint dust (old coatings can be nasty in many worlds)

### Decon routine (daily)
- Rinse boots and outerwear away from your sleeping area.
- Wipe down exposed skin; treat hot spots early.
- Hang gear so it can drip-dry; stagnant wet fabric becomes a biology experiment overnight.

## Social/operational preparation (if humans are present)

### Assume ownership even in “empty” districts
If you encounter maintained cores:
- Don’t cross marked lines, fresh seals, or inspection tags casually.
- Visible lights and beacons can mean remote monitoring even if no one’s around.
- Have a story ready that fits local logic: “contractor,” “inspector,” “salvage permit,” etc.—but only if you can back it with plausible props (bad lies escalate fast).

### Trade and interaction
If people are rare, they will value:
- Dry goods, sealed medical supplies, tools that resist corrosion, batteries, filter media.
They will distrust:
- Anyone contaminating their clean zones, bringing animals close, or ignoring tide/weather discipline.

## Emergency planning (the three failures that end trips here)

### 1) Heat injury
- Stop, cool, electrolytes, shade/ventilation.
- Do not “push through” in this humidity—your body can’t dump heat effectively.

### 2) Foot failure / skin infection
- At first sign of maceration: dry, barrier cream, change socks, reduce water exposure.
- Any cut that touched canal mud: clean aggressively, disinfect, monitor for redness tracking.

### 3) Route loss due to haze + water rise
- Always maintain an elevated fallback line: catwalk → roof → raised service road.
- If you feel turned around, stop moving and mark position; moving while uncertain is how you walk into a basin that becomes a pond.

## Suggested packing checklist (condensed)

**Wear/carry**
- Anti-slip puncture-resistant boots; spare laces
- 6–10 pairs socks (liner + outer), blister kit, antifungal
- Ventilated rain shell, quick-dry layers, brimmed hat
- Nitrile gloves, work gloves
- Respirator + spare filters/masks; sealed eye pro
- Headlamp + flashlight + spare batteries; small lantern
- Dry bags, desiccants, wipe-down cloths
- Water you can trust + electrolytes; high-calorie sealed food
- First aid focused on skin/feet + rehydration
- Marking tape/chalk; compass; offline map
- Lightweight tarp + bug net; cot/hammock or high-R pad

**Optional but high-value**
- Small thermal monocular
- Stainless/coated multi-tool; corrosion-resistant knife
- Portable fan
- Collapsible probe pole for testing ground depth

## Mindset: how to succeed here
- Move like you’re on a shoreline, not in a city.
- Treat “dry” as temporary and “solid” as a hypothesis.
- Keep one sanctified dry layer and one sanctified clean zone.
- Let the animals have the corridors; take the edges.
- Plan around water level changes the way you’d plan around daylight in a normal world.
