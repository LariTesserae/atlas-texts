## What this place is (in traveler terms)

Plan for a **dry, abrasive, low-light volcanic uplift zone** under a **permanent dust deck**, with:

- **Unstable, newly-fractured rock** (knife ridges, plate slopes, sudden amphitheaters, fresh talus).
- **Heat from below** (hot pockets in slots/fissures; warm stone; uneven thermal bands).
- **No usable water cycle** (no streams, no reliable melt/condensation; “lake flats” are mineral precipitates).
- **Dust storms without rain** (visibility collapse; inhalation hazard; sandblasting).
- **Occasional vents and condensates** (likely sulfur/metal compounds; potentially toxic/corrosive).
- **Seismic/tectonic micro-events** (cracks, rumbles, delayed collapses).
- **Sparse stone structures** that may be wayfinding, warnings, or something you don’t want to trigger by tampering.

Assume the land is *responsive but not hospitable*: it won’t chase you, but it will happily break you.

---

## Core survival principle: you are a sealed system

Because water and biological resources aren’t part of the environment, you must arrive able to function as a mostly closed loop for:

- **Hydration**
- **Respiration protection**
- **Light**
- **Navigation**
- **Medical stabilization**
- **Shelter from dust and wind**

If any one of those fails, you don’t “push through”—you leave.

## Pre-trip preparation (do this before you jump)

### 1) Medical and physical readiness
This terrain punishes small mistakes.

- Be able to hike **10–15 km/day** over broken talus while carrying **15–25 kg**.
- Have recent training (or refreshers) in:
  - **Ankle/knee protection and mobility on scree**
  - **Basic climbing movement** (3rd/4th class scrambling) and downclimbing
  - **Wilderness first aid**, specifically:
    - bleeding control
    - splinting suspected fractures
    - eye irrigation and foreign body management
    - heat stress and dehydration recognition

If you have asthma, chronic sinus issues, or panic in confined spaces: slot canyons + mineral dust are a bad mix unless you bring stronger respiratory gear and have tested it.

### 2) Decide your mission style: “touch-and-go” vs “expedition”
- **Touch-and-go (1–6 hours):** minimal camp gear; heavy on comms, breathing/eye protection, navigation, water.
- **Expedition (1–7+ days):** you need a storm-capable shelter, redundancy in lighting/power, and a strict water budget.

Do not let “it’s warm” trick you into underpacking shelter; wind-driven grit can shred skin and gear.

### 3) Establish your return plan like it will fail once
Multiverse travel is notorious for “the exit is where it was, until it isn’t.”

- Bring **two independent ways to relocate your entry point**:
  - a **radio beacon** (with spare battery) you can home in on
  - a **visual/reflective marker line** you can follow even in dimness (retroreflective tape + stakes/cairns you build yourself)
- Bring **a hard time limit** after which you return no matter what (fatigue + low light + seismic uncertainty compounds).
- Leave a **cached emergency kit** at or near the entry point if possible (water, mask filters, headlamp, battery).

## Hazards you should specifically engineer around

### A) Dust and abrasive wind (your #1 daily threat)
**Failure mode:** eye injury, respiratory irritation, equipment abrasion, navigation loss.

**Prep:**
- Full-seal goggles (not “sunglasses”).
- A real respirator system (details below).
- Dust covers for zippers, lenses, electronics.
- Plan “storm posture”: where you go and what you do when visibility drops.

### B) Dehydration with no resupply (your #1 strategic threat)
**Failure mode:** headache → confusion → bad footing → injury → you can’t walk out.

**Prep:**
- Carry *all* water.
- Add electrolytes; warm environments still strip salts even when you don’t see sweat.
- Do not rely on mineral flats or “frost” crystals—assume **toxic and non-water**.

### C) Unfinished terrain (your #1 injury threat)
**Failure mode:** lacerations from obsidian, ankle fractures on plates/talus, rockfall triggered by your own vibration, collapse after distant tremor.

**Prep:**
- Helmet, gloves, tough pants, ankle-support boots.
- Trekking poles for probe/stability.
- Rope/harness only if you know how to use them—otherwise you carry false confidence.

### D) Vents, condensates, and “pretty mineral films”
**Failure mode:** inhalation of SO₂/H₂S/metal fumes; chemical burns; gear corrosion.

**Prep:**
- Treat vents like active industrial hazards.
- Don’t scrape crystals or films; don’t camp downwind of vents.
- Carry a **multigas meter** if you can (SO₂, H₂S, CO, O₂, LEL). If you can’t: act like you have one and it’s always reading “maybe.”

### E) Low, sideways light and lack of landmarks
**Failure mode:** you get turned around and waste water/time.

**Prep:**
- Navigation that doesn’t depend on a sun line:
  - inertial/dead reckoning (phone can help if protected and powered)
  - physical breadcrumbing (reflective tape/stakes/cairns you build)
  - paper notes: “from entry, 312° for 600 m to scar face; then down slot…”

## What to pack (practical, human-scale)

### Respiratory + eye protection (non-negotiable)
- **Half-face respirator + P100 filters** (minimum).  
  Better: **full-face respirator** (saves eyes during storms).
- If you expect vent fields: consider **multi-gas/acid gas cartridges** in addition to particulate filters.
- Backup: disposable N95s are only “better than nothing” here; don’t make them your plan.
- **Sealed goggles** (if not full-face), plus a spare.

### Water + electrolytes
- Baseline: **4–6 liters/day** depending on exertion and how hot those “throat” canyons get.
- Add **electrolyte packets** (at least 1–2 per liter you expect to sweat out).
- Carry **hard-sided bottles** for part of your water; bladders can puncture on obsidian.

### Clothing and body protection
- **Boots:** stiff sole, good edging, high-ankle support; bring gaiters (dust intrusion is relentless).
- **Gloves:** durable work gloves + thin liner gloves.
- **Pants:** abrasion-resistant (softshell or canvas-like hiking pants).
- **Shell:** windproof jacket with a hood that seals well around mask/goggles.
- **Knee/elbow protection** optional but useful if you’ll scramble in slots.
- **Buff/neck gaiter** to seal gaps and keep grit off skin.

### Shelter and storm posture
For day trips: at least an **emergency bivy** + a way to anchor yourself from wind.

For multi-day:
- A **low-profile, wind-worthy tent** or a **bivy + tarp** you can anchor with rocks (no soil stakes).
- Bring **extra guyline** and abrasion sleeves (cord will saw itself on sharp stone).
- Groundsheet that can survive pumice/obsidian.

### Light and power (assume constant dim)
- **Headlamp (primary) + headlamp (backup)**, both with spare batteries.
- A small **area lantern** helps with morale and tasks inside shelter.
- Power bank(s) in dustproof bags; keep charging ports covered.

### Navigation and marking
- Offline maps (if any exist), but don’t assume GPS is stable.
- **Compass** (helpful even without sun), but assume local anomalies near fresh basalt/ore.
- **Reflective tape**, small reflectors, or LED breadcrumbs.
- Notebook + pencil (dust laughs at touchscreens).

### Medical and repair
- Eye wash ampoules (sterile saline) + eye pads.
- Trauma supplies: pressure bandage, gauze, tape, clotting gauze if trained.
- Splinting: SAM splint, elastic wrap.
- Blister care (your feet will be under constant micro-abuse).
- Tweezers (obsidian splinters happen).
- Repair: tenacious tape, zip ties, needle/thread, spare buckles.

### Optional but wise
- **Helmet** (seriously): rockfall and head strikes in low light are common.
- **Multigas meter** if venting is part of your route.
- **Small geophone/seismic alarm** is nice in theory; in practice, it may just increase anxiety. Your best seismic tool is conservative terrain choice.

## How to arrive safely (first 30 minutes on-site)

1. **Do not step forward immediately.**  
   Put on mask/goggles before you start breathing hard.

2. **Establish your anchor:**
   - Mark the entry point with something you can find in a dust curtain:
     - reflective markers at three heights (ground, chest, above head)
     - a simple rock arrow/cairn *you* built (distinct shape)
   - Turn on your homing beacon if you have one.

3. **Do a “ground truth” check:**
   - Is the air breathable without irritation?
   - Any sulfur/metal smell? If yes, back away from low areas and fissures.
   - Is there active grit movement? If yes, expect a visibility event.

4. **Pick a conservative initial route:**
   - Avoid slot canyons until you understand wind behavior.
   - Avoid knife ridges until you’ve seen how gusts hit you.
   - Travel on **broad, stable-looking benches** when possible.

## Daily movement rules (the ones that keep you unbroken)

### Footing and terrain
- Treat every edge as sharp enough to cut gear.
- Never trust “flat-looking” mineral crusts; they may be brittle sheets over voids or loose powder over glass.
- In talus fields: move slowly, test each step; avoid traveling directly below partners (rockfall).
- Don’t linger under fresh scar faces or amphitheater walls.

### Wind and storms
When a dust front hits, your goal is not to “keep making progress.” Your goal is to **stop becoming lost and abraded**.

Storm protocol:
1. Get off ridgelines (gust amplification) and out of slots (funneling).
2. Find lee shelter behind a large block or inside a shallow recess.
3. Cover all skin; seal mask and goggles.
4. Clip yourself to your pack or a rock anchor if gusts can knock you over.
5. Wait it out, track time, sip water, don’t wander for “a better spot” once visibility collapses.

### Heat pockets
- Slot canyons and fissures can be hotter and more stagnant than you expect.
- If you start feeling “baked” or headachy: climb out to airflow rather than pushing deeper.

## How to treat the stone structures

Assume three possibilities at once:
1) wayfinding markers  
2) warnings/boundaries  
3) components of something that reacts to disturbance

Practical rules:
- **Do not move stones.** Don’t “improve” cairns or doorway-lintels.
- **Do not camp inside rings or under lintels.** Those are classic “alignment” shapes for things you can’t see.
- Photograph, sketch, note orientation, and move on.
- If you notice multiple structures “pointing” toward vents, fissures, or transitions: treat that corridor as *higher risk*, not “interesting.”

## Food and metabolism strategy (because you’ll underestimate effort)

- Bring **high-calorie, low-crumb** foods (crumbs become grit paste in your mask).
- Favor fats + dense carbs (nuts, energy bars, hard cheese, tortillas).
- If the air is dry and dusty, your throat will feel raw; bring **lozenges** or soothing gel packs.

---

## Hygiene and contamination control

- Keep a “clean zone” inside shelter: mask off only when you can control dust.
- Wipe seals (goggles/respirator) regularly; grit will break the seal and irritate skin.
- Don’t rub eyes—flush them.
- Keep electronics in bags; dust will destroy charging ports and camera mechanisms fast.

## Psychological preparation (this matters more here than most places)

This environment is engineered to make humans make mistakes:
- constant dimness with no sun cue
- silence punctuated by sudden cracks
- no living reference points
- the feeling that the ground is provisional

Countermeasures:
- Use **timers** (eat/drink on schedule; rest on schedule).
- Narrate your route in notes like a pilot (“leg 2 begins at glass flat; bearing 040; distance 800 m”).
- If you start “pushing because it’s quiet and nothing’s happening,” that’s exactly when the rockfall happens.

---

## Emergency decision tree (simple and strict)

Leave immediately if:
- Your **respirator fails** and you can’t replace it.
- You can’t see more than **10–20 m** for more than an hour and you’re not in a secure shelter.
- You lose **route certainty** (don’t know where the anchor is) and your water margin is under 50%.
- You encounter a vent area that causes **eye/throat burning** even through protection.
- Someone falls and you suspect **fracture, head injury, or deep laceration**: stabilize, then retreat—this terrain is not where you “wait for it to improve.”

## A realistic packing list for an “ordinary human” 2–3 day visit

If you want a concrete baseline, this is a sane starting point:

- Water: **12–18 L total** (split across bottles + bladder), electrolytes
- Full-face respirator (or half-face + sealed goggles) + spare filters
- Helmet, gloves, gaiters
- Windproof shell + abrasion-resistant pants
- Two headlamps + lantern + spare batteries + power bank
- Bivy or 4-season-ish low-profile tent, extra guylines
- Sleeping pad (insulation from warm-but-uneven ground) + light sleeping bag (nights may still chill at elevation)
- First aid focused on eyes, cuts, sprains/fractures
- Navigation kit + reflective markers + beacon
- Food: **3000–4000 kcal/day**
- Repair kit (tape, ties, needle/thread)
