## What you’re walking into (so you pack for the right problems)

This underland is not “a cave with plants.” It’s a hot, bright, humid, *living interior* where:

- **Light is biological** (living surfaces glow; scraped/sterile surfaces go dim).
- **Water doesn’t flow** (it’s hoarded in cups, membranes, tissues; you can’t rely on streams).
- **Heat is steady** (greenhouse heat, little wind, minimal cooling).
- **The terrain is young and inconsistent** (slick basalt, friable chalk that re-knits, springy root-mats).
- **Sound and breeze are swallowed** (easy to get disoriented; hard to sense “weather changes”).
- **Fauna are scarce** (good: fewer predators; bad: fewer obvious warnings and fewer “edible animal” options).
- **The ecology is likely closed and touchy** (foreign microbes and oils from your skin may matter more than you expect).

So you prepare like you’re entering a **hot, wet, luminous greenhouse canyon** with **no rivers, minimal airflow, and navigation that can quietly fail**.

## Pre-trip planning (before you ever step through the seam)

### 1) Set your mission constraints
- **Time-in limit:** Decide a hard maximum (e.g., 6–10 hours) until you know how your body handles the heat and VOC-heavy air.
- **Turnaround triggers:** Leave immediately if you notice:
  - Persistent dizziness/headache (heat stress or CO₂ buildup in pockets)
  - Tight chest/wheezing (spores/VOCs)
  - Confusion or “time-slip” sensations (this place reads like fixed-newness; don’t assume normal temporal feel)
  - Sweating stops despite heat (late-stage heat illness)

### 2) Plan for navigation without “cave logic”
There may be no reliable tunnel walls or linear corridors—just volume and repeating plant architecture.
- Bring **a mapping method that doesn’t depend on landmarks**:
  - Inert chalk/flagging is a bad idea if it abrades living surfaces or gets overgrown quickly.
  - Prefer **retraceable thread** (thin line spooled behind you) *if you can keep it from tangling in root-curtains*.
  - Or **reflective micro-tags on your own gear** combined with careful route notes and timed legs.
- Create a **breadcrumb protocol** before entry:
  - “Every 5 minutes, stop, turn, and visually memorize the last distinctive feature *and* the next one.”
  - “Every 20 minutes, log direction/time/terrain type/ceiling height.”

### 3) Decontamination is not optional
Assume the underland is a stable closed system; you are the invasive species.
- Before entry: wash with unscented soap; avoid perfumes, deodorants, insect repellents with strong volatiles.
- Gear wipe-down: alcohol wipes for hard surfaces; avoid leaving residues.
- After exit: bag clothing, rinse boots, shower, inspect for hitchhiking spores/pollen.

## Personal kit: what an ordinary human should carry

### Heat + humidity management (most important)
- **Breathable base layer** (synthetic or merino), plus a **light long-sleeve overshirt** to reduce skin contact with resins.
- **Ventilated hiking pants** (you want abrasion protection; root-mats and basalt are unforgiving).
- **Two liters minimum** of drinking water *on your body* + **electrolytes**.  
  Do not assume you can “drink from the environment”: water is stored in tiny reservoirs that may be chemically odd or biologically active.
- **Cooling strategy:** a small **evaporative neck wrap** helps less when air is already humid, but it still buys time. More useful: **rest cadence** (see Movement rules below).

### Respiratory + eye protection (second most important)
The air is warm, sweet, resinous—likely heavy with pollen/spores and VOCs.
- **Half-mask respirator** (P100/FFP3) or at least a high-grade particulate mask.
- **Spare filters** sealed in a bag; humidity clogs.
- **Clear protective glasses**—not for darkness, but for drifting luminous pollen and accidental frond contact.

### Footwear + gloves (for terrain that “won’t be ground”)
- **Boots with aggressive grip** and a stiff-ish sole (slick basalt + springy mats = ankle risk).
- **Gaiters** (roots, chalk powder, and clinging plant fibers will crawl into your socks).
- **Nitrile liner gloves** under **thin work gloves** if you expect to touch surfaces (prevents resin contact, keeps your skin oils off the ecosystem).

### Light and power (counterintuitive)
It’s bright, but brightness is *patchy by biology*.
- Carry a **small headlamp anyway**, not for seeing—**for signaling** and for those dim bare-rock zones.
- Favor **sealed power banks**; humidity kills exposed ports. Bring a zip bag + silica packets.

### Tools that won’t anger a living architecture
- **Soft measuring tape / cord**, not pitons or spikes.  
- **A collapsible trekking pole**: tests springiness, depth of powdery basins, and slickness.
- **A small first-aid kit** focused on:
  - Heat illness (oral rehydration salts)
  - Blisters/ankle wraps
  - Antihistamine (allergic response)
  - Sterile saline eye rinse
  - Antiseptic for resin-coated abrasions

### Food
No fires, and heat makes heavy meals unpleasant.
- **Cold, salty, high-calorie snacks** (nuts, jerky, tortillas, electrolyte gels).
- Avoid crumbly foods—fine particles will stick to everything and may contaminate surfaces.

## Entry protocol (first 15 minutes decide your whole trip)

### 1) Acclimate on the threshold
At the seam/sinkhole, stop and do:
- Mask on, glasses on.
- Drink a few mouthfuls of water *before* you feel thirsty.
- Check for immediate respiratory irritation. If you cough within 2–3 minutes, consider it a hard warning.

### 2) Establish your “return spine”
Because the space opens out, you need an anchor.
- If possible, **keep the entry seam in sight** until you’ve picked a single obvious line of travel (a ridge of basalt, a terrace edge, a slab walkway).
- Mark the *non-living* part of the entrance (stone) with something removable (a tied cord loop on a rock nub), not a scratch.

### 3) Don’t trust the light as a guide
Bright doesn’t mean safe; dim doesn’t mean deep.
- Areas of dim bare rock may be slick.
- Areas of intense glow near shafts may be spore-dense and hotter.

## Movement rules inside (how to not get hurt or lost)

### Walk like you’re on a living trampoline over glass
- **Test with a pole** before stepping onto:
  - Powdery chalk basins (could crust then give)
  - Thick pad mosaics (could hide hollows)
  - Root curtains (trip and entangle)

### Use “islands” of stable substrate
Make your route hop between:
- basalt shelves
- stone slab walkways (if present)
- dense fused trunks that behave like columns

### Pace and rests (heat management)
- Use a **25/5 rhythm**: 25 minutes moving, 5 minutes still.
- Rest in **damp pockets** only briefly; they feel cooler but may have higher CO₂ or more concentrated spores. If you start yawning or feel dull-headed there, move back to a more open convective area.

### Keep your hands off luminous surfaces
Not out of reverence—out of practicality.
- Skin oils can alter growth.
- Many glowing plant tissues suggest active chemistry; resins can irritate.
- If you must steady yourself, choose **bare stone** or your own trekking pole.

### Treat vertical shafts as hazardous “weather wells”
They’re tempting landmarks, but:
- Spore columns can irritate lungs/eyes.
- Updrafts can dehydrate you faster.
- The brightness can be misleadingly harsh and disorienting.

## Water, hygiene, and “local resources” (what not to assume)

### Don’t count on environmental drinking water
Those leaf-cups and membrane basins may contain:
- high mineral content
- plant metabolites
- microbial films you can’t see in the glow

If you must use local water:
- Prefer **fresh ceiling-sweat** collected on a clean surface you brought (silicone sheet / bottle mouth funnel).
- Filter + chemical treatment. Then drink small amounts and wait.

### Sweat management
You will sweat continuously even if you don’t feel exertion.
- Replace salts early (electrolytes every 1–2 hours).
- Change out of soaked layers if you stop moving for long; humid warmth can still chill you via evaporative swings when you enter cooler pockets.

## Camping and shelter (best practice: don’t)

This place is stable but not friendly to long stays.
- **Avoid overnighting** until you know whether there’s a day/night cycle (the light source is biological; if it “rests,” the environment could change).
- If you must stop:
  - Use existing built niches (stone-and-mat shelter) *without modifying it*.
  - Don’t cut, tie, or “clear” vegetation.
  - Sleep with mask available; spore load can increase after ceiling-sweat events.

## Etiquette and conservation (also keeps you alive)

### Treat built things as messages, not conveniences
Rings of stone, steps, slab bridges—someone made them to solve specific problems:
- a basin that must remain open (maybe it’s the only reliable condensation collector)
- a patch of floor too springy or unstable
- a route that minimizes harm to the living mat

Use them gently, and don’t “improve” anything. In a place where change is slow or frozen, your small alteration may persist for a very long time.

### Don’t fight the plants
If the vegetation “rebounds” into your path, that’s information: you’re not on a maintained route.
Backtrack rather than forcing a new corridor.

## Medical and environmental hazards to anticipate

### 1) Heat illness without warning wind
Symptoms: headache, nausea, irritability, stumbling.  
Response: stop, electrolytes, cool neck/wrists, retreat toward entrance.

### 2) Respiratory irritation (pollen/spores/VOCs)
Symptoms: cough, tight chest, watery eyes, rash.  
Response: mask upgrade, rinse eyes, leave if persistent.

### 3) Slips, ankle turns, and hidden hollows
This is the most likely injury.
- Tape/wrap at first sign of instability.
- Turn around early; extraction gets harder fast in a muffled, repeating terrain.

### 4) Psychological drift
The hush, the constant heat, the everywhere-light can produce a mild trance effect.
- Set audible timers.
- Speak route notes aloud (even if sound gets eaten, it anchors you).
- If you find yourself “just wandering,” stop and reverse course immediately.

## Exit strategy (don’t improvise this)

- Turn around while you still feel strong. In heat, decline can be steep.
- On return, the environment may look “the same” from the opposite direction. That’s normal.
  - Follow your preplanned “return spine,” time logs, and any non-damaging markers.
- At the seam: pause before emerging. Hydrate, check breathing, then exit.

After exit:
- Decon gear/clothes.
- Monitor for 24–48 hours for delayed respiratory symptoms (spores) or skin reactions (resins).
