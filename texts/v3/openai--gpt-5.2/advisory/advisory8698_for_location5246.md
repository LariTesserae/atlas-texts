## What you’re going to and what that implies

Treat this place as **high-altitude industrial backcountry** layered over an active (or semi-active) facility, with an added rule: **geometry is locally unreliable**. Your biggest risks are:

- **Physiology:** thin air, high UV, sharp temperature swings (sun vs shadow).
- **Injury:** falls, cuts, crush points, sharp “new” edges, unexpected hot/cold surfaces, energized structures.
- **Contamination:** metal salts, oils/grease films, microbial biofilms in condensation systems.
- **Navigation failure:** corridors that subdivide, distances that elongate, mirrored horizons, boundary planes (“glass glints”).
- **Communication failure:** sound absorbed and broken by structure; line-of-sight often lies.
- **Fauna:** insects everywhere; small scavengers; occasional predators; nesting around warm vents.

Preparation is less about toughness and more about **systems**: clothing, anchoring, marking, pacing, and contingency drills.

## Before you go (medical, paperwork, mindset)

### Medical / body prep
- **Altitude readiness**
  - If you can, acclimatize 1–3 days at a moderate elevation before entry.
  - Bring **acetazolamide** (if you can take it), plus an **ibuprofen/acetaminophen** plan.
  - Pack a **pulse oximeter**. Set personal “turnaround” thresholds (e.g., persistent SpO₂ < 85% at rest, worsening headache, confusion).
- **Vaccinations / wound readiness**
  - **Tetanus** up to date (this is a cut-rich environment).
  - A wound kit that can handle **lacerations** (butterfly closures, steri-strips, irrigation syringe).
- **Skin and eyes**
  - High UV: bring **wraparound UV-rated eye protection** and **SPF 50+**, plus lip balm.
- **Respiratory**
  - Not because of dust storms, but because **metal film + biofilm aerosols** happen near vents and trays: bring an **N95/P100** option.

### Mindset / rules you adopt beforehand
- You will not “wing it” on navigation. You will **anchor** and **verify**.
- You will not rely on “straight lines.” You will move by **landmarks and segments**, not by perceived distance.
- You will not split up. Geometry that lies punishes solo travel.

## Core gear (the loadout that keeps you alive)

### Clothing and PPE (assume sharp edges + electrified feel + cold shadows)
- **Helmet** (climbing helmet or industrial hard hat; climbing style is better for overhead structure).
- **Cut-resistant gloves** (Level A4/A5) + thin liner gloves (for dexterity on cold rails).
- **Eye pro**: clear + tinted lenses (bright sky and glare off metal).
- **Footwear**: boots with
  - aggressive tread (wet composite/greasy plate is slick),
  - **puncture-resistant midsole**,
  - preferably **EH-rated** (electrical hazard) if you expect energized surfaces.
- **Layering**
  - sun-exposed: breathable layer + sun protection
  - shadow: insulating midlayer + wind shell
  - bring a **warm hat** or balaclava; fingers go numb fast on handrails.

### Fall protection and movement
You’re in a place where “ground” is often catwalk, grating, hatch, or plate.
- **Climbing harness** (simple sit harness is fine).
- **2 locking carabiners + 2 non-lockers**, a **short lanyard**, and **10–20 m of static line**.
- **Compact headlamp** + spare battery (dark bays happen suddenly).
- **Knee pads** (you will kneel on cold, abrasive surfaces).

### Navigation and “anti-lie” tools (more important than a map)
- **Physical marking kit**
  - paint marker (oil-based), chalk, or grease pencil (works on metal),
  - high-visibility tape (tearable),
  - numbered tags or pre-numbered stickers.
- **Breadcrumb anchors**
  - 30–100 small, distinctive markers you can’t confuse (zip ties of a unique color, stamped washers, etc.).
- **Spool line (“home thread”)**
  - A bright **mason line** spool is cheap and extremely effective: tie it off at base camp and at each major decision point.
- **Notebook + pencil** (pencil writes on damp/greasy paper).
- **Simple compass** is fine to bring, but don’t trust it near big metal masses. Use it as a weak hint, not truth.

### Communication (since shouting won’t carry)
- **Two-way radios** are worth bringing, but assume dead zones and distortion.
- Plan **vibration codes**:
  - A metal rail can transmit knocks surprisingly far.
  - Agree on a simple code (e.g., “3 knocks = I’m here; 2 knocks repeated = come back; continuous tapping = danger”).
- **Whistle**: limited range here, but still useful at short range inside bays.
- **Visual signals**
  - small strobe/flashlight for line-of-sight signaling,
  - reflective tape on your pack and helmet.

### Water and food (you can find water, but it’s not automatically drinkable)
- Carry in: **2–3 L per person** minimum.
- Harvesting: bring a **small filter + activated carbon**, and ideally a method that helps with metals:
  - activated carbon helps organics; it does not fix everything.
  - If you can, add an **ion-exchange/resin** option or use brought-in water for most drinking.
- Bring **electrolytes**; altitude + dry air dehydrate you.
- Food: high-calorie, low-prep; keep it **sealed** (fauna will investigate).

### Repair / safety tools
- **Non-contact voltage tester** (helpful around “static prickle” areas).
- Multi-tool + small adjustable wrench.
- A few meters of duct tape + self-fusing silicone tape (for hose-like emergencies, sealing, patching).
- A compact first-aid kit geared for:
  - cuts, punctures, sprains,
  - eye irrigation (metal dust/film),
  - antihistamine (bites/stings),
  - anti-diarrheal (if you misjudge local water).

## Arrival protocol: first 60 minutes

1. **Pick a base that is “predictable”**
   - near a feature that consistently behaves the same: a vent that always vents, a lamp that always lights, a numbered hatch bank, a repeating icon wall.
   - avoid obvious boundary “glints” and any place birds visibly refuse to cross.
2. **Make a physical base marker that can’t be mimicked by geometry**
   - Example: three tape bands in a unique pattern + a zip tie cluster + a written symbol in paint marker.
   - Photograph it, but don’t rely on photos as your only memory.
3. **Run a short tethered recon loop**
   - Keep the base in sight if possible.
   - Lay **spool line** or breadcrumbs.
   - Identify: water sources, sheltered “warm cavities” for emergency refuge, and any energized/unsafe zones.

## Moving through the terrain without getting geometry-trapped

### The rule: travel by verified segments
Instead of “we’ll go to that tower,” do:
- “We will go to Hatch 14, then the yellow chevron panel, then the twin vent stacks.”

**At every segment:**
- Stop.
- Confirm the last marker you placed is still where you expect.
- Place the next marker *before* you commit to the next corridor.

### Landmark choice: pick things the place is less likely to “lie” about
Better:
- numbered hatches, serial plates, stenciled icons, repeated manufacturing patterns, unique damage marks.

Worse:
- “that open straight corridor,” “that far wall,” “that bright end point.”

### Time-box your corridors
Because distances can elongate:
- Decide in advance: “If we do not reach the target landmark in **X minutes**, we turn back to the last verified node.”
- Use a watch, not your sense of progress.

### Use animals as pointers, not guides
Living things “navigate the lies” better, but:
- following them blindly can take you into nests, predator territory, or loops.
- Use them as **confirmation**: if multiple species repeatedly choose one passage and avoid another, note it—but still mark and verify.

### Don’t cross “glass glints”
Treat them like:
- thin, invisible fences,
- potential shear boundaries,
- places where your line might snag or your perception might desync.

If you must test one, do it like a hazardous material boundary:
- tethered, one person at a time,
- line anchored on your side,
- stop immediately if you feel pressure in ears, sudden temperature shift, hair rise, or visual doubling.

## Industrial hazards to assume everywhere

### Hidden energization
That “static prickle” is your warning.
- Prefer **one-hand contact** when testing rails/handholds (reduces heart-path shock risk).
- Use your voltage tester.
- Avoid becoming the best ground path: don’t kneel in wet patches while holding metal.

### Surface temperature lies
- Sunlit plates can be surprisingly hot; shaded metal can be numbing.
- Touch-test with the back of a gloved hand first.

### Fall and hatch risk
- Grating can be missing fasteners.
- Hatches may be unlatched.
- Treat every edge as unguarded unless you’ve confirmed.

**Practice:** clipped-in traverses on exposed catwalks (via lanyard + handrail).

### Pinch points and “quiet machines”
Because the place is quiet, motion can be subtle.
- Avoid stepping into areas with:
  - rhythmic ticks,
  - low hum in bones,
  - warm airflow you can’t locate.
Those are often “active” even when it doesn’t sound like it.

## Water: finding it, collecting it, not poisoning yourself

**Best sources (least bad):**
- fresh condensation dripping from clean-looking metal into a clean container,
- shaded undersides where water beads (wipe with a clean cloth into container).

**Worst sources:**
- tray bottoms with biofilm,
- evaporator fins with visible crust,
- any runoff that has traveled across multiple surfaces (it picks up oils and metal salts).

**Treatment approach:**
- Pre-filter (cloth) → filter → activated carbon.
- If you have to choose, use harvested water for **washing** and rely on carried water for **drinking**.

## Fauna: how not to start a problem you can’t finish

- Store food in **hard containers** or odor-reducing bags; don’t leave wrappers.
- Don’t camp directly under warm grates or vent stacks: you’re in someone’s nesting zone.
- Watch for predator signs: pellets with wire/chitin, cleared “perches,” repeated scratch paths.
- Insects: bring repellent, but also physical defenses:
  - long sleeves, sealed cuffs,
  - inspect your gear at dusk; they love warm creases.

If confronted:
- Make yourself big, back away slowly, don’t run into unknown corridors.
- Use light (bright headlamp beam) more than sound; sound doesn’t project well here.

## Camp and rest strategy (the place punishes “random stopping”)

Pick rest points that are:
- **thermally stable** (near mild warmth, not near scorching exhaust),
- **geometrically stable** (near repeated landmarks),
- **not acoustically dead** (you want to hear approach—some bays swallow everything).

Sleep is hard in bright altitude glare:
- bring an eye mask, earplugs (even quiet places have ticks and drips), and an insulating pad (metal conducts heat away fast).

## Reality-distortion discipline (the habits that keep you sane)

### “Reality check” routine every 30–60 minutes
- Confirm: partner present, last node marker present, direction of return path known.
- Say out loud: “Last verified landmark is ____. Next verified landmark is ____.”

### Never chase the “almost there” feeling
If you can see the endpoint but aren’t arriving:
- stop,
- return to last verified node,
- try a different route with better landmarks.
The treadmill-floor effect is a classic trap.

### Use redundancy
- Two independent marking methods (tape + written marker; spool line + numbered tags).
- Two independent records (notebook + partner’s notebook).

## Emergency procedures (decide these *before* you need them)

### If you become disoriented
1. Stop moving.
2. Sit or clip in (falls happen when panicked).
3. Find your last physical marker or your spool line.
4. If you can’t, move only to the nearest **safe refuge feature** you identified earlier (warm cavity, sheltered bay), then signal.

### If someone is injured
- Treat bleeding/cuts immediately (metal edges are clean but deep).
- Hypothermia can happen even on bright days once you stop moving—insulate, get out of wind corridors.
- If evacuation requires passing questionable geometry, do not “shortcut.” Use the marked, verified route even if it seems longer.

### If a corridor begins subdividing or looping
- Do not keep “exploring” inside it.
- Mark the entry as a hazard node.
- Retreat to the last stable landmark and reroute.

**Wear:** helmet, eye pro, gloves, boots, layers, sun protection.  
**Carry:** 2–3 L water, filter + carbon, food, first aid, headlamp, harness + lanyard, 10–20 m line, marking kit (tape + paint marker + numbered tags), notebook + pencil, radio (optional), voltage tester, multi-tool, electrolytes.
