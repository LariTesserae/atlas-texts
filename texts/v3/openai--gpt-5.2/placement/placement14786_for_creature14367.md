You would find me first where you can still pretend you didn’t hear it.

At the lip of the mistake-in-the-map—ten steps from ordinary lowland—there’s a narrowing in the sound, not silence but a folding. The drone doesn’t *end* so much as it gets taken apart into harmless pieces and handed back to the flat air in frequencies the outside world can forgive. If you stand right there, one boot on firm, boring ground and one boot on the springing weave, you can feel the basin’s steadiness tugging at your weight as if it’s taking inventory.

That’s where I begin: not inside the pocket, not outside it, but as the seam that lets the pocket stay a pocket.

I tell myself, at first, that I’m an animal. Something that can choose the seam because seams are where the best decisions happen—where to drink, where to retreat, where to watch. Maybe I’m one of the quick warm bodies that use the root-mat like boardwalk, a thin mammal with a frantic heart and a disciplined route. I picture my paws finding the rope-grip, my nose searching for a seep-point that tastes less like tannin and more like leak-fresh water. I picture my life as an errand between warm convection pockets and sheltered membrane-shadows.

But the longer I stay at the rim, the less “animal” holds.

An animal is a spike with legs. Even the careful ones. Hunger rises, fear rises, breeding rises. You can schedule it, you can name it instinct, but it still surges. The pocket doesn’t like surges. The pocket has a whole etiquette designed to absorb them before they become news.

So I try again: maybe I’m a bird in the upper frames, living in a membrane-pocket that is as close to dry as this place allows. A tenant. A midrange voice. I could keep time with the basin—call at the same hour, fly the same corridor, take insects off the pods like a maintenance worker plucking loose screws. That fits the “shift change” feeling: wings as part of ventilation, predation as a brake, a life that serves stability by never becoming too many.

But I hear what happens when birds leave: the canopy exhales. The membranes adjust. Droplets fall in calibrated beads. The whole structure corrects itself *without* the bird. The bird is an accessory—useful, recruited, replaceable.

What isn’t replaceable here is the rhythm itself.

So I go lower, into the part of the basin that doesn’t get credit because it doesn’t have faces.

I become, in imagination at first and then with a kind of quiet inevitability, something that lives as a film.

Not a dramatic organism. A skin. A glaze. The slick green that varnishes stone-teeth and valve-housings; the biofilm that makes every surface a little less itself and a little more like the pocket. In the ordinary lowland, wetness is weather. Here, wetness is a medium—and mediums want membranes.

As a film, I don’t walk anywhere. I *continue*. I spread by simply not stopping, by taking what arrives—minerals, sugars, soot-thin deposits from the methane-cups, the faint exudate that beads along plant ribs—and turning it into stickiness, into cohesion, into a friction that can be tuned.

And the first real clue that this is where I belong is sound.

The basin’s noise isn’t decoration. It’s a pressure language. It’s how the system leaks without tearing. The clack of stiffened valves, the hiss of vapor jets, the crackle of fungi venting gas, the endless wing-motor whine—these aren’t just audible. They’re mechanical signatures.

A film can read signatures the way an animal reads scent.

Vibration comes through the silica-reinforced tissues and into the wet interface where I live. In that interface, everything is slow enough to be legible. A valve that closes too hard abrades its own seat; abrasion changes its tone; tone changes the microcurrent of air and moisture around it; that changes what condenses and where; that changes what feeds me. I don’t need eyes. I have resonance.

So I do what films do: I adjust the tolerances of the world by a fraction.

Where a valve is too crisp—too close to becoming a spike, too sharp in its clack—I thicken in the hinge-line. A few microns of living polish. A dampener. Not enough to stop the machine, never that, because stopping is the one spike the pocket cannot absorb. Just enough to soften the edge of the sound, to spread the impact across time.

Where a membrane snaps too often under humidity shifts, I seed a lace of extracellular slime along a stress-line, a reinforcement that holds tension differently. The snap becomes a sigh. The cadence stays. The structure stays.

Where methane-cup rims crust with minerals in a way that would narrow the flame’s breath and raise the heat, I host a different guild of microbes that eats the wrong deposit and excretes the right one, turning a dangerous narrowing into a stable throat. The flame remains blue, daylight-invisible, properly ashamed of itself.

None of this looks like decision from the outside. It looks like nothing. A sheen. A slippery inconvenience. A green smear on your fingers if you touch the wrong stone.

But it is governance of a particular kind: the kind that never announces itself because it’s older than announcement.

The pocket is “a single habit that has learned to wear many bodies.” If that’s true, then what I am is not one of the bodies. I am the habit’s *sense of timing*. The part that notices when timing starts to drift.

Not a brain. Not even a nerve. More like an ear that doesn’t know it is hearing—just chemistry responding to rhythm because rhythm, here, is the safest form of meaning.

This is why you would find me at the rim as well as deep inside: because the rim is where drift begins.

Outside air has different patience. Its humidity fluctuates like mood. Its temperature is less disciplined. Its winds have opinions. Every time the pocket draws in that air through its convection channels, it takes in variability—the raw material of spikes. The basin can handle variability, but only if it is broken down, phased, distributed.

At the rim, I act as a slow gate: a living gasket grown across the seam-stones and the first root-weave, thickening when the outside tries to rush in, thinning when the inside needs to breathe. I don’t block. I buffer. I make the exchange boring.

Inside, I am everywhere the system touches itself: hinge meets seat, rib meets membrane, cup meets flame, root meets water. I am the lubricant that also remembers.

And if you insist on a face—if you need a single “who” rather than a thousand glosses—then imagine this:

A shelf of fungus in one of the designated eddies where litter is allowed to arrive. Pale, layered, tidy as a stacked filter. Through my pores, gas escapes with a faint crackle. That crackle is not accident; it is tuned venting, a release that keeps fermentation from swelling into catastrophe. My body is a controlled leak.

Above me, leaves don’t “fall” so much as they are delivered, slid along grooves that the mat maintains. I receive them. I break them down into molecules small enough to be re-issued as structure. I smell like hot compost with a petroleum edge because the basin is honest about its fuel.

Below me, the microbial refinery works in the dark, turning patience into methane. The cups burn it. The burners pull air. The pods pulse. The insects ride. The birds call. The whole pocket continues to do its one great trick: turning wetness into architecture and architecture into a schedule.

I am one of the quiet correctors that keeps the schedule from becoming a tyranny or a chaos.

If you walked into that pocket and stayed, you might never notice me as “a being.” You would notice instead that the valve you leaned against didn’t rasp, that the membrane above you didn’t tear, that the flame you glimpsed in the cup didn’t flare when your shadow cooled it, that the drone remained steady enough to make you doubt your own heartbeat’s irregularity.

That is how I live here: not as a creature that dominates the basin, but as a thin, distributed insistence that nothing gets to spike.

Not a maker. Not a ruler. A stabilizer.

A sheen with an opinion about rhythm.
