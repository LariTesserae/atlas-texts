You would find me where the chamber is least like a floorplan and most like an instrument.

Not down in the cistern pockets—those are for hands that don’t mind the cold slick patience of water, for knees that can kneel on tile without flinching, for people who can smell rot before it declares itself. Not up in the highest rafters either, where bat-villages braid their noise into a living ceiling and every beam is already claimed by a thousand hooked feet.

Midway.

On the crossings.

On the narrow bridges that cut the open volume like sentences, where you can feel the draft’s logic on your skin and hear, in one standing breath, three different drip-rhythms arguing about the day. Where a call from the far scaffolds arrives with its consonants intact because the architecture has learned how to carry human meaning the way it carries water: guided, pitched, kept from pooling.

At first I look like a newcomer, because that’s how this place reads everyone until proven otherwise—another body lowered down a throat of light, blinking at the normal daylight, moving wrong for a while: stepping into the subtle slope of a drainage pitch, pausing where you’re not meant to pause, reaching for a rope too high on the fibers and learning (fast) how skin pays for ignorance here.

They would make me useful quickly. They always do. A basket. A coil. A task that repeats until your muscles stop asking questions.

But even while my hands learn the obvious work—the scraping, the tying, the lifting—I keep doing the other work without meaning to: listening.

The chamber trains that into you. It doesn’t give you silence to stand in and decide who you are. It gives you a constant, layered report. The place is always telling on itself.

A wheel a half-step off its usual complaint. A new bright ping where ceramic has cracked and begun to answer drops differently. The hush that means a colony shifted, not because of whim, but because light cooled somewhere up in the wells. The sudden overconfidence of crickets in a seam that used to be too dry for them, which means a gutter is backing up even if you haven’t seen it yet.

I start, in their eyes, as one of the Sweepers—because people who listen hard often get handed the jobs that prevent sickness. The work nobody romanticizes. The guano scraped before it sours a rim. The mold cut back before it turns rope sweet and weak. The dead insects raked out of corners where they would otherwise become a whole economy of the wrong kind.

And then—because governance here is attention wearing a belt of tools—listening becomes a kind of appointment.

They already have Light-keepers and Water-guiders and Ropewalkers, but there’s a role that isn’t named as cleanly because it sounds too much like pride: the one who keeps the chamber legible.

Not a ruler. Not a priest. Something closer to a reader.

I would be set to the crossings with chalk and a small strip of hammered metal—nothing fancy, just a tongue of bright stuff that can be wedged into a crack and made to sing if the draft hits it right. I’d tie thin cords along certain beams, not for climbing but for vibration: a way to feel, through your fingertips, what the air is doing in parts of the room you can’t see.

In a place where the rules are “don’t block a pitch” and “don’t let a shaft go dull,” information is a material. It’s as practical as rope.

So I become—slowly, because this chamber is made of slow solutions that last—a kind of Sound-keeper.

I learn which noises are normal. That’s the first and hardest literacy here: not hearing, but knowing what to ignore. The bats are not a warning by themselves; they are weather. The constant drip is not a leak; it is breath. The swifts’ harsh cries are not alarm; they are traffic.

The warnings are the *changes*.

A delay in the chamber’s daily exhale, like lungs that hesitated before sighing. A draft that comes down a shaft warm when it should be cool—meaning a reflector has shifted, a throat has partially closed, the sky above is feeding the wrong kind of heat into the system. A sudden silence at the upper haze that doesn’t mean peace, but absence: something has disturbed the bats enough to move them, and if the bats move, insects will redistribute, and if insects redistribute, mold follows, and if mold follows, rope goes soft.

My job is to catch the first domino while it’s still standing.

To do it, I’d end up shaping my own voice the way the chamber has taught everyone to. Calls not for beauty, but for carry. Consonants chosen because they don’t smear in echo. Warnings built like knots: short, repeatable, impossible to misunderstand at distance.

And at some point—this is where it stops being only a job and starts being what I *am*—I realize I’m not only listening *to* the chamber.

I’m listening *as* it.

That low hum you described—the steady, below-everything sound of warm air moving through made passages—starts to feel less like background and more like a mind thinking without words. Not mystical, not a god. An emergent creature made of habit and pitch and maintenance, alive the way a well-kept system becomes alive: responsive, corrigible, capable of health.

The Wellfolk talk about veins and lungs because it’s accurate. I would be one of the nerves.

Not the only one. Nothing here is singular for long. A Sound-keeper doesn’t stay a solitary expert the way surface towns make specialists. The chamber dislikes single points of failure. So I would teach, and be taught, until “me” is partly a practice held by many people—shared call patterns, agreed meanings, chalk marks that aren’t just marks but a memory of what a draft did last season.

If you insisted on naming me the way outsiders want to name things, you could call me:

- an **Echo-warden** (though it sounds grander than it is),
- a **Crossing-reader**,
- a **Keeper of the Hum**.

But among the ones who set—among the people who treat finishing as identity—I think I’d be described with a verb, like they describe themselves:

Not “a listener,” but *one who makes the chamber readable*.

And if you looked for me in the world, you wouldn’t find me by searching for a face.

You’d find me by standing still on a bridge while the light cools and warms, by letting the chamber’s manufactured daylight rest on your eyelids, and by hearing a small, almost lost change in the ongoing argument of drops and wings and rope—then hearing, a moment later, a shaped call go out across the void to answer it.

That would be me: not a master of the hollow, not its owner, but one of the ways it knows itself in time to stay habitable.
