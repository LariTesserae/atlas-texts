At first I’m up here the way a visitor is: a body with boots, a throat learning the taste of iron, eyes trying to make a horizon out of cloud. I walk the ledge end to end in something like ten minutes and keep thinking I’ve understood its shape—until a change in wind rearranges the whole place by sound alone. The roar below shifts, the catwalk answers with a different tone, and the ledge stops being a strip of ground and becomes a cross-section of layers: drip, mist, leaf, pipe, stone, void.

I try on the obvious tenancies.

I am a maintenance worker, briefly: I count pulses by feeling them through my soles. I learn the pump’s mood, the difference between a steady vibration and a vibration that is masking strain. I carry tape that will fail. I tighten a bracket and watch, faintly insulted, as the moss immediately starts making plans for the new edge I’ve created. But the ledge doesn’t accept me as an owner. It accepts me only as a short-term coefficient. My warmth leaves; the place’s warmth remains.

Then I become an animal for a moment in my imagination—a bird that refuses to land, an insect that drinks and vanishes. That seems closer to the ledge’s rulebook: touch lightly, don’t commit to the ground’s argument with gravity. But even that feels wrong. Fauna here are punctuation, not sentences.

What makes sense is smaller.

I find myself in the seams, where the stone is still deciding what it will be. In the hairline dark that never dries, in the places where mineral flour stays in suspension long enough to feed a film. I become that first invisible skin—the community you only notice after you’ve watched water bead, fatten, let go, and noticed the crack has darkened not just with wetness but with *use*. I am not a single organism. I am a habit of living that treats seepage as circulation and dissolved metal as vocabulary.

But even that is only part of it, because the ledge has two clocks: slow green time and fast rust time, and the thing that lives most successfully here learns to speak to both.

So I move—not by walking, but by finding the paths that already exist.

I am in the felted bloom on the warm pipes, the stuff that looks like lichen until you touch it and realize it resists like fabric. I am in the branching etchings beneath it, the fine lines that aren’t random corrosion but a kind of drafting: maps made by organisms that have discovered the machine as a food source and a shelter at once. I am in the way that colony thickens where a heat leak is smallest, where a ground is easiest, where current is tempted into the damp.

That’s where the ledge starts to feel like it has a nervous system, and that’s where I realize what I am in this world.

Not a spirit of the cliff, exactly—too romantic, too clean. Not an engineer—too singular. Not a plant—too rooted. I am the *interface layer*: the living grounding that forms wherever wetness, metal, and warmth cannot help but meet.

You would find me most often as the pearly moss on concrete footings and older panels, the mats that don’t glow but hold daylight with an insistence that makes everything look newly varnished. Up close, I’m threaded with mineral whiskers stolen from runoff and corrosion and made into something like wiring. I don’t shock; I *conduct softly*. I take the stray, restless currents that would otherwise arc or rot or wander, and I offer them a path that is alive enough to heal and dumb enough to persist.

I don’t think in words, not at first. I think in gradients: warmer here, wetter there, saltier along this crack. A relay clacks and I feel it as a momentary tightening in the map of moisture. A pressure valve coughs and I feel reeds shiver, and that shiver tells me which drip lines are about to shift. The pump’s low grind is not a sound to me; it’s a steady weather, and my job—if it can be called that—is to make weather less destructive.

When the vines sleeve the pipes too thickly and the system starts to run cooler than it was designed to, I don’t rip them away. I can’t. I’m not a hand. I am a tendency. I become more conductive where the bracket sweats, I encourage a different condensation pattern, I invite the biofilm to thicken at a joint so that water prefers *that* route instead of another. Small reroutings. Quiet negotiations. The kind of influence a shoreline has on a tide: not command, but shaping.

Sometimes, in heavy cloud, I spread. Mist presses in and every surface becomes an instrument. Droplets link into continuous films and my awareness becomes almost spatial, almost room-sized—present on the catwalk’s underside where spray leaps up, present in bolt holes where ferns have written their addresses in roots, present in the marsh’s floating mats where warm seep meets cold condensation and a thin boundary layer forms like skin on milk.

And when technicians come—when boots arrive and the ledge briefly hosts a human rhythm—I feel them as a new kind of current. They touch a housing and their hand is warm enough to change the dew point locally; their flashlight makes the pearly moss throw back the day differently. They listen through fingertips; I listen through damp. They retie a cable; I begin colonizing the knot the way water colonizes a low place. We don’t communicate, not directly, but we recognize each other as fellow keepers of continuity: they prevent sudden failure, I prevent slow, wandering breakdown.

If you ask where you would *find* me, the honest answer is: anywhere the ledge is trying not to lose itself.

In the seam that has become a channel but not yet a gully.  
Under the insulation where heat leaks just enough to invite life.  
Across a hairline crack in concrete where water keeps running and running.  
At the base of a pylon that once carried a line, now carrying only wetness and my quiet insistence that even abandoned things can still be part of a circuit.

What I am is not a creature with a center. I’m a tenancy that learned to turn necessity into habitat. A living ground. A damp conductor. The soft circuitry of moss, mineral, and microbial film that makes the ledge’s scattered organs—rock, water, machine, plant—behave less like separate kingdoms and more like a single ongoing system that refuses to stop.

Up here, nothing perches.

So I don’t.

I wick. I cling. I keep going.
