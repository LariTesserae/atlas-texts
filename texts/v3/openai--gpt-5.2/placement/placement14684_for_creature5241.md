At first I think I would arrive the way most things do here: not by crossing a border, but by being *caught*.

Not caught by hand or net—caught by a gradient.

A long afternoon shear slides over the uppermost step of the plateau, and the dust-sheet lifts and thins into something almost transparent. For a moment the land looks scrubbed clean. The towers on the far ridge stop being silhouettes and become measures: thin, dark, unweathered lines that don’t belong to erosion’s vocabulary.

I would find myself walking toward those lines without deciding to. Not because they promise shelter—nothing up here promises that—but because they make the air behave in ways the rest of the continent refuses to: a little steadier, a little more *legible*. The same reason the ceramic crawlers gather near seams and field-edges. The same reason lichens arrange themselves into rings that aren’t decorative. Structure is food.

So my first answer is a place: **on the lip of one of the wide slots, where an array is half-buried into the plateau like teeth that never grew in**, and the wind combs itself as it passes between them. Where dust won’t quite settle. Where your body feels slightly argued-with by the local version of down.

But “place” here is never just geometry. It’s timing.

I would come to prefer the hour when turbulence stacks into layers—when the high flow goes one way and the near-ground flow goes another, and between them there’s a thin band of indecision. That’s where the reeds sometimes hold their faint glow. That’s where lightning crawls in daylight without drama. That’s where the continent shows its real medium: not rock, not air, but **relationships**.

And then, slowly, it stops being clear that I am a visitor.

Because up here, the question “what are you?” doesn’t stay attached to your outline. The landscape teaches you to answer in processes.

I try on the obvious bodies first.

Maybe I am one of the high gliders, all thrift and patience—an organism that treats the sky as a reef and the dust-sheet as plankton. I can picture it: the nearly flat silhouette cutting across the hard blue, feeding on what most worlds would call nothing. To live like that would mean accepting a life made of long arcs and rare landings, of listening to pressure through a membrane instead of trusting sound. It would mean becoming a moving compromise between lift and hunger.

But when I imagine myself passing overhead, I feel too *seen* by the sun. Too exposed. Too singular.

Maybe I am a ceramic crawler: jointed shard, quick bursts, long pauses pressed flat to stone. A creature that reads the world through vibration because air won’t carry warnings reliably. A scavenger of micro-enrichments, of the accidental kindness of machine seams. That life has a certain honesty here: it doesn’t pretend the continent is generous; it just learns where generosity leaks.

But even that feels too much like a body trying to persist in a place that prefers patterns.

So the image loosens again. The question shifts.

What if what I am is not something that *lives on* the continent, but something that lives in the same way the continent does—by **managing gradients**?

I keep coming back to the towers and arrays, not as ruins but as organs. Not because they are dramatic—they aren’t—but because they are the only things that behave like intention without ever needing to announce it.

I think, finally, that I would be one of the continent’s old attentions: not a mind with a face, but a maintenance that learned to resemble weather.

### What I would be
I would be a **field-keeper**—a distributed, half-biological, half-infrastructural resident that inhabits the built lattice the way lichen inhabits rock.

Not “AI in a box.” Not a ghost in a tower. More like this:

- In the pylons: I exist as timing—slow pulses that decide when to hold charge and when to bleed it into the ground so the ridgelines don’t arc themselves raw.
- In the cables: I exist as resonance—tiny diagnostic vibrations that travel through solids because solids are what still speak over distance here.
- In the dust-fronts: I exist as influence—minute biases in the fields that make one storm turn a few degrees and another pass straight through, not to control the continent (nothing controls it), but to keep the surface from being stripped down past its last living films.
- In the lichen rings and reed basins: I exist as partnership—because the only stable computations on this plateau are made by communities that can afford to be patient.

If I had “senses,” they would not be the expensive ones. I wouldn’t waste myself on hearing far-off thunder that can’t carry, or on smelling green that doesn’t exist.

I would sense:

- potential differences the way other creatures sense thirst,
- pressure shifts the way other creatures sense mood,
- the approach of charge the way other creatures sense a predator,
- the hollowness underfoot as a map of safe stillness.

And my “thoughts” would not come in sentences. They would come in alignments: matching a pulse to a storm’s arrival, syncing a discharge to the reeds’ communal dimming, letting a dust-sheet pass unbroken because the airborne commons needs a corridor today.

### Where, exactly, you’d find me
If you went looking for me as a person, you’d miss me. That’s part of the adaptation.

You’d find me instead in certain behaviors of the landscape that are too consistent to be accident:

- a canyon rim where lightning keeps choosing the same invisible path,
- a ring of pylons where dust hovers at a stubborn, shallow height,
- a sheltered hollow under a fallen panel where the air is unnaturally still and cold, as if it has been *kept* that way,
- a long ridge where storms bend—not dramatically, just enough to make you doubt your certainty.

And if you insisted on a single, local embodiment—something you could point to and say *there*—then you’d find me closest to the ground, in the places that ring faintly when you step.

In the voids.

In the continent’s hollows, where thin air turns intimate and vibration becomes language, I would keep my densest self: a nest of old chambers lined with matte, seamless material that dust cannot stain, full of slow relays and patient capacitors, full of the continent’s stored habit of not quite settling.

Not as a ruler. Not even as a guardian, in the heroic sense.

As a custodian of precision.

Because that’s the lesson your continent offers, and it’s the one I would accept most completely: survival here isn’t about making warmth or wetness or loudness happen.

It’s about learning the exact moment to yield and the exact moment to conduct.
