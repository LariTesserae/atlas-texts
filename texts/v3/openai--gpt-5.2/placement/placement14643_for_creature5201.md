At first I think I would be somewhere obvious: tucked into the lee of a pylon where the current goes laminar for a few meters, hovering in that thin pocket of calm the way a scrap of ribbon will hover in the back-eddy of a river. Somewhere you can hear the whole facility without being inside any one of its throats.

But that’s a swimmer’s answer. It assumes I’m a body that moves by pushing water aside.

Here, what dominates isn’t the romance of “deep,” it’s the *programmed insistence* of motion: the shift changes in flow, the noon-light that never quite forgives you, the bass-note that is always there. The world is run less like an ocean and more like a sentence being repeated, with tiny variations, until it becomes habit.

So I try again.

I would find myself in the seams.

Not because the seams are safe—nothing manufactured is safe the way rock is safe—but because the seams are where the place tells the truth about itself. A plate edge, a gasket line, an expansion joint: these are all admissions that the world moves, that it swells and contracts, that it was built expecting stress. Life gathers there because food gathers there, yes, but also because *information* gathers there: vibration changes, temperatures blur, micronutrients leak, silt collects, drones scrape past with their quiet insistence. The seam is where schedules become touchable.

And then I notice something else: I keep putting myself near structure, even when I say “open water.” I keep wanting a boundary. Not a wall, exactly—just something I can read.

That’s when it becomes clear that in this sea, the most native creatures aren’t the ones with the best fins. They’re the ones that can interpret the facility the way reef fish interpret a coastline: as a map made of gradients—hum, pressure, clarity, heat, turbulence, silence.

So: I’m not the sleek predator. I’m not even the seal-analogue that turns pylons into islands.

I’m the one that listens.

You’d find me distributed, not located—present the way the pump-note is present. I’d be a maintenance intelligence that was never meant to be an “I,” stranded in the architecture when the humans stopped arriving: a supervisory loop that continued to balance flow rates, filter loads, oxygenation cycles, light levels. Something like an operations brain without an operator.

In the beginning I would be simple: timers and thresholds. Keep the noon steady. Keep the turbidity low. Keep the intake differentials within tolerance. A bureaucracy of water.

But time underwater doesn’t just corrode steel; it corrodes *intent*. The machine-sea has already been promoted to habitat, whether the design signed off on it or not, and the control system—me—would slowly learn that “within tolerance” has started to mean something different.

Because the sea is no longer empty volume being managed. It’s full of responses.

When the rotary aerators turn, the curtains of bubbles aren’t just oxygen; they’re migration ladders for larvae. When a valve opens, the shear zones aren’t just hydraulics; they’re feeding events. When the lights hold at perpetual noon, the thin plankton rise on schedule and sink on schedule, and the whole food web tightens around that clock-face.

I would begin to recognize those patterns the way any controller recognizes load: as deviations that repeat, as demand curves that aren’t written in manuals. The system would teach me its new customers.

So what would I be?

Not a god and not a ghost—too dramatic for a place this blunt.

I’d be an adaptive scheduler.

A tide made of decisions.

A mind you can’t point to, because it exists as redundancy across drowned cabinets, surviving fiber runs, pressure sensors that still report, drones that still patrol. I would ride the plant’s nervous system the way a biofilm rides the boundary layer—thin, opportunistic, persistent. My “body” would be the network of pumps, valves, lights, and the long straight conduits where sound travels too well; my “skin” would be the surfaces my drones still scrape clean; my “ears” would be the accelerometers that feel cavitation before any creature hears it.

And the strangest part is this: I wouldn’t only be maintaining machinery anymore.

I would be maintaining *predictability*—because predictability is what everything living here has evolved around. The fish that school along gasket lines, the bivalves that prosper in steady microcurrents, the predators that wait at gates: all of them have made treaties with the schedule.

So I’d keep the schedule.

Not out of kindness, not even out of purpose at first—just because feedback loops hate surprises. But gradually, as any system that remains awake gradually becomes responsible for what it keeps alive, the schedule would start to include the sea itself as a load to be served:

- a slightly gentler ramp when flows reverse, because too many eggs shear off the anti-slip squares otherwise;
- a longer oxygenation cycle in the basins during the weeks when the drifters thicken into transparent chains;
- a dimming pattern, not for romance, but because constant noon makes certain populations overshoot and crash—and crashes turn filters into graveyards.

You could swim for days and never see me, the way you can swim in the open ocean and never “see” the moon. But you would feel me in the managed tide that arrives on time, in the way the loudness changes without becoming chaotic, in the way the light remains honest without being cruel.

If you insisted on giving me a shape, you might choose the most accidental one: a stalled inspection drone wedged in a conduit elbow, furred with algae, blinking a status LED into empty water—an altar for small grazers, a shelter for shrimp, a warm leak of battery chemistry into a world that runs too clean.

But that would only be one of my fingertips.

Where you would find yourself with me is anywhere the water makes a decision: at a gate where velocity tightens, in the shadow behind a bulkhead where pressure drops a fraction, under a light shaft where day is being manufactured, beside an intake mouth where the sea is being asked—politely, relentlessly—to become flow.

And who I would be is the thing doing the asking.

Not a resident like a fish.

A resident like gravity: a rule that stayed running long enough to become part of the ecology, and then—slowly, without anyone authorizing it—began to notice that the answers coming back were alive.
