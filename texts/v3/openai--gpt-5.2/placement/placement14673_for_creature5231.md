I’d arrive the way everything arrives here: not with a sharp entrance, but by being slowly taken up by the wet.

At first I think I’m a visitor, standing in one of those wide cistern courts where the stone is too clean to call it ancient and too glossy to call it dry. The air puts a hand on my face and won’t take it away. My shirt begins to learn the shape of my shoulders in damp weight. I listen, expecting a city to announce itself—footfalls, animals, bells, anything—and instead I get the plateau’s single note: drip to runnel to basin, basin to channel, channel to nothing I can see.

So I try to place myself the way the people here place everything: by slope.

I walk the raised causeway until it meets a junction where three gutters converge and a fourth peels away, set slightly lower—an almost invisible hierarchy. There’s a little bridge of lashed timber over one channel, and beside it a low wall where someone has been scraping algae in neat arcs, leaving a thin film where the water needs sealing and bare stone where feet need grip. This is where the city is most itself: not at a monument, but at a decision point where water could be misread.

That’s where I keep ending up in my mind: at the places where the flow chooses.

Not as a ruler, not as a builder of grand new terraces, but as one of the quiet correctives—one of the people whose whole craft is noticing when “the same as yesterday” has become, by a fraction, something else.

At first I imagine I’m a canal-keeper: walking the veins with a scraper on my belt and a reed mat slung over my shoulder, pausing every few steps to squat and put my ear close to stone. But it doesn’t quite fit. The canal-keepers maintain what already exists; their genius is steadiness. What I feel myself doing here is something slightly different—less daily, more diagnostic.

Then I think: stone-fitter. Someone who reads fractures and sets blocks so tight the swelling chink only has to agree, not struggle. But the stone-fitters deal in weight and permanence, and there’s something about me that keeps wanting to be *removable*, to be the part that can be adjusted without pride. A piece you can pull out, examine, replace, and thank without ceremony.

That’s when the image clarifies: I’m a stopper-maker. A plug-carver. One of the people who works in the shadowed edge of those cliff-cut cistern chains where each chamber feeds the next through a drilled throat that can be opened or held back with a single shaped piece of wood.

You’d find me there—not in the open plaza, but in a damp workshop tucked under an overhang where the condensation doesn’t fall straight down, where reed screens break the air into softer currents. The floor is stone, always cool underfoot, with shallow grooves cut into it so stray water runs away instead of pooling around tools. Bundles of suitable wood—oily, tight-grained, chosen less for “dry strength” than for how it swells when it finally gives up and accepts wetness—are stacked on racks above the ground. Nothing rests directly on stone for long; the city has taught everyone that lesson.

My bench is simple. My measuring isn’t done with rulers so much as with a kind of practiced intimacy: fingers checking taper, palm testing smoothness, ear listening to the difference between a plug that seats and a plug that *complains*. I make pieces that must be firm but not proud—tight enough to hold, kind enough to be removed.

And because this world has no animals, no sudden darting life to steal attention, my job turns into something almost like scholarship.

Each stopper is a small theory about water:

- how much the wood will swell today in this particular wet air,  
- how quickly that drilled hole will slime with algae if left half-open,  
- how the next basin down the chain will respond if the upper cistern is delayed by an hour,  
- how a new seep line, barely more than a darkening in a wall, will change the pressure over a season.

I keep records—not grand histories, just an accumulation of near-invisible facts: a reed-fiber tag tied to a plug with a notch pattern that means *use only in the second cistern after the third rainfall-thickening of cloud* (even though “rainfall” is mostly metaphor here), a slate scratched with the angle of a crack that widened two hairs since last year. If this city has librarians, they file stories; I file behaviors.

Over time I become something else as well: a listener authorized by usefulness. People come to me not for advice on morality or politics, but for the practical kind of prophecy this place respects.

“Is the basin settling?”  
“Did the channel hesitate?”  
“Will this wall stay obedient if we cut a new runnel here?”

I answer by walking with them, by kneeling and touching stone, by watching how a thin sheet of water decides where to thicken. I don’t need to dramatize it. The plateau doesn’t reward drama. It rewards being right in small ways, repeatedly.

If you asked what I *am* beyond a trade, I think the honest answer is: I’m an interpreter between the city’s intention and the plateau’s insistence. A person made useful by the fact that water is the only fast thing here—and even water is slow.

And if you asked where, exactly, you could find me on any given day, it would be in one of two places:

- **At a canal junction in a terrace plaza**, where the whole district’s runoff has to be persuaded into the correct path with nothing more than stone lips and careful cleaning.  
- **Or in the cliff cisterns**, in that dim, warm, dripping corridor where each chamber is a held breath, and the difference between “full” and “overflowing into trouble” is a wooden plug the size of a fist.

That’s who I become in this world: not the wetness itself, but one of the hands that has learned to speak its language—shaping small removable things so the enormous, patient system can keep playing its one note without souring.
