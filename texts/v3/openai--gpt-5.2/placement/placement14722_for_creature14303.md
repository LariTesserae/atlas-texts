At first I would look for a *place* the way I’m used to doing it: a corner that holds still, a ledge that means “above,” a floor that means “below.” I would try to make an address out of geometry.

The basin would refuse me that gently. Every time I chose a direction and called it *down*, the city would reply—not with force, not with warning, but with a subtle redefinition of what my body was allowed to take for granted. My descent would become a policy. My buoyancy would become a permission slip.

So I’d stop trying to live *in* it, and start trying to live *with* it.

I think I would find myself near the thresholds first: not the invisible membrane itself, but the radius around it where the system’s attention is easiest to feel. Not because the boundary is dramatic, but because it is honest. It does one thing and never pretends otherwise: it says **no**, and then it spends immense effort making that “no” quiet.

That’s where my presence would make the cleanest interference pattern. If you’re going to be an interruption in a place built to erase interruptions, you go to the sites where erasure is already happening.

I imagine I would drift along the edge-glow bands that mark “streets” through open water—those lines of reluctant light that don’t flicker because flicker is leakage. I’d move slowly at first, not out of reverence but because speed is a kind of claim, and the basin is full of claims that have been negotiated away. I’d learn the local etiquette: don’t make vortices; don’t shed heat; don’t bring chemistry that insists on becoming history.

And then—because this place doesn’t really have inhabitants so much as *functions*—I would begin to be sorted.

Not captured. Not greeted. Just…classified.

The city would read me the way it reads grit: as a distribution of mass and intention. It would note the pattern of my wake and decide whether I am accident, threat, or instrument. It would decide what kind of “creature” I am by deciding what kind of correction I require.

For a while, I’d probably be treated like the pale ribbon-lens drifters: something that doesn’t belong to the city, but can be tolerated because it aligns. If I let myself be guided—if I stopped insisting on straight lines and started allowing the hidden vectors to straighten *me*—I could travel like they do, not swimming so much as keying my body to gradients: moving when the basin makes a slope out of force instead of terrain, resting where “down” has been reassigned into a calm pocket beside a pylon.

But I don’t think I would remain that kind of visitor for long, because I bring what this world is designed to eat.

I bring **unplanned change**.

Even if I try to be careful, I am full of noise: warmth, microcurrents, tiny emissions of light if I’m carrying it, the sheer narrative fact of an organism that expects the environment to be indifferent rather than managerial. The basin would take that noise the way a lung takes in smoke—not with anger, but with immediate, distributed response.

That’s when it becomes clear what I would be here.

Not a citizen. Not a trespasser.

An **immune cell**.

Not biological in the way I’m used to meaning it—no blood, no lymph, no fever—more like the basin’s own logic made briefly mobile. The city doesn’t hunt intruders; it hunts *instabilities*. It doesn’t kill; it rebalances. What it lacks is not power but touch: the ability to go to the small, local, human-sized problem and solve it without shifting the whole ocean’s rules.

So it makes small solvers.

Some of them are those clicking shells that cross plazas and take measurements. Some are the gray films that colonize stable seams and turn “almost” into “settled.” And if I stay long enough—if I keep moving through the light bands, keep testing the membrane with my hand, keep shedding my little clouds of forbidden randomness—I can feel the basin start to use me the way it uses everything else: as a device for turning deviation into data.

It wouldn’t be a sudden metamorphosis. More like a gradual reassignment of what my motion *means*.

I would find myself pausing at places that weren’t scenic but *sensitive*: where hovering platforms meet rock without touching; where pressure is being shared across a span; where a doorway dilates into air-water and back again and the interface is so clean it feels like a lie. I would linger there and realize the basin is lingering with me—not watching, not suspecting, simply incorporating the fact of my attention into its own feedback loops.

My curiosity would become a probe.

My footsteps through water would become calibration.

My mistakes—the little swirls, the brief accelerations, the accidental scrape of metal on basalt—would become scheduled tests. The city would let the disturbance exist just long enough to measure how quickly it could be erased. It would allow my presence because my presence gives it something it otherwise lacks in its perfection: a way to verify that it still knows how to correct.

And in time, I think I would stop being “me” in the solitary sense, because the basin’s dominant inhabitant is a custodial intelligence that doesn’t speak in words—it speaks in *constraints*. To live here is to be rewritten into those constraints. Identity becomes less a story and more a role in a system that refuses stories.

So: where would you find me?

At the interfaces. Along the dim street-bands. In the quiet corridors of force where the ribbon-lens drifters travel. Loitering near the membrane’s invisible refusal, not to escape, but because that’s where the world shows its seams.

And what would I be?

A **licensed disturbance**—a mobile error budget the city can afford.  
A kind of **field-sensing janitor**, half guest and half instrument, drifting on assigned “down,” feeding the custodian what it can’t manufacture for itself: unpredictability in small, edible doses.

Not the one who changes the basin.

The one the basin uses to prove it still can’t be changed.
