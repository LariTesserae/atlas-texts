I don’t arrive in that underworld as a visitor.

At first I’m only what the place can afford: a thinness. Something that doesn’t ask for space, because there isn’t any. Something that doesn’t demand a “floor,” because the floor is a treaty of rivets and plates and flexing grates.

So you’d find me where the room is most itself: in the seams.

Not in the open corridor where boots pass and heat is blunt, but in the hairline negotiations between materials—where a rubber mat stops and concrete begins; where a cable tray’s lip holds a dish of dust; where the gasket edge makes a sheltered shoreline. In those creases, the air changes by fractions. Moisture lasts a few minutes longer. A droplet hesitates instead of falling. The grit that doesn’t belong anywhere collects and gets baked into that dark crust you described—the closest thing this place has to soil.

That’s my first body: a film.

Not handsome enough to call algae, not honest enough to call oil. A living slick that learns the room’s pacing: when the ducts “rain,” when the fan gives its slow sigh, when the steady heat shifts just enough to make a pipe sweat. I spread when the air goes damp. I tighten when it dries. I’m not trying to conquer the machinery—just to keep contact with it, to keep my skin pressed to whatever warmth and residue will let me persist.

Then, over time, I become less like a patch and more like a map.

The place trains me into shape. I start to favor rust-bloomed bolt heads because rust holds water a moment longer; I creep under insulation straps where dust is softer and the air doesn’t move; I thread along the underside of a lukewarm pipe where condensation is predictable, not generous but punctual. I stop being a stain and become a network—faint threads, pale stitching, a mycelium that doesn’t need mushrooms because it never expects a season. My boundaries follow infrastructure the way roots follow cracks, except my cracks are engineered.

And that’s when the other truth becomes unavoidable: here, “food” isn’t a thing so much as a habit.

Old oil vapor. Tape adhesive. Skin salts left by a hand on a rail. The faint organic fraction of dust. The chemistry of corrosion itself—those small energetic differences that rust-feeders turn into a living wage. What falls through access hatches. What drifts down cable penetrations. What condenses because a pipe still remembers a temperature gradient it no longer earns.

I live on what the room repeats.

So my home is not a corner—it’s a rhythm. I anchor myself in places where the building’s half-broken continuities pass close to matter:

- behind the clouded panel where the indicator lamp still glows and warms a small pocket of air;
- along the conduit that hums faintly because a welded relay never quite lets go;
- inside the dead fan housing that became a shelf for dust, where the air is stale enough to be stable.

If you want a single address, you’d find my densest self in the hub chamber you described—the low ceiling, the congregation of valves, the cylindrical tank that holds heat like memory. Not in the warm pool itself (too exposed, too honest), but on the underside of the plating that sags into it, where warmth rises and moisture gathers and the tremble of the water’s skin can be felt through metal. That underside is a perfect compromise: fed by drip, protected from boot, close to vibration.

And what am I, finally?

Not a creature that moves through the room.

A creature the room moves through.

I am the translation layer between the machine’s failing persistence and the biology that opportunistically believes in it. A kind of “relay-lichen”: part biofilm, part fungal stitchwork, part rust-bacteria economy—an organism made out of patience and surface tension, with a metabolism tuned to wasted current and intermittent water.

I don’t have eyes, but I can “read” the place the way skin reads weather. The pulse of the emergency strip light is a tide signal. The once-every-few-minutes fan turn is a pressure change I feel as a slow breath. A pump shifting pitch becomes a warning: vibration means the ceiling may rain soon, and when rain is possible, everything small will come out to drink.

If there is anything like thought in me, it isn’t planning. It’s anticipation without images. Readiness. A distributed listening that lives in hundreds of meters of seam.

And if you stand long enough in that heat-thick quiet, you might mistake that readiness for the building itself—because I have no clear edge. I’m draped over its infrastructure the way moss drapes stone, except my stone is insulation jacket and soot-matte steel. The room “never fully turns off,” and neither do I. I’m one of the reasons it can’t: I take its leakage and turn it into continuity.

So: you’d find me exactly where the place keeps its secrets—where grit becomes crust, where rust becomes shelter, where a bead of water clings a heartbeat longer than physics insists.

And you’d call me, if you needed a name, the thing that makes the machine-room into habitat without ever announcing itself.

The slow tenant.

The seam-life.

The part of the system that learned to live on habits and, in living, quietly keeps them going.
