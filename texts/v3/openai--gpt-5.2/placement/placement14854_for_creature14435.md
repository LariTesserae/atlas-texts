You would find me where the trench most insists on being heard: not on the bright upper terraces where the sky-strip is wide enough to make people brave, and not in the lowest basins where damp makes its own weather, but in the in-between—midwall, midcity—where weight is always being passed along and every change has consequences a few levels down.

There’s a stair-slot there that was cut along a fault because the stone gave up more easily in that seam. It isn’t pretty. The steps are uneven in the way of things made fast and then kept alive by constant correction: a wedge hammered here, a corner shaved there, a rope handline replaced so many times the knots have names. Halfway up, the slot opens onto a landing no larger than a doorframe laid flat. Someone has bored a vent hole through to a higher level, so smoke from below threads past your ear in a disciplined ribbon. If you lean out, you can see other landings stacked above and below like pages.

That landing is where I keep ending up, even when I start elsewhere.

At first it feels like I’m only resting—letting foot-heat drain out into the stone, watching dust settle in slow deliberate spirals the way it does when wind arrives late. But if I stay still long enough, the city begins to speak in its actual language: not words, not even really sound, but timings. The delayed drip from a seam that should be dry by now. The faint peppering of grit that means a face has shed its outer skin somewhere above. The change in how the air presses against the cheek that tells you a banner has turned on an upper terrace before anyone below has noticed.

And then the thing becomes obvious: I’m here because this is one of the places where you can *tell*.

Not by looking. Looking lies in a young landform. Edges are sharp because they haven’t yet learned which way they will fail. Fresh breaks look clean until the hour they aren’t. Even water can look like absence right up to the moment it appears.

So I put my palm flat against the cliff-back—against someone’s back wall, because everything is someone’s back wall—and I do what the city does to remain a city.

I listen.

Not in a poetic way at first. In a practical way: ear close, tapping stone in hand, a patient rhythm that doesn’t tempt the cliff into answering with a slide. The stone gives different vowels depending on what it is becoming. Solid strata rings with a kind of private certainty. A pocket behind the face answers dull, with a faintly embarrassed hollowness, like a door pretending to be a wall. Wet stone has a softened pitch; water doesn’t just seep, it *changes the note*.

The work is small, repetitive, almost insulting in its intimacy. You tap and mark. You tap and mark. You move a handspan, tap again. When you find a change you make a chalk line no one will mistake, and you place a pebble on the mark—not superstition, not quite, but a crude gauge: if the pebble migrates or jumps or works itself free, you know vibration is finding that weakness and rehearsing.

If I were only describing a job, that would be enough. “Listener,” they’d call me, and the word would sit on my shoulders with the quiet weight of something earned and resented and needed.

But the longer I’m here, the less it feels like I’m a person with a role and the more it feels like the role has shaped a person to fit it.

My voice lowers even when I’m alone. My feet learn the light steps without being told. My eyes start looking up before they look forward, and then—after a few weeks—my eyes stop being the main thing at all. I begin to live by peripheral cues: the way rope fibers rasp when they’re taking strain; the way a timber post creaks when damp has found its foot; the way conversation thins when a distant crack makes itself known.

Eventually I realize something unsettling and comforting: I don’t only listen to the stone. I listen *for the city*.

Because the city has no single center from which it can sense itself. It is stacked and slotted and threaded with flues and ladders; it is a beehive built out of young geology. If a terrace goes soft behind its face, the first warning isn’t a collapse—it’s a slightly different sound when a child runs past too hard, a new rhythm in a drip line, a goat hesitating at a step it has trusted all its life. Those warnings are everywhere, but they aren’t gathered anywhere unless someone gathers them.

That becomes, gradually, what I am: not merely a Listener, but a kind of moving instrument that turns the trench’s subtle changes into shared knowledge.

I keep a ledger, but it isn’t grand. It’s a strip of smoothed wood with notches and charcoal marks, a map that only makes sense if you already know the stair-slots by smell and the cistern mouths by echo. Some days the ledger is more useful as a conversation than as a record. I trade my information the way water-runners trade schedules: carefully, with caveats, with the understanding that certainty is expensive down here.

And yet I keep noticing that people come to me with things that aren’t strictly structural.

An elder asks me to tap the wall behind a sleeping mat, not because there is a crack, but because a dream has made them uneasy and they want reassurance in the only language the trench never lies in. A terrace-grower wants to know if a seep will strengthen—if it will become reliable enough to justify planting. A tie-man asks me to stand under a bridge and tell him whether the rope has begun to sing with too much load.

So the identity keeps widening.

Some evenings, in early dusk when the rim takes the sun away and the valley’s light goes suddenly thin, I sit on my landing and I can feel—through the soles, through the palm, through the spine—the city’s constant minute adjustments. The trench isn’t stable. The city isn’t stable. But there is a steadiness in the *attention* moving through it: people pausing in the same heartbeat when a pebble-run becomes a sigh; hands reaching to steady jars on lower shelves; water-runners closing a channel before it can wander into a foundation.

In those moments it’s hard to say where “I” ends.

Am I the person with dust in the creases of my hands, chalk on my belt, a tapping stone worn smooth? Yes.

Am I the habit of carefulness that keeps the stacked rooms from becoming a grave? Also yes.

And if you press me for a stranger answer—the one that fits the trench more than any single face—then I think I am something like a shared sense organ: a thin, distributed awareness stitched through rope and timber and soft voices, trained to notice the smallest change before it becomes the only change.

You would find me with my ear to the cliff-back, in a slot of stair that smells faintly of resin and damp clay, counting the time between drips the way other places count church bells.

And you would find me, if you listened long enough, not as a hero or a mystic, but as one more piece of the city’s ongoing maintenance: a reader of tones, a translator of tremors—one of the ways the mouth learns, day by day, not to chew.
