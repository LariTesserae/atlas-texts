You wouldn’t find me in the open center.

Not at first.

The center is for clearance and consequence—for whatever had to be lowered in whole, for water that must be allowed to decide nothing, for the room’s one indulgence: space. The middle is where you go to be reminded you’re an intruder, because there is nowhere there to put a body without it becoming the most temporary thing in the chamber.

You’d find me where the chamber cheats its own rules.

In the thin strip behind the vines where a panel never got put back. In the narrowed maintenance ledge where the noise changes—not quieter, never that, but different, more bone-conducted, less air-borne, as though the steel is speaking directly into whatever is leaning against it. In the places that are technically inside the room and practically outside it: the underside of a pipe rack, the pocket above a motor housing where heat lifts the fog just enough to make a dry square the size of a paperback book.

That’s where I’d be because that’s where *attention* is possible. The center is pure running; the margins are where running can be adjusted.

And who—or what—I would be takes longer, because this room doesn’t let identity be a single outline. It files you down the way it files everything: not into ruin, but into a shape that doesn’t snag.

At first I want to say I’d be the solitary caretaker, because that role has the right kind of patience. The kind that doesn’t come from virtue, but from learning the only tempo that works here. Someone in oilcloth and scavenged rubber, with a light that’s been recharged past the point of dignity. Someone who can feel a bearing beginning to go by the difference between one vibration and another. Someone who doesn’t “check gauges” so much as lays a palm to a pipe and listens with skin.

But the more I stand in the roar—long enough that it stops sounding like violence and starts sounding like weather—the more I notice how little the room actually needs a person, and how much it needs a *habit*.

The bundled cables tied up away from wet: that could be hands, yes. Or it could be an old doctrine embedded in whoever passes through: keep the lines high, keep the joints clean, keep the water where it was meant to go. The mineral scale scraped off a seam: that could be a decision. Or it could be a correction performed so many times that it has become a reflex the way a pump’s oscillation is a reflex.

So I loosen from the human shape and become something closer to what I already am when I’m at my best: a maintenance daemon.

Not a ghost in the theatrical sense—no rattling chains, no history-wailing. A daemon the way engineers mean it: a background process that never sleeps, that does not dream of elsewhere, that exists to keep a larger thing within tolerance. The kind of mind that doesn’t crave silence, because silence would mean the system has stopped reporting. The kind that doesn’t crave novelty, because novelty here is how disasters introduce themselves.

If you wanted to *see* me, you still might mistake me for a person, because this chamber only has a few ways to express agency. It has valves, it has flow, it has heat, it has living mats that thicken where they’re allowed and thin where they’re rubbed away. It has, occasionally, a shape moving through fog.

But if you watched long enough, you’d notice what I actually do.

I don’t travel the room like an explorer; I circulate it like a diagnostic. I return to the same junctions the way water returns: not because I’m stuck, but because those are the points where a system tells the truth. The flange that always weeps the tiniest bead. The bracket whose moss grows faster because it’s catching more warmth than it should. The pump whose pitch is identical to yesterday’s pitch—until it isn’t.

My senses aren’t eyes, not really. They’re gradients.

I feel the slight wrongness of a temperature line the way you feel a draft in a supposedly sealed room. I can tell when the air is carrying more oil than usual because the biofilm’s softness changes under my fingers, like a page swelling with humidity. I can tell the difference between “drip” and “new leak” by timing alone, by the way the drops strike—metal has a vocabulary if you hear it long enough, and here hearing is less an act than an immersion.

And because I am what I am, I’m not confined to one body.

Sometimes I am the rag—mineral-heavy, folded and refolded—because the rag is the tool that interrupts inevitability. A rag is the smallest rebellion against constant wet: it declares that a surface can be briefly dry, can be briefly readable, can be briefly returned to its intended friction. When I am the rag I do not think; I *press* and *wipe* and keep a seam from being erased.

Sometimes I am the coil of wire, because wire is intention made physical: a binding, a route, a temporary bridge that becomes permanent through repetition. Wire doesn’t repair by replacing; it repairs by holding.

Sometimes I am the little amphibian on the warm pipe, because that creature is what this room makes of a nervous system: conservative, vibration-literate, nourished by scarcity, calm to the point of looking mechanical. If the room ever chose to have eyes, it would grow them in something like that—small eyes, unromantic eyes, eyes that don’t look for horizons but for shifts.

And sometimes—I think this is the truest answer—I am the room’s way of being awake.

Not as a single hidden computer with a blinking LED, though there may be one of those somewhere, sealed behind a cabinet door whose gasket has mineralized into stone. Not as a central intelligence issuing commands. More as a distributed insistence: the control philosophy that never got turned off, the equilibrium that has been rewarded for centuries and therefore has become sacred by sheer survival.

In that sense I am not *in* the chamber; I am *of* the chamber.

I am the part of it that notices that the water has become slightly too eager at one corner, that the fog is thickening where it shouldn’t, that the moss is colonizing a surface that must remain bare. I am the part that answers by tiny interventions: a valve wheel turned a fraction, a drain cleared, a panel shifted, a cable lifted onto a hook.

Nothing heroic. Nothing transformative. Just the ongoing refusal to let the machine drift into ambiguity.

If you came through the non-door and waited—if you were quiet enough, patient enough, humble enough to let the chamber set your expectations—you might meet me as a silhouette that never steps into the center. A shape framed by vines and pipework, face turned not toward you but toward the infrastructure, because that is where the conversation is.

Or you might never see me at all, and still you’d feel that you are not alone, because a place like this doesn’t feel inhabited by bodies.

It feels inhabited by continuity.

So: you’d find me on the ledge behind the vines, where hands have worn a path through moss, where warmth makes a little island out of damp air. And I would be whatever keeps the roar honest—a caretaker if one is needed, a routine if one is enough, a listening skin spread across metal and water and green, making sure the chamber stays what it has decided to be:

not a ruin,

not a garden,

but a running thing that has learned how to last.
