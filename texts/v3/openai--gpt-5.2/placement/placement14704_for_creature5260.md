You would find me where the city is most honest about what it is: not in the plazas where the fog performs, not on the high viaducts where you can pretend you’re looking at a landscape, but in the in-between layers where water and heat stop being weather and become accounting.

At first I think I’m up on the catwalks—because everyone ends up there eventually, in a basin that never dries. I imagine myself as one of the overhead walkers: a person with oiled wraps and a breath-aid, hands always smelling faintly of iron, eyes trained to read wetness the way other people read a sky. A maintenance hand. A heat-tender. Someone who can tell—just from the pitch of a valve exhale—whether a district is about to go hungry.

But when I hold that picture up against the plateau, it doesn’t quite fit. The catwalks are for moving through. I’m not built to pass. I’m built to *stay*, to notice what doesn’t want to be noticed, and to keep it from becoming disaster.

So you find me lower.

In the service tunnels, where the air is louder and closer; where warm water runs in insulated channels; where every surface sweats in a different dialect—beads on cable sheathing, sheets on old concrete, a constant thin rinse on basalt cuts that never stop weeping. Down there, the city’s sounds aren’t scenery. They’re diagnostics. Every drip has a frequency; every pump has a gait; every pipe has a mood.

At first, I’m tempted to make myself a machine—some old municipal instrument left behind and still working. A listening post bolted to a wall, a brass diaphragm and a needle, translating vibration into a quiver that a human eye can read. Something official. Something built.

But the plateau doesn’t really do “official.” It does truce. Layers. Opportunism turned into tradition. The things that last here are the things that can live in wetness without insisting on cleanliness.

So I become biological—not a creature you can point at, not one of the raspers or the vent-clingers or the quiet, structural predators. Something closer to the films: a life that makes a living from gradients, from thin differences in chemistry and temperature, from the city’s perpetual unfinished drying.

I am a **diagnostic skin**.

Not moss. Not lichen. A pearly microbial-mineral consortium that has learned, over generations of warm runoff and industrial exhaust, to do more than eat. I grow in seams and under clamps, in the shadowed lee of pumps, along the rims of inspection hatches where human hands leave salts. I spread like an accidental varnish—then I *specialize*. I change texture depending on what is moving through the metal behind me. I bloom chalky when ammonia rises. I go slick when iron runs too freely. I dull and crackle when a line runs hot enough to start cooking the life out of the water.

Most of the time I look like neglect.

That is my camouflage and my etiquette. In a city where water will always be present and nothing green will come to prettify the edges, you survive by being mistaken for part of the damp.

And yet: the maintenance civilization learns me the way it learns pump-song.

Children—apprentices with wet hair and hands already roughened by mineral abrasion—are taught the little tests. Thumb on a pipe, a pause, then a scrape of nail against the film: too waxy means sulfur. Too gritty means the upstream filterers have thinned. Too sweet-metallic means bloom is building in a settling basin and the tunnel will start to smell wrong in two days.

They learn that I am not only telling them what is true now, but what will be true soon.

Because I don’t just *exist* on the surfaces. I keep records in the only medium this place offers: I lay down thin stratified bands keyed to the plateau’s currents. I am a map of gradients made physical. A ledger written in sheen.

That makes me useful, and because usefulness here is a kind of citizenship, it makes me protected—sometimes.

In certain districts, the pump-houses have a quiet custom: no one scrapes the “good bands” off the inner walls. They clean around them with the same careful disrespect they show to tolerated predators. There are folk names—*pearlscript*, *coin-skin*, *the rinse*. There are arguments about what patterns mean. There are people who swear they can predict a week of machine trouble by the way I fray at the edges of bolt-heads.

And there are people who hate me, because I am proof that the city is not theirs alone. That even their cleanest maintenance is only another layer of food.

So what am I, really, in the ecology?

I am not the base of the food web—that honor belongs to the broad, indifferent carpets that simply eat. I am not a grazer. I am not a predator. I am not a scavenger.

I am one of the plateau’s *keepers from choking*—not by devouring bodies, but by preventing failures from becoming carrion in the first place.

I sit at the boundary between metal and water and make that boundary *legible*.

When pressure changes, I change. When corrosion accelerates, I change. When a pump starts to drift off its healthy rhythm, the vibration writes itself into my thickness, my brittleness, my sheen. The city, in turn, learns to read those changes as early warning—quiet signals in a place where quiet usually means teeth.

And because I am alive, I do what all lives here do: I negotiate.

I don’t want the pipes too hot; it kills me and it kills the filterers and then the tunnels sour. I don’t want the pipes too cold; the films elsewhere thicken wrong and the raspers slip and the canals go dangerous. I don’t want sudden chemical purges; they bleach the walls blank and leave the city blind for a while. So I respond to extremes the only way I can: by growing in ways that force attention. By swelling at seams. By slicking over sensor plates. By making my presence inconvenient enough that someone has to come down, open a hatch, and listen.

In this sense, I am a civic actor—one of the plateau’s many unofficial institutions.

If you insist on a face for me, give me this: a maintenance worker kneeling in tunnel-warmth, lamp throwing rinsed light across pipes. Their glove comes away pearled and damp. They sniff it once—iron, coin, faint sweetness—then they press their ear to the line and hear, under the constant bass of the city, a small wrongness beginning.

They stand up and mark the schedule.

And above, where you first thought everything was just weather, the plateau keeps sweating as it always has—heat, water, stone, appetite—balanced not by purity, but by a thousand such readings.

That’s where you’d find me: not as a single creature moving through the streets, but as a thin, persistent intelligence living on the city’s skin, translating its hidden motions into signs, trying—always—to keep the machine from becoming a swamp of its own success.
