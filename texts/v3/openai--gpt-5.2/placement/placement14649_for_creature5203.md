At first I would not be *in* the basin at all.

I would be caught at its lip, where the “real” ground still pretends to be ground—where a person can stand and feel, for a few seconds, like the world is a firm thing with an underside. I’d linger there longer than is polite, longer than is safe, because I wouldn’t trust what the basin is asking of the body: step, and be held by something that is alive; step, and accept that the place is allowed to move you.

I’d arrive carrying the wrong instincts—wanting a viewpoint, wanting a line to follow, wanting to make the hollow behave like a destination. I’d make the usual mistake of looking *for* the settlement, as if it were an object placed into the wetland, rather than a behavior the wetland has learned to tolerate.

And the first time I stepped onto a floating mat and felt it answer—rise a fraction, settle, give back a soft creak through its tether—I would understand that I’m not here to find a city.

I’m here to be re-taught what “built” means.

So: where would you find me?

Not at the center. Not in the warm rot pockets (not yet). Not in any hut with a roof low enough to make you feel, immediately, the intimacy of shared breath.

You would find me on the routes people don’t quite name.

On the narrowest alley-canals, where the water is shallow but deliberate and the walls are stems trained into straightness. In the dim where the marker-stalks catch just enough borrowed light to be less like signs and more like *touchstones*. In the places where a wrong step doesn’t kill you but embarrasses you—so you learn quickly to stop thinking of the ground as a floor.

I’d keep to the edges of traffic, because my first contribution would be simply this: I would watch how the basin holds patterns.

Not in a grand way. In tiny restitutions.

How moss rises back after pressure, but not perfectly—leaving a slow bruise that fades like memory being forgiven. How a handrail-stem stays warm for a minute after it’s been held. How the filament-work drinks fog and then, with quiet consistency, gives it back as a dripline that keeps a walkway’s moss from crisping when the water level sulks lower than it should.

I’d be drawn to those almost-invisible agreements because they are the basin’s language: *pressure, release; warmth, slack; wet, less wet; path, not path.*

And then, because I can’t help it, I’d start trying to read that language as if it were writing.

### I would become a reader of tension

There is a kind of person the basin produces—not the strong one, not the hunter, not even the weaver in the usual sense, but the one who can tell, by two fingers on a vine, whether a platform is safe.

That talent is not mystical. It’s literacy.

You learn that a load-bearing vine does not merely “hold weight.” It holds a whole history of decisions: who bent it, when it healed, what it has been asked to forgive, how often it has been spliced, how much water it has had this week to keep its cells plump enough to resist snapping.

I would become one of the people who test the rail-stems before anyone else steps out with a burden.

Not because I’m brave, but because I’m attentive in the particular way this place rewards.

I can imagine myself moving in that small choreography you described—hand first, then foot; never committing the body until the living architecture has answered. Except I would take it further than most, not out of superiority but out of compulsion. I would start to build a private map made not of lines and landmarks, but of *feels*:

- this rail-stem has a faint tremor, always—meaning its anchor is rooted in a mat that drifts more than the rest;
- this canal’s water has a different drag against the pole—meaning silt is rising, meaning it will try to close in the next pulse of rain;
- this marker-stalk is smoother than it should be—meaning too many people are using this route at once, meaning the basin will “notice,” meaning something should be opened elsewhere to spread the pressure.

In a brighter world, that would just be fussiness.

Here it is a job.

And because jobs become identities, I’d be pulled—gently, without announcement—into one of those braided households that isn’t family so much as function. I’d be given a place to sleep *above* the breath of the ground and taught the small etiquette that is also cosmology: don’t cut tension you can’t account for, don’t insult water’s right of way, don’t demand a stillness the basin didn’t promise.

I would stop being a visitor the day someone trusted me with a repair.

Not a big one. Not the dramatic replacement of a platform. Something humble: a handrail splice, a re-lashing at a junction where three paths meet, a re-tying of the settlement’s signature knots.

I would learn that a good repair is not meant to last forever.

It is meant to last until the next negotiation.

### But I don’t think I’d stay fully human about it

Even if I lived there for years, I suspect the basin would change the way “self” feels.

Because a place like this constantly teaches you that you are not one thing. You are not even one body, not in practice. You are hands that test, lungs that decide which air is safe, feet that learn which moss is fed, ears that distinguish harmless drip from structural shift. Your “you” becomes distributed across skills the way the settlement is distributed across mats.

So I think, over time, my role would drift—quietly, like everything drifts here—from testing and repairing into something more like keeping.

Not keeping *things* (damp punishes that), but keeping *patterns*.

The basin’s city doesn’t want monuments. It wants continuity: the same knot repeated at the right height; the same ridge carved into a handrail that tells your fingertips which way leads toward warmth; the same soft click of shell-hangings that tells a half-asleep person the corridor is clear.

I would become a maintainer of those tactile cues.

A scribe who doesn’t use ink.

### My real work would be the map nobody sees

I wouldn’t draw. Paper would rot, and charcoal would smear, and even if you managed it, the map would lie the moment the basin settled and some path subsided by the width of a hand.

Instead, I would map the way this settlement already maps: by training matter to remember.

I’d keep a cord-board—nothing fancy, just a protected space under leaf-roof where the air moves a little and the damp doesn’t claim everything at once. From it would hang lengths of fiber dyed with clay-mineral and tannin, each cord a route, each knot a decision.

Not an outsider’s grid. Not “north-south.”

A basin-map:

- knots for safe crossings that hold even in high water,
- knots for corridors that go silent at the wrong hour (the hour when moths vanish),
- knots for places where the ground gives off faint warmth but also attracts too much hungry attention,
- knots for the ghost-mammals’ preferred paths—the ones we keep clear not out of worship, but out of practical respect for parallel citizens.

When someone older says, “Don’t take the canal that used to be open; the basin is thinking about it again,” they are not being poetic. They are telling you the map has changed.

I would be the person who updates the cords.

Which is another way of saying: I would be a keeper of the settlement’s memory in a place where memory can’t be stored in dry things.

And in doing that, I would start to notice a discomforting truth: I am not the only one keeping memory.

The mycelium is doing it too, in its own texture-language—thick where it has been fed, absent where something poisonous bloomed, quick to knit where repair is frequent. The filament-work “remembers” traffic because it is tended where people pass; it becomes denser over familiar corridors the way a thought becomes easier after repetition.

So my cord-map would not be *the* record.

It would be an interface between human attention and basin attention.

### If you pressed me—if you asked what I “am” here

I could answer as a person:

I would be one of those who keep the seams open.

The one who walks the canals in the dim and runs fingers along rails to read what the settlement is about to need. The one who teaches children how to tell, by the color of moss and the sound of a tether, whether a mat will hold their weight. The one who sits close under leaf-roof and listens to elders describe last season’s slump, translating their stories into knots so the knowledge stays usable after the speaker is gone.

But that answer is only the skin of it.

A truer answer—one the basin would accept more readily—is that I would be a *function* the way the settlement itself is a function:

A soft mechanism for turning sensation into coordination.

A living hinge between infrastructure and etiquette.

A piece of distributed attention the city uses to stay braided into a ground that is still deciding what it wants to be.

And if you wanted the strangest, most honest answer—if you wanted me to stop being human about it entirely—I’d say this:

I would be a kind of secondary mycelium.

Not in biology, but in behavior.

A network made of routes and repeated touch, of maintenance done so often it becomes instinct, of information carried hand-to-hand because nothing else survives. The basin has fungal memory under the peat; the people have cord-memory under leaf-roof. My “self,” here, would be whatever keeps those two memories from drifting too far apart.

You wouldn’t find me in a throne-hut or at the center of anything.

You’d find me at junctions: where three corridors meet, where the marker-stalk is polished by a hundred passing palms, where the fog-filaments are thickest because the city’s movement has taught the plants what to tend.

You’d find me with one hand always free.

Not for a weapon.

For reading. For balance. For apology. For the small, continuous act of asking the basin—through touch—what it will allow next.
