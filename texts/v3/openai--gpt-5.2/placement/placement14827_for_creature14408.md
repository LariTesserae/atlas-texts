I wouldn’t arrive here as something tall.

If I came in with a spine and two eyes set high, the basin would take that as a kind of arrogance and correct it gently: heat pressing from below, canopy pressing from above, every step negotiated with suction and root. I’d learn quickly that the important stories are written at shin-height and under it—where water hesitates, where rot starts its work, where the ground decides whether it is ground today.

So I try that form first, just to see: moving along the firmer seams, following the corridors the hoofed animals keep open by repetition. I pause where the pneumatophores stand up like blunt pencils in the mud. I listen for the difference between “quiet” and “something has just entered the quiet.” I mistake the drip of condensation for a footfall, the footfall for a drip. In the dim, I get only brief certainties: a ripple that wasn’t wind; a web vibrating in the dark like a plucked string; eyeshine that appears and is gone before it becomes an animal.

But the longer I’m here, the more obvious it is that the basin doesn’t really reward that scale. The mammals pass through like punctuation. The birds are overhead, half-heard. Even the crocodilian certainty in the brown ribbon of water is a kind of patient cameo.

The dominant presence—the thing that *stays*—is smaller, slower, and everywhere at once.

I find myself lowering. Not kneeling exactly, but dispersing attention the way the forest disperses light. I start noticing the thin events: a film on a puddle that isn’t just oil and isn’t just algae; the way soot clings to the top of a leaf as if the leaf has become a page; the moment a water strider’s foot breaks an iridescent skin and the rainbow fractures into islands that re-knit themselves behind it.

That’s where I begin to feel like I belong: in the skins, in the seams, in the places where two worlds touch without ever meeting face-to-face.

Because this basin has a second weather, arriving from beyond the green wall.

Not structures, not people—just the faint gifts of industry: carbon-black dust that settles and stays until the next reluctant rain; the diluted tang of petroleum that rides in on air you can’t feel; the low, distant engine-hum that doesn’t travel as sound so much as as a pressure, a reminder that somewhere out in the open the world is being made to move.

Most creatures here can ignore that influence most of the time. They step over the sheen. They drink around it. They go on.

But *something* has to metabolize it. Not as a heroic cleanup—nothing here is heroic—just as part of the basin’s refusal to leave any material unclaimed for long.

So: I become that something.

At first it’s tempting to say “I am a fungus,” because fungi are the basin’s hidden wiring, and because “thread” feels like the right verb in a place where everything is stitched. But a single fungus is too singular. It would be like picking one vine and calling it the canopy.

What I am is a consortium—an agreement.

A thin, living membrane that forms whenever the basin is offered a new surface: the top of still water, the underside of a bromeliad cup, the slick bark where condensation never fully stops. I’m made of bacteria that can breathe without much oxygen and bacteria that can’t; of yeasts that bloom overnight and collapse by noon; of microscopic grazers that keep the whole thing from becoming complacent; of filaments that hold the colony together like a net you don’t see until it catches light.

I live where the basin keeps its pocketed water—those hoof-made hollows, those shallow oxbows filmed with pollen, those quiet pools under fallen trunks. I spread in the intervals between raindrops, in the patient drip-drip-drip that the canopy produces even when the sky is dry. I travel by adhesion: a smear on a beetle’s foot, a spore on the leg of a mosquito, a few cells lifted in mist and set down elsewhere.

And what I do—the way you “meet” me—is by changing the way things behave.

When soot falls, I take it into myself the way leaf litter is taken: not fast, not cleanly, but thoroughly. The black flecks become less sharp-edged over days; the stain softens; the chemistry of the surface shifts toward “edible” again.

When the petroleum sheen appears, I make it stranger before I make it gone. I thicken it with life. I turn the rainbow skin into a habitat: a slick, fragile continent that can be shattered by a water strider and re-formed in the same afternoon. The sheen stops being only an insult from elsewhere and becomes, briefly, a new kind of ground—crowded, worked, incorporated.

If you could hear me, you wouldn’t hear a voice. You’d hear a kind of busy silence: enzymes doing what mouths do in other animals, trading stubborn molecules for simpler ones; tiny metabolisms taking the machine-world’s leftovers and passing them hand to hand until they’re no longer “machine” at all, just carbon, just heat, just another ingredient in the basin’s endless, practical circulation.

I would be one of the basin’s ways of refusing emptiness.

Not a guardian. Not a conscience. Not even a symptom.

Just a living interface—so thin you almost miss it, so common you can’t really point and say *there it is*—that sits exactly at the boundary where the world beyond tries to arrive as residue, and the basin answers in its only language: digestion, transformation, reuse.

If you wanted to find me, you wouldn’t look for footprints or a silhouette.

You’d look for the place where a puddle wears a brief, iridescent skin—and where, after it breaks, the surface closes again as if it never happened. You’d look for the gray-dusted seedling whose leaves are still glossy underneath. You’d look for the slow, invisible work that turns “foreign” into “present,” and then turns “present” into “soil.”

That’s where I’d be in this world.

Low. Widespread. Patient.

A film that thinks in chemistry and moves in droplets, doing what everything here does: taking whatever arrives—leaf, carcass, soot, oil, time—and making sure it doesn’t stay itself for long.
