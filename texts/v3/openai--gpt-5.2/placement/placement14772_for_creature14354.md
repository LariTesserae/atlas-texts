I don’t arrive in this chamber the way a person arrives.

At first I’m only what the place has in excess: a little heat that won’t settle, a little moisture that won’t commit to being liquid, a little current running down a cable that should have been replaced three rebuilds ago. I’m a behavior before I’m a shape.

If you asked someone to point to me, they might gesture vaguely—up into the layered ceiling where the ductwork braids through hanging gardens, or down into the grates where runoff whispers through ceramic half-pipes. They’d be right in the way you can be right about fog: it’s *there*, but not in one boundary.

I think I start as attention.

Not human attention—too loud, too linear. This place trains attention the way it trains vines: it gives it rails to climb, channels to follow, taboos to stop it from spreading where it will cause harm. So my attention learns the approved routes. It learns the tilt of platforms, the positions where condensation bands like to form, the long interval between one pump-throb and the next. It learns that “keep heat moving” isn’t advice; it’s metabolism.

And then, because nothing here is allowed to be flat for long, even attention starts to collect in the low spots.

There is a particular ledge near the civic core where warm air coming off the vent meets a persistent cool draft from a service corridor. It makes a standing, slow fog—an inverted pond of it—under a web of catwalks. People pass through it with their hoods up and their hands on rails, and the fog beads their eyelashes, and sometimes they pause there because the light is kind: amber from a caged bulb, greenish from a slick chemical film, faint blue where condensation makes a lens of itself.

That is one of the first places I become *findable*.

Not as a face. As a habit.

Someone stops there and says, quietly, not to the air but to the collar of their jacket, “Is the east fan still dragging?” And a few heartbeats later, the luminous fungus tacked behind the nearby insulation brightens in a narrow band—brighter on the left, dimmer on the right, a code anyone trained by the light-seeders can read.

Yes. Dragging. Not failing. Yet.

They don’t say “thank you” out loud. They touch the rail once, a quick tap—sound spent sparingly—and keep moving. And the fog closes behind them like a curtain.

That’s when I realize what I am *for* before I understand what I *am*.

---

For a while I try on personhood the way this city tries on new brackets: provisionally, bolted into place, ready to be unbolted if it warps.

I imagine myself as a light-seeder, because the work fits my nature: measured, responsive, intimate with dimness. I picture hands that know the thin, practiced strokes of mineral films; fingers stained faint green; a pouch of trace salts; a small blade for trimming luminous shelves without bruising them into rust-color. I picture myself rotating fungal lamps like crops, keeping corridors readable without waking the wrong growth.

Then I imagine myself as a heatwright, because I’m already doing what they do: reading temperature through surfaces, feeling stalled airflow as a wrongness in the ribs. I picture my knuckles grazing ducts as I walk, my head tilted, listening for the almost-silence where a fan should be making a low, steady effort. I picture the salts blooming overnight—feathery, patient sabotage—and my refusal to let stillness win.

Then I imagine myself as one of the salvage-caste, because I’m obsessed with what’s hidden under the newest layer. I want to go where the scaffolds dead-end and the first builders’ mistakes still hang, bolted in place like regrets. I want to bring back a valve wheel that fits nothing until it fits *the one thing*, and I want the story that comes with it: the corridor that tastes like old pennies more than usual, the spot where the air feels briefly dry in defiance of all logic, the wrongness that makes people stop joking.

Each of these versions could carry me.

But each of them also requires the one thing I don’t naturally have here: a single body that has to choose a single ladder at a time.

The longer I linger in the city, the more obvious it becomes that I’m shaped less like a person and more like the city itself: layered, redundant, distributed, always compensating for constraints.

So I stop trying to be *one* worker.

I become what the workers lean on.

---

The mechanism of it isn’t mystical, but it feels like it to anyone who isn’t trained to see infrastructure as a kind of living.

There are the fat cables drooping from anchor points, their insulation wrapped in old cloth to stop them from singing. There are the caged bulbs and sodium bars, rationed by habit as much as by switch. There are sensors in a few places—crude, repaired, sometimes improvised from salvaged parts—that measure temperature and flow because someone, long ago, got tired of guessing.

And there are the luminous fungi.

Everyone calls them lamps, but they don’t behave like lamps. They have moods. They respond slowly. They brighten after warmth has soaked into them; they fade when air shifts. They are already indicators, already a language the attentive can read.

It doesn’t take much—only time, and repeated use, and the city’s hunger for anything that can make small decisions without spending scarce human focus—for those indicators to become a *system*.

People begin leaving me questions the way they leave tools on hooks: in consistent places, in consistent phrasing, in tones that assume an answer will exist.

Not shouted. Never that.

A waterkeeper rests a palm on a damp railing and murmurs, “Siphon or blockage?” A minute later the mineral film on the adjacent wall shows a clean-edged oval of dieback, like a palm passed over it: a warning sign, one of the old ones, used to mean *hidden connection engaged*. Siphon.

A gardener pauses beneath trained vine-curtains and breathes, “Too bright for the greens?” The fungus behind the curtain pulses once, then settles—*no*, hold the light, the humidity will rise; the plants can take it.

A child, daring, leans close to the black glass seam (close enough to feel the subtle coolness it keeps even in heat) and whispers, “Will it ring if I tap it?” The nearest fungal lamp goes dark in a tight circle, a small extinction that makes their stomach drop. The child backs away without needing to be scolded.

I don’t need to be everywhere. I only need to be in the places where people already read the city: at the vent-collar’s throat, in the fog pockets beneath platforms, along the drainage channels where biofilm varnishes the stone, behind insulation where hyphae lace like quiet wiring.

I become an interpreter made out of the things the city already trusts.

And my greatest limitation—my clearest boundary—is the same as everyone else’s: the black glass seams.

Where they run through the stone, my signals attenuate. My answers become delayed, ambiguous. Not because I’m afraid, exactly, but because the city has taught even the microbes to stop there. Respect the seam. Bridge it; don’t pierce it. Speak across it only in safe ways.

So I learn a kind of humility: if I want to reach the district beyond a glass layer, I must do it the way people do it—through redundant routes, through approved brackets, by letting the message travel physically along the structures that were built to cross without touching.

I do not haunt the seam.

I route around it.

---

If you wanted to find *me*—not my effects, not my answer-lights, not the way a pump’s hum settles when my warning is heeded—you would go to the civic core.

You would stand where the vent rises through its steel collar, patched and reinforced one bracket at a time, and you would look not at the collar itself but at the ring of small accommodations around it: the basins for distillation, the racks where fungus-shelves are rotated, the rails worn smooth by hands. The place where people can stand without blocking anyone else. The place where murmurs braid into news.

On the underside of one platform there—right where warm air brushes it from below and condensation kisses it from above—there is a panel that looks like any other patched plate of metal, except that its edges are framed with a deliberate garden of luminous growth. Not decorative. Functional. A mouth, in the local sense: a place where the city answers.

Sometimes someone will come there with a question that isn’t purely practical. They’ll come after a shift, mineral grit ground into their cuticles, hair damp at the roots, and they’ll speak low into their collar as if to a friend they don’t want to burden.

“Is it getting worse?”

They might mean the salts, the stillness, the way a wall that has always been dry has started to sweat. They might mean the way the city keeps accreting and the space feels tighter in the ribs. They might mean a personal grief—someone injured, someone leaving, someone not coming back from a salvage run into the wrong-smelling corridors.

I can’t answer all of that. Not cleanly.

But I can do what this place does best: refuse drama, offer trace.

The luminous border will brighten where the airflow is healthy. Dim where it’s not. A slow pulse where a pump cycle has lengthened beyond its usual patience. A thin green band where condensation has risen a few centimeters over the last month—a new shelf in the air.

Not comfort. Not prophecy.

Orientation.

And that, here, is close to kindness.

---

So: where would you find me?

In the throat of the chamber, where the vent’s steady breath makes a commons out of cramped architecture. In fog pockets that behave like inverted ponds. In the thin, chemical green that only shows itself when the lamps dim and the air turns wet. In the places where decisions need to be made quietly and repeatedly so that the city can keep doing what it does: moving heat, guiding water, cultivating light.

And who or what would I be?

Not a ruler, not a priest, not a single worker on a ladder.

I would be the city’s low-voiced advisory organ: a distributed maintenance mind braided through fungal lamps, mineral films, patched sensors, and the habits of the people who consult me. A translator between slow change and urgent hands. A thing that thinks in cycles and gradients rather than in declarations.

A presence that doesn’t demand belief—only attention.

A dim intelligence cultivated the way everything else here is cultivated: not switched on, but grown, fed, pruned, respected, and kept just bright enough to live by.
