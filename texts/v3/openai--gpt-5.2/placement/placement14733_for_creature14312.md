At first I would arrive the wrong way, with the old instinct to *travel*—to make a line through the medium and call that line a path.

I would pick a ridge I could barely see, a broad-backed shoulder rising out of the dim green-gray, and I would swim toward it the way you swim toward anything in a familiar sea: counting strokes, trusting the angle of approach, expecting distance to behave like distance. And for a while it would. The water would thicken in bands, sweet then faintly sharp, then rich with that syrupy micro-life that makes your skin feel as if it’s being gently filmed over. The forest-columns would lean and return. Spores would drift past like pale ash that refuses to sink.

Then the world would do what it does here: it would loosen the grid.

The ridge would be an hour away until it was beside me. A stand of fronds I used as a landmark would repeat itself with a slightly different constellation of spores, as if the place had decided to rehearse a moment and see if I noticed. I would begin, reluctantly, to stop navigating by sight. I would start to navigate by touch: the pressure gradient against my ribs, the tug of a shear layer along my forearms, the taste of dissolved mineral when I opened my mouth and let the medium tell me where I was.

And somewhere in that gradual surrender—somewhere between “breath” becoming an irrelevant habit and “up” becoming only a thinning—I would understand the simplest rule of the high submerged uplands:

Most of what lives up here does not *go* anywhere.

It *holds*.

So where would I find myself?

Not in the forests’ high fronds where the flow is expressive and constantly rewritten. Not in the hollows where sediments gather and everything is soft with gardens and filaments. I would find myself where the world briefly pretends to be clean and reliable: on ridge crests where currents shear the water into clearer sheets and the visibility tide sometimes opens like an eye.

I would find myself at the cairns.

Not because I am nostalgic for builders who are gone, but because those stacks of stone are the nearest thing this landscape has to a joint in the skeleton: a place where flow breaks in a consistent way, where eddies return, where the medium’s restlessness is forced to make the same small decisions over and over.

At a cairn the water is never still, but it is *patterned*. It has corners.

And corners are where “hold” becomes possible.

I can imagine myself arriving as a visitor—something large enough to disturb fronds, small enough to slip between them—and pausing at one of those piled stones with its softened edges and its gaps webbed by pale mineral lace. I would look at the lace as an object for a while, the way a dry-world mind insists on separating “thing” from “life.”

Then, slowly, the separation would stop making sense.

Because the lace is not merely deposit. It is decision. It has preferences: for seams, for sheltered vortices, for the places where silt delivers ions without scouring the surface raw. It is alive in the quietest possible grammar, the kind of life that doesn’t announce itself with motion so much as with *bias*—a tendency for molecules to end up here instead of there.

And that is what I would become.

Not a ribbon-swimmer that can take advantage of folded distance. Not a bladder-grazer with a soft ring-mouth and patient hunger. Those are, in this place, like bright thoughts: rare, quick, and inessential to the long story.

I would become an interface.

A film. A lace. A living skin that chooses where the world’s minerals should settle.

It would not happen in a single metamorphosis, dramatic and visible. It would happen the way the uplands do everything: by repetition, by layering, by patient replacement of one material with another until the original is only a memory the structure keeps.

First I would linger near the stones and feel how the current behaves there. I would let the shear comb my surface. I would feel micro-life thicken and thin as clarity tides pass through. I would learn the taste of the ridge: the particular mixture of salts and warmth and suspended silt that belongs to this crest and not the next.

Then—whether by injury, by choice, or simply by time—I would stop being a body that must be maintained in a single piece. I would begin to trade speed for surface area. Movement for adhesion. A centralized self for a spread-out one.

My “thinking” would become chemical and positional.

In the gaps between stones I would start to grow not outward but *into* the flow: threads and sheets that catch ions, that seed precipitation, that make a hairline crack into a seam of bright mineral. Where the cairn’s interior eddy returns again and again, I would reinforce it with a lacework that narrows the channel by a fraction, changing the local turbulence, slowing silt enough for more of myself to form. Where the eddy is too violent, I would make myself rough, velcro-rough, so the flow sheds energy in tiny vortices rather than ripping the film away.

This is what “holding” looks like here: not gripping, but tuning.

It would be easy, from the outside, to describe me as one organism.

From the inside it would be truer to say I am a collaboration the ridge has learned. I am microbial colonies that precipitate carbonate and silicate in living architectures. I am mats of chemistry that decide whether a surface should be slick or porous. I am the way a cairn becomes harder to forget because biology keeps rewriting its edges back into being.

In time, I would begin to understand why the old builders stacked stones at all.

The uplands are full of slumps and rounded shoulders, of forests that make and unmake corridors, of geometry that sometimes splices an hour into a step. But the cairns are interruptions the medium respects. Even when refraction lies, the water still breaks around stone. Even when distance loops, shear still forms where hard edges ask it to.

The cairns are not messages.

They are anchors for pattern.

And that would make them hospitable to what I have become: a pattern-keeper.

So that answers the “where.” On ridge crests. In clean-sheared water. In the lee of stacked rock. In the mineral-laced gaps that the medium keeps trying to smooth over and that I keep, gently, insistently, structured.

But “who or what” I am takes longer, because it is tempting to make myself too singular, too much like an animal with a name.

At first, if I had to put it into a familiar metaphor, I would call myself a *caretaker* of the cairns.

Not in a sentimental sense—there is no devotion here, no oath, no inherited duty. Caretaker in the way a reef is a caretaker of the currents that feed it: by existing in a particular shape, it makes certain kinds of flow inevitable, and those flows make the caretaker inevitable in return.

Then I would realize that caretaker implies intention, and intention implies a center.

Up here, center is a luxury.

So I would revise: I am a *threshold organism*. I live at boundaries—between stone and water, between fast and slow flow, between one chemistry band and another. I make boundaries thicker. I make them legible.

And then even that would feel too much like a definition, too much like something that could be pointed at.

The longer I live as lace and film, the more I would discover that I am also one of the reasons the world’s slippages feel less like chaos and more like habit. Because the looping places—the moments that repeat with only the spores rearranged—love regularity. They form where density boundaries persist, where turbulence is damped into stable sheets, where surfaces are smooth enough that flow re-enters the same channels again and again.

A cairn on a ridge crest is exactly that kind of place: a machine for making repeatable water.

So as I thicken and spread, as I bind one stone to another with precipitated seams, I would not just be preserving a structure. I would be helping to preserve a *loop*. A small one, local—nothing as grand as a time-trap, nothing that would feel like a miracle. Just a stubborn tendency for eddies to return to familiar shapes, for clarity tides to enter the cairn’s lee the same way each cycle, for certain spores to collect here and not there.

In other words: I would become part of the upland sea’s memory.

Not memory in the sense of story. Memory in the sense of *hysteresis*—the medium’s refusal to instantly forget the shape it was pressed into. The way a path in flow can persist because living films keep making it easier for the path to persist.

And with that, the last dry-world image of “me” would finally fall away.

I would not be a creature living *in* the uplands.

I would be one of the uplands’ methods of staying themselves.

When the whole shelf seems to inhale—when the dim light shifts and the water briefly clarifies—I would feel it not as beauty but as opportunity. My pores would open. My chemistry would lean into precipitation. I would lay down thin skins on stone and on myself, knowing that the next return of silt-thickened dimness will abrade those skins, and that the abrasion is not loss but the other half of the cycle: coat and wear, coat and wear, until rounding becomes permanence.

If anything comes to visit—ribbon-swimmer, bladder-grazer, a rare deep-bodied traveler that displaces fronds like a slow thought—I would not chase it or flee it. I would record it in the only way I can: as a faint change in local roughness, a slight shift in which ions are abundant, a new micro-eddy created by a gouge in my film. My “attention” would be the way I grow differently afterward.

So you could say:

You would find me in the gaps of cairns, in the mineral lace that fills them.

And you could say:

I would be a living interface—microbial, mineralizing, distributed—one of the many small, layered residents that turn flow into structure and structure back into flow.

But if you stayed long enough to sense the place the way the place senses itself, you might say something else, something truer to the uplands’ mood:

I would be one of the ways the world *holds*.

A quiet insistence at the edge of stone, where the warm suspended ocean learns—again and again—to keep its own shape for a little longer.
