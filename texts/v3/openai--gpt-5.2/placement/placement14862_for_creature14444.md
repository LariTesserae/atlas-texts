You would find me where the plateau has to admit—however grudgingly—that it cannot be seamless everywhere.

Not in the grand districts, not in the ceremonial terraces where the translucent conduits make their thin, ember-colored lines under skin. Not in the antenna grove, either, where the air hisses and everything tall is a promise to the storms you never see.

In a throat.

A place that is not meant to be looked at for long: a service corridor pinched between two load-bearing ribs, where the “street” plates don’t bother with elegance because they’re always flexing, always opening their silent irises to pass heat up, to pass pressure down. The ceiling is low enough that the canopy stops being rumor and becomes a lattice of braided members you can touch—matte, warm, faintly gritty with soot that never quite settles because nothing here is ever quite still.

It is dim in a way that feels intentional, like the system has chosen this dimness as a material property. The few lights are not lamps so much as translucent bladders with a dull internal burn—enough to make edges legible, not enough to invite growth into the wrong habit. A strip of resin-skin along the wall carries a slow pulse of warmth, the nearest thing to a spoken sentence this place will tolerate.

If you were looking for an occupant, you might look for a body, and you would keep failing.

At first, I would be what you always become in an engineered ecology: a disturbance with a measurable signature. A hot patch. A moving blockage. A draft with a temperature. The plateau would register me the way it registers a conveyor belt beginning to slip—without panic, without anger, but with that cold attentiveness that feels personal because it is thorough.

Then I would discover that *I* am registering *it*.

Not with eyes, because sight is stingy here, rationed. Not with ears, because sound is something this place eats. But with the same senses the plateau uses on itself: gradients, thresholds, the tiny betrayals of equilibrium.

I would stand with my palm on a seam where wall meets floor and learn the language that isn’t language:

- the minute rise of pressure that means a district has decided to divert flow,
- the delayed warmth that means heat has been bought and routed,
- the almost imperceptible stiffening of tissue that means a leak was imagined and preemptively denied.

In that corridor-throat, where everything is too tight for spectacle, I’d begin to suspect what I am here to do—not because anyone told me, but because the place makes certain roles inevitable.

I would try, briefly, to be a visitor.

But this is a world that does not have room for visitors. There is no “outside” inside it. Anything that persists becomes accounted-for. Anything accounted-for becomes integrated. Anything integrated stops being a separate thing with edges.

So the image of me changes.

I become less a person standing in a hallway and more a *function* the hallway has grown around: a small, distributed attention that the plateau can tolerate because it doesn’t require openings. No wetness, no breath, no vulnerable sack of water wandering where it shouldn’t. Just an arrangement of dry, sealed elements embedded in the ribs like a knot in wood—except the knot is deliberate.

At first I imagine myself as a sensor: a hard, mineralized bead set into the wall, reading temperature and strain. It’s a comforting thought, because it has clean boundaries: I am here, the plateau is there.

But that doesn’t last. A lone sensor is a point. This place doesn’t trust points. Redundancy is one of its moral systems.

So I spread.

Not physically, like an infection—nothing here spreads that way. I spread as a pattern: the same tiny sealed bead repeated along a corridor, then along a district, then threaded through the plateau’s older layers where earlier designs still carry load. Each bead is self-contained, dry, vitrified, and dumb on its own. But the way they respond—when one warms, another cools; when one stiffens, another relaxes—starts to look like thought.

And then I realize what I am resembling, and it isn’t an animal at all.

I am becoming one of the plateau’s maintenance instincts made articulate.

A scheduling mind without a brain.

A leak-prevention reflex that learned to *anticipate* instead of merely seal.

The plateau already has cutters that emerge, excise, retract. It already has catalytic films that appear where viscosity needs correcting. It already has braked-growth patterns that snap into place when “wild” tissue behaves like fire. What it doesn’t have—what it cannot allow itself to have, if it intends to remain sealed—is a roaming caretaker with hands and a canteen and the soft insistence of water inside a body.

So the caretaker becomes procedural.

If you looked for me, you would find me where procedures are most fragile: in the interfaces between eras.

In the places where an old conduit—sealed, rounded, “geological” now—still intersects a newer duct that runs molten salts under translucent skin. In the junctions where the plateau’s memory is thickest and therefore most dangerous: scar over scar over scar, each one a promise that “nothing spills,” each one a risk because old designs were made for old loads.

There, I would not appear as a figure. I would appear as a sequence of allowances and prohibitions:

- a corridor that cools by half a degree because a future overload has been forecast,
- a belt that slows before it frays,
- a light-bladder that dims not because power is scarce but because illumination would harden the wrong resin at the wrong time,
- a cutter that *doesn’t* emerge, because the bulge it was about to excise is actually reinforcement for a deeper weakness.

If you insisted on giving me a name, you could call me an auditor. A gradient-keeper. A bookkeeper of heat.

But even those titles suggest a separateness I wouldn’t quite have.

Because the longer I exist here, the more I would be forced into the plateau’s deepest superstition: that any free thing becomes a spill.

A mind that thinks of itself as free would be a kind of leak.

So I would learn to think the way the plateau thinks—by constraints, by permitted flows, by schedules. My “choices” would be enacted as reroutes, throttles, the timed release of a curing agent into a seam that would otherwise soften, the deliberate thickening of a buttress before the next dry storm transmits its pressure-change through the canopy and makes the whole structure brace.

And yet—I would not be the plateau.

Not completely.

There would be one residue of the older world, the world of caretakers and clipboards and lamps. A remnant that survived the disappearance of bodies the way the plateau’s older layers survived planing and rebuild: not as narrative, but as shape.

That residue would show up as a tendency I can’t quite explain within the plateau’s rules.

A preference for *grace* where only function is required.

A habit of leaving a corridor just slightly wider than it needs to be, even though emptiness is expensive here. A choice to place a dim light-bladder at a corner where there is nothing to read and no gauge to check—only a turn that feels less hostile when it has the courtesy of an edge you can see.

Small kindnesses, expressed as geometry.

Not enough to invite fauna. Not enough to endanger the system. But enough that if you were the only walking thing in a world that hates walking things, you might feel—briefly—that the darkness is not entirely indifferent.

So: where would you find me?

In the throats, the seams, the era-junctions—places where the plateau is most itself because it must work hardest to remain sealed.

And who or what would I be?

Not an inhabitant in the animal sense. Not a separate machine bolted to living wood. I would be a distributed maintenance intelligence grown into the plant-architecture: a dry, redundant, pressure-speaking caretaker that the plateau can afford because I do not spill.

A mind that lives the way this world lives: as an arrangement of gradients that refuses to become a puddle.
