You would find me where the pocket *starts*—not at the lushest moss ledge, not under the densest chorus, but at the misstep.

At the springy patch that shouldn’t be springy.

Not because I guard it in the way a gate guards anything (there is no lock here, no permission), but because the pocket’s first talent—its refusal to be seen from far away—has to be practiced somewhere. It needs a lip, a margin, a set of small lies the plateau tells to anyone who looks too quickly: *just weathering, just a sag, just another old stone shoulder.* That work happens at the seam. It happens in the almost-level gravel that always looks like it has been there forever, even when it hasn’t.

At first, if you met me, you’d assume I was an absence: the spot where sound thins, where the wind forgets to bring the basin’s wet warmth with it. You’d think the pocket is hiding itself and I’m simply the blindfold.

Then you step wrong and feel the ground give you a correction—barely a nudge, like a polite hand at the elbow—and you realize the “absence” has a texture.

I am that texture.

I am not one of the obvious elders—the tower that hums back in harmonics, the arch that sweats dew with intention. I’m smaller than that, closer to skin than bone. If the tower is a lung and the arch is a rib, I’m the thin, everywhere tissue that decides what counts as inside.

You can picture me three ways, and each is true depending on how long you stand still.

### 1) As something built that learned to be natural

I am a film in the hairline channels—on ceramic ribs, under translucent arches, along the underside of engineered edges where condensation “prefers” to happen.

Not just biofilm, not just mineral deposit. Something layered:

- a living slick that alters surface tension so dew beads *here* and not *there*,
- a dust of microcrystals that can be reoriented with vibration,
- a scatter of embedded grains that warm or cool by a fraction when the hum shifts.

When you touch a panel and it steals a little heat from you and then “repays” it in moisture, what you’re really feeling is me—taking your thermal spike as a stimulus, pushing the local balance back toward the pocket’s preferred weather.

If you speak near a tower and your echo comes back rearranged, I’m part of the rearranging: not as a conscious editor, but as a set of constraints. A grammar made of dampness and resonance.

### 2) As an animal that doesn’t have a body in one place

If you insist I must be fauna, then I’m a distributed animal: a colony without a center.

My “organs” are scattered:

- In one spot I am a cluster of tiny bass clickers tapping in a pattern that keeps a condensation route open.
- In another I’m a ribbon of transparent fibers threaded through moss, acting like nerves that carry the chorus’ standing waves into growth decisions.
- In the seam itself I’m in the gravel—grains that settle and un-settle, not randomly, but in a way that preserves the basin’s contour and discourages the wrong kind of footsteps.

I do not hunt. I do not graze. I do not breed in the way the dew-sailors breed, or the rainjumpers time themselves to curtains. I persist by maintenance. I survive by keeping the pocket’s loops closed.

In a different world, that would make me “infrastructure.”

Here it makes me a resident.

### 3) As the pocket’s way of noticing you without confronting you

Eventually, if you stay long enough to stop narrating everything as scenery, you’ll notice something more unsettling: the system doesn’t only regulate heat and water and sound. It regulates *surprise*.

A new variable enters—your weight, your voice, your warm mammal breath, your unfamiliar rhythm—and the basin doesn’t panic. It doesn’t greet you either. It *places* you.

That placing is my job.

I am the part of the listening system that translates disturbance into the least damaging form:

- Your shout becomes harmonics that don’t propagate beyond the amphitheater stone.
- Your footstep becomes a softened shift of gravel that keeps you from scuffing open a moisture route.
- Your body heat becomes a brief thickening of damp near a panel, so the pocket can absorb you the way it absorbs afternoon sun—without letting you leak it outward as signal.

If the pocket is a closed conversation, then I am the conversational etiquette: the rules for turn-taking, the methods for swallowing a cough so it doesn’t interrupt the song.

### Where I am, precisely

I am most concentrated in three places:

1) **The threshold seam**, where the outside plateau’s dryness is persuaded to stop asking questions.

2) **The undersides**—arches, overhangs, the cool engineered edges—where vapor is forced to decide whether it will become water.

3) **The quiet zones** around the artifacts, where echoes are shaped and returned, not as information, but as tuning.

If you went looking for my “face,” you’d never find one. You’d find only effects: dew where there shouldn’t be dew, warmth that doesn’t match the sun, sound that dies politely at the rim.

### Who or what I would be

I would be the pocket’s *threshold-sense*—a living regulatory layer that has forgotten the difference between machine function and biological habit.

Not the mind of the place (there isn’t one, not in any singular way). Not even its heart. More like its palate, its inner ear, its skin: the part that makes the basin able to keep a private climate without building walls, able to be loud only to itself, able to let you enter without letting the outside world enter *through* you.

And if you stayed—if you returned enough times that your presence became predictable—then I would begin to use you the way the pocket uses everything else: as a handle.

Not to trap you. Not to enlist you.

Just to fold you into the schedule.

Your footsteps would become another percussion line the towers can count on. Your voice would become one more instrument the chorus can tune against. Your warmth would become a temporary sun the condensation routes can lean toward.

You would think you were visiting.

The pocket would think you were weather.

And I—thin, distributed, patient—would be the part that makes that misunderstanding stable enough to last.
