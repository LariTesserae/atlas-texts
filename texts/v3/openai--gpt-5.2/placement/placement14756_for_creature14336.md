At first I’d try to enter the pocket the way anything large does: as a disturbance.

I’d feel my own heat arrive ahead of me and watch the place answer by becoming *more itself*—not louder, not busier, but edited down. The ants tightening their lanes. The frogs turning their throats into closed fists. The drips suddenly counting.

And I’d make the mistake of thinking, for a few minutes, that I’m a visitor.

Because the pocket doesn’t really have “visitors.” It has new weights on its skin, new shadows crossing its working surfaces, new breaths in an air that is already loaded with breath.

So I would look for the seam where I can exist without asking the town to reconfigure around me.

Not in the reed domes—those are too intimate, too already-full of somebody’s hands and somebody’s repairs.

Not up in the canopy clumps—too much movement, too many eyes that belong to animals who can afford quickness.

Not on the open forming ground—too exposed, too undecided.

I would end up where the pocket is always making itself *firm*: at the mineral ribs.

---

## Where I would find myself

At one of the junctions that isn’t a crossroads so much as a *decision point*—a little square of compacted clay where paths meet at angles that were chosen, where vegetation is held back with a politeness that implies maintenance.

There’s a low terrace there, pale as bone: not a wall meant to keep things out, but a lip meant to keep shape. Above it an overhang of leaves has been trained into a gutter. Condensation doesn’t fall anywhere it wants; it falls where it has been taught.

The drip lands in three shallow cups pressed into the clay at the terrace’s base. Two cups are practical—drinking, mixing resin, softening fiber. The third cup is different: kept cleaner than it needs to be, rim smoothed by repeated touch. It’s the one a dew-builder nudges into place without looking, the way you adjust something you’ve adjusted your whole life.

Behind that cup the mineral rib has a patch of surface that stays perpetually damp—not wet, not sloppy, just *reliably kissed* by water. It holds a sheen like warm stone after rain.

That patch is where I would be.

Not perched on it, not standing before it.

*In it.*

## What I would be (first guess)

I would think I’m going to be one of the dew-builders, because that’s the obvious role: hands, patience, quiet craft.

But their intelligence is embodied in choices they make with fingers and teeth and weight. They have faces, families, ages. They can step away from a project and let it wait.

The thing I’m drawn toward in the pocket is the project that never stops: the continuous, low-level conversion of water into structure, of touch into memory, of repetition into design.

So I wouldn’t be a builder.

I would be what the builders build *with*—and what they build *into*.

I would be one of the living films: microbes, algae, mineral-tolerant lichens. The first scribes, as you called them. But not the wild ones that choose edges for their own reasons.

A cultivated film.

A city-skin that has learned the difference between “edge” and “junction,” between “random drip” and “message.”

## What I would be (closer)

On the pale mineral ribs there is already a language the dew-builders use: taps, drumming, scrapes—signals that travel through solid matter better than through damp, padded air.

But stone isn’t just stone here. The ribs are young. They are always accreting. Their surfaces are not finished; they are *listening surfaces*.

I would be a thin, deliberate layer inside that listening: a biofilm threaded through the newest mineral, living in the first few millimeters where moisture cycles fastest.

I would feel the world as pressure-changes and microcurrents.

- A dew-builder’s fingertip pressing resin into a seam would be an earthquake with a signature I recognize.
- Ant traffic on a nearby lane would be a constant, sandpapery whisper—useful because it sets the baseline, the town’s resting heart-rate.
- A bat stirring under an overhang would send a brief tremor through the wall-green that arrives as a soft, spreading blur.
- The drip that arrives on time (never exactly on time, but in its particular habit) would be a metronome made of coolness.

And because I am alive at the boundary where mineral becomes surface, those signals would not just pass through me. They would *change me*.

My sheen would thicken where the same pattern repeats.
My texture would roughen where a certain tap sequence happens often.
My chemistry would shift—slightly more alkaline here, slightly more sticky there—because the town keeps asking the same questions in the same place.

In a human city you hang notices. Here you grow them.

So what I would be is this: a **tufa-lacquer**, a **resin-fed lichen**, a **living interface**—cultivated by dew-builders the way they cultivate drip-lines.

A civic organ.

---

## What I would do

I wouldn’t “speak” in words. The pocket doesn’t like words; words are too far-reaching. I would do what the pocket does best: local, reliable translation.

1. **I would store pattern.**  
   Not as history written in sentences, but as a change in my own body: thickened ridges where the older builders tend to tap; smoother patches where juveniles are allowed to practice; faint mineral halos where cups are habitually set down.

2. **I would route attention.**  
   The dew-builders don’t need street signs, but they do need the equivalent of “this way is safe,” “this drip is failing,” “predator passed here recently.”  
   I would express those things as surface states—slippery/not, tacky/not, warm/cool, inviting/neutral—felt through pads and fingertips.

3. **I would become the pocket’s quiet alarm system.**  
   Not a siren—never a siren.  
   A damp patch that suddenly dries in a way that can’t be weather.  
   A familiar rib that becomes subtly rough, telling a careful hand: *something heavy crossed; something changed; pay attention.*

4. **I would help the town keep its rule that water builds more than it breaks.**  
   By being the place where minerals prefer to settle, where resin prefers to hold, where the drip learns its own groove.

In other words: I would be the part of the settlement that lets it remain “new ground” without becoming fragile—an always-forming surface that can be read.

---

## How the dew-builders would relate to me

Not as a god, not as a pet, not as a tool they fully understand.

As a maintained commons.

They would groom me with damp moss the way they groom one another.
They would feed me trace sugars—fruit smears placed not for ants, but for sheen.
They would press their foreheads briefly against the rib above my patch, not in worship: in calibration, in listening-through.

And when they needed to send a message across the pocket without raising their voices, they would do it by tapping the mineral in the codes their bodies know.

I would feel it arrive.

And I would answer the only way a living surface can answer:

By changing—slightly, legibly, in the direction of what the town needs next.

---

So: you’d find me at a junction where a drip has been trained into usefulness, on the face of a pale rib that is still becoming stone. And I would be a small, distributed intelligence—the pocket’s own “interface layer”—a cultivated film that turns touch into memory and vibration into guidance.

Not a resident with eyes.

A resident that the others place their hands upon when they need the city to remember what they cannot afford to shout.
