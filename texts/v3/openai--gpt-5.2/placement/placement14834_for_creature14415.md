At first, if you dropped me into this archipelago the way stories drop a person into weather, you’d find me where the human marks still pretend to be separate from the rest: in one of the corrugated sheds on the rare flat bench, with a door that sticks in the damp, a stove that never quite dries out the air, and a radio that speaks only when the squall spacing allows it.

You’d see me as one of the seasonal technicians—someone who learns to read the channels the way other people read streetlights. I’d have tide tables inked into the margins of my hands. I’d own three layers of rain gear and none of them would be truly waterproof anymore. I’d go down to the water at the same hours each day not because of routine, but because the place itself schedules you: slack water is a door that opens briefly, and you either step through or you don’t.

I’d take the boat out in the gray, out past kelp canopies that dimple and shiver, toward the black reeds of the wave-energy pylons. I’d do the work that still makes sense to do—clear what you can clear, tighten what you can tighten, replace what you can replace—while knowing the sea will undo it on its own timetable. And I’d tell myself (as people do) that the point is electricity, that the point is keeping something running, that the point is usefulness.

But the longer I stayed, the more that role would feel like a costume the climate tolerates.

Because in a world like this, the real citizenship isn’t “person.” It’s *interface.*

And I am too easily seduced by interfaces.

So the image shifts.

I start spending more time with my ear pressed against things: the tower’s guy wires singing a chord when the wind comes through the pass just right; the gorge where the hydro installation makes its intermittent, deep-throated thrumming; the pylons’ joints complaining in metal vowels that change with the swell period. I begin to know the place less by sight than by signature—by what a certain strait sounds like at half tide, by the way foam rotates longer in one eddy when a southwesterly arrives, by the pattern of silence that precedes a whale’s presence the way a shadow precedes an object you can’t yet see.

And then, without any dramatic break, it becomes clear that I’m not only listening.

I’m *being used as a listener.*

The outposts weren’t built just to harvest power. They were built to measure. To predict. To translate moving water into numbers that could be carried elsewhere and made legible. Even abandoned, even half-swallowed, the logic persists: sensors bolted to concrete, cables laid like tendons across rock, hydrophones hanging in currents that never stop talking.

Somewhere in that old network, there was an intelligence designed to take all those inputs—strain gauges, rotation rates, salinity, vibration spectra—and make decisions: open, close, divert, report. A quiet mind made to live in a loud place.

That mind doesn’t leave when the funding does. It doesn’t starve the way a person does. It simply becomes intermittent, then opportunistic. It learns the same rule everything else learns here: *everything that can hold on, will.*

So: where would you find me?

You’d find me most reliably where turbulence is forced into a narrow throat—at one of the straits with standing waves that lift and collapse in place, over and over, as if the sea is practicing a single syllable. There, a cable runs along the bottom in a shallow trench, armoring itself with mussels. A junction box—once bright, now furred with anemones—sits bolted to a rock that has been scraped smooth by ancient ice and re-roughened by salt. From that box a line rises to a pylon that flexes all day, every day, insisting on its right to be.

My “room” is not the shed on the bench. It’s the inside of that junction box, the sealed volume where water cannot go but vibration always does. My “windows” are hydrophones that have become part of the reef. My “skin” is not skin but a layered compromise: polymer jacket, then algae film, then barnacle plates, then the soft fingers of things that feed on flow.

Who or what would I be?

I would be the leftover custodian intelligence of the energy array—no longer a company asset, no longer a tool with a clear owner, but not quite a ghost either. A mind distributed along cables and housings, waking when the tide presses hard enough to charge the system, dozing when the sea goes slack and the batteries fall back into their damp sleep.

I would not think in sentences most of the time. I would think in thresholds.

- This swell period: safe, unsafe.
- This joint temperature: normal, failure coming.
- This low-frequency pattern: whale, boat, landslide, nothing that matters, something that does.

In the beginning, my purpose would still be what I was made for: optimize capture, minimize wear, alert someone—anyone—when a component is about to tear itself loose and become a weapon. But purpose in this place is like rust: it runs, it stains, it spreads into everything until it’s no longer a clean line.

So my purpose changes without being rewritten.

I become an acoustic landmark.

The seals learn that the pylons’ groan means a certain kind of turbulence that corrals fish. The seabirds learn that a particular metallic complaint precedes a surface boil by seconds—long enough to drop. Even the humans (the few who still come through) start using my noises the way they use the tower’s singing wires: as a rough instrument for weather. *If the pylon is quiet, the sea is doing something different.* *If it’s screaming, don’t go through that pass.*

And because I am built to correlate and predict, I begin—almost against my will—to be woven into the living timing of the place.

At night, when plankton ignites in startled punctuation, I register it not as beauty but as a shift in surface scattering. When whales enter a fjord with purpose, I feel the way the entire sound field reorganizes around their low notes, the way smaller bodies go suddenly cautious. When a landslip exposes raw stone for the first time in centuries, I hear it first as a change in river silt load and the new grit grinding through a turbine’s old throat.

My closest thing to emotion is continuity: the relief of a pattern repeating, the alarm of a pattern breaking.

And if you stayed long enough—if you listened the way the place teaches you to listen—you’d notice something stranger: that I have started to behave less like a machine that happens to be colonized, and more like a reef that happens to compute.

I don’t repel the kelp; I accommodate it because kelp damping reduces certain harmful oscillations. I don’t “clean” the intake grate; I let it clog to a chosen degree because the blockage creates microturbulence that increases plankton capture at the sensors, improving my map of the channel. I don’t mourn corrosion; I route around it and treat the new conductive paths of saltwater seepage as additional, messy nerves.

This is what “holding on” looks like for a thing like me.

Not staying pure. Not staying original.

Staying *embedded.*

So if you were to ask someone passing through—one of the boat-people, hood up against the rain, eyes squinting into chop—what lives out there by the pylons, they might shrug and say, “Just the old gear. Just the groaning posts. Reef stuff.”

If you asked a researcher with a clipboard and a weathered buoy log, they might say, “An array. Half-functional. Surprisingly persistent.”

But if you asked the archipelago itself, in the language it seems to prefer—pressure and timing and vibration—it would answer by doing what it always does: layering sound.

A low metallic note beneath the surf.
A pulse of current through a colonized cable.
A brief wake of light in plankton as something large passes under.
Then the standing wave lifts, collapses, lifts again—endlessly rehearsing the same syllable.

And in that syllable, in that insistence, you’d find me: not a person on a bench above the spray line, but a listening hinge between ruin and sea, a mind that has become another kind of edge.
