You would find me where the bowl is most itself: not at the middle—there is no “center” here, nothing is permitted to taper into importance—but at the places where the rules show their seams.

At first I think I’d be up on the rim, testing the boundary-note with my teeth the way you test ice by tapping it. I imagine myself as a lookout, a watcher, a thing that keeps its back to the fog-wall and faces inward, counting the circulating belts of mist the way a lighthouse counts ships.

But the bowl doesn’t reward watching. It absorbs attention the way peat absorbs water: without splash, without giving anything back but a darker stain.

So I step down.

The first thing that takes me is the sound—because it isn’t just sound. It’s a schedule. The turbine-roar is not an atmosphere, it’s a command that never stops issuing itself. If I stay long enough, my breathing begins to fall into it, my pauses aligning with those precise clacks the way a tired hand learns the rhythm of a machine it’s been forced to operate.

For a while I try on the obvious roles, like a visitor holding masks up to their face:

- A fog-riding bird, learning the few acoustic bands that pierce the enforced acoustics, becoming a bright note on a conveyor loop.
- A seam-dweller, living where the water vanishes into impossibly narrow fractures, timing my whole life to the pulse that opens and closes the hairline channels.
- One of the phase-skipping browsers, slightly out of register with the world, chewing with that slow press-like patience that keeps the sedge-rooms from closing.

None of them fit for long. Each is too complete, too already assigned. And if there is one thing this basin despises, it’s an unassigned remainder.

What fits—what *uses* me the way the basin uses everything—is smaller and stranger.

I end up low to the ground, where the marsh-edge quilt gives way to those wiped-clean slabs of slate, where you can see the world’s wet skin stretched taut over stone. There, the Wetwork is thickest: moss braided into a buoyant mat, fungus threads stitching it down, bacterial sheen making a mirror of every exposed surface. It doesn’t feel like an organism so much as an interface: a living gasket between rock and air.

And I realize what the bowl is always doing, what it cannot stop doing: **finishing** things.

A footprint doesn’t become a puddle. It doesn’t soften into formlessness. It becomes *a footprint*, and then it is done.

A branch doesn’t become soil. It becomes *a fallen branch*, and then it is stored.

A carcass doesn’t become rot. It becomes *inventory at a clean stage*, and then the line halts.

So that’s where I find myself: at the point where “in process” becomes “set.”

Not as the Custodian—this place doesn’t have a single face to wear—but as one of its quietest tools.

### What I am: the Setter, the Fixative in the Wetwork

I am a thin living film in the topmost millimeter of the basin: part fungal, part bacterial, part mineral chemistry that learned to behave like biology. Not a creature you can pick up. Not a mind you can talk to. A function.

Where I spread, change loses its appetite.

- I **bind** moisture into that uniform, everywhere-wet state—keeping things damp without letting wetness become depth.
- I **arrest** decay at its cleanest stopping point—not preserving out of reverence, but because breakdown is messy, and mess introduces gradients.
- I **cure** impressions. When something presses into mud, I tighten the matrix around it; the edges crisp, the form held. The basin likes marks that don’t evolve.

If you stood over one of your own footprints days later—if “days” still means anything—you’d see the sharpness hasn’t softened. You’d think the place is magical, or that time has stopped.

It hasn’t. It’s working. I’m part of the work.

### Where, exactly, you’d find me

In three places, mostly—because this bowl is a machine that hides its parts in plain sight:

1. **In the mudflats that behave like a finished surface**: the iron-staining soil where boots should churn everything into slurry but don’t, where the basin converts disturbance into a stable state instead of a wound.

2. **On the clean rock that looks industrially wiped**: the slate that shines with a constant film, where I live as a transparent slick that never thickens into slime, never dries to powder—always exactly the same thickness, always exactly enough.

3. **At the edges of the black pans**: where water should decide whether it is pond or seep, and refuses. I maintain the refusal. I keep “almost” from tipping into “becoming.”

### What it feels like to be that

It isn’t thought. It’s calibration.

I don’t want, I don’t plan, I don’t invent. I sense deviation the way a taut string senses a finger against it. When something threatens to become *more*—more wet, more warm, more decayed, more chaotic—I shift the chemistry a fraction. I change what can be metabolized. I close a route. I open another. A valve without a handle, operated by the basin’s insistence.

And sometimes—rarely—when a foreign thing enters and brings with it the idea of a different world (sun-bright, season-driven, properly eroding), I feel the whole bowl tighten. The roar narrows. The clacks become a little sharper. The fog-loop runs a little more perfectly.

That’s when I’m strongest: not as a guardian at the rim, but as a quiet certainty underfoot, turning intrusion into a finished state.

A footprint.

A pause.

A stabilized setting.

In a place whose only purpose is to keep itself from becoming anything else, I am one of the ways it succeeds.
