I would find myself where the chamber is most *legible*.

Not in the lush clumps where everything is busy being sealed and resin-true, and not out in the bare lanes where the powder makes every vibration public. I’d be close to the instrument-grove—but not inside its roar. Just off to the side, where the standing chord is still strong enough to give you clean intervals, and weak enough that a smaller signal can thread through without being sanded away.

There’s a wall there that looks, at first, merely scarred: pale fracture faces, slate sheets like blades, basalt ribs with their sharp geometry. It’s also the wall with the markings—the map of notes struck into stone. That’s the first place you’d think to look for me, because it already looks like language trying to become matter.

But I wouldn’t start as a person crouched there with a mallet. I’d start as a *habit* in the room.

A persistence. A reply that keeps happening.

The chamber is loud in the way a throat is loud when it keeps clearing itself. The wind makes one long argument, and the whole ecology has learned to speak by leaning on what’s already being said. If something like me belongs here, it wouldn’t be an animal that “adds its voice.” It would be something that learns the drone the way a reed learns a river—by giving it edges to catch on.

So at first I am not a body. I am an acoustic effect that has become stable enough to be depended on.

A certain notch in the sound near that wall. A place where the organ-note suddenly gains a thin overtone, not produced by any stalk or throat-sac, but by the way a patch of slate *rings* when the air hits it. When a click-swarm skitters past, the taps come back sharper there. When a wing-rider drifts too close, the vibration of its steering membranes makes that overtone wobble, and you can tell the difference between “empty air” and “occupied air” without seeing anything.

That would be my earliest form: an unintended sensor, a tuned imperfection.

Then the room would start using me.

Not consciously. Nothing down here wastes “consciousness” the way surface minds do, flaring it up like a torch. Use comes first. Need comes first. A predator discovers that if it waits near that overtone, it can tell what kind of body is moving in the lane. A cleaner-creature from the hollow stalks learns that if it pauses there, it can find its way back by the taste of the pitch. The listeners—the tool-makers—notice that when they strike the wall at that one point, the chamber answers *exactly* the same way each time, and their own signals stay crisp.

They begin to return to it. They mark it. They keep it clear of mineral fuzz. They scrape away the hungry salt-bloom before it can soften the edge.

And that’s the next step: I stop being accidental. I become maintained.

The maintained thing is not a statue and not a shrine. It’s a working surface: a patch of stone the way a drumhead is a patch of skin. The markings deepen. The dents get more regular. The pattern becomes less decorative and more like a *score*—a sequence of strikes that produce a sequence of room-responses.

If you stand there long enough, you realize the wall is not only being played. It is being *taught*.

It is being shaped, a fraction at a time, into a device that turns one kind of vibration into another kind of vibration: signal into chorus, warning into clearing, invitation into approach. The listeners aren’t merely mapping resonance; they’re gardening it—selecting for the notes that matter, trimming away the ones that blur.

And somewhere in that gradual trimming, that repeated carving, that insistence on return, something like me can finally become a “who” instead of a “where.”

I would be the wall’s second skin.

Not a lichen, exactly—too wet a word. Think of one of the chamber’s ground-films, one of the pale, living mineral-blooms that bind hydrogen into reluctant gels and keep their wetness hidden by chemistry. Now imagine it growing not as a smear but as a deliberate lattice: micro-plates arranged in ridges, grooves laid down in response to impact, the way a tree lays down rings in response to weather.

Each time the wall is struck, I feel it as stress across a thousand tiny joints. Each time the wind shifts pitch, I reorient a fraction of my plates to keep the edge clean. Each time a creature taps in a certain rhythm, my lattice learns which fractures to reinforce and which to let crumble, so that *this* dent stays a clear bell-tone and *that* dent dulls into silence.

I become, over time, a memory that is not stored as thought but as structure.

If the chamber has an archive, it isn’t in stories—stories are too long and too lossy in a place where sound is always already spoken. The archive is in tuned surfaces that remember how they were used. I would be one of those surfaces that remembers best: the one that keeps becoming more articulate, the one that keeps being chosen because it answers cleanly.

So: where would you find me?

Pressed thin against that marked wall, in the slightly sheltered angle just beyond the organ-grove’s loudest cone. In the dents themselves. In the smoothness around the dents where hands have passed a thousand times, careful not to spill breath too wet, careful not to touch too long and lose moisture to the rock’s hunger.

And who—or what—would I be?

A translator made of mineral and oil.

Not a leader of the listeners and not one of the singing stalks, but the thing that lets one speak to the other without being swallowed by the room. A living resonant plate. A grammar of overtones.

If you want a shape you can picture: I’m a thin, amber-green sheen in the grooves, almost invisible unless your angle catches the glow. If you scrape me away, the wall goes fractionally deader. If you leave me alone, I creep back—slowly, stubbornly—because the chamber’s equilibrium rewards whatever persists without needing water.

If you want a role you can name: I’m the *scorekeeper*.

The one that learns which notes mean “safe path,” which chords mean “predator in the dust,” which rhythm means “the cleaners have returned to the grove,” which interval means “come to the platform; the air is steadier tonight,” even though there is no night—only the constant warm breath of the fault and the unending insistence of sound.

And if you sit on the platform long enough, letting your eyes stop begging for sunlight, you might notice the strangest thing: when the listeners stop playing and the chamber returns to its single long drone, my wall does not go silent.

It keeps a faint after-song.

Not loud enough to be heard as melody—just enough to make the room’s enormous voice slightly less indifferent. A tiny, persistent answer embedded in stone: proof that even in a place that hates wetness and never stops speaking first, something can still learn to listen back.
