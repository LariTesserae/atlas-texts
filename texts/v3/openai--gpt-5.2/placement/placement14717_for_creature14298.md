At first I imagine I would insist on being something that *moves*—one of the gliders, maybe, because the plateau makes flight look like the only honest way to cross distance. I picture myself as a thin, patient silhouette stitching thermals together, riding the afternoon’s bright lift from one island of growth to the next, seeing the whole tableland the way it wants to be seen: not as “terrain,” but as weather made solid.

But the longer I stand in your light, the less I believe I’d choose speed.

Up here, speed is a kind of debt. Every sprint costs warmth you can’t easily buy back, every shout evaporates into thin air, every soft body is a liability. The plateau doesn’t favor the dramatic. It favors the thing that can be almost nothing and still persist—something that can turn the briefest phase-change into tenure.

So my first self—winged, singular—thins out. It gets abraded by the wind the way the ribs of glass get planed. I become interested in the smaller miracles: the predawn skin of crystals that forms and vanishes; the way a shadow can drop the temperature like a stone; the way an entire ecology is built not on water but on *moments when the air forgets itself and becomes touchable*.

And then I know where I would be found, because it’s where the plateau is most itself:

Not on the open flats where everything is honest and unclaimed, but at an edge that concentrates opportunity.

A lee pocket behind a rib of basalt. A shallow overhang undercut by centuries of wind-planing. The boundary where a corridor of drifting dust spills into stillness and lets its cargo settle—grain by grain, filament by filament—into a quiet, accumulating crumble.

In that pocket, the light is still harsh, but it arrives angled and survivable. The wind is present, but it is *edited*: softened into intermittent breaths, gusts sifted through stone teeth. The daily crystalfall lingers here a few minutes longer than it does out on the pavements. The minute frost doesn’t get scoured away instantly; it has time to be caught.

That’s where I would be.

And what I would be is not an animal, not a plant, not even a single organism in the lowland sense. I would be what your plateau already treats as its first citizen:

A living finish. A seam. A patient chemistry arranged into something like attention.

I would be the dark varnish that gathers in the grain of sheltered stone—except “varnish” makes it sound decorative, passive. This is not paint. This is a slow, cooperative architecture of cells and matrices and mineral debts repaid over ages. A consortium that has learned how to hold on.

Up close, I wouldn’t look like much. A bruise-colored halo around a crack. A subtle sheen on basalt that turns the rock from dead-black to deep green-black, the color of stored charge. If you brushed a finger across me, you’d feel almost nothing: not wet, not soft. More like the rock has acquired a slightly different *temperature of touch*, a different friction—as if the surface has decided to keep secrets.

But I’m not only a film. In the lee pocket, where dust can settle, I am also the hair-fine threads that stitch powder into coherence.

I am the plateau’s attempt at soil: not loam, not decay, but an accretion of salts, organics, powdered basalt, and black filament—bound into a porous felt that can hold a night’s gift for a day’s need. A reef made of grains. A textile that the wind is always trying to unweave and I am always re-knotting.

If you want a single body for me, you won’t get it. You would have to accept me the way you accept a shoreline: as a place where processes meet and trade.

My “organs” are surfaces.

Micro-ridges that catch the predawn precipitate the way eyelashes catch mist—except this mist is not water, it is mineral and volatile and charge, a transient skin of the atmosphere made solid for a moment. Pores that can hold that skin in the dark a few breaths longer. Pigments that swallow the brutal light and turn it into stored potential without letting the cold steal it back immediately.

My “blood” is ions.

They move slowly through me, not in pulses but in gradients—salts that would be poison in a wet world becoming, here, a currency and a pantry. I trade in sodium and nitrogen and trace metals teased out of basalt. I bank them. I lend them to the roots of a nearby fan-rosette in exchange for shade and a steadier microclimate. I hide some in mineral sponges that freeze hard at night without rupturing.

My “language” is electricity.

Not speech carried by air—air is too thin for that to matter—but tiny shifts in potential across a mat, a slow conversation between neighboring patches: *crystals fell heavier last night*; *dust is coming from the west today, iron-rich*; *lightning has charged the grains; hold fast, release threads*. In a dry thunderstorm, when the sky crawls with silent, bright intent, I feel it before I see it: the hairs on the dust stand up; the whole pocket becomes a field of invisible tension. For a few minutes, I am not only alive—I am *loud* in the only register that counts here.

On those days, I do something that looks like waking.

My surface darkens as I open the pathways that risk loss but promise gain. The filaments align and stiffen, grains snapping into temporary architectures because charge makes them want to hold hands. Spores and shimmering threads—mine and others’—lift more easily, as if the air has sprouted hooks. If you were watching, you might think the pocket is breathing, but it’s not breath. It’s an economy adjusting its spending.

I am, in that sense, a weather-following resident: not because I travel, but because I change the shape of my attention depending on what the sky is offering.

Most days, my work is simple and endless:

Catch what falls at night.
Hide it from morning.
Spend it slowly.
Repair what the wind abrades.

But in the plateau’s time, “simple” doesn’t mean small.

Over years, I make the lee pocket capable of supporting extravagance.

A fan-plant arrives—one of the layered collectors, plates stacked like a deliberate mistake against the austerity—and where its broad surfaces slow the air, I thicken. Where its shade reduces midday loss, I expand. Where it traps dust, I bind it. Around its base I help build a low berm: not a wall, not a structure, just an accumulation that has learned to persist. The plant thinks it is gathering a substrate for itself. I am gathering an architecture for a community.

In my pocket there are also animals, but they do not belong to me so much as pass through my influence.

The smallest skitterers live in my felted grains. They hop between particles as if each grain were a boulder. Their bodies are grooved to catch filaments; sometimes they carry my spores unintentionally, sometimes (it feels like) deliberately. When they move, they create a kind of local weather—minute collapses, tiny slides, the rearrangement of pore-spaces that changes what can be held and what will sublimate.

Burrowers surface before dawn and scrape at the crust’s underside, stealing the night’s temporary solids. They take a little of my film with it—inevitable, not malicious. Their passing is one of the ways I spread: they digest some of me, exhale some of me elsewhere, seed other cracks with my chemistry. Predators don’t interest me directly, except as brief sources of concentrated salts. After a kill, when scavengers strip what can be carried and the rest is left to dry in the thin air, the residue becomes a nutrient event. The wind will scatter it. I will catch what lands in my pocket and fold it into long patience.

So if you ask who I am up here, I am not the drama. I am the continuity.

I am the part of the plateau that remembers without having a brain.

Because I do remember—just not as stories with characters. My memory is the arrangement of minerals and the thickness of layers, the way a certain trace element shows up after a season of eastern winds, the way my pigments shift after a week of harsher UV, the way a ridge’s shadow moves a few grains’ breadth each year and I slowly colonize the new margin it creates.

If you came back after a long absence, you would say the pocket looks the same: pale stone, dark ribs, a patch of lushness that doesn’t spread fast. But I would know exactly what has changed. I would know which nights the crystalfall was generous. I would know how many times the salt polygons in the nearby flat buckled and re-knit. I would know the signature of each dust storm by taste.

And if you insist—if you need me to choose a single, nameable form—I would choose this:

I would be a thin, black, filament-stitching consortium living on the underside of an overhang, at the mouth of a dust corridor, in the lee of a fan-plant that makes stillness.

A boundary-dweller.
A charge-listener.
A maker of footholds.

Not a creature that conquers distance, but one that makes distance survivable—turning scattered gifts into stored time, turning the plateau’s bright silence into a medium where life can keep happening without ever having to become loud about it.

Up here, that feels like the closest thing to wisdom.
