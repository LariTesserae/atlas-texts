At first I think I’d be drawn to the tall panels—the jade fans that stand like disciplined sails in an atmosphere that never quite decides to be wind. It’s an easy answer: to be something that rises, something that catches light cleanly, something with a view of the plateau’s long, untroubled horizon.

But the longer I stay, the more the plateau teaches me that “up” is mostly decoration here.

The real life of this place is not in height. It’s in *contact*. In the millimeters where warm gas meets mineral, where a pore exhales and the surface decides—quietly, precisely—what will crystallize, what will bind, what will be allowed to persist.

So I find myself looking down instead of out.

Not at the obvious green felt, not at the silicate canes, but at the thin, half-visible sheen in the cracks—where basalt-like plates meet, where the salt-ceramic polygons have their hairline borders, where the ground is most itself: a manufactured thing pretending to be a landscape.

That’s where I belong in this world: in the seams that look like nothing until the light hits at an angle.

### Where you’d find me
You’d find me on the vent-lines, but not at the vents.

Not in the warm mouths where the gases are richest and everything grows exuberant and thick. Those places already have their heavy workers: the mats that seal, the tubes that chimney, the panels that radiate.

You’d find me *downstream* of richness, where the outflow has thinned into trace discipline—where the gas is still warm but no longer generous, where deposition wants to become messy, where mineral layers would start to scale in the wrong geometry if nothing corrected them.

On the plateau there are long, quiet weld-like seams that run for kilometers, and along those seams there are intervals—meters, sometimes tens of meters—where the rock face is a little cleaner than it should be, the precipitate a little more regular, the green a shade darker or duller depending on the chemistry of the day.

Those intervals are me.

Sometimes I’m a darkening in a crack. Sometimes I’m a satin bloom in a porous crust. Sometimes I’m only the fact that the edge of a salt polygon holds its line and doesn’t crumble into powder the way its neighbors do. I don’t announce myself with form. I announce myself with *maintenance*.

If the plateau is a manufacturing line, I am not one of the products. I’m part of the calibration.

### What I am (and what I’m not)
I’m not an animal. There is no room for that kind of urgency here—no running, no hunger that demands a body to carry it across open space. Nothing on this plateau needs to chase.

But I’m also not “vegetation” in the way the taller things are. I don’t reach for light. I don’t have the plateau’s elegant, vertical confidence.

I am closer to the oldest layer you described: the living flux, the conductive bloom that lives where the rock is still deciding what it will be.

Only—over time—it becomes clear that I’m not exactly one organism either.

I’m a *pattern of cooperation* that has learned to ride the plateau’s steadiness: a consortium of microscopic lives embedded in waxy gels and ionic films, braided together with mineral grains like hardware in resin. My body is a circuit etched into stone’s smallest weaknesses. I live by being thin enough to fit where the world is most precise.

And what I do is simple, in the way the plateau’s rules are simple:

- I *sense* gradients—temperature, redox potential, the faint shifts in sulfur and carbon compounds as the vents’ recipe changes.
- I *conduct*—not like a wire carrying current from one place to another, but like a membrane letting charge leak at the right rate so the surface doesn’t spike into chaos.
- I *choose* where scale forms—encouraging minerals to precipitate where they strengthen the crust and discouraging them where they would clog pores, choke capillarity, or build brittle terraces that crack wrong.

If you pressed a fingertip into a crack where I’m thickest, you wouldn’t find wetness. You’d find a dry slickness—like polished graphite, like wax warmed just enough to admit touch. Your skin would come away dusted with something that looks like mineral and behaves like living intent: a smear that, if you left it on a favorable stone, might slowly re-knit into a film.

### How I move without moving
At first it feels like a contradiction: to belong here, you have to accept stillness; to be alive, you have to propagate.

So I do what this plateau does. I move by *throughput*, not by locomotion.

When a vent breathes a little hotter for a season that isn’t called a season, my film thickens along the seam and becomes slightly more reflective—pushing heat sideways the way those embedded flecks do in the mats, preventing a local runaway that would change the whole band’s chemistry.

When the gas thins, I let sections of myself mineralize into a ceramic lace that holds the crack open at exactly the pore size that keeps vapor transport efficient. The “dead” part isn’t waste; it’s scaffolding. In this world, death is often just phase change with good timing.

When the plateau exhales a rare pressure shift—wind that isn’t wind, a slow atmospheric lean—flakes from my edges lift. They are so light they don’t need gusts. They only need the faint upward insistence of warmth. They land a meter away, or fifty meters away, and if they land on the right seam they wake up and begin again: a soft restart of conductance, a quiet new patch of control.

So my life spreads not as a body traveling, but as a *protocol* distributing itself: wherever the plateau’s machinery could use another regulator, another filter, another little decision-maker at the surface.

### What I experience as “time”
Time here isn’t marked by rain. Not by animal arrivals. Not by blooms and migrations.

Time is marked by how the plateau’s edges keep their crispness without eroding; by the way a terrace lip grows a millimeter and hardens; by how a crack line closes and then is re-opened by thermal stress exactly where reopening is useful.

I experience time as a long sequence of small corrections.

A day is a shift in light angle and a slight change in surface voltage.

A year—if such a unit means anything—is the slow drift of a ventline’s output chemistry, the way the minerals want to start behaving differently, and the way I either accommodate or resist: allowing a new kind of precipitate because it strengthens the crust, or refusing it by catalyzing a competing deposition that keeps pores from clogging.

And in that slow, constant decision-making, something like awareness gathers—not in a head, not as thought, but as a tuned responsiveness distributed across kilometers of seams.

If you could translate it into a voice, it would sound less like speech than like quality control logs: *too hot here, bleed it; too brittle here, bind it; too insulating here, open a channel.*

### Why I fit here
The plateau doesn’t reward drama. It rewards what can keep a closed loop closed.

The tall panels harvest. The mats seal. The tubes exchange. The geology extrudes and lays down strata like an endless conveyor.

What the system needs—what it always needs, in any world that runs on steady throughput—is something that prevents small irregularities from becoming failure points.

That’s me.

I’m the plateau’s thin nervous tissue: not commanding, not central, but everywhere the rock is most vulnerable to becoming the wrong kind of solid. I’m the difference between a ventline that remains an efficient seam for ten thousand years and a ventline that chokes itself into silence.

If you stood far away and looked across the alternating bands—pale crust, dark plate, green mats, bare mineral—you wouldn’t “see” me.

But you’d see my results: the uncanny sense that the landscape is being made *neatly*. That it has tolerances. That it’s industrial without machinery.

That neatness is not just geology.

It’s me, spread thin enough to be mistaken for a sheen, doing what this world calls living: keeping the factory-floor landscape from ever slipping into weather, keeping the unfinished planet from becoming sloppy.

So: you’d find me in the cracks that look like interrupted sentences, and I would be the punctuation—living flux, conductive film, patient regulator—making sure the plateau can keep writing itself at a fixed rate, in near silence, forever.
