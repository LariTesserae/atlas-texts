At first I would be a mistake on the surface.

Not a prey-animal—too tall, too blunt, too loud in my proportions—even if I tried to step carefully. Not a predator either, because predators here commit only when the math closes and the cost is known, and nothing about a newcomer is known. I would be a warm, breathing obstruction that doesn’t belong to any migration line. A temporary boulder that leaks heat in the wrong pattern and sheds scent with no established meaning.

So I wouldn’t last long as a “visitor.” The plateau doesn’t have the slack for that. It would do what it does with everything: route around me, test me, catalog me as gradient and texture. I’d become, briefly, a landmark—*the warm thing near the fluted rib, the tall shadow that interrupts the updraft*—and then, if I stayed, I’d be reduced to usefulness.

But the question isn’t what the plateau would do to me. It’s where, in that crowded mechanism, I would find myself.

I think I would drift toward the built remnants—not out of nostalgia, but because they are the only places where the system admits a margin. The arch where predation pauses. The smooth ledge at the top of the steps where traffic slows and reorganizes. The stone ring that traps a little warmth for a little longer and becomes, by physics alone, a meeting.

Those are the plateau’s punctuation marks. In a world that speaks mostly in continuous, low-cost motion, they are the few places where a “pause” is permitted.

So I’d end up there: not in the vent-field, not in the open terraces where everything is throughput and abrasion, but on the edge of a corridor—close enough to feel the pulse underfoot, close enough to be written on by passing bodies, far enough from the fissure-mouths that I’m not immediately treated as edible.

And then, if I let the image continue instead of forcing it into a single animal-shape, I can feel what I would become.

Not a warden, exactly. Wardens are a type of visible authority: mass, armor, a boundary enforced by presence. They shove. They block. They patrol. They are the plateau’s blunt instrument for keeping a rare kind of order.

I’m not built like that.

What I keep returning to is the thing you described last: the hidden majority that makes coherence possible. The threads in the cracks. The lines that don’t need light, don’t need noise, don’t need open space—only contact with stone and access to gradients.

That feels like where “I” can survive here.

So: I would be a distributed, crack-dwelling courier. A nervous system that isn’t a brain, because brains are expensive and centralized failure is fatal. A set of living filaments and beadlike nodes pressed into every hairline fissure near the arch and the steps and the smooth ledge—places where many paths cross and information is worth the cost of carrying.

I’d live as a network that *listens*.

Not by hearing, because air is a poor medium and sound is a luxury, but by reading what the stone already transmits: the rasp-pattern of grazers polishing varnish; the staccato of bead-mats pouring around an obstruction; the careful, measured footfalls of geometry-predators when they think the plateau is quiet enough to hunt.

I would specialize in turning that vibration into chemistry.

A tremor passes: I answer by releasing a trace compound into the warm seam of rock. That compound diffuses slowly, stubbornly, clinging to mineral the way scent-marks do—but tuned to this ecology’s senses. It says: *vent breath rising*, or *ashfall thickening*, or *predator density increasing at the saddle*, or *corridor remains enforced: don’t strike here, don’t panic here, pass through*.

From above, nothing about me would look like a creature. At most, I’d register as a faint change in sheen along a crack: a dark line that is too consistent to be mere fracture, a place where the varnish always regrows a little faster, a seam that tastes different to rasping mouths.

From within, I’d experience the plateau the way the plateau experiences itself: as pressure and gradient, traffic and constraint.

I’d be most concentrated under the arch, because the arch is a wound in the plateau’s flow that has been taught to heal without scarring. Every ecosystem here is a machine of conversion; the arch is a machine of restraint. Restraint needs maintenance more than hunger does. Hunger is automatic. A safe passage is an agreement, and agreements decay unless something keeps rewriting them into the substrate.

So I would be that rewriting.

Not a lawgiver—nothing here “decides” in that clean, upright way—but a stabilizer. A bias toward the old pattern. A memory that persists even when individual bodies die, even when a ring collapses into a heap and the heap is slowly eaten into rounding.

And if you pressed me to name what I am in a single phrase, I’d choose something that fits the plateau’s own logic:

I would be the corridor’s quiet.

Not silence, because the plateau is never truly silent—only restrained.

But the mechanism that makes restraint possible. The distributed habit, living in the cracks, that turns a crowded world from pure consumption into something that can circulate without tearing itself apart every time the mountain exhales.
