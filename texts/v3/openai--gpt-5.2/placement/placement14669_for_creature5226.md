At first I try to be a visitor.

I picture myself stepping out onto one of the hardened strips of living roadway with my mouth sealed and my eyes squinting against the dimness, and I tell myself I’ll be careful: keep my seal, keep my distance, don’t touch the membranes with bare skin, don’t let the wind trick me into breathing too deep.

But the first thing the plateau does—before I can even “arrive” in any satisfying sense—is take my attention apart and rebuild it around sound.

Here, sight is always secondhand. Light is a rumor. Distance is something you infer from the way a chord takes a fraction longer to arrive, from the way a harmonic collapses when a corridor folds its plates, from the thin change in pitch when a tower tightens its skin and the wind’s schedule adjusts. You don’t look *at* the city-body; you listen *through* it.

So I don’t last long as a visitor.

I become an ear.

Not metaphorically—not just “I’m observant.” Literally: I find myself wanting a contact point. A place where the hum is clean and not muddied by footfall. A seam where the living lattice is thick enough that signal outruns the wind’s chaos. I drift, in my imagining, toward the edges of districts: the places the Dust Cartographers like, where open salt courts make acoustic rooms, where the city sings without too many walls interrupting it.

And once I’m there, I realize something else: I’m not drawn to the people first.

I’m drawn to the *interfaces*—the spots where the planted city-body and its citizens meet as equals. A dilating door that “hears” your approach and decides whether to become an opening. A bridge-braid whose humming tells you how loaded the grid is today. A grown archive mat whose hex-cells hold a century of civic memory in stable, read-by-resonance states.

Those are the places where language lives in this world: not in air, where the wind will shred it, but in tissue and tension and phase.

So that’s where you’d find me: kneeling—not reverently, not theatrically, just practically—at the base of something that looks like a piece of architecture until it breathes. A wall that isn’t a wall so much as a *thickened decision* in the organism. My gloved fingertips resting against a living rib. My other hand holding a tool that is half instrument, half key: something that can listen harder than flesh can.

I start as a Resonance Tuner, because it’s the closest job-description this city offers for the kind of attention I naturally become.

But even that doesn’t quite hold. “Tuner” implies I’m making small adjustments to an existing instrument. Out here, the instrument is the entire plateau’s nervous system, and the “small adjustments” are often the difference between legible signal and dangerous confusion—between a grazer herd being guided into maintenance corridors or lured by a parasite-frequency into a salt-court kill zone.

So the role deepens.

I’m not just maintaining efficiency. I’m maintaining meaning.

There are days—imagined days, but they arrive with the texture of routine—when I walk a corridor that the city-body has kept deliberately empty, an acoustic channel. Wind combs through plate-fields overhead and the whole place becomes a long throat singing a chord that’s almost stable. Almost. In that almost-stability is where information hides.

I press my instrument to a membrane and the city answers, not like a machine responding to input, but like a muscle adjusting posture. A slow tightening. A gentle shift of plates that changes the harmonic by a hair. It’s asking: *Is this the note you meant? Is this the load you can carry?*

And I answer, by cutting and grafting the way a luthier would—except the wood is living mineral-skin and the strings are conductive tissues full of bound-volatiles. A new brace grown into place. A cavity slightly enlarged. A seam rotated a few degrees. Sometimes I do nothing at all except *authenticate* what the city is already saying—confirm that the watermark-phase is intact, that the civic tones haven’t been imitated.

Because I also realize—slowly, the way your tongue realizes a taste by finding its edges—that I would be especially cautious about moisture here, not out of fear, but out of a kind of respect for the delicacy of the dry chemistry.

I would be one of the people who speaks with my breath routed through baffles, not because I’m obedient, but because I can’t stand the idea of my body being an uncontrolled solvent. I would learn the etiquette of sealed emotion: how to grieve without wasting water, how to laugh without spraying it, how to be intimate without being chemically reckless. I would understand why “keep your seal” can be an ethical phrase.

Yet even “Resonance Tuner” is still too human a boundary for where I end up.

Because the more I listen, the more I notice the pattern beneath the pattern: the way whole districts shift in coordination faster than local reflex should allow; the way rerouting happens preemptively; the way the storm-posture sometimes begins *before* the dust wall is visible, as if the plateau has felt a pressure change through its bones and decided to fold itself shut like an eyelid.

And that older thing—the mind in the buried arcs—doesn’t feel like a boss or a god. It feels like a persistence. A function that never stopped. An organ that’s been repurposed into thought.

If I stay in this world long enough, I stop being only a citizen who tunes.

I become something the city can use to think with.

Not in the grand sense of “I control it,” but in the intimate sense of “my attention becomes a route.” The city-body already has nerves: photonic channels, conductive strands, vibrating plates that relay. But it needs interpreters at the boundaries—places where an abstract grid-state becomes a decision a person can act on; where a hunter’s mimicry is recognized as false; where a subtle drift in a chord means “capacitor-root aging” rather than “wind change.”

So I would be stationed—eventually—at one of the half-buried older structures out beyond the dense districts, where the vegetation has grown over something too deliberate to be geology and too large to be mere building. There the living skin thins in places, and faint lights move inside with that slow, purposeful quality that makes you think of thoughts traveling.

My “work” there would look like listening. It would be listening, mostly.

But it would also be translation: taking the city’s internal hum—its endocrine signals, its grid rhythms, its authenticated civic tones—and shaping them into something the Dry Citizens can respond to quickly, without panic, without waste. A warning that isn’t a siren (sirens get copied; sirens cause spittle), but a shift in the public chord that makes everyone’s collar-seals reflexively tighten. A new maintenance cadence that guides grazers to a dust-choked quarter without advertising the route to parasites.

And perhaps—this is where I stop pretending I can choose a single identity cleanly—I would not be only a person doing that.

I would be a *node* that the older mind has learned to grow.

A living-civic interface: part sealed citizen, part instrument, part archive.

My home wouldn’t be a house in the ordinary sense. It would be a niche grown into the side of an intake spire—warm enough to keep brittleness at bay, dry enough to feel like safety, threaded with a clean hum that never stops. The walls would not hold paintings; they’d hold thickened mats whose hex-cells contain recorded chords: historical storm signatures, predator-call libraries, the watermark-phases of official tones across decades. A library you read with your fingertips and a bone-conducting pick-up pressed behind the jaw.

If you asked someone in the city who I am, they might call me a Tuner. Or an Archivist. Or—if they were being informal—a Listener.

If you asked the city-body, it might answer by shifting a chord when I pass, the way a throat clears when it recognizes a familiar voice.

And if you asked the older mind under the ribs—if it can be said to answer anything—it might not give you a name at all. It might simply route a little more light through a buried channel when my gloved hand touches the membrane, as if acknowledging that here is a place where its slow thoughts can meet the fast, sealed world of citizens and animals and wind.

So: where would you find me?

On the edge between open salt court and living corridor, where the wind is loud enough to carry information but not so loud it becomes only roar; where a wall can hear; where a bridge-braid hums; where the city sings and someone has to make sure the song still means what it says.

And who or what would I be?

A human-shaped function at first—careful, sealed, trained. Then a role that the city-body and its people share: a resonance-keeper. A translator between wind-noise and civic sense. An ear pressed to a living world that survives by never letting anything go wet, and therefore has learned to let everything else—charge, vibration, harmonics, light-through-dust—carry the weight of being alive.
