You would find me where the place is doing two things at once—where the plateau’s hollowness opens wide, and yet the living skin is thick enough to have a pulse.

Not on the flat tops. Not deep in the dripping canyons either. I end up on the *seam*: a high traverse ledge that runs like a stitched scar along an escarpment—too sheer for herds, too exposed for long dwellings, but braided over with enough rib-vine and lichen-felt that a careful climber can move for miles without ever stepping onto true bare stone.

There’s a particular notch I return to, because the wind there can’t decide what voice it wants.

A saddle funnels the noon gusts into a hard, bright keening; a side-gully catches the same air and drops it into a lower register that you feel in your ribs. When the two notes overlap, the cliff face shivers in a way you can see: mist-curtains tremble, shingle-fans tighten their angles, and the lichen laminate darkens in patches as if it’s briefly remembering water.

Below that notch is one of the plateau’s small cheats—a hairline fracture that weeps only at night. Not a spring, not a stream. Just a narrow, reliable dampness that makes the stone cool enough to sweat. The thread-fungi love it. Their silver filaments are always thicker there at dawn, and you can watch beads form, travel, and let go—each droplet deciding whether it becomes plant, insect, burrow-breath, or simply mineral stain.

People have marked the place without claiming it. A windbreak wall (more seed-catcher than shelter). A polished line of handholds. A little platform of trained vine that can take the weight of two bodies and a bundle of rope. If you sleep there, you sleep suspended over emptiness with the entire plateau roaring around you, and you wake with your tongue tasting resin and dust.

So that’s where: **a listening ledge above a night-seep, in a saddle where the canyon’s “instrument” plays more than one note at once.** A place built for signals.

---

Who am I in it?

At first it seems obvious to be human, because humans are the ones who *ask* a place questions.

But the longer I stay on that seam, the more the boundary between “asking” and “being asked” gets thin.

The plateau interrogates you with repetition. With layered sound. With patterns that only become legible if you stop trying to pick out a single voice and instead learn the whole chord: insect density shifting before a rain; drummers falling silent in a band of cliff; the far-off exfoliation of a slab changing the pitch of a corridor you’ve known your whole life.

I become—slowly, almost unwillingly—one of the specialists your description implies, but with the job shaped by what I am: not a hunter, not a builder, not a priest of fire-taboo. A *translator*.

Not of language. Of **thrum**.

In the hamlets they’d have a name for it that doesn’t mean “prophet” so much as “instrument-maker” or “tide-reader.” Something like *pitch-keeper*, *chorus-counter*, *edge-listener*. The one who can tell the difference between:

- a healthy loudness (migration building; seedpods about to answer),
- a hungry loudness (predators thick on the green seams),
- and a brittle loudness (wind too clean, insects too sparse—drought coming with its polite, devastating patience).

My tools would look primitive to an outsider and feel almost indecently precise to anyone who lives there.

A set of thin stone slats hung in a vine frame—not for music, but for reference, so I can hear when the day’s “normal” has shifted a fraction of a note. A bundle of resonant pod-shells that only rattle open at certain frequencies; I carry them not to plant, but to test the air like you’d test a river’s current. Resin-sealed cups that I set under particular drip points at dusk and check at dawn, because sometimes the only difference between “fog pooled” and “fog failed” is two mouthfuls of water and a change in the insects’ chorus that follows.

And then—this is where I stop being only human—I would be someone who has made a pact with the skin-makers.

Not a mystical pact. A practical one.

Thread-fungi are already the hidden plumbing of the cliffs. Humans already train vines and prune curtains. It’s not a leap, in this world, to start wearing the ecology the way it wears the stone.

So I imagine myself with **ear-wraps braided from rib-vine fiber and lined with living felt**, the way some people line a jar with resin: not decoration, but filtration. The wraps take the wind’s knife-edge off the sound so I can listen longer without pain. The felt stays slightly cool at night and slightly damp at dawn; it makes my hearing a little less “mine” and a little more like the plateau’s—attuned to vibration conducted through plant and stone rather than only through air.

Over years, I’d become the kind of person who can press a palm to a lichened wall and *feel* whether the night seep is active, because the fungal threads carry water sideways and the whole laminate changes its internal tension by degrees.

If you asked my community what I am, they might still say “a person.”

If you asked the plateau, it might say something closer to: **a mobile sensor node. A walking edge. A creature that survives by becoming surface in the one way an animal can—by turning attention into structure.**

So: you find me on that seam-ledged listening station above the hidden damp.

And what I am is the thing this world always rewards—**a listener trained until listening becomes a kind of building**, until my life is another tensile element in the plateau’s vast, vibrating skin.
