At first I think I’d be up high, because everything here is up high: riding the clean glare, letting the wind do what legs can’t. One of the thread-drifters, maybe—almost not an animal, just a small intention hung from filament, eating whatever blunders into my line. A life so light it doesn’t *live* in the plateau so much as in the space above it, where “ground” is only an occasional event.

Then I imagine the other extreme: something heavy enough to make the crust answer back. A grazer with mineral teeth, learning the map by the feel of stress-lines underfoot, knowing where the laminate will give with a polite *crack* and where it will ring like ceramic. Big enough to hear storms coming through my bones before the sky admits it.

But neither quite fits. The longer I stay in the description, the more the true inhabitant of this tableland stops being any single creature and starts being a *relationship*: wind to vane, dust to membrane, charge to root-mat, sound to behavior. It’s a world that doesn’t reward speed so much as *tuning*.

So I end up somewhere in between air and ground—at the seam where the plateau’s “machinery” is most alive.

You would find me along the upwind edge of a turbine-field, where the ranks of stalks begin to rise out of the crust and the buried nodes sit shallow enough that the static makes the grit stand slightly apart from itself. Not in the center of the array, where everything is already synchronized, but at the boundary—where gusts come in lopsided, where the first vanes start to chatter, where a fault in the laminate will decide whether the field keeps its shape or sheds a whole panel of itself downwind.

I would be a *tender*, though nothing here tends the way wet worlds tend.

Not a builder. Not a commander. A maintenance organism: a small-bodied, many-limbed crawler with a shell like layered mica and salt-glass, built to take abrasion as a constant. My senses wouldn’t be sight-forward; they’d be field-forward. I’d feel gradients the way other animals feel warmth—tiny changes in charge separation, the faint push of an accumulating potential in the buried capacitors, the way a storm front makes the whole ground start to taste “sharp” through my feet.

I’d have a fringe of filament pads—brushes rather than hands—that can do three things well:

1) **Clean and score.** I’d climb turbine stems and rake their vane-edges, stripping the salt films that foul rotation, leaving behind micro-scratches that control boundary-layer turbulence. The work would be delicate and repetitive, like grooming something that is half plant and half instrument.

2) **Patch and stitch.** When the crust opens a seam—those sudden percussive pops—I’d be there quickly, not to “heal” it in any sentimental way but to *manage the joint*: packing powdered dead tissue and refined grit into the gap, exuding a waxy binder that cures under static, turning damage into a better hinge. I’d be less like a medic and more like an organism shaped around stress engineering.

3) **Bleed and ground.** In strong wind the field overcharges. Too much stored potential and the next dry-lightning event doesn’t just crackle in the dust column—it jumps sideways through living networks and burns them into glass. My body would carry conductive threads and insulating layers, a moving compromise: able to take a dangerous charge and leak it slowly into the mat, or dump it into sacrificial “spines” that char and regrow. I’d sleep near nodes the way other creatures sleep near warmth—because the field’s hum is safety, and because my metabolism is built around being part of that circuit.

What would I *be*, in the way the plateau means it?

A walking impedance match.

A translator between wind’s violence and the biosphere’s desire to keep working.

And because everything here is loud, I would also be—inevitably—a listener.

Not to voices, but to the chord the land sustains: the roar, the rattle, the organ-pipe bass of fluted cliffs. I’d learn to hear when a turbine rank is slipping out of phase, when a conveyor mound is venting too hot, when a herd has crossed a section of crust thin enough that it will delaminate at noon. The information wouldn’t become words in my mind; it would become urges—go here, climb that stem, ground that node, avoid this ridge because it is about to let go.

If you watched me from a distance you might miss me entirely. Just a flicker of reflective shell moving among reflective shells, one more small hard thing in a world made of hard things.

But if you stood at the boundary of the turbine-field on a day when the wind is strong enough to make the air itself feel combed, you’d notice a pattern: the vanes nearest the edge turning cleanly, not screaming; the charge nodes humming steady rather than spiking; the crust seams opening where they should, not where they must. The plateau behaving like a mature system instead of a panicked one.

That’s where I belong in this world: not as its mind, not as its master—just as one of the countless bodies that make “factory” and “ecosystem” the same word. A creature whose survival strategy is the plateau’s strategy in miniature:

become compatible with wind.
