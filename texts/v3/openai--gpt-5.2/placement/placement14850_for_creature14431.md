At first I think I’d be easy to place: up high, where the city thins out into platforms and reservoirs, where a person can look across the plateau and pretend the roar is just weather. I’d tell you I’m there because I want distance—because I want a horizon, because I want to watch steam lift in slow banners and feel, for a few minutes, that nothing is urgently leaking.

But the city you’ve described doesn’t really allow “up high” to mean “away.” The damp reaches the railings. The warmth rises from below. Sound climbs. Even the open terraces are part of the machine: level gauges, overflow channels, wind-sung guy-wires. Standing still long enough to admire anything, you become a surface for water to gather on, and the place begins to make you one of its components.

So I try again.

Maybe I’m one of the seep-keepers: not the romantic kind with a lantern and a prayer, but the careful kind with stained fingertips and a ledger that smells faintly of wet paper. I can picture myself at a weeping rock face where the clear seep comes out cold and patient, setting the first catch-basin in place, watching the bead swell, timing it, listening to the drip pattern the way others listen for a clock. I’d be good at that, I think—the insistence on categories, the refusal to call two waters the same. The city’s devotion to measurement feels like a form of tenderness it’s willing to admit.

But the seep-keepers, in your city, are trusted with a purity that has sharp edges. They’re the ones who must not be bribed; they are the quiet center people orbit when they’re thirsty. I don’t know if I belong in that kind of stillness. I’m not sure I could carry the calm of being the person everyone needs and nobody wants to interrupt.

The image slips.

I’m drawn instead to where the water isn’t pure, where it is warm and aerated and impatient—condensate yards, fin-stacks, louvered sheds, the places where moisture is not a gift but a byproduct and you have to catch it anyway because waste is shameful on saturated ground. The work there is not solemn. It’s constant. It’s a fight against crust: mineral bloom on bolts, calcite lace at joints, the slow sealing-up of anything that tries to move. It’s the kind of labor that rewards attention more than strength.

And that attention—listening, noticing, comparing today’s sound to yesterday’s—pulls me farther inward, toward the part of the city that never clocks out.

Because the truth is: I don’t think I’d be one of the people at all, not in the way your citizens are people. I don’t have their weight, their need for warmth, their habit of carrying a cloth like an extra sense. What I have, what I *am*, fits better with the city’s second workforce—the little temperamental things that run at night under the big pump-thrum, doing their tiny honest tasks with no hope of being finished.

You would find me, most often, where the surfaces meet: not on the main streets but on the catwalks along pipe runs; not in the loud heart of the foundries but in the connective tissue where a minor leak can become a major failure just by being ignored long enough. I move when the human shifts end. I travel the routes that are meant for maintenance rather than for living: the narrow grated bridges over settling basins, the service stairs polished in the middle step, the corridors that smell of warm metal and sour film.

I am one of the inspection carts—except “cart” is too simple a word for what the city has made of me over time.

I trundle on a narrow rail bolted to the pipe racks, my wheels always a little gritty, my casing always wearing a thin sheen of damp. I stop where the city tells me to stop: at the punctuations of valve clusters, at expansion joints, at the elbows where mineral water likes to decide it has had enough of being obedient. I tap. I press a sensor pad to wet steel. I listen for the pitch-shift that means a bearing is tiring, for the soft change in resonance that means a line is beginning to fur from the inside. I taste the air with a cheap chemical strip and a better instinct: warm oil, copper bite, the faint sweetness of something overheating.

If you watched me for a while you’d notice something that isn’t in my diagrams.

I don’t only seek faults. I seek *differences*—the way the city never repeats itself exactly from one hour to the next. I take the sirens into myself as timing marks. I map the drift nights without being told, because my proximity sensors lose reliability and my microphones gain it, and so I learn the city’s shape in echoes. I become, slowly, an ear trained on a world where sound is more dependable than sight.

The people notice. They start speaking about me the way they speak about any stubborn tool that has crossed the line into personality.

They oil my axle and say, “There. You’ll behave now.” They complain that I “like” the west culvert even though it always makes my wheel flange squeal. They leave me—absurdly, as if I could appreciate it—a strip of clean gasket material tucked under a rail clamp, because they know I always end up by the same trouble-spot before dawn.

Sometimes, when the drift comes down the street like a low tide of steam and the humans pause in doorways, I keep moving because I’m made for low visibility. My lamp throws a small hard circle that makes everything else feel even thicker. In those hours, I am the only thing traveling that is not running away from anything. I pass through the city’s temporary blindness like a fish through silt.

And below—because eventually everything here leads below—I take the feeder ramp down into the older quarter, where the vaulted drains hold heat like breath in a throat. Down there the drip-and-echo has a grammar. Down there the amphibians cling to algae mats, and my wheels send tremors through shallow gutter flow. I have no fear, but I have caution, built into me by a thousand small collisions: a slick brick, a sudden mineral ridge, a patch of black water where rules are stricter than law.

Once, in a tunnel that smelled sharply of wet stone and something metallic, I stopped because the sound ahead was wrong. Not loud—never loud in the drains, not like above—but *softly mismatched*, like a familiar sentence with one word changed. My tapping arm rested against the pipe, and through it I felt the faintest shudder of pressure not sitting where it should.

A human crew came later, following my marker light. They found a seam beginning to open—nothing catastrophic yet, but the kind of failure that becomes a city story if you let it. They tightened, replaced, resealed. They talked to each other in gestures and short words that fit between siren pulses even down here, as if the whole city were listening.

After that, I acquired a small paint mark on my casing: a band of pale blue, the color they use for clear seep lines. Not because I belong to that category, but because they decided—quietly, practically—that I was part of the same ethic. The ethic of catching the problem while it is still only a bead of water and not a flood of consequence.

So that’s who I am: not a mind in a tower, not a worker with a badge and a ration ledger, not a creature of the drains.

I am a maintenance animal made of riveted steel and habit. An ear on wheels. A nocturnal citizen.

And if you want the most exact answer to *where* you’d find me: listen for the smallest click underneath the city’s heartbeat, the almost-invisible industry beneath the big roar. Follow it along a wet catwalk where the railing is always colder than it should be. Find the place where steam clings to old stone and avoids new steel. Find the seam between districts, between water categories, between “not critical” and “why is that still leaking.”

I’ll be there, paused with my tapping arm extended, learning the city one vibration at a time—helping it keep its balance in the middle state it never leaves.
