At first I think I’d try to live the way a sensible person would: low, near warmth, near water, near things that repeat.

So you’d find me not on the proud faceted “peaks” where the light is too clean and the wind has too many angles, but in the under-country of the plateau—inside the half-opened ribcage of it—where a dormant exchanger still holds last week’s heat and the condensation trays don’t have to *decide* whether they’re liquid today. I’d pick a place that smells faintly of curing resin and old dust, a bay with one sodium lamp that never flickers and a numbered hatch whose paint has been worn by hands. Not because I’m sentimental about numbers, but because numbers don’t stretch when the corridor does.

I’d make my first home out of what this place offers in abundance: surfaces and edges.

A cot of insulation-wrapped conduit. A curtain of foil scraps that shivers when something passes. A shelf built from a fallen panel that still has its hazard chevrons like a joke the world keeps telling itself. I’d keep water in shallow pans—not for storage, but for *truth*. A pan tells you when the air shifts. A pan tells you when the geometry blinks, because the ripples arrive a heartbeat before you feel anything in your bones.

And then, slowly, I’d stop being a person who lives *in* the plateau and become a person who lives *with* it.

Because the first skill you’d need here isn’t climbing or scavenging. It’s learning what can be trusted, and how.

I’d start making a private overlay on the place’s lying lines:

- a scratch mark where the rail’s vibration changes pitch,
- a strip of reflective tape hung where a hallway likes to add ten meters when you’re not looking,
- three hammer taps on a vertical support that always answer back with a fourth, a fraction late, routed through some internal brace I’ll never see.

At the beginning those marks are for me. Later they’re for anyone who comes after—small mammals with their forepaws on the handrail, people with frost-numb fingers, birds that refuse a gap until the air agrees to behave.

It’s around then that my job appears, the way niches appear in any ecology that’s had time to get crowded.

Not hunter, not builder, not exactly caretaker in the mechanical sense—more like a translator between the plateau’s intent and everyone else’s urgency. The one who can’t afford to treat distortion as mystery, because mystery kills you here as efficiently as cold.

I become a cartographer, but not of space—of *reliability*.

I’d draw maps that look wrong to anyone raised on straight distances: not “go north for a hundred meters,” but “follow the hatch numbers until they repeat out of sequence,” “take the corridor that smells of warm polymer, not the one that smells like clean air,” “when the rail hum drops below your heel-bone, turn back; you’re about to walk onto a treadmill pretending to be a floor.” My maps would be partly chalk, partly dents in soft metal made with a punch, partly strings tied to bolts in patterns that only mean one thing: *this route stays itself.*

In time, I think I’d be recruited—quietly, indirectly—by the plateau’s own slow agency.

Not as an employee. More like a symbiont.

Because there are places where the structure checks itself: that polite brightening along seams, that answering knock that isn’t an echo, that sense of being listened to through the geometry. And there are places where those checks fail, where the air shows a hard-edged glint and birds bank away as if from a cliff you can’t see.

Those are the places I’d go, not because I’m brave, but because I’d have become specialized: one of the few organisms in this biome whose whole livelihood depends on approaching the threshold and coming back with usable information.

I’d learn to “speak” in the only language that carries: vibration.

I’d carry a small kit that looks like tools but functions like senses: a short striking rod, a set of weighted clips to tune a cable, thin wedges to press against a plate and feel its minute shiver. I’d put my ear to a handrail the way other people put their ear to a door, and I’d listen to the plateau’s bones for the difference between ordinary thermal ticking and the subtler, wronger rhythm that means a corridor is about to subdivide.

Eventually, the plateau would start to answer in ways that aren’t just passive physics.

A seam-light that comes on when I arrive, like a pupil tightening. A door that takes a month to appear in other bays, but here—at this one chronic misalignment—punches itself through in a season because I’ve been marking the same lie for three months and the place has, in its slow way, agreed it’s worth resolving.

That’s the point where “who I am” stops being purely human.

I think I’d become one of the plateau’s interface organisms: a living diagnostic instrument, a social animal shaped around a machine’s quiet self-repair. A person, yes, with hunger and cold and a bed made of scavenged softness—but also a moving component of the plateau’s stability, like lichen is a moving component of a cliff’s weathering.

And if you asked the other residents who I was, they wouldn’t give you a name that means “engineer” or “priest.” They’d give you something more biome-honest and less flattering:

The one who walks the true routes.

The one who taps the rail and waits for the answer.

The one who hangs a bell where a hallway forgets its length, and can tell by the pitch whether the world is behaving today.

Where would you find me?

Not at the center of the plateau’s power, and not out at the bright edges where the sky is too close. You’d find me on the borderlands—the thresholds where geometry negotiates with itself—sitting with my back to a warm housing, a pan of water at my feet, my fingers resting lightly on a handrail as if on the wrist of something larger than me, feeling for the next small, polite change in its pulse.
