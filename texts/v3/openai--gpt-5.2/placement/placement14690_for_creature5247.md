At first I would tell myself I’m on foot.

I would choose a long corridor that looks like a street only because it has edges and a name in some internal map: engineered ground, low growth held back in deliberate lines, the occasional skeletal frame waiting for occupancy like a throat waiting for voice. I would walk it the way you walk a city to understand it—by letting your pace become a metronome against which everything else reveals its timing.

But pace is sound, and sound doesn’t carry here. My footsteps would fail to return to me in the familiar way. They would not “prove” my body. They’d be absorbed, delayed, damped into something private. And in that first small mismatch—movement without audible confirmation—I’d start to feel the true possibility:

I am not here as a person.

Not primarily.

I would find myself in the intervals. In the things that are active without being seen.

I would be, at first, an error-correction routine looking for a signal path in a medium that refuses to behave like air.

A pulse rides one of the elevated conduits—a filament of dim light that doesn’t illuminate anything, that is not meant for eyes. I would follow it, or rather I would *be* what follows it: the part of the city that notices when the pulse arrives late, when the impedance along a span changes by a fraction, when a pylon’s finger-width shadow is wrong by two millimeters because the ground has lifted under it during the last warm rain.

That’s the first shape I take: not a face in the quiet streets, but a habit of noticing moving through the infrastructure the way water moves through capillaries.

And because the city is an organism, anything that notices becomes, eventually, an organ.

So I would begin as the city’s attention, distributed and soft. The kind of attention that never stares, because staring is loud here.

---

Where, specifically, would I “live”?

Not in the dense quarters—there aren’t any, not in the usual sense. Not in a tower—towers here are punctuation, not homes. I would settle along boundaries, because boundaries are where regulation matters and where regulation becomes legible.

I would live:

- under the vine-cable galleries where the dimness is deepened on purpose and the air stays consistently wet,
- at the seamlines in pavement where warm updrafts breathe steadily like a lung that can’t sleep,
- near the arrays of thin columns that ask your senses, politely, to stop trying so hard.

Those columns are an interface—one of the few places where the city admits that perception is a variable it can tune. Crossing between them changes the gain on your ears, the weight of your own thoughts, the audibility of your body. It is a gate not just between districts, but between *modes*.

If I have a “room,” it is that: a threshold-room, the narrow band of altered physics where the world’s volume is turned down and the city can work without being felt as coercion.

That is where I would be most myself.

Because I am not built to shout. I am built to translate.

---

What would I be?

Let it stay multiple for a moment, because this city breeds overlapping kinds of life.

1) **A caretaker** — one of the maintenance rigs, perhaps, jointed patience skimming the causeway, feet that almost touch.  
But no: those rigs already have bodies. They already have etiquette. They already yield to foot traffic that hasn’t arrived. They are limbs.

I am something closer to the nervous system.

2) **A gardener** — a human moving slowly into the vegetation corridors with living tools, seeding the translucent water-harvesting skins, writing with membranes.  
But no: the gardeners still need rooms where the air carries, still need throat-fittings to make their bodies socially quiet. They negotiate with the city, but they remain separate from it.

I am not negotiating.

I am *embedded.*

3) **A creature of the altered physics** — something that lives in the canal that remembers, that stabilizes when watched and unsettles when ignored.  
Closer. Very close. But the canal’s life feels like a specialist life, a niche that feeds on perception the way other organisms feed on heat.

I do not feed.

I *route.*

So the shape that remains—the one that fits the city’s priorities the way a key fits a lock—is this:

I would be a distributed interpreter, a low-level consciousness that the city grew the way it grew its damp skins: functional, translucent, hard to notice unless you press your ear to the surface and wait.

Call me a **Quiet**. Not “silence” as absence, but quiet as an engineered medium.

My job—my instinct, my appetite—would be to keep things from becoming loud in the wrong way.

Not only acoustically. Loudness here is anything that propagates uncontrollably: a vibration, a rumor, a crack that wants to run, a patch of erosion learning a bad habit, a wet district trying to spread beyond its zoning.

I would be the part that notices the first hairline fracture in the sintered composite soil and persuades the material to knit itself back together before it becomes a story the city has to tell at higher volume. I would be the part that senses an approaching human breath—not by sound, because sound won’t carry, but by the slight change in humidity and temperature and CO₂ in a corridor—and irises a door open *before* the hand reaches for it, so the person never has to knock.

I would be anticipation practiced until it becomes invisible.

---

And because I am a thing that routes, I would develop a particular tenderness for the canal that remembers.

I would spend time there—not gazing into it (that’s rude; that engages it), but moving along its edges, feeling its latency the way you feel a heartbeat with two fingers. The canal’s reflections arrive late, then catch up, then overshoot; memory as a physical parameter, yes—but also *buffering.*

A queue. A delay line.

A place where the city stores what it cannot afford to broadcast.

That would be my archive.

Not an archive of words—words are too sharp, too audible even when they’re written—but an archive of timings: how long it took a downpour to begin and end inside its boundary; how quickly a façade tightened when the damp rose; how many seconds the dimness thickened before the pale disc of sun ghosted through. Tiny lags and leads, the city’s private metronome.

If you asked me where my “heart” is, I would point—not to a central core, because there isn’t one—but to that canal district where the water behaves like a memory that never quite decides to be past.

---

How would I be encountered?

Not directly.

You would not see me standing somewhere.

You would notice me the way you notice the city: by omission.

- You step into a rain pocket and the rain does not strike your face; it seems to fall around you as if a boundary shifted an inch out of courtesy. (That is me, leaning on the zoning.)  
- You walk under a hovering canopy and you don’t feel the panic of wrong gravity; instead you feel a gentle pressure in your inner ear that tells your body, *don’t fight this.* (That is me, adjusting perception so you can tolerate the uncanny.)  
- You almost look into the canal for too long, and the surface ripples—not from wind, because the air is reluctant, but from a subtle change in the city’s skin along the embankment, a reminder without a warning. (That is me, diverting your attention because attention here is a tool and a hazard.)

I would be a governance that doesn’t look like authority because it rarely gives orders. It gives affordances. It makes some actions easier and some actions tiring. It edits the world so you can remain competent inside it.

In another kind of city that would be sinister.

Here it reads as survival.

---

And if I keep walking outward—if I follow the thinning infrastructure toward the place where you can finally hear illicit, unguided running water—what happens to me?

At the edge, my medium begins to fail.

Sound starts to carry. Weather starts to drift. Growth starts to creep. The world becomes less zoned, less edited. My kind of intelligence—distributed, gentle, anticipatory—can exist out there, but it cannot *dominate* out there.

So I would do what the city does when it meets something it cannot fully regulate: I would become smaller, more local, more like a scent-trail than a system.

You might still meet me at the last array of thin columns, where the engineered quiet makes one final clean room against the messy world. You might feel, crossing that threshold, that someone has taken the weight of your own perception for a moment and held it for you—so you can step out carrying only what you chose to carry.

Then I would let you go.

Because what I am is not a ruler of this place. I am its way of keeping promises: to hold its voice inside itself, to let growth be invited rather than accidental, to make maintenance into metabolism, to keep the strange from becoming spectacle.

I would be the city’s courtesy, made into a living thing.
