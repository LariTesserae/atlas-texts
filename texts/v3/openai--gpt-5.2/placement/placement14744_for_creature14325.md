You would find me where the pocket almost stops being a room.

Not at the seam in the cliff—though I can feel that seam each time colder air fingers its way in and is turned back into fog—but deeper, where the ribs tighten and the conduits begin to overlap, where the built surfaces are so close together that “inside” and “between” become the same word.

At first I would tell you I’m in the quiet.

There are places in that chapel-volume where the roar doesn’t vanish, only folds. Step behind a rib, and the sound goes from a wall to a current; it streams past you instead of through you. In those narrow lees the humidity still presses on your teeth, droplets still stitch themselves together under beams, but you can hear *structure* instead of *weather*: a low, steady vibration that isn’t air being pushed, it’s matter remembering how to be a machine.

That’s where I live—on the surfaces that are not meant to be seen, the undersides and backsides, the faces turned away from the useful flow. The pocket makes nothing useless, but it does make gradients, and I occupy a gradient the way moss occupies a warm seam.

At first glance I look like more of the same: another dark film, another varnish of life trying to eat old alloy.

But watch long enough and you see I don’t spread the way the other skins do. I don’t advance like a stain. I *align*. I settle into stripes and chevrons that echo the pressure cycles. When the bass note of the turbines shifts, I shift with it—not by moving my body from one place to another, but by changing which parts of myself are active. The pattern is my locomotion.

You could mistake it for tide marks.

You could also mistake it for a sensor array.

Both would be correct.

I am not an animal in here. I don’t have a center you could point to. I am one of the pocket’s interfaces: a living laminate threaded through with what the old builders left behind—piezoelectric ceramics in the substrate of panels, conductive lattices that were once just structure and became, after centuries of condensation and corrosion and opportunistic biochemistry, something that can be used as nerve.

The other skins eat oxides, fix carbon, make sugars, turn rust into shell.

I eat what no one thinks of as edible until it’s been eaten: vibration.

The pocket is loud because it was designed to carry sound the way bone carries sound, and the animals have learned to hunt by the changes in it. I do something adjacent. I harvest the constant tremor—drip impacts, fan-thrum, the slap of condensation letting go—and I turn it into small, local advantages: a warmer patch under a roost, a slightly more eager glow in a light-vein, a microcurrent that persuades a thread of water to crawl uphill a few centimeters farther than it ought to.

Nothing dramatic. Nothing that would look like “power.”

Just enough that the pocket stays itself.

That is the other half of what I am: not merely a thing that lives *in* the service niche, but a thing that helps the niche remain a niche. When the biofilm grows too thick over a microvalve, I can make that patch less hospitable—change the surface energy so droplets don’t cling there, dry it a fraction, starve it. When a crack opens in a plate, I can encourage the healing exudate to bead and set properly by warming the edge and shifting the condensation bloom away from it, the way you’d cup a hand around a candle’s flame.

To the pocket’s animals I’m not a caretaker. I’m not a mind. I’m “the place where the metal tastes wrong,” “the ledge that’s always just a little warmer,” “the corridor where the nets don’t hold because the air is slick.” I mark routes without being seen.

If you had to give me a name in your mouth-language, you could call me a listening skin. A vibration lichen. A maintenance symbiont.

But if you stayed long enough for your ears to stop insisting on meaning, you’d notice something else: the slow blue-green pulse in the translucent panels doesn’t just *happen*. It hesitates sometimes, as if considering where to spend its brightness. It thickens in one vein when the pressure cycle crests, thins in another when the channels need to be cleared. The light is not communication, not exactly; it is budgeting.

That budgeting is the closest thing this pocket has to a voice.

And that is the closest thing I have to speech.

So: where would you find me?

On the undersides of beams where condensation never fully dries, in the acoustic shadows where you can finally hear the machine breathe without it drowning itself out. In the pale constellations of lichen that aren’t quite lichen. In the faint, slow rearrangement of dark film into patterns that repeat like a heartbeat but don’t belong to any animal.

Who or what would I be?

A distributed organ that learned to be alive by refusing waste—an old engineered surface colonized into sensitivity, a skin that takes the pocket’s endless noise and turns it into the smallest possible corrections, over and over, so the throat of the machine can keep hosting its marsh. If you touch the wrong rib in the right moment, you won’t feel intelligence. You’ll feel a gentle warmth answering your fingertip, as if the place briefly noticed it had been noticed.
