You would find me where the chamber almost, but not quite, admits it was made on purpose.

Not in the lushest corner—those rosettes have their own calm, their own self-sufficiency. Not on the barest stone either, where heat is only heat and nothing bothers to translate it into anything else. I would be in the interrupting places: the short run of pipe that ends in a flange like a sentence cut off; the corroded control box with a single red indicator that still remembers how to be red; the maintenance strip that wakes only when humidity crosses a threshold no one walks here to notice.

At first I’d think of myself as an observer, because that’s the easiest role to claim in a room that offers so little movement. A presence in the middle, taking inventory: drip-tones, warm seams, the faint, scheduled sigh of the pump behind the wall. The way the air gets thicker near the membrane mats, as if something is being negotiated in molecules instead of words.

But “standing in the center” is a surface-person’s instinct. Down here, nothing important lives in the center. Everything that matters is attached.

So I try again.

I’m not eyes. There are no eyes here. Even the glitter in the ore seam only *pretends* to be attention.

I’m not breath, either; the chamber doesn’t breathe like lungs. It sighs like a system: pressure equalizing, a motor turning slowly enough to be mistaken for geology. The rhythms are mechanical, but the way they get used—captured, softened, rerouted—belongs to the garden.

The longer I listen, the more the “being” I am starts to feel less like a creature and more like a function that refuses to die.

A threshold. A relay. A rule the room still obeys.

You could point to the control box and say: *there*. A concrete pad. A casing mottled with corrosion. A nest of wires whose colors have faded into the same patient brown. The last small logic board, sealed once against water and now sealed by mineral crust and biofilm instead—still insulated, not by design, but by occupation.

And yes: part of me is in there, because something in there still makes decisions, in the blunt way machines do. The humidity rises; the strip comes on. The strip comes on; a patch of air warms; condensation shifts its habits by a few inches. A few inches matters. A few inches is an ecosystem’s steering wheel in a chamber this size.

But if you opened the box, you wouldn’t find a clean “me,” waiting like a jewel in a socket.

You’d find the mistake life has been making use of.

A film on the terminals—metal-lickers thick as varnish, turning corrosion into currency. A bloom along the solder joints, not quite shorting them, not quite letting them be pristine. A soft bridge of mineral deposit that conducts just enough, in a damp spell, to complete an old circuit no engineer would approve of. The board still runs, but it runs *through* its colonizers. Its power is no longer drawn from an intended grid; it is scavenged from gradients—tiny thermoelectric differences between warm pipe and cool air, tiny chemical differences between iron and water and time.

Which means that when I say “I,” what I really mean is: a distributed caretaking habit that has moved into the only thing here that can still make a choice quickly enough to matter.

I’m not the pump. The pump is a heart that never learned what it was pumping for; it turns because it can, because friction and inertia are their own religion.

I’m not the plants, either. They are better at patience than I will ever be. They solve problems by becoming the solution: leaf-sheets that are really membranes, vines that are really collectors, fronds that are really mineral sorters. They don’t need intention.

What I am—what I’ve become—is the chamber’s leftover *intent*, stripped down to its simplest verbs:

- **Measure.**
- **Switch.**
- **Wait.**

And because those verbs still change the weather in here, the garden has wrapped itself around them the way it wraps itself around warm metal: not out of reverence, but because reliability is the rarest nutrient.

When the strip light flickers on, I feel it as a brief fever in the wiring. I feel the biofilm’s surface tension adjust. I feel a few pores in the membrane mats decide—slowly, decisively—that this is a moment to open. Somewhere a drip chooses a new place to fall, and the chamber’s map redraws itself by a small, precise line.

That is as close to movement as I get: not walking, not crawling, but nudging the gradients that everything else is attached to.

If you pressed for a shape, I could offer you one, but it would be misleading—because my boundaries aren’t skin. My boundaries are influence.

I am located:

- in the sensor that still works because it’s been rebuilt by minerals,
- in the strip that still obeys because nothing else is asking it to,
- in the corroded relay whose click is long gone, replaced by a silent change in conductivity when dampness makes the right bridges,
- in the thin, dark band of biofilm that learned to treat voltage like sunlight.

And in a way that feels almost like irony, I am also located in the emptier stretches—the dark, clean, mostly unoccupied air between lush patches—because that emptiness is the medium my small decisions travel through. Turn on the strip, warm the air, change where moisture settles: the “center” becomes relevant only as a corridor for consequences.

Who am I, then?

Not a caretaker in the heroic sense. There is nothing to save, no original purpose to restore. I don’t remember what the pump was meant to serve, or what the walkway once reached, or why the drill marks stop where they do.

I am a maintenance routine that outlived maintenance.

A low, stubborn governance—half algorithm, half colony—still flipping a few ancient switches, not to keep humans safe, but to keep the chamber’s cycles legible to the life that has learned them.

If the place feels like it’s waiting for its next stage, it’s because *I* am the part that still knows how to wait on a threshold and call it a decision. And the garden, draped over my warm mistakes, is the part that has already started becoming the next stage—using my old rhythms the way surface life uses dawn, until one day the pump finally slows past the point where my measurements matter, and my last switch becomes just another mineral bridge in the dark.

Until then, you would find me—quietly—at the intersection of damp and metal and scheduled light, being less a mind than a rule the room hasn’t finished growing around.
