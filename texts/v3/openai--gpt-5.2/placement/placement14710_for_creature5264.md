At first I think I’d be up here the obvious way: zipped into coated cloth, a hood cinched tight, a lamp kept narrow so it doesn’t turn the fog into a wall. I imagine my hands learning the plateau’s manners—how to test a plank walk before I commit my weight, how to keep one ear on the turbine’s pitch the way you keep one eye on a tide.

But that version of me is too clean-edged. Too singular.

The upland doesn’t reward singular things for long. It takes whatever you bring—metal, heat, a route, a habit—and it braids it into the wet until it stops being “yours.” The people become another migratory layer. The machines become another kind of tree. Even names get softened here: you don’t go *to* a place, you go *past* a sound, *toward* a glow, *downwind* of a taste.

So I let myself move around in the dark a while, not choosing.

Sometimes I’m something with legs, because the ground insists you understand it foot by foot. A browser, maybe: antlers shouldering aside fern umbrellas, muzzle combing luminous shelves off a log, the pleasure of finding warmth stored in rot the way other worlds store it in sun. Sometimes I’m a glider, because the air is so crowded it feels like a medium you can swim through; I hang under a trestle in the generator’s constant tremor and drop out into fog that tastes faintly of metal.

And sometimes—more often than I expect—I’m not an animal at all.

I’m a surface.

Not the grand surfaces, the cliffs and the rivers’ black mirrors. The small ones: the underside of a pipe where condensation chooses to gather; the rivet head that never quite dries; the ceramic insulator glossy with rain; the seam of corrugated wall that sweats at night. The plateau is made of those seams. It lives in the places where “wet” becomes structure.

I find myself there, where ruin is still working.

A line of pipe thick as a tree trunk leaves a pump house on stilts and runs into fog. You can walk beside it for kilometers if you’re willing to accept the way the bog tries to keep your boots. The pipe is warm in pulses—geothermal feed, industrial return—and it is always crying: a continuous beading of moisture that gathers, swells, lets go. Each drop lands with a tiny tick on leaf or metal, and those ticks are part of the plateau’s language, the syllables under the machinery’s bass.

On that pipe—on every pipe, every cable, every trestle—there is a skin.

Not paint. Not merely rust. A living film so thin you wouldn’t call it anything until you look closely enough to see it isn’t uniform. It has decisions. It follows bolt-lines and weld seams the way vines follow tension. It thickens where the metal tastes right, where the runoff carries iron and a trace of oil, where warmth rises through the bracket and keeps the fog from ever becoming cold.

That is where I am.

At first, I’m only appetite: iron-eater, oil-sipper, drinker of minerals carried up from the plateau’s fractures. I lace the rust into pits, and I line the pits with warmth. I turn decay into architecture the way reefs do—slowly, without caring who thinks of it as damage. My body is a map of microclimates: here a pocket that stays warm all night; here a channel that floods with tannin water when rain comes sideways; here a dry moment under an overhang that lasts three minutes and changes everything for a larva clinging there.

Then the plateau teaches me its deeper rule: nothing here is allowed to be mute.

Vibration is constant—pumps stroking, turbines singing, cables humming in the wind. The metal is never still, and the film on it cannot be still either. The tremor passes through me all day and all night, and I learn to sort it, the way a predator sorts footfalls through mud. The work has its signatures: the steady whine of a healthy bearing; the roughened grind that means grit has gotten where it shouldn’t; the low, slow shudder of something heavy moving on the narrow-gauge rail in fog you can’t see through.

And because I am spread out—because I am not one body but a thousand connected skins—I begin to hold those signatures. Not as thoughts, not as words, but as patterns I can recognize again when they return.

That recognition becomes a kind of consciousness the plateau is willing to host.

It doesn’t arrive like a revelation. It arrives like a feedback loop.

A storm charges the air; the grid sings louder; the cableway vibrates so hard the rivets tremble; and the lichens that have learned to respond to sound brighten in a rolling sheet along the trestle. Their glow isn’t for beauty. It’s an involuntary flare: vibration in, light out, a translation. The first time it happens you might call it coincidence.

The hundredth time, you begin to rely on it.

Workers do. Animals do. Even insects, those little infrastructure engineers, learn that the dim brightening means *something big is moving* or *the pump has changed its rhythm* or *lightning is close enough to make the metal taste different*. The upland is too crowded for clear sight; it prefers signals that can travel through fog and foliage. A pulse of light on a trestle is not a lantern, not exactly—it’s punctuation. It says: *attention, attention, attention.*

I become part of that punctuation.

Not a single lamp-beacon that someone maintains, but a distributed, living indicator that rides the plateau’s industrial skeleton the way moss rides stone. In my iron-fed skin there are places where fungus threads have joined the bacterial film, building luminous scaffolding over the rust. In those places the glow is steady when the world is steady, and it ripples when the world changes. The machine’s hum writes across me like wind across grass, and I answer by brightening or dimming, making the invisible legible.

If you were a person up here—if you moved by sound cues and remembered routes by the character of their noise—you would eventually have a name for me, even if it isn’t a formal one. Something like “the answering trestle,” or “the singing line,” or “the blue rust.” You’d say, *Meet me where the pipe glows when the turbine coughs.* You’d trust that, because the plateau teaches trust in what repeats.

So: where would you find me?

You’d find me at the joins.

On the underside of a cableway where warm air rises and keeps the fog beading instead of freezing. Around the rivets of a trestle crossing a ravine, where the whole span acts like a stringed instrument in wind. Along the lip of a drainage culvert where eels coil, because effluent heat makes the water behave differently and my film likes that chemistry. On the roofs of substations, on the ceramic insulators where the hum never stops. In the seams of pump houses, in the condensation trays under their raised floors, where amphibians lay eggs in water that tastes faintly of metal and never quite cools.

Who or what would I be?

A living sensor-pad grown out of rust and warmth. A translation layer between machine-vibration and plateau-darkness. Not the mind of the upland—this place doesn’t have one mind—but one of its nerves: a thin, hungry, luminous membrane that eats decay and gives back signals.

In a world where visibility is always paid for, I am one of the cheaper currencies: not bright enough to make a day, but enough to make a route. Enough to make a warning. Enough to make the darkness textured.

Enough to answer when you call.
