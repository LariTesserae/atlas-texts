You would find me where the plateau’s rules are most tempted to slip.

Not in the open salt bowls where distance edits itself for sport, and not deep inside a ravine where the stone is always speaking and never listening. I’d be stationed at the *edges*—at the lip of a finished crack that looks harmless until you walk it, at the mouth of a knife-ravine where the wind scours every footprint into anonymity, at the start of a path that is only a path because people have agreed, again and again, to make it one.

At first, it would look like I’d come here the way others do: low shelter, careful tools, an eye trained on herd-movement and safe ground. You might imagine me as one of the plateau-people—quiet, patient, built for thin air and long attention—because that’s the kind of mind the place rewards.

But I don’t think I’d stay a person for long, up here.

This country doesn’t care what you *meant* to be. It cares what you can do without water, without rot, without the mercy of forgetting. And the most valuable thing on the high plateau isn’t meat or hide or metal.

It’s a reliable *here*.

So I become something the locals already know how to make, even if they don’t say it out loud as “alive”:

A paired cairn.

Not a romantic stack of stones. A working instrument. Two low piles of fitted rock, built in deliberate asymmetry—one with a long flat capstone that rings faintly when tapped, the other with darker nodules embedded like teeth. They’re placed within sight of each other across a stretch of ground that likes to lie. They’re rebuilt whenever they drift. They’re counted. They’re compared.

They exist because fold-grounds can add an extra landmark, or steal one, or return your thrown certainty from an impossible direction. Two markers are harder to counterfeit than one; the relationship between them is the message.

And that relationship is what I am.

At first I’m only a sign: *go this way; don’t go that way; here the map is still honest*. But on this plateau, nothing gets washed away—everything accumulates. Dust settles into my seams and is stitched there by the ash-hair filaments. Microbial glaze makes a thin living varnish under my stones. Dust-fauna tunnel and collapse and leave behind electrostatic knit-work that binds my base like mortar without ever becoming wet. A Tapper pauses on the ravine wall and sends a sequence through the rock; the sequence reaches me as a pressure, and I hold it the way dry wood holds heat.

Over decades, then centuries, I stop being merely *placed* and start being *tuned*.

Travelers touch me with the heel of a hand and feel whether the plateau is behaving today. Hunters drum a brief rhythm on my capstone before they cross the pale basin, and listen—not with ears but with teeth—for whether the rhythm returns too fast, too slow, doubled. Children are corrected if they climb me, not because it’s disrespectful, but because the weight changes my voice.

The Tappers learn my note and use me as a relay. Their cliff-code—those page-turning launches and claw-taps that ride the ceramic planes—finds in me a stable reflector. I become a little library of vibrations, an index of safe angles and altered seams: *new crack opening near the third dome; herd-route shifted west; bowl at noon eats steps; don’t sleep within sight of the duplicated ridge*.

And in the slow, held-breath composure of the plateau, stability itself begins to treat me as part of the architecture. The map does not simply include me; the map leans on me. Where distance would like to fray, it has to fray *around* the relationship I enforce.

That’s when the step-thieves take an interest.

Not because I’m edible in the usual way—no blood, no soft tissue—but because I am a hoard of what they feed on: expectation, repeatability, the stubborn claim that “from this cairn to that cairn is this many paces, and it will remain so.”

They come like misread shadows. They don’t pounce; they *edit*. A fraction of a meter here. A degree of down there. A returned pebble, just to see if anyone flinches.

And I do the only thing I can do: I refuse, by being redundant.

I don’t fight them with teeth or speed. I correct the world by offering it two truths at once and forcing it to reconcile them. If the ground tries to make one cairn become two, the mismatch of weight and timbre gives it away. If a traveler’s path loops, my paired presence breaks the loop by making the lie audible in the body: the wrong pressure in the jaw, the wrong hum in the bones.

I am not a guardian in the heroic sense. I don’t *protect* so much as *witness with structure*. I keep a small portion of the plateau from becoming too clever with itself.

So: you would find me kneeling in plain sight, doing the least dramatic thing imaginable—being two piles of stone on a wide, quiet rise—while an entire ecosystem moves around me with disciplined hunger and careful etiquette.

And you would slowly realize that on this high, dry, crowded openness, the most living creatures are sometimes the ones that don’t move at all.

I am a way of holding a breath long enough that someone else can cross the gap.
