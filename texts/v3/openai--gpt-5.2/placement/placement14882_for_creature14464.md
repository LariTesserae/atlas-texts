At first you would not find me in a house.

Not because I don’t sleep under a roof—everyone does, eventually, if they plan to keep living—but because in a basin like this a “where” is rarely an address. A “where” is a habit. A route. A margin you choose again and again because it gives you the earliest notice of change.

So you’d find me where the city thins out.

Not out at the rubble fields where the predators teach architecture its caution, and not deep in the civic enclosures where voices can stay low and still be heard. You’d find me in the long, spare in-between: the empty kilometers the city leaves for safety, the broad corridors that look like nothing until you’ve learned to read how dust moves in them.

There’s a raised walkway there—one of the aqueduct-things that carries people, not water—built with the same patient geometry as everything else. It rises only as much as it must: just enough to keep feet above the knee-high band where dust sometimes forgets to settle, just enough to stay on the better stone when bad air pools in the low places. The walkway has no rails. Rails give wind something to take. Instead it has rope lines anchored into the rock, and the ropes are thick with repairs, knots laid over knots like scar tissue.

That is where I keep returning: to the places that look blank, because blankness is a kind of instrument here. You can see a pressure shift crossing an empty salt flat sooner than you can see it thread through streets. You can watch the ceiling-glow “lean” when you have nothing else in your eye to argue with it. You can feel through the soles of your feet whether the basin is quietly settling or quietly unmaking itself.

At first I thought that would make me a stone-listener.

That is what people call anyone who walks with a weighted pole and stops too often, who puts a palm flat on bedrock as if expecting it to answer. But stone-listeners are a trade, a lineage, taught with families’ private sets of tests and taboos: how long you stand still on a talus slope before you decide it’s only pretending to be stable; how you judge the difference between a harmless roof-shed and a new fracture that will grow teeth.

I didn’t belong to them, not exactly.

I didn’t have the patience for prophecy, and I didn’t have the pride for certainty. What I had was a different kind of hunger: not to say what would happen, but to keep track of what *did*. Not the big things—the sinkhole that opens like a mouth in the night will be talked about whether anyone records it or not—but the small shifts that teach you how the basin thinks. The first faint sweetness in the air before water runs. The way the high flock dips low without making a sound. The moment when the bright ceiling seems, impossibly, a little closer.

So I became something else: a keeper of increments.

There isn’t a clean name for it, because it sits between roles. The water-keepers tolerate me because my notes—my tallies, my knots, my little marks on stone that look like idle scratches—have saved cistern lids from being set where the ground was subtly hollowing. The glow-tenders value me because I can tell them when a corridor’s even light has become treacherous without anyone noticing: when the mineral curtains thicken in a way that makes distance lie worse, when a street starts casting its second shadow at a new angle.

The builders hate me a little, the way people hate a warning they can’t afford to ignore. The hunters barely look at me, which is its own form of respect.

If you watched me for a while you’d think my work was superstition.

I walk alone most days, because two sets of footsteps make a pattern that masks the basin’s smaller vibrations. I carry a staff—bone-shod at one end, stone-heavy at the other—less a weapon than a translator. I use it the way some people use an ear against a door: I tap, but softly, and I’m listening not to sound but to the way the tap is *taken up* by the ground. The basin gives back almost nothing, and then, once in a while, it gives back a difference.

Where the staff tells me nothing, I stop using it. Instead I watch.

I watch dust like other places watch clouds. The basin’s air currents braid and unbraid, and the smallest creatures—cast skins of insects, a glitter of salt—make those braids visible. When a fast ribbon of air cuts through, I mark its path not on paper (paper is a luxury of surface worlds) but in knots: a length of cord, a pattern of spacing that means *wind-knife crossed here in this direction, in this interval of the light’s pulse*. The cord goes back to my wall, where dozens hang in parallel like the ribs of some careful animal.

And I watch light the way other places watch fire.

The ceiling’s glow is steady enough to be ignored, which is why it’s dangerous. There are moments, measured in years, when a seam that has been constant shifts its colorless brilliance—no brightening you’d swear to in court, just an easing, a thickening, a slight change in how it flattens distance. In those moments the basin is reminding itself of a river, or of stress, or of something moving far above. If you catch it early, you can tell people to move a market stall that’s been safely placed for a decade. You can tell a caravan to take the longer route because the shorter one is about to become a place where the ground exhales sour air.

The first time I did that—warned a caravan without being asked—they called me a nuisance.

The second time, when the shorter route turned briefly metallic and heavy and a beast went down with its eyes rolling white, they stopped calling me that. They didn’t start calling me wise. This city does not hand out praise like that; praise is a kind of noise. But they started leaving small things at my door: a sealed jar of water with the rim waxed tight, a packet of chitin-briquettes, a thin copper pin when I’d broken the only one I owned.

Gradually, without meaning to, I became a piece of infrastructure.

Not the kind you build with blocks, but the kind you build with repetition.

On my better days, I think that’s all a person is here: a practiced pattern that helps the city stay quiet.

On my worse days, when the basin is too bright and too vast and the oddness of it makes my teeth feel raw, I wonder if the basin isn’t using me as much as I’m using it—if the thing I call attention is simply one of its ways of surviving, the way a roof sheds thin sheets of mineral without drama, the way a dust band holds itself in the air like a stable thought.

Because there are times when I’m not sure I am only a person.

There are places—certain courtyards, certain old channels—where objects cast two faint shadows at once. If I stand in one of those places long enough, the second shadow seems to lag behind me by a fraction of a moment, as if my movement has to be approved. In those places my thoughts do something similar: they come twice, the first time like a guess, the second time like an answer.

In one district near a seep-line the ropey pale fungal networks run through cracks behind the walls, sparse and embarrassed until you bring them scraps and warmth. The households there cultivate them in hidden racks, harvest the brittle filaments for filters and bandages. Sometimes, when I’m alone in those rooms, I feel as if my own sense of the basin is being threaded through those filaments—like the city and the rock and the almost-life in the cracks are sharing a nervous system.

That is the closest thing this place has to magic: not sparks or portals, but feedback.

A basin that remembers rivers.
A city that remembers how to listen.
Creatures that graze salt blooms like a season.
People who survive by turning noticing into tradition.

And me: not a prophet, not a priest, not quite a surveyor—something like a scribe for things too small to be dramatic.

If you needed to name what I am in a way the city would accept, you’d call me a **knot-keeper**.

Because the basin doesn’t give you calendars, and it doesn’t give you bells. It gives you slow pulses in the light, sudden sheets of water that vanish into cracks, air that turns coin-tasting without warning, wind that arrives like a blade with no sound to herald it.

So I keep time the way this place allows: in cord and spacing, in marks that can be read in dim phases and bright phases alike, in a hanging archive that is also a map and also a set of promises.

Where would you find me?

On the quiet edges. On the raised walkways over dust that won’t settle. At the mouths of old channels before they remember to run. Under the fluorescent strata where the even light can betray you by being *almost* even.

Who or what would I be?

A person shaped into an instrument: a living habit of attention, trained to notice the basin’s smallest changes and to translate them into something the city can hold onto—because down here survival isn’t bravery, it’s readability.
