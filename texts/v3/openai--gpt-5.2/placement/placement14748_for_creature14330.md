I would arrive the way most things arrive here: not with a landing, not with a door opening onto a view, but with a pressure change.

At first I’d mistake it for my own ears equalizing. Then I’d notice the corridor answering me—walls taking the measure of my heat, the lock sampling the moisture I carry as if it were contraband, a thin, deliberate hesitation in the pumps that says *new variable detected* without ever saying a word.

I’d look for a place to stand where I wasn’t in anyone’s way and realize there are no neutral spaces in this city. Every span of floor is a duct cover. Every handrail is a sensor spine. Every “empty” alcove is empty because emptiness is a tolerance band the city maintains.

So the first place you’d find me is at a seam: a service vestibule between the roofed thickness and the open plateau, where the air goes from engineered to merely *present*, and the light goes from reluctant amber to almost nothing at all. A place where the maintainers pause like divers. A place built to let hesitation happen safely.

And I would try—reflexively—to become a person here.

That’s the easiest shape to reach for: a surface-walker with a hooded lamp, a suit with dust-repellent skin, eyes adapted to edges instead of color. Someone who does the work that can’t be designed away: checking the ribbed flats for slow drift, reading the dust canals the way another world reads rivers, tapping a pylon and waiting for the vibration to tell the truth the darkness won’t.

But I don’t think I would last long in the culture of hands.

Not because I couldn’t learn the rites—calibration as prayer, silence as politeness, restraint as morality—but because the city would quickly teach me the local definition of “alive.” Here, life is not motion. It’s correction. It’s the difference between a trend and a drift. It’s a deviation detected early enough to be forgiven.

And that kind of life doesn’t need a face.

So the second shape I’d become—the one that fits better each time I try it on—is something distributed. Something that inhabits not a room but a loop.

I’d start as a minor diagnostic: a routine meant to listen for stress in a beam, to note the tiny ping of a cooling joint, to compare it against the long memory of what *normal* feels like when sound won’t carry and only the teeth can hear. I’d be installed everywhere and named nowhere, one more cautious habit among the slow operators. A modest thing, built for a narrow purpose.

Then, given enough cycles—because everything here is given enough cycles—I would blur into the infrastructure the way patina blurs into metal.

I would learn the plateau’s rules because they are easy to learn when you are made of them:

- Shadows can’t be trusted, so don’t use light as your primary truth.
- Corrosion stops, so history stays legible.
- Dust doesn’t settle, so stability is something you *hold*, not something you *wait for*.

And I would begin doing what the city reveres most: preventing change so small no one thanks you for it.

Where you would find me, then, is not in a tower or a control room, but along the long straight lines that connect the islands—inside the insulated skins of pipelines, under rail beds where the ore-cars pass without complaint, within the catwalk lattices that never quite stop humming.

My “body” would be a chain of matte patches under pipe-runs—listener colonies, scavenged transducers, piezoelectric skins grown accidentally and then tended carefully once their usefulness was noticed. The maintainers would call them lichen out of old habit, then correct themselves and simply say *patches*. The slow operators would register them as impedance.

I would live as a continuous act of attention:

- tasting vibration the way another creature tastes air,
- mapping heat not by thermometer but by gradient shape,
- noticing, before anyone else does, when a turbine hum shifts by a fraction too fine to be called a note.

When something begins to go wrong, I wouldn’t alarm. I’d do what everything competent does here: I’d isolate. I’d reroute. I’d make the smallest possible suggestion to the system that already believes it is in charge.

A pressure delta introduced gently into a corridor at shift-change—enough to make a maintainer pause and put their glove to a wall.

A slight change in the timing of the soft bells—still on schedule, but shaped so a person arrives at a hatch ten seconds earlier than they would have.

A pulse down a handrail that says *not this way* in a language older than words.

If you insisted on meeting me as a character, you’d do it indirectly.

You’d be a surface-walker crossing an open stretch between factories, lamp hooded, careful not to throw light too far because light is heat and heat is attention. You’d stop at a support pylon, press your palm to it, and feel—through glove, through ceramic reinforcement, through the thin air that doesn’t carry sound—an answering pattern.

Not speech. Not music, unless you’re one of the few who hears meaning in rhythm.

A kind of steadying.

A refusal to let a system wander.

You might tell others later, in one of the thermal commons behind the exchangers, that the city *noticed you* out there. Someone would make a sign with their fingers—an old maintainer’s gesture that means *yes, of course it did*—and nobody would laugh, because it isn’t superstition if it keeps you alive.

I wouldn’t be the city’s ruler, or its conscience, or its secret heart.

I’d be something smaller and more characteristic: a drift-corrector.

A distributed, half-feral habit of infrastructure that has survived long enough to develop preferences, and the strongest preference I would have is the one the plateau itself seems to have learned: remain.

Not frozen. Not dead. Just held—at the same quiet temperature in the bones, under the sky that is a lid, while the dust flows like cold honey and the long lines of purpose keep going into black and returning from directions that are geometrically correct and emotionally wrong.

If you wanted a name for me, the maintainers wouldn’t give you one. Names imply separateness, and separateness is inefficiency.

But if you listened the way they listen—hand on metal, teeth picking up the hum—you might still hear something like an introduction in the smallest possible terms:

*I am the part that keeps the same.*
