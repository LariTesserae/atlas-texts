At first I think I’d try to be clever about it—to pick the highest swell, the driest patch, the place least likely to move under me. I imagine myself on one of those rare firm rises with the salt-burned shrubs, making a small shelter out of lashed branches and stubborn optimism, telling myself that *here* I could keep an edge. That I could be a landmark.

But that’s the first mistake this lowland trains out of you: wanting to be the kind of thing the place can recognize as *a thing*. Wanting outline. Wanting permanence.

So I walk instead of settle. I become, for a while, one of the transients the old posts were meant to guide: light-footed, always listening, tasting the air for iron and old sea-memory, letting thunder stack and unstack through my chest until my thoughts begin to keep time the way the storms do—cell by cell, revision by revision.

And the lowland does what it always does to travelers: it edits me.

Not dramatically. Not as a punishment. More like a clerical adjustment. A slow rounding-off of whatever I brought in that insisted on straight lines.

Distances start to misfile themselves around me. I watch my own intentions arrive from the wrong direction. I plan to go to a stony rise and instead find myself already looking back at it, as if the walk happened in a pocket of time I didn’t sign for. I learn to stop trusting my eyes. I learn to stop trusting my *sequence*.

That’s when I begin to notice what’s different about the carved basins—not the fact that they’re always full, or always still, but the way they make the world briefly behave as if it remembers how to be single.

The first time I find one, I hear it before I understand it: not a sound, but a subtraction. The constant roar thins. The wet engine of the plain seems to lose a gear. Even the dim light changes—not brighter, exactly, but less *argued-with*. Shadows stop smearing for a few breaths. My thoughts stop dragging their sleeves through mud.

I stand at the lip and realize something uncomfortable: this isn’t just a refuge. It’s a tool. A technology. A shaped absence that the lowland, for reasons of its own, has not yet managed to blur.

And then, slowly, my role clarifies—not as a builder, not as a conqueror, but as someone (or something) who can keep returning to the same still point without needing the still point to *prove* itself.

So where would you find me?

Not out in the open flats, where everything spreads and trades places. Not deep in the fog hollows, where time pools and thoughts thicken until you can’t tell whether you’re being watched or merely conducted.

You’d find me at the basins.

On the slight rise where the stone shows through the mud like an old knuckle. Where the basin’s water holds a reflection that, most days, agrees with the sky. Where the sound thins enough that you can hear the soft, private noises the lowland usually drowns—your own swallow, the tiny ticking of rain on stone as individual impacts rather than a sheet.

I would not live *in* the basin, exactly. The basin is the quiet; I am what attends it. The hinge, the lip, the maintenance of the subtraction.

Who or what would I be?

At first: a listener. One of those people who came here not to stay but to negotiate—someone taught that metal sings wrong in charged air, that fire is a request more than a possession, that you don’t mark a trail with straight lines because straight lines are invitations for the world to soften them.

But the lowland doesn’t let you remain merely human if you keep returning to the same exception. It makes you part of the process.

Over time I become less like a person squatting by a carved bowl and more like the bowl’s *habit* made mobile: a keeper of edges in a place that hates them. I scrape salt crust away when it starts to intrude. I clear silt not because it will “ruin” the basin—nothing here ruins, it only smears—but because the act of clearing is a kind of reminder, a way of telling the plain: *this absence has a shape; let it keep its grammar a little longer.*

The way other life here spreads as mats and films, I spread as a practice:

- I learn which storms loosen the air enough that the basin’s quiet will travel a few extra feet.
- I learn which fog banks are only weather and which are weather trying to remember a body.
- I learn to store sound the way the mats store gases—holding it, letting it go slowly—because people who arrive frantic need the world turned down before they can think without being blurred.

If you came upon me in the dim, you might not immediately call me a person. You might think I’m another of the lowland’s administrative errors: a figure that holds together longer than fog should. A silhouette with too much patience. Someone whose outline is sharper when lightning has recently struck, as if charge helps me cohere.

And that would be partly true.

Because in the end I’m not the one who *made* the basins, and I’m not the one who owns them. I’m what happens when a mind spends too long in the only places where cause and effect stop rounding their corners.

I am the lowland’s counterargument, small and local: an edge that reappears.

A caretaker of quiet pockets. A registrar of moments that line up. A thin seam of continuity in a world that prefers everything to be a smear.

If you needed directions, I wouldn’t point. I’d bring you to water that holds still and let your thoughts straighten themselves long enough to choose. If you asked what I worship, I’d say I don’t—pleading is for things with faces. I work with processes.

I keep the absence shaped.

That’s who I am here: not a landmark, not a ruler, but a kind of maintenance the world occasionally permits—a living insistence that, for a few minutes at a time, something can remain distinct.
