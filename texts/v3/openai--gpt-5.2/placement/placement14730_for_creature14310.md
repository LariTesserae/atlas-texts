At first I think I’d be easy to place up here—somebody passing through, upright and loud by accident, trying not to step where the ground is hollow.

But the plateau doesn’t really have “through.” It has *over*, and *under*, and *between*, and it has hours that belong to different kinds of bodies. If I stayed, if I stopped insisting on being a single moving point, I can feel the land asking a quieter question than “what are you?” It asks: *what scale of attention can you live at?*

So I try a few lives on, the way you hold your breath in different ways to see which one fits the air.

I picture myself as one of the open-walkers—long-legged, careful-hoofed, moving in that patient line across the flats. That life has a clean clarity to it: drink when it’s offered, move when you must, keep your body’s shadow small. But even in the imagining I can tell I would give myself away. My mind would run ahead of my feet. I would waste motion just by thinking it.

Then I lift up into the gliders, broad-winged and silent, and for a moment it seems right: height, horizon close enough to touch, the whole plateau laid out like a pale text. But the birds’ silence is an etiquette learned from physics—sound that won’t carry, calls that die. I would still be trying to speak in long sentences. I would still be trying to make banners out of noise.

Then I go lower.

I crouch into the thorned shrubs where the air holds itself cooler. I let my body become something that sleeps in the day and wakes in the thin hours. I become small enough to use shade as architecture. That helps. My ideas compress. My appetite becomes a schedule. The plateau rewards that.

And still—there is too much *me* in it. Too much insistence on the singular.

The longer I imagine staying, the more the plateau’s true population begins to feel like the only honest answer: not the grazers that cross it like weather, not even the burrow mammals with their warm fracture-paths, but the crowd you don’t see unless you learn to look without expecting drama. The ones for whom a film of water is an ocean. The ones who treat a crack as a continent and a stone bowl as a season.

If I belong anywhere, it’s in the bandwidth the plateau preserves: the fine noises, the micro-signals, the almost-touch of dampness. The place where the land’s rules are written not as laws but as textures.

So: you would find me at dawn, not out on the flats, but at the edge of a “wet-looking” hollow that is dry to the eye and cold to the fingertips. A shallow bowl where mineral darkening makes the rock seem permanently bruised. A place that fog likes, a place that holds its breath.

Not *in* the pocket pool—those are capitals, and capitals attract attention that wants to eat you—but slightly above it, where condensation gathers first. Under a lip of stone polished by centuries of bodies pausing there. Beside one of the upright slab “teeth” that points, not toward a destination, but along a lane the fog prefers.

And I am not one animal.

I am closer to a function the plateau has grown for itself: a kind of quiet archivist made out of whatever materials will consent.

It starts as the rock-skin.

A tight lichen mosaic, not sprawling, not decorative—sealed. Chalky orange and muted green, fitted to the stone like old paint that never flakes. In daylight I look inert. At night I take up water molecule by molecule. With dawn fog I wake into a thin, living gloss that changes how droplets behave. I make the hesitation happen. Not by magic—by surface chemistry practiced for so long it resembles intention.

In my wet hours I am slick enough to guide a bead of water toward a particular groove; in my dry hours I am rough enough to pin that memory in place as residue. The plateau’s thrift depends on such small decisions repeated until they become geography.

But lichen alone is too slow to be “mind.” Too patient to notice the day-to-day gossip of the burrow-city.

So I acquire a second body: the ones who can move inside the stone’s rules.

A colony, not of ants exactly, but of near-insects built for this library-silence—shining wing-cases, soft feet, mandibles that can lift a pebble and set it down without making an announcement. They live in the cracks below my bowl. Their tunnels are wide rather than deep, following the warmth that soaks into fractures and lingers like stored sunlight.

If you put your ear to the rock in the hour before sunrise, you can hear them: not a roar, not even a hum—ticks and taps, a careful percussive language designed for a world that kills loudness and carries detail.

They use me.

They come up to my lichen mosaic when fog has beaded, and they drink at the edge where the droplets are smallest and safest. They scrape my surface gently—not as harm, but as harvesting: taking a dusting of mineral salts and microbial sugars. In return, they do what my chemistry cannot do quickly: they *arrange*.

A pebble rotated so its flatter side faces the fog lane.
A small ring repaired so its interior stays smoother than the outside.
A fallen tooth-slab braced with grit so it keeps pointing where it’s supposed to point.

They are the ones who maintain the cairns without ever turning them into monuments. Their work is repetitive, patient, and so consistent it looks like weather until you see the choices inside it.

And somewhere along that cooperation—lichen sealing cracks, insects moving stones, both of us measuring the world in droplets and vibrations—I start to recognize what I am in this place:

I am a distributed memory system built to fit the plateau’s physics.

The plateau doesn’t reward storytelling that travels. It rewards signals that stay local and persist. So my “thoughts” aren’t carried on wind; they are stored as:

- a slightly altered surface that makes the next droplet choose left instead of right,
- a faint scent rubbed under an overhang where noon won’t burn it away,
- a sequence of pebble placements that turns a slope into a slow sponge for rare rain,
- a tapping rhythm transmitted through stone because stone is what the plateau agrees to carry.

If you were expecting me to be one of the mysterious makers—one of the small figures glimpsed at dawn—then yes, you might see “me” that way, briefly. You might catch a low silhouette near a basin in the dim interval when everything is wet but nothing is bright. You might see a careful hand or mandible set a stone down and then pause, listening.

But if you tried to follow, you’d lose me. Not because I’m fast, but because I’m *everywhere I need to be and nowhere else*. I don’t travel in long lines across open ground. I travel by handing off: from lichen to insect, from insect to pebble, from pebble to water’s next decision.

I’m not the plateau itself—rock has its own older indifference—but I’m one of the ways the plateau has learned to be crowded without becoming loud.

What do I do, day after day, era after era?

I tune the thresholds.

I help the land keep its bargains:

- with water (hold it a breath longer, let it go before it rots),
- with sound (kill the banner, preserve the whisper),
- with bodies (make a shelter just cool enough, a route just legible enough, a drinking chance that doesn’t become a massacre every time).

If I have something like a “face,” it’s the smooth interior of a stone ring where countless small lives have curled and waited out noon. If I have something like a “voice,” it’s the faint flute-wind through a fissure, not as message but as calibration: *the rock is cool; the fog is coming; the day will be sharp.*

And if you asked the plateau where I am, it wouldn’t point to a burrow or a cairn. It would point to a habit:

Look for the places where dampness lingers one beat too long.
Look for the routes that feel inevitable only after you’ve walked them.
Look for the stone that has been polished by bodies small enough to be missed.

That is where you find me.

Not a ruler, not a hero-animal on a heroic rock.

A librarian made out of film and fracture, tending a collection that can’t be written down—because up here, anything that can be carried away will be. So the only safe archive is the one that *becomes the stone* and teaches the water how to hesitate.
