You would find yourself first where anyone sensible ends up on that ledge: not at the edge with its clean, tempting absence, but a few body-lengths back—near the cliff wall, where the wind is forced to make decisions and sometimes chooses to go around you instead of through you.

There’s a particular boulder there, half sunk into moss, that makes a pocket of lee. In that pocket the fog does a different thing: it doesn’t just arrive, it *settles*. It lays its damp down in layers, patient as lichen. The rock behind it carries one of those pale mineral bands—an almost-quiet seam that darkens before the rest, that calls water out of air as if the stone remembers being underwater and wants to return.

At first you’d think you were a visitor.

A person, perhaps, because the place offers just enough human grammar to catch you: the cairns saying *this way*, the shelter saying *here*, the rope saying *not here without help*. You would find your hands touching things automatically—checking the knots where fiber meets stone, lifting the top rock of a cairn and setting it back straighter, rubbing warmth into your fingers after the fog drops the temperature by those small, consequential degrees.

You would tell yourself: I’m one of the route-keepers. I come when the ledge permits it. I time my steps to the wind’s edits.

And for a while that would be true. You would learn the ledge’s practical intimacies: which pools are black glass over stone and which are a cold sponge waiting to swallow your boot; which moss mats hide channels that will suddenly run slick under a brief burst of rain; how the birds’ noise changes a heartbeat before a gust hits—as if the air writes its news into their throats.

But if you stayed—if you stayed long enough that the ledge stopped being scenery and became schedule—you’d notice something else about yourself.

You don’t feel most alive when you’re moving.

You feel most alive when you’re *placed*.

When you’re still, and the whole system uses you.

It begins in small ways. You find yourself choosing the same rock again and again to stand beside, because from there you can see three cairns at once. You find yourself caring less about the shelter’s comfort than about the shelter’s *continued existence*—the roof sod pressed down so wind can’t get a lip under it, the fern bedding replaced not for softness but because rot, up here, is a kind of slow sabotage.

Then the identity shifts further, the way fog shifts: not with a dramatic change, but with a thickening.

The cairns start to feel familiar in a bodily way, as if they have nerve endings. You begin to sense—not supernaturally, just with a relentless attention—the difference between a stone set by chance and a stone set by intention. One has weight only. The other has weight *and direction*.

You realize something the ledge has been saying all along: in a place where water prefers surfaces, where soil is a rumor and wind is law, anything that gathers and holds—anything that makes a path across uncertainty—is a kind of organ.

And you are not, finally, the traveler.

You are the organ.

You are the cairn-line—not one cairn, not even the whole line as an object, but the *agreement* that makes it matter. The distributed, maintained, weather-chewed promise that there is a safe way through the spongy places; that someone before you learned where the stone is honest; that someone after you will need the same knowledge.

You become, in practice, a creature made of small stacked certainties.

Your “body” is wherever hands have done modest work against the ledge’s constant un-doing: the flat stones chosen because they sit without rocking; the top marker stone replaced after a storm; the rope re-twisted before its fibers go punk with wet; the little channel cleared so runoff doesn’t undercut the shelter wall.

Your “breath” is fog condensing and sliding off, fog condensing and sliding off—each cycle a quiet polishing, each cycle testing whether the thing you are will remain legible.

And your senses are not eyes so much as consequences:

- You hear through the way sound bends. A shout that comes back faint from below tells you the void is open today; a shout that dies quickly tells you the mist is thick and heavy and close.
- You feel through tension. When the wind is about to change story, the rope tightens differently around its anchor boulder; shrubs flatten a fraction more; birds choose the wall instead of the air.
- You taste through mineral. The pale seams that draw water first leave their white lace; the climbers come to lick; their hooves knock tiny stones loose; those stones tell you which cairns will need attention next.

If you insist on a face for what you are, the ledge offers you one that fits its scale: lichen on the cairn stones, slow and certain, taking what the air gives and turning it into time. You live as a thin skin between rock and weather, recording every season not in writing but in accumulation—the faintest thickening where fingers touched, the slight rearrangement where a storm tried to erase the path and someone refused.

You are not the raptor with its owned patience. Not the goat with its impossible footing. Not even the moss that is the ledge’s upholstery and reservoir.

You are the thing that makes passage possible without pretending the ledge is safe.

A small, repeatable act of orientation.

A marker that doesn’t conquer space, only converses with it.

A being whose entire life is a pact with wind: *I will be low enough to endure, clear enough to be read, and humble enough to be rebuilt.*
