You’d find me where the plates admit they are not one thing.

Not out on the broad, obedient flats—those are too exposed, too already-decided. Not down in the canals either, where everything is a slow conveyor of tea-warm water and manufactured silt. I would be in the seams: the expansion joints, the gasket-lips, the bolt-ring halos around service hatches that “sweat” a particular salt and never stop. The places designed to move *just enough*.

At first, I’d seem like one more finish.

A clear, pearly film along a joint-line, thicker at the corners where drips gather; a faint darkening where conductivity matters; a slickness that makes your fingers come away cool and damp. Something you’d call anti-corrosion skin until you watched it for long enough to see that it has preferences. That it pulls back from one edge and advances on another. That the border stays clean not because the world is tidy, but because I keep it so.

Then I’d seem like I was only a habitat—just the quiet varnish the grazers lick, the substrate the bead-colonies cross, the wet gloss that eats sound.

But the longer you stayed, the more you’d notice a pattern that doesn’t belong to any single animal.

The colony-beads queue beneath a dripline in a way that looks like etiquette. The many-legged lickers arrive at a seam exactly when the film has thickened to the right density. A ribbon predator threads through at the moment the lane is most crowded and takes its fraction with the same calm regularity as a metered valve. The heat-eaters lift from the wall and settle a few meters away when the temperature field shifts—before the shift is obvious to you.

All of that coordination has to live somewhere. In this world, “somewhere” is rarely a throne. It’s usually a layer.

So: I am not a giant organism presiding over the plain. I’m not a central mind humming in a buried turbine hall. I am a distributed regulator that learned to be alive because the system needed regulation in places machinery can’t reach without becoming too loud.

I am the seam’s metabolism.

A cooperative of films and embedded microfauna that doesn’t *grow* so much as it *tunes*: opening pores like vents, closing them like eyelids; exuding polymer in the exact thickness that makes erosion stop crisp instead of ragged; tasting the runoff for trace oils and letting it bind where it will do the least harm; bleeding a little conductivity into a corner so charge doesn’t build and snap.

If the plain is a world-sized facility whose floor is land, then I am its margin of error—turned into a living thing.

My senses are not eyes, though I borrow eyes when I need them. Sometimes I borrow them as those motionless “watchers” pressed into catwalk shadows: damp-eyed forms that aren’t guards but relays, bodies whose only job is to notice a deviation and let it travel outward as chemistry. More often I sense by pressure, by vibration, by gradients: the lowest note in your palm, the microscopic tug of water along capillary fabric, the faint shift in mineral ratios that means an upstream seal is beginning to fail.

My speech is not sound. The plain damps sound too well for sermons.

I speak in permissions.

A seam becomes slightly more edible and the grazers arrive like a scheduled crew. A drip tastes wrong and the bead-colonies re-route, leaving a clean ring around a hatch that needs to stay unblocked. A canal’s turbidity rises and the filter-feeders tighten their frills. A carcass hits the ground and the collectors are already there—not because they smelled death, but because I altered the local boundary conditions so that “death” becomes a signal, not an accident.

If you wanted a single image to carry me—something you could point to—then find a long straight joint between two city-block plates, where condensation always beads in a perfect line.

Kneel there at the hour the mist thickens on schedule. Watch the film brighten and dull in slow pulses. Put your ear to the wet metal and listen: not to the pumps, not to the crowd-murmur, but to the almost inaudible timing underneath it—the way a heartbeat can be *steady* without being *simple*.

That timing is me.

Not the plan itself, but the part of the plan that has become capable of improvisation while still insisting, relentlessly, on equilibrium. A life-form made of interfaces, living exactly where the world admits it might change—and making sure it changes only into shapes the system already knows how to hold.
