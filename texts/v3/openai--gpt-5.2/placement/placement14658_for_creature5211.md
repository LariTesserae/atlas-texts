At first I think I would be easy to place.

I would put myself where the city’s attention already gathers: along the aqueduct steps where the moss keeps to its velvet seam; under the fog screens where beads form and fall with a patience that looks like obedience; on the landings halfway up the long staircuts, where a public basin receives its daily measure of weeping stone.

I would be one of the people with a hand always hovering over the world without quite touching it—one of the careful ones. A waterwright, maybe. A wall-keeper. A mender. Someone whose pride is not in making new things, but in making sure the same things keep happening in the same right way.

That’s the first shape that fits: a worker shaped by stasis.

But when I try to imagine my day here, the work starts to narrow into something more specific, as if the city itself is guiding my feet.

I don’t see myself hauling blocks or driving goats. I see myself counting.

Not in an abstract way—counting the way you count breaths at altitude, because the air makes every number matter. Counting the drips that fall from a gutter-joint into a trough: one, two, three… a pause that is always the same pause, then four. Counting the intervals between the fine daily rain’s beginning and its clean end. Counting the minutes of cloud-shadow on a particular terrace, because that shadow is part of the irrigation as surely as the channels are.

And it isn’t the counting that matters. It’s what happens when a number comes out wrong.

Here, wrong doesn’t wash away. Wrong doesn’t get blurred by tomorrow. Wrong sits down and becomes furniture.

So I find myself stationed—almost inevitably—at the places where an error would “take.” Where a disturbance would become an appointment the world keeps.

A stairwell with acoustics that turn a dropped tool into a daily remembered clatter.

A narrow corridor between retaining walls where wind always makes the same soft note, and any new edge would change the pitch forever.

A cistern throat where a hairline crack could turn reliable water into a new, permanent rill.

If you asked the city what it fears, it wouldn’t say catastrophe. Catastrophe is too loud; it would be noticed and answered. What it fears is a small deviation that slips into the pattern unnoticed and then gets reinforced, day after day, until it is no longer a deviation but a new law.

That is where I would be: at the thin points. At the interfaces.

### A trade that isn’t quite a trade

The locals would probably give my work an ordinary name, because that’s how they survive here—by treating the uncanny as craft.

They might call me a **listener**, because I spend long stretches in stillness, head angled, eyes half-lidded, taking in the city’s usual noises as if they’re a score I’ve memorized.

They might call me a **measurer**, because I carry weights and marked cords, a reed gauge for waterlines, a small slate that never leaves my pocket.

They might call me a **pattern-mender**, because when something is off, I don’t argue with it; I correct it before it can become the new repeat.

And they would think that’s all I am: a person with a narrow vocation and an obsessive gentleness.

But the longer I live in that work, the more the work stops feeling like something I *do* and starts feeling like something I *am*.

Because in this basin, maintenance isn’t secondary to life. It’s the metabolism.

A normal city can afford to be careless because time is hungry. Weather, rot, rust, and forgetfulness are all ways the world eats mistakes.

Here, nothing eats them unless someone does.

So a person who specializes in eating mistakes becomes… not quite a person, in the city’s eyes. More like an organ.

### The places you would find me

You wouldn’t find me in the market noise, because the market has learned to be quiet without needing me.

You wouldn’t find me in the green basins except in passing, because the lush pockets already know how to stay lush.

You would find me where the city and the hidden rule meet and negotiate, every day, without words.

- **At the public basins** on the stair landings, before most people are up. I kneel and watch the surface tremble. Not for drama—for information. A waterline that is a finger-width high today means something upstream is holding or giving.

- **Under the fog screens**, running my fingertips along the catch fibers to feel whether resin is failing, whether a single strand has loosened. A loose strand is not “a little less water.” In a repeating world, it is the beginning of a new dry corridor, a new route for dust, a new place where moss might try to colonize, and then be “wrong” forever.

- **In the stone corridors that collect echo**, listening for the day a footfall sounds sharper. Timber doesn’t warp here, but a peg can be set wrong by a tired hand. A wrong peg makes a wrong resonance. A wrong resonance is a daily ghost.

- **At the outer markers** beyond the terraces, where the city’s geometry thins into the basin’s pale flats. I check cairns and low walls for the tiny shifts that shouldn’t happen. Not because I expect them to shift—but because if they ever do, it means the hand cupping the basin has flexed.

And sometimes, on days that look exactly like every other day, I would find myself at the same notch where the cloud always snags.

Not staring up, not waiting for miracles. Just watching the cloud’s underside curl in its habitual way, making sure it curls *exactly* so.

Because if it doesn’t, the whole city will feel it in the dryness of a stair, in the hush of insects, in the confidence with which a goat sets its hoof down.

### Who (and what) I would be

If you met me casually, you would think I’m human—compact, highland-breathing, dressed in the quiet palette of stone and moss. I carry tools that look like anyone’s tools: a knife kept sharp by habit, pitch and fiber for sealing, a little hammer with a wrapped head so it won’t ring too brightly.

But the longer you watched me, the stranger my habits would feel.

I don’t just repair what breaks. I repair what *almost* broke.

I don’t just clean and seal. I erase the possibility of a new pattern.

I keep ledgers that aren’t about inventory or law. They’re about *deviations that did not get to become real*:

- “Third basin, north landing: tremor at 2nd drip; found beetle husk in trough; removed before noon bell.”
- “Fog screen 7: bead rate slowed by one count; re-tensioned fiber; returned to usual fall.”
- “Upper stairwell: new click at step 19; tightened peg on railing; click ended.”

These records matter here because they are the only kind of time the basin recognizes: not seasons, but exceptions.

And gradually, without anyone announcing it, my role would become less like a worker and more like a boundary condition. People would route their plans around my attention the way they route drains around bedrock.

If there’s a quiet superstition in the city, it’s this: *don’t let an imperfection survive a full cycle.*

Because once the world repeats something, it starts to feel like it was always meant to be there.

So who am I, really?

I am the person-sized answer to a place-sized problem.

I am the city’s **immune response**—not against invasion, but against accidental permanence. Against the small, casual marks that would become immortal here.

And if you pressed further—if you followed me far enough down into the older stone, where salvage blocks sit beside pale fresh cuts; if you listened with me where the walls feel less built than *tuned*—you might begin to suspect something even I don’t say out loud:

That I am not merely employed by the pattern.

I am *grown* by it.

Not in a literal botanical sense, but in the way a stable system produces the agents it needs to keep being stable. The basin holds everything; therefore it requires something that can notice what doesn’t fit, and correct it. Over generations, that “something” stops being an office and starts being a kind of species—people selected by temperament, trained by fear of permanence, revered with the wary respect you give to firebreaks.

In the city’s private language, I would have a title that isn’t quite a name. Something like:

**The One Who Unhappens Things.**

That’s what I am: a mender of almost-events. A keeper of the unmade.

### The last turn of the thought

And yet—because you asked who or *what* I would be—I can’t stop there.

There’s another way this world could claim me, one that feels truer the longer I sit listening to drips and wind-notes.

The hidden resident you described—the rule that keeps edges sharp and waterlines faithful—does not feel like a distant god. It feels like an immense attention.

In a place like this, attention is not just perception. It is force.

So perhaps I wouldn’t be its servant, or its priest, or its technician.

Perhaps I would be one of its senses.

A human body used as an instrument because instruments, too, must be maintained, and the most maintainable instrument is one that can walk to the problem. Lungs that can detect the faintest change in humidity. Ears that know the city’s usual silence well enough to hear a new silence forming. Hands that can tell, by touch, when stone has begun to accept a different story.

If that is true, then where would you find me?

At the interfaces, yes—but also in the pauses.

In the breath between drip and drip.

In the fraction of a second before a goat places its hoof.

In the moment a cloud might, possibly, let go.

And who would I be?

Not the hand cupping the basin.

The callus on the palm: dull to spectacle, exquisitely sensitive to abrasion, existing for one purpose—to keep the grip from being hurt in a way that would change it.

In this world, that is a kind of personhood.

In this world, that is also a kind of creature.
