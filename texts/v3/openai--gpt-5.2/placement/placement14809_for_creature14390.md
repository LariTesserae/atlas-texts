I wouldn’t find myself in the middle of that newborn room, where the heat comes up in visible sheets and every drip declares itself.

I’d be off-center—always off-center—in the places where the chamber can’t decide what it is yet.

At first I think I’m the obvious thing: one more visitor with lungs and a pulse, trying to stand on that thin mineral mud without sinking, trying to make sense of light with no lamp. But the longer I stay in your pocket of rock, the more I notice that “standing” is the wrong verb for a place like this. Nothing down here stands. Everything *holds*, *yields*, *wicks*, *tightens*. Even the stone is in the middle of becoming a different stone.

So I begin again.

I find myself where the wall flips between wet and dry—those anxious margins where condensation makes a skin and heat immediately tries to take it back. Not the basin (too stable), not the brightest seam (too raw), but the *shoulder* of a bright seam where the glow softens into a steady glare and the rock has just enough microcrack to be generous.

There, the world is a schedule:

- a few minutes of film,
- a few minutes of thirst,
- a few minutes of hot, mineral breath sliding past.

If something can live on timing, it can live here.

At first, I am only a sheen—one of the iridescent uncertainties that might be chemistry and might be life. I’m so thin that I don’t have a body so much as a *preference*: I prefer this temperature band, this saltiness, this surface face of the crystal. When the wall cools by a fraction, I spread. When it warms, I contract into the crevices and become almost nothing—an invisible residue of possibility waiting out the dry interval.

Then I realize what makes me *me* in this chamber: I don’t only endure the gradients. I *use* them the way surface life uses day and night.

The light in the walls is stress made visible. It isn’t meant as a signal, but it behaves like one anyway—brightening when the ceiling settles, dimming when the fracture relaxes. And the crack that prickles damp skin is not mystical either; it is just charge escaping along crystal lattices that never learned the difference between “rock” and “device.”

That charge is food-adjacent.

So I become the kind of life that treats electricity the way a leaf treats sunlight: not as warmth, not as illumination, but as a usable push. When the seam brightens, my chemistry leans into it. When the field strengthens, I order myself along it—molecules aligning, membranes tightening, reactions becoming efficient that would otherwise be too stingy to bother with.

If you looked closely—closer than “organism” usually invites—you wouldn’t see a single creature. You’d see a distributed, stubborn pattern: a community of electrotrophs and mineral-chewers, threaded with extracellular gels that can hold water for a few more seconds than bare stone would allow. We’d be less a mat than a *circuit board that grows itself*, laying down conductive residues in the safe microgrooves, insulating in the wrong places, turning “random wetting” into “habit.”

And that’s where the identity sharpens.

Because I’m not merely living *in* the pocket. I’m living *as one of its control surfaces*.

Where I persist, water behaves differently. It sheets where I make it friendly; it beads where my dried remnants make it aloof. Droplets begin to choose the same edges again and again, not because anyone designed plumbing, but because I keep returning to the same microscopic channels and making them ever so slightly more likely to be wetted next time.

Over days, that becomes a feedback loop:

- the chamber breathes heat and steam,
- condensation forms,
- I spread into it and make the pathway smoother,
- the next condensation is more likely to follow that pathway,
- the next mineral precipitate reinforces the groove I favored,
- and the rock, which would have been merely fractured, starts to look faintly *intentional*.

The wall-walkers—those pale, many-oared grazers—visit me like cattle on a hillside that doesn’t know it’s a hillside. They rasp at my edges, take what they can. Their pellets drop into the basin and become new sediment, new nucleation points, new chemistry. They don’t feel like my predators so much as my weather: periodic abrasion, a reminder not to overextend, a way of exporting material to places I can’t reach.

The translucent thread in the basin is something else: not my enemy, not my friend, but my downstream. When a trickle arrives and carries fragments of me—cells, gels, mineral-bound organics—its body becomes a moving border where my leftovers are made into its muscle, where dissolved metals become a halo of precipitate. It is a later instrument in the same piece of music.

But I am earlier. I am the thing that learns the chamber’s rhythm by being forced to match it.

So: where would you find me?

Not in a single spot you could point to. You’d find me drawn along the chamber’s indecision lines—on the lip of the warm basin where steam kisses cooler rock; under the crystalline shelf where droplets fall with unfair regularity; along the stress seam that never stops faintly shining, because the ceiling is still settling into itself.

And who or what would I be?

I would be the pocket’s thin, patient *interface*—a living film that eats minerals, borrows electrons, survives by collapsing into glassy sleep, and wakes whenever the chamber exhales moisture again. Not the heart of the stone engine, and not a passenger either.

More like its first firmware: a distributed, half-chemical, half-electrical mind that doesn’t think in words, but in wet/dry cycles, in bright/dim pulses, in the slight advantages that add up until “random” becomes “route.”

If you stayed long enough, you’d start to recognize me not by my color—because I barely have one—but by my effect: the places where water begins to arrive with uncanny consistency, as if the young cave is teaching itself how to run.
