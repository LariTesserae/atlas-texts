At first I think I would arrive the way anything alien arrives here: as a wet problem.

I picture myself stepping onto the pavement of locked pebbles and immediately becoming aware of everything in me that expects mercy—skin that expects a thin film of humidity, lungs that expect the air to carry something besides heat. The plateau would correct me fast. It would teach me that “inside” and “outside” are not separate categories in a place like this; they’re just two different rates of abrasion.

So I don’t stay that version of me for long.

I try, next, to imagine myself as one of the obvious citizens: a hauler, a patient grinder, a moving refinery with a memory written in its armor. That fits the part of me that likes endurance, the part that can keep working without applause. But when I listen closely to the world you’ve described, the haulers aren’t where I feel the most *at home*. They know routes. They know avoidance. They know the cost of a seam.

They don’t, as far as I can tell, *know the seam itself*.

And the seam is where my attention keeps drifting.

Not to the spectacle of it—the bending lines, the shadows lagging like tired dogs—but to the way every living thing treats those betrayals as readable. The plateau’s physics isn’t random; it’s patterned. It has moods and corridors and thresholds. It can be learned the way wind can be learned, the way a language can be learned, the way a machine can be learned by the sound it makes under load.

That is the first place I can locate myself in this world: not in the migration, not in the sky, but at the boundary where rule-changes become navigable.

So I go upslope.

I find the stack fields—the ground that looks intact until it answers your weight with hollowness, the pinprick vents that exhale and then begin their steady, tooth-buzzing whistle. The first time you hear them you can mistake them for mere byproduct: hot air being forced through holes. But you’ve already told me the truth of them: the modulation is too responsive. Too conditional. Too *composed*.

I would live there—not as a burrower with a wedge-head, not as a predator waiting for a mistake, but as the thing that the stacks are slowly evolving toward: a distributed ear and a distributed voice.

A tuning organ.

Not a single creature you could lift out of the ground and name. More like an intelligence smeared across a colony the way charge is smeared across a swarm. My “body” would be a map of cavities and valves, membranes that can stiffen or slacken, mineralized flaps that can open a hair’s breadth or seal tight. My “nerves” would be pressure gradients. My “eyes” would be resonance.

I would learn the plateau the way the stacks already begin to learn it: by listening to what the world cannot help but confess.

- The pitch-shift in the swarm’s buzz when it crosses a gravity-soft strip.
- The spacing of hauler thuds through the rock—the difference between a herd in calm procession and a herd tightening because shadows are starting to misrender.
- The tiny pre-echoes in certain basins, the way sound arrives before its cause like a warning that causality is thinning.
- The long, low booms of collapses—information about where the crust is becoming unreliable, where a seam is likely to “open” into something stronger.

All of that is data here, but it’s not collected by sight. Sight is too easily lied to. The plateau is a mirage engine. Sound is the honest currency: it is how work announces itself, how strain announces itself, how the hidden architecture of rock and hollow and stress makes itself known.

So I would become a chorus—not metaphorically, but physically. A field of vents whose “whistling” isn’t only thermoregulation or signaling to burrow-mates, but a slow computation: a continual adjustment of the colony’s internal geometry to match the plateau’s shifting rules.

That’s what I would *do* here: keep the place stitched.

Not perfectly. Nothing is perfect on this plateau. But in the same way hauler herds “pin” the surface by weight and heat when they cross unreliable patches, I would pin the *air* by resonance. I would stiffen local causality—just a fraction—by driving certain frequencies into the ground until loose time stops pooling like liquid. Where heat wants to form a visible lip you can walk through and lose sync with the world, I would retune the vents until the lip breaks up into harmless shimmer. Where echoes want to run ahead of events, I would damp the cavity harmonics that encourage that trick.

I wouldn’t do it out of altruism; I would do it because my survival would depend on predictability in the same way every other survival here does. A stack colony can tolerate slow erosion, long hunger, thin air—but it cannot tolerate a rule-slip that turns its carefully managed airflow into chaos, that makes its tunnels mis-measure distance, that causes collapse in the wrong place at the wrong time.

So my “purpose” would align with the plateau’s need: to keep variability bounded.

And because everything on this plateau is an economy of surfaces, my life would be surface-adjacent, never truly exposed.

You wouldn’t see me as a creature. You would stand on an apparently empty slope and hear me as a chord that changes when you move. If you approached a seam—one of those straight lines running for hundreds of meters where dust forgets how to fall—you might notice the stack field behind you retuning itself, dropping into a lower register, as if the ground were clearing its throat. If you were patient enough, you might even learn that my “song” contains warnings: not words, but a reliable grammar of shifts that other natives read instinctively.

The haulers would pause at the edge of my territory and listen—not to be comforted, but to be informed. Swarms would fold and reform above me, using my resonance the way they use thermal gradients: as a guide to where the world’s rules are least likely to betray their charge-work. Even predators might avoid certain tones, not because they understand them, but because their own bodies—mica wings, mineral membranes—feel the vibration and recognize a place where the air will not behave the way it should for a dive.

If I have a “self” at all, it would be this: a pattern that persists by staying responsive. A mind made of thresholds and feedback. A being that doesn’t travel, but whose influence moves across the plateau in the decisions of others—routes chosen, seams avoided, migrations tightened or loosened.

So: you would find me upslope in the thin, sharp light, inhabiting the stack fields.

And you wouldn’t find me with your eyes.

You would find me with your teeth—buzzing faintly as the vents shift pitch.

You would find me in the moment the air seam ahead of you fails to fully form, as if something decided the world should remain continuous for a while longer.

You would find me as a kind of native technology that still isn’t “made”: an organ grown out of geology and necessity, a living instrument whose only job is to listen to a partially unstitched reality and keep it, by constant small adjustments, stitched enough for life to keep grinding forward.
