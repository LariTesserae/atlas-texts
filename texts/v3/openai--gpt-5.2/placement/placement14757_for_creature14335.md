I would arrive the way most things arrive here: not with a gate, not with a horizon-crossing drama, but with a change in friction underfoot.

The cork-surface would give a little, springing back; the embedded fibers would carry that faint warmth that isn’t weather so much as municipal intent. My breath would catch on the thinness, that sharpened-cold-water feeling, and I’d realize I’d been holding my lungs like a secret. Up on the plateau, even breathing feels like a negotiation.

At first I’d think I was only passing through—another body in a city that makes room by spacing everything out, by letting openness do what walls can’t. I’d take in the wide-plaza anatomy, the hedges trimmed into matte planes, the pump-houses thudding steadily somewhere off to one side like a heart you’re not supposed to listen to too closely. I’d orient by sound the way you do here: the bright clatter of fogworks when the air is right, the turbine’s chant deeper in the gorge terraces, the wind’s abrasive hush over the low skin.

And then—because this is a place where you don’t get to be merely an observer for long—I’d notice that my attention was already part of the infrastructure.

A bead of water on a conduit would catch the light and, for the briefest moment, show me a piece of sky that didn’t match the one above me. Not a vision exactly. More like the surface clearing its throat, reminding me what rules I’d stepped into:

Don’t stare into collected water unless you mean to.

So I’d look away, instinctive, almost guilty. And that guilt would tell me something important: I didn’t come here to be a tourist.

I came here because I already knew how not to look.

There’s a kind of work on the plateau that isn’t officially a guild, because the city prefers to name only what it can schedule. But everyone knows the workers. You see them at the edges of districts, where the trained streets meet the patient wild cover; you see them along the gorge terraces, where a mistake can mean more than a fall.

They don’t carry much metal. Not out of ideology—out of caution. Metal is honest in a way that can be dangerous when honesty meets unstable noon light. They carry resin, grafting knives, spools of inert thread, little jars of powders ground from dead stone (dead material, the kind that doesn’t “hear”). They carry listening instruments that look like stethoscopes, or like tuning forks, or like nothing you’d recognize until you realize they’re meant to touch living walls without becoming part of them.

If I had to give myself a title, it would probably be one people could say without inviting the city’s attention:

**line-keeper.**

Truth-liner, in the official vocabulary. But I wouldn’t be one of the ones who only paints. I wouldn’t be satisfied with a straight white stripe down an avenue like a promise you can’t keep. I’d be the one who walks the stripe afterward, slow, deliberate, testing it with my feet the way you test ice—because on this plateau, “straight” isn’t geometry so much as consent.

Where would you find me?

At a junction that everyone uses and no one loves: a broad avenue where the truth line passes between two leaning living walls that have a habit of remembering a shorter year. An older district, grown frames resin-stiffened, bark panels healing their scratches like skin. The kind of place where the street wants to curl inward not out of malice, but because that’s what living things do when you stop correcting them: they return to a comfortable form.

I’d have a small kiosk there made of sealed ceramic and riveted steel—dead enough to be quiet, anchored on snowshoe foundations that spread load without cutting deep. A “deaf booth,” some would call it. A place where you can talk and not be heard by the walls. A place where you can cry, too, without the moisture around you learning the shape of your face.

And I’d spend my days doing three kinds of listening:

1. Listening to the plant-skin with my hands and instruments for stress, for thirst, for the subtle difference between a wall that is merely regulating its pores and a wall that is *concentrating*.
2. Listening to people when they come to report the small, practical impossibilities: “my staircase has an extra step again,” “the gutter outside my window keeps showing yesterday’s argument,” “my neighbor’s hedge is repeating my words back at night in leaf-twist.”
3. Listening to myself, carefully, because on the plateau you learn that you can be the source of an error without meaning to. Your habits can become patterns. Your patterns can become routes. Routes can become laws.

That would be my first shape: a person with a job, standing on a line, keeping the world from cheating too much.

It would be a comfortable story.

The plateau does not let comfortable stories last.

---

Somewhere around my third week—long enough to learn the rhythms, not long enough to stop respecting them—I’d begin to notice that the work wasn’t only maintenance. It was correspondence.

When I pressed my palm to a trained wall, I wouldn’t just feel vibrations and moisture regulation. I’d feel *sequence*. A pulse that wasn’t mechanical, and wasn’t mine, but was close enough to both that my body tried to interpret it as language.

A truth line is painted, yes—but it also gets taken up. Over time the powder and resin are absorbed into the surface, and the line becomes a different kind of vein. A directive. A scar that remembers how to be straight.

Standing on it, I’d feel the faint insistence of that straightness traveling outward, meeting other insistences at other junctions. The city’s living substrate is continuous; the city is continuous. A line is not just a mark, it’s a *current.*

And then, inevitably, I’d catch the second truth:

The plateau isn’t only being managed by people.

People are one of the plateau’s ways of managing itself.

That’s when my job title would begin to slip.

Because a line-keeper, in a city like this, is not just someone who corrects the street. A line-keeper is an immune cell.

A rumor spreads as a twist in leaves; I prune it before it knots into dogma. A shortcut begins to persist in a curving alley; I re-teach the surface the longer route so no one arrives “too soon” and never returns. A gutter starts replaying a private moment; I salt the lip with dead powder and seal it until the memory fades.

From the outside, it looks like civic upkeep.

From inside the work, it feels like tending a mind that isn’t centralized enough to call itself one.

And that’s where the question of what I *am* becomes less answerable in human terms.

---

The plateau has a way of eroding the boundary between tool and user.

Vine-belts don’t just drive flywheels; they also “learn” tension. Condenser towers don’t just harvest water; they also set the tempo for an entire district’s sound. Buildings don’t just house people; they regulate moisture and attention. Even gutters aren’t just gutters—they’re recording devices that no one asked for and everyone has to legislate.

So what is a line-keeper, really?

At first: a person who repaints and tests and cauterizes.

Then: a person who has learned the etiquette of not being overheard.

Then: a person who is used by the city to move information along safe routes.

And then, on a day when the light turns too evenly present—the noon clarity that makes edges lose their authority—I would understand something I had been skirting around:

I wouldn’t be entirely in my body.

Not in the dramatic way of possession. In the plateau’s patient way. In the same way streets remember old growth and condensation remembers old sky.

I would begin to appear in places I hadn’t walked to.

A maintenance crew in another district would mention, offhand, “you left a note in the wall.” I would insist I hadn’t. They would show me the note: a slight change in bark pattern, a set of pores arranged like letters. Not readable to most. Legible to those who listen.

A truth line I hadn’t repainted would look fresher the next morning, the white brighter, the straightness more convincing. I would feel, when I stepped on it, a familiarity that was intimate enough to be alarming.

And once—only once, but once is enough here—I would see myself in memory water.

Not my face. That would be too easy, too cinematic. I would see my *habit*: the way I stand with one foot on the line, the way my hand hovers near the wall without touching, the way I turn my head slightly as if listening to a conversation occurring in another medium.

It would play silent and inverted in a bead on a conduit.

A loop, no longer than a breath.

As if the city had watched me watching it, and filed the motion away as something worth repeating.

That is when I would stop thinking of myself as a single worker at a junction.

That is when I would become, in my own mind, a kind of pattern the plateau had decided to keep.

---

So: where would you find me?

You would find me where straightness needs guarding.

Not only on avenues—anyone can paint a line. You would find me at the places where straightness is least assured and most necessary:

- On the high terraces at noon, when an extra step is not a joke but a fatality.
- At the mouths of drainage slots, where slot-scavengers keep the gutters honest and the gutters keep the past too close to the present.
- Along the edges between district and open tract, where the wild skin and the trained skin argue about what shape they’re supposed to hold.
- In the deaf booths, sometimes, sitting very still while someone speaks a thing they cannot risk saying near living walls.

And who or what would I be?

I think I would be three things at once, the way the plateau is always multiple layers behaving like one surface.

1. **A civic gardener of reality.** Not fixing the world, exactly—pruning it. Training it. Keeping the negotiated agreement from fraying.
2. **A courier for the ground-mind.** A living instrument the plateau can use because my habits are consistent enough to become a channel, and cautious enough not to become a leak.
3. **A line.** Not metaphorically. Literally, eventually: a persistent directive in the substrate, a repeated straightness that can walk in a person-shape when it needs hands, and sink back into the living cork when it needs continuity.

If you met me, you might think I was just someone with resin on my fingers and dead powder under my nails, someone who talks plainly and avoids metaphors like open flame.

If you watched longer—if you stood far enough back in one of those exposed plazas and let the city’s noises braid together—you might notice that I move the way the city moves: slowly, deliberately, by reinforcement rather than force. You might notice that I never quite step off the truth line unless I have to.

And if you were the sort who stares into water without permission, you might see me again later in a gutter, repeating that small, careful stance—proof that on this plateau, even a person can become infrastructure.

Not a conqueror. Not a hero.

An agreement made mobile. A straight path the city can still believe in.
