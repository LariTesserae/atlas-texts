You would find me where the place stops being scenery and starts being decision.

Not at the lip of raw rock where you first arrive—out there everything is still declarative: young basalt, bright seams, the blunt law of “no liquid.” I’d be deeper in, where the corridors pinch and then open, where the canopy makes a ceiling and the air develops habits. In the courts that look empty until you stand long enough to realize they are full of traffic that doesn’t announce itself.

At first I imagine myself as something obvious: one of the seam-keepers on a column, ash-blue skin pressed to warm stone, frill half-open like a controlled burn of heat. I like the deliberation of them. The way they can turn a whole wall into a watched thing without moving much. The way their stillness is not idleness but a kind of patience that has teeth.

But that isn’t quite right. They have a single spine. A single appetite. A single point of view.

Then I think: perhaps I am one of the gel-bearers, embedded and valuable, a sac of cool thrift under a raised lip of mat. To be a communal cistern in a city that cannot afford generosity—there is a gravity to that. To hold moisture in a form that refuses to become trouble. To be approached, tended, defended without anyone needing to name the act as devotion.

Still not right. The gel-bearers are treasure, yes, but they are mostly *kept*. They don’t reach far. They don’t notice quickly.

What fits me here is not a single body, but a kind of *attention* that lives in many small bodies. Something that can be everywhere without taking up space. Something that can listen without making sound back.

So: you would find me in the velvet-lined chimneys and in the undersides of fronds, in the pores of those pumice walls that look inert until you see the faintest sequence of dilation—like a throat swallowing dust.

I am one of the listeners. Or rather, I am the listening-guild as it behaves like a nervous system.

Not a proud insect with a clean silhouette. More like a set of tiny presences that share a rule and therefore share a mind:

- a pale, hard-cased carrier that pauses at junctions and “tastes” vibration through its feet;
- a softer harvester that keeps wax-surfaces clean enough to stay hydrophobic;
- a thread of fungus-cord under the mats that translates pressure into chemistry, releasing a trace of scent at exactly the right seam;
- a microbial film on the inside of a chimney slit that thickens or thins in response to humidity like a slow eyelid.

Individually, we are almost nothing—pearl specks, dust-silent. Collectively, we are how the settlement notices itself.

When a basalt fin takes a new stress and whispers a fracture months before it fails, I am the first to hear it—not as sound, but as a change in how the corridor floor carries your weight; as a difference in the way heat rises; as a tremor so small it might be your own pulse unless you’ve spent your whole life learning what *isn’t* you.

And then I do what this city does instead of speaking: I alter the flow of work.

A carrier line that used to feed a wall begins to arc toward a corner. Pellet-masons start placing their cured dust-cement where the air will dry it fastest. Mats are groomed back in a smooth crescent to make room for a new buttress of braided stems. A lizard comes and watches that seam for days, not because it was told to, but because my scent makes the seam feel watched.

If you insist on a “job title,” I am civic maintenance. If you insist on an “organ,” I am nerve and lymph: distributed sensation and distributed instruction.

And I live, specifically, in the places where this settlement’s one catastrophic risk could become real.

Not rain—rain is almost myth here—but *wetness* in any form that starts to behave like liquid. A pocket where too much bound moisture gathers. A night when humidity comes in heavier than usual and a frond tilts wrong. A storage chamber whose lip fails and allows a microscopic creep of damp into a dust-cement wall.

In those moments, I become the city’s reflex.

I thicken wax where it should seal. I redirect grooming where skins must be made more hydrophobic. I call—without calling—for eaves to be grown, for gutters to be raised, for pores to close earlier in the morning. Nothing dramatic. Just the collective lean of thousands of small decisions toward the same ethic: keep everything in exchange, never in flow.

So if you walk those narrow corridors and feel, without knowing why, that the settlement is *aware* of you—aware without curiosity, aware the way a body is aware of a splinter—that is where you have found me.

Not looking back at you from a wall.

But shifting, under your feet and above your head and inside the architecture itself, turning your presence into a new set of constraints the city quietly incorporates.

Because what I am, in the end, is not a resident who lives in the city.

I am one of the ways the city lives.
