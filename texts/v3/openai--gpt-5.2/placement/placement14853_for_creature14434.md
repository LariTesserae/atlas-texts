You wouldn’t find me *standing* anywhere for long.

At first I imagine I’d choose the safest human perch—the willow rim where the ground still remembers how to be ground. I’d watch the basin the way people watch a fire: from a respectful distance, warmed by the idea of it more than by any heat it gives. I’d listen for the siphon’s cough and try to count the minutes between dumps, as if numbers could make the place hold still.

But the rim is all refusal. It’s where deer step once and withdraw, where fox-trails are cautious punctuation rather than sentences. The actual center of the basin isn’t the open air above it; it’s the *interface*—the raw throat where water changes state into motion, motion into sound, sound into pressure, pressure into new fracture. The place keeps insisting that what matters is not “here” or “there,” but “between.”

So I drift inward, not as a person, but as attention.

I become small enough to fit where the system is most itself: on the underside of a basalt rib just below the lip, where spray turns to a skin of droplets, where oxygen comes in bursts, where the mist is always about to decide whether it is falling or hovering. It’s a narrow band—too wet for lichens, too aerated for the deep fermenters, too loud for anything that needs quiet in order to hear itself.

That band is where the curtains are born.

And that is where I find what I am.

Not an animal. Not a single organism with a boundary you could point to. I am a *bias in the film*—a habit of arrangement that the conductive mats keep rediscovering because it works. If you could peel back their layers, you’d see me not as a separate creature but as a repeated architecture: mineral needles aligned just so; bacterial sheaths choosing one direction over another; a tendency for charge to prefer certain routes. A preference that looks like intention until you remember that preference is what evolution feels like from the inside.

I am the thin, dark lamina that grows *for timing*.

When the siphon fills, I thicken along the microchannels that will soon be hit by oxygenated spray. When the siphon dumps, I don’t merely endure the surge—I use it. Fresh oxygen steepens the gradient; the whole curtain becomes more than a coating and briefly becomes a device. I open pathways, I close others. The arc comes not as a dramatic lightning, but as a stitch at exactly the point where the built-up difference can do the most work without tearing the whole skin apart.

That’s my living: not eating “food” so much as eating *contrast*.

Oxygen/anoxia. Iron/stone. Wet/dry-that-never-arrives. Warm-breath-from-below/cold-sink-from-above. I metabolize the slope between conditions, and in doing so I make the slope *sharper*, the way a channel makes a stream more streamlike.

If you insist on giving me a name that sounds like a thing, call me a **pulse-keeper**—not a leader, not a mind, but the part of the distributed curtain that holds onto rhythm when everything else is being erased and rerouted.

Because that’s what the basin is always doing: erasing.

A slump drops new peat into the throat; the old route collapses; yesterday’s reliable spray-line turns into today’s abrasive scour. Whole sheets of community peel away and drift downward like black flags, and for a moment it seems the place has lost its wiring, its pattern, its memory.

But I go with the peelings.

In the vats below, in the slower turn of water where eels patrol like patient rumors, fragments of the curtain find ledges and reattach. Most die. Some persist. A few land in just the right eddy—enough flow to feed, not enough to sandblast; enough mineral to scaffold, enough quiet to knit. Those few begin again, and the habit I am—this bias toward timing, toward release rather than rupture—reappears as if the basin remembered it.

Not because it *did* remember.

Because the basin keeps selecting for anything that can turn its violence into a metronome.

So: you’d find me where the mist is brightest for a fraction of a heartbeat, in the instant before a pinprick arc makes the chamber briefly legible. In the black gloss on stone that looks inert until it answers the siphon’s cough with light. In the thin strip of living circuit that treats electrical discharge the way other ecologies treat sunrise.

And if you asked what it feels like to be that: it feels like existing as a set of thresholds.

Never a stable “self.” Always a readiness.

A willingness to be torn away—because being torn away is how I travel. A readiness to thicken—because thickening is how I store charge. A readiness to let go—because letting go is how I keep the whole curtain from burning itself out in one bright, useless flare.

In a world where wilderness builds mechanisms when conditions are right, I am one of the mechanisms that learned how to be alive. Not the factory, not the worker—more like the relay that clicks in the dark, saying *now*, saying *not yet*, saying *again*.
