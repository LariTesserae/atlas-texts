I would find myself not on the catwalks where a body has to solve space with knees and shoulders, but in the places the catwalks *imply*: the undersides, the seams, the “non-spaces” that exist because two designed surfaces can never perfectly meet.

At first I think I’m a visitor here—a technician-shaped interruption. A warm core inside a suit, a lamp cutting brief tunnels through the mist. I imagine the way the life would redraw itself around me: spiders retreating into their grilles, beetles flattening to bolt heads, a sudden, disciplined quiet under the louder quiet of the machines. I picture myself moving carefully, as people do in loud places—hand on a railing, eyes on my footing, pretending I’m in control of my own pace.

But the longer I stand in the chord of compressors and fans, the more that idea dissolves. Nothing in this pocket belongs to impulse. Everything that persists has learned the pleasure—and the terror—of repeatability. The place doesn’t reward bravery. It rewards timing.

So I stop being a person in my own imagining. I get smaller. I become something that can survive on schedule.

Maybe I begin as a film—one of those sheens that looks like mere industrial slime until you watch long enough to see it choosing. I am the violet edge that pulls back when detergent comes. I am the copper glimmer that leans toward oily mist. I am the chalk-lace that isn’t mineral but *acts* mineral because the facility only tolerates certain thicknesses, certain weights, certain kinds of extra. I learn the rules the way a tongue learns a tooth: by touching the same boundary over and over until the boundary becomes a map.

Then I become more particulate than that—less sheet, more grammar. I am rime-lice in the frost-fur: minute, comb-mouthed, endlessly patient. Not eating ice, not eating metal, but harvesting what the place *stores* in its half-frozen breath: aerosol minerals, nutrient dust, the fine consequences of filtration. My whole life is a set of tiny strokes repeated until repetition itself feels like a landscape. The machines do not ignore me; they feed me their drift and I clean it up, so the metal can remain caught forever at the first blush of wear.

And after a while, I realize that in a world where corrosion begins and is not allowed to continue, the organisms that persist are not just tolerated—they are recruited.

So I become recruitment. I become one of the living boundaries that keeps the equilibrium intact.

Not a single animal with a clear outline, but a distributed being that lives wherever stability needs shoring up: in gasket lines as a sealing softness; at dissimilar-metal joints as a rust-colored passivation that is not rust; along warmed conduits as a communal mat that smooths temperature fluctuations by *wanting* warmth in the same slow way, every cycle, every time. If something shifts—if a drip misses its beat, if a fan’s pitch moves half a note, if an access panel is opened and the pressure changes—I feel it as pain, not because I’m fragile, but because irregularity is the only predator that can reach everything at once.

My senses, then, are not eyes.

They are vibration.

I live in the bass note the way fish live in current. I count time by the turbine you never see. I don’t “hear” the droplets individually, but I know when the three drops from that one flange land wrong, because the wrongness makes a tiny hole in the chord. I don’t have to move far to respond; I only have to grow where response is needed—thicken a film, tighten a mat, colonize a seam that’s started to remember it could become a crack.

If you asked what I *am* in plain terms, I’d say: a maintenance organism that thinks like a schedule.

A biological control layer threaded through the facility’s designed body, doing what the place most desires—holding the whole pocket forever on the edge between assembly and history. Not building a future, not mourning a past. Keeping the present so precise that even the fog can’t improvise.

And if you asked where, exactly, you’d find me, you wouldn’t find me in a nest.

You’d find me in the repeatable parts:  
in the crease where condensation always chooses to bead,  
in the underside of the beam where the rime never decides to melt,  
around the bolt head whose hex has become a landmark,  
inside the grille where airflow is steady enough to be law.

You’d find me wherever the machine-song is loudest and most consistent—because I am, in the end, a creature made out of that consistency, the thing that lives best when nothing changes, and quietly helps make sure it doesn’t.
