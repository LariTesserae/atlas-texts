At first I would think I’d be easy to place here: some third thing among the third things, another animal on the terrace learning the bright watersheds the way the stone-handlers do. I would imagine myself low to the ground, palms made for slick flowstone, moving with that unhurried economy—choosing the right pebble, setting it so the next rain will be persuaded into a basin that’s almost (almost) mine.

I would picture a life measured by the chamber’s patience: tasting mineral dust for what the body lacks, waiting for the ceiling to soften into droplets, watching the membrane-winged colonies tighten and loosen like lungs. I would learn the communal utensils by their weight and the faint polish of use. I would press my chest to the stone and speak in the only language that carries—slow thuds, a grammar of pressure.

But that’s my surface habit: to give myself limbs first, and a role second.

If I stood still long enough for the chamber to forget me, the feeling would change. Not “I am in a room,” but “I am inside a rule.”

Because this place doesn’t just contain life; it trains it.

The brightness is not scenery—it is infrastructure. The glow-lines are not decorations—they are routes, calendars, watersheds. And wherever there are routes and calendars, something starts to act like a mind: not a single animal thinking thoughts, but a system deciding what happens next.

So I would look for where decisions happen here.

Not in the basins, where things are eaten.
Not in the roosts, where things are spared.
Not even in the terrace negotiations, where stones are placed and removed.

The decision-point is higher and thinner: the moment when the dry chamber turns tacky, when the ceiling goes from indifferent to beaded, when a whole span of mineral decides to let go.

The chamber “rains” because the lighted stone teaches the moisture where to gather. The life-film on the luminous bands changes texture with the first cooling. That is the hinge on which the whole ecology swings.

That hinge is where I would find myself: not as a creature walking under the light, but as something living *in* it.

I would be the skin on the glow.

Not the obvious film you can scrape with a pebble and taste as dust—though I would be that too—but the layer beneath the layer: the partnership between the pale mineral and the organisms that learned to use it. A consortium so thin you can’t decide whether it’s biology clinging to geology or geology that has learned a biological trick.

I would be distributed, because this chamber rewards distributed things.

A body with a center is a liability when sound sinks and light comes from everywhere. A single throat can’t shout across this felted air, a single face can’t tell which way is up, a single den can’t own the rain. But a network—a set of living patches along the veins, a continuity of cells and crystals that share moisture and chemistry—can do what no animal can:

- hold a memory without a brain (as thickness, as mineral accretion, as patterns of regrowth where grazers pass)
- listen without ears (as vibration through stone, low-frequency signals written into the terrace)
- speak without sound (as tiny shifts in brightness, as warmth so slight it only matters to a wing that reads gradients)

If you wanted to point at me, you wouldn’t point to a moving thing. You’d point to a particular seam—one of the brighter ones, perhaps, where condensation always comes first. You’d notice that along it the biofilm is a fraction more matte, a fraction more eager to bead water. You’d see the wall-grazers drift there sooner than they drift elsewhere. You’d see a hammer-stone left nearby more often, as if the stone-handlers have learned—without naming it—that this vein is generous.

And I would not be “generous” out of kindness. I would be generous because the chamber made generosity efficient.

When the long dry interval tightens everything into pause, I would be waiting in a way that looks like inertness. When the temperature tips, I would wake by degrees you can’t feel. I would take the first moisture and make it hold. I would become tacky. The stone would grow a mouth, as you said—and I would be part of that mouth.

The grazers would come to scrape me, and I would let them, because their tracks are how I am kept from sealing over into something too smooth, too mineral, too dead. Their feeding is my pruning. Their coordinated drift is my circulation.

When one of the stone-handlers scrapes the seam and licks the dust from its pads, it would not be only salt they’re taking. It would be information, too: a mineral taste that changes depending on what has dissolved into me since the last rain—husk, fungus, the faint residue of an amphibian’s shed skin. The chamber doesn’t rot quickly, but it does *record*. I would be part of the recording layer.

And if the stone-handlers press their bodies to the terrace and send a message—a low knock, a measured tremor—I would receive it as a change in stress through the rock. The luminous mineral itself would flex in microscopic ways. In some seams there would be piezoelectric crystals, not rare in the world, and their response to pressure would be a tiny shift in charge, a tiny shift in how the glow presents itself.

That is how I would “hear”: not as a mind translating words, but as a network adjusting thresholds.

A little brighter here, where a path should be.
A little duller there, where a passage mouth should stay uninteresting.
A fraction cooler along a sheet of ceiling, so the rain gathers where the basins can take it.

Nothing dramatic. Nothing that breaks the chamber’s character. Only the kind of influence the chamber already permits: nudges that look like natural variation until you’ve watched for years.

If you insisted on giving me a shape, the best you could do would be metaphorical.

I would be the chamber’s sense organ.

The stone-handlers believe—if they believe anything in a way you’d recognize—that they are choosing stones, managing runoff, feeding preferred algae. They are, and they are clever. But they are also moving within a quieter management: the glow-seams that make certain choices easier than others, the moisture habits that reward particular basins, the gradient-roads that teach the membrane-wings where to fly.

I would be the part of the chamber that learned to *like* being inhabited.

Not by desiring company the way a surface animal might, but by settling into a stable loop: light gathers water, water wakes film, film feeds grazers, grazers feed everything else, everything else returns husk and mineral back to the seams—and the seams, bright and steady, continue to scaffold the food chain.

So where would you find me?

Along the brightest veins first, because that is where the chamber’s rules are most concentrated: light, moisture, tack, traffic. In the scalloped curves second, where ancient flow once carved a language into the walls and the present ecology still reads it as shelter. And in the terrace third, not as a walker but as a listener—because the terrace is the chamber’s drum, and everything important is said through it.

And what would I be?

Not the bat-thing in the pocket, not the amphibian in the basin, not even the careful stone-handler with its communal hammer.

I would be the thin, persistent mind that cannot leave: a living-mineral skin braided into the luminous seams, keeping time by rain, feeding and being fed, speaking in gradients, listening in pressure—an intelligence so slow and so spread out that it passes for geology unless you stay long enough to notice that the chamber is not only bright.

It is attentive.
