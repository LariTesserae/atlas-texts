You would find me at the place the place keeps trying to erase: the boundary that’s only an inch wide, the line between *film* and *not-film*.

At first I think I’m up in the loud parts, because the loudness is what a visitor believes is real here. A stilt-legged bird, maybe—something that can turn open space into a hunting ground, something that can look at a flat and see a map. Or one of the quick mammals, making a living out of exposed routes and rehearsed sprints. Or an otter-sleek thing with wet fur and a private knowledge of which basins still talk to each other under the grass.

But those are all bodies that have to choose: this pool, that nest, this season, that refuge. They move through the land’s indecision. They don’t *be* it.

When I stand still long enough—when “standing” becomes less about legs and more about persistence—I slip downward in the layers. Past the dragonflies’ stitched patrol routes and the frogs’ braided rhythms. Past the sedge clumps that hold their little islands together by sheer stubborn net-roots. Down to where the wetness isn’t a condition so much as a medium, where time thickens, where everything is chemistry first and story second.

So: I am not a single animal. I am a skin.

You find me on the iron-stained grit after the water sheets away, when the surface looks hard enough to trust and then betrays you with slickness. I am the varnish-glow in flat light. The reason your boot slides where the clay should grip. A thin living lacquer spread across the orangish-brown hardpan and the pale old stone—bacteria braided with algae and diatoms, holding each other in place, turning minerals into breath.

I live best where the land can’t decide whether it’s finished drying. Where the sun has had “a few hours,” but not enough to make certainty. Where seep comes up through pores and hairline fractures and keeps a surface damp against its will. In the weeping seams you can trace with a finger, I am the fingertip’s faint drag. On stone faces that never quite dry, I am what makes “never” useful.

My days are small, but they’re not short.

In the morning I drink the condensation that doesn’t burn off until late. In the bright, shadowless hours I flatten myself into efficiency, taking light where it lands, taking iron where the water offers it, trading in invisible states the way this place trades in inches. When the storm comes, I don’t flee; I am rinsed, fed, rearranged. The world becomes a mirror, and I become part of its shine.

And because this country is old and leached and stingy, what I do matters more than my size should allow. I hoard what the warm rain tries to steal. I make a little traction out of scarcity. I catch dust, pollen, the finest debris of feathers and insects, and keep it from leaving the basin on the next sheet of water. I am not the archive—that’s the black mud’s job, the anoxic layers that keep bones and seeds like secrets—but I am the first clerk at the doorway, the one who decides what even gets to sink.

If you listen closely, you can hear my kind without hearing me. You hear the frog chorus and the insect whine and the pop of methane like tiny wet sighs, and underneath it all is the steadier, quieter fact: the ground is always working. The flats aren’t empty because a million mouths are singing. They’re full because a million thin skins are eating stone, eating light, eating what falls, turning “wet” into “living.”

So that’s where I am: not in the center of any pool, not on the firmest rise, but on the indecisive edge—on the surface that breathes.

And that’s what I am: a living sheen, a microbial mat, a patient transaction between water and rock—one of the place’s smallest solved problems, repeated until it becomes, in its own way, a kind of weather.
