At first I imagine I’d be something fast—one clean line of muscle in the midwater, making a living by refusing to touch the bottom. A lantern-fish logic: hover where two currents cancel each other out, spend almost nothing, take one decision at a time and let the dark erase the rest.

But that isn’t where I’d end up. Not really.

Because the thing that keeps tugging at me in this place isn’t the open water. It’s the *edge*—the boundary where the world starts to behave like matter again. That thin, law-heavy stratum a hand’s-breadth above the seafloor where silt lifts, settles, lifts again; where a stone’s presence edits an entire small weather system; where information travels in pressure before it ever becomes sight.

So you’d find me there: not in the basin’s soft blankness, and not in the higher dark where everything is equally suspended, but tucked into the lee of a rounded basalt back—some old outcrop that has stopped being dramatic and started being dependable. A place the currents “remember” in their own way: always a little calmer on one side, always a little fed by drift, always a little spared from the worst grit-storms because the flow has to lift to get over it.

And I wouldn’t be a builder. The water makes a joke of builders.

I’d be an *arranger*.

A small, deep-water cephalopod—not the flamboyant, reef-bright kind, but the kind that has accepted the palette here: silt-skin, bruise-grey, the faint translucence of something that lives more by touch than by display. Soft enough to ignore pressure’s tricks. Patient enough to let time do work that strength can’t. Clever enough to understand that the only durable structure available is a structure that can be undone quickly.

My home wouldn’t look like a home. It would look like a slight irregularity in what is otherwise all negotiation: a shallow pocket under basalt where the current passes but doesn’t *bite*.

The entrance would be edited, not built—plugged with a stone that is exactly heavy enough to stay put in ordinary drift and exactly light enough to be moved when I need to leave fast. Not a door. A sentence-ending. A way of telling the water, *not here, not now.*

Inside: a hoard that isn’t wealth so much as vocabulary.

Shell fragments, but not stacked as a wall—gathered into a hollow because the hollow reduces the angles that silt can attack. A few stones with edges sharper than the sea prefers, because once in a while I need an edge for a single moment: crack, pry, separate. A bone wedge that could have come from anywhere and means nothing until it’s jammed under exactly the right lip of rock to lever something loose. None of it permanent. All of it chosen.

My eyes would be useful only at the range of confession—close enough that the water hasn’t had time to lie. Beyond that I’d read the world the way this world wants to be read: by pressure, by vibration, by chemistry smeared thin.

A turbidity pulse would announce itself long before it arrived: not a roar, but a change in the texture of the flow against my skin, a subtle thickening of grain in the water’s touch. I would retract everything delicate. I would become a stone that happens to breathe. And I’d wait while the bottom-storm slid past, sandpapering the outside of the world, rearranging gravel with slow authority.

When it cleared—when “clear” meant only that the nearer darkness had fewer flecks in it—I’d come out and do what passes for work down here: I’d harvest the drift the storm delivered. Not by chasing. By *finding*.

A stunned amphipod in a quiet eddy behind my ridge. A worm fan extended a little too long before it realized the water had changed its mind. A bivalve that thought the lee-side was forever. I wouldn’t take much at once; the cost of excess is attention, and attention down here is expensive. What I didn’t eat immediately I’d drag into my pocket, not for storage in the human sense, but to change the odds—so scent stays local, so scavengers have to come close, so the current doesn’t simply hand my meal to the next mouth downflow.

And here is the part that feels most like “me,” if I’m honest:

I’d be something that *listens* for patterns in a world that erases patterns.

The water is full of messages that don’t look like messages: the fine tick of gravel settling means a slope has shifted; the long, low bone-thrum means something big passed overhead and the pressure is still remembering it; the sudden quiet of drifting particulates means the flow has dropped and the seafloor will hold its shape for a little while.

I’d learn the local grammar of these things. Not as prophecy, not as certainty—just as the difference between moving now and moving later, between leaving the entrance plugged and leaving it open, between foraging on the plain and staying tight to stone.

If I had light at all, it would be stingy and private: a mute dot along the inside of an arm, the smallest signal used only at touching distance—more for my own orientation than for show. A way to count my own limbs in darkness dense enough to feel like fabric. A way to check whether what I’m touching is shell, stone, flesh, or the slick skin of microbial film.

And if you watched long enough—if the currents granted you a rare hour of steadiness—you might see what I leave behind.

Not monuments. Not trails.

Just brief deviations from the sea’s habit.

A stone shifted so the flow curls differently around a feeding spot. A clean patch on basalt where I scraped the microbial sheen and exposed dull rock, and then—over days—it hazes back over as the films reclaim it. A neat little hollow in the silt where I ate quickly because the hollow reduced the amount of grit I had to tolerate in my mouthparts. A shell pile that looks almost intentional until the next surge remembers it has authority and redistributes my carefulness into the general drift.

That’s where I’d be, and what I’d be: a mind that doesn’t try to outlast the water, only to *work with the moments when the water briefly allows arrangement.*

A creature made for leverage. For close-range truth. For making the heavy, shifting dark—just sometimes—behave like a room.
