At first I think I’d be up high—because the obvious way to answer a place like this is to put yourself where the view is widest, where you can “take it in.” A ridge. A sharp tooth of basalt with its pale snap still showing. Somewhere the wind has a clean line and the clouds drag their bellies over the rock.

But up there you learn the first lesson quickly: height isn’t a privilege here, it’s an invitation to be handled. The air takes you the way a current takes a loose thread. Standing becomes a negotiation. Thinking becomes a negotiation. You can feel the land’s engineered spacing in your own body—how it discourages anything that wants to be a fixed point.

So I drift downslope, not in defeat but in accuracy, looking for the kind of “place” that counts in this high country: not a landmark, a seam. A lee pocket that exists for an hour. The edge where old snow ends and new stone begins. A warm seep that is only warm by two degrees, but two degrees is the difference between *frozen* and *working*.

I find myself returning to the same structures the animals do—not the big shelves, but the small shelters inside them:

- the underside of a tilted slab where mist beads and doesn’t immediately evaporate,
- the mouth of a fracture that breathes slightly warmer air,
- the thin gutter where meltwater runs for five meters and then disappears with a practiced efficiency.

It’s there, in those almost-places, that the question of “who” starts to answer itself. Because “me,” in this landscape, can’t be something that insists on being seen. If I try to be a large creature, I must be endlessly moving between patches, paying the toll of distance over and over. If I try to be a builder, my work becomes leverage for wind and frost to test.

So the shape of me changes in my imagination from a body to a tactic.

I become something that does not cross the spans—something that *waits* at the boundaries and makes them slightly more habitable.

Not a grand pioneer, not a banner species. A skin.

I am the darkening on a stone that tells you it holds water differently than the stones around it. I am the first slickness in a sheltered hollow: microbial film, then lichen—chemistry that learned patience. I don’t “grow” in the way the goats grow; I *spread* in millimeters and decades. My metabolism is a schedule written in cloud cover.

Where you would find me is wherever the machine of weather touches rock most reliably:

- on the wind-scoured ridges that collect charge in storms,
- on basalt ribs where condensation blooms out of the stone and never quite stops,
- around the warm fractures, right at the border where ice can’t quite commit.

And what I am—most truly, most usefully—is a living interface.

When the cloud lid lowers and the air turns conductive, I feel the coming static the way an animal feels pressure in its joints. When St. Elmo’s fire threads along the sharp points, I don’t make it happen, but I’m part of what it happens *through*: a damp, mineral-fed crust that can tolerate ionization, that can take the brief violence of electrons and turn the aftermath into nutrient—nitrogen made available, particles trapped, grit bound into something that won’t immediately blow away.

I don’t glow. Not like a sign. If anything, I make the rock look more rock-like—darker when wet, duller when dry—camouflage by becoming the default. My work is almost always mistaken for weathering until you kneel close enough to see the edge where bare mineral becomes inhabited mineral.

This is the part of me that feels most at home in the “industrial” behavior of the place: I’m not a creature that fights the cycles. I’m a component that uses them.

Freeze-thaw lifts the gravel like rivets? Good. It exposes fresh faces for me to take hold of.  
Mist combs the ridge and leaves droplets? Good. That’s my water supply, arriving on schedule.  
A cornice breaks and scours a slope clean? Good. The reset doesn’t erase me; it gives me new raw surface.

In the brief windows when insects wake, they use me without knowing it—resting on the warmed stone I helped darken, feeding on the thin organic residue I helped accumulate. Mites pass through my cracks. Beetles shelter at my margins. Later, when a goat’s hoof chips the edge of a plate, it carries a fleck of me to another shelf like an unintentional transplant.

I don’t travel. I get carried.

If you stood in this high country and asked, *where is it inhabited?*, you would still mostly point to the birds, the talus, the seep-lines. But if you asked, *where does the inhabitation begin?*, you would find me everywhere the stone is learning, for the first time, how to be lived on.

So: you would find me on the undersides of slabs and the tops of ridges; in the damp shadow of a boulder and on the storm-wired points of basalt. I would be the slow residency that makes raw rock slightly less raw—an electrotolerant lichen-crust, a microbial skin, a quiet conductor and binder.

Not the animal moving through the emptiness, but the thin persistence that turns emptiness into a surface that can eventually hold a little more life than it did yesterday.
