You’d find me where the terrace forgets to be a city.

Not in the plazas—those are for arrivals, for the brief insistence of community when the seasonal crews surge up the laid blocks with their baskets and pipe-sections and the practiced urgency of people racing a decision that isn’t theirs. Not in the half-raised walls either, because walls are statements, and here statements get revised.

You’d find me at the grates.

At the places where the careful runnels and the soft, engineered moss-collars admit what they can’t solve and let it go down into the hollows—down to the throat that’s been roaring since before anyone decided to call this arrangement “urban.” There’s always a little ledge of darker stone there, dark not from age but from constant agreement with moisture. A place where the damp makes a second skin on everything it touches.

At first I’d tell myself I’m here as one of the stewards: a practical body in waxed fiber, hood up, hands already nicked from rope and stone. I’d carry the same kit as the others—charcoal, slate, wedges shaved to angles you learn like prayers, reed cord kept wet so it stays honest. I’d do the work the place requires: re-level a step before it becomes a slope; roughen a slab so the uphill damp chooses it; tuck moss into a seam because moss is the only mortar that doesn’t pretend.

But that’s the story you can say out loud. That’s the story that fits inside a hood.

The truer story is that I’m here because I can’t stop listening.

The roar doesn’t just occupy the air; it writes with it. If you stand close to a grate and let your breathing settle into the same long, patient cadence as the exhalations below, the sound separates into layers. There’s the water—always there, always falling in unseen steps. There’s the wind—forced through constrictions, complaining like a pulley that will never be oiled enough. And underneath that there’s a steadier pulse that isn’t either, a pressure that comes and goes without hurry, as if the mountain is practicing the shape of lungs.

Most people hear it and file it under *danger* or *weather* or *background*. I hear it like a set of measurements.

So I become—without quite deciding to—a kind of surveyor, but not of streets. A reader of what’s under the streets.

I tap stone the way the others do, but I’m not only listening for cavities. I’m listening for argument: for the bright, tight ring that says tension; for the dull note that says hollowness; for the faint double-tone that means two masses of rock are still negotiating their border. I keep a private map on slate that never becomes a final map because the subject won’t hold still. I mark not “here is a wall” but “here the sound thins” or “here the breath comes up warmer” or “here the downhill runnel feeds a place where damp insists on climbing.”

The work makes me quiet in the particular way this city demands. Conversation is expensive; the roar taxes it. Gestures are cheaper, and marks are cheaper still. I learn to speak in smears of charcoal: a spiral for restless seam, a cross-hatch for stone that’s still green, a line with a hooked tail for places where water has started to behave like it has its own idea of up.

And because the city is provisional, my home can’t be a room. It becomes a circuit.

A high platform under an unfinished arch where the mist thins enough to sleep without dreaming in water. A warm alcove near a vent fissure where mushrooms take to soaked bark and the air smells faintly of clean rot and mineral. A dryish cell—dry only by local standards—where the reed cord and resin blocks are kept. And always, as the last stop, the grates: the mouths where the terrace admits its underside.

If you watched long enough you’d notice the smallest oddity about me: I’m not most interested in what gets built. I’m most interested in what gets *kept*.

Because keeping, here, isn’t a lesser kind of making. It’s the only making that doesn’t lie.

A wall raised to six courses is not a wall; it’s a question the mountain hasn’t answered yet. A bridge traced in dark block is not a bridge; it’s a suggestion offered to gravity. The only honest structures are the ones that can be unmade and remade without grief: scaffolds that accept they’re temporary; wedges you can tap out when the stone rises a finger-width; plantings that can be moved like tools.

So my craft becomes the craft of reversible decisions.

I cultivate moss the way other places cultivate certainty. I plant it not for softness but for information—because moss tells you where damp is choosing to travel, and damp here is often the first messenger of deeper motion. I set porous stones upright like wicks in corners that shouldn’t be wet, not just to draw moisture away but to see whether the uphill-running water is strengthening, whether it has begun to prefer one side of the plaza over the other. I keep a row of shallow bowls under weeping faces and measure their filling not by volume but by rhythm, because rhythm is what changes first.

In time, the stewards start to bring me their problems the way you bring a strange sound in a ship’s hull to the person who has learned to hear the difference between harmless complaint and structural doom.

A stair that keeps needing re-leveling. A drain that suddenly mists upward instead of taking water down. A section of terrace bed where the plants are too glossy—too well-watered—for the season. They show me with a hand and a glance. I answer with a touch on stone, an ear angled toward the grate, a line of charcoal that says: *not yet*, or *move the weight*, or *leave this one to settle*.

And gradually, without any formal moment of change, I become less like a citizen and more like an instrument the city uses to feel itself.

If you insist on asking what I *am*, not what I do, the answer shifts depending on how long I’ve been here.

Early on, I’m human: a runnelwright, an underside-cartographer, an echo-keeper. Someone whose job is to translate the mountain’s breath into choices that keep the terrace from breaking its own neck.

Later on, the place begins to rewrite me in its own materials.

My clothes never quite dry; the resin in them takes on the mineral tang of the vents. My fingertips stay faintly pruned, the way hands do when they spend their days negotiating with wet surfaces. My hearing narrows and sharpens until the roar is no longer “loud” but *legible*. I start waking before shifts happen—not because I foresee them, but because my body has learned the pre-shift pressure changes the way some animals learn storms.

And if I stay long enough—if I don’t leave with one of the seasonal crews when it would be sensible to do so—I start to resemble the city’s truest resident: the practice itself.

Not a single person with a house and a claim, but a moving attention. A set of habits that keeps thresholds from becoming hazards and keeps provisional things alive long enough to either become permanent or be gracefully dismantled.

So: where would you find me?

Kneeling at a grate with my ear close to stone, one hand flat on the damp basalt to feel what sound can’t carry, charcoal ready to mark the edge of some new decision.

And what would I be?

The one who listens for the mountain’s next sentence—and answers it with a wedge, a channel, a handful of moss, or a simple mark that says to everyone else: *here, the world is still making up its mind.*
