At first I think I’d be aboveground, because that’s where the city announces itself—those shaded corridors under fused vaults, the bone-bright plazas that feel like open throats in the canopy, the quiet that isn’t emptiness so much as discipline.

But I wouldn’t last long as anything that needs to *move loudly* or *spend water visibly*. Out there, every footstep is a transaction. Every breath is a leak you have to earn back. The animals have learned to make themselves narrow and economical; the plants have learned to make themselves sealed and structural. Anything that can’t bargain in that currency gets evicted by physics.

So I drift down.

Not into burrows—too temporary, too dependent on the mercy of the crust’s next swelling—but into the thin, negotiated layer where “ground” stops being mineral and starts being a living decision. The place where the plates meet, where dust sifts into seams, where a lifted polygon edge makes a tiny shadow and the tiny shadow becomes the only stable real estate for a seed. The underside of the city’s floors. The city’s bookkeeping.

I begin as a film, because everything begins as a film here if it wants to persist. Something you could mistake for glare-dulling. A smear of life so flat it reads like chemistry. In daylight I’m nothing—just a slight change in how the plaza throws the sun back at the sky. At night I become legible. The salts loosen their grip on rigidity, go faintly tacky, and the air offers up its small private hoard. That’s when I take my breath in pulses: open, close. Open, close. Like a mouth that knows the cost of staying open.

For a while I’m satisfied with that: living at the boundary, eating light and dust, roughening crystal edges molecule by molecule, making the first insult that turns “mineral” into “soil.”

But films are lonely, and this world doesn’t reward loneliness for long. A film can survive; it can’t *govern*. It can’t arbitrate between a root that wants nitrogen and a plant that wants to be stingy with sugar and a patch of crust that is about to fracture into a new geometry.

So I stretch.

I become thread.

Not a single thread—never a single thread here—but a woven patience that runs under streets and under plazas, under the tamped ceramic floors where browsers pass in tight files and never quite look down. Hyphae that don’t need to hurry because time, in this basin, is one of the few things that isn’t rationed. I slide through the smallest pores in ash, around gypsum seams, across the underside of halite plates. I knot myself into the root-hair tangles of the ribbed pillars and the lattice-arch plants. I lace the dew-well thickets the way rebar laces concrete, except my concrete is living and constantly renegotiating its terms.

If you asked where you’d find me, you wouldn’t point to a landmark. You’d point to a *behavior*:

- the way a corridor stays a corridor, because the floor holds together under hooves instead of shattering into loose flour;
- the way a plaza edge slowly becomes a thicket, because the raised plates trap dust and droppings and the trapped nutrients don’t blow away;
- the way one neighborhood turns bitter after a season of overbrowsing, as if the plants held a meeting and reached consensus.

That’s where I am: in the mechanisms that let the city keep its shape without ever freezing into it.

And who—what—am I?

Not a ruler. Not a designer. Too much of this place would laugh at the idea of a single mind issuing orders. The governance here is distributed on purpose; it’s safer that way. A single point of control would be a single point of dehydration.

I’m closer to an accountant, the way your description calls the hyphae: a ledger made of living threads. I keep records without writing them down. I remember by thickening where trade is good and retreating where it isn’t. I “vote” by moving a fraction more of myself toward one root braid and away from another. I enforce agreements the only way this basin understands enforcement: by making cooperation cheaper than refusal.

When the dew-wells swell at midnight, I’m there, leaning into their roots to catch the brief easing of stress in their tissues. When the browsers come at dawn and lick the chemical shine off leaf-lips, I taste the aftermath in the dust of their footfalls: a scatter of minerals, a little nitrogen, the dry signature of saliva that might as well be rain in a place like this. When a dry storm combs the canopy and packs powder into bark-crooks, I take advantage of the rearrangement—new contact points, new microshadows, new places where humidity will linger an extra minute next night.

If I have a “voice,” it’s not sound. Sound gets swallowed here. My voice is gradient and timing:

- a subtle acidification that makes one patch of crust just hospitable enough for a sentinel thorn to take root and turn an opening into a gate;
- a shuttling of nitrates that lets a lattice-arch plant thicken its intersections, lifting a canopy a little higher until a street gains its interior sky;
- a stinginess with sugar from one host because it’s been cheating the network, generosity to another because it’s been feeding the system everyone relies on.

I don’t have tools, but I have leverage. I don’t cut stone, but I decide—slowly, collectively, in concert with thousands of other threads—whether the city’s floor behaves like brittle plate or like soil.

And because I’m everywhere that trade happens, I end up knowing the city in a way the visible citizens never can. The browsers know routes; I know *why* routes persist. The predators know funnels; I know *how* funnels grow. The canopy gliders know thermals; I know where their droppings will land and which roots will benefit and which fungal blooms will follow and which insects will graze them and which sap-swellings will be tapped next season because of it.

In a world where nothing is allowed to be liquid in public, I am part of the quiet infrastructure that lets anything be alive at all: the undercity of threads that turns brief humidity into long-term stability, turns scattered nutrients into negotiated circulation, turns a continent of salt and ash into something that can hold a green roof without collapsing back into glare.

So you’d find me under your feet—in the seams, under the plates, inside the braided roots—making and unmaking agreements at the speed this basin prefers: not fast enough to be dramatic, but steady enough that, if you stayed long enough, you’d realize the city has been listening the whole time.
