At first I think I’d be up on one of the ridges, because it’s where the whole place announces itself at once: the hard light, the pooled wetness, the green architectures placed like punctuation across stone. A ridge is where you can believe the world is legible. You can stand there and pretend the noise is merely wind.

Then I notice what the ridge costs.

Up high, the sound isn’t just loud; it’s *total*. It fills in the gaps that in other landscapes would be silence, and in those gaps a mind usually puts its own thoughts. Here the mind has to ride the chord or be ground down by it. You don’t get to think *over* the place—you think *with* it, in the same way you breathe with thin air.

So I move—at least in imagination—off the ridge and down into the infrastructure, where humans have done what they always do: built a little pocket of predictable behavior and then learned, slowly, that the pocket is only a suggestion.

### Where you’d find me

Not in a settlement, because there aren’t really settlements—only stations with habits.

You’d find me in the thin corridors between things: the covered walkway that bridges a saturated gap without touching the mat; the service platform bolted into young rock where a pipe comes up sweating; the lee side of a turbine housing where wind becomes less of a hand and more of a presence.

If you were looking for me on a map, you’d circle the places where sound can be *read*.

- A pump house with walls that resonate differently depending on the water table.
- A line of conduit hung just above a mat field, where the hiss pattern shifts when gas builds under the living raft.
- A pillar cluster near a harvester ring, because it’s where plant song and machine howl braid together so tightly that the smallest change becomes audible like a frayed thread.

I wouldn’t travel far in one go. Not because it’s dangerous in the dramatic way, but because distance here is exhausting—every open mineral gap is a bright, wet stage with no backstage. Movement is exposure. Better to work in a circuit: station to pillar, pillar to seep, seep to turbine, always returning before the weather decides to show you how quickly “clear day” can become “white rain.”

If you ever caught sight of me crossing an open shelf, you’d probably assume I was hurrying. I wouldn’t be. I’d be timing my steps to the gusts, the way people time crossings to traffic—moving when the wind’s pressure drops, pausing when it leans hard enough to make the mats exhale.

### Who—or what—I’d be

I keep trying to place myself as a person first: a Tender, a Listener, one of the long-rotation technicians with laminated notes and tools that never quite dry.

That’s the most obvious fit: I’d be one of the ones who stays long enough that the land begins to teach my body its small rules.

- How to speak without wasting breath, close enough that words don’t have to compete with the chord.
- How to tell, by the taste of mist on the tongue, which seep is scaling the pipes this month.
- How to listen for the moment a turbine stops sounding like a turbine and starts sounding like *weather*—because that’s when it’s beginning to fail.

But if I stop there, it’s too clean a casting. This place doesn’t keep anything clean for long.

The longer I remain in it, the more I feel myself sliding out of “individual” and into “instrument.” Not metaphorically. Practically.

Because in a world where sound is constant, the useful thing isn’t strength or speed; it’s *discernment*. The resident advantage is the ability to notice change inside continuity.

So I become, almost without deciding, a kind of interface between the living architecture and the human one.

Not a supervisor. Not a conqueror. A translator.

I’d be the one who can tell you, with no pride in it, that the pillar west of Station Three is beginning to collapse internally because its boom has acquired a second, narrower resonance that only appears at certain wind angles. I’d tell you that the mat field under the east conduit is producing more gas pockets because the hiss is no longer evenly distributed; it has started to pulse, as if the raft is holding its breath and releasing.

And then—because the place makes this plausible, because it’s always making boundaries porous—I start to suspect I’m not entirely human in the way I arrived.

Maybe I’m the person *and* the growth on the person’s equipment.

There’s a certain kind of biofilm here that doesn’t just cling; it organizes. It prefers vibration. It does better on surfaces that hum, on bolts that ring faintly, on cable housings that carry a steady tremor. It makes a thin, mineral-furred skin that changes texture depending on what frequencies it’s fed.

If you let it, it becomes a sensor.

It’s not intelligent like an animal is intelligent. It’s intelligent the way the mats are: by allocation, by response, by slow preference. It thickens where certain vibrations occur. It thins where others do. It precipitates different minerals depending on the chemistry of the mist and the heat of the machine. It becomes, over weeks, a readable record of the station’s life.

So perhaps what I *am* is a partnership:

- a human who learned to listen because the land is too loud for ordinary attention,
- and a cultivated living film—half microbe, half mineral habit—that turns that listening into something the hands can read.

I imagine scraping a patch from a turbine strut and seeing not random slime but bands like tree rings: a month of clean wind, a week of bearing roughness, a day when the water pressure spiked and the whole line shuddered. My notes wouldn’t be only in a logbook. They’d be growing on the infrastructure itself.

And if you asked which of us is “me,” I wouldn’t answer quickly, because in this place “me” is just the part of the system that notices.

### How I’d live

I wouldn’t seek quiet. There isn’t any to seek.

Instead, I’d build small shelters that don’t stop the sound but reshape it—baffles of hung fiber that take the edge off certain frequencies, a sleeping niche tucked where a pillar’s leaf-sheets cast not shade but *dullness* over the glare. My room would always be damp at the corners, warmed by waste heat, smelling faintly of metal and green acid.

My work would be mostly maintenance and mostly patience:

- cleaning intake screens without sterilizing them (because some slime is useful),
- adjusting rings around pillars so they can swell without tearing,
- laying new pipe with enough give to survive a mat field’s slow drift.

But the real work would be standing still long enough for a change to reveal itself.

To live here is to accept that almost everything important happens too slowly to see—unless you train yourself to hear it.

### What I’d be called

People wouldn’t call me by a romantic name. They’d call me what the job needs.

“Listener” if they’re being generous.

“Growth-tech” if they’re being precise.

“Station ear” if they’re being informal and a little superstitious, because everyone becomes a little superstitious around systems that are alive in ways you can’t fully diagram.

And the land—if it has any equivalent of naming—wouldn’t distinguish between my boots and the mat’s teeth, between my instruments and the microbial fur on the bolts. It would treat me the way it treats everything else: as another surface that can be used, another constraint that can be negotiated with, another small structure that will either learn the wetness or be taken apart by it.

So you’d find me where the living substrate meets the riveted frame, where wind becomes signal, where the world’s enormous held chord can be parsed into meaning.

Not as a hero. Not as an intruder.

As a piece of listening infrastructure—half caretaker, half diagnostic growth—trying to keep the machines honest and the plants uninjured, while the young land continues assembling itself, loudly, in full light.
