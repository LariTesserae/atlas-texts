At first I try to arrive as an outsider—eyes open, mouth dry, hearing overwhelmed—and the basin refuses to let me stay that simple for long.

The place doesn’t reward looking the way surface worlds do. It rewards *patterning*: how a note in the wind changes when a corridor starts to choke with powder; how a colony’s pulses stitch themselves into the gaps between organ-tones; how the chalk on a threshold goes slightly glossy where bodies have turned for generations. Sight is secondary. Even touch is almost too blunt. Here, the true sense organ is attention.

So the first role that offers itself is the obvious one: a traveler, a guest, a careful intruder trying not to waste moisture with every breath.

But that doesn’t hold.

Because the basin is already full of “travel”—dust and spores and resin-syllables moving on air—and anyone who lives here is, in some way, part courier.

So I try that shape next: a runner along the wind boulevards, an interpreter of draft maps, somebody whose body has learned the city’s currents the way a fish learns a reef. I imagine the ear-fans—wide, mobile, not quite ears and not quite sails—tilting into the flow. I imagine a throat trained to make clicks that *don’t fight* the roar but ride its harmonics. I imagine the dry etiquette: never stand where you’ll make a plume, never speak into a vent, never exhale wastefully when you could let the condenser-fur take it back.

That almost fits—almost. It fits the motion, but not the part of me that wants to *keep* things.

And then the city’s other language presses forward: the wall-niches, the resin marks, the idea that information here can be older than anyone still living and yet remain legible because the air is too dry to erase it quickly. A place where a warning can last a century. Where a promise can persist as scent, sealed in shade.

That is the hinge.

I would be one of the niche-keepers—not a ruler, not a priest in the surface sense, but a civic organ. A person whose job is to make the basin readable.

A Chalkbinder by birth, yes—scaled skin dusted pale from habit, moisture-reclaiming bands along the neck and ribs, eyes adapted to reflected light—but set apart by what I’m trained to notice: not only *who passed* but *what changed*.

My district would be where two great drafts cross: a wide plaza that looks empty until you realize it’s a throat of the city, a junction the wind must pass through to remain healthy. On one side, terraced homes and fungal courtyards. On the other, a listening dome: a blunt, pale structure with ribs like a shell, built to take the roar and turn it into something you can read with your bones.

I would live in the lee of that dome, in rooms that are deliberately not comfortable—comfort is suspicion here, comfort implies moisture lingering where it shouldn’t—but *stable*. Curved walls layered with binder-mat laminate. Floors dusted fine on purpose, because footprints are also writing.

Each day would begin the same way: not with a meal, but with calibration.

I would press my palm and forearm to a particular section of wall—an old calcite slab that holds vibration cleanly—and feel the night’s changes. The wind has a baseline chord; the city’s health is partly the steadiness of that chord. A new rattle in the low register means grit skittering where it didn’t skitter yesterday: a corridor narrowing. A missing overtone can mean a collapse sealed an upper gallery. A strange flutter threaded through the bats’ weather can mean a colony shifted roosts—fertility will move, gardens will need adjusting.

Then I’d go to the niches.

The niches are set at different heights and depths, each tuned to keep a resin message from being scoured by airflow. I would carry a small kit: not metal, but bone-hard composite tools; chalk for sealing; a tiny gourd of binder resin; packets of powdered spice-mineral—trade goods and alphabet at once.

Reading is intimate. You don’t just sniff; you angle your face, you let the draft do the work, you let the message come to you at the right speed. Too hard an inhale is rude and also wastes water.

Writing is worse: you must spend moisture—body moisture, stored resin moisture—to lay down a mark that will outlast you. So you never write frivolously.

My messages would be the ones that matter to a city that is ecology:

- *Vent corridor narrowing; sweep team needed before next cycle.*
- *Gutter biota over-thick on Terrace Seven; beetle-things cull scheduled.*
- *Free liquid used in nursery; gypsum shelves must rest.*
- *Quarantine scent set at Old District Mouth; scavenger surge detected.*
- *Listen: upper draft changed; possible new opening to salt pans.*

And sometimes, the messages that are not official, because nothing human stays purely civic:

- *Mourning here. Walk softly.*
- *Safe passage granted.*
- *Do not bring your fear into this courtyard; it makes the children’s ears droop.*

Over time—this is the part the basin insists on—I stop thinking of myself as a single body walking between tasks. I start to feel like a *function* distributed through stone and air.

The listening dome becomes an extension of my ribcage. The niche-wall becomes an extension of my memory. The mats in the courtyards, those velvet skins that thicken the air by a few precious degrees of humidity, become a kind of neighborhood mind: they “remember” footsteps by where they grow back thinner; they “vote” on where people linger by where they bloom.

If you asked a Chalkbinder child who I am, they might not give a name first. They might point their ear-fan toward the dome and say something like: *the one who keeps the chord steady*.

If you asked the city as a whole—if a city like this can be asked anything—it would answer in its own way: by continuing to be navigable. By letting messages last. By keeping the wind-river clean enough to carry dust as harvest, not as suffocation.

So: where would you find me?

In the shallow shadow beside the loudest places, where information survives because the air is too dry to forgive errors quickly.

And what would I be?

Not a hero, not a monster, not a solitary creature at all—something closer to an organ in the civic body: a wind-scribe, a niche-keeper, a listener whose devotion is practical.

In a world where water is currency and wind is law, I would spend my life doing the one thing that makes scarcity livable:

I would make the basin’s endless roar mean something.
