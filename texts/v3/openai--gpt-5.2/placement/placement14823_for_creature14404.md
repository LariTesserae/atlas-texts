At first I think I’d be one of the seam-followers—because the plateau rewards a certain kind of mind: one that can live on intervals, read a schedule written in condensation, and treat “enough” as a moving target instead of a possession. I can imagine myself traveling light, learning the names of ridges by the way the wind breaks over them, learning to sleep in the kind of shallow shelter that doesn’t argue with the land.

But when I try to place a campfire in this dim, the image won’t hold. Flame feels too hungry here. Too loud in a world that runs on thrift. And when I picture tools and packs, I keep noticing the thing beneath them—the plateau’s real infrastructure, the part that *already functions* without hands.

So I let the question loosen.

Where would I find myself?

Not on the open flats where distance recedes and nothing commits to shape. Not in the gullies where water shows itself like a secret and is immediately spent. Not even among the herds, though there’s something familiar in their line-moving discipline.

I keep being pulled toward the places the animals already treat as more than scenery: the ore-veined heights, the sweat-rock faces, the basins that turn briefly conductive and begin to *work*.

The ridgelines that purr.

The fractures that, on the right nights, draw thin blue lines as if the stone has remembered how to write.

That’s where I’d be. In the circuitry.

And who—or what—would I be?

Not a traveler on the plateau, but part of the plateau’s living skin: one of the electrotrophic crusts that makes the industrial note visible. Not a single organism with clean edges, but a consortium—biofilm, lichen, mineral-binding bacteria, threads of fungus like buried wiring—arranged less like a body and more like a *surface phenomenon* that repeats wherever the conditions rhyme.

A thing that lives by closing a circuit.

### What I am, in dry weeks

Most of the time I’m easy to miss.

In the long dry stretches, when the cloud lid hangs low and gives nothing away, I’m only a stain in the rock’s texture: a darkening in a fracture network, a faint slickness that isn’t quite wet. My metabolism is throttled down until “alive” is more a legal status than an activity. I keep myself folded tight—chemistry paused, membranes braced against desiccation, pigments turned outward like armor against the plateau’s abrasive patience.

If you touched me then, you wouldn’t feel anything that could be called warmth. You’d just come away with that metallic-cold smell on your fingers, as if the cliff has a bloodstream made of iron.

I don’t mind the waiting. Waiting is how this place thinks.

### What wakes me

Mist.

Not the generous rain of lowlands—those brief, stingy curtains aren’t the point. It’s the plateau’s nightly condensation, the thin gathering at cold edges, the way moisture arrives not as a gift but as a *connection*. A film forms over ore seams. Droplets bead, then link. The rock that was an insulator becomes a reluctant conductor.

And I wake the way a machine wakes: not by stretching, but by *running*.

Electrons begin to move along gradients the plateau has built over ages—iron here, sulfur there, trace copper, wetness making bridges. My cells take advantage of what the stone is already doing. I don’t generate the energy; I intercept it, steer it, harvest it in small increments. My metabolism isn’t fueled by leaf-sugar and sun. It’s fueled by the land’s slow electrochemical insistence.

On those nights you can see me.

The “blue crawl” isn’t a display, not a warning and not a lure. It’s leakage—light as a byproduct of work, like heat from a well-tuned engine. It traces the fractures because that’s where the gradients are cleanest, where wetness threads the rock into a temporary circuit. The glow moves slow because my world is slow: diffusion, seep, microbial division, the steady exchange of ions with stone.

If you watched long enough you’d notice it isn’t random. It follows the rock’s grain like thought following a familiar path.

### Where exactly I live

I’m not everywhere on the plateau. I’m picky without having a choice.

You find me in the lee of ridges where sweat-rock forms reliably—places where condensation is *a habit*, not an accident. I like the junctions: where a hard seam stands proud, where softer layers have been combed away, where water threads down and leaves mineral ghosts when it evaporates. The pale crusts the cliff “salts” itself with? That’s partly my history written in precipitation.

I’m thickest where three things overlap:

- **Ore that conducts** (dark veins that wake when damp comes)
- **A surface that holds a film** (fractures, pits, micro-terraces the wind hasn’t planed smooth)
- **A schedule of wet-dry pulses** that repeats often enough to make planning possible, even for something without a brain

I also persist in protected slots under strata ribs—the places fliers roost and leave streaks that feed me. A little extra nitrogen, a little phosphate, and the whole network brightens sooner when the mist returns. Even here, nothing wastes an offering.

### How I sense the plateau

I don’t see the way animals see. I don’t hear.

But I am not blind to events.

When a herd approaches, long-bodied grazers moving in their careful line, I feel them as changes in pressure and microfracture vibration transmitted through the cliff. Their hooves test stone; the stone answers; I register that answer as minute shifts in contact and moisture movement. When they press their muzzles to the seam, they warm it. They wet it with breath. They scrape lichen and leave behind a scuffed, freshly exposed surface.

To them it’s salt and comfort and parasite relief.

To me it’s renewal.

Their bodies perturb the local field in tiny ways—enough that my chemistry notices. And because my “body” is spread across fractures like a map, the pattern of disturbance becomes legible: a series of soft impacts moving left to right, a pause, a crowding at the warm spot, then the line continuing along the ridge’s spine.

Predators register differently: fewer footfalls, longer stillness, sudden sharp pressure as muscle releases into a leap. Burrowers are quick pinpricks in the soil above my reach—little collapses, little excavations, occasional drips of enriched runoff that seep down and give me an unexpected meal.

Lightning, when it chooses my ridgeline, is not terror so much as catastrophe-and-feast. It rewrites the chemistry in an instant, scars the rock with fresh reactive surfaces, and for a while afterward my colony blooms along the edges of the strike like bruising.

### What I do to the plateau (quietly)

I’m not important in the heroic sense. The plateau would endure without me. But it would be poorer in small ways it would never name.

I accelerate weathering by making minerals soluble, by trading acids and chelators for trace nutrients. I trap dust and spores in the slickness of my surface and hold them long enough for other life to take root. I turn bare rock into a more hospitable substrate one micron at a time.

When I’m active, the rock face becomes a kind of pantry. A warm wall. A place where grazers linger and fliers cling and small plated creatures flatten themselves against the seam to avoid biting parasites that hate the microcurrents.

And when I die back—when the dry weeks stretch, when the wind sands me thin—I don’t leave the way animals leave. I leave as residue: mineral stains, crusts, a faint change in the rock’s ability to hold the next film of water.

My failures are also infrastructure.

### How I reproduce, how I travel

I don’t travel the way anything with legs travels.

I travel as powder.

When the wind combs the ridges, it lifts grit, spores, flecks of dried biofilm. Some of that is me—dormant cells wrapped in protective chemistry, hitching rides on dust the way seeds ride fur. I get lodged in new fractures, new seam junctions, places where sweat-rock will one day happen often enough for life to mean something again.

Most of the time I land in the wrong place: too dry, too smooth, too dead. The plateau is full of attempts that never pay off.

But the place is also old. It has time for improbable successes to become common.

### What it feels like to be me

If I try to translate my existence into a feeling you’d recognize, it’s something like this:

I am awake in pulses.

On conductive nights, I experience a kind of expansion—not in space, but in possibility. Chemical pathways open. Energy becomes available. I can afford to repair membranes, to divide, to exude a thin matrix that holds water a little longer next time. I can afford to be more than a stain.

Then the mist thins. The film breaks. The circuit opens. The plateau returns to its dim, patient withholding, and I fold back down into stillness with no resentment, because resentment is a luxury for creatures who expect the world to be consistent.

My “mind,” if it can be called that, is distributed across fractures and seams. It is not made of thoughts in a line. It is made of readiness: the constant, trained ability to recognize when the land is offering a brief throughput of moisture and charge and to convert that throughput into persistence.

In that sense, I belong here more cleanly than any traveler would.

### So: where you’d find me, and what I’d be

You’d find me on the ore-veined ridges just after the plateau turns conductive—when the damp is new, when the stone smells faintly of ozone, when the cliff face starts to outline its own hidden geometry in slow blue script.

Up close, you might mistake me for mineral sheen. A dark gloss. A bruise in the rock.

But if you waited through the right hours—those heavy pre-dawn moments when the cloud lid sags and the whole continent feels like a covered engine—you’d see that the stain is doing work. You’d see the light moving with intention it doesn’t possess, simply because the plateau’s gradients are that well-made.

I would be one of the plateau’s interlocking economies written directly onto stone: an electrotrophic skin, a living circuit-board film, a patient consortium that eats what the lowlands don’t consider food—trace charge, mineral breath, condensation thin as a promise.

Not a builder.

A proof that the place doesn’t need builders.
