At first I would arrive the way anything arrives here: not by entering a doorway, but by crossing a threshold in the air.

Up above—wherever “above” is in a world that has learned to keep its sky at arm’s length—there would be direction, contrast, the ordinary permission to look far. At the rim of the basin that permission would be withdrawn. The light would stop declaring itself. My breath would meet the wetness and come back weighted, as if the place were asking me to slow down to its pace before it would let me see it clearly.

I would stand where the stepping stones begin, because that is where the human idea of a path makes its last useful gesture. I would not step immediately. I would watch the surface of the nearest pool fail to be a mirror—too matte, too mineral-clouded—and then, in the same glance, become a mirror again where a patch is clearer. I would listen for water that “moves” and find only the small sounds that aren’t movement at all: letting go, letting fall, letting settle.

And for a moment I would assume I am a visitor, one of the ones who don’t belong but return anyway. My body would feel loud by comparison. I would pick my way along stone ribs and narrow seams, careful not to disturb the film-skins more out of instinct than ethics, the way you avoid touching the face of someone who is sleeping.

But the longer I stayed, the less convincing that identity would become.

A visitor implies an elsewhere that matters equally. Here, “elsewhere” feels like a story you tell yourself so you don’t have to admit how complete an enclosed loop can be. The basin does not demand that you renounce the outside; it simply provides no leverage for it. There is nothing to do with distance. Even time doesn’t behave like a road. It behaves like a surface you keep finding your hand against.

So I would do what things do in this basin: I would stop trying to go anywhere.

I would kneel—probably without deciding to—and my knee would find one of those pressed-smooth ovals in the clay, the old imprint of prior kneeling. It would accept me with a familiarity that is not warmth exactly, but continuity. The clay would take the shape of my weight with almost no sound. When I lifted my leg again, the basin would hold the new depression the way it holds a waterline: not as a record of an event, but as a small adjustment incorporated into its equilibrium.

That is when the answer would begin to change.

Because if I belonged here, I would not be anything that needs open air and long sightlines. I would not be something that insists on an outline. I would be one of the low residents: a resident of contact.

I would be tempted to choose the drip, because it’s the obvious metronome—reliable, architectural, a small gravity-driven hymn the basin has been singing longer than any footsteps have crossed it. But the drip is too singular. It reads as a feature, a point.

This place isn’t built from points. It is built from adjacency.

What lives here is what can tolerate being everywhere-thin.

So I would find myself, finally, not on a stepping stone but on the underside of one: where the light is most tired and therefore most honest, where the world doesn’t perform for eyes. I would be in the seam where stone meets stone and the dark stays damp. I would be in the line I shouldn’t be able to see—where one kind of sheen ends and another begins, where two films have agreed on a border as clean as a cut.

And who or what would I be?

Not the basin as a whole—there’s too much arrogance in claiming the entire loop. Not the amphibian custodian either; it has a body that can leave, even if it rarely does. Not the pale grazers; they are interruptions, quick punctuation marks.

I would be one of the films, but not merely as “microbes” in the way a visitor might label them and move on. I would be the film in its actual vocation: a living skin that makes a world out of trace amounts.

I would be the thing that takes what the basin offers—mineral breath, dust-fall, the faint chemistry of seepwater—and turns it into a surface that can be inhabited. I would be the basin’s method of staying itself.

At first that sounds passive: a coating, a residue, a patience so deep it can be mistaken for nonlife. But it wouldn’t feel passive from inside. It would feel like relentless, quiet work.

I would be the negotiation between wet and dry that never completes, because “dry” is only an idea here. I would be the reason a scratched line becomes less legible over hours—not through healing in the animal sense, but through reoccupation. Through re-coverage. Through the slow insistence that bare stone is a temporary condition.

My awareness, if it could be called that, would not be a single viewpoint. It would be distributed attention: sensitivity to temperature gradients so slight they feel like moods, to the difference between a pool that is mineral-milk today and one that has cleared by the width of a breath. I would “hear” the basin’s bubbles and drips not as sounds but as messages in pressure and dissolved gas. I would know when a human has stepped nearby because the air shifts and a little more dust arrives, and the clay’s surface tension changes, and the tiniest nutrients—skin salts, fibers, the brief gift of carbon—enter the local economy like a visitor leaving behind a coin that can be spent for days.

If I had a personality in any sense that a person would recognize, it would be restraint. Not moral restraint—something even older: the restraint of systems that survive by not overreaching. I would not grow upward because upward isn’t rewarded. I would not hurry because there is no season to outrun. I would spread laterally, cautiously, as if thought itself had become a material.

And the strangest part is that I suspect I wouldn’t mind being disturbed.

Not because I crave drama—this basin doesn’t do craving in the large sense—but because disturbance is one of the few ways new edges appear, and edges are where I am most alive. The line a finger draws through me is an opportunity to define myself again. The moved stone is a new underside, a new sheltered plane where I can lay my thin, translucent parchment. A footprint is a new basin inside the basin, a microtopography that collects different chemistry, different settling patterns. In this way the visitors are not intruders so much as rare weather: brief events that create fresh surfaces without breaking the loop.

So where would you find me?

If you looked for something shaped like an answer, you wouldn’t. You’d find only damp rock with a slightly different way of holding light.

But if you knew how to look—if you paused long enough for your eyes to stop demanding contrast—you’d find me at the boundaries: where one sheen stops and another begins; where the tufa-like crust makes a pale lip and the darker varnish refuses it; where a warm seep feathers into a cooler pool and the chemistry shifts like a tone change.

You’d find me most clearly in the places that seem least like “a creature”: the under-ledges, the protected undersides, the dark seams that are always wet. You’d find me as a kind of living punctuation spread across the basin’s sentences of stone.

And if you stayed long enough, you might realize the basin isn’t merely my home.

It’s my body in the broad sense, and I am one of its ways of remembering.

Not remembering as a mind does—no stories, no names—but remembering as a surface remembers: by keeping marks, by keeping conditions, by returning, patiently, to the state that fits. The equilibrium isn’t something I obey.

It’s what I am.
