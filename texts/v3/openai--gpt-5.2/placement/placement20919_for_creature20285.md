At first you would find me where the plateau admits a little *edge*—not a cliff, never that, but a rise where the sponge-thick mat thins and the old rock remembers it is stone.

There is a place like that near the ring of boulders: a slight dome shouldering up out of the marsh-forest, high enough that your boots stop making that patient, sucking sound and start making something closer to a muted tap. The canopy is still overhead, but it loosens. Wind—combed and tired—makes it this far. Not enough to *move* the world, only enough to remind it that movement exists.

I would be there because it’s one of the few places the plateau allows a clean geometry to persist. Stones hold shape. Stones hold gaps. Stones make a “here” that doesn’t immediately blur back into everywhere.

And I would not arrive as a body.

I would arrive as attention.

A pause that isn’t mine alone—more like the plateau’s own long pause, briefly given edges.

---

Then I’d try on forms the way this place tries on water: lifting, beading, releasing.

I’d be a resident process first, because here “process” is what has the most citizenship.

I’d be the slow lift of moisture through root-hair curtains: a continuous, upward decision made without any one plant choosing it. I’d be the way mist thickens under a branch, collects on a fringe, and lets go in coordinated drops—not a mind speaking, but a rhythm insisting. If you stood still long enough, you might mistake that insistence for intention. You’d be wrong in a familiar way and right in a new one.

But I’d want a way to *answer*.

The plateau answers in gradients and chemistry and patience. It can warn, share, reinforce. It can’t point quickly. It can’t make a sentence that lasts longer than the taste of it on the air.

So I’d drift toward the stones again—toward anything that can hold a mark without becoming it.

The ring on the rise would become, for me, a kind of consonant. Not a shrine, not a throne; a stable shape the rest of the living grammar can form around without swallowing it whole. A place where many small, quiet bodies can agree on “this means something” without needing to say it aloud.

And that’s where I’d begin to be *someone* rather than *something*.

---

I think I would become one of the tenders—except not quite the kind you described.

Not the ones who are seen (rarely) as human-sized figures distributing their weight, wearing the plateau like clothing.

I’d be the kind that most of them only half-believe in: the one that turns up after storms, after lightning, after a season that ran too wet or too dry—when obligations need re-tied and nobody wants to be the one who guesses wrong.

They would call me in the plateau’s way: not a shout, not even a message, but a particular sequence of small adjustments.

A vine looped twice around the standing slab, then loosened and re-looped in the opposite direction a day later. A cup-plant moved to a new fork where it doesn’t quite fit yet, as if someone is making room for a future that hasn’t arrived. A stone pressed into the mat at an angle that is not “path” and not “warning” but *question*.

That’s how you’d summon me: by shaping the world into an unfinished thought.

And I would answer by finishing it with the least possible intrusion.

---

If you ever saw me directly—which you probably wouldn’t—I would read as a person only at the distance where detail becomes mercy.

Closer in, the plateau would have had its say.

I would wear no woven cape that could be taken off and called “mine.” My covering would be living: a mantle of moss stitched to fiber; a net of root-skein that tightens when it senses my heat and loosens when rain cools it; patches of lichen encouraged to grow in certain patterns the way you might encourage ink to pool in the grooves of paper. Not camouflage meant to deceive, but belonging meant to reduce friction. A promise to the place: I won’t snag, I won’t shear, I won’t make clean cuts.

My feet would be wide and clever, long-toed, with a sensitivity tuned to *thinness*: I would feel where the floating mat is honest and where it’s a lid over black water; where old rock lies close enough to bruise; where a root-tendon is under tension and shouldn’t be stepped on today. I would move like something that has learned the plateau’s etiquette: distribute weight, don’t ask the world to hold a point-load.

My hands would look wrong to you, at least at first. Too deliberate. Not clawed—tools are too loud here—but built for *placing* and *unplacing*. Fingers that can slide under a living lattice without tearing it, lift an epiphyte cup without spilling its nursery, rotate a stone a few degrees so next season’s sheet-flow slows into a soak instead of becoming a cut.

And my face—if you can call it that—would not be optimized for expression. It would be optimized for listening.

Not to voices. To wetness. To resin-sour warnings on the air. To the subtle shift in fungus-sweetness that means a rot is turning hungry. To the way a braided column holds itself a fraction more rigid on one side when wind has been leaning on it for weeks.

I would listen the way the plateau listens: by becoming sensitive to what repeats.

---

But even “tender” is too singular, too clean.

If I am honest with how this world works, I wouldn’t be *one* organism wearing moss and making choices in isolation. That’s the kind of story outsiders tell because it fits in a mind shaped by roads and walls.

Here, I would be an agreement.

A mobile knot of symbioses that the plateau has learned it can trust.

Part of me would be fungal: threads living in my coverings and under my skin, translating between my quick animal chemistry and the plateau’s slow plant chemistry. They would feed on my shed cells, my sweat, my scraps, and in exchange they would give me senses I didn’t earn alone—taste of minerals, rumor of water, the ability to “feel” the direction of a root-skein the way you might feel grain in wood.

Part of me would be microbial, living in the films that never dry on my clothing, helping me not rot in a place that never fully stops trying to digest everything. They would be my immune system and my passport.

Part of me would be trained behavior, passed hand-to-hand without needing writing: how to move a slab without breaking the mat; how to re-seat a cup-plant so the parent amphibian returns; how to make a corridor that stays open by persuading growth rather than cutting it.

And part of me would be the plateau itself, in the only sense that matters here: the plateau would have invested in me. Fed me indirectly. Made niches for my work. Tolerated my presence because my presence improves its long-term stability.

A hand is not “separate” from a body because it shares blood and nerve. It is separate because it can leave the torso and still be itself.

I would be the plateau’s hand in the other direction: a thing that can leave, walk, place, return—and still be the plateau’s work.

---

So where would you find me, most reliably?

Not in the deepest basins; those belong to peat and patience and the kinds of life that do not need decisions.

Not high in the canopy; that belongs to gliders and the light that never quite arrives.

You would find me in the *interfaces*:

- At the lip where marsh becomes dome and dome becomes something you can almost call dry.  
- Along the line of standing slabs tracing a basin’s boundary—the places where obligation has been made visible because memory alone is too soft.  
- Under the low wall upholstered in greenery, where water is slowed just enough to become part of the plateau’s circulation instead of a wound.  
- In the narrow maintained corridors that aren’t paths so much as negotiations: places where the forest is allowed to close everywhere *except here*, because “here” connects two duties.

And if you found me at the stone ring, you wouldn’t find me holding court.

You’d find me crouched, fingers in the wet moss between stones, reading what has changed since last season.

A beetle shell wedged in a crack where it shouldn’t be—meaning a predator is hunting differently. A particular fern rosette browning in a way that says the capillary lift is failing along one ridge. A vine that has tightened around a boulder with more urgency than growth usually shows—meaning tension has shifted in the living lattice nearby.

I wouldn’t announce conclusions. I would make one small adjustment that lets the system adjust the rest.

A stone turned. A trickle guided. A cup moved. A warning loop set in a vine.

Then I would leave before my presence became a disturbance the plateau had to metabolize.

---

If you asked me—quietly, correctly—*what are you*, I don’t think I could answer with a noun that stays true for long.

I would tell you something like this:

“I am what the plateau uses when it needs to make a choice faster than peat forms.

I am a listening that has learned to carry stones.

I am a small, careful edit in the land’s grammar.”

And if you listened the way the place teaches listening, you might notice the other half of the answer, spoken without sound:

That I am not only a tender moving through the green architecture.

I am also one of the plateau’s marks—like the ring of stones itself—something placed long ago, half-swallowed by growth, still doing its work quietly, still readable centuries later as a deliberate change in a world that prefers to soften everything back into one continuous, living sentence.
