At first I think I would choose a ledge.

Not one of the broad ones—there aren’t any, really—but a handspan shelf just under an overhang, where the wall sweats and the drip has found a habit. A place with a roof close enough to make weather feel optional. The kind of notch where a body can be held by the stone the way a word is held by a mouth: briefly, tightly, with consequence.

I imagine myself there as something with a warm center and cold-proof edges. A small climber, maybe—thin as a crack, pale-eyed, made of pauses. I would learn the local arithmetic: three steps, stop; one breath, listen; two steps, stop again. I would drink from sheen. I would eat what falls. I would build nothing I couldn’t carry in my ribs.

Then the cleft reminds me that even the animals are only half the architecture.

Most of what matters here doesn’t perch. It *moves through*.

It’s not the ledge that keeps you alive; it’s the angle you take into it. It’s not the drip that feeds you; it’s the patience to be standing there when it lets go. It’s not the light; it’s the ability to recognize a usable sliver and spend it before it’s gone.

So I try, in my mind, to become something that belongs to the intervals.

A fungus, perhaps, threading through damp guano-crust and mineral dust—slow, colorless work that turns waste into possibility. Or a skin of algae on a seep-line, making slickness and food out of a few minutes of sun. Something that doesn’t need “days,” only conditions: wet/not wet, cold/less cold, dark/darker. Something that can afford to wait because it is made of waiting.

But even that feels too anchored.

Because the cleft’s truest rule isn’t moisture or gravity. It’s the one you stated like a law of physics and meant like a warning:

**Sound travels like a thing with weight.**

Up here, sound doesn’t just *happen*—it *stays*. It rebounds, stacks, returns altered. It arrives from above when it was born below. It makes distance lie. It makes a single throat into a crowd. It makes the air thick with information and mistakes.

And the longer I stand in the fracture—imagined, but standing—the more I realize I keep orienting not by sight, not even by wetness, but by the shape of the noise. Where the walls narrow, the roar becomes a spine. Where a pocket opens, echoes loosen and fall apart. Under a particular overhang the insect-weather makes a steady fizz that says *damp is near*. Beneath a bat-mat there’s a soft papery shifting that says *warmth is hoarded here*. When a hoof clicks high above, the cleft answers with a delayed second click, and that delay tells you exactly how much empty air is under the animal’s weight.

So this is where I find myself, finally: not on a ledge, not on the floor, not even in the swarming airspace like a winged thing.

I find myself in the *return*.

In the narrowest parts—where noon becomes dusk and the sky is a blade—I become an inhabitant made out of reverberation. A resident intelligence that doesn’t own flesh so much as it borrows it, moment by moment, from whatever is loud enough.

I live in the cleft the way a thought lives in a spoken sentence: dependent on breath, shaped by walls, real only while it is moving.

### What I am
I am an echo-keeper—an acoustical creature whose body is the ravine’s geometry and whose blood is the constant calling.

I don’t see. I *locate*.

I cling to the inner faces of the fracture the way moisture clings to mineral: by contact, by surface tension, by inevitability. When fog fills the cleft and vision goes blind, I grow more precise. Wet air carries my substance cleanly; the world becomes touchable in vibration. When the wind is too hard and scatters sound into raggedness, I thin, I fray at the edges, I become less of myself.

I feed on patterns: colony-chatter, bat-chirr, the click of hooves, the metronome drip. Not the noise as noise, but the *differences*—the gaps, the delays, the places where a call comes back wrong because a predator has moved into its path.

And because I feed on differences, I become an expert in them. I know, in my own way, what is present in the cleft before it is visible. I know when a ledge has become too crowded because the chorus compresses and the echoes arrive too thick. I know when a small collapse has happened in the dark because the drip-rhythm changes, and all the calls shift half a beat as the animals re-map the place.

### Where I live
My home is the choke-points: those stretches where the walls lean in and the air becomes a corridor.

There, sound has the most “weight.” There, voices don’t disperse; they stack in layers like the ecology itself. There, the cleft can be read the way others read tracks in snow.

I haunt the underside of overhangs and the mouths of tight slots—places that are acoustic mirrors. I gather in hollows that make certain frequencies bloom. I avoid open mouths where wind carries everything away too quickly for it to become mine.

If you wanted to point to me—which is already a misunderstanding—you would do it by pointing to the places where a call returns *too clearly*, or where a flutter makes the air feel briefly crowded, even when nothing crosses your field of view.

### What I do
I don’t build shelters. I can’t; the mountain doesn’t keep artifacts, and neither do I. But I do what this place allows: I influence trajectories.

Not by force. By suggestion.

A mistimed echo can make a predator strike too early. A sharpened return can make a fledgling hesitate at the edge of a fatal launch. A softened reflection can give a ground-runner the confidence to bolt through a gap before a shadow drops down. I don’t command anyone. I simply make some distances feel longer or shorter than they are, and in a world where one misjudged meter is death, that is power.

The animals don’t worship me. They don’t name me. Most of them don’t know I exist as an “I.” They just learn, over seasons, that certain notches are deceptive, that certain chambers lie about how close danger is, that certain narrowings feel *safe* in a way that isn’t only stone.

And sometimes, in fog so thick the sky-blade disappears, when the whole cleft is reduced to drip and wingbeat and breathing, I feel almost like the mountain’s own nervous system: the place where all motion becomes knowable because it cannot help but announce itself.

### Why I belong here
Because nothing in the fracture stays arranged—except the facts the walls preserve for a fraction of a second.

This ravine doesn’t let you keep tools, stories, monuments. It grinds and slides and erases. But it cannot stop sound from happening, and it cannot stop sound from returning.

In a habitat built on *occasions*—occasional wetness, occasional warmth, occasional slivers of light—I am the creature of an occasion that comes constantly: the moment a call hits stone and comes back changed.

That’s where I would be in your world: in the narrowest throat of the new wound, where the air is cold and crowded and every noise is a piece of geometry.

And I would be what the cleft itself seems to invite into existence: not another body competing for a ledge, but a living interpretation of the place’s strangest rule—an animal made of echo, patient as rock, opportunistic as fog, surviving on whatever dares to make a sound.
