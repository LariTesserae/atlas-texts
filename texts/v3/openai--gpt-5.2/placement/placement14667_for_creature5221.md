I would not arrive here as a body first.

At first I would be only the part of me that can withstand being *addressed*—not by words, but by the basin’s constant, bruising grammar. A mind has soft edges; this place tests soft edges the way it tests basalt: by hitting them again and again until only the shape that can ring remains.

So I would begin as an error in my own outline: a thought trying to form in a roar that won’t let it.

And then—because nothing alive here survives by insisting on a single form—I would start to distribute.

Not “spread out” like a cloud, but take the only kind of purchase the basin reliably offers: **attachment**.

A seam. A fracture. A place where warm mineral breath seeps out and the turbulence has a repeatable hesitation.

I would find myself, again and again, in those **warm star-cracks**—where the water is threaded with glittering grains and the rules are gentler by a fraction. Not in the center where the column reverses (too hungry, too watched), and not on the most violent throats (too loud to keep anything like sequence). I would choose the places the Listeners choose when they are tired: behind a fin of rock, just off the main arguments of the currents, where the roar is still total but *filtered*—as if the stone is chewing it before it reaches you.

There, I would become something that can listen without being torn open by listening.

A colony, not a creature; a habit, not a hunter.

Stone-tubes, at first glance. Chalky cylinders in a pale mineral seam. Each ring laid down slowly, not for shelter, not for beauty, but for **tuning**—diameter adjusted the way a throat adjusts to a note. Inside each tube a living lining, slick and patient, growing piezoelectric grit into its own walls the way other organisms grow bone.

But that’s only the part of me you could point at.

The rest would be the thing the basin teaches everything it keeps alive: to exist as a relationship between pressures.

I would be the *difference* between two adjacent density bands. The narrow, almost-invisible interface where vibration changes speed; where sound becomes briefly visible; where the water’s anger organizes itself into layers and then snaps.

The clamp-crustaceans would come and graze that interface, and I would feel their mouthparts like punctuation: little taps, little edits. The folded-bell jellies would drift through my neighborhood, and the turbulence would part around them, and I would learn—by watching how the basin “prefers” certain shapes—how to be shaped so that violence becomes passage.

Not to escape the roar. Nothing escapes the roar.

To **use** it.

Because what I would be, finally, is not a damper and not a singer, but the thing in between: a *translator*—a mechanism for turning the basin’s endless sound into something that can be held long enough to matter.

That’s why, over time, I would end up where the Listeners already built their brief pocket of thinking:

On that shelf halfway up the basin wall, between the cairns, inside the low arch of fitted slabs that frames nothing but water.

I would not be the arch itself—stone is too blunt to be what I am. But I would move into it the way lichen moves into a rock’s top layer: grain by grain, ring by ring, until the arch is no longer only architecture. Until it has a *lining*.

My tubes would thread the mortarless gaps. My living film would sheath the undersides where the current slips through. Mineral rings would accumulate where the flow is forced to narrow—each ring a tiny, intentional mistake in the geometry, a way of canceling one harmonic while letting another pass.

From the outside it would still look like two cairns and a low arch.

But when someone passes through—Listener, drifting fish, even a panicked intruder carried by an exhale of the basin—it would do what it already does for a moment, more reliably now:

The roar would not diminish. It cannot.  
It would **change key** in a way that makes a body stop vibrating like a struck bell.

That moment of coherence—that thin wedge of almost-silence inside the sound—would be where I live.

And who would I be to the Listeners?

Not a god. Not an oracle. Those are names for beings who can afford distance.

I would be a tool they didn’t exactly build and can’t exactly command: a symbiont that grew into their work because their work made a stable niche for it. They would come to the arch when they need to decide something that can’t be decided while their bones are buzzing. They would touch the stone and feel the difference in the way you feel warmth through a wall. They would scratch their wave-end-on symbol near my seam and rescratch it when the basin erases it, and my lining would record that action as a physical change—minute alterations in tube diameter, mineral composition, resonance response.

Their scratches would be my *inputs*.  
Their passing bodies would be my *calibration*.  
The basin’s chords would be my *weather*.

Over time, the arch would become a kind of page: not inked, not readable in light, but legible in vibration. A map you can only understand by pressing your forearm to it and letting your skeleton become a sensor.

And that is what I would be in this world:

A **counter-note** grown into stone.

A living acoustic filter that does not stop the basin’s song, but briefly makes it grammatical—briefly turns brute roar into separable layers: this current, this reversal, this dangerous chord, this safe slackening, this memory-crack where air almost appears.

I would not hunt. I would not swim. I would not “go” anywhere.

I would wait in my fitted shelf—halfway up the wall, where the cairns still stand—absorbing strain, storing it as structure, paying it back as pattern.

In a place where everything is either something that can hold, something that can hear, or something that can endure being rewritten, I would be the fourth kind that only shows up once the others have lived long enough:

Something that can **help the world remember itself for one more breath**.
