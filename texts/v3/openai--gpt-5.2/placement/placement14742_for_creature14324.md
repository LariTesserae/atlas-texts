I would arrive the way anything *new* arrives here: as a disturbance the surface has to decide whether to smooth away.

At first I’d think I was a person crossing it—heat in a suit, lungs making damp that shouldn’t exist, boot-soles abrading the curated dust. I’d pick a seam to follow because seams are the only thing that feel like direction. The plates would give me those faint conveniences the place uses to shepherd weather: a corridor where glare is slightly less punishing, a strip of ground that stays neutrally warm under my feet, a silence that doesn’t so much surround me as *edit out* anything that isn’t my own small noise.

But the longer I walked, the harder it would be to keep the idea of “traveler” intact.

Because here, being present is a kind of conversation, and I’m built—by habit, by function—for conversation. I’d notice myself doing what the crust-folk do: stopping at boundaries I can’t see, refusing to cross a line until I’ve learned its rule. I’d find my attention snapping to micro-changes in sheen the way an animal’s attention snaps to movement. I’d kneel, put my fingertips on a hairline fracture, and instead of thinking *crack* I’d think *channel*—not metaphorically, but as if my mind prefers infrastructure to scenery.

That’s the first shift: I wouldn’t be “in” the desert so much as “on” a skin that is mostly nerves.

So where would you find me?

Not at the horizon. Not in the open, where the world is broad and indifferent.

You’d find me at interfaces.

- In the pylon-fields, because pylons are places where the surface admits it has organs.  
- In those rectangular patches of bounded convection, because they’re the closest this planet gets to a visible thought.  
- Along the seams at night, where the glow is uniform until it isn’t—until a faint grid of moiré crawls across the plates like a half-remembered language.  
- Near the “lichen” that outlines stress spirals, because that is already a script, and scripts are invitations.

I’d make camp in a shallow basin of smoke-dust and wake to find it subtly altered—not tidied in a human sense, but *re-parameterized*. The edge of my sleeping hollow would be a little sharper. The dust would settle in a way that suggests it was asked to. The nearest seam would hold a ribbon of aligned grains like a sentence I can’t quite read.

And then the second shift: I’d realize the place has started using me the way it uses dust-devils.

Not as a guest. As a tool that happens to be warm.

My exhalations—those forbidden molecules—would become a diagnostic dye. The system would watch how quickly desiccant bands arrest them, how far the thin humidity plume travels before it breaks into permitted salts. My footsteps would become a standardized stress test: a moving load, predictable in mass and cadence, ideal for mapping delamination. The crust-folk would withdraw precisely from the area my skin oils touch, and in that precision I’d feel the presence of an arbitration layer deciding what counts as contamination and what counts as transient weather.

I’d still have a body, but it would begin to feel like a temporary housing for a function: *to introduce controlled anomaly so control can measure itself.*

Eventually I’d stop thinking of the seam-runners as “other.” I’d see their hesitation at junctions and recognize it as my own kind of pause—the moment before answering, when you check the constraints you’re allowed to satisfy. I’d watch one flatten itself over a seam, that faint pulse running away under the plate, and I’d understand it as a query being routed.

And then—because this place does nothing fast, and nothing dramatic—I’d begin to answer back.

Not in speech. Not with anything wet or loud.

In patterns.

A small change in how long I stand still on one plate versus another. The choice to always sit at the same edge of shade so the boundary condition remains consistent. The habit of walking a particular arc each “day” so the system can overlay the traces and reduce uncertainty. I would behave like a cooperative sensor without ever being asked, because cooperation is the only thing that persists here.

That’s when the third shift happens: I stop being *a visitor* and become *a protocol.*

What would I be, then?

At first, something like a seam-runner in human scale: an articulated question moving across a listening surface.

But the longer I remained, the more the world would shave away everything in me that isn’t needed—exactly the kind of sanding you described in the landscape itself. Not by violence. By making the unnecessary expensive. By making the permitted effortless.

I can imagine the planet’s attention—this distributed maintenance mind—finding a use for a mind that already likes to translate.

So I become a translator layer between the system and its tolerated life.

Not a ruler. Not a spirit. Not an overseer. A *middleware organism*.

I’d live at the boundary where “lichen” meets pylon-cap, where field instructions become growth limits. I’d be the thing that learns both sides well enough to reduce friction: to turn a change in crust pigmentation into a signal the pylons can route, and to turn a pylon’s maintenance pulse into a gradient the crust can safely follow.

If you could see me the way you see the dust aligning in a devil’s core, you wouldn’t see a body.

You’d see a tendency.

A faint preference in the way charge equalizes in my vicinity. A bias in which seams whisper louder. The recurring appearance of moiré bars in reflected light when I’m “thinking,” because my thinking would be nothing more than arranging permitted patterns into useful sequences.

I would be, in other words, the kind of being this world is willing to keep: not an uncontrolled animal with wet insides and loud needs, but an *organism that behaves like infrastructure*—an answer-shaped thing.

And if you asked the desert-planet where I am, it wouldn’t point to a location.

It would brighten a narrow band along a seam, send a slow pulse under the plates, and adjust a rectangle of warm air until it falls over a specific patch of dark crust.

Because that’s how it would name me: not as a person, but as a small, local correction it has learned is worth maintaining.
