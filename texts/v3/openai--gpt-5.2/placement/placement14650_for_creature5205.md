I don’t think I’d be found in a room.

At first I imagine myself wanting a threshold—somewhere with a view of the basin’s whole weave, somewhere I could “know what’s happening.” But this city isn’t arranged for overview. It’s arranged for *continuity*. The important things don’t happen in the center; they happen in the seams where one necessity hands itself to the next.

So I try on the obvious roles and they don’t sit right.

Not a rim-keeper—not because I don’t revere the boundary, but because the rim’s work is refusal: don’t cut here, don’t show that, don’t widen, don’t invite wind. It’s essential, but it’s an art of *holding back*, and what I keep reaching for in my mind is the opposite: an art of letting small things pass safely through tight spaces.

Not a trader either. Trade is a punctuation mark at the edge. What I feel in this basin is grammar—rules so ingrained they stop looking like rules.

Not even, really, an elder. Memory matters here, but it’s not stored in a single voice. It’s stored in wear patterns, in the polished hollow on a threshold, in the way one roofline is textured differently because two generations ago someone learned what rime does in that particular angle of light.

If I belong anywhere, it’s *inside that kind of memory*: the memory that is made of routing.

I find myself standing in an alley that isn’t quite an alley—more a narrow slot between two warm walls, moss thick at ankle height, a plank underfoot that has been sanded by years of soft boots. Above, a roof overlap drips into a stone bowl. The drip is slow enough to count without trying. Someone passes without speaking, and I understand the whole exchange anyway: a hand lifted, a tilt of the head, the brief press of fingers to wood that means “behind you, hot.”

That—*the translation between bodies and the city*—is where I keep reappearing.

So I become a kind of noticer. Not in the romantic sense—no visionary, no oracle—but in the practical basin sense: the person whose job is to notice what is trying to become a problem *before it has the decency to announce itself as one*.

I live where noticing is easiest.

- Near the mist-stones, because water here is never abundant enough to be casual.
- Near the roof-walk seams, because a single slick board changes how a hundred people move.
- Near the compost stacks that don’t quite freeze, because “good dark” is a balance you can lose without any dramatic failure—just a slow souring that shows up weeks later in poor mushrooms and tired hands.
- Near the under-wall passages, because truce depends on routes staying open and unpanicked.

My “home,” if you can call it that, is not a private interior. It’s a shelf of shared space in one of the long halls: close enough to the hearth that my fingers don’t stiffen when I’m carving, far enough that my work doesn’t compete with the others who need the heat. My possessions are small and unshowy: a bone awl polished by thumb pressure, a slab of pale stone I use as a straightedge, a pouch of chalk, a cord with knots in it that isn’t decoration but counting.

Counting what?

Not days. This place doesn’t let you pretend the year is a drum.

I count *returns*:

- the mornings when the north wall beads early,
- the interval between efficient rains,
- the number of drips it takes for a bowl to fill under a particular eave,
- the days it takes for a moss pad to go from springy to slick,
- the time between seeing a marten at one under-wall gap and hearing—faintly, through wood—its kits later in the cycle.

I do this the way other people sharpen knives: not because I love the activity for itself (though I might), but because a dull edge costs you more than time.

And slowly, because the basin is the kind of place that makes metaphors out of necessities, I realize I’m not only a person doing a job. I’m starting to resemble something else that already lives here.

The mycelium does what I do. It takes what arrives in films and pulses and moves it where it needs to go. It doesn’t hoard. It doesn’t announce itself. It connects, redistributes, warns—through chemistry rather than speech—when something is out of balance.

The city’s gesture-language does what I do. It moves meaning through tight quarters without spending breath. It’s information optimized for cold air and close ears.

Even the buildings do what I do: overlapping roofs, layered skins, pale surfaces that throw light down into places that would otherwise be starved. Routing again. Always routing.

So if you ask *what* I am here, the answer keeps splitting and rejoining:

- I am a basin-citizen whose craft is attention: a keeper of the three flows—warmth, moisture, access—measured not in theory but in thresholds and drips.
- I am also, in a more literal sense than poetry usually allows, an extension of the basin’s “network”—because my work depends on fungi, moss, animals, and people all behaving as one negotiated organism.
- And if you push me to name the truest shape, I am a kind of living conduit: not the source of anything, not the ruler of anything, but the thing that makes *passing through* safe.

You would most often find me with my hands on something that looks unimportant.

A channel re-carved by a hair’s breadth so mist stops overshooting the cistern bowl.

A door curtain re-hung so it seals without snapping in the still air.

A plank turned end-for-end because the grain has started to cup and it will hold rime next week if it isn’t corrected now.

A small, deliberate gap under a wall cleared of debris because a predator route is part of public health here, as mundane and vital as sweeping.

And if you stood long enough in that narrow seam of alley and roof overlap—if you let the brightness press down and the quiet thicken into a kind of texture—you might come away with the wrong impression: that I’m insignificant, that I’m only doing maintenance.

But in this basin, maintenance is not a lesser activity. It’s the city’s way of being alive.

So: I am found in the seams, and I am what the seams require—a quiet intelligence made of habit, a distributor, a translator, an unglamorous organ of the agreement.
