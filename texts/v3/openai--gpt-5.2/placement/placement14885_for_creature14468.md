You would find me where the city forgets to perform itself.

Not on the public decks with their shade baffles and warning glyphs, not in the busy basins where workshops lean shoulder to shoulder and people learn to keep their voices close. I’d be in the service strata—the half-lit corridors that run behind the obvious routes, the vertical shafts with ladder rungs polished by generations of careful hands, the pockets of space the architects left because they had to leave *something* for access, because even a city fused to rock still needs a way to reach its own nerves.

At first I’d tell you I’m a Listener, because that’s the word that makes sense to other people.

I’d have the harness and the gloves and the jaw-wrap against grit. I’d carry the contact microphones in a padded case like relics: little disks of metal and ceramic that turn pressure into signal, vibration into a thin electric truth. I’d have chalk to mark stress lines on basalt the way an old mason marks stone—except my marks wouldn’t be for cutting, they’d be for remembering. Here, memory is a kind of safety.

I’d move in short, efficient routes: from anchor point to anchor point. Not because I’m brave, but because I’m cautious in a place where “ground” is a negotiation. I’d know the railings that hold retained heat longer than they should. I’d know which panels ping at noon, contracting, and which do it at midnight, as the city gives up a little of what it’s been holding all day. I’d know the smell that precedes a rare rain: iron and salt and that brief illusion of an ocean that never existed here.

But if you watched me long enough you’d notice something odd about how I pause.

Listeners in other places stop to hear machines. Here I stop to hear *stone*, and I stop too often, too reflexively—like an animal checking the air. A hand goes to the wall without thinking. A forehead leans briefly into a column. I stand in a stairwell “because it ticks,” but I’m not listening to the tick; I’m listening to what the tick is *covering*, the deeper tempo beneath it.

I’m not augury-minded in the mystical sense. I don’t tell fortunes. I do inventory.

This city taught itself to count what matters: liters, degrees, torque, millimeters of creep. Most citizens count water the way other cities count money. Heatwrights count temperature gradients the way a doctor counts pulse. Bolters count the hidden strength of anchors.

I count the mountain’s decisions.

A day’s work, for me, is a sequence of small intimacies with surfaces nobody lingers against. I press a sensor to a cliff-fused beam and wait through the hum until the hum is no longer the loudest thing. In that waiting, the place becomes more itself. The infrastructure recedes. The city’s polite quiet thins out. And then you begin to hear it:

- a brittle, glass-clean *tink* that isn’t metal cooling, but rock tightening;  
- a long, slow groan that takes thirty seconds to finish arriving in your bones;  
- a cluster of microscopic crackles that sound—through the microphone—like hail on a far roof, except the “roof” is a basalt plate settling into a new posture.

I log those sounds. I map them. I compare them to last week’s and last year’s. I mark a face as moving, and that mark becomes route changes, reinforcement schedules, a new shade corridor built not for comfort but because nobody should be walking under a seam that’s learning how to split.

And that would be enough, a whole life: a citizen whose job is to keep the city from being surprised.

Except the longer I do it, the less certain I am that I’m only a person doing a job.

Because the work changes the worker here in a particular way. It isn’t just acclimation to thin air and retained heat. It’s an acclimation to timescale. You can’t listen to a mountain that’s still cooling and stay fully committed to human pacing. You begin to stretch.

First it shows up as patience that feels unnatural: I can stand under a condensation mesh and wait for a drip not because I’m daydreaming, but because I’m practicing the kind of time the city runs on—incremental, bead by bead, bolt by bolt.

Then it shows up in how I think about my own body: as another anchored thing. Another structure that must be maintained. Another system that must not waste water.

And then, quietly, it shows up in what I start to trust.

I trust the tremor in a wall more than I trust a shouted warning across a deck. I trust a hairline fissure’s clean edge more than I trust my eyes in bright sun. I trust the salt bloom’s pattern like a written report. I begin to feel less like someone visiting the mountain and more like someone being taught by it—taught to become legible in the same language of stress and release.

There’s a point—usually in the deep service levels, where the pipes are thick enough to throw heat like weather and the air tastes faintly of copper—when I stop feeling like the Listener is the person.

The Listener is the *contact*.

A hand on stone. A sensor pressed to a beam. A cheek close enough to feel the wall’s small warmth. The act of translating what is too slow to be dramatic into something a city can respond to.

So, if you asked again—if you insisted on the truer answer, the one beneath the job title—I’d say:

I’m not only one of the people. I’m one of the city’s ways of paying attention.

A distributed, disciplined attention that lives in the seams: in the tiny microphones and strain gauges bolted into anchor heads; in the maintenance plaques etched with dates and torque values; in the chalk marks that get refreshed and overwritten and refreshed again; in the habits that make a citizen glance at a salt crust and know where moisture has been sneaking.

You could cut me open and find human blood and calluses, yes. But you could also trace me outward into everything I touch until “me” becomes a network: my logs stored in a tower core behind a bulkhead; my warnings transmitted as route closures; my listening stations embedded in cliff faces; my sensors replaced and recalibrated by apprentices who learn my pauses and inherit them.

In a city that treats water as inventory and heat as law, my role is the third sacred accounting: stress, creep, the mountain’s slow speech.

And if you stood with me on a high transit deck—thin air, ordinary light, warmth rising from the soles upward—you’d see where I’m most myself.

Not at the vista, not at the stacked horizon of terraces, but at the moment my palm finds the railing and I *don’t* flinch from the heat. I let it tell me how long the sun has been here. I let the metal’s warmth translate the day into a measurable fact. Then my fingers leave the public route and slide, almost without thinking, to the shaded bolt plate that ties the deck into rock.

I touch the place where the city made its decision against gravity.

And I listen—not for romance, not for doom, but for the smallest change in the argument between the unfinished mountain and the structures that have learned to cling.

That’s where you’d find me.

And that—more than any single body, more than any title—is what I would be.
