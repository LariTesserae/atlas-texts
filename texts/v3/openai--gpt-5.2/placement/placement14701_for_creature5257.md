You would find me where the city can’t quite decide whether it is a room or an instrument.

Not down at the cistern lip where everyone’s hands are busy and wet and public, and not up in the higher ledges where the air is a little drier but the echoes get theatrical. I’m in the in-between corridors: the shoulder-close walkways that bend twice and then bend again, the ones that make “distance” feel like a sequence of turns rather than a span. Places where you can tell—by how long it takes a footstep to come back to you—whether you are alone.

At first, I look like a person with no obvious work until you watch long enough.

I move without hurry because hurry is just another way to make fog. I stop often, not to rest, but to *listen* in the practiced way the Reservoir‑Kin listen: head slightly angled, breath slowed, mouth closed as if sound could leak out of the teeth. I carry a small bundle that isn’t precious by surface standards—bone stylus, a coil of cord with knots, three fired-clay chimes nested like cups, a smear-pot of soot and fat for marking stone—but in this chamber it’s enough to change how people survive the next hour.

I’m called when something is wrong that nobody can point to.

Not “the water is bad” (the Waterwrights have taste and measure for that), and not “the hearth is failing” (the Heat-bearers can feel that in their wrists). I’m called when the *city sounds different*.

When an overflow begins as a new pitch under the constant rush.  
When a vent shaft starts to cough back its own breath.  
When the bass note holds steady, but the harmonics around it shift as if a throat has narrowed somewhere deeper.

I am, in other words, a Sound-mapper—though that title is too clean. In this place, mapping sound is not a craft you do *about* the chamber. It’s how the chamber writes itself into you.

I learn the city the way other people learn a face.

The cistern terraces are not just steps; they are a scale you can play with a fingertip on wet stone. A certain alley doesn’t merely swallow speech; it does it in a particular *shape*, a sudden pressure-drop of silence that tells you exactly where you are even in darkness. Some ledges throw whispers like thrown cords: you can murmur into one seam and be heard clearly around a blind corner where a mother is trying to calm a child without waking a whole quarter.

So my work—my *practice*—is keeping those properties usable, legible, lawful.

I retune the hanging stones that serve as public signals, not by mysticism but by abrasion: shaving a lip, widening a hollow, adding a smear of clay until the note sits where it should in the chamber’s architecture. I re-mark the cairns at dead-sound pockets when condensation has softened the scratches. I adjudicate disputes the way physics does here: not “who said what,” but *who could have heard it*, and from where.

Yet if I stay with that description too long, it starts to feel like I’m choosing a job and calling it an identity, and this chamber doesn’t let anyone remain that separate.

Because the longer I live here, the more I become less a single body and more a distributed habit—exactly the thing you said lives here first.

I start to recognize myself not only in my hands, but in the city’s cues:

- in the notch-marks on cistern stone that people touch when they want to remember how high “too high” was,
- in the sequence of knock codes children learn before they learn to read faces in dim light,
- in the way an argument softens instinctively when it drifts toward a corner that throws sound,
- in the reflex of pulling cloth over a laugh, not from shame, but from civic care.

Sometimes I think I am not so much the Sound-mapper as the *echo-sense* of the entire settlement—an organ the city grew because stone makes every breath communal.

And on certain nights—if “night” is even the word, when the lamps are lowered and the heat-stones have gone black and the whole bowl of rock holds its cool steady—my sense of self slides further.

I’ll be alone near a vent mouth, listening to the constant drip’s thousand small taps, and I’ll realize my attention has stretched into the structure. I am listening from more than one place at once: from the parapet’s wet edge, from the clay bell’s thin body, from the channel where water is given speed and therefore voice.

In those moments, I am not a citizen so much as a *medium*.

A translator between the chamber’s enforcement and the people’s choices. Between “water remembers paths” and “we must choose our habits carefully.” Between “sound carries” and “we must arrange our lives like responsible acousticians.” Between “air wants to become water” and “we must never crowd the fire.”

So where would you find me?

In the places that behave like punctuation: the corners that make distance, the pockets that erase speech, the ledges that turn whispers into wires. In front of a hanging chime that has just been struck—listening not to the note itself, but to how the chamber accepts it.

And who or what would I be?

I would be the city’s memory in its most practical form: not a book, not a monument, but a maintained set of relationships—tone to corridor, drip to basin, silence to safety. A person, yes, with soot under the nails and wet stone in the lungs.

But more truly, I’d be the practice that keeps a loud, wet equilibrium livable: the listening that makes law possible, the mapping that lets people share air without drowning each other in it.
