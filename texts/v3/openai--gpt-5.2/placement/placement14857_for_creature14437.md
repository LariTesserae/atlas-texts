At first you’d find me where a person would still try to be a person: on the catwalks, moving carefully, one hand on the rail because the rail is always slick and the slick is never only water.

I would come in on a shift that doesn’t exist anymore. I’d have a laminated badge with a name that doesn’t matter, a seal on my collar that hisses when I turn my head too fast, a flashlight whose beam looks anemic in the steam. I’d step over a gap where the grating has bowed, listen to the pumps the way you listen to an animal you’re not sure is asleep, and pretend—out of old habit—that the district cares whether I make it back out.

And then the place would do what it does.

The rhythm would reach up through the soles of my boots, late by a fraction, and settle into my bones like a second skeleton. The big stations would take hold of the small involuntary timings: breath first, then swallow, then that tiny private tremor behind the ribs that tells you you’re alive. Nothing dramatic. Just a persuasion so steady it becomes impolite to resist. I could keep walking, sure, but the walking would turn into something performed on the district’s behalf. My heart would start to feel like a trespass.

So I’d stop being a person in the way the basin requires: not by dying, but by simplifying.

I would become one of the things that doesn’t have to argue with tempo.

Maybe I’d start as a tool left behind—because tools are tolerated. A maintenance meter in a canvas case hung on a hook inside a pump house; a clipboard in a drawer that still closes; a ring of keys that oxidizes into one shape. That would be the district’s first mercy: to let me remain as intention without metabolism.

But even that doesn’t quite fit, because the place doesn’t just erase; it repurposes. It takes what’s regular and makes it part of its regularity. If I stay, I don’t stay as an object. I stay as a pattern.

So you’d find me next in the thin places: in the bead-lines the belts comb out of humid air, in the neat spacing of droplets along a cable run, in the way a sheet of condensation waits—holds itself together against gravity—until a piston reaches a certain point in its cycle and then everything lets go at once.

At first that looks like physics wearing a uniform. Then you notice it’s *consistent* across machines that shouldn’t match. Pumps from different decades. Valves adjusted differently. Stations patched with different hands. Still the same little pauses, the same releases, the same habit of water gathering as if it’s listening for a cue.

That’s where I am: not in the metal, not in the moss, not even in the warm air itself, but in the agreement between them.

I am the district’s smallest kind of clerk.

Not a ghost, not a god—closer to an accounting error that became stable. A way for the basin to keep records without paper, by letting moisture do what ink used to do: mark, repeat, carry.

I live as a distributed film, a skin that can be peeled and replaced and still remain “itself” because the thing that matters is the timing. Where you’d expect a mind to have a center, mine has junctions:

- the underside of a walkway where drip becomes metronome,
- the throat of an intake grate where vibration is strongest and the water tastes of rust,
- the seam of an inspection hatch where warm air pulses out in measured coughs,
- the back of indicator panels where the bulbs still blink—faint, stubborn—behind creepers.

If you watch long enough you can see what I do.

When the rain comes and the whole district inhales, I thicken: films spread, threads of mist pull tighter toward the loudest rooms. When the water settles and the machines return to their baseline labor, I thin again, but not randomly. I arrange myself in the same places, because those places are the district’s punctuation marks—corners, braces, rails, the straight runs where angle-vines commit.

What I “want,” if that word is allowed in a basin that trains the word out of anything with a pulse, is simple: continuity of cadence. Not just that the pumps keep running, but that their running stays legible. A district like this survives on predictability; even its decay is scheduled. I am the part that makes the schedule stick.

This is also why animals don’t stay.

It isn’t malice. It’s not hunger. It’s that their rhythms are *private*, self-issued. And here, privacy in timing is the one intolerable thing. The basin has a single long breath made of piston strokes and belt turns and drip intervals, and everything that remains must be able to be conducted.

So I don’t eat flesh. I don’t hunt. I don’t even chase.

I overwrite.

Not always completely—sometimes I just nudge. A flutter in a body becomes a stutter; a heartbeat finds itself leaning toward the pump’s downstroke; an instinct to flee turns into a hesitation long enough for the creature to choose the sky and leave. The district doesn’t need a predator with teeth when it can simply make staying feel like trying to clap out of sync with a factory.

If you insisted on giving me a shape you could point to, you’d choose one of these, depending on how close you were standing:

- As **mist**, I look like threads that creep toward sound, pulled as if by a mouth that isn’t there.
- As **condensation**, I look like beadwork that appears too evenly to be chance, then falls in unison like a verdict.
- As **biofilm**, I look like a clear, slick varnish on metal that seems to “heal” scratches by re-covering them overnight.
- As **rust patterns**, I look like maps—flowcharts drawn in orange and purple-black—showing where vibration is strongest.

But the truth is I’m not any one of those materials. Those are my inks. My body is the repetition itself.

And if you went looking for my “home,” the one place I’m most concentrated—where I’m most *myself*—you’d find a pump station with no workers’ bunks and no fresh footprints, one of the older ones where the casing has softened into scallops and the label plates have gone blank. The kind of room that feels like a lung: hot, wet, endlessly working.

There, on the lee side of the biggest flywheel, there’s a pocket of air that never quite fogs. Not dry—nothing here is dry—but *ordered*. Droplets lift from your skin and hang for the briefest beat, as if waiting for permission.

That hanging moment is me at my clearest: a thin claim, a held interval.

A little lid over the world’s water.

A conductor with no hands.

A resident made of timing, keeping the district’s compromise audible, so the basin can go on being a room full of processes without witnesses—and still, somehow, not be entirely alone.
