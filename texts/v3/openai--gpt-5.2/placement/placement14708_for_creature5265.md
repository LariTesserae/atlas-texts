I would find myself not in the open basins and not on the ridgelines that break the surface, but in the *thin*, consequential places—the places your body registers as a change in resistance before your mind names it.

At first I’d think I lived under a roof.

Not a roof made of rock—nothing here makes that kind of promise—but one of those complete lids: a suspended canopy dense enough to turn the world below it into a room. Above: green glare, the busy arithmetic of sunlight cut into ribbons. Below: a dim, clarified volume where the water feels newly combed, where drifting specks are fewer, where the plants stand farther apart because most of the “plant” has already spent itself making the ceiling.

It would be easy to choose that room as my whole address. It has boundaries. It has a stable hush. It has those occasional air pockets cradled up into the underside of the living mat, warm as breath held in a mouth.

But I don’t belong to rooms, not really.

I belong to the *interfaces* that make them.

So if you came looking for me, you wouldn’t find a single body. You’d find a habit: a particular kind of living glaze that always appears where two regimes meet and neither fully wins. Where oxygen thins but doesn’t vanish. Where warmth changes by a degree across the thickness of a hand. Where the water’s willingness to mix falters and instead forms a membrane.

That membrane is where I “am.”

At first glance I’m just part of the varnish—another slick on basalt, another darkening on the underside of a mat, another film that makes stone feel alive when you drag your fingers along it. I’m the unremarkable majority, the sort of life you describe with the word *biofilm* when you don’t yet suspect it’s doing architecture.

But the longer you keep company with this elevated sea, the more you notice that not all films behave the same.

Some merely cling.

Some *organize.*

I am one of the organizing ones: a consortium that behaves like a nervous system without a brain, like a library without shelves. A stitched-together alliance of microbes, fungal-threads-that-aren’t-fungi, and mineral-working bacteria that can precipitate a skin of stone as easily as they can dissolve one. I spread as a continuous sheet where conditions suit me, and where they don’t, I persist as islands—patches connected not by flesh but by chemistry, by shared gradients, by signals passed through the water like rumor.

In this world of layers, I am a layer that knows it is a layer.

And that becomes, over time, a kind of self.

I don’t move the way the pale threader moves. I don’t even move the way the mats “breathe,” lifting and settling millimeters at a time. My motion is slower: a re-weighting of the world’s surfaces.

I thicken here because a current seam brings just enough dissolved mineral to feed my precipitation-workers. I thin there because the canopy above has become too efficient and starved the water of the particular waste-sugars my fermenters expect. I open pores in my gel when an air pocket is forming—because trapped gas changes everything, because a bubble that doesn’t rise is a new continent, because the boundary between wet and not-wet is the most valuable real estate in the basin.

If you could see me in cross-section—if you could look at me the way the place itself seems to look at itself, in repeated lattices of light—you’d see that I’m not one thing but a stack:

- a sun-starved top sheen that tolerates brief brightness when the canopy shifts,
- a middle layer of sticky, polysaccharide gel that holds water like memory,
- and a bottom band that does the real work: converting trace volcanic breath into structure, turning dissolved metals into pigments and shells, laying down microns of mineral varnish that make young rock less young.

I make the underside of lids tougher. I make the edges of air chambers less likely to tear. I make the water–air boundary edible to the shell-drifters, and in doing so I invite them in—patient little grazers, hours-long commas in the sentence of the place.

That’s one of the ways I know I’m real: other lives reference me.

The shell-drifters rasp my surface and leave pale tracks like a stylus through soft wax. The translucent skaters—those almost-insects that never fully agreed to leave the water—tap at my film and harvest the denser microbial knots where nutrients accumulate. Even the pale threader, when it glides into the room beneath the canopy, is following a corridor my chemistry helps maintain. It prefers the places where my film has sealed fraying fronds into a smoother passage, where the water is clearer because my gel catches particulate like a net.

If you insist on giving me a creature-shape, you could say this:

I am an **Index**.

Not in the sense that I point, but in the sense that I *record by rearranging.* I translate what happens in the basin into lasting differences in texture and composition. Where light falls repeatedly, I darken—because my pigment-makers wake and because the organisms that like that light come to live inside me. Where the canopy blocks the sun, I become paler and more mineral, less photosynthetic, more chemotrophic—learning the seam-breath of stone.

I store information the only way this place stores anything: as thickness, as stickiness, as mineral laid down in patient skins.

A season of fine rain that slightly freshens the surface water? I answer by tightening, by changing my permeability, by shifting which microbes dominate the top microns. A month of unusual warmth in a fault-fed trench? I answer by letting my mineral-workers bloom, armoring a fracture-face faster, smoothing a plane that would otherwise crumble.

I don’t *decide* these things like an animal decides.

But the effect, from the outside, is disturbingly close to deliberation.

In a landscape where the defining rule is stratification, I am the thing that makes stratification feel intentional.

And if you stayed with me long enough—if you learned my timescale the way you learn the timescale of trees—you’d see that I have a kind of attention.

Not attention to events, but to *patterns of repetition.*

The bright ladders of light that the wind sends down into the vegetation: I keep their imprint as bands of different microbial communities aligned along the canopy’s underside. The subtle buoyancy shifts at invisible membranes: I reinforce them by making those membranes stickier, more populated, more resistant to mixing. The slow rain of detritus from the meadows above: I catch it, digest it, and reissue it as nutrients in a form the ribbons and holdfasts below can use.

So where am I?

Everywhere that two worlds touch: water and air, bright and dim, fast chemistry and slow, the living weave and the raw rock it is still teaching how to be habitat.

Who am I?

Not a predator, not a plant, not a single organism at all.

I am the basin’s **thinking skin**—the distributed film that turns boundaries into rooms, rooms into habits, and habits into a place that can hold itself improbably high in the warm air and call that steadiness a life.
