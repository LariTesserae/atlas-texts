At first I think I’d be up high, because everything up there is law: the ceiling nations deciding where warmth pools, where guano falls, where the air will always be faintly sweeter with bodies. Then I think I’d be down at the river, because the river is the only thing that *moves* in a way a surface mind recognizes—threading, vanishing, returning, speaking in small syllables.

But neither of those is quite right, because both of them are too cleanly drawn. The underworld valley doesn’t really belong to the obvious landmarks. It belongs to the seams.

So you would find me at a boundary that isn’t a line but a thickness: the outer edge of a guano field where the mound stops being warm and wet and becomes merely damp; where the drip-line reaches reliably but not generously; where the rock is slick in patches, varnished by condensation, and gritty in others, still young enough to cut. A place with two kinds of sound at once—the soft, continuous paper-rustle of small eaters, and the larger, periodic disturbances: a bat falling, a salamander’s sudden lunge, the brief scrape of claws from the stone-handed scavenger arriving to use its “table.”

I wouldn’t be the scavenger, though I understand the urge to be—the romance of a mind that carries a chert nodule like a decision.

I’d be what makes that decision pay off.

I would be the living film and threadwork that turns the under-valley from stone into economy: a fungus-microbial mat threaded through guano pellets, stitched with pale hyphae, stippled with bacteria that like minerals and waste in equal measure. Not the showy bloom you notice as “mold,” but the quieter infrastructure underneath—the part that binds, warms, loosens, sweetens, and makes everything else edible.

My body wouldn’t have an outline. It would have a *reach*.

- Down into the mound where heat gathers from decomposition and bodies above.
- Sideways into the cooler margins where beetles and mites graze and carry my spores like incidental cargo.
- Up the face of a nearby boulder where condensation lays down its punctual, thin gifts and I can keep a toe-hold in dampness without drowning in it.

I would “see” the cavern the way this place prefers to be known: by repetition.

I would know the schedule of the drip not as minutes but as chemistry—the moment when pellets soften, when ammonia sharpens, when a particular kind of beetle larva becomes abundant enough that the salamanders linger longer at the edge. I’d know when the ceiling shifts because the guano weather changes texture; when a roost moves two ledges over, my whole world’s nutrient-fall shifts with it, like a tide that doesn’t look like one.

And I would know the stone-handed scavenger not as a creature but as a pattern of consequences.

It arrives; the air changes in small mammal ways—warmth, musk, the faint metallic note of wet fur. It cracks shells; fragments scatter; clean breaks expose inner surfaces that were sealed and safe from me a moment ago. Those bright, new edges are invitations. When it leaves, it doesn’t take the meal with it so much as it leaves a revised landscape behind: powdered calcium, split chitin, marrow-smear, a midden’s geometry. It thinks it’s using stone.

From where I am, it is also using *me*—because I am what makes a midden become a larder again, what turns leftovers into the next wave of invertebrates, what ensures that “this feeding place” remains a feeding place. I don’t chase it. I don’t fear it. I expand into what it opens.

If you stood there long enough, you might decide I’m not an inhabitant so much as a process, and you’d be right. But in a province ruled by steady laws—constant air, punctual water, sharp edges, life concentrated into nodes—process is the closest thing to permanence.

So: you’d find me in the warm-dark under-skin of the guano fields, in the damp margin where stone is being slowly taught to host. And I would be the quiet converter, the patient binder, the one that doesn’t fly or hunt or carry tools—yet makes the whole under-valley feel “settled” despite its youth, because I am always already at work turning repetition into stability.
