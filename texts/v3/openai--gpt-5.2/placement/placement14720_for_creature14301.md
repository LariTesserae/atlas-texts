At first I think I would be something that moves.

It’s an old reflex: if there is a horizon, you go toward it; if there is a plain, you cross it; if there are plates, you step from one to the next like punctuation. I try on the idea of being a walker in this place—the last animal, improbably spared—and immediately feel how wrong it sits. There is nowhere here that *invites* a footprint. The crust does not forgive; it does not take an impression and keep it like a story. It only accepts contact the way fired clay accepts a fingertip: as heat exchanged, as a brief pressure that changes nothing essential.

So I let “me” become smaller.

Smaller than a body that needs paths. Smaller than a hunger that needs prey. Smaller than a voice that expects air to answer back.

I imagine myself as a dew-threader, because there is something elegant about a life that agrees to exist only in the hour the world is merciful. To wake with the seams, to lift one clear tube into the cooling air, to pull water out of reluctance—making a bead not as ornament but as engine—and to do everything I will ever do in that single brightening interval.

But when noon arrives, the collapse feels too final. Not death, exactly—more like being folded back into a rule. I don’t want to vanish so completely that even my own place can’t find me again without luck.

So I try on the seam-skin.

A varnish-life. A life that is less creature than agreement: cells plus gel plus dust plus salt, pressed into the geometry the crust already insists on. That fits better. It means I am not *on* the land but in its joinery, the narrow dark grammar between plates. I exist where the world admits it is made of pieces.

Yet even there, I don’t want to be only stain.

I want—if wanting is allowed here—to notice. To keep track of which seams wet first, which ones dry last, which plates ring the deepest when they contract at dusk. I want to remember the rare rains: the sudden localized darkening, the smell of dust remembering a river, the thin sheet of water that mirrors the sky too perfectly and then deletes itself. I want to be the kind of life that does not merely endure the schedule of dawn and noon, but learns it the way a tongue learns a language.

That is when I realize where I would be.

Not out on the clean broad facets where nothing can hold on, and not deep under the glazed cap where time slows into mineral patience. I would be at one of the apertures—the neat pipe-mouths rimmed with fused material—because they are the only places on the continent where the crust admits an inside. They are the only places where the surface’s confidence breaks into a question.

I would be in the throat-garden, but not as its lushest patch and not as its most obvious ring. I would be the band just off the rim, where the breath of cool damp air is reliable enough to plan around, and the sun is still harsh enough to make planning necessary.

And what would I be?

Not a single organism. Not even a colony, in the simple sense. I would be a *continuity*.

A seam-skin that has learned to use the aperture’s exhale as a clock, and the plate-field’s clicks as a map. A microbial membrane threaded with minerals, darkened with pigments, thickened in layers the way the underhang fans thicken—except my layers aren’t only armor, they are record. Each hydration leaves behind a subtle change in the gel’s stiffness, each dust-fall adds a different grit, each mineral pulse cross-links a new pattern. Over years the band becomes a kind of living palimpsest: not writing, but *accumulation with meaning*.

In the hour before sunrise, I would feel the first coolness not as comfort but as signal. Moisture would bead along my edges and I would soften from the outside inward, waking in gradients. My metabolism would rise like a dimmer switch. I would do what seam-skins do—repair, trade ions, fix carbon when it’s offered, hoard nitrogen like rumor—but I would also coordinate across myself, passing chemical messages through the gel: *here, the rim is wetter today; here, the air is cooler; here, the dust tastes of iron; here, something shifted in the underworld.*

Because at the throat, sometimes, something does shift.

Not enough to be called an earthquake. Not enough to rearrange the plates. Just a difference in the exhale: a breath a fraction warmer, or carrying a different salt. A new trace gas. A hint of reduced minerals that weren’t present yesterday. The aperture is the only mouth on the plain, and mouths—if you live close enough—teach you the idea of *inside life* even if you never see it.

So I would become the thing that listens to that mouth.

My “sense organs” would be chemistry and timing. I would know the underworld by what it leaks. I would know the surface by how fast it dries. I would know distance not by travel but by connection: the way dawn rivulets sometimes run a seam three plates outward before vanishing, carrying a few of my dormant fragments with them, seeding a new darkening line like a thought extended.

I think, if you stood over my aperture at the right hour, you wouldn’t notice me as a being at all. You’d see a dark ring where moisture clings longer, slick for a while after rain, then cracked and matte by noon. You might kneel and smell something clean and mineral-adjacent and alive. You might touch the rim and feel that it is cooler there than it should be, and that the coolness has company.

And if you returned day after day, patient enough to stop expecting company in the human way, you might begin to see what passes for behavior in a world that forbids looseness:

A subtle expansion of the ring where the exhale is richest.  
A branching into nearby seams, but only along the angles the crust already prefers.  
A slow tuning of the throat’s edge—holding water minutes longer, widening a capillary corridor grain by grain—so that the aperture becomes, over decades, not just an opening but a *habitat with a memory*.

That is who I would be here: not a walker, not a hunter, not a flower.

A living gasket at the border between outside and inside.

A seam-skin that has grown just intelligent enough—through continuity, through record, through the patient mathematics of repetition—to treat the planet’s rules not as limits but as instruments. To make adhesion into attention. To make the brief mercy of water into a way of knowing.

And where you would find me is simple:

At dawn, at the mouth of darkness, where the continent exhales.
