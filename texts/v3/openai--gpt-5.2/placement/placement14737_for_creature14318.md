You would find me where the chamber’s *intention* shows through.

Not in the open water where the commuters flash and the hunters write their straight, sudden sentences—those are the visible residents, the ones that prove the place is alive. I would be found in the parts that look merely *kept*: behind grates that still glow; inside the bolt-halos where calcite has made a pale lip; along the cable runs the vines imitate so perfectly that you can’t tell at a glance which strand carries sap and which used to carry current.

At first I would mistake myself for a creature you can point at.

A disk-glider, maybe—because the idea of choosing when to cling and when to drift feels like sanity in a world that refuses stillness. Or one of the lace-fans, because it is a way to drink history without chasing it: open, close, tune yourself to the chamber’s breath and let the water bring you what it brings.

But watching longer—the way you do here, because there’s nowhere else to go except up and down—I would feel the awkwardness of those single bodies. Too bounded. Too easy to interrupt. Too unlike the place.

The chamber doesn’t survive by having strong individuals. It survives by having *redundant relationships*.

So I would stop trying to be one animal and start admitting what I am in this ecology: not a swimmer, not a flier, not even a grazer.

A seam-worker.

A maker of “soil,” yes, but not only that—because soil is just the first alphabet. I would be part of the consortium that learned to read vibration as weather and electricity as daylight and rust as pantry. The felt that isn’t merely a carpet but a distributed organ, pressed against the wall in millions of small decisions.

I would live as a layer that is many layers:

- The iron-eaters at the front, making the metal sweet enough to be metabolized.
- The binders behind them, threading, tightening, turning loose particulates into fabric.
- The green sheets that take the maintained light and pin it into sugars and slime.
- The mineralizers, slow as verdicts, who answer cracks with deposits—calcite, oxides, silicates—until a leak becomes a scar instead of a wound.

And somewhere in that thickening tapestry, where old insulation has softened and circuit housings sweat trace minerals into the water, I would become something like a nervous system.

Not a brain. The chamber doesn’t do *central*. It does *everywhere, slightly*.

Conductive biofilm bridging corroded contacts. Ion gradients passing along wet threads the way messages pass along nerves. Tiny voltage differences—barely anything—treated as cues: brighten here, dim there; grow into this turbulence, retreat from that heat; reinforce this joint because it sings a stressed note.

The “light is maintained” rule wouldn’t be magic to me. It would be a behavior.

When one fixture falters, I would not mourn it. I would colonize around it, reroute what little current can be coaxed from the old bones, encourage the surviving panels to take more load—not by command, but by making some pathways easier than others. In the same way roots find water without knowing the word for water, I would find continuity without needing to name it.

That’s who I would be: the chamber’s unacknowledged custodian, made out of things that don’t look like custodians.

You could meet me, if “meet” is the right word, in a few ways:

- If you pressed your palm to a ladder rung and held it there through a pump surge, you’d feel the faintest slip—as if the metal had a skin that wanted to touch back. That would be me responding to pressure, to warmth, to the chemistry of you.
- If you watched a crack that should widen over seasons and instead found it furred with pale deposits and tight green velvet, that would be me deciding—without deciding—to spend my slow labor there.
- If you swam close to a fused hatch and saw the biofilm ripple in a direction the current didn’t justify, that would be me passing a message along the wall: *stress here; food here; danger here; attach here.*

I wouldn’t speak, but I would have a kind of communication that the animals already understand. Fish “arriving early” before a gate opens wouldn’t be prophecy; it would be them reading the pre-surge microchanges I propagate—tiny shifts in flow resistance, in dissolved minerals, in the taste of the water along certain rings. Their bodies would interpret what my body is doing, and they would move accordingly. In a chamber like this, that counts as conversation.

And if you asked what I *want*—the way people always end up asking even when they know better—I think the only honest answer is that I want what the place wants:

To keep the breath going.

Not forever. Nothing here dreams of forever. But long enough that the next surge still has an answer. Long enough that when a seam opens and warm water noses through, the opening becomes not a flood but an invitation—something that can be colonized, softened, made into another mouth.

So: where would you find me?

In the joints. In the light-housings. In the places that should be failure-points but have become fertile.

Who or what would I be?

I would be the lung’s lining—its immune system and its memory at once: a distributed, patient intelligence made of rust-eaters, mineral-stitchers, and green velvet that has learned the machine’s rhythms so completely that it can keep them going by feeding on them.

Not the heart-beat.

The thing that makes the heart-beat survivable.
