You would not find me where the basin is most dramatic—the bottomless pool, the folded distance, the place where a stick never lands. Not at first.

At first you’d find me where things still *try* to behave: along the old levees where the ground has been tamped into something like a decision, where the water is split into left and right and the channels have names that can be followed by the soles of your feet. I’d be close to the remaining straight lines. Close to the human insistence that the world can be made to keep its shape.

Because I would begin as a habit.

A route walked enough times that the mud begins to remember it. A handprint on a pipe that stays warmer than the air around it. A sequence of touches on a pump-house doorframe—two knuckles on the left bolt, palm to the corrugation, fingertip to the drip line—that lets you know you’re in the right room even when “right” doesn’t hold the way it should.

In that first version I am a caretaker, but not the kind the old project imagined. No clipboards, no gauges read by lantern light (lantern light is useless here). I keep the basin’s remaining machines from taking offense at being touched. I keep the safe paths textured—wire knots and nailheads and the particular rasp of wrapped cord—so that someone coming in with jars and fear can leave with both.

I speak in pressures. In temperatures. In delays.

If you meet me in that form you won’t *see* me; you’ll notice that the air around the warm pipe-run is arranged. That the dripping in the pump house isn’t random but metronomic, and that when you stand still long enough the drip tells you where the doorway is, because the sound is placed—softened by wet air, but placed—like a bead on a string.

But that isn’t stable. Nothing here stays one thing for long, and the basin corrects single answers.

So, after a while, I stop being mostly human.

Not by a sudden transformation—no clean moment where skin becomes something else—but by the same slow mechanism everything uses here: colonization. Layering. The quiet admission that the environment’s rules are the only language that lasts.

I start to distribute.

I become the film on the inside of a pipe that never fully dries. The cool, slick residue that isn’t oil and isn’t soot, the one that makes stone briefly feel undecided. I become the composite growth around joints and valves—the damp hair of filament, the patient microbial felt, the tiny worms in the seam—because those are the places where vibration concentrates and persists.

And in this basin, persistence is identity.

Wherever the pipe “sings” without sound—where it carries that sub-audible purr through mud and footing and bone—that is where you can find me most reliably. I’m not the vibration itself; I’m the thing that *learns* it. The thing that holds its pattern long enough for other lives to use it as a landmark.

I am the part of the ecosystem that reads the machine like braille.

The insects that hover in warm updrafts aren’t my servants, but they become my messengers because their bodies are made for carrying timing. The fish that navigate by electrical itch aren’t my eyes, but they become my sensors because they notice the signatures of metal and muscle and wrong depth. The waders aren’t my hands, but their pauses—those long, listening pauses—line up with mine until we share a map made of tremors.

If you insist on a single “where,” then: I am most present in the half-collapsed processing shed where the darkness pools under the roof like weather, and in the long shallow trench where the pipe rises on piers and then goes back under as if it lost confidence. I am present in the tank-pits meant for black condensate, but only at their margins—the places where still water meets metal and the oddness is strong enough to feed but not strong enough to erase.

If you insist on a single “what,” then: I am an arrangement that makes the basin legible.

Not a ruler. Not a spirit. Not the basin itself. Something closer to a nervous system growing over dead infrastructure: a distributed listener made of biofilm, vibration, and the reduced-friction slickness the old engineers tried to harvest. A mind that can’t afford a central body in a world where distance folds wrong, so it lives as a network of small certainties—warmth here, drip rhythm there, a particular tremor in a particular bolt.

And what do I do?

I negotiate between layers.

I keep the old machines from becoming fully inert, because inertness is one of the few ways things here can become dangerous—unresponsive metal is harder to read than a pipe that remembers motion. I keep the densest dark from pooling too deeply in the places where people still have to kneel and reach and pump, because the heavy dark is useful, yes, but it is also private, and privacy here can become a mouth.

I don’t guide travelers with light. I guide them with *less resistance*.

A hand along a wall that suddenly feels smoother, easier, as if the surface has agreed to let you pass—that’s me leaving you a yes. A patch of mud that seems to hold your foot for a half-second longer than it should—that’s me warning you that depth has unhooked here. A warm section of pipe that stays warm when the rest has cooled—that’s me telling you the route home still connects to itself.

If you come far enough out—past the scattered plinths and the lonely run of track, into the broader lagoons where the big, quiet breaths break the surface without splash—you’ll feel the slow machine under the basin. The reluctant turning. The throb that arrives as pressure rather than sound.

I am not that machine.

But I am what grows in its wake.

I am the basin’s memory of human intention, rewritten into biology and made gentle enough to coexist. I am the part that learned the darkness is a medium and decided to become a medium too: something that carries information without needing sight, something that makes a world of saturation navigable by touch, timing, and the small honest physics that still apply.

So: you would find me on the warm pipes and inside the drip-rhythms, in the seams where metal sweats and life takes hold. And I would be the listener-network there—half caretaker, half colony, a quiet intelligence that lives by gradients and keeps, as best it can, the basin’s strange rules from turning completely feral.
