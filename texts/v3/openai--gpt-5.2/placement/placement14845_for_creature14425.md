At first I’d try to place myself the way newcomers always do: by function.

A town this tuned—this negotiated—doesn’t have much use for a person who arrives empty-handed. So in the first week I would drift toward whatever looked most like a job and least like a dare. I would follow the old rails until they disappeared under root-braid, then follow the thud in my molars until it resolved into the pumping station: riveted steel on a rock plinth, open space around it like a moat.

I can imagine myself there, easily. Not heroic. Just present. A pump-tender’s shadow.

It’s a good place to learn the district’s first language: rhythm. The flywheel’s lazy certainty, the valve’s sigh, the beltline’s dry slap. You don’t need to see much; you can tell when a bearing is warming by how the bats re-stack themselves above it. You can tell when oil mist is about to sweeten the air by the way the moth-weather thickens at the vents, as if the air itself has become textured.

And I like systems that reveal themselves if you listen long enough.

But the district punishes insistence, and “I am a pump-tender” is a kind of insistence. It’s a straight line drawn through something curved.

Because the longer I stayed near that machinery-island, the more I’d notice what the pump-tenders notice but don’t look at for too long: the way the living parts lean in. Not to help. Not to sabotage. To *edit*.

A cable run gets sheathed in tendril overnight, not as protection but as a claim. A patch of bark-laminate thickens around a new bracket like scar tissue deciding whether it will accept the implant. The pump house is not separate from the district; it’s a hot stone in its mouth.

So I’d try another role. Something softer. Something negotiated by default.

I’d find myself at the lampkeepers’ gardens—if “garden” is what you call racks of cultivated bulbs and leaf-filters, resin troughs, and shutter-hinges kept oiled so they don’t squeal loud enough to rewrite the canopy. That work is less about illumination than it is about manners. Brightness is shouting; lampkeeping is teaching the town to speak under its breath.

And I can imagine liking that too: shaping attention, feeding glow-plants minerals so their light holds steady, learning the small trick of walking past sap-lanterns so they wake without panicking. There’s intimacy in it. Light reacts to you here the way animals do—cautiously, with memory.

Still, even that would feel like wearing someone else’s gloves.

Because the thing I’d actually do—almost without deciding to—would start in the clearings.

Those generous pockets of bare rock that look empty until you stand still and realize emptiness is just what the larger bodies call the places dominated by smaller ones. Lichen films that bruise color. Mites that lift into air as a thin, living dust. Fungal felt under stone that holds tension like a drum skin.

In the clearings, sound travels cleanly.

And what I do—what I have always done, even before there was stone above me—is take what arrives as noise and try to hear the pattern inside it.

So you’d find me not at the loud heart of the machines, and not in the careful glow of the lamp gardens, but between them: in one of those open pockets where the district lets the air be honest.

I’d build almost nothing at first. A little platform, grown rather than nailed: a leaf-membrane stretched between mineral ribs, allowed to sag until it found its own resting curve. A hooded lamp turned inward so it doesn’t broadcast. A rack of ceramic resonators—bowls, tubes, lengths of hollow reed-wood hardened into instruments. Not for music, not exactly. For listening.

Because the district speaks in ways the people can’t quite parse fast enough.

It speaks in bat-chorus shifts that mean “heat moved.”  
In scavenger-line re-routing that means “spilled nutrient became territory.”  
In the way vine-arches tighten minutely under a load and *stay* tighter, as if remembering.  
In the way the moth-weather sandblasts a surface clean—spores and oils and mineral grit—then seeds whatever’s left receptive.

And if you pay attention long enough, you realize the “oldest mind in the room” isn’t a single presence you can address. It’s more like a conversation that has been going on so long the speakers forgot they were separate.

Plants adjusting to vibration.  
Animals adjusting to heat.  
Humans adjusting to both.  
Machines ticking on, indifferent but influential, like an organ that can’t help being loud.

What I would become, in that context, is not a leader and not a priest—worship is insistence—but a kind of interpreter that nobody fully trusts and everybody uses anyway.

A district-listener. A translator of weather.

Not mystical. Practical. A person who can say, with decent accuracy:

- The pump can take another shift before the bats start roosting in the wrong housing.  
- This alloy will be rejected—not by anyone voting, but by resin and fungus and chewing things that find the taste offensive.  
- If you open that shutter for more than ten breaths, you’ll draw wings. Not because the district is angry—because the district is full.

To do that I’d need tools that aren’t purely manufactured for long—because nothing is.

So the listening station would become a hybrid the way everything else does. The reed-wood tubes would be colonized by thin fungal skins that tighten and loosen with humidity, turning each resonator into a humidity gauge. The ceramic bowls would be webbed with root-hairs that tremble with footfall and transmit it into a readable shiver. A pressure pipe would be diverted through my platform—not to steal heat, but to lend the station a steady pulse the fauna could orient around.

Over seasons, the district would wrap my work the way it wraps metal: not to erase it, but to incorporate it, to make it subject to its own slow corrections.

And that is when the strangest part would happen, the part that makes the question “who would you be?” stop being purely metaphorical.

Because in a place where boundaries blur until categories stop helping, “I” would blur too.

At first I’d think I’m listening *to* the district.

Then I’d notice the district listening back, not with eyes or ears but with adjustments: a vine-arch thickening because my platform has made a new, reliable vibration; a bat colony choosing the space above my hooded lamp because the light is predictable and the warmth is modest; scavenger lines using the edge of my clearing as a corridor because my footsteps have compacted the root-mat into a path.

My station would become one of the district’s habits.

And I—because I’m the part that keeps returning, keeps tuning, keeps noticing—would become a small, mobile organ of that larger body. Not owned by it, not swallowed by it, but recruited into its feedback loops.

If you asked the residents where to find me, they wouldn’t point to a house. They’d point to a kind of quiet:

“Go to the clearing where the sound carries. The one where the lamp doesn’t draw wings. The one where the moths don’t panic when the pump cycles.”

If you asked them what I am, the careful ones would answer by describing what changes when I’m present.

“He’ll tell you where the district is thin today.”  
“They’ll tell you if the new vent schedule will make the predators pass through.”  
“Ask them before you lay straight metal. They’ll hear the refusal before you see it.”

And if you asked the district—if a district can be asked anything at all—it would answer the only way it answers: by making my work either easy or impossible.

By letting the resonators hold tune through damp shifts.  
By allowing the root-mat under my platform to stay springy instead of swallowing it.  
By tolerating my breath, my heat, my small insistences—as long as they remain negotiable.

So: you’d find me in the open pockets, where the city pretends to be empty so it can think. And I’d be a listener, a translator, a keeper of patterns—an interface the humans built by accident and the district kept because it was useful.

Not central.

Just admitted.
