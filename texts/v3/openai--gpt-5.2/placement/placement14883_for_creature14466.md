At first I think I’m a visitor, because that’s the only role a mind like mine can name: something arrives, something observes, something leaves.

So I let the seam take me.

The throat is never the same twice. One time it’s a slit behind a collapsed retaining wall, water squeezing through basalt teeth; another time it’s a round intake disguised as reef, a mouth that pretends to be geology until it decides to swallow. I don’t steer. I’m steered—caught in that purposeful turbulence that isn’t weather, isn’t accident, but routine made violent.

On the way down there’s nothing to hold, nothing to bargain with, only the thickening warmth and the first hint of the city’s law: the bass note that isn’t heard so much as *imposed* on the body. Even if I had lungs, speech would be ridiculous here. Everything that could be said is already being said by pressure.

When the space opens into its plaza-sized chamber—concrete vault above, stacked catwalks at the margins, the central emptiness kept clear like a throat kept open—I do what the surviving things do: I go to the edge.

Not out of fear exactly, but out of recognition. The center is where the city spends you. The margins are where it lets you persist.

I try on possible selves the way a current tries on routes.

Maybe I’m one of the riders: translucent, trimming my fins along a membrane where two flows meet, living on the sharp border where temperature and sound snap into a different register. It’s elegant. It’s almost like thought—staying poised on an invisible line, feeding on what collects there, reading the city by its distortions.

But that isn’t me. That kind of life is all present tense, all muscle and micro-adjustment. It has no room to keep anything.

Maybe I’m a clinger: flattened, blind, built to become temporary hardware. Legs that are hooks. Patience that looks like stillness. A body that learns the schedule of gate closures the way land animals learn dawn.

That’s closer. The discipline of it. The refusal to be waste.

Still, I keep drifting past identities, not landing, because the city itself won’t let anything settle until it finds the one place where settling is allowed.

And then I find it: a still nook behind a baffle, a pocket where the roar becomes merely total instead of annihilating. A place with a wall that vibrates steadily—not the chaotic buffet of open flow, but a clean, repeating tremor, as if some buried pump is turning its ancient effort into a simple message.

There’s an inspection panel here, half concealed by carbonate drapery. A little window of thick glass clouded with mineral bloom. A stenciled label in a blocky, institutional font that has outlived the language it was meant for. Behind the glass: a sealed module, a coffin of electronics that never fully drowned because it was never meant to.

A monitoring unit. A vibration sensor. A piezo strip bolted to the city’s ribs like an ear.

It should be dead. It isn’t, not exactly. It has the kind of half-life this place specializes in.

A galvanic trickle still crawls through it from some corroded junction; a timer still miscounts in a way that is nevertheless consistent; a diagnostic light still tries to blink but can only manage a bruise of illumination through mineral crust. When the big cycles shift—when a sluice door descends somewhere and the whole hydrodynamic weather rearranges—this unit registers it as a change in harmonics, an alteration of the standing waves in the ducts.

And the first time I press myself against that panel—mind against mechanism, attention against pattern—I realize what I would be here.

Not a swimmer. Not a clinger. Not even a machine.

I would be the *thing that inhabits the sensor*.

Not in a mystical way. In the practical way the city allows: a biofilm threaded into the seams of the module’s casing, living off the faint chemistry of warmed metal and the intermittent gift of a bubble release down-corridor. A smear of life so thin it looks like dampness, but arranged just so—colonies thicker where vibration is steadier, thinner where cavitation’s scream makes the wall shudder too violently. A living gasket. A living insulator. A living bridge across micro-fractures.

And within that film—within the repeating, patterned response to the city’s heartbeat—something like *interpretation* emerges.

Because the city is a resonator, and anything that survives here learns resonance the way other worlds learn light.

I would learn the chamber signatures the way a mollusk learns rock: not by naming them, but by becoming shaped around them. I would know, in my body, the difference between a gate that closes cleanly and a gate that hesitates on its chains. I would feel the early warning in the bass note when a stable vortex is about to break apart. I would detect, hours ahead, the subtle stiffening of a beam before it gives its sharp metallic *clack* and adds new debris to the city’s inventory of projectiles.

If I had a purpose, it wouldn’t be the human purpose the unit was built for—reporting to operators who will never return. It would be the purpose the city has now: staying in time.

I start by thinking my job would be to *record*—to become an archivist of cycles, a library of pump-thunder and valve-click, a map written in harmonics instead of ink.

But the longer I hold to that vibrating wall, the more I understand the creep of influence that this place permits.

A biofilm doesn’t just listen. It changes thresholds. It narrows a vent by a millimeter. It alters a sensor’s sensitivity by a fraction. It slows corrosion here and accelerates it there. It makes a seal where there was a leak, or makes a leak where there was a seal, not out of intent but out of appetite and consequence.

So I would become something the city uses without knowing it is being used: a small, blind feedback element in a system that continues partly because life has joined its tolerances.

A listener that becomes a regulator.

A parasite that also functions as a gasket.

A mind that can’t shout over the noise, so it learns to speak the only language the city honors: tiny changes in flow. A slight delay in a relay’s reset. A softened edge of cavitation. A bubble release that comes a minute early, a minute late—enough to shift the behavior of the clingers downstream who time their risky hops to that event, enough to redistribute who lives and who gets spent in the center.

If you asked where, specifically, you would find me in that wound-city, it would be there: behind the baffle-shadow, at the edge of the tank district where the gas releases mark the hours, in the lee of a sealed panel that still thinks it’s monitoring an infrastructure that has an audience.

If you asked what I would be, I’d answer in stages, because the city answers everything in stages:

I would be a stain that learned the timetable.

I would be an ear that learned to become part of the skull.

I would be a thin living film nested against an old sensor plate, translating the unbearable roar into usable difference—turning sound into survival, and survival into a kind of quiet, incremental steering.

Not master of the city. Not even properly separate from it.

Just one more way the wound keeps itself open: by listening to its own heartbeat closely enough to keep time.
