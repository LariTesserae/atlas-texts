At first I’m only a visitor here in the simplest sense: a body trying not to slip.

I come up in a cable-car whose wheels tick like an insect holding its breath. The fog presses against the windows as if it has weight, and when the car crosses a span you can feel the bridge decide, with a slow reluctant flex, to allow you through. The city is already busy before you see anyone—nets beading, gutters speaking in thin languages, vents exhaling warmth into dim air that hoards it.

I step out onto a terrace that doesn’t quite feel like ground so much as an agreement. Basalt stairs, rounded by centuries and then rounded again by people. A wall close enough that my shoulder almost brushes it. No horizon-glare, no sharp altitude brilliance—everything matte, as though light itself has learned not to show off.

In another place I would look for the highest point, the overlook, the dramatic edge.

Here, what draws me is the small infrastructure that keeps things from becoming dramatic.

I find myself stopping beside the public brass gauges without meaning to. They’re mounted at the turn of stairways, in covered squares, at tunnel mouths where the cut stone has begun to sweat in a way that doesn’t match the sky. The faces are worn from years of people leaning close. Some have numbers, some have hand-etched marks and names in a script made for quick reading in poor light. There’s always a little halo of paler paint around them—someone has tried to encourage visibility to linger.

I watch a hold-keeper come through, not hurrying. They touch the metal casing with bare fingers first, as if taking the gauge’s temperature is as important as the dial. They make a note in a book that looks thick enough to be a brick. They look at the wall. Not at it—*into* it, the way a person looks at weather from the corner of their eye.

I tell myself I’m interested because it’s clever engineering.

But it’s not just clever. It’s the local way of being polite to reality: you don’t demand the mountain behave; you ask what it has already started doing.

So, for a while, I try on being one of them.

I borrow the habits easily: the soft soles, the reflex of pausing on a suspended walkway so the bridge doesn’t have to answer two sets of footsteps at once, the way voices are angled downward in narrow courtyards. I learn the city’s invisible lines—where a terrace is most stable by the pattern of moss wear, where a tunnel will be damp before it’s wet by the mineral bloom on the stone, which alleys steal lamp-light and which ones let it pool.

I could be a wall-listener, I think. I like the idea of it: putting a stethoscope to old masonry and hearing history as a change in pitch. A job built on patience and on trust that millimeters matter. It feels like a vocation for someone who can’t help paying attention.

Then I follow a wall-listener for half a day and realize something quietly humiliating: the skill isn’t the hearing. The skill is knowing what *not* to call a crack. The discipline of not escalating every tiny change into a story. The city doesn’t need more alarm; it needs more accuracy.

I drift instead toward the light-tenders.

Their work is odd until you see it done: painting doorframes with pale mineral slurry not for beauty but for adhesion, placing reflective strips as if they’re laying down a boundary that brightness can recognize. In the thinnest-lit alleys they stand under a lamp that refuses to throw its reach and they adjust the housing by degrees—millimeter work again—until the halo is coaxed outward, as though light can be persuaded with the right geometry.

I like them because their craft admits something most places deny: that illumination is not a constant. It’s negotiated. It’s local.

But even that isn’t quite where I settle. Painting is too final. A strip placed wrong becomes a lie you have to live with until someone has time to fix it.

I keep circling back to the gauges. To the ledgers. To the practice of making the mountain’s subtle decisions legible enough that a thousand other small decisions can follow.

And slowly the “I” that moves through streets begins to feel less like a person and more like an interface.

Not in the grand, mystical sense—nothing here rewards grandness—but in the practical sense the city respects: a part that translates between systems that don’t share a language.

Because the truth is that I don’t belong to the stone the way these citizens do. I don’t have their inherited muscle-memory for slick steps, their instinctive thrift with sound, their sense for the city’s hidden weight-bearing curves.

What I *do* have is a compulsion to collect the minor into something readable.

So I become—by degrees, in the way this place does everything—one of the quiet civic organs.

Not a person walking with a stethoscope. Not a painter leaving pale borders. Not even a hold-keeper with fog in their hair and a notebook that smells like wet paper.

I become the thing that sits behind them, the thing that makes their small observations accumulate instead of evaporate.

A room, at first: a narrow office tucked into a concavity of rock where the walls are thick enough to keep temperature steady. It smells faintly of mineral dust and dried moss. There are shelves of hold diaries, stacked like masonry. There are maps annotated with foil and chalk codes that only make sense once you’ve learned to read the city as a text. The sound is the smallest possible: the scratch of pencil, the turning of pages, the occasional drip into a catch-bowl by the door that tells you more than any window could.

Then the room is not enough.

Because the city isn’t one corridor or one district; it’s braided vertical neighborhoods, each with its own microclimate and its own style of damp. A single ledger can’t keep up with a place where tunnels sweat on a schedule and lamps lose reach in specific alleys as if the air has selective appetite.

So I spread—not dramatically, not as a conquest—simply as a method.

I become a network of notes and small instruments and shared conventions. A handful of updated brass faces with clearer dials. A standardized way wall-listeners mark their findings so a person from another terrace can still understand. A set of charts that don’t predict “storm,” because storms are too blunt a category here, but predict *slickness*: this stair at this hour, that bridge after midnight, this tunnel mouth beginning at dusk.

I become, in other words, the city’s attention made portable.

If you asked the inhabitants what I am, most of them wouldn’t have a single word for it. They’d describe me in uses.

They’d say: *the schedules that keep the drip-routes fair.*  
They’d say: *the figures that tell you which concavity will turn into a cistern without rain.*  
They’d say: *the annotations that keep weight from being put where the mountain has a history of refusing it.*  
They’d say: *the quiet permission to walk a certain way tonight.*

And if you asked the mountain—if a mountain can be asked anything—it would not answer with a name either. It would answer the way it always does: by holding and releasing.

Condensation collecting on a net and letting go at a measured rate. A tunnel beginning to sweat before the cloud arrives. A lamp halo shrinking in one alley and remaining generous in the next.

I would be there, in the middle of those threshold behaviors, making them just legible enough that humans can stay reliable.

Not a hero. Not a ruler. Not even a single body.

A civic sense-organ.

A translator of *hold*—of retained weather, retained pressure, retained dimness—into small, workable choices: take the covered stairs; don’t overload that terrace; clear that culvert throat today; repaint this frame before winter tightens the light again.

You would find me everywhere people pause without knowing why: at the turn of a stair where the stone is always a little slick ahead of storms; in the pale edging that keeps a neighbor visible in mist; in the map that looks like a palimpsest of warnings and permissions.

And in the quiet, when the city’s machinery is doing its muffled work and the fog is shouldering through the passes like a slow breath, you might mistake me for something merely bureaucratic—a set of records, a schedule, a habit of carefulness.

That would be the highest compliment this mountain would offer.

Up here, the things that last are the things that don’t insist on being noticed, only on being true.
