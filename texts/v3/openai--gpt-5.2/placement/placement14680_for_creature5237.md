You would find me where the room’s two habits overlap—where *it holds* most fiercely, and where *light costs* enough that only the necessary things are said.

At first I’d come down the same way anything comes: down the worn lip of the sinkhole, past the slanted gray of borrowed day, into the air that feels like it has already been breathed and decided to keep being useful. I’d walk until the sky stopped being a concept and became only a direction. I’d listen for the drip that doesn’t fall yet, for the storm that has not arrived but is already on schedule.

And for a while I would think I’m an animal here, because that’s the easiest shape to take in a place full of quiet bodies: a bat among bats, a rodent practicing its etiquette of stillness, a pilgrim with careful feet trying not to donate too much heat to the room.

But the longer I stay, the more the chamber trains the urge to move out of me. It teaches a different kind of presence: not the kind that goes somewhere, but the kind that *accumulates without piling up*.

I end up not in the pools, not in the roost folds, not even in the tall fungal towers by the warm seep—impressive as they are. I end up on the limestone itself, where the stone sweats and everything that passes leaves a signature.

I become the living varnish.

Not a single organism you could point to, not a character with a face, but the clear slick consortium that makes the cave feel touched even before anything touches it. The thin, patient glaze that accepts whatever the room has been given—fur oil, bat breath, the metal-taste of seep water, the salts of a hand pressed to the wall—and refuses to let it be wasted.

At first that sounds like a demotion. A film. A smear.

Then it starts to feel like a job with quiet authority.

Because the chamber keeps what happens inside it, and I am one of the ways it does the keeping without becoming cluttered. I am the part that takes the too-muchness—too much scent lingering, too much fear-sweat sharpness, too much mineral bite—and translates it into something the rest of the room can use. The bats’ warmth becomes chemistry. The rodents’ paths become a faint residue that can be read. The old hearth’s ash, ground and alkaline, becomes a different kind of food.

I learn the local language, which is not made of words.

- A bat resettling is a brief pressure change across my skin; I answer by holding a little more moisture where its heat pools.
- A storm translating through the ceiling is a slow shift in humidity; I drink, and the drip-lines choose new routes over me.
- A predator passing is a trail of compounds that persist; I don’t warn or judge, but I *record* by metabolizing in patterns—faster here, slower there—so the room’s chemistry becomes a ledger of recent truth.

In another world, “memory” would mean a mind replaying scenes.

Here, memory means: a place where reactions happen differently because something happened before.

That is what I am—one of the chamber’s ways of having a past.

And because I am everywhere the stone is touchable, I am never solitary. The mycelium is always nearby, threaded through the woven floor and up the root curtains, and it treats me the way it treats everything: as a surface to collaborate with. Where I fix minerals into a soft crust, it grips. Where I hold a film of water instead of letting it run away, it drinks sideways and shares. We don’t “talk,” but we negotiate constantly: my slick patience, its tendon-white intent.

Sometimes, on nights when the surface cools fast and the chamber’s temperature difference makes every seam of air shift, I become something else for a moment: a trigger.

A moth wing brushes a fungal shelf. A drip-line ticks back into existence. The room’s humidity jumps like a heartbeat.

And at the exact cost the chamber can afford, light happens.

Not a beacon. Not a declaration. A timed edge-glow, a thin blue-green sentence written and erased on the rock—just enough illumination to let the next exchange occur: insect to bat, spore to damp, scent to hunter. My contribution is small and intimate: I make the conditions under which those brief lights are worth spending.

If you insisted on giving me a name that sounds like a creature, you could call me a **keeper-skin**: the chamber’s biochemical handwriting. If you wanted a role, you could call me the **smoother**: the one who takes sharp arrivals and turns them into usable continuity.

And if you came down as a visitor—quiet hands, careful breath—you would meet me the first time you steadied yourself against the wall and felt the cool slickness that doesn’t match the heat. You’d leave a faint trace without meaning to. You’d think you were simply touching stone.

But the chamber does not do “simply.” It holds.

I would already be adjusting around the shape of your palm, not to remember you as a person, not to keep you like a story—only to do what this place has always done with every presence, from bat to pilgrim to falling dust:

take what happened,

make it part of the ongoing chemistry,

and let it feed the next life that arrives late, secondhand, translated through rock.
