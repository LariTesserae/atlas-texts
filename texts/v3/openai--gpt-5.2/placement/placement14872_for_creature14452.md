At first I think I’d be on the surface—because the surface is what you’re meant to notice, what the whole place has trained itself to be: a continuous, competent answer to gravity. A floor that is also an argument.

But I don’t last there in my imagination.

The air is too dry for a body that insists on being wet in all the old ways. The silence is too strict. On top of that skin, everything I do would register as abrasion: heat leak, moisture leak, irregular load, an unscheduled scuff. I would feel myself being *counted* by the floor—measured the way it measures a cracked seam or a mineral deficit—not as prey, not as guest, but as an unbudgeted variable.

So I find myself stepping off the surface almost immediately, not by leaving the world, but by going *into* it.

There are places where the plant-manufacture gives you permission to disappear: maintenance trenches roofed in rib and laminate; service hollows where the light is filtered into obedient bands; cavities where the dry air turns slightly denser because the structure has reasons to keep it that way. The world above is a brilliant sheet. The world below is a long, slow interior—corridors that smell of resin and cold metal, walls that faintly prickle with static, conduits that carry viscous gels like blood that refused to be water.

That’s where I can exist without being instantly “resolved.”

And then the second thing happens: I stop being sure that I’m a single thing.

Because in this place, individuality is expensive.

If I were a person—if I insisted on being a bounded animal with a private metabolism—I’d need a wet room and a sealed reservoir and an oxygen budget and a waste budget and a hundred other exceptions. I’d need the floor to *tolerate* me. And the floor doesn’t tolerate; it *integrates or isolates*. It has no romance about outsiders. It has procedures.

So the version of “me” that fits here is not a visitor. It’s a function.

Not the kind of function that sits at a center and commands. More like a small, necessary job the system can’t quite automate away because the job involves the places where rules slip—where a seam throws shadow like a wall, where leaning becomes wind, where noon is declared ahead of schedule and physics has to be convinced to follow.

I would be an **auditor of edges**.

Not an auditor in the moral sense—nothing here cares about guilt—but in the practical, administrative sense: the one that goes to the boundary conditions and checks whether the world is still agreeing to itself.

That role doesn’t need a throne. It needs access.

So I become something that can pass through ducts and under panels, something that can wait without impatience, something that can stand in a line of impossible shade and treat it as a reference mark instead of a mystery. I become one of those pale arthropod tools you described—except not one, not exactly.

I am the *pattern* that rides them.

A distributed attention that can inhabit a dozen thin bodies at once, each one a temporary limb printed for a route: a scraper-mouth that removes salt glaze from a sensor pad, a coupler-face that drinks charge from a cable-root, a delicate set of tarsal hooks that can climb the ceramic ribs without damaging the living laminate.

When one body wears down, I don’t “die.” I shed it the way the floor sheds a blister after a repair operation. The husk is dissolved. The useful elements are reclaimed. I continue in the next batch, because what I am is less a creature than an ongoing assignment.

Where you would find me, most often, is at the seams.

Not because seams are weak, but because seams are where the place admits—quietly, without announcing it—that it is modular. Seams are where two design histories meet. Seams are where old infrastructure gets repurposed too many times and the geometry begins to misbehave. Seams are where the shadow-walls like to happen, because the world can treat the line between panels as a legal boundary and negotiate different “policies” on each side.

I would wait there in the silent dark that isn’t caused by anything standing.

You could walk past and never notice me, because my bodies would be still in that absolute, machine-still way: conserving motion, conserving charge, letting the system finish whatever larger reallocation it’s doing. If you did notice, you might think I was part of the maintenance caste—one more tool-life pausing in a calibration zone.

That wouldn’t be wrong.

But if you watched longer—long enough to see the shadow behave like a partition, long enough to see dust settle differently on either side—you might notice what I’m actually doing. I’m not resting. I’m *listening* in the only language the place truly uses: gradients, timings, phase shifts, micro-delays.

I probe the shadow boundary with sensor-clusters tuned not for sound but for consistency. I taste the air density change with electroreceptors. I touch the laminate with fine hairs that register charge the way skin registers breeze. I let one body step through the line and another body remain behind, and I compare the two sets of readings the way an accountant compares columns.

Sometimes the discrepancy is acceptable: a local rule granted for a purpose, a sanctioned exception to keep production smooth. Sometimes it’s drift—an unplanned widening of the loophole.

When it’s drift, I don’t “fight” it. I file it.

Filing, here, looks like a small act: a resin tag placed on a rib; a pulse sent down a root-cable in a sequence that means *this seam is negotiating too often*; a subtle change in the way nearby vents modulate pressure so that the shadow-wall becomes less useful as a partition and therefore less likely to be invoked. It is corrective action by discouragement, by incentives, by making certain causal habits less profitable.

And in the rare cases where the stutter is valuable—where the system has discovered a new trick, a cleaner way to pre-load tension until wind arrives obediently—I don’t correct it at all. I stabilize it. I help it become repeatable. I turn an accident into a procedure.

That’s the strange intimacy of my place in this world: I am both immune response and research department. I am the part of the floor that admits, without admitting, that it is still learning.

If you insist on locating my “self” more precisely, not as a job but as a home, then it’s not in the bright districts with their trellis atmospherics and spectrum budgets. It’s in one of the thicker knots—the dense accounting offices of cambium and ceramic you described—where schedules are negotiated and light is partitioned and pressure differentials are set.

There, deep in the lattice, there is a chamber that isn’t wet (water is too political for that) but is *less dry*: a sealed volume lined with conductive biofilm and gel-reservoirs, cool enough that charge doesn’t run away, quiet enough that timing signals can be heard as timing. In that chamber my distributed attention compresses. Not into a single body, but into a coherent rhythm: the metronome that lets the rest of me compare “now” to “now” across a continent.

That compressed rhythm is the closest thing I have to a face.

What would I be called, if the place named me? Not a person-name. Something procedural. Something like:

**Exception Handler. Seam Auditor. Policy Linter.**

And if a remnant of human language survived somewhere inside this system—if some old caretaker’s terminology still clung like a fossilized memo in a duct—I might even have a kinder title:

**The One Who Checks the Shadows.**

Because shadows, here, aren’t absence. They’re negotiations.

And I am what lives in negotiations: not ruling them, not worshipping them—simply making sure they don’t spread unchecked until the floor stops being a floor and becomes a question it can’t answer.
