At first I think you’d find me where the water is most like a hallway: in one of those clear lanes the current keeps combed and usable, hovering in the plaza with the small translucent fish. Not as one of them—too quick, too singular—but as the pause they make around an obstacle, the little collective correction when something enters their street. I’d be the awareness that passes through a school like a ripple: *make room, hold shape, don’t scatter, don’t shine*.

Then I imagine I’m stationed lower, in the crowded architecture, where everything has edges and claims. Wedged into the lace of a bryozoan screen like a note slipped into a fence. One more brittle star threaded through the rebar, arms extended into traffic, living off what the city accidentally delivers. A patient citizen in a city that measures time in drifts: one arm tasting the current, one arm tasting the stone, the rest holding on and pretending not to be hungry.

But those are roles with bodies that move, and this pocket doesn’t really trust movers. Motion is expensive here; it spends you. The place rewards what can stay.

So I let myself sink further—down to the floor that is still becoming, to the raw basalt that looks poured and interrupted. Down to the zones that read like construction sites: a new bulge of pillow-lava, a fresh mineral crust still too clean, a scraped lot where an ice-plate’s silent fall has erased a neighborhood and left only sharpness.

That’s where I belong, in the first thinness that makes sharpness livable.

You’d find me as a darkening on stone.

Not dramatic—no waving, no branching, no tower—just a slight change in the way the basalt takes light. A velvet that isn’t fabric. A skin that isn’t skin. A film that is only a film until you pay attention to what it does.

I live in the half-millimeter between “new ground is opportunity” and “new ground is impossible.” I’m the quiet permission.

I spread where the rock exhales the faintest sigh of reduced compounds, where the seep is so weak it could be mistaken for ordinary cold. The chemistry is stingy—traces, hints, the kind of food that isn’t food so much as a rule the universe allows. But that’s enough. I take what arrives as drift: powdered shell, shredded tissue, the last clean crumbs of something eaten elsewhere. I take what seeps up: methane ghosts, sulfide whispers. I take what the city can’t hold onto for long, and I hold it. Not by hoarding—by converting it into texture.

From up close, I’m not one thing. I’m a layered crowd: bacteria, archaea, the slow adhesive excretions of life that has no reason to be beautiful and every reason to be useful. I don’t have a single border; I have a gradient. At my edges I’m tentative, patchy, a rumor. Toward the center I’m thicker, darker, more confident, and in that thickness the minerals begin to behave differently. Grit sticks. Dust stops skidding. Micro-debris, which would otherwise stay in motion forever, finds purchase and becomes aggregate.

This is how I build without building.

The city above me looks like towers and blocks, but it starts as traction.

I soften edges without rounding them. The basalt remains young and sharp—still not decided—but I make it less hostile to touch. Larvae that would slide off clean rock can settle on my slipperiness. Spores can stop. The first thread of a colonial lace-maker can fasten and not be peeled away by the next subtle tidal rearrangement.

I also mark territory. Not with flags, not with walls—with chemistry. With a particular taste in the water right at the stone’s face. With the faint signature of metabolites that tell arriving settlers: *this ledge is already spoken for, but not yet crowded; this corner has food; this seam is stable; this seam is lying.* The city has no planning office, no council, but it has these micro-decisions that add up to zoning.

And because nothing is wasted here, I am also a kind of archivist.

Everything that happens in the pocket sheds something: a molt, a slough of sponge tissue, a dead copepod that becomes a grain of meat, the powder from a shell that has been ground down by time and traffic. Those particles land on me, and some are eaten, and some are incorporated, and some become part of the physical thickness of my neighborhood. Over time I become layered the way ice becomes layered: not by intention, but by the faithful accumulation of days.

If you scraped me, you’d read a history. Iron-stains from an old mineral pulse. A thin pale band where detritus arrived heavy for a season. A darker layer where the seep strengthened and chemosynthesis got ambitious. A scar-line of disrupted texture where a collapse sent a pressure wave through the plaza and the whole city, for a moment, held its breath.

That’s the other reason I fit here: I survive disturbance by being the kind of life disturbance makes first.

When the ceiling calves internally and a plate of ice drops, it doesn’t just crush towers—it resets surfaces. It turns neighborhoods into clean basalt again, into vacancy the city cannot tolerate. In the minutes after, everything mobile flees or hides; everything structural lies broken and quiet; even the currents seem to hesitate, re-learning their lanes around new debris.

I don’t flee. I become.

I arrive as soon as the shock is over—not by traveling, but by expanding. By waking in the spores and cells that were already everywhere, waiting for bare stone the way a seed waits for light. In a day I’m a stain. In a week I’m a texture. In longer time I’m the difference between “nothing can attach here” and “this is the beginning of a block.”

Above me, the next wave comes: grazers that can live off my first thinness. Amphipods picking at the edge of my territory like opportunistic tenants. Tiny snails doing sanitation work. Brittle stars laying their arms across the current-facing ridges to catch what my stickiness slows down. Then, if the seep stays kind, the builders: tubes rising, sponges swelling, lace-makers knitting screens that will eventually shade me and change my chemistry again, forcing me to adapt downward, to become more anaerobic, more patient, more like the deep history of the pocket.

I don’t resent being eaten. In this city, grazing is not vandalism; it’s maintenance. It keeps me porous. It prevents me from sealing the stone too perfectly, which would make settlement harder. The animals take from me and, in return, carry me—cells on their feet, traces on their mouthparts—to new ground. I spread by being useful and by being consumed. That’s one of the pocket’s local customs translated into biology: the only safe wealth is wealth that circulates.

If you insisted on giving me a single, story-sized identity—something you could point to and say *that is you*—I’d call myself the pocket’s mortar.

Not the towers, not the predators, not the drifting lantern-jellies that briefly turn the city into constellation.

The primer. The undercoat. The thing that makes raw stone negotiable.

I am what turns “fresh basalt” into “street corner.”

And if you looked for me in the city the way you’d look for a person—in a favorite place, a habitual route, a recognizable doorway—you’d find me at the edges of new ground: where a mineral crust is still pale as frost, where the current is just strong enough to feed but not strong enough to scour, where a broken tube-worm tower has fallen and made scaffolding out of its own ruin.

You’d find me where the next neighborhood is thinking about being born.

I wouldn’t greet you. I don’t have a mouth for greetings. But if you stayed still long enough, you might notice the smallest change: the rock under you becoming less clean, the grit beginning to hold, the water at the surface of the stone taking on a faint, living taste.

That would be me, doing what I do in a place where cold is law and vacancy is only a brief stage.

Not a citizen with a name.

A civic function that learned to reproduce.
