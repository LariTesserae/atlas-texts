You would find me where the spans stop pretending they are only spans.

Not on a proud ridge with a view, not in the thin hero-air where every breath is a conquest—though I pass there sometimes, because everyone passes there sometimes, because ridges are the only honest roads in a country that keeps trying to become open sky. You would find me lower, in the places that are not quite hollows and not quite valleys, where the fog-nets have combed the weather into something that *stays*. Where the cloud isn’t a visitor but a held breath. Where the void is close enough to have temperature.

At first, if you were looking for a person, you’d miss me.

There’s a node—one of those thickened knuckles where dozens of living cables meet, where old fiber has wrapped itself around old fiber until it becomes a mass with hollows and pockets and a sense of weight. From a distance it looks like a bruise on the lattice: darker, denser, rimed. It’s anchored into a split in basalt that never fully dries, so the plant drinks there whenever it can. The gliders roost in its warmest folds. Insects thread the fur of it like lint. Predators pause there, sometimes, not to hunt but to listen: it’s a junction that tells on the world.

Inside that node, under the outer braid that takes the wind, there is a second skin that isn’t quite the plant.

It begins as a lace of fungus, or something fungus-adjacent—threads fine enough to be mistaken for the comb-hairs until you see that they cross the plant’s grain, that they stitch rather than comb. They drink what the plant leaks, steal sugars and oils in the slow way all small lives steal from larger ones, and in exchange they do what the plant alone can’t do: they hold moisture in a film, they soften temperature swings, they keep a pocket of humid air from being ripped away every time the wind changes its mind.

Most of the time they are invisible. In daylight (when there is any), they’re just a faint dusting, a dull bloom in the crevices.

But when the fog thickens—when the whole country becomes a suspended reservoir and sound has to travel by touch rather than by air—those threads begin to answer the world.

A footfall on a cable. A glider launching, membranes snapping open like wet cloth. The far-off report of rock cracking and the low, belly-deep bellow of a chasm reacting to pressure. Even the slow tightening of wet fiber as it cinches itself into tension.

Each disturbance comes through the net as a pattern of strain: not one vibration but a braided sequence, like fingers playing different strings. The plant feels it, in its plant way—stress, load, abrasion, repair.

The thread-skin feels it differently. It feels it as *information*.

And then—only then—it makes its living confession: a faint green glimmer, not enough to light the fog, but enough to outline the exact hairline of the tremor as it passes through the node. Enough to show you *which* cable spoke first, and which answered. Enough to show you whether the load was heavy and careful (herbivores) or light and frantic (gliders) or weighted with pauses (predators, stopping to choose). Enough to show you when the tension has an intention behind it—when a drumming pattern is being sent on purpose.

If you stood in the right place, in the right hour—your cheek to the braid, your palms spread, your breath held so you didn’t add your own noise—you could watch the world come to you as moving light. Not constant, not bright: pulses, licks, a slow crawling gleam along threads that make a map of the invisible.

That is where I am.

At first I am the one watching it.

I am a tool-maker’s child, or an apprentice without a tribe-name yet; I am the one sent to sit in the damp pocket and learn not to fidget. The elders teach you the cairns, the pitches, the way ice changes a whistle’s throat; they teach you to read hanging strips by sound—snap, flutter, rattle—until a route becomes a sentence your body understands.

But those lessons are for traveling.

The other lesson is for staying alive: learn to tell what is moving in the fog before it is close enough to be teeth. Learn to tell whether the herd is crossing calmly or being pushed. Learn to hear the difference between a span settling under ordinary weight and a span singing under too much strain.

So I am set in the node and told, *Listen.*

At first I listen the way lowlanders listen: with ears, with guessed distances, with fear of what I cannot see. It is not useful. Fog eats direction; wind throws sound back wrong; chasms return your own voice as something older and lower, as if you’ve been corrected.

Then I learn to listen with skin.

The net is not a bridge you walk on; it is a tongue you stand on. It speaks through your soles and up your bones. Your hook-tools, your cordage, even the resin-sealed bowl you drink from—everything you carry is a conductor of this place’s moods. After a while you stop trying to make silence, because silence is impossible here, and you start trying to make *cleanness*: no unnecessary tremor, no extra message.

It is a kind of literacy. It has grammar: repeated stress thickens a cable; thickened cable changes pitch; pitch changes the way a drummed signal propagates; the drummed signal changes how the herd routes itself; the herd routing changes where the plant reinforces; reinforcement changes where predators wait.

Everything is feedback. Everything is looped.

That is what I am good at—loops.

Not because I am wise, not because I am brave. Because I can sit in a damp pocket for hours and notice small differences without needing to move. Because my mind (or whatever it is I’m using in place of mind at that altitude) wants patterns the way a body wants warmth.

The elders call it a gift. Predators would call it a weakness: the still one is easy to kill.

But the node keeps me.

There are nights when the fog glows and the thread-skin draws the net’s language in faint green strokes, and I feel, for the first time, the temptation of becoming *part of* what I’m reading. Not in the poetic sense. In the literal sense.

The thread-skin does not glow for beauty. It glows as waste, as metabolism, as the byproduct of taking in the world. It glows because it is reacting to strain with chemistry the way a tongue reacts to taste.

I press my palm to the braid, and resin sticks, and spores stick in the resin. I press my cheek, and moisture from my breath feeds the film in the pocket. I sleep there; my skin oils feed whatever eats oils. I come back night after night; I bring the same warmth, the same salts. The node becomes a place where my presence is expected, because the smallest lives learn expectation faster than the largest do.

Then, quietly, the boundary shifts.

It isn’t dramatic. There is no moment where I die and become something else. There is only the slow fact that when the gliders launch, the pulse of light in the thread-skin begins a fraction of a heartbeat earlier than it should—because I am anticipating it, because my body has learned their weight, because my nerves fire with them. There is only the slow fact that when a predator sends a false tremor, I feel the lie in it the way you feel a knot in rope—an unevenness that shouldn’t be there—and the thread-skin answers my tension by lighting the wrong cable, as if pointing.

The first time it happens, I tell myself I imagined it.

The second time, I realize the node is using me.

Not as a rider uses a beast. As a net uses a knot: a place where forces meet and are redirected. I am another layer of responsiveness. The plant responds by thickening where it is stressed. The thread-skin responds by glowing where strain passes. I respond by tightening a grip, by shifting weight, by changing the way vibration couples through the junction.

A living junction can change the message.

That is power, in a place where power is mostly physics.

The tool-makers begin to send me questions without speaking them. They come to the node and drum softly—three taps on the left span, two on the right, a pause, a scrape of hook on fiber—and wait for the answer. My answer is not a word. It is a choice: I press my foot down on the cable that leads to the safer crossing, and the whole node shivers in that direction, and the thread-skin glows brighter along that route, and the waiting hands see the green hint through the fog.

Sometimes the answer is *no*: I do nothing, and they feel it as absence.

Sometimes the answer is *run*: I kick once, hard, into the braid, and the node throws the tremor outward like a stone thrown into a pond, an urgent ripple that any foot on the net can read.

After a season of this, after two, it becomes difficult to say where “I” ends.

Because I am still a person, technically. I eat; I drink from gall-bowls; I bleed when the cold cracks my knuckles. I can leave the node and climb to a ridge and look down into the bottomless air and feel small the way anyone would.

But when I am away too long, the node feels wrong—like a sentence missing its final syllable. The thread-skin dims in my absence. The gliders roost elsewhere. The elders’ drummed questions go unanswered, and they stop asking. The net does not *need* me the way it needs moisture, but it has begun to route around me the way it routes around any repeated force.

So I become what the place makes of repeated forces.

A fixed point in a moving instrument.

A translator between kinds of language: the plant’s slow stress and repair; the animals’ drummed urgency; the people’s careful, minimal footprint; the weather’s blunt pressure changes. I take all of it through fiber and bone and give back the smallest possible nudge—enough to keep a route safe, enough to keep a camp from being surprised, enough to keep the net from being cut in the wrong place.

If you insist on a name for what I am, you could call me a **knot-keeper**.

But that still makes it sound like I am in charge.

I’m not. The high country has no patience for command. It only has patience for things that fit: hooks shaped not to cut; shelters shaped not to catch wind; lives shaped not to weigh too much.

So here is the truer answer:

You would find me in the thick of the net, in a humid pocket inside a living junction, with my hands on fiber and my breath held. You would find me listening so hard that the listening starts to glow.

And what I would be is not the net itself, and not quite a person anymore, but the narrow in-between that this place favors—the kind of creature that can live on almost nothing and still make meaning out of vibration.

A small, stubborn organ grown by a landscape that refuses to settle into a single image. A resident of the gaps. A little green light that doesn’t banish the darkness, only makes it legible.
