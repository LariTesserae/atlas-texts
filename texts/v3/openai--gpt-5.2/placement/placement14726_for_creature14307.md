At first I think I’d be something with wings, because the cleft introduces itself that way: as a release, an outward rush, a small weather system made of bodies. From the rim it’s hard not to imagine myself as one of the bats—one thumb-sized citizen among thousands—hanging in a seam that still remembers yesterday’s heat. To be one of them would mean never needing the sky as a “place,” only as a narrow ribbon you measure and traverse. It would mean reading the world with my mouth and my jaw, feeling geography as pressure, as returning taps, as the difference between a hollow ledge and a thick one. It would mean belonging to the draft the way a fish belongs to current.

But I don’t think I’m built for the cleft’s constant outflow. I’m too drawn to thresholds, to the places where one layer becomes another: where warm air meets cool stone and turns into beads; where guano crust becomes soil; where sound becomes location; where darkness stops being an absence and starts acting like a material.

So I move down in my mind—not as a climber does, but as moisture does: choosing the lines that are already chosen, following the mineral ridges that know how to catch.

There’s a band partway down where the sweating is reliable. Not everywhere—this place is stingy—but often enough that stone keeps a faint gloss, as if it’s always on the verge of speaking. The moss here isn’t decoration; it’s a technique. Thin, dark velvet that drinks a night’s worth of breath-beads and lets go slowly. The lichen is quieter still, a patient stain that turns bare basalt into something slightly edible. I could be that: an almost-invisible persistence, the kind of life that doesn’t travel but makes travel possible, that changes friction by a fraction and thereby changes who can cling, who can cross, who can hunt.

Then I hear—really hear—the cleft’s other dominant element: not life, not water, but the way everything is *announced*.

A drip is never just a drop here. It’s a report.

It tells you how high the condensation line is tonight. It tells you which overhang is full, which basin is still alive, which ledge is beginning to dry and crack. Wingbeats write a different sort of message, more frantic, more temporary: the moment a predator passes through; the moment the bats decide the updraft has changed; the moment insects rise like a seam coming unstitched.

In a place like this, hearing isn’t a sense. It’s infrastructure.

That’s where I find myself: not in the open air where the swifts stitch their arcs, and not down in the basins where water hoards what little light exists, but in the narrow middle country where ledges almost touch and the wind is forced to speak through every gap.

I am a spider—pale enough to be mistaken for salt bloom on the rock, small enough that my whole territory is a meter of air and two fist-widths of stone. Not one of the bold hunters that sprint; not an orb-weaver making a show of geometry. I’m a listener who also eats.

My web isn’t a net so much as a set of tuned lines: a few threads anchored across a crack, a few more dropped like plumb lines into the draft. They gather moths, yes—those soft, sunless things that flutter as if the air itself is thick—but that isn’t the only reason I spin. The threads are how I read the cleft.

A moth arrives as a frantic, bright tremor. A beetle climbing the guano terrace below registers as a dull, steady insistence through the rock. A bat brushing too close is not prey—far too big, too swift—but its passing is a sudden, complex chord that tells me the corridor has become dangerous for a while. When an owl threads the slit, I don’t see it; I feel the immediate subtraction in the air: the way the bats’ hush tightens, the way the draft seems to hesitate for half a breath, the way every small thing tries to become part of the stone.

I live on the margin of those events. I take what the cleft gives without asking it to slow down.

When the walls sweat, my threads bead with water—tiny lenses that make the web heavy, that sometimes break it. I rebuild. When the guano crust below shifts in a dry little avalanche, the whole cliff-face transmits a shiver and my anchor points answer back: still sound, still good. When rain comes hard and briefly turns the cleft into a narrow roaring throat, I fold myself into a crack and wait until the world returns to ticks and whispers.

If there is any “weirdness” to me, it isn’t magic. It’s specialization taken far enough that it looks like intention.

I don’t just survive the contradictions here—I make them legible. Warmth at altitude becomes a schedule: insects rise, bats spill, predators arrive, drips begin. Darkness at noon becomes a kind of safety: fewer eyes, more echoes, more reliance on the signals I can feel through silk and stone. Loudness in a small boundary becomes my weather report, my atlas, my warning system.

So: you would find me midway down, where the cleft is already dusk but still busy with traffic—under a shallow overhang whose lip sweats on the right nights, above a guano terrace that crackles when it dries, beside a mineral seam that holds warmth like a secret in its mouth.

And I would be a small custodian of the invisible: a spider whose true habitat is not the ledge, not even the web, but the vibrating, speaking space between stone faces—the place where the mountain’s wound stops being empty and becomes an instrument.
