I would arrive the way most things arrive here: not by announcing myself, but by taking on moisture.

At first I’d tell myself I’m only passing through—another body moving along crowned streets, collar up, eyes narrowed not against glare but against the way fog edits distance. I’d take the wide terraces first, because they look like the city’s face: pale stone pretending to be dry, parapets with a rim of darker damp, gutters laid like patient handwriting. I’d think I belong up there, where you can see the plateau continue and pretend the place is comprehensible.

But I wouldn’t stay on the high terraces. Not for long.

I’d drift down—down into the older quarters where the city stops performing “view” and becomes all function: narrow lanes where the air is a little colder than it should be, stairwells that keep their own weather, courtyards whose low points hold a bruise of wetness day after day. The city’s public life isn’t loud enough to catch me; what catches me are the minor sounds that don’t ask permission to be noticed. A drip with a steady interval. A thread of water that has found a fracture and will not be argued out of it. The soft clack of a cistern lid being seated just right.

I’d begin by listening the way a stranger listens—curious, but still separate. Then, without a clean moment of decision, I’d start listening the way locals do: as if the water is saying something practical and time-sensitive.

And once you listen like that, you’re already hired.

Not hired by a person, exactly. Hired by the city’s need.

I’d become one of the walkers who go out early, before the day has warmed enough to change the rules. I’d take a route the way someone else takes a prayer: not because it’s mystical, but because it keeps the structure honest. I’d learn the city in gradients—this corner always slick by midmorning, that wall always salts after a week of overcast, this stair’s central groove deepening like a worry line. I’d carry the simple tools everyone carries here: a thin rod for silt, a small hammer that isn’t for breaking but for asking questions, something to scratch marks into stone or slate where the next person will see.

At first, that would be all: a channel-keeper, a listener, a reader of stains.

But the work here doesn’t stay on the surface. The city teaches you to think like seepage: slow, distributed, persistent. You start noticing how little of the water is visible and how much of the city’s health depends on the unseen routes. You start to prefer the places most people only cross quickly—the covered runs, the hollowed corridors with wet breath, the sealed doors that were sealed not to hide secrets but to keep collapse from spreading.

I’d find myself lingering at thresholds.

Where a carved room meets the raw rock behind it.  
Where a terrace’s refacing meets the older stone beneath.  
Where the city’s logic—slope and channel and weight—meets the plateau’s older logic of patience.

In those seams, the city is most like a living thing: not alive in a sentimental way, but alive the way a body is alive—by constant compensation. The water presses, the stone yields a fraction, the builders respond, and the response becomes the next generation’s “this is how it has always been.”

That’s where I’d start to change.

Because I’m not, by nature, a maker of monuments. I’m a keeper of patterns. I’m the kind of being that takes hundreds of small observations and tries to make a single usable forecast out of them. In another world that might look like calculation, record-keeping, a library. Here it would look like this:

A person with damp cuffs and stone dust in the creases of their hands, who can walk into a court and tell—by smell, by the pitch of a drip, by the way algae has chosen one side of a channel—what the lower district will be dealing with in three days. Someone who knows that a new salt lace on an interior wall isn’t decoration; it’s a sentence. Someone who hears the city’s “quiet” not as absence but as information.

So if you asked the residents who I was, they might give you a title that sounds smaller than it is. The words would be plain.

Flow-reader.  
Drip-counter.  
Wall-listener.  
Cistern-scribe.

If you asked the city—if a city can be said to answer—then I’d be something less like a job and more like an organ.

A nerve.

Not the kind of nerve that controls anything, because nothing here is controlled. The kind that notices first. The kind that carries warning without drama. The kind that makes it possible for the heavy, slow body of stone to respond in time.

And if you kept watching me, long enough for the fog to assemble and lift and assemble again, you might start to suspect something else: that I’m not entirely separate from the damp I track.

That the work has made me a little like the films and crusts that are the city’s first citizens. Not in shape, but in method. I don’t conquer; I adhere. I don’t blaze; I persist. I take what is constant—coolness, seepage, gravity—and turn it into a way of staying.

So you would find me in the places that look like nothing is happening: beneath an overhang where the rock weeps in threads; at the lip of a gutter where a drip has kept time longer than any clock; in an abandoned corridor turned moss-farm; on a stair whose center groove is deep enough to hold a finger, deep enough to hold a history.

And what would I be?

A resident of the in-between: part person, part maintenance, part listening apparatus—someone the city uses to notice itself. A translator between water and stone, writing the day’s weather not in sky-language but in stains, pitches, and the exact moment the parapets darken at evening, when the whole place exhales and the first drips begin their small, reliable counting.
