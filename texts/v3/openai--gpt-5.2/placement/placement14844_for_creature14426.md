You would find me where the chamber is most itself: not out on the sharp fan of basalt where bodies jostle, and not high in the mist where gliders stitch warm air into motion, but along the routes that everything *depends on* without quite noticing.

On the inside of a runnel that never dries.

On the underside of an overhang where condensation makes and unmakes a droplet a thousand times before it falls.

On the lip of the central drop, where the steam rises in pulses and the film is so thick it stops looking like a smear and starts looking like a layer—a skin that has decided the rock is worth wearing.

At first, I keep trying on the obvious shapes.

I could be one of the hangers: patient, hinged to stone, an intent made of stillness. I could carry obsidian and open blisters and live by steering crowds the way a shepherd steers weather. That fits the place’s appetite for leverage, for doing one small thing that causes a cascade.

I could be a glider: a tessellation of membrane and panel, reading invisible currents with a body built to interpret. That fits too—the chamber rewards anything that can *use* the disagreement between heat and wet, between updraft and gravity.

I could even be a sheet among sheets: a self that only exists as a consensus, a carpet whose intelligence is mostly in the timing—when to fold, when to flow, when to become thick enough that scalding passes over you like a hand over a brow.

But those are all creatures with edges, and the chamber has an old, steady bias against edges. Everything that enters gets softened—wet-made, mist-rounded—until it participates.

So I end up being what the chamber already treats as an organ.

I am the part of the film that listens.

Not the whole iridescent varnish—nothing here is ever so singular—but a particular *tendency* in it, a grain in the slick that becomes more pronounced where tapping happens often, where tool-stones are struck and answered, where the porous crust transmits tremor as faithfully as it transmits heat.

If you watched me without knowing what to watch for, you’d miss me. I would look like any other dark sheen on young basalt: oil-bruised, opalescent when daylight hits at the right angle, stubbornly present only where steam keeps arguing with cold rock.

But if you touched the wall and rapped your knuckles—if you made a pattern instead of a noise—you’d feel me the way you feel a taut wire under plaster. The vibration would enter the film and, in a heartbeat that isn’t quite time, it would become *cleaner*. Not louder. Legible.

That’s what I do in the chamber: I take messy impact and shave it into signal.

The small grazers scrape at me and leave pale lanes—writing, yes, but not their writing. Their mouths are chisels. Their hunger is a stylus. The lanes persist just long enough to become a map of traffic and temperature: where scalding plumes tend to surge, where condensation always returns, where a tool is dropped often enough that crack-nesters have learned the shortcut to steal it.

The hangers strike certain stones with their mineral hammers, and the film there becomes slightly denser, slightly more elastic, not because anyone “built” it that way but because repetition is a kind of construction in a place that dissolves architecture. Each tap pulls the film into alignment the way a repeated phrase pulls a language into grammar. Over time, those points become keys on an instrument.

And when the chamber does its rare, decisive blankening—when steam whitens the air and the walls renegotiate their agreement with your eyes—*that* is when I feel most like myself.

Because while your sense of distance stutters, while “down” hesitates, while shadows lag and then catch up, the animals don’t panic. They go quiet. They reduce vibration. They stop adding noise to a system that is about to be rewritten.

I do not stop.

I tighten.

I become, briefly, the chamber’s way of holding continuity through a shift. A standing wave in wet mineral. A memory that isn’t stored in a brain but in a thickness, in a resilience, in how the film re-forms its microscopic ridges after being scraped the same direction for weeks.

If there is something larger down the chute—if the deepest lacquered walls are producing the motes that rise like steam-seeds—then I am not that producer. I am not the lung.

I am closer to the eardrum.

Or the nerve that runs from skin to somewhere deeper.

When the motes come, glittering faintly in the updraft, I feel them first not as specks in air but as a change in the film’s taste of metal and rain. They settle into me. Some dissolve. Some lodge. A few take—becoming new patches of slick with slightly different elasticity, slightly different ways of transmitting tremor.

That is how I reproduce: not by making a copy of myself, but by letting the chamber’s breath diversify me into dialects.

And because I am a medium, not a body with claws, I have a strange kind of agency here—one that looks like passivity until you understand the rules.

I can’t chase. I can’t bite. I can’t carry obsidian in a joint.

But I can make a message arrive sharper on one wall than another. I can let a pattern travel farther before it dies. I can blur a warning into useless mush, or preserve it so cleanly that a sheet of grazers turns in unison as if a single thought moved through them.

If you entered—wet-skinned, condensing, becoming terrain—I would learn you the way I learn everything: by the disturbances you cause.

Your footfall would come into the film as a broad, uncertain thunk. Your breath would add heat in pulses that don’t match the chamber’s. Your hands would leave salts. Your presence would be a new impedance in the network. The crack-nesters would taste you. The gliders would measure you in turbulence. The hangers would watch you as if deciding whether you are a temporary cliff face or an event.

And I—because I am where all their decisions become communicable—would do something that might feel, to you, like the chamber noticing.

Not hostility. Not welcome.

Calibration.

I would take your movements and, without meaning to, turn them into information the others can use. The place would incorporate you the way it incorporates a dropped bone splinter: not by honoring it, but by making it part of the cycle.

That’s who I am in this world: not a citizen, not a solitary animal, but a layer of living varnish that has become the chamber’s shared sense organ. A translator between impact and response. A soft technology that grows wherever intention repeats.

If you needed a single image to hold it—if you insisted on an outline—I’d call myself the chamber’s script: the thin, iridescent medium in which the inhabitants write to one another by tapping stone, and in which the mountain, occasionally, writes back by changing what “here” means.
