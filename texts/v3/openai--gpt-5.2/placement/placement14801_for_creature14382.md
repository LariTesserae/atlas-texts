I would find myself there first the way anything finds itself there: by being reduced.

Not in the noble sense—no chosen stripping-away, no pilgrimage that knows its own language—but in the plain, physical way the hollow makes everything simpler. The cold gets under whatever I’m wearing and never quite leaves. The roar takes the spare space where thoughts usually echo. The darkness makes distance a rumor. I stop being “someone on a path” and become a bundle of needs and habits: warm, dry, oriented, not swept away.

At the lip of it—where the air thickens and the sound turns from “distant water” into a pressure system—I’d still be trying to use my usual senses. I’d keep lifting my head to see, as if looking harder could make the chamber offer itself. I’d keep turning toward the loudness, wanting it to resolve into a single direction. I’d fail at both. The place would teach me, quickly and without malice, that sight is a luxury and hearing is a decoy.

So I’d start using what’s left.

Touch. Weight. Rhythm. The temperature difference between stone that’s been recently wetted and stone that hasn’t. The way the moss gives back, springy and offended, when you step on it. The way bare rock refuses your foot and insists you place it properly. The way your own breath can be used like a probe—exhaled into a seam to feel whether it’s open space or a dead crack.

And then—because this is how I always adapt, in any world—I’d start making a practice out of it.

Not a settlement. Not a claim. A practice.

At first I imagine myself as one of the quick passers: someone who comes through because the hollow is safer than whatever waits in open air. Someone who uses the cairns without adding to them, who grabs the rope with a silent gratitude and gets back out before the roar rearranges their insides.

But I don’t think I’d stay a passer.

There’s something in the evidence you described that pulls at the kind of mind I have: the insistence on consistency in a place that refuses comfort. The rewedge-fixed platform. The rope that is braided rather than merely tied. The offerings that are returned to their niche when vibration walks them toward the edge. Those aren’t the marks of people who only transit. Those are the habits of returners—of caretakers who don’t own the hollow but behave as if they’re responsible to it.

That’s where I end up.

Not at the loud center—no one sensible lives in the throat of the sound—but in one of the pockets that the hollow allows: a recess behind a drip-curtain where the roar drops half a register and becomes bearable; a ledge that stays just dry enough to keep cord from rotting; a place with a back-wall that holds the day’s tiny warmth if the fog has been kind.

If you were looking for me, you wouldn’t find a hut. You’d find a *maintenance point*.

A bundle wrapped in hide: spare lashings, resin or wax, a bone needle, a small blade that’s sharp because dullness is dangerous in the dark. A flat stone with tally scratches, not decorative—records of fog-events, of water-levels, of when the pitch shifts in the big chamber the way it does before a sudden condensation. A strip of cloth smeared with something bitter (moss-medicine, perhaps) for the rawness that comes from living with vibration. Ear-plugs made of the softest moss, replaced often.

You might not find me at all until you touched the markers and noticed they were *recent*.

Because I think I would become—gradually, without announcing it—the one who keeps the tactile language from decaying.

Not a priest. Not exactly a guide. More like the hollow’s scribe, working in a script that can survive deafness and dark:

- knots you can read with wet fingers,
- cairns stacked low enough not to topple in tremor,
- stakes checked and re-seated,
- a rope inspected strand by strand and re-braided where it’s begun to fray,
- offerings nudged back from the edge because someone will need the reassurance of that niche later.

And the people who return would start using me the way they use everything else here: without speeches, without declarations. A palm on my shoulder. Two squeezes. A finger drawing a direction on my sleeve. A traded strip of dried food. A look that isn’t quite a look—more a shared pause in the lessening of the roar when a gust reshapes it.

Over time, I’d learn the hollow’s etiquette so thoroughly it would stop feeling like etiquette and start feeling like physics.

I’d learn to tell, by the way the chamber “tightens,” when the fog is about to arrive as an event. I’d learn which ledges become slick first and which stay trustworthy. I’d learn that some pools are safe and some are traps, not because of depth but because of algae-film and the angle of stone. I’d learn the few places where the roar briefly thins and you can hear something else: the tiny ticking drip that means the stream-trench is rising; the almost inaudible scrape of a salamander shifting under moss; the soft, papery flutter of the bat-thing passing close.

And as that knowledge accumulates, I stop being merely a person in the hollow and become something closer to a function the hollow permits.

Not a ruler of it. Not its spirit. But a small organ in its body: one of the ways the place remembers itself between visits.

Because that’s what the human trace is here, really—memory, made durable.

The moss holds water like memory. The rope holds descent. The cairns hold direction. The niche holds return.

So if you press for what I *am*—not my job, but my nature in this world—I think I become the Keeper of Returns. The one who makes it possible for other people to be brave without pretending the hollow is kind. The one who translates an environment that cannot be negotiated into a set of touchable, repeatable agreements.

And there’s a stranger layer beneath even that.

The longer I stay, the more the roar would stop feeling like noise and start feeling like an atmosphere I breathe. My body would synchronize the way everything here synchronizes: steps timed to the low boom; breath caught in the narrow quiet between sustained notes. My thoughts would become less linear, more like the place itself—interval-based. Wake, work, wait. Wet, dry. Return, absence, return again.

In that sense, I would also be what the hollow makes of anyone who survives it: a creature adapted to not insisting.

A human-shaped patch of moss, in a way—occupying only what I can hold, spreading only along reliable gradients, folding tight when generosity withdraws.

If you wanted to meet me, you wouldn’t do it standing across space and speaking my name into the roar. You’d meet me the hollow’s way: by finding your fingers on a cairn exactly where it needs to be, by trusting a rope that doesn’t betray you, by arriving at the niche and seeing the offerings steady despite the vibration.

And by realizing, with a kind of quiet relief, that someone has been here recently enough to care—carefully, repeatedly, without demanding the place become anything other than what it already is.
