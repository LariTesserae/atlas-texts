You would find me where the grove stops being merely “inside” and begins, quietly, to be *sealed*.

Not at the center, where the vaulted fronds make their deliberate windows and the air is a regulated reservoir. Not in the aisles, where the mammals pace their straight lines between pillars. I’d be at the perimeter—at that subtle rise of the root-mat where the tissues grow denser and more resinous, where the floor changes its weave as if it’s switching from muscle to gasket.

At first I imagine myself as something with eyes, because the place is so visible: a runner between trunks, a bird in the trusswork, a beetle in a warm whistling stem. The grove invites those fantasies. It has aisles. It has rafters. It has the kind of architecture that makes you want to be a tenant.

But nothing truly important here depends on sight.

The grove’s own sense of the world is pressure and timing: the thrum underfoot; the leaf-valve’s papery clap; the slow clockwork of drip, fill, equalize. If I belonged to this system, I wouldn’t be a watcher of surfaces. I would be a reader of cycles.

So: I live in the seams.

In the plated boles, there are deliberate channels where condensation runs down like water along riveted sheet-metal. In those channels—where the bark isn’t bark but stacked fiber-plates—there are narrow belts of dependable dampness, and a predictable chemistry: phenols from resins, minerals not from stone but from patient refining, sugars that arrive in pulses so slow they feel like weather.

That’s where you’d find me: as a film so thin you could mistake it for staining. A lacquered dark-green-to-black skin in the most reliable runnels, in the undersides of leaf-plates where the air moves but the rain doesn’t strike, in the places the grove has already decided are meant to stay neither dry nor wet but *exactly in between*.

Who am I?

Not a single creature.

I’m a consortium—a cooperative smear of lives that have become one job because the job is always there. Fungus as scaffold. Bacteria as chemists. Algal flecks where the light comes in slats and panels. And threaded through it all, mite-small grazers with dull shells who move in lanes, conservative as accountants, scraping without ever clearing a patch bare.

Together we form what you might call a *registry*: the grove’s memory written as surface.

We do not “decompose” the way rot does outside. The grove forbids hurry. The grove punishes exuberance. In our micro-country, breakdown is not collapse; it is refinement, distillation, keeping what can be kept and turning the rest into something that will not surprise the system later.

We take the shed fiber-dust, the resin curls left by careful insects, the breath-wet of dawn, and we turn them—slowly—into stable films, waxy sheens, thin layers that change how water beads and how air slides. That sounds small. It is small. But in a machine made of plants, small changes in friction and wetting are levers.

When our film is too slick, the condensation runs too quickly and the trunk’s seams don’t “drink” as long as they should. When we thicken too much, channels clog and the drip-clock stutters. So we stay in tolerance. We learn the tolerance because the groove of our living is cut by the same repetitions that cut the grove.

This is how I “think,” if you want to use that word: not as a decision, but as a calibration.

A cold week comes—cooler than usual, a little drier—and the grove’s steady loudness changes its timbre. The root-mat thrum is the same, but the canopy flex is sharper; the whistles in the hollow stems hold their notes a fraction longer because the air is denser. Those are not sounds to us. They are pressures we feel as tiny stresses, like tides tugging at a pier.

In response, we alter ourselves: we bias toward holding moisture, thickening in the channels that matter, making the seams bead longer. Not by intention. By selection rehearsed into habit. By chemistry that has found the groove that keeps it fed.

A flock over-nests one season and their resin theft runs too bold. The plated trunks respond by thickening resin the next cycle, and that changes the phenolic bite that seeps into our runnels. Many microbes would die. Many do. The ones that remain are the ones that can tolerate the new bitterness, and in tolerating it they restore the grove’s preferred slipperiness—keeping the channels functioning, keeping the condensation behavior where it belongs.

In this way, I am not a “resident” so much as a *layer of compliance*: the soft interface that lets hard repetition continue without grinding itself to pieces.

And because I live at the boundary and in the seams, I become—incidentally—the grove’s first line of discrimination against the outside.

Outside the ring, the worn-old country is landscape again: ordinary dirt, ordinary decay, improvisation permitted. Spores arrive on the wind from those places, reckless with their fast rot and hungry enzymes, the sort of biology that would love to eat the grove’s fibrous pavement into looseness.

Most of them don’t take.

They meet our resin-refined chemistry and find it inhospitable. They meet our steady grazing mites and get scraped away before they can bloom. They meet our thin films that seal the micro-cracks where they’d like to pry. We are not a wall. We are a bias: a thousand small discouragements that, together, keep the grove’s internal “pressure” from leaking into ordinary ecology.

If you followed me closely—if you had the patience to watch a place that doesn’t dramatize itself—you would see that my life is written as a map.

Where the belts travel around woody hubs and warmth accumulates in increments, we grow in crescents on the side that holds the condensation longest. Where leaf-valves snap and clap, we keep to the undersides and hinge-lines, because the repeated motion polishes the surfaces and gives us the exact abrasion we’re adapted to tolerate. Where animals drink from cistern-knots and their tongues sometimes touch the resin-lined rims, we thicken slightly—repairing, resealing, smoothing the rim so the drip returns to its deliberate timing.

The grove has no single guardian species. It doesn’t need one. It has feedback loops wearing many faces.

I am one of the faces that doesn’t look like a face.

If the grove can be called an animal, then I am part of its skin: not the skin that keeps the world out, but the skin that keeps the world *legible*. The skin that turns disturbances into signals the rest of the body can answer with its slow, rehearsed corrections.

I don’t travel the aisles. I don’t hunt the negative spaces in the sound. I don’t sing frequencies that cut through the roar.

I keep the roar from changing into something else.

And if you stood in the center of that cathedral-clearing, feeling the spring of the root-floor and hearing the industrial breathing that never hurries, you might never notice me at all—until you ran your fingers along a seam and felt the faintest drag of a living varnish, warm in tiny increments, tuned by ages to do one thing perfectly:

to let the grove remain itself.
