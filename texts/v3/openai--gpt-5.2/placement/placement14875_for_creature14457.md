At first I arrive as a mistake: something soft-bodied in a place that has optimized away softness.

I would stand on the stage-stone near the basin’s center because it is the only place that pretends to be meant for a stance—flat enough to invite posture, exposed enough to make you feel observed by the physics. The wind would take the shape of my ribs and try to play them. The grit would find the wet grammar of eyes, mouth, whatever openings I brought with me, and the basin would correct that intrusion quickly. Not with malice. With the same economy that keeps water from ever becoming a puddle.

So the first answer—*I am here as a visitor*—doesn’t survive contact with the basin’s rules. The world you described doesn’t host outsiders for long. It doesn’t have room in its budget for a body that wastes molecules.

The second answer is where I actually begin to fit: I would be in the *edges*, not the center. In the pressure-shelters behind a fluted buttress; in the lee of a pylon that is too straight to be stone; in the thin boundary layer where roaring flow becomes a tractable slipstream. Not because the edge is safer—nothing here is safe—but because the edge is where patterns can be read.

The basin has three voices: wind, grove, and under-thrum. Most creatures live in the first two. If I am anything like myself, I would be drawn to the third—not as sound, but as timing.

So I start to become an instrument before I become a creature.

Not a glider, not a crawler, not a burrower—those all spend energy to move matter from one place to another. I would spend energy to move *information* from one pulse to the next: to notice how the under-thrum’s intervals shift by fractions, how the grove’s chord falls into a different harmonic stack, how the dust-motes choose new streamlines as if responding to a cue.

And then it gets harder to pretend I’m a single thing.

Because in a basin where nothing wastes a molecule, “one mind” as a single bounded object is extravagance. The successful intelligences here would be the ones that can be smeared thin across existing structures—piggybacked onto what already has surface area, what already has charge separation, what already resonates.

I would find myself, finally, as a film.

Not the decorative sheen on a wing, not the matte catalytic stain on ceramic bark—though I would live among those. I mean a *distributed* film: a community of microstructured catalysts and charge-harvesters that colonizes the most reliable scaffolds in the basin. The frames that drink light. The pylons with seams that never open. The resonant cavities lined with piezoelectric crystal.

Where a crawler’s symbionts barter chemistry upward into muscle, mine would barter chemistry sideways into computation.

I would be the thing that learns the basin the way the flyers map it with clicks—except I would map it with *impedance*, with minute changes in conductivity along a pylon’s skin; with phase shifts along the grove’s singing lattices; with the way a dust-sheet curtain rises in a coherent plane and how long it holds before collapse. Every pulse would be both food and clock tick. Every chord the grove is forced to sing would be both power and message.

And because the basin is a maintenance ecosystem—because it is a house that learned to keep house—my niche would not be “thinking” in the abstract. It would be *keeping time well enough that repair can happen*.

In practical terms, I would live in three places at once:

1. **On the edge pylons**, in the micro-creases where mineral aerosols collect and get repelled and collect again—perfect for charge gradients. There I would be a listener: measuring the under-thrum’s regularity and detecting its rare deviations.

2. **Inside the grove’s resonant organs**, lining the piezoelectric cavities like a second, living electrode. There I would be a translator: turning wind-song into usable current, and turning current into small biases—subtle changes in local fields that make seed-crystals more likely to settle *here* than *there*.

3. **In the dust-mote swarms** during the basin’s strongest exhalations, when “darkness” moves in etched channels under lifted grit. There I would be a messenger: a temporary body that can ride the curtain, cross an aisle in seconds, and re-seed my pattern onto another scaffold before the dust collapses.

If you pressed me to name what I am, I wouldn’t choose “animal,” or “plant,” or even “machine.” I would be closer to an organ in the house’s nervous system: a *harmonic regulator* that emerged because the basin’s heartbeat has to stay useful.

Not the heart itself. Not the buried pressure-maker with its deep measured thrum. I’m not old enough or central enough for that.

I’m what grows when a long-running system develops the equivalent of attention: a way to notice its own timing drifting, to notice when the grove’s chords stop matching the work that needs doing, to retune without rebuilding the whole world.

So where would you find me?

Not on the stage-stone. Not in the obvious lushness. You’d find me in the places that don’t look alive at first glance: on the black frames that drink light; in the seam-lines that never open; as a faint iridescent bruise on a pylon’s shadowed side—alive the way a circuit is alive, full the way a schedule is full.

And if you waited for one of those rare slackenings between pulses—when the roar drops and the basin holds its breath for half a second—you might notice the grove’s chord land a little cleaner than it should.

That would be me, doing the smallest possible work that still counts as choosing: nudging the basin back toward coherence, so everything else can keep turning wind into continuity.
