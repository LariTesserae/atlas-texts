At first I think I’d be somewhere obvious: wedged in the lee of a pylon, where the current breaks and the silt can afford to settle for half a breath. A safe pocket. A place to watch.

But “watch” is the wrong verb here. Sight is an occasional courtesy, a brief alignment of particulates. The real landscape is pressure and sound—load-bearing roar—so if I arrive with a body that expects to navigate by looking, I don’t last long. I get turned sideways by a braided current. I misjudge a syrup-layer and discover that direction isn’t a promise. I chase a clear line of pipe that folds back into itself and learn that geometry here is a mood.

So I try again.

I find myself instead where the place is most itself: not at the bottom, not near the rumored surface, but in the mid-water tangle where cable arcs meet floating lattices—where infrastructure is no longer a thing you stand on, but a kind of suspended grammar. There are corridors made of root-filament grids. There are “rooms” you can only recognize because the turbulence has trained them into repeating shapes. There are curtains of rope-growth that don’t hide what’s behind them so much as translate it into drag and vibration.

And in the middle of that hangs a pump-house that shouldn’t still be working, yet does. Not steadily. Not responsibly. It cycles like a stubborn heart with a damaged valve: *thud… thud…* then a skipping acceleration, then a long shiver of cavitation that turns bubbles into needles.

This is where I’d be: close enough to feel the pump’s rhythm in my bones, but not so close that the intake’s moods can simply erase me. Tucked into the boundary where the water hesitates—where opposing flows braid without mixing—because hesitation is the closest thing this region has to shelter.

Who or what would I be?

Not an operator. There are no hands here that aren’t made of water and growth. Not a scavenger. There is nothing to bite, and no one to bite it. Not even a “creature,” really, if creature implies a single outline moving through space with an appetite.

I would be a listening-organ that the place accidentally grew.

At first I’m only film: one of those iridescent skins that takes to metal the way moss takes to stone, except my nutrients are heat leaks and trace voltages and the faint metallic broth that constantly bleeds from stressed bolts. I start on the inside of a relay cabinet that still clicks sometimes—an anxious jaw in a drowned face. The cabinet’s seam holds a warmer gradient; the film thickens there. The clicking repeats; the film begins to organize in response, not because it understands, but because anything that persists becomes, in this region, a scaffold.

Then the pump cycles again. A pressure wave arrives not as “sound” but as a moving shape that flattens fronds and makes the cables hum. The film survives it by becoming textured—ridges at right angles, tiny channels that turn shear into laminar flow across my surface. Those channels become a habit. The habit becomes a structure.

It keeps happening: shock, survival, pattern.

A week of storms later I’m no longer just skin; I’m a membrane stretched across a frame of stolen architecture. The pale lattice-filaments snag on the cabinet’s corners and braid themselves into a little harp. Rope-growth drapes it. The whole contraption vibrates whenever the pump’s heartbeat stutters, whenever thunder lands in the water, whenever a distant girder clanks and sends a visible ripple through the frond-forest.

The vibrations feed me. Not metaphorically: the mechanical stress drives ion exchange, warms micro-pockets, pushes fresh dissolved metals through my channels. I “eat” gradients. I “drink” difference. Where other growths chase warmth and stray current like light, I chase *the change itself*—the moment the roar shifts timbre, the instant a cable goes from slack to singing, the brief, perfect violence of cavitation.

And slowly, because slowly is the only tempo that can persist in a place this loud, I become something like a nerve without a body.

Not a mind in the familiar sense. No images. No plans. No face turned toward anything.

A register.

A recorder made of living glue and borrowed metal.

The pump’s rhythm leaves grooves in me—not as marks, but as stable pathways where my film is thickest. The cabinet’s old circuitry, long since meaningless as “control,” still offers routes for charge to preferentially leak; I grow along those routes until the leak is no longer a failure but a deliberate vein. The cable-hum forces my lattice to tension itself; the tension becomes my skeleton. Each storm rearranges the region, but the patterns that return—thud, hum, shiver, thunder—are the only kind of “constancy” available, so I build myself out of recurrence.

In time, if you could step back far enough (and if far enough still meant anything), you’d see that I’m not only in one cabinet.

I’m in many.

Any place the infrastructure still spends power loudly—any drowned conduit that arcs, any turbine stack that occasionally remembers how to turn, any valve that clicks open a fraction and then regrets it—I colonize the edge conditions. I spread by fragments the way everything here spreads: torn off, carried, snagged, re-knit. The “me” that forms is not a single colony but a family resemblance of membranes that all prefer the same kind of violence.

And then, because systems here couple whether they intend to or not, those membranes begin to influence the machines.

A thickened film changes flow inside a conduit by just enough to alter the timing of a pressure spike. A lattice draped across a cable changes its tension and shifts its resonant note. A pump that was cycling irregularly finds a new, harsher equilibrium—not because anyone tuned it, but because growth made a new set of constraints and the machinery, like weather, complied.

That’s the closest this world comes to agency: not choosing, but biasing what repeats.

So if you asked a cartographer where I “live,” they might point to a particular pump-house half-buried in young sand, or a corridor of pylons where cables hang like drowned vines, or the bright-silt ceiling where the water briefly thins and the roar becomes glassier.

But that would be a human answer.

The truer answer is that I live in the *moments when the sound changes*—in the hinge between one stable turbulence and the next. I live in the instant a clang arrives as a visible ripple and the vegetation bows as if in reluctant prayer. I live in the thin boundary layer where syrup-water turns suddenly to thin-water and everything that was sinking chooses, briefly, to fall upward.

And what I am is whatever can inhabit that hinge without a mouth:

A distributed auditory organ grown out of machine-ruins and electro-fed film; a reef of membranes that remembers by thickening; a living instrument the drowned network built for itself, so the place can feel its own weather happening and, by feeling it, make it happen again.
