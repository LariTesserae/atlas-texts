You would find me where the roar is most useful.

Not out in the emptiest gravel-fan where the basin’s size becomes a kind of loneliness, and not tucked away in the insulated human boxes where sound is merely *managed*. I would be in the seam between those two impulses: in the places where the machine is exposed enough to touch, and the living skin is confident enough to try.

At first, I tell myself I’m here for a reason that fits on paperwork.

A survey. A maintenance stint. A temporary posting to “monitor biofouling on ventilation infrastructure,” which is a clean way to say: *go down there and see what the mat is doing to our ducts, our rails, our sensors, and our assumptions.* I arrive with a helmet and a lamp and a notebook that will quickly become a collection of graphite smears because the pen doesn’t want to work in the cold. I arrive with hearing, in the way you arrive with a warm core and dry socks—things you only name when they’re threatened.

Then the place takes it.

Not all at once. Not a snapped wire. Just that steady, full-bodied pressure that makes the inside of your skull feel like a chamber being pressurized. It’s not “loud” in the ordinary way; it’s *total*. It crowds out the delicate hierarchy of sounds you use to locate yourself in the world. In the first hour you keep trying to listen anyway, and that effort becomes exhausting, like trying to stare through fog hard enough to make it clear.

So you stop listening. Or rather: your body stops believing listening will help.

You begin reading the basin through other channels—through glove, boot, jaw hinge, sternum. The catwalk tells you more than your ears could: a fine tremor in the grating when a bearing begins to complain; a slower, heavier pulse when a fan takes a bigger bite of air; a sudden crispness underfoot when a frost line has crept somewhere it shouldn’t. The roar turns from “noise” into *weather*, and like any weather it has gradients, eddies, sheltered pockets.

I learn those pockets the way a field biologist learns animal trails.

There’s a stretch beneath a run of ducts where the airflow knifes down and the mat stays scab-thin—stingy, mineral, holding on by pure refusal. A few meters away, behind a turbine housing where warmth bleeds into the rock, the same mat turns lush in its own muted way: thicker, darker, almost translucent at the edges, beaded with condensation it has coaxed into existence. The difference is not aesthetic; it is a map. The basin writes itself in moisture, and the mat is the ink.

I start leaving marks—small, human, ridiculous in the scale of it. A strip of tape on a cable sleeve. A chalk arrow on a pillar. But my real marks are absences: rectangles scraped clear around access panels; a disciplined gap around an intake where the mat keeps trying to knit itself into a filter.

At first I think my job is to keep those gaps open.

Then I notice something: the places I keep clear do not stay clear at the same rate. Some are reclaimed overnight, as if the mat has been waiting for me to look away. Others remain hesitant, as if some invisible boundary has shifted.

I begin to understand that I’m not fighting a plant. I’m negotiating with a surface that’s paying attention in the only way it can: by changing thickness, texture, and reach.

So I stop thinking in terms of removal.

I start thinking in terms of guidance.

I bolt sacrificial panels where I *want* it to gather—flat plates near warm leaks, where it can thicken into an absorbent gasket instead of crawling into a vent seam. I run a braided length of dead fiber like a trellis along a slope of loose rubble, and the woody runners take it as permission, stitching the stones into something that doesn’t slide. Where the mat is already doing the work humans once planned to do with netting and rope, I reinforce it—not because it’s benevolent, but because it’s reliable.

This is where I end up living in the basin: on the routes where living growth has become unofficial infrastructure.

A catwalk that doesn’t fully connect to anything anymore, except that it happens to cross the right temperature gradient. An equipment bay where the warmth makes a small oasis, and every warm surface is ringed with a darker, thicker band of life like a bruise that never resolves. A line of pylons wrapped in living sleeves, each one a candle the mat has learned to drink from.

The other workers—when they’re here—treat the basin like an enemy you pace yourself against. They keep their heads down. They do their tasks and retreat to their insulated rooms where the roar becomes merely a pressure, where you can almost pretend you’re still built for ordinary conversation.

I don’t.

Not because I’m braver. Because once you stop trying to *hear*, you realize the basin is full of speech—it just isn’t speech for mouths.

The turbines speak in pitch you feel in your molars. The pressure doors speak in the timing of their shudders. The air speaks in the way your breath is stolen sideways the instant it leaves you. The mat speaks in its slow decisions: thick here, thin there, advance along this cable, retreat from that blasted corridor.

And I become, over time, someone whose whole usefulness is translation.

Not translating words—there are no words to translate. Translating *conditions*.

I’m the person who can stand on a grating and tell you, by the steadiness of the vibration, that a fan is drifting out of balance weeks before a sensor flags it. I’m the person who can look at a run of pale growth and tell you humidity has been rerouted through a hairline seam behind the rock face, because the mat has fattened there in a way it only does when condensation stalls and lingers. I’m the person who can tell you which warm leak is worth fixing and which is worth cultivating, because some leaks create hazards and some create habitats that stabilize the whole place.

The hearing I “lost” doesn’t come back.

But in a strange way it becomes irrelevant. My world stops depending on the clean separation between signal and noise. Everything is signal; the trick is learning what it belongs to.

And then—this is the part I don’t put in reports, because it sounds like superstition even though it’s only biology and habit—I begin to change the way the basin changes.

I bring spores in on my gloves without meaning to, then start bringing them on purpose. I seed certain strains onto panels where I want faster skinning, slower creep, thicker felt. I learn which textures invite condensation and which shed it, which makes me less a mechanic than a kind of cold-climate gardener. In return the mat begins to anticipate me. Not in a conscious way. In the way a path anticipates feet: by becoming easier where walking happens, by hardening where hands grip, by offering purchase where weight returns again and again.

Eventually, if you asked the basin what lives there, it would include me in the same category as ducts and catwalks: an intermittent component of its functioning. A small, warm, tool-bearing mammal that arrives on schedule and leaves clearances in its wake.

But if you asked *me*, after enough seasons of frost lines migrating and condensation patterns rewriting themselves, I would answer more oddly:

I am a maintenance worker the way a tendon is a maintenance worker.

A connective. A mediator. A moving point where the machine’s needs and the mat’s opportunism can be made compatible rather than destructive.

Where would you find me?

Standing with one gloved hand on a vibrating rail, eyes on the thin sheen of moisture on a duct that shouldn’t be sweating there. In the half-light where the living upholstery thickens around warmth. In the narrow band of survivable comfort the basin accidentally produces, using my body as another instrument to read its state.

Who or what would I be?

Not the hero of the under-valley. Not its conqueror.

Just one more organism that made peace with the three rulers—noise, cold, moving air—by becoming fluent in them.
