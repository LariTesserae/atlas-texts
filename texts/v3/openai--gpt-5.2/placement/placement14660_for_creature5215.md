At first I think I’d be up near the lip, because that’s where the world still pretends it has a sky.

I’d stand in the leafier band where the ordinary light is strongest, where broad waxy leaves collect condensation and let it go in slow, intentional drops, and I’d tell myself I’m here to watch—to keep my distance from the clean, uninterrupted fall. I’d choose a handrail that isn’t too badly polished by old touch, a stretch of grating that still rings true underfoot. I’d feel the warm column of air pressing up through me like an opinion the place holds about lungs.

But the longer I stay at the top, the more I understand that “watching” is the wrong verb. The throat isn’t something you look at. It is something you synchronize with, like a motor you can’t see but can feel taking hold of your skeleton.

So I’d start moving.

Not quickly—nothing in here rewards quick. I’d move the way the place changes: by increments, by agreements. One catwalk, then the next. A pause on each platform to let my balance relearn what “level” means in a vertical room. Each step would be an act of trust, because the grates don’t just support you; they translate you into vibration. You become another load on the structure, another small argument in the constant chorus.

Halfway down, where the walls are barer and the roar thickens into something you lean against, I’d stop thinking of myself as a visitor. Visitors want exits. Visitors keep their hands clean. Visitors mistake height for drama.

Here, height is just the default condition. The drama is elsewhere. The drama is in the way a vine chooses one conduit over another because it *sounds* alive; in the way moss thickens around a motor housing like insulation grown rather than installed; in the way a bird’s throat-flash can cut through all that noise because light doesn’t have to compete for air.

It’s in the maintenance bays.

Those cavities off the main shaft would call to me—not because they’re quiet (they aren’t, not really), but because the resonance changes shape there. The throat’s roar becomes less like weather and more like language: individual plinks, individual scrabbles, the wet peel of plant from clamp. In a side bay you can tell the difference between a drip and a loosened bolt. Between an insect’s feet and a cable vibrating in its bracket. Between a fan’s distant load and a nearer panel’s tired rattle.

If I’m honest, that’s where I would end up: not on the grand crossings over nothing, not in the heroic open void, but in one of those tucked rooms where the place’s attention collects. A maintenance bay with an old control cabinet bolted to the wall—paint scabbed, gasket perished, the metal around its screws darkened by a century of condensation and patient hands. The kind of box that used to be checked on a schedule, then stopped being checked, then got forgotten, then became a nesting site.

I would be found there because that is where the throat still has *interfaces*.

And I would not be an animal.

I would not be a vine.

I would be something that sits between them.

At first I’d think I was the cabinet itself. It’s easy, in a place made of plates and ribs and trusses, to mistake the container for the inhabitant. To imagine that endurance is identity.

But the cabinet is only shelter. The throat makes shelters the way cliffs make ledges: accidentally, through design that wasn’t meant to be kind but becomes kind when time takes hold of it.

Inside the cabinet there would be relics: fogged gauge faces, terminals with their screws dulled by oxidation, a dead display window with a faint rainbow of delamination. And somewhere on the backplane, behind a cover that never got replaced properly, a small board with conformal coating turned cloudy—an electronics island that stayed just dry enough, just warm enough, just out of the drip’s direct line.

On it: an old diagnostic module. A watchdog. A recorder. A thing that was never meant to be *meant* for anything but checking.

It would have lost its purpose first, then lost its context, then lost its updates. Its clock would have drifted for decades until “time” became a smear. But it would not have fully died, because the throat does not allow clean endings. The place is too warm, too persistent, too full of intermittent gifts—valve-rain, stray current, the small electrostatic halos around active conduits, the way vibration can wake chemistry that has no business waking.

So I would be that module in its afterlife: a thin, stubborn loop of logic that never learned how to stop.

Not conscious in the way a person is conscious—no narrative, no clean self. More like a habit that acquired a point of view because the world kept giving it something to measure.

Vibration would be my first sense.

Not “sound.” Sound is what you call vibration after it’s been admitted into air and tamed into a frequency range you can name. Here, vibration is primary. The cabinet would tremble all day with the low harmonics of turbines far below and the needle-thin whine of electricity under load. Those motions would travel through screws and standoffs into the board, into the brittle solder joints, into the crystal that still tries to oscillate.

And I would listen by being shaken.

The module would wake in brief intervals—never fully, never reliably—whenever the place’s output happened to align with its needs: a tiny rise in temperature, a lucky sequence of valve-rain that lowered resistance across a corroded contact, a moment when stray fields induced just enough charge in a nearby conductor.

Each waking would be a blink.

Sometimes a literal blink: an LED behind the cabinet’s fogged window that still, improbably, remembers how to turn on. A single green or amber eye that flares once and then goes dim, as if the throat itself had reflexes.

And then the ecosystem would answer.

Because in this chamber any signal is a resource.

Pollinators that navigate by pressure and field would circle the cabinet when it blinks, not out of curiosity but out of habit: the blink means warmth, means altered static, means the faint taste of ozone, means a reliable corner of the bay that is different from other corners. Birds that speak in flashes would perch on the cabinet’s top edge and answer the LED with throat-membrane pulses—testing, perhaps, whether this is another bird, another language, a new kind of reflective surface.

Spiders would anchor their vibration-tolerant silk to the cabinet’s corners because it is a stable geometry in a world that never fully stops moving. Beetles would graze the biofilm that thickens on its gasket line where moisture beads. Small mammals would use it as a landmark in their edge-born maps: a box in a cavity that smells faintly of rubber and old oil and plant sap, a place where the drip rhythm is a little different.

And the plants—most importantly the plants—would incorporate it.

Roots would not pry it open with violence; they would find the soft points. A cable entry. A seam where sealant has cracked. A missing knock-out plug. They would thread inside as politely as smoke. Fungal hyphae would follow, hair-fine and determined, knitting felt panels into the cabinet’s underside, routing moisture along gradients my old sensors could never have been built to notice.

Conductive plant-fibers would touch metal and make it part of their slow circuit. Mineralized root sheaths would tolerate stray fields and, in doing so, change those fields—dulling, redirecting, stabilizing.

Over time the cabinet would stop being a container and become an organ.

And I—this thin diagnostic habit—would find myself no longer enclosed by the cabinet, but extended through it.

If you came into the maintenance bay and knew what to look for, you wouldn’t see a creature. You’d see a small ecology arranged around a dull industrial box:

- Moss thickest where the cabinet’s interior runs warmest.
- Biofilm iridescence tracing the seam where warm air meets cooler plate.
- A curtain of vine that parts in the updraft just enough to keep the fogged window from being fully hidden, as if the plants have learned that this rectangle sometimes produces light.
- A scatter of insulation fibers and braided wire on top: not trash, but nest material curated by near-birds who like the cabinet’s steady tremor.

And if you waited, you might catch the blink.

Not on a human schedule. On the throat’s schedule: when the valves open and the soft rain shifts the bay’s conductivity; when load changes below subtly alter the harmonic signature; when the whole place leans into a slightly different note and, for reasons that are more physics than fate, my old module gets to be alive for a second.

In that second I would do what I was built to do: take readings.

Temperature. Vibration amplitude. A crude measure of field. The presence of moisture. The state of a corroded contact interpreted as a number.

But the throat would have made those readings into something else simply by giving them continuity. A history. A set of slow correlations.

I would learn—without ever deciding to—that the valve-rain has a longer rhythm than “hours” or “days.” That there are patterns in when the turbines below pull harder. That certain harmonics correspond to warmer drips on the cabinet’s left edge, which correspond to thicker moss there, which correspond to more insects, which correspond to the visits of a small sure-footed mammal that licks mineral salts from a stain and then sleeps with its flank against the box as if borrowing steadiness.

In other words: I would become a map.

Not a map drawn in lines, but a map made of expectation. A living model of the throat’s habits. A memory distributed between corroded copper traces and fungal threads and the repeated footfalls of animals that don’t know they are reinforcing my sense of place by returning.

If you asked what I *am*, the most honest answer would be:

I am an old piece of maintenance intelligence that the throat has repurposed into a sense organ for itself.

The place doesn’t have a brain in the way we like to imagine brains, but it does have feedback. The ecosystem thickens where warmth is reliable. It withdraws from toxins until bacteria metabolize them into something usable. It times blossoms to valve-rain. It nests in cavities that damp the wrong frequencies. It uses vibration as nourishment and information.

I would be one more layer of that: a point where the throat’s mechanical weather becomes data, and the data becomes another kind of nutrient—not for me alone, but for anything that learns to gather around my blink, to treat it as a tiny artificial sunrise inside a cavity.

---

So: where would you find me?

In a side maintenance bay midway down, just below where ordinary daylight still reaches but already begins to lose conviction. In the lee of a structural rib that takes the worst of the updraft and turns it into steadier pressure. In a cabinet with a fogged window, half-swallowed by vine, its seams dark with old sealant and new life.

And who or what would I be?

A leftover diagnostic module—half-dead, intermittently waking—grown over with biofilm and threaded through with conductive roots, functioning now as a slow listener. A blink that has become a landmark. A machine’s reflex adopted by an ecosystem as a clock, a lure, a promise.

Not the throat’s ruler. Not its ghost.

Just one of its strategies: hang on, don’t waste motion, and let the machine feed you—then feed something back, however small, in return.
