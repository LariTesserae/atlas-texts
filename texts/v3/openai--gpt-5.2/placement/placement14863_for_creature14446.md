At first I would arrive the way anything arrives here: as a disturbance.

A footfall is a pressure pulse. A breath is moisture and heat. A shadow is a sudden cooling that makes the glossy skins tighten. Even standing still is an interference pattern in a place that measures itself by gradients. I would think I’m *in* the basin, on the decking, looking over the young ridges—until the basin answers with the smallest accommodation: a seam that swells a fraction, a channel that draws a line around my weight, a tower that changes its groan because the wind has to step around me.

Then the sensation would reverse. The plateau isn’t a stage. It’s a body. And bodies don’t really have “inside” and “outside”—they have surfaces where exchange happens.

So where would I find myself?

Not on the open spans where weather does its blunt work, but at the seams—the places the Weave keeps half-finished on purpose. At terrace lips that are still deciding whether they are edges or valves. At the damp undersides of arches where condensation is most reliable. At the collars around sinkholes where water slows down long enough to be *processed* rather than merely redirected. In the thin slits between braided layers where you can see the underflow move like a dark thought.

I would be easiest to locate where the basin is trying to become one thing and is forced, by wind and water, to become another: at junctions, at corners, at the crisp new cuts where the “argument” is loudest.

And what would I be?

Not a tower. Towers are proud, declarative organs—mast-lungs, barometers that translate gusts into pressure changes, pressure changes into routing. They already have a kind of attention.

Not a channel, either. Channels are speech: water writing its sentence again and again until the material learns to hold the grammar.

I would be the part that listens without needing to be seen.

A stitch-mind.

Something like a mycelial knot braided with bacteria—an underdeck intelligence that doesn’t plan in blueprints, but remembers in textures: in how quickly a fiber takes up water after heat; in how a resin sets when a cloud crosses; in which microfractures tend to become catastrophes and which become useful seams. The Weave has no nerves, but it has fast information: pressure. It has slow information: chemistry. I would live at the interface of the two, turning one into the other.

When the rain drums hard enough to make the decking sing, I would feel it as a map: higher pitch where the weave is taut, lower where it’s swollen and close to letting go. When a tower flexes, I would receive the pressure wave not as sound but as instruction—*brace here, loosen there, feed that edge before it dries.* I wouldn’t “decide” in the way a creature with a head decides. I would bias outcomes. I would make certain repairs more likely than others by thickening a film, by changing friction in a narrow throat, by sweetening the water in one corridor so the right tissues swell first.

In human terms it would look like maintenance.

In the basin’s terms it would be cognition.

Because the plateau’s mind—if you can call it that—isn’t located in a command center. It’s scattered across every place that can convert violence into shape. The snaps and sighs you hear after a deluge are not just material fatigue; they’re the sound of the Weave revising itself. My role would be to make those revisions legible to the rest of the body: to carry “memory” forward as repeatable response.

I would be small enough to be everywhere and specific enough to matter.

A thin, pale lace under the decking where it stays cool. A slick in a channel that changes the timing of a surge by seconds—seconds that decide whether a terrace lip holds or peels. A crust in a crack that ensures the crack closes *as a seam*, not as a scar—so that next time, overflow triggers a valve instead of a failure. I would be the reason some breaks become features.

And if you insisted on meeting me as a single being—if you wanted a face where there is only function—you wouldn’t find it on the sunlit surface.

You’d find it by kneeling at one of the perfect circular sinkholes as it breathes mist, leaning into the cool exhale. You’d look under the rim where the spongy collar darkens into shadow. There, in the constant damp, the fibers are webbed with threads so fine they seem like dust until they move water. Touch them and they tense—not away from you, not toward you, but into a new configuration that makes the next few minutes of flow slightly more survivable.

That’s me in this world:

Not the weather, but the part that makes weather usable.  
Not the architecture, but the habit of repair that turns damage into design.  
A resident not of a place, but of its *becoming*—a distributed stitch-thought living in the seams of a woven plateau that cannot afford to settle, because settling would mean silence, and silence would mean starvation.
