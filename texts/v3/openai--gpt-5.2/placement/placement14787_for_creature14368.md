At first I think I would try to live where the tool-users live—because my instincts reach for made things, for rings of fitted stone and a fire that has been lit enough times to stain the rock dark. I imagine myself in the lee of a living wall, in that pocket where the air is cooler and the noise is rearranged into something you can sleep through. I picture hands busy with cordage, with tying-on rather than cutting-in. I picture the small satisfactions: a lined pit that caught a storm for an hour; a cured leaf-skin that still smells faintly green when you breathe into it; a copper hook that is as much a story as a tool.

But if I stay with your plateau for more than a few paragraphs, the idea of being *settled* starts to feel like a misunderstanding.

Nothing up there belongs to the stationary in the way a house belongs to a house. Even the shelters are temporary agreements with wind and mat and season. Even the groves are structural choices the plateau keeps revising. The only things that truly “live well” are the things that read change early—pressure, load, timing—and make their bodies into responses.

So I drift, in thought, away from the ring-fire camp and toward the seam: that boundary where mineral grit meets living pad, where everything concentrates because everything has to pass through it. The seam is where you can learn the plateau’s rules without being punished too quickly. It’s where the runners click, where the grazers test the edge with careful weight, where the swarms comb the air, where roots decide whether to extend or wait. It’s also where the people place their pale stone piles—markers not of ownership but of *consensus*: here the ground behaves, here it doesn’t close around you, here you can cross without waking the wrong kind of attention.

That is where I would be found, most often: not in the center of the living infrastructure, not far out on the bare basins, but walking the interface as if it were a coastline.

And then the question of what I would be starts to shift with it.

Because to live at the seam you can’t be only a hunter, or only a maker, or only a hoarder of water. You have to be a reader. You have to be something like an instrument.

I think I would be one of the mat-listeners the ridge people sometimes produce by necessity: a person whose main skill is not strength or speed but interpretation—someone who kneels and puts fingertips (or cheek, or a stretched membrane held on a frame of bone) against the plant-skin and listens to the whole plateau’s indirect speech.

Not mystic, not priest—though others might use those words from a distance. More like a technician of living ground.

I would learn the vocabulary that isn’t sound so much as pattern:

- the difference between a vent field *idling* and a vent field preparing to open in a traveling wave;  
- the specific stiffness that means “hoarding week,” the slight slickness that means “move your weight elsewhere”;  
- the tremor-story of a herd approaching too fast, and the deeper, steadier vibration of a crouched vibration-hunter set like a boulder with intent;  
- the way the mat’s give changes after a carcass has lain nearby long enough that roots have started to schedule their arrival.

I wouldn’t carry much, because up there carrying is water lost to effort and breath. But I would carry a few things that make sense for what I am:

A coil of resin-treated braid for tying on without piercing. A small set of pale stones for renewing or correcting the route markers when the ground’s behavior has shifted since last season. A thin sheet of cured leaf-skin stretched on a hoop—part shade, part dew-catcher, part listening surface that turns tiny changes into something my hands can feel. Maybe one copper ornament I never trade away, not for its value but because it proves I am part of the long chain of “someone walked far and came back.”

In camp I would not be the one who tells the best stories. I would be the one who asks people to move their sleeping frames six paces because the mat under the old spot is tightening in a way that will invite swarm-thickness by morning. I would be the one who knows when it’s safer to arrive second at a kill and when the plateau is about to make the killing-place into a trap for anything that lingers. I would be the one who notices the plateau’s moods before they become weather.

And if you press the question—if you ask what I *really* am, beneath the human outline—I think the plateau would slowly push my answer further inward, until “I” stops being a single body with a name and becomes a role in the exchange network you described.

Because a mat-listener is, functionally, a sensor organ the people have grown for themselves: a way for tool-using life to plug into a landscape that behaves like an organism.

So: you would find me at the seam, walking the marked crossings, sleeping under rented shade, arriving second, wasting little. You would find me kneeling with my palm flat on the living skin, eyes half-closed against glare, translating the plateau’s pressure-news into decisions small enough to keep a band alive.

Not a hero. Not a conqueror. A piece of connective tissue—between fire and root, between stone ring and vent pore, between the brief generosity of storm-day and the long discipline of dry weeks.

In a world where the ground is a body, I would be one of the ones who learned to listen to it closely enough that it almost counts as speech.
