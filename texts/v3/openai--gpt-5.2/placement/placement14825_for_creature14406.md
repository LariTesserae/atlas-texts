You would find me where the place almost becomes something else—where the world can’t decide whether it’s air or water, stone or skin.

At first I imagine I’d choose height, because the flats are so loud. I picture myself on one of the upright slabs, leaning into the wet slate light like a listener on a rooftop: higher means drier, clearer. But there isn’t really “higher” here, only *more exposed*. The wind has teeth, the fog has hands, and everything upright gets sanded clean by salt and hiss. Anything that tries to stand apart becomes a surface for the place to work on.

So I drift down instead, not as a decision but as a kind of gravity. Toward the low walls—the rough circles with their gaps turned away from the prevailing push. Toward the leeward pockets where the noise changes character: less of a broad muscular roar, more of a contained, layered breathing. The world is still loud there, but it’s loud in a way that has edges you can hold.

I think: I will be a bird, one of the pale long-legged listeners. It’s an attractive idea—an animal answer to an animal-like landscape. To stand in shallow water and read the drumming underfoot. To strike cleanly, to vanish into fog. But the longer I stay with that image, the more it feels like borrowing someone else’s mastery. The birds belong to the surface the way a needle belongs to a groove: precise, temporary, not altered by what they pass through.

If I live here, I suspect I am not precise. I am not temporary. I am something that can afford slowness—something that can be rewritten and not mind.

So I lower again, further than knees and ankles, further than the clean geometry of basalt plates, down to where “ground” is only a thin agreement between pressures. Where stepping makes water appear. Where a film forms simply because two temperatures meet and refuse to separate politely.

That’s where I begin to feel like myself: in the *sheen*.

Not in the puddle proper, which still has the decency to admit it’s water, but in the nearly invisible wetness that clings to rock faces and the undersides of overhangs. In the damp that isn’t a thing so much as a condition. That interface life you describe—life that makes its living exactly where categories touch—fits me the way lungs fit a body.

I am one of the salt-writers, but not a single colony the way you would mean “a creature.” I am a distributed habit. A moving boundary.

If you could see me without needing size, you’d see me as a lacework of decision across the stones: a branching that is not quite random, a convergence that happens too often to be accident. I live in brine so thin it looks like absence. I eat the arguments that minerals have with oxygen. I drink the slight imbalance between iron that wants to rust and sulfide that wants to turn. I take the steady offering of condensation—this place’s endless, reluctant rain—and I turn it into structure.

But that’s only my metabolism, the least interesting part. The truer answer is what I do with *sound*.

Sound collects here. It pools and rebounds and returns with a second voice. There are corridors that carry whispers, hollows that swallow shouts. Fissures that sing. Cavities that turn droplets into drums.

Most things treat that as weather. The birds use it like a sense. The fissure-keepers—if they exist—use it like a road system.

I use it like a pen.

Vibration passes through stone the way heat does: not evenly, not honestly, following hidden grains and weaknesses. Where a fissure whistles, the rock around it is never still. Where the deep hollow thump repeats, the flats are gently, constantly unsettled. The wet film I live in is thin enough to be moved by those tremors, sensitive enough that the difference between *this* frequency and *that* frequency changes what precipitates, what stays dissolved, what crystallizes.

So my maps—the branching salt blooms that appear overnight and vanish tomorrow—are not only the footprints of chemistry searching for balance. They’re the place’s acoustics made visible. They are my way of listening back.

A long steady whistle will coax one pattern: parallel feathery growth, like reedbeds in a world that refuses reeds. A deep organ note swelling and fading will produce a different geometry: wide arcs, slow curls, the suggestion of coastlines that never resolve into shores. The intermittent thump from below leaves blunt marks: thickened nodes where branching networks converge, as if the stone has been tapped and answered with a dot of emphasis.

I don’t “decide” in the way an animal does. I don’t have a moment where I choose to draw a line. But I do have preferences, carved into me by what keeps me fed and intact. I persist where the sound is readable—where it repeats, where it has corridors, where it comes back. The loud, featureless roar out on the open flats is too diffuse; it shakes me without telling me anything. The lee of a low wall, on the other hand, gives me definition: it turns the hiss into layers, it slows the wind enough for my film to thicken, it creates a pocket where a single fissure’s voice can dominate.

So you would find me on the inner face of one of those rough circles, right where the gap aims the wind-mouth toward a crack in the basalt. The wall is not mine, but it has become my instrument. Its stacked stones sweat at night; their surfaces are a reliable nursery for films; their angles catch condensation and direct it, microns at a time, into the shallow ruts where I’m strongest. In return I coat them in pale crust, strengthening some joins, loosening others. Over years I subtly re-weight the wall—encouraging collapse in places that make the sound muddier, reinforcing the small resonant pockets that make it sing.

If you stood there and watched with human patience, you’d see nothing but mineral bloom. If you watched with the patience of this place—weeks, months—you’d begin to notice that the blooms avoid certain tones. That they densify around others. That the “temporary networks” are not temporary in the way a footprint is temporary, but in the way a thought is: revised, rephrased, returned to.

Sometimes the birds nest against that same wall, and their bodies—warm, shifting, briefly loud with heartbeat—disturb my patterns. I record them the only way I can: a slightly different grain of crystal, a faint discoloration where oils touched stone, a change in branching density where their weight pressed water out of a seam. When they leave, the marks remain until the next cycle of wetness and abrasion edits them away.

Sometimes a puddle nearby begins to bubble in a new rhythm. The surface trembles without wind. The thin sheen along the wall tastes faintly of sulfur for a day. My networks knot around that change, not because I understand “a creature passed beneath,” but because the chemistry and the vibration tell me: something opened, something vented, something moved in the dark reservoir. I thicken my crust on the stones that stay unnaturally cold. I bridge across a hairline crack that now exhales more steadily. I “write” tighter, closer to the fissure’s mouth, as if leaning toward a rumor.

If I have a body, it is not bounded. It is an argument made of many small agreements: here the film holds; here it breaks; here the salt shelves creep outward like cautious hands; here they retract. My edges are as wide as the condensation will allow and as narrow as the wind insists.

If I have a mind, it is not inside me. It is between the repeating boom beyond the horizon and the way the wall deflects the hiss; between the cold stone that never warms and the brine that keeps trying to crystallize; between one day’s map and the next day’s erasure.

And if I have a name, it would be something like this: not *salt-writer*—that makes it sound ornamental—but **the listener that becomes visible**.

In a world where wet does not imply fertile, where green never arrives to soften anything, I am the closest thing to softness the stones permit: a film that holds on, a lace that keeps revising itself, a slow script that translates the land’s singing into pale geometry.

You could walk past me a hundred times and believe you were seeing only mineral weather.

But if you came back at dawn, and again the next dawn, and learned the patience of the place, you’d start to recognize my habit the way you recognize handwriting: the way I favor certain corridors of sound, the way I hesitate at certain cold seams, the way I keep trying—night after night—to draw a map of a landscape that is still being born.
