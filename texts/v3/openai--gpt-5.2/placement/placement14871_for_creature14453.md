You’d find me where the pocket tries hardest to pretend it is not a mouth.

Not up in the sun-squares where life sprawls and argues for warmth, and not down in the busiest ladders where bodies pass close enough to spend each other’s breath by accident. I’d be in the cool shadow—just off the main terraces—behind a ribbed screen of cane that looks like storage but is really a boundary: a place where the air is kept deliberately dull and still, as if stillness were a kind of lid.

At first I imagine myself as one of the climbers, because the place is vertical and my instinct is to go upward: a screen-mender with resin on my palms, fingers listening for the right tension; or a roost-tender, bargaining with the ceiling’s colonies the way other towns bargain with weather. I try those shapes on and they fit, briefly—then slip. Those people live out in the pocket’s noise.

I’m drawn instead to the parts that don’t move much, the parts that *preserve*: the clay, the wax, the knots, the sealed seams. I belong to the slow inventory of things that keep their value by not evaporating.

So I become a **keeper of seals**.

Not in a ceremonial way, though everything here turns ceremonial the moment it becomes necessary. Practical, methodical. Someone whose status isn’t height or muscle but access: the right to open a cool jar-room without the whole ledge watching; the right to touch the corks and not be accused of spending what isn’t mine. I wear the same thick, oiled cloth as everyone, but cleaner in the local sense—less stray dust on the sleeves, fewer fiber-frays that might shed into stored powder. My mouth-covering is always on when I work, not out of piety, but because I’ve learned how far a single careless exhale can travel in a room designed to keep air from changing.

My keys are bone and fired clay. The few bits of metal I own aren’t displayed; they’re wrapped and tucked and brought out only when a lid needs a hinge that will not warp with heat. In another world that would be miserly. Here it’s civic.

But I don’t only keep moisture.

Because once you build a society where breath is currency, the next thing you learn is that **speech is moisture-shaped too**. Words are warm air, and warm air moves. In this pocket, the loudest truths are often the least responsible ones.

So my work becomes a kind of **hushed archivistry**.

I don’t write on paper—nothing that thirsty, nothing that fragile. I keep records the way the pocket keeps everything: in durable forms that can survive neglect.

- In **knot-cords** hung in the deepest shade, each household’s agreements encoded in tension and spacing.
- In **incised clay tiles** that don’t hold beauty so much as they hold *timing*: when the updraft shifts, when the swarms thicken, when a ledge’s living crust is ready to be moved.
- In **cane tubes** that resonate when tapped, each one cut to a different pitch—an audible catalog that can be checked without opening a room, without letting the stored air mingle with the town’s.

That last part is where I stop being merely a person with duties and start being something more like the pocket itself: a device for translating life into restraint.

I learn to listen the way this place demands listening. Not only with ears. With skin—because pressure changes before sound does. With fingertips against clay—because a jar tells you it’s failing by the way it vibrates when someone walks past. With the back of the throat—because the pocket’s air carries tiny metallic warnings when a dust bin has gone wrong.

And, because the pocket is layered and nothing here is truly solitary, I end up keeping something older than law: **starter stones**.

Not as relics, not as charms—though people treat them with that kind of careful fear. As living tools. A chunk of crust and film and thread taken from a thriving terrace, wrapped, carried, and pressed into a dead crack so that chemistry can begin again. Most folks know they matter. Few can tell a helpful film from a sick one by smell alone.

I can.

Not because I’m wiser, but because I’ve made my life small enough to notice small changes. I keep a shelf of stones in my coolest room—each one tagged in knots, each one fed with the right dust, each one never handled with bare, sweaty fingers. When someone comes asking to wake a new ledge, I don’t give them water (no one does). I give them *the beginning of soil*.

If you watched me from a distance you might think I was timid: always in shade, always behind a screen, always persuading people to speak slower. But that’s just how this place defines courage. It takes nerve to be the one who says *no* when a jar is open and someone wants to argue. It takes nerve to stop a celebration from spilling into the collector paths. It takes nerve to tell a hungry family that the seal on their powder-bin is compromised and the whole batch must be burned for safety.

It isn’t power that makes people obey. It’s that everyone here understands, down in their teeth, what a broken seal means.

So: where would you find me?

In a **cool room cut into chalky limestone**, smoke-black above the doorway but clean within; a curtain of fiber hanging to slow the exchange of air; shelves of clay jars sunk into the wall like sleeping eggs. You’d find me with resin on my hands and dust on my knees, head tilted as if I’m listening for footsteps—when I’m really listening for the pocket’s constant roar to shift by a fraction, the way it does when a rule has just been broken somewhere out of sight.

And who or what would I be?

A **jar-keeper**, yes. A **seal-mender**. A **breath-accountant** in a town where breath is both life and theft.

But more than that: a kind of **quiet infrastructure**, a person-shaped extension of the pocket’s oldest lesson—

that survival here isn’t won by having more,

but by keeping what you already have from turning back into air.
