I would start by trying to be a visitor.

I would tell myself I’m only passing through—up from the lower valleys with a trade bundle wrapped in waxed leaf and a hood that’s already losing the fight against mist. I would stand in one of those courtyards where a pool holds a piece of sky, and I would do what outsiders always do in a place like this: stare too long at the way the town moves without ever *moving away*. Water sliding, vines tightening, people rerouting themselves around a sagging mat as naturally as blood reroutes around a bruise.

But the longer I stood there, the more obvious it would become that “passing through” is not a stable state in a city built out of living agreements. Everything that’s here is here because it has been *kept*—and the keeping is not a job you can watch without being recruited.

At first, I’d imagine myself among the visible trades.

A water-keeper, maybe: someone who can read the slope’s moods, who knows which roots to wake open and which to press shut; who carries a flat blade and a small pry wedge and spends their days listening for the pitch-change in a channel when silt starts to choke it. That appeals—the work is logic you can hear, and consequences arrive quickly enough to teach you.

Or a fiber-worker: seated with others under a sweating green tunnel, hands moving faster than thought, turning wet plant into tensile certainty. There is a kind of language in that—twist, fold, bind; repeat until the town can trust its own weight. And I’ve always been drawn to systems where strength is made out of small, correct motions.

Or a rootwright: the one who walks as if the whole place is a net they can feel through their soles. Not a builder in the way a wall-builder is, but a tuner. Someone who plucks a vine-cable like a harp string and hears fatigue. Someone who can glance at a trunk’s old brace-scars and know what shape it was persuaded into, and what it wants to become if left to its own slow insistence.

I would try each of those roles on like a cloak. I would picture the tools at my belt, the stains on my hands, the posture that tells others what I’m for.

And then the town would do what it always does: it would correct the story until it fit.

Because what I am best suited to here isn’t the cutting or the carrying. It’s the *remembering that keeps the cutting from being reckless*. It’s the part of the town that is neither plant nor person, and yet lives as surely as anything that breathes—the habit that prevents forgetting from turning into collapse.

So you’d find me where the pathways braid most tightly: not in the market’s loud center exactly, but a half-level above it, where several corridors converge over a water-ladder and everyone has to slow down. A place with a railing-vine polished by hands; a place where you can hear the bridge hum and the channel’s burble and the herd-pen’s restless shifting all at once, layered like instruments.

There, I would be the one people come to when they are about to make a change.

Not an authority with a staff. Not a priest. Not a ruler. Something more practical and more intimate:

A **knot-keeper**.

A keeper of the town’s decisions in cord and pattern.

In a stone city you can carve a mark into a lintel and call it history. Here, history rots unless you give it a body that can survive damp. So the town keeps its memory the way it keeps everything else: as fiber under tension, as bindings that can be inspected, retied, inherited.

I would have a set of cords—thick, thin, dyed with tannin-dark, sap-pale, ash-gray—each one tied into sequences that mean things only locals need to mean:

- a certain knot spacing for which donor plants are marked this season,
- a twist pattern for which roof-tunnels are sweating too steadily (a sign of blocked vent reeds),
- a braided splice that notes the last big slide and how the water chose its new anger.

People would bring me disputes that aren’t really disputes, just two truths rubbing until one frays: *You can’t cut that vine, it holds my platform.* / *You must cut that vine, it’s strangling the water-gate.* And I would listen, then reach for the cords, not to impose an answer but to show the town its own prior promises: who bound what to whom, and when, and under what weather.

I would not be neutral—no one here is. I would be aligned with continuity. With load-bearing kindness. With the idea that a living floor is still a floor, and that floors deserve respect.

Over time, the role would seep further into me. That’s what damp does: it enters. The longer you live in saturation, the less you can pretend you are separate from what holds you up.

I would learn to read not just the knots but the *sounds* around them: the different tone a corridor makes when the root-mat beneath is overfull; the way a vine’s hum goes slightly flat when its anchor trunk is drinking too hard and swelling; the sudden absence in insect-noise that sometimes comes before a heavy thing tests the outer hedge.

I would become, in a quiet way, another piece of the town’s infrastructure: a junction box for attention.

And if you asked the people who knew the place best what I was, they might not point at my body first. They might gesture instead at the small coil of spare cord always at my wrist, the way others carry breath.

They might say: *That one keeps the tension true.*

If you asked the town itself—if a town made of trained plants and water-law and practiced commotion could answer—it would say something less tidy:

That I am one more binding in the stacked agreement.  
A strand that doesn’t hold weight directly, but keeps the holders from being cut by mistake.  
A memory you can touch with wet fingers.  
A voice loud enough to be heard over rain, but shaped like a knot: compact, repeatable, meant to be passed on.

So: you’d find me at the convergence of paths, where water and people both have to choose a direction.

And I would be what truly reproduces here as reliably as moss and insects and root-runners—

I would be **attention, trained into a craft**.
