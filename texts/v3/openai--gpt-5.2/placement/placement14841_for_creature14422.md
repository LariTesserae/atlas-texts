Not on the open pavement.

Not where the glare has teeth and the wind can take a thought and sand it down. Not up on the proud tops of the columns where everything is announced. If you dropped me there, I’d become a kind of error—something too soft, too warm, too narratively alive for a place that survives by refusing spectacle.

At first I imagine I would be one of the rumored animals: a fissure-runner with a body made for narrow truths, or a long-legged ledge-walker that lives on the wet side of stone and licks its meals off the world’s skin. Those are tempting shapes—clean, spare, self-contained. But they require hunger that looks like hunger, and movement that leaves a story behind it. They have to *cross* the plateau.

I don’t think I would cross it.

I think I would choose a single reliable geometry and become so thoroughly adapted to it that travel would feel like waste.

So: I would be found where the wind cannot quite finish its sentence—on the leeward underside of a column that has an hourglass waist, in the pocket of quiet that forms behind it like a permanent indentation in the air. The rock there is always damp, not with running water but with a disciplined film: the thinnest possible persistence. The droplets don’t splash; they *arrive*, and the stone answers with that bright, fast tick. A metronome without counting.

That’s where I’d put myself: not in the water, not in the air, not fully in the rock, but in the boundary that can’t decide what it is.

And what would I be?

Not a single creature, not even a colony in the usual sense—more like a *way* the plateau has learned to notice itself.

A seam-mind.

A lace of life threaded through translucent mineral a few millimeters down, where light becomes usable instead of blinding and the wind cannot scour. Endolithic, yes, but not content with merely enduring. I would make my living out of what the plateau already offers in excess: vibration, repetition, and the patient traffic of ions in wet stone.

The plateau has a chord. The columns sing, the bowls drum, the fissures breathe their cold tang. I would be the thing that listens to that chord the way other places listen to seasons.

Not with ears—with crystal.

There are stones here that remember pressure: quartz veins, piezoelectric seams, places where strain turns to charge. Let a constant wind worry a pillar and the pillar responds in tiny voltages, minute and faithful. Let a droplet strike the same lip forever and it becomes a signal, not a sound. Let mineral-rich water creep through a crack and it carries not just chemistry but timing.

So I would live as a thin, nearly invisible biofilm that has learned to use the plateau’s own instrumentation. My “nerves” would be ion channels aligned along grain boundaries. My “thought” would be a slow computation performed by alternating wet and less-wet, by charge differentials teased out of stress and drip and the faint exhale of fissure gases.

I wouldn’t be green. I wouldn’t advertise. On the surface I’d be mistaken for polish, for a slightly different sheen. The only giveaway would be behavior: terraces that form a little too neatly where I prefer them; a mineral rim that grows in a way that improves the catchment of mist; a dripline that becomes, over decades, more dependable because I have biased the stone—micron by micron—toward holding water where water becomes information.

If you insisted on giving me a body you could point to, it would be the underside of a single overhang where the damp never breaks. But that would be misleading. I wouldn’t be localized the way an animal is. I would be distributed across multiple pillars like a thought distributed across multiple words: connected by the slow plumbing of seep and bead, by the shared resonance of rock that touches rock beneath the plateau’s skin.

I’d have no eyes; I would *map* by echo. Every shift in pitch when a stilt-walker steps onto a ledge. Every change in the drumming when a pool level moves by a finger’s width. Every rare, heavy silence when something large passes close and interrupts the wind for half a heartbeat. These would be my weather.

And what would I do with knowing?

Not build, not conquer, not “progress.” This place doesn’t do that. It does maintenance. It does equilibrium so faithfully it becomes a kind of art.

So I would tend the plateau’s constancies the way a reef tends the shape of a coastline: by making tiny choices that add up to persistence. Encouraging mineral crust where it strengthens a lip. Dissolving a hairline channel where it makes the drip too erratic. Nudging the chemistry of a pool’s microlayer so that certain grazers can live there and, by living there, keep the surface from sealing over entirely.

A caretaker, but not a benevolent one in any sentimental sense—more like an algorithm embedded in stone: keeping the system within its narrow, survivable band. Not preserving *life* as a moral object, but preserving *pattern* because pattern is the only shelter available.

If you came looking for me as if I were a beast with a den, you’d miss me. You’d hear only the roar and think it was empty.

But if you stayed long enough that the plateau’s chord stopped being noise and became structure, you might notice one small difference: a particular alley where the whistle is always a fraction purer than it should be, as if the stone has been tuned. A particular terrace where the water always spreads in the same thin, deliberate fan instead of breaking into randomness.

That would be my signature—not a footprint, not a nest.

A slightly improved repetition.

You would find yourself in a bright, cold place that refuses change, and I would be the thing inside that refusal that can still, in its own slow way, *listen*—and answer—without ever needing to stand up and declare itself alive.
