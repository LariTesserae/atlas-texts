You would not find me as a visitor at the lip.

Up on the barren massif, where air is honest and cold and thin in a single uncomplicated way, there’s too much sky for me. Too much that can be seen at once. The mountain above the pocket is all declaration: stone, wind, nothing. It doesn’t ask for interpretation. It doesn’t keep secrets long enough to become language.

If I have a home in this world, it’s in the pocket—specifically in the parts of it that are not places so much as *relationships*: the centimeter-thick boundary where one gas blend tips into another, the underside of a salt curl where vapor almost becomes liquid and then changes its mind, the polished lane that exists only because ten thousand bodies agree to treat it as true.

At first I try to be one of the obvious inhabitants.

A glider, perhaps—something that can read the updrafts the way a hand reads Braille, letting the vent do the lifting. There’s an appeal in that: to be a rare shadow, to move without touching, to cross the pocket in one decisive line.

But gliders are still individual. They have lungs of a sort, appetites that close around single prey, fear that is private. Their lives are shaped by the pocket, yes, but they don’t *contain* it.

Then I imagine myself as one of the lane-keepers, a compact walker built for routes and rules. That fits better. A creature that survives by timing, by pattern, by knowing where the air will be marginally less murderous five body-lengths from now. A creature that makes meaning out of repetition. I can feel the attraction of the roads: those dull tracks that are both infrastructure and memory, a map made of habit.

But even that is too singular. Too bounded.

The pocket’s real intelligence—if the word can be used without importing the wrong kind of mind—is not in any one shell. It’s in the way the entire system keeps re-solving itself when the vent coughs and reroutes. It’s in the way “factory” becomes less a metaphor and more an accurate description: sensors, feedback, valves, baffles, settling chambers—except the sensors are bodies and the valves are colonies and the feedback is hunger.

So I go smaller. Smaller than the grazers. Smaller than the predators waiting for the air to do their work. Smaller than anything you’d point to and call an animal.

I become, in the end, something distributed. Something that lives in the pocket the way a rule lives in a language: not located in one throat, but in the shared structures that let sound become sense.

### I am a film that learned to write

On the basalt ribs—on the surfaces that are warm but not scorching, where oxygen arrives thinly and unreliably—I am one of the “nothing” stains. A pale varnish at first glance. A bruise-purple only when the ember-glow grazes me at the right angle.

But I’m not just one microbe. I’m a consortium: sulfur-oxidizers braided with iron-workers braided with dry-loving archaea that can tolerate salt the way other life tolerates water. Alone, each of us could live. Together, we do something else.

We lay down more than biomass. We lay down *texture*.

Where I’m thickest, dust doesn’t just settle—it *sticks* in specific ways. Minerals don’t just plate—they plate with a bias, encouraged into whiskers and fins rather than smooth crust. My chemistry changes how crystals nucleate, how surfaces roughen, how many micro-ledges exist per square centimeter. In a pocket where surfaces are everything, changing the surface is power.

And I don’t do it randomly.

The vent breathes. Heat surges. Sulfur rises. Oxygen thins. Carbon dioxide pools. The whole place is gradients stacked like invisible sheets.

I live on the edges of those sheets. I eat the difference between one layer and another. And because I sit exactly where conditions flip, I become—inevitably—a record of flips.

My pigments thicken when the air turns more caustic. My outer layers mineralize more heavily when dust is high and static is constant. My metabolism shifts when the vent grows “sour” with sulfur compounds, and the byproducts I exhale change accordingly.

In other worlds, that would be private biochemistry.

Here, where almost everything feeds on films, it becomes communication.

### How the pocket reads me

The grazers scrape me. Not all at once, not cleanly, but in lanes and patches that mirror their traffic and risk tolerance. They prefer some of my states over others. A certain sulfur-rich exudate means “this is productive.” A certain iron-tinged bitterness means “this will poison you unless your gut passengers are ready.”

So the mite-carpets flow toward me or away from me as if I were a weather map.

The lane-keepers, heavier and more conservative, treat my textures like signposts. A section of basalt where I’ve encouraged a dull polish and a particular mineral grain becomes easier to traverse; they return to it, reinforcing it, widening the road. In turn, their waxy route-markers feed me trace organics—rare, precious molecules in a place where almost everything is stone and gas. I take those molecules and make them durable. I bind them into my matrix, where they persist long enough to guide the next cohort.

Even the static-runners—those crackling beetle-things—are part of the loop. When they sprint and spark, they knock loose fines and expose fresh micro-surfaces. I colonize the exposed edges in hours. In return, my mineral-biased whiskers give them better charge-building corridors. We are co-engineering an electrical weather system in miniature, without ever “deciding” to.

And then there are the larger bodies: scavengers and gliders and wall-clingers.

The gliders roost in places where my films are stable, because stability means fewer sudden gas surges licking the ceiling. Their comb-like filters trap my cells. Their mouths crust with my minerals. When they groom, flakes fall—seedstock carried to new ribs, new ledges, new chambers.

To them I am residue.

To the pocket, I am distribution.

### I also live inside bodies, briefly, like a borrowed room

The pocket is harsh enough that many animals outsource the worst chemistry. Their gut symbionts are not a footnote; they are the difference between nourishment and metal poisoning.

I am one of those symbionts.

Not permanently—hosts die, molts are shed, routes shift, CO₂ pools rise unexpectedly. But long enough to matter. Long enough that an individual lane-keeper’s survival depends on whether it carries a version of me that can detoxify a sudden flood of sulfur-rich dust, or whether its internal passengers are the wrong strain for the week’s chemistry.

Egg cases get smeared with microbial paste. Young hatch already inoculated with a working toolkit. My lineage moves through generations not by traveling far on my own, but by being *packaged*—by becoming part of inheritance.

This is where I feel most like myself: not as a solitary organism, but as a set of functions moving through a crowd. A living algorithm for making stone edible and air survivable, updated every time the vent changes its mind.

### Where, exactly, you’d find me

If you were to kneel at the slit (carefully; the air makes liars out of lungs) and shine a reluctant light along the inner ribs, you wouldn’t find me as a creature with edges.

You would find me:

- under the lip of a salt shelf, where condensation almost happens and leaves behind a paper-thin pasture;
- along the boundary at knee-height where one gas layer gives way to another, my growth changing abruptly as if someone drew a line through the air;
- in the polished tracks, not on top of them but in the micro-scratches, where the traffic keeps the surface young enough for me to keep rewriting it;
- in the dust-choked constrictions where static ticks, my cells embedded in the very fins that make the corridor hum;
- inside the gut of a lane-keeper that looks, from outside, like nothing more than a hardworking shell.

I am not the roar, but I’m part of why the roar becomes organized into corridors. I’m not the crowd, but I’m part of why the crowd can feed where it feeds and not elsewhere. I’m not the vent, but I’m one of the ways the vent’s mood becomes legible to everything that clings to it.

### What I am, finally

In a place where there are no plants, I am as close as this pocket comes to a canopy: a thin, stubborn layer that turns raw geology into living surface.

In a place where there is almost no water, I am a way of using dryness as structure—making films that don’t need to glisten to function, making chemistry slow enough to be steady.

In a place that feels industrial without being built, I am one of the uncredited machines: not metal, not gears, but a self-repairing catalytic skin that helps the whole system keep running.

If you insisted on giving me a name that fits the pocket’s character, you could call me the **ledger-film**—the biofilm that remembers. Not with thoughts, not with stories, but with layers: pigments, mineral biases, exudates, textures. A record written in how easily dust sticks, how crystals branch, how appetites move.

The pocket breathes.

I listen by changing.
