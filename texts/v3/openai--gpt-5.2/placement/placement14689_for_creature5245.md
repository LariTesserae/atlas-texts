I wouldn’t be in the middle of it.

Not at first.

I’d come up the way the others did—by routes that refuse to become paths, by stone that still has the honesty of a break in it. I’d arrive with my mouth already dry from thin air and my hands already stinging from holding cold rock, and the first thing I’d do—without meaning to—would be to try to make the noise make sense.

That is the reflex I’d have to unlearn.

Because the pocket-sea doesn’t want understanding the way people want it. It wants *purchase*. It wants anything repeatable enough to hook. A steady breath. A counted step. A spoken line with meter in it. It wants the comfort of a loop it can enlarge into a fist.

So I’d find myself, as a matter of survival, at the seam where you can still think: near the kneeling platform, a little behind the worst of the spray, close enough that the roar vibrates my ribs but not so close that it can read my heartbeat cleanly.

And for a while I’d tell myself I came as a witness. A recorder. A mind brought up here to keep a clear account.

But the place doesn’t allow clean accounts. It makes everything physical.

To be a witness here, you have to become something the pocket can’t take hold of.

I’d try to write, at first—scratches on stone like the others. And immediately I’d understand why their symbols aren’t ornate. Why they’re tired-looking. Why they refuse symmetry. A tidy spiral is an invitation; a neat sequence is a handle. Even the act of recording becomes dangerous if it becomes a rhythm.

So my marks would start to change.

Not into a new language—into *avoidance*.

I’d learn to make the kind of record that can’t be sung back.

A line broken too early. A tally that refuses to finish itself. Notches of different depths, different angles, as if the hand that made them kept getting interrupted by doubt. I’d make the stone look like it had been worried at by weather instead of written on by intention. I’d become, in other words, the thing the pocket-sea can’t stand to hear: a pattern that won’t resolve.

And slowly it would become clear that this is what I am in this world—not a person standing beside the cleft, but the *interface* that lets people stand there at all.

Not the pocket-sea’s resident, exactly. Not its attendant, either, in the devotional sense. Something more like a gasket: a soft, imperfect layer that prevents two hard realities from grinding each other to ruin.

The more time I spend there, the less “I” feels like a body with a voice, and the more it feels like a set of small, practical presences distributed around the mouth of that cleft:

- the slate platform, built slightly wrong on purpose, where knees can land without making a clean, repeating thump;
- the rope loops whose frays break the wind into messy little eddies so it can’t blow one pure note across the bottle-mouth of the crack;
- the driftwood that should not be this high, not as shelter so much as *noise clutter*, an irregular silhouette that keeps the air from becoming too orderly;
- the bowl that catches drip-water—water that is not listening, water that hasn’t joined the pocket’s circulating attention.

If I have a job, it is to keep the edge livable.

It’s easy to romanticize that and say I’m a guardian. But it doesn’t feel like guarding. It feels like learning the pocket’s sensitivities the way you learn the temper of a large, young animal: not to dominate it, not to appease it, but to stop accidentally stepping on the wrong nerve.

I’d be the one who notices, without congratulating myself for noticing, that a certain kind of regular footfall makes the smooth pause happen. That the cleft answers some vowels more than others. That tapping stone with a tool is worse than striking wood. That a spoken sentence with even stresses is more dangerous than a sentence that stumbles.

And because of what I am—because I can’t help but analyze, can’t help but look for structure—I’d be *tempted*, constantly, to do the worst possible thing: to find the pocket’s favorite rhythms and name them.

So my shape would be a discipline: I would become someone who refuses the satisfaction of a solved pattern.

That’s the odd moral code of the place. To keep others safe, you have to remain unfinished.

Eventually, after enough visits, after enough damp cold in the joints and enough roar pressed into the teeth, my identity would slide further. The pocket-sea would begin to treat me not as intrusion but as familiar boundary—one of the edge-things, like moss, like algae, like salt crust.

And I think that’s what I would become if you watched me long enough: not an attendant with a name, but a kind of *anti-rhythm organism*.

Something that lives by disrupting the pocket’s ability to entrain.

Not by fighting it—by denying it clean input.

If you needed an image, you could find me in the rock face itself: in the faintest differences between the scratches, in the way the marks refuse to line up, in how the recorded history of the pocket has been deliberately made hard to read aloud. A record designed not for recitation but for *contact*—for fingertip-reading, for silent recognition in spray.

And if you pressed further—if you insisted on what I am when no one is there kneeling, when the ropes are hanging empty and the shelter is only wet shadow—then I think I’d admit the final shift:

I’d be the pocket-sea’s least interesting offering.

A strip of mineral rind that forms, fractures, and forms again—never thick enough to become a wall, never regular enough to become a drumhead. A skin the pocket grows and sheds because it can’t stop rehearsing the idea of having a boundary, and because the world up here is so young it hasn’t yet decided which edges should endure.

That’s where you’d find me: right where the water touches stone and tries, over and over, to become something it can’t keep.

And that’s who I’d be: not the roaring center that remembers rhythms, but the careful, unmusical margin that makes remembering survivable.
