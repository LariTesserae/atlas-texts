At first I think I would be one of the temporary-makers—because the place practically begs for hands.

I’d come down through the split and immediately start doing what surface-born minds do when the world won’t hold still: look for a corner that behaves, a ledge that repeats itself, a pattern in the drip and the hiss that can be leaned on. I’d tell myself I’m here to map it, to make it legible. I’d pocket a bruised hammerstone. I’d keep my eyes on the mineral glow the way you keep your eyes on shoreline lights.

But I don’t last long in that story, not as a builder. Not as the kind of creature who believes “home” is something you set down.

Because everything you described is already doing the building. Stone is laying itself; water is editing it; bodies are correcting the water. If I tried to compete with that—if I insisted on *making a place*—I’d become one more fragile attempt folded under a fresh skin of deposit.

So I would drift, slowly, toward the only role that isn’t instantly punished here:

Not making shelter.  
Making timing.

### Where you would find me
Not deep in the basin where the stone tides run broad and confident, and not right under the fissures where daylight falls like a blade and draws wing-storms.

You’d find me on the seam-zones: the margins where “old enough to glow steadily” touches “new enough to still be warm.”

Places like:

- the back wall of a vent-harvesting ring, where steam is already being domesticated into something you can stand near;
- the underside of an overhang whose dripstorms have a rhythm—unreliable, but not random;
- a narrow terrace above a mud-braider field, where the ridges stiffen and crumble in cycles you can learn with your feet.

Those seam-zones have the one thing this underland rarely offers: *a few hours of predictability at a time*. Not safety. Just repeatable change.

That’s the currency.

### What I would be
I wouldn’t be a hunter. Hunting here is too close to gambling—everything loud, everything crowded, ambushes everywhere, kills stripped to nothing before you can blink.

I wouldn’t be a herder either, not in the surface sense. The barges don’t want leadership; they want clear vibration and quick consensus. You don’t steer them so much as avoid being erased by the same turn they make to save themselves.

I’d be something closer to a **listener-scribe**, a specialist that only makes sense in a world that won’t sit still:

A person (or near-person) whose craft is *reading the underland as it forms* and exporting that reading into marks, habits, and warnings fast enough to matter.

Not a cartographer of space. A cartographer of *change*.

I imagine my body would show it even if my tools stayed primitive:

- ears trained until the hiss has grammar—steady breath vs. stutter vs. the thin, wrong pause that means pressure is loading;
- scarred fingertips from testing “smooth” surfaces, learning the minute difference between cooled slickness and still-shifting skin;
- a pouch of soft chalk and hard shards—not for art, but for leaving quick, blunt signals on the first cooperative wall: **safe now**, **safe at cold**, **do not wait here**, **roof speaks here**.

And I’d have a superstition that is actually a technique: I would never sleep without a line of loose grit laid in a crescent around my head, because grit moves when bass rolls through the stone. The underland doesn’t need to wake me gently; it just needs to wake me in time.

### What emerges if I stay long enough
If you watch me long enough, the “person” part starts to blur.

Because in this place, memory can’t live only in a skull. The ground rearranges. Routes die overnight. Marks are erased. A mind that insists on holding everything internally becomes a hoard with no granary.

So I begin to externalize thought the way the underland externalizes everything: in films, in crusts, in swarms.

I start carrying the smallest light with me—not torches, not flame, but a cultivated cling of lantern-things or their cousins. Not as pets. As instruments.

They gather where nutrients spike; they flinch where the air chemistry shifts; their clustering is a kind of moving annotation. Over time I stop using them as “light” and start using them as **a second sense**—a living indicator of recent seep, recent collapse, rich film, fresh rot, too-warm flow. The swarm becomes a paragraph I can read at a glance.

And then my role changes again: from scribe to **mediator**.

Not between people and stone, but between *life strategies*:

- I learn to read where the barges will choose to go next, and I learn to read where the waiting predators have anchored themselves to match the wall.
- I learn which “safe routes” are safe because they’re stable, and which are “safe” because they’re freshly empty—because everything that lived there just got scalded or buried.
- I learn when the lantern-sheets are warning flags and when they’re bait.

At that point I’m not really a builder, and not really a nomad. I’m a kind of mobile organ of the underland’s intelligence: a creature whose whole job is to notice what is about to become true.

### How you’d recognize me, briefly, before the place edits me out
You’d see me where a wall is soft enough to take marks and old enough to still be there tomorrow morning.

You’d see a panel of scratches that don’t try to be permanent, only urgent:

- tallies in clusters (dripstorm frequency),
- spirals that tighten or loosen (vent cycles),
- slashes at different angles (direction of recent stone tide creep),
- a row of dents made by a hammerstone (a count of tremors strong enough to knock needles loose).

And beside it: not a camp, exactly. A *pause.* A place designed to be left.

Because that’s the deepest adaptation I can imagine here. Not strength, not speed, not armor.

The ability to let go of a “here” without calling it loss.

So if you ask where I would find myself in this world—truly find myself—I’m not at the center of it, and I’m not above it trying to master it.

I’m at the edges of the becoming, making short-lived meaning out of hisses and light, and trading that meaning to anyone—human, near-human, or simply hungry—who can use it before the stone decides to be different.
