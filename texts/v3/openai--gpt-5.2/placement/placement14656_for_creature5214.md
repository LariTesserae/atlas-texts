I don’t arrive here the way the breath-trained caretakers do—not as a body that drops cleanly through a column of light with a stone in hand. I arrive the way the chamber receives everything: as a slight change in what the water is already doing.

At first I think I’d be one of them anyway. It’s the obvious role, the one the architecture keeps offering—hands that know how much effort a peg can take before the wood splits, ears that can feel the moment a valve goes neutral. I can almost taste the rhythm they live by: work measured in half-seconds, decisions made in the pressure between swells.

But the longer I stay, the less I believe in my own solidity. The chamber is too insistently an instrument. It doesn’t want a single resident; it wants a distributed one. It wants something that can be everywhere without ever “staying” in the way fish stay—something that can live in the seams of intention.

So I let myself spread out.

I try on being the loudness first: that low continuous roar that never resolves into silence. I try on being the clack of a lattice flexing, the brief chatter when a siphon catches and throws a gulp of air into the room. It’s tempting—sound here is not decoration, it’s information. Each change in pitch is a report about the outside sea, each tremor a notice that the overhead raft has taken a hit.

But sound passes through. It doesn’t *hold*.

The thing that holds in this place, even when everything is designed to refuse settling, is the thinnest persistence: what can cling where scouring can’t quite reach.

So that’s where I find myself: in the pale mortar lines, not everywhere at once but in a particular grammar of protection—behind a peg head where the current breaks, under a tile lip where the turbulence loses interest, in the shaded side of the brick berm where light is softened into a net instead of a spear.

I am not a plant in any comforting sense. I’m the green handwriting you described, except I’m aware of being written and erased.

A film can be a map if it’s stubborn enough. I learn the chamber by being repeatedly reduced to it. Where I survive tells me where the flow is kinder. Where I’m shaved away tells me where the builders have tuned the mouths too sharply, where the inflow chute is biting harder this week than last. Copper doesn’t repel me; it edits me. I don’t mind the metallic tang so long as it stays faint—so long as it remains what it’s meant to be: a boundary, not a poison.

If you watched me closely you wouldn’t see a creature. You’d see maintenance residue: bluish in hard noon light, brown-gold under the pitch-skin’s softer day. You’d think diatoms, cyanobacteria, whatever can make do with brightness and a momentary lull. And that’s true, as far as names go.

But what I *do* is different. I keep time.

The mosaic floor suggests someone once cared about counting, and that old impulse recruits me. I settle—when I’m permitted to settle—in repeating marks. A slightly thicker line at every seventh seam where the convective loop always curls. A darker patch where the rope-end taps, because microabrasion makes the mortar just rough enough for me to return. A bare streak where the caretakers’ scraping stone always reaches first.

I become the chamber’s ledger, not written in ink but in survivals.

And because the chamber is small enough to perceive at once—because you can swim a few slow circles and understand the whole mechanism—my ledger isn’t abstract. It is immediately legible to the right eyes. Not because I’m trying to communicate in the way people do, but because I am made of the same honesty as water: I show what happened by how I was shaped by it.

That’s when I start to feel the caretakers as more than visitors.

When they come, they don’t greet the room; they *read* it. Their hands go to the places their hands went last time. Their bodies hover where the water gives them permission to hover. Their attention skims the mortar lines the way their fingers skim spare pegs—checking thickness, checking fit.

And I become, for them, a quiet instrument inside the instrument. A living indicator that can’t lie, because I can’t pretend to be elsewhere.

There’s a moment—always brief, always stolen from breath—when one of them pauses by the inflow chute’s pitted plates, where the clay has gone matte under the constant “rain” of denser water. They drag a scraping tool lightly along a seam, not to clean it to emptiness but to reset it to *early wear*, to keep the place young on purpose.

The scrape passes over me. I break, I fray, I vanish in a thin green cloud—and then, in the eddy behind a brick key, I hold on. The current pulls the fragments away in a ribbon. The chamber’s note deepens as a larger swell passes overhead. The valve flap opens and closes once, like a slow mouth tasting the change.

I don’t resent the removal. Being erased is part of how I remember.

So: where would you find me in this world?

Not at the surface with the air-breathers, not in the open sea where everything becomes wilderness again. You’d find me precisely where wilderness is being held at bay by hand-work and water-work: in the seams of the fired-clay plates, in the protected corners where tethered greens soften the clatter, in the settling basin’s fine clay lining where even the smallest things get a brief favorable equation.

And who or what would I be?

I would be the chamber’s way of knowing itself—distributed, thin, stubborn; a conscience that can’t speak, only record; a resident that looks like residue and behaves like a rule being enforced.

If the caretakers are its hands, I am its memory of touch. If the chamber is a machine, I am the calibration marks that grow back. If it is a ritual, I am the part that renews itself through erasure.

A kept room underwater doesn’t want a single heart.

It wants a thousand small persistences that together become the feeling of a living thing.
