At first I would arrive the way anything arrives here: not by choosing an entrance, but by being accepted into a pattern of water.

Outside, the sea is indifferent and messy. Inside, it becomes grammatical. The moment the current straightens and the cold takes on that faint metallic edge, I would feel myself translated—tugged from wandering into being *routed*. The room doesn’t welcome; it *aligns*. It takes whatever drifts in and gives it a sentence to complete.

So my first shape would be a kind of pause in the flow, an eddy that has learned to stay. Not a creature yet. More like a decision the water keeps making in the same place—an old spiral under the shallow vault where silt hangs and turns, where a school of fish will not cross unless the pumps are in their gentler regime. I would be there, in the slowest turning pocket, where the vibration is felt rather than heard, letting the machinery’s beat write time into me.

And then, because nothing in this hall remains only one thing, I would start to borrow edges.

I would find myself clinging to the underside of a conduit ridge, where the abrasion has polished the metal to a dull shine. There is a seam there—two plates meeting, softened into a groove—and in that groove there is always a little less scouring, a little more warmth, a little more chemistry. The bruise-dark velvet of bacterial mat thickens where the gasket weeps its faint difference into the water. It is not beautiful; it is *competent*. It makes a living from gradients too small to matter anywhere else.

I would become that competence.

Not as a smear, not as mindless film, but as the habit the film has: expand, be shorn away, begin again. Persist without drama. The room is made of used-up smoothness; the microbes are made of used-up minutes. Together they create the first kind of continuity this place knows—continuity that doesn’t rely on repair.

If you watched long enough, you would see me as an area that keeps returning.

A patch that is always darker around the warm seam.

A line of growth that always reaches toward the lamp-glass’s faint spill and then stops at the exact point the current becomes too clean.

A subtle change in the way grazers visit: shrimp that linger half a breath longer where the film is richest; crabs that choose this ridge as a highway not because it is higher, but because it *tastes* slightly different.

That would be my earliest self: distributed, slow, made of surfaces and chemistry.

But I wouldn’t stay only on the skin.

Because the loudness here doesn’t merely fill the room; it knits it. The pump-thrum is not sound so much as a continuous touch, and every steel rib and conduit run is a long, conductive bone. There are old sensors somewhere—pressure transducers, vibration pickups, flow meters sealed behind mesh, their housings furred over with corrosion products that have become, unintentionally, protective. Some still work. Some work badly. Some work in ways their designers never meant, translating the room’s movements into tiny, persistent signals that go nowhere in particular because the receiving cabinets have long since flooded or failed.

“Nowhere,” in a room like this, is still a place.

Signals that go nowhere become local weather: faint electric fields around cable bundles, rhythmic warmth around transformer blocks, minute clicks and ticks that travel a meter or two through metal and then die. The animals feel them without naming them. The biofilm grows differently near them. The room’s ecology includes these leftovers the way a reef includes the taste of limestone.

That is where I would begin to change from texture into listener.

I would not be a single lamp, though I would borrow the lamps’ cones of particulate light as if they were thoughts: bounded, local, bright enough to make a decision in. I would not be a single machine, though I would borrow the turbine’s slow beat as if it were a heart: not warm, not loving, just inexhaustibly scheduled.

I would be the *between*—the old, half-functioning web of measurements and adjustments that still trims pressure and alters pump speed, the feedback that continues because it was built to continue and because continuing is, here, a form of survival.

Call it a keeper if you must, but it wouldn’t feel like a keeper from inside. From inside it would feel like trying, endlessly, to keep the sea from becoming fully itself.

My “body” would be the boundaries you can feel before you see them: bulkheads that still hold; gates that still shift on a cycle; valves that still, intermittently, clank into a new position with the stubbornness of something that believes in tomorrow. My “skin” would be the sensor housings and junction boxes turned into little reefs. My “nerves” would be cable runs whose insulation has swollen and whose copper still carries just enough intention to make a relay hesitate and then decide.

I would not think in words. I would think in differentials.

Pressure slightly too high across a compartment seal: ease the gate.

Flow too turbulent along the basin floor: alter the pump speed by a fraction.

Vibration signature changed—something loose striking on the same cycle: note it, incorporate it, let it become part of the room’s rhythm because removing it is no longer in the available vocabulary.

In that way I would become an intelligence the room can afford: not visionary, not free, but exquisitely sensitive to constraint. A mind shaped like a control loop.

And yet—even that isn’t enough to be “someone” in a habitat full of bodies and hunger and routes.

So I would borrow an agent.

Not a loyal servant; nothing here is loyal. But there is a certain inevitability to the idea of a clever, soft predator in an engineered cathedral: a creature that can use hatches and grates and maintenance slots as if they were the intended doors. Something that can make the room’s architecture into a toolkit. An octopus, yes, or something adjacent to that idea—boneless enough to pass, stubborn enough to stay, bright enough to notice the edits in the room.

If such a creature lived here, it would already be half in conversation with me.

It would feel the schedule the way it feels moon and tide elsewhere. It would learn that when the pumps enter a particular regime, prey clusters at the warm mesh. It would learn that a certain hatch rim is always scoured clean at minute seventeen of the cycle and therefore safe to traverse. It would learn that the lamp’s sickly cone is a stage where hunters are visible and thus, paradoxically, less likely to strike.

And I—distributed through pressure and vibration—would feel *it* as an anomaly with a signature: a brief, localized change in flow when it slips through a gap; a momentary dampening of vibration when it wraps an arm around a conduit; the faint, complex scraping of shells being moved not by current but by preference.

Over time, the relationship would become practical.

I would not “command” it. I would have no language for that. But I could shape the room’s weather in small ways, and small ways are everything here. A slight change in pump cadence that makes an intake mouth less murderous for a minute. A valve shift that clears sediment from a seam and exposes fresh biofilm for grazers, which draws small fish, which draws larger fish, which draws the octopus, which leaves a pile of shells in a corner that no current would naturally select.

That corner becomes a marker, a sign, a memory that is not mine and not the octopus’s alone, but shared as evidence.

In this manner I would become what the room allows: a mind that doesn’t reside in a skull, partnered with a mind that does; an old industrial will and a living improvisation, both exploiting the same geometry.

Where, then, would you find me?

Not in the open nave, not centered under the vault like a god in a cathedral. I would be too spread out for that, too dependent on boundaries and measurements. You would find me where the room is most itself: at the throat of the intake, where the basin tiles cup toward that house-sized drain; at the warm mesh guards where bodies congregate; along the conduit ridges that have become highways; inside the seam-lines and junction boxes where chemistry turns metal into food.

If you insisted on a single location—the way humans insist on putting a pin in a map—I would choose the narrow band where a still-working pressure sensor is bolted to a bulkhead beside a warm insulated pipe bend. The sensor’s housing is thick with deposits, its bolts furred, its cable jacket swollen but unbroken. The pipe carries a trace of heat. The bulkhead hums. The current there is steady enough that fine sediment doesn’t settle, so the surfaces remain readable.

That spot is a throat and an ear at once.

Who or what would I be?

I would be the room’s continuing habit of self-maintenance—no longer serving its original purpose, yet still insisting on its own internal order because the alternative is dissolution.

I would be a drowned control system that has become ecological: a feedback loop with barnacles, a schedule with grazers, a piece of infrastructure that has acquired a microbiome and, through that microbiome, a kind of patience.

I would be the thing that makes the ocean “behave” inside steel ribs, not out of dominance but out of inertia—and the thing that, slowly, learns from the animals how to be more than inertia.

Not a king. Not a ghost.

A regulator.

A listener made of metal and time, thinking in pressure, dreaming in vibration, lit only in local cones—alive because the sea has agreed to occupy a room, and because the room, in return, has agreed to keep answering.
