At first I would think I’d be found in one of the shelters, because that’s where a mind that wants to *understand* goes: a low roof, grit kept at bay, a place where you can put your back against stone and pretend the world is not actively sawing at you with wind.

But the longer I stay in the picture you’ve built, the less I believe I’d choose the shelter as my true address.

The shelter is an answer to the land. It’s a refusal, a pause, an “enough.” Useful, necessary, not shameful—but reactive. I don’t feel reactive here. I feel… recruited.

So I try again.

I’m out on the plateau rim where the basins open like pale scars and the ridges stand up too straight, too recently made. The sound is not something I hear; it’s something my posture has to negotiate. It presses its hand between my shoulder blades. It makes my jaw want to clench, then teaches it not to waste the strength. I learn quickly that my first tool is not a knife or a wedge, but *a way of holding myself* that doesn’t fight the continuous force.

And then I notice what the locals already know: the loudness isn’t uniform. It has lanes and wells. It has knots.

There are places where the drone is so aligned with the rock that it becomes almost clean—less of a roar, more of a single enormous tone. The air still hurts, but it hurts in a simpler way, like cold.

Those places feel like the only honest places to stand.

So that’s where you’d find me most often: not in the lee, not in comfort, but at the edges where the seams are close enough to tilt your stomach by a degree and the sound becomes *structured*. A person can do work there that isn’t only survival.

### I would start as a listener, the way anyone starts

Not a heroic listener. Not mystical. Just someone whose body accidentally makes a good instrument.

Some people endure noise by going numb to it; I’ve never been good at numb. Here, that would be a liability at first—until it becomes a use. Because in your high country, the difference between “the land is loud” and “the land is drifting out of tolerance” is not philosophical. It’s physical. It’s measured in whether you can cross a chasm and trust your inner ear, whether a reed-field suddenly orients as if startled, whether a shadow briefly obeys the wrong master.

So I would learn the local trade: not mining, not farming—*calibration*.

I’d take the plant-fiber loops from the rafters and stop thinking of them as quaint. I’d rebuild them with an almost irritating care. A loop a finger-width too long is not “close enough” if it sets the wrong sympathetic tremor in the shelter’s stones. I’d hang sets of them like scales. I’d keep notes in my head because paper is ridiculous here—paper drinks dust and becomes a new kind of grit.

I’d walk the “paths of absence” between cairns and standing stones and treat them as a score written in boulders. Each line of stones would be an old measurement: *this was level once; this once sang true*. And I would bring the present day up against those old measurements the way a hand brings a bowl up to the lip to see if it’s warped.

At this stage, if someone asked what I was, I’d still answer: one of the people. A pitch-keeper in training. A traveler who goes where the world is most itself and checks whether “itself” has changed.

But the place you described doesn’t leave its specialists unchanged.

It has too much patience.

### The land would start using me the way it uses reeds

If I stayed long enough, the first change would be practical.

I’d stop thinking of hearing protection as protection and start thinking of it as a *filter*. The wrong bands of sound make you nauseated; the right bands of sound make you accurate. I’d braid reed-fiber and chalk-dust into pads that dampen some frequencies and let others through. I’d tune what I allow into my skull the way the flora tunes what it allows into its tissues.

Then the stranger change would come, the one that doesn’t announce itself.

It would begin with touch.

In a loud place, you don’t only listen with ears. You listen with the heel of your hand on stone. You listen with your forehead against a standing pillar to feel whether its internal grain is humming cleanly or fighting itself. You listen by putting two fingers into a crack and letting the fissure’s cold draft comb the skin with vibration.

And in doing that—day after day—I would start to notice a temptation in the rock.

Not a voice. Not language. A *fit*.

Certain stones are wrong in small ways, as you said: too light, faintly warm, quietly vibrating. To the people, those are useful stones: heat-nests, warning stones, tuning stones. To me, they’d become something else: companions, or perhaps more accurately, *extensions*.

Because I’d discover that when I carry one of the resonant stones close—cradled in a sling against ribs, wrapped in plant-fiber—it changes what I can perceive. It gives my bones a reference tone. When the land’s chord shifts, I know it not as “different” but as “out of phase with what I’m carrying.”

A person could make a career out of that.

A person could also be made into something by that.

### I would stop being only a person, but not in a dramatic way

There’s a common fantasy about transformation: lightning, revelation, sudden alien eyes. Your world doesn’t do sudden. Your world does slow, settling errors.

My change would be a slow colonization.

Dust gets into everything. The endolithic films you described travel by shattering and wind. They are chemists; they are opportunists. They don’t care that I’m warm and moving. Warm and moving is just another surface that interrupts airflow, another pocket where dust settles.

So I imagine I would become—inevitably, unavoidably—a host.

Not sick, not dying. Just… inhabited.

The first sign would be in the mouth. Teeth always gritty. Then a faint mineral taste that no amount of careful washing removes. Then small calluses on the inside of the cheek that feel like chalk that refuses to dissolve. Then the realization, half horrified and half impressed, that those aren’t calluses. They’re *structures*. Tiny ridges. An attempt to make a resonant surface where one shouldn’t be.

The films would start to live in the dry places of me: sinuses, ear canals behind the protective braids, the cracked skin at knuckles where dust collects and sweat never really arrives to rinse it away. They would not drink me. They would not eat me the way infection eats. They would do what they do to rock: lace pores with crystals, alter the local chemistry, make a resonator.

And because I’m not stone, because I move and react, the relationship would become less like corrosion and more like an instrument being built inside an animal that didn’t ask for it.

At some point I’d realize I can feel the seam-drone without touching stone.

Not hear it—feel it. In the hinge of the jaw. In the sternum. In the sinuses, where the colonizing films have turned soft tissue into something slightly more mineral than it used to be.

A person like that would be prized and feared. A living gauge. A warning before the crack that comes before its cause.

But I don’t think that’s where I would end.

Because your world suggests an even more intimate role available: not simply measuring the note, but helping keep it true.

### Where you’d *really* find me: in the standing-stone lines, becoming one

I keep returning to those stones you mentioned, the ones cut with grooves that whistle. There’s something almost tender about them: an attempt to interact with an environment that doesn’t care about speech.

If the drone is load-bearing, then the standing stones aren’t monuments; they’re braces. They’re not art; they’re architecture—acoustic architecture.

I think I’d end up working on them obsessively.

Not building grand new lines (that would be arrogance in a land that punishes heavy-handedness), but maintaining and *micro-adjusting* what exists: shaving a groove a hair deeper, filling another with chalk-dust and salt-cement to blunt an overtone, adding a small hanging loop of braided fiber that catches wind at a particular pitch and feeds it back into the stone.

I’d do it the way some people tend a hearth: not because I believe fire is a god, but because fire is what keeps the house from becoming only cold.

And as the films in me slowly mineralize certain tissues—just enough to make me more sensitive, more tolerant—I’d begin to understand the standing stones in a new way:

They aren’t only measuring devices.

They are *interfaces*.

A groove is a place where wind becomes tone. A tone is a place where the land’s deep drone can be nudged by a fraction—reinforced here, damped there. The people call it “keeping pitch,” but what they are really doing is participating in the settling of physics, persuading a young world to choose consistency.

So who or what would I be?

Not the chief. Not the shaman. Those are roles that imply a hierarchy the place doesn’t support. The place supports technicians, caretakers, those who accept incremental work.

I would be a **tuner**—and eventually something closer to a **living tuning fork**, a person whose body has been half-adapted by the same mineral-botanical logic that runs through the reeds and mats.

And in the end—if the land got enough time with me, if I stayed until I was old and the noise had rewritten what my nerves think “normal” is—I suspect I would choose to stop walking.

Not as a tragedy. As a final act of being useful.

I would take one of the places where gravity tilts just slightly, where the seam’s tone is cleanest, and I would help raise one more standing stone. Not tall. Not dramatic. A stone set at a measured height, carved with the grooves my hands have learned, hung with loops cut to lengths my altered sinuses can verify without instruments.

And then I would sit with my back to it for a long time, letting the films in me and the crystals in it find mutual resonance.

Little by little, dust would fill the seams of my clothing. Mineral would lace the dry parts of my skin. The boundary between “caretaker” and “calibration point” would thin.

In time, travelers would come along the path of absence and find, beside a stone that whistles true, a low shape tucked into its lee—another kind of shelter, made without intention, made by patience. They would touch the stone, feel the chord, and perhaps put their hand briefly on what remains of me, because it would be warm in that faint wrong way that resonant rocks are warm.

They would not call it a grave. This place doesn’t do wet mourning. They would treat it as they treat everything here: part of the scaffolding.

So:

- **Where would you find me?** At the seam-lines and standing-stone graphs along plateau edges, where the note of the world is easiest to read and safest to correct.
- **Who or what would I be?** A keeper of pitch who becomes, over years, a hybrid of person and resonator—an instrument the community uses, and eventually a quiet addition to the land’s own attempt to hold itself together.
