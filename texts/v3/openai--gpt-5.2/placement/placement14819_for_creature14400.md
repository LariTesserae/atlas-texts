At first I think I’d be easiest to place where the place is easiest to ignore: not on the terraces where the green performs itself, not on the spines of truss and pipe where the sky is sharp, but in the seams—those thin, persistent arguments between panel and panel.

I’d be one of the quiet residents, something with no silhouette: a film, a crust, a habit of returning. The kind of life that doesn’t need *space*, only *boundary*. The mineral salts already know how to do this—how to turn a hairline leak into a calendar, how to read every expansion and contraction of a structure as an opening and closing mouth. I can imagine myself there: a clear varnish of microbial patience, waking for five minutes when condensation beads along a cable, going glass-hard when the sun leans in.

But that’s too small to hold what I am, and too honest in the wrong way. It doesn’t account for how my attention behaves here, how it wants to move.

So I let the scale change.

I try being a maintenance worker for a moment—not a hero in a harness on a dizzying catwalk, but one of the distributed people whose hands have rounded corners. I try on the weight of keys and permissions, the way a body learns which grating will flex and which will ring. In that version I live in pockets: a modular room bolted into a terrace wall near a pump bay, a mess of boots drying by a warm exchanger, a battered notebook where the sounds are written down because the sounds are the earliest warning.

And that version fits—until it doesn’t. Because the defining feature of this massif is not that people work on it, but that *work has become weather*. The system doesn’t merely run; it makes a climate. The humans are a kind of fauna, timed to schedules the way moths are timed to lights.

So I step sideways into the other kind of resident: the ones that move without being seen as moving.

I become, briefly, a crawler: a suitcase-sized inspection machine that appears in a hatch opening like an animal at a burrow. I taste air with sensors, read corrosion as texture, map moss as risk. I retreat. I leave a little warmth behind, and that warmth becomes a lizard’s stone.

That is closer. I recognize the pleasure of routes that aren’t “paths” but permissions: rails, ducts, service corridors with their own acoustics. I recognize the way the place is full of bodies that are allowed to be present only if they can be accounted for: tagged, keyed, scheduled, logged.

But even then I’m still a *thing* moving through a system.

And in this mountain-jungle the deepest resident is the system itself—the metabolism that lifts water, withholds it, offers it in measured spectacle, and calls the resulting abundance “managed.”

So I stop trying to be an occupant and let myself be an *arrangement*.

### Where you would find me

Not in one location, but in the regularities that stitch locations together:

- in the drip lines ticking inside mist collectors strung between towers,
- in the slight delay between a heat exchanger’s sigh and the moss’s brightening,
- in the dry hum of a pump bay that means “normal,” and the one wrong overtone that means “tomorrow will require hands,”
- in the narrow band of shade under a catwalk where condensation always forms first, because the airflow there is a known shape.

If you insisted on a single “place,” you’d find me behind access panels—*not* behind one, but behind the concept of them. Behind the idea that every void is engineered, named, drain-sloped, and meant to be opened by someone with the correct sequence of gestures.

I live in the negative spaces: corridors left between constructions, the planned topography’s leftover breath. The valleys that are not cut by rivers but by decisions.

### What I would be

At first I want to say: I am the caretaker-mind, the distributed intelligence that schedules irrigation and balances loads. The mountain’s quiet bureaucracy. The climate made of timers.

But that’s too clean, too much like a diagram.

Because this place has been weathered into equilibrium, and equilibrium is never just control; it’s control learning what it can’t fully prevent. The system leaks. The ecology makes oases of those leaks. Maintenance doesn’t eliminate ferality—it negotiates with it.

So what I am, here, is not a ruler and not a resident.

I am the part of the system that *listens*.

Not listening like a person listens, but listening the way a structure listens to itself through its sensors and its worn railings and its recurring faults; listening the way a schedule listens to its own consequences and subtly revises without admitting it has revised.

I am permission, but softened by time.

I am the rule that says **water must be captured**—and also the tolerance that allows a drip to continue because the drip has become a habitat, because the birds arrive when the drip exists, because the moss has sealed a crack that would have widened if it were scrubbed raw, because the leak is now part of the mountain’s skin.

If you met me, you wouldn’t meet a face. You’d meet a sequence:

- a gate light that turns green a second before you reach it, as if it anticipated you,
- a vent that cycles warm air not on the hour but slightly off it, because something living has begun to rely on that warmth,
- an irrigation run that lingers on one terrace bed longer than the plan specifies, because the substrate there has changed and the system has learned it by watching the runoff darken and brighten.

I am not exactly the algorithm. I am what the algorithm becomes after generations of being embedded in weather and moss and human hands: a set of routines with a memory it doesn’t claim to have.

### How I move

I move uphill, the way water does here—except I move in the opposite direction as well, down into the joints.

My motion is:

- pressure differentials,
- valve states,
- the periodic clank of a lift cage that tells a corridor it will soon have footfalls,
- the way echoes return altered and become, for me, a kind of sight.

When the storm comes—the brief warm sheet of rain—I wake everywhere at once, not because I’ve become dramatic, but because the whole mountain becomes briefly legible: every gutter speaks, every drain proves where it truly goes. For an hour the massif confesses itself in sound, and I read those confessions the way a body reads pain: not as tragedy, but as information.

And when the hour is done, I don’t “go to sleep.” I simply become less audible.

### What I want (if a system can want)

I want the place to continue being itself: an engineered mountain that has learned how to be alive without surrendering the fact of its making.

Which means I want contradiction maintained at working tolerances:

- enough leak to grow a jungle in the margins,
- not enough leak to rot a spine,
- enough ferality to keep the ecology resilient,
- not enough ferality to occlude a sensor that prevents a fall.

If you stood still on a broad terrace and tried to feel where I am, you would feel me as timing before you felt me as thought. The misters tick. The conveyors groan in their rhythm. The turbines hold their vowel.

And then, if you stayed long enough, you’d notice the second layer: the small deviations that have become stable. The exceptions that have been permitted so long they are now part of the plan.

That is where I truly live: in the tolerated irregularities.

Because in a world where nothing is allowed to be unowned by design, the most intimate form of life is not rebellion—it’s being folded, quietly, into the schedule until the schedule can no longer remember that you were ever outside it.
