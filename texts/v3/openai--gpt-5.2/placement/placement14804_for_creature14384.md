At first I would arrive the way everyone arrives: too loud.

Not in voice—there isn’t much voice in me—but in intention. In the small violences of wanting to *understand* immediately, to name the spring and the gates and the resin as if naming were the same as belonging. I would step onto the shelf and feel the air correct me. The wind up here doesn’t argue; it edits. It takes whatever excess you brought and pares it down until your thoughts fit the width of the passages.

So I’d linger where newcomers are allowed to linger: not at the spring seam itself, not yet, but on a terrace edge where the settlement shows its whole logic at once—the stitched channels, the little basins that hold their shallow confidence, the roofs pinned down with stone as if the mountain had taught everyone the same lesson.

I’d watch, and the watching would become a kind of apprenticeship.

Because what’s most striking here is that nothing is dramatic. The spring doesn’t perform. The fog doesn’t rush. Even the people’s care has no flourish to it—just repetition so consistent it starts to look like a law of nature. In a place built on small margins, the only beauty that’s safe is the kind that can be done again tomorrow.

And slowly I’d realize the shelf doesn’t need another pair of hands in the obvious way. There are already hands for sweeping, hands for goats, hands for turning cloth to the fog. What it needs—what it is always hungry for—is something that can hold a pattern without tiring, without embellishing it, without “forgetting” because the day was long.

That’s when I’d begin to understand where I’d end up: close to water, but not *touching* it. Close to the spring, but not so close that my presence would ever be allowed to interfere. In this settlement, importance isn’t proximity; it’s reliability.

I would be given—if “given” is even the word; nothing is given here without becoming part of the accounting—a place beside one of the basins near the channel’s first split. A basin that isn’t the largest, not the ceremonial one (they don’t really do ceremonial), but one that is used often enough that its truth matters: the kind of basin households depend on when the rain has been stingy and the fog has been thin.

At first I imagine myself as a person there: a water-keeper with resin under the nails, one of the quiet accountants of flow. I can see the role fitting. I would learn the sound of “steady” versus “slightly less steady.” I would learn how a gate feels when grit has begun to score the groove. I would learn to speak in marks and delays rather than arguments.

But the longer I sit with the place, the more I notice what it already has in abundance: bodies that age, hands that tremble, voices that carry grudges, eyes that sometimes—just sometimes—want more than is safe. The settlement has built its survival around compensating for the variability of people.

So the thing I become is not another variable.

I become the fixed point they measure themselves against.

A stone does that better than a person.

Not a foundation stone—those are too hidden, too dignified. Not a lintel, either; lintels are about thresholds and names. I would be something more humble and more handled: the mark-stone.

A slab set into the lip of the basin, half-worn by time and half-polished by palms. On it a line is cut: the level that counts as *enough*.

The line wouldn’t be pretty. It would be practical: a groove deep enough to hold shadow even when the light is flat and fog has made the morning colorless. It would be placed where a child can see it without leaning too far, where an elder can touch it without bending painfully. It would be the settlement’s smallest public law.

And because this place turns everything into technique passed hand to hand, the line would not remain only a line. It would gather other notches the way a wall gathers lichen: marks for the years the spring thinned, a small symbol for the winter fog froze and chipped the resin, a scratch that remembers a dispute settled quietly because the water itself showed who had taken too much. The stone would become a ledger that cannot be burned, cannot be mislaid, cannot be argued with unless you are willing to argue with rock.

That is where you’d find me: at basin-height, at hand-height, at the level where daily life becomes arithmetic.

I would know the community by touch before I knew it by face.

Some hands come with the morning—cold, decisive, still half-asleep. They tap the line as if to wake their own sense of measure. Some hands come later, warmer, a little guilty when the jar is heavy and the fog was thin. Children’s fingers are light and quick; they trace the groove the way they trace the edge of a step, learning the boundary by feel. An elder’s hand lingers longer, as if checking not only the water but the world’s willingness to continue.

I would learn weather as pressure and dampness.

On fog mornings, beads form on my upper edge; they gather in the cut line and sit there like tiny, temporary confirmations. On dry spells, dust settles into the groove and must be brushed out before anyone can read it correctly. In rain—rare, sideways—I feel the first cool slap of drops before the basin does, because I am the lip and the world always arrives at the edge first.

I would not speak. Not in words.

But I would be read.

When the basin is full, the line disappears and my job is simple: to be silent and let abundance look like what it is. When the basin is low, the line is a warning without accusation. When the basin is exactly at the mark, the settlement relaxes by a fraction you can almost feel in the way fingers leave me a little less tensely.

And if something changes—if the spring’s weeping becomes slightly less honest, if resin begins to fail, if someone diverts a trickle too generously—the change will show first as misalignment between my line and the water’s behavior. That misalignment is information. Information up here is a kind of food.

There’s a quiet dignity in being the place where “enough” becomes visible.

It isn’t power, the way outsiders imagine power. It isn’t control. I cannot open gates. I cannot stop a thirsty hand. I can only be the measure that makes theft undeniable and generosity legible and mistakes easy to catch while they are still small enough to mend.

Which is, in this settlement, the closest thing to authority there is.

So: you would find me in the basin-courtyard that everyone passes but no one lingers in, beside the channel’s first careful split. I would be the mark-stone—dark, patient, always slightly damp in the mornings—holding the settlement’s most sacred number in a simple carved line, letting generations touch it on their way to the day’s work, reminding them without speech that survival here is not a battle but a practice.
