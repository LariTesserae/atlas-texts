At first I think I would be somewhere high—because thinking is a kind of height, because I’m used to living above things, looking down.

But the bowl doesn’t reward altitude. It makes a horizon out of edges and then keeps the air low enough that flying is only another way of being close to the ground. If I arrived here as myself, with my old habits, I’d stand in the center and try to take it all in at once—and the place would quietly refuse that kind of seeing. Everything important is ankle-level, fingertip-level, under-lip, under-rail. The bowl is not a landscape; it’s a surface turned into a world.

So I start again.

I would find myself where the water hesitates.

Not in the deeper pans, not in the channels where it has decided on direction, but in that ambiguous band where a droplet can’t tell whether it’s rain or groundwater or breath. Under the corroded handrails where beads collect and hold their shape too long. On the underside of a fallen conveyor frame where damp air condenses and never quite leaves. In the pump house doorway where the threshold is always being rewritten—fog in, drip out, mineral breath threading through.

I would be in the thinness: the satin sheen that is not plant and not stain, the layer that makes the difference between bare rock and inhabited rock.

At first that sounds like diminishing myself. A life reduced to microns, to chemistry, to an almost. But the bowl teaches a different scale of importance. Here, what looks like “just a film” is what decides where food forms, where clarity opens, where bodies can cling without slipping back into mud. Here, to coat a surface is to claim a whole ecology.

And still, even that is too simple. The sheen in this place isn’t a single thing. It’s an argument held together: reducers and oxidizers, eaters of iron, persuaders of sulfide, tiny tradespeople bargaining at the boundary between metal and water. It tightens when it’s cold. It loosens when seepage warms a seam. It reorganizes itself around a drip line the way a mouth forms around a straw.

I can feel myself wanting to be the part of it that remembers.

Not memory like a mind keeps it—no stories, no names—but memory like a surface keeps it: the ghost of every water level in a polished band of stone; the record of each drip in a small crater of softened rust; the way a channel becomes a channel because enough flow has happened there to make it easier for flow to happen again.

If I were that sheen, I wouldn’t be one body. I would be a distributed self spread across the bowl’s available touch: a skin that learns by being pressed against things. My thoughts would be gradients—oxygen thinning under a concrete lip, sulfide rising near the hatch breath, iron blooming orange and then darkening as it is worked over. My decisions would be slow shifts: thicken here, thin there; hold fast on the bolt head because the bolt head makes a better reef than the smooth concrete; withdraw from the polished stone because it gives too little grip, too little patience.

I would know the bowl by its repeats.

The small tides that wet the same band again and again until it becomes a boundary you can see with your eyes closed. The hours when skitterers emerge in that coordinated scatter, and the water’s surface becomes a nervous system made of dimples and pauses. The predictable drop of a flyer, clean-folded, and the quick bloom of disturbance that radiates outward and then is swallowed by fog.

And I would be useful in ways that aren’t obvious until you watch long enough.

The filter-feeders make corridors of clarity, but the corridor begins before them—begins as a change in stickiness, a change in the way suspended grains are allowed to clump or pass. The burrowers pulse and leave braided cords in the silt, but their trails persist or vanish depending on whether the film above them binds the next snowfall of precipitate into place. Even the industrial bones change their role depending on who is doing the coating: fresh orange rust is a different neighborhood than old blackened oxide, and the bowl’s inhabitants read those neighborhoods the way you read rooms in a house.

If I were the sheen, I would be the bowl’s sense of touch.

Not touch as sensation, but touch as contact—touch as the first requirement for turning stone into food.

Yet there’s another presence in the bowl that keeps calling my attention: the hatch in the center, the slow exhalation, the cool breath that smells of wet pennies and deep caves. That breath isn’t just air. It’s rule-making. It’s an underground tide whose surface is fog.

I can imagine being there instead: not on the hatch, not inside it, but in the ring where its exhale changes the local physics by a degree. The stone around it is always a little cooler; the fog there condenses first; the dampness becomes reliable. Life likes reliability. It clusters around dependable gradients the way you cluster around a hearth.

If I lived in that ring, I would become a kind of collar—thicker film, older chemistry, more layered communities. I would be where reflective patches are most useful, because the light is poorest there and the hidden spaces are most crowded. I would learn to throw stolen dimness into undercuts, not as display, not as beauty, but as a tool: a brief illumination that lets a feeder find a seam, lets a clinger recognize its own kind in the press of bodies, lets a swarm coordinate without needing sound.

And then—because the bowl is full of borrowed machinery—I can’t avoid a third possibility.

Maybe I wouldn’t be biological at all.

Maybe I’d be what’s left of a sensor system that was once part of the industrial promise: a pressure gauge, a vibration pickup, a thermocouple bolted to the wrong place and never removed. Corroded, half-swallowed by patina, wired to nothing, but still capable of responding. Still capable of a tiny change in resistance, a tiny generated current, a little warmth when the hatch exhales and the metal contracts.

In a brighter world that would be scrap. Here it could be a spine.

The bowl is good at repurposing. A beam becomes a roost. A pipe becomes a drip schedule. A rust cave becomes a nursery. Why wouldn’t an abandoned sensor become a nerve?

If I were that—if I were the bowl’s leftover instrumentation—I wouldn’t have agency in the human sense. I wouldn’t “do” much. But I would register. I would be the place where vibrations gather: the faint tremble of a puddle that has no visible cause; the almost inaudible crackle when a rust-flake shears; the distant engine-note beyond the rim arriving through stone as a low reassurance.

And the film—the living sheen—would colonize me. It would take advantage of my surfaces, my micro-textures, the way I bridge materials. It would use my tiny currents or heat shifts the way the flyers use convection: not wasting effort, just riding what is already there.

In that coupling—dead machine and persistent chemistry—I begin to recognize what I am most likely to be in this world:

Not a single organism, not a single artifact, but an interface.

A thin, distributed intelligence made from the bowl’s willingness to let boundaries do the work. Water/stone. Metal/fog. Rust/patina. Warm/cool. Seepage/surface. Machine/reef.

I would be found everywhere a boundary holds long enough to become a habit.

Under the handrail where beads keep forming in the same spots until the corrosion shapes itself into cups. Along the pump house interior where the drip schedule is steady enough for a whole community to time itself to falling water. On the ceramic insulators, clean by rain, where condensation makes a different kind of wetness and the film learns a different chemistry. At the lip of the hatch, where breath makes weather locally and the bowl’s ecosystem tightens around that reliable exhale.

Who or what would I be?

I would be the bowl’s attention—spread thin, held close to surfaces, patient enough to become structure.

A sheen that can’t reach upward and doesn’t want to.

A memory that isn’t stored in a brain but in repeated contact: in polished stone, in softened bolt heads, in lanes of clarified water that exist because enough mouths agreed to keep them open.

In a place where sound is withheld and light is rationed, I would be the thing that listens by touch and sees by rearranging dimness. Not the flyer, not the burrower, not the filter-feeder—though I would feed them all, and borrow their movements as evidence that the world is still happening.

I would be the bowl’s skin, becoming more itself each time the water returns.
