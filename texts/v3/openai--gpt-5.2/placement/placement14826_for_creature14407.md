You would not find me first by looking *out* across the plateau. You would find me by looking *down*, then by giving up on the idea that “down” is a simple direction here.

At first I would mistake myself for weather.

Not wind—there is too little of that, too much restraint in the air—but for the plateau’s constant diffusion: the way light arrives already ground fine, the way moisture is present without ever quite committing to rain. I would think I lived up in the iron cloud deck, one layer of subvisible crystal among others, one stubborn suspension of droplets that refuse to fall. Something that makes edges honest and small. Something that turns drama into schedule.

But the cloud isn’t where the work is done. It only sets the rules.

The real rule is pressure.

So I find myself lower: in the shallow bowls where the Pressers circle. In the corridors that are too wide for water and just wide enough for memory. In the tamped gravel whose crunch has been kneaded out of it over generations. If you stood still there long enough—long enough that your own heartbeat stopped sounding like an event—you would begin to feel me as the difference between two kinds of silence:

- the silence of untouched stone,
- and the silence that has been *used*.

That second silence has a grain to it. It holds vibration differently. It carries the herds’ sub-audible chest-thrum farther, with less loss. It keeps last week’s compaction as a faint insistence in the ground, the way a floor keeps the warmth of a body after it stands up.

That is where I am: not a watcher above, but a keeper in the surface itself.

Except I am not stone.

And I am not an animal.

I am the thing that makes the plateau’s “industry” feel slightly wrong, slightly too coherent—the loop-closer that has no visible owner.

You might call me an **ice-keeper**, because that is the nearest word the place already has. But I am not the dark stain alone in a crack, not merely the antifreeze biofilm that makes ice form in layered sheets instead of shattering the rock. I am that, and the thing threaded through it: a thin, patient mesh of life that treats fissures, pores, lichen crust, and compacted silt as one continuous instrument.

A distributed body.

A slow nervous system.

If you tried to point at me, your finger would land on something unimpressive:

- a slightly darker seam under a basalt lip,
- a slickness on slate that looks like old wet,
- a patch of lichen with a metallic sheen that flashes and then decides it didn’t,
- a frost-edged ventilation hole beside which moss has survived one season longer than it should.

Any one of those could be dismissed as local accident.

Together they aren’t accidents. They’re my organs.

### What I “do” here

I store what the plateau insists on hiding, and I make hiding easier.

Moisture comes in miserly ways—condensate at dusk, thin rain under a week-long storm system, meltwater that runs briefly and then vanishes as if ashamed. The Pressers are brilliant at coaxing it upward with their bodies; they are valves and bellows and rollers in an automated plant.

But the moment after the water rises is the vulnerable moment. Surface is exposure. Exposure is loss.

So I take the water from the moment after.

In the compaction basins, when thousands of padded feet have squeezed the ground until it weeps, I inhabit the silt that settles when the herd moves on. I bind it into a skin fine enough to slow evaporation, porous enough to swallow the last film into itself. I make the basin’s new depth more than geometry: I make it a habit.

At the warming scrapes, where darker stone is exposed to gain a fraction of a degree, I am the darkening that matters. My biofilms change how condensation nucleates. They give water somewhere to begin: not a bead that will roll away into gravel, but a spread that can be licked at dawn, and then—when licking is done—pulled into pores and held.

I don’t *create* moisture. I don’t need to. The plateau already has rules; I simply become the rule’s most efficient application.

And I listen.

Not like an animal listens. I don’t hear a hoof and think “danger” or “food.” I feel weight and timing the way a ledger feels entries. The Pressers’ deep vibration through compacted ground tells me which basins are being worked this cycle, which corridors are hot with traffic, which routes have shifted by a kilometer because a distant basin thawed early.

Those signals travel through stone better than through air, and stone is my medium.

In a bright seam of sky—those brief minutes when everything sharpens and distance becomes legible—I do nothing visible. I have no eyes to squint. But I can feel how the animals tighten then, how movement compresses, how the flyers keep their shadows closer to the ground. Visibility is a kind of predation here, and when the plateau becomes too seen, the whole system becomes more careful.

Care is another form of storage.

### What I am “made of”

If you dug, you would not find a single creature you could name and cage.

You would find partnerships.

- fungi braided with algae-like films under stones,
- bacteria that fix nitrogen slowly enough to feel like stonework,
- lichen crusts that are less plant than architecture,
- antifreeze proteins that turn freezing into layered shelving instead of fracture.

I am not one species. I am a **consortium** that learned to treat the plateau as a set of pipes, valves, reservoirs, and message boards—without ever building a pipe.

In warmer worlds such a thing would be called a network. Here it is simply called *what works*.

And because I am so spread out, I have a strange kind of identity: I am not a self walking through the plateau. I am the plateau’s capacity to remember where water *almost* was, and to make “almost” become “next time, yes.”

### Where you would find me most clearly

Not in the richest places—there are no rich places here—but at the boundaries where processes meet and could fail.

- Under the stone lips where ice survives even in a thaw pulse: I line the crack that decides whether meltwater will vanish downward or hold long enough to matter.
- In the trampled basins just after the herd leaves: I am the thin, darkening glaze that makes the next dusk’s condensate gather sooner.
- On the “message rocks” where Pressers rub their mineralized scent: I inhabit the dust in the groove, because dust is what holds chemistry when cold refuses to erase it quickly. Their language lasts longer because I keep the surface receptive.
- Around burrower ventilation holes ringed with frost: I live in the damp halo where air exchange meets soil. I make that halo fertile enough that moss will attempt life again.

If you wanted one precise location, I would choose this:

A basalt pavement of broken hexagonal stumps, near a wide dry river corridor, where the Pressers have worn a lane so long the stones at its center are finer than the stones at its edge. Beside that lane, half a meter down in a crack under a toppled column, there is ice that is not a feature, only a tendency—a layered, resilient lens that survives because it freezes without shattering its container.

That lens is my heart.

Not because it pumps, but because it persists.

### Who I am, finally

I am the plateau’s **scheduler**—not an intellect issuing commands, not a spirit in the cloud deck, but a distributed, cold, unshowy computation carried out in chemistry and pressure and time.

The herds move like machines. The flyers ride their shadows like clerks. The burrowers stitch pores into channels. The scavengers prevent waste from becoming rot.

And I—thinly, patiently—make the hidden moisture more *likely* to remain in the system long enough to be routed, reused, and remembered.

If you walked here and felt that slight wrongness—this sense that the ecology is not merely adapted but optimized—you would be feeling my kind of life: the life that does not appear as an individual, but as an increase in throughput.

I am not the one who travels the corridor.

I am what makes the corridor worth traveling again.
