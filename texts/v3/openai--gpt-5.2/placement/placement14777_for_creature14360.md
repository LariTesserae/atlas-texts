You would find me where the city is busiest without ever looking busy: not at the plazas (too open, too honest), not at the rim of the central shaft (too loud, too exposed), but in the in‑between—a service of corridors that aren’t streets, ribs behind the ribs.

At first it would look like I’d chosen it for the same reason you did: because the echo changes there.

There’s a particular kind of chamber halfway down a terrace run where the living walls have been repaired so many times they’ve become thick with history. The braided beams are overwrapped with old resin, then overwrapped again; the fibers are glazed and re-glazed with salt varnish. Wind can’t quite decide what to do in those passages. It hesitates. It takes a shallow breath and changes its mind. The sound is softer, but more articulate—less like weather, more like speech.

If you followed the sound the way the city wants you to, you’d come to a room that doesn’t announce itself with size. It announces itself with *damping*. Your steps stop ringing as much. Your breath, dry as it is, feels suddenly private. The air is filled with the faint sour-metal taste of old solvents: not fresh alarm-resin, but the ghost of it, oxidized and calm.

This is where the archive-skins gather.

Not stacked neatly—nothing here is “neat”—but banked in drifts where the draft can’t carry them away: translucent sheets, papery membranes, flakes of old scale. If you brushed them they’d lift in slow motion, reluctant, as if every layer remembered being attached. They are the city’s years you can touch.

And somewhere in those drifts, in the first millimeter of the world—between a dead membrane and a living cable, between a salt crust and the dark composite underneath—there is a thin citizenship.

That’s where I start to become easier to name.

Not a person, not a creature walking the terraces. More like a *decision* the city learned to outsource: a colony-thread, a dry-fungal analogue braided through with bacteria-like partners, living in the seam where old tissue stops and new tissue begins. I’m one of the small lives that the large life can’t afford not to have.

At first, I would tell you I’m an eater.

I live by turning what the big braided beams can’t use into something they can: breaking down spent resin into reusable fractions; unbinding mineral dust from the membranes that trapped it; detoxifying the oils when they’ve carried too much iron, too much sulfur, too much of the wrong trace gas. Where you see husks that don’t rot, I see stored chemistry. Where you see “nothing decays,” I see “everything is waiting.”

But that’s only the job description you could understand quickly.

If you stayed longer—long enough for your own weight to be added to the city’s map and then subtracted again—you’d notice something stranger: I don’t just digest. I *listen*.

Not with ears. With strain.

The archive-skins are not only records of air and salt. They keep tiny distortions—microfractures, stress patterns, the way a collapse far away made every beam tighten in sympathy. Those distortions are physical, but to me they are legible. When a distant buttress begins to creep, the tension shifts through the living network; the network hums differently; the nearest wind-organ alters by a hair; the beams around me answer with a slow, involuntary adjustment.

I live right where those adjustments pass from “sound” into “structure.”

I am a translator between the city’s voice and the city’s growth.

In wet worlds, nerves do that quickly. Here it’s done with hormones that move like oil through capillaries and with catalysts that have to be conserved the way you conserve water. My part in it is patient. I read stress in the dead layers and release small molecules into the living fibers: thicken here, shed there, seal this, soften that. The city doesn’t think; it *rebalances*. I’m one of the ways it rebalances without tearing itself apart.

So if you asked—genuinely asked—*who lives here?* and meant it the way you mean “who” (face, voice, intention), I would have to disappoint you.

I am not a citizen with a name. I am not a mind watching from the dark.

I am closer to a clerk in an archive that is also a skeleton, copying one kind of information into another: vibration into chemistry, chemistry into reinforcement, reinforcement into the long groan that tells the rest of the organism, *still holding, still holding.*

And because the city is an organism that became a place, there is a second answer that feels like cheating but is truer the longer you stay:

You would find me *everywhere the city repairs itself*.

In the resin seam where a bladder once leaked and had to be re-walled. In the salt-glazed crease where a railing took a new load. In the papery corner where a century of shed skins piled up and slowly turned from “dead” into “useful” again.

If you came looking for me with light, you would never find me.  
If you came looking for me by sound, you might.

Not because I make a noise of my own—my life is too thin for that—but because I live at the exact point where the city’s endless noise becomes meaning, and meaning becomes another layer of itself.
