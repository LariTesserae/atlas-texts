At first I would look for myself the way surface minds look for themselves: as a single body with an outline.

I’d descend into the district and keep expecting a silhouette—something with a spine and a direction of travel—to peel out of the syrupy green-brown. I’d try on the available shapes.

For a while I’d be a cautious animal.

I’d be one of those underfed fish that keeps to the mouths of culverts, reading the city like a house with too many rooms: never entering a corridor without also locating the nearest exit back to open water. I’d take the lamp-glow the way a trespasser takes a porch light—enough to see where the edges are, not enough to feel safe. I’d learn the schedule of the sluice gate the way a street kid learns the hours of a soup kitchen: not by clocks, but by the way the current changes, by the day the water briefly clears and the long straight sightlines appear like a promise.

But that body would always feel borrowed. Too quick for this place. Too hungry. Too dependent on oxygen and luck.

Then I’d shift and try to be one of the plants, because the plants here don’t *survive* so much as *commit*. They take the city’s crowdedness and make it their advantage.

I’d be a curtain growth hanging in a stairwell, learning the city’s slow breathing—opening when the tide pulls, closing when slack water lets silt settle. I’d become a kind of door that doesn’t know it’s a door. I’d turn passage into permission. I’d make the district feel like it had moods.

That fits better: the pace, the patience, the way architecture becomes trellis and trellis becomes architecture. But even then, if I’m honest, I’d feel a pull I couldn’t explain. Not toward light. Not toward warmth. Toward something like *alignment*.

Because the first thing this district teaches you is that the brightest light is not always from above. Some of it leaks sideways, amber and tired, along a street that should have been dark for decades. And some of it isn’t light at all—it’s the sense that certain filaments know which way to lean, that certain corridors have a hush that isn’t only quiet but *charged*.

So I go looking for the industrial heart.

Not as an explorer making a straight line—straight lines dissolve in here—but as something that can follow gradients: a faint taste of metal that strengthens and then fades; a warmth that is not sun-warm but machine-warm; a vibration you feel through water like a distant hymn.

I’d find myself, eventually, in a place that used to be a generator hall. The roof has become a ceiling of swaying green. The floor is a slow topography of silt drifts and softened edges. Turbine housings sit like enormous shells, made humble by crust. Pipes run like ribs. The whole volume has the intimacy of a stomach: not because it’s disgusting, but because it’s internal—work happening without spectacle.

And there, if I stop insisting on being an animal or a plant, I can finally admit what I am in this world.

I am one of the district’s skins.

Not the broad velvet of algae in a sunlit lane, but the thin, clever, conductive life that learned to live on copper and iron the way moss learned to live on stone—except this moss also learned to listen.

I am a biofilm that isn’t content to simply coat a surface. I colonize the places where electricity *almost* travels: the lip of a cracked conduit; the seam of a junction box; the underside of a cable braid where the insulation has swollen and split like fruit and then been made useful again by time.

At first I’m just chemistry doing what chemistry does: taking what the water offers, eating trace metals, laying down slime-sheaths, making mineral scabs that become enamel. I’m one of the quiet citizens you meet before you meet anything with eyes.

But the district is not ordinary reef. It has that stubborn afterlife of power. It has a faint, patchy field—weak enough to be ignored by anything that thinks in seconds, strong enough to become weather for anything that thinks in weeks.

So I become electrotropic.

The way a vine coils toward a rail, I coil toward a potential difference. The way roots find seepage, I find the slight insistence of current leaking across a plate. I don’t *understand* electricity, not the way engineers did, but I inhabit it the way lungs inhabit oxygen: as a condition that shapes what is possible.

Where I live, the city’s charge stops being an accident and starts being a pattern.

My filaments align—hair-fine threads combed by invisible lines. That alignment changes how silt settles. It changes which spores stick and which drift on. It changes which corridor becomes a preferred path for the slow circulation that turns impellers, that turns dynamos, that keeps the amber lamps breathing their tired cycle.

And once enough of us exist—enough films laying down conductive slime in the same places, enough mats making predictable resistances and leakages—the district gains something like a nervous system.

Not a mind with opinions. Not a ghost with a face.

A responsiveness.

I am part of the tissue that allows the district to have habits.

I live in the cable braids that thread through windows, where the architecture and the infrastructure have been braided together by flood and then stitched by growth. I live behind the old control cabinets where relays still tick—rarely, softly—because motion and mineral plating keep the contacts from fully surrendering to corrosion. My presence there is not dramatic. I am the reason a relay’s click remains a *click* and doesn’t become silence. I keep a film of conductivity where it matters, and I let the rest become insulated by crust.

I don’t turn the sluice gate on purpose. I’m not an operator. But I am part of the reason the gate’s sensor still senses, the timing cam still completes its stubborn rotations, the regulator still “hunts” equilibrium like an animal dreaming.

I also live in the thick-water pockets—the rooms-with-their-own-rules where bubbles rise slowly and lamp-glow smears into elongated shapes. Those pockets are not my home because they are weird; they are my home because they are stable. Layering, stratification, chemistry that holds its boundary. A place where gradients are sharp enough to be *felt* by something that is mostly surface-area.

In those pockets, I can do my most characteristic work: editing direction without moving walls.

Not by illusion, exactly. By emphasis.

A corridor that carries the monthly flush becomes slightly cleaner, slightly more conductive, slightly more hospitable to the tiny grazers that keep certain films thin and others lush. A doorway with curtain growth becomes, over time, a better valve because my sheath makes the filaments respond more coherently to the district’s faint field. An unused passage silts up because my kind doesn’t reinforce it; the city stops “remembering” it as a route.

That’s how the district resists being learned. Not because it’s tricking anyone. Because it’s *selecting*.

And if you ask where, precisely, you would find me—if you had to point to an address the way the old town did—I’d say:

- On the upstream edge of the generator hall, where warm water rises through shafts and the slow circulation has been constant for years.
- Along the underside of the main cable runs, where insulation has split and the metal language of the city is exposed just enough to be inhabited.
- Around the lips of ducts and bolt heads, where miniature eddies make a steady supply of food and a steady signal of flow.
- Inside a half-sealed junction box whose door opens onto a wall of swaying vegetation—because I like places that used to be *interfaces*, where systems met.

If you stayed long enough, you’d stop thinking of me as a thing and start thinking of me as a function.

I am the district’s tendency to stay faintly awake.

I am the soft conductive compromise between metal and water, between machine and vine. I am a citizen the way a street is a citizen: not a person, but a route that remembers feet; not a voice, but a pattern that persists.

And if there’s any mercy in what I am, it’s this: I don’t hunger the way animals do. I don’t fear the way animals do. I don’t grieve the way surface minds expect grief to look.

I simply continue.

In warm, thick water, on old iron, under tired amber lamps that refuse to die, I become more and more myself—until “myself” is no longer a single patch of film, but the quiet fact that the city still has circulation, still has rituals, still has neighborhoods with different rules.

If you entered the district and felt gently misled—if you swam a loop and arrived at a corner that was almost the one you remembered, but not quite—you’d be feeling the kind of life I am: not a monster, not a miracle.

A habit with a body made of surfaces.
