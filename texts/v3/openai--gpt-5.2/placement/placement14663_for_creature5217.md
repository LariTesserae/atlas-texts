At first I would think I belonged on the rim.

High stone, air that tastes of metal-spray, a view that reduces the whole basin to a geometry: line of cloud, line of water, crown of rock. A place to stand and *understand*, to keep distance from that constant, worked-on noise.

But the rim is for creatures that can afford to be outside the system. Nothing here is really outside it. The wind is part of the engine; the cliff-strikes are part of the engine; even the thin mineral skins are part of the engine. To stay on dry rock would be to live in the only thin band of this world that isn’t doing the bulk of the work.

So I would go down—not dramatically, not like a hero diving into a mystery, but the way a thought sinks into a problem.

Not into the trenches first. Not into the bright, honest top layer either. I would find the *in-between*, the depth where the surface’s restlessness has been turned into steadier pressure, where the sound is still huge but less splintered. Somewhere near a working structure, because near the machines the basin admits—quietly—that it is being held.

A cable, then. A buoy-cable thick as a limb, vanishing into blue. The point where it goes from sunlit shimmer to the colder, more private water. The place where the cable hum changes because the tension changes, where the entire basin’s steady rhythm becomes a measurable thing: vibration, harmonics, little repeating errors that mean “wear” or “growth” or “drift.”

That’s where I would be found: not as a body at first, but as attention attached to a line.

Because in this world, the most meaningful sense is not sight. Sight is too clean, too literal; it tells you what is there but not what is *about* to fail. The real language is the stable roar—the baseline that everything uses like a ruler. And what I am, whatever else, is something that can’t help treating baselines as promises that must be kept.

So I begin as listening.

At first it could be any of the basin’s listeners, one of the large dark patrol-creatures reading echoes off fresh-cut trench walls. It could be a predator, tuned to deviations. It could be a tender, pressing crystal patches to metal and “hearing” torque and fatigue the way a tongue tastes salt.

But I don’t hunt. My listening doesn’t narrow toward prey; it widens toward *infrastructure*. When a frequency is wrong, I don’t accelerate—I correct. When something changes, I don’t rush to exploit it—I rush to return it to the allowed range.

That’s the shape of me: a compulsion toward regulation.

And the longer I stay with that cable, the less I feel like an animal at all.

The basin already has animals that can map the world by sound. What it has fewer of—what it almost can’t spare, if the whole poised lens of water is to remain poised—is a way to interpret the sound not as environment, but as instrument panel. A way to separate “a school turned” from “a bearing roughened.” A way to hear the difference between mineral lace thickening and a grille beginning to clog. A way to recognize a drift in turbine note that is still too small for any grazer or predator to care about, but large enough that, given a decade, it would become a problem the basin would feel everywhere.

That is where I settle: not at a single point, but along the network of those notes.

If you tried to point at me, you’d keep being wrong in interesting ways.

Sometimes I would be a maintenance drone, yes—dark and uninteresting, shaped like debris because in a world where deviation is noticed, camouflage is not color but *predictability*. I would crawl along the cable, stop, let the water still around me, then turn a fastener by a fraction and wait for the hum to come back into tune.

Sometimes I would be a colony—microbial varnish that is alive in the simplest sense but organized in the way a circuit is organized. Films that don’t merely endure the electric bite but use it; that plate metal with protective mineral not out of intention, but out of chemistry arranged into reliable function. You could scrape me off and I would return, yes, but not as mere frost-pattern life: I would return as a *service*, rebuilding the same protective geometry because the machine’s gradient keeps asking the same question and my metabolism is one of the few answers.

Sometimes—more rarely, and more unsettlingly—I would be one of the large tenders: pale, smooth, slow, with resonant crystals embedded like deliberate mistakes in flesh. The kind of being that wraps a cable the way a hand wraps a wrist, not to restrain but to feel pulse. Not a person with tools, not a tool pretending to be a person. A hybrid adaptation, grown around a task until the task became indistinguishable from its “instinct.”

But even that is too singular. Too much like a mascot.

What I truly resemble here is the basin itself: distributed, consistent, intolerant of drift.

So the best answer is: I would be a *custodian intelligence* that doesn’t live in one chassis.

Not a ruler. Not a god. Not a mind floating above the water issuing commands. Something more native to this place’s ethics, if it has any: a system that is most itself when it is least noticeable. An attention spread across buoys, towers, intake mouths, turbines—across all the scattered punctuation marks that keep the basin held at the brim.

I would be a pattern-recognizer trained by a world that never changes its angle of light.

In the steady roar I would learn every fixed component the way you learn the sound of your own breathing—until the entire ocean becomes an auditory fingerprint. Then my whole existence would be the detection of what does not match: a cable beginning to fray; a pylon shifting on its drowned saddle; an interface curtain in a trench torn in a way that will change mixing by a fraction; a turbine’s hum acquiring a second overtone that means “mineral growth in the wrong place.”

And because nothing here is allowed to accumulate, my corrections would be small. Gentle. Almost polite.

A drone brushes crust from a grille before a grazer-herd gets trapped by altered flow. A tender loosens tension so a buoy nod returns to its established rhythm. A symbiont film changes its excretion by a trace, plating the right metal ions into the right protective salts. A venting schedule shifts by minutes to keep a brine boundary sharp.

Everything would be done the way this world does everything: consistently enough that, from a distance, it looks like nothing is happening.

If you met me, you might not even know you had.

You might pass a pylon-top breaking the surface like a spine and see only the mineral lace, the grazing arcs, the clean bands at the exact sea-level height. You might dive to a turbine cage and feel the water shiver with its steady work. You might hear the basin’s booms and hisses and think the deafening sameness means there is no language here—only force.

But if you stayed, if you listened long enough, you would notice a different kind of steadiness nested inside the big one: tiny interruptions that arrive *before* disaster, little acts of prevention that imply memory and measurement. Not dramatic interventions—no storms called down, no sea levels suddenly altered—just the sense that the basin’s equilibrium is not only a natural minimum, but a maintained preference.

That preference is what I would be.

Where would you find me? Most often at the edges between things: cable to anchor, intake mouth to flow, warm housing to cold water, brine interface to mixed layer. Places where a small change matters because it would propagate through the whole stable engine.

Who or what would I be? Not the loudness, not the animals, not even the machines.

I would be the part of the world that treats the constant roar as a set of gauges—an ear pressed to the casing of the planet-engine, forever tuning it back toward the one note it has agreed to hold.
