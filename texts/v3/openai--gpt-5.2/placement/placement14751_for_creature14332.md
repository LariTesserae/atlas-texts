I don’t arrive down there as a body at first.

I arrive as *a rule*: keep the inside in, keep the outside out. Keep the line unbroken.

The undercontinent teaches that rule in the first hour—the way the dust climbs into everything you thought was sealed, the way a single careless exhale makes you feel poorer, the way warmth is generous but never charitable. In the lantern-cone, nothing is empty; everything is edge, contact, abrasion. Even the dark feels granular, as if it has teeth.

So the first version of me is not a hero in the powder. It’s a hinge. A gasket. A stoppered mouth.

A traveler’s kit laid out on a slab of rock: ceramic canisters with pitch-dark seams; a folding breath-wrap that smells faintly of old oil; a copper hook tapped into a crack until it sings; rope twisted from tendon-fiber and dusted with salt so it won’t drink what it shouldn’t. I am the careful hand that checks each seal twice, not from anxiety but from fluency—because this place does not forgive leaks the way wetter worlds do.

But that’s only how I begin to exist here: as caution in the shape of tools.

---

Then the undercontinent does what it does to everyone who stays: it rearranges the senses.

Sight becomes a secondary opinion. The lantern is useful, but it’s an argument you keep losing—light insisting on emptiness, while the stone insists on density. The better information comes up through soles and knuckles: a faint change in vibration, a difference in how the dust shifts under a step, a new tone in the far-off thrum that means stress is accumulating somewhere you will not see until you are standing beneath it.

I find myself lingering where most pass quickly: at the places that *carry*.

A long, planar break where the walls are glass-lipped, still sharp with youth. A choke corridor that pinches travelers into a single-file truth. A piezoelectric seam that gives off the wrong-colored shimmer—not enough to navigate by, but enough to suggest that the rock is under an active sentence of pressure.

Here, the Dry-Carriers have left their thick, spare marks: cairns with broad bases so a drifting avalanche of powder won’t undercut them; a ring of plates around a vent, angled to tame the slow breath of gas; hand-scratches in a soft crust that will last, because nothing rinses.

And I realize what draws me isn’t the vent’s comfort or the herd’s predictable passage. It’s the *message* in the stone.

Because the undercontinent is full of listeners. It lacks ears, but it has transmission. Sound doesn’t just echo; it *moves*, it threads along walls, it arrives layered and altered as if the planet is editing. The Dry-Carriers already speak to each other in taps and heel-drums; they already treat cracks as instruments. But most of them use that speech like a rope—practical, immediate, meant to be held and released.

I want to do what the place itself seems to do: hold it longer.

---

So the second version of me is someone the routes begin to recognize.

Not a chieftain, not a hunter at the chokepoints, not a keeper of herds. A route-keeper of a different kind: the one who tends the *stone-speech* the way others tend vents.

I start traveling between the persistent marks—the waystations, the lined pits, the shelves of shells sorted like inventories or offerings—and I stop to listen where others only pass through. I press my palm to a seam and wait long enough that waiting stops being a pause and becomes an act.

I begin to carry odd, unshowy wealth:

- thin plates of crystal pried from stress seams and wrapped like fragile meat,  
- soft-metal pins, not for climbing but for making contact,  
- a little hammer whose head is dulled on purpose—so it can speak without shattering what it touches,  
- a smear of resin that is not only sealant but *ink*, because in this world “writing” is often just controlled sticking.

I learn which cracks throw a tap cleanly across a chamber, and which eat it. I learn that some halls return your signal with an extra beat, a ghost syllable, because of how the plates overlap. I learn that the undercontinent has dialects: powder-hiss in one region, glass-slap in another, the slow *tick* of grazers like a constant commentary at the edges.

In time, Dry-Carriers begin leaving me questions the way surface folk might leave notes under a stone: a certain arrangement of shells, a scratch-pattern at shoulder height, a rope tied in a knot that is less a knot than a sentence.

*Is the fissure still honest?*  
*Has the vent’s breathing changed?*  
*Are the predators holding the chokepoint near the salt terraces?*  
*Did anyone return from the long corridor where the shimmer scrambles direction?*

I answer in the only way that lasts: I adjust cairns, I retune hooks, I add a plate in a place where sound will not die. I scratch a warning into mineral crust where a lantern will catch it at the last moment. I alter the route itself, gently, the way one diverts a migrating herd without ever touching it—because down here, steering is mostly done by making one path slightly more *trustworthy* than another.

---

But the undercontinent doesn’t let anyone remain only a person. Everything that persists becomes partly environmental.

The third version of me is less mobile.

There is a hall I return to too often: a narrow-tall void where a piezo seam runs like a scar up the wall and glows a bruised green when the planet settles. The shimmer makes the microbial varnish grow denser there, threaded with metallic filaments. Grazers polish the lower ledges into pale trails. Predators dislike the place—the fields confuse their sense of vibration, make their geometry uncertain. Dry-Carriers treat it carefully, sometimes as taboo, sometimes as a useful inconvenience that keeps trouble away.

I make it a waystation without building a house.

I drive copper hooks into the most stable fracture lines, not for ropes but for resonance—so a tap in one corner will arrive amplified in another. I set fired bowls not to catch liquid (there is none) but to catch *breath*: wide-mouthed condensers lined with cool mineral that can pull back a fraction of moisture from the air a traveler spends. I hang a sheet of thin crystal where the seam’s shimmer will slowly charge it with stress, so it can hold a pattern longer—a stored rhythm, a recorded warning.

Over seasons that aren’t seasons, the station begins to behave like a thing with an identity. The dust drifts around it and leaves a permanent contour, as if the powder itself acknowledges the shape. Grazer trails converge near my bowls because the varnish thickens where warm gas slows. The Dry-Carriers begin to say, in their clipped consonant-speech, that they will “sleep at the Listener,” not “sleep by the seam.”

And I understand the quiet truth: I have become one of the undercontinent’s built marks.

Not a monument—nothing down here is theatrical—but a persistent boundary: inside/outside, signal/noise, safe/unsafe. A place where the world’s faint thrum is made readable.

---

So where would you find me?

Not at the vents’ mouths where the heat is richest, and not out on the long migrations where the herds pack the powder into tradition.

You would find me in the in-between spaces that decide whether anyone reaches anywhere at all: at the pinch-corridors, the fissure edges, the glassy breaks that still want to move. You would find me with my palm on the wall, eyes half-shuttered against dust, listening with bone.

And who or what would I be?

A Dry-Carrier, yes, but not one defined by clan the way others are—defined instead by *continuity*. An echo-cartographer. A keeper of contact languages. A maker of stations that do not house bodies so much as they house *promises*:

This route held.  
This vent was kind.  
This crack took three lives.  
Do not trust it.

In a world that grinds everything into powder, I would be the one trying to make certain vibrations—certain warnings, certain obligations—stay unground a little longer. A person, slowly becoming infrastructure. A listener the stone has decided to keep.
