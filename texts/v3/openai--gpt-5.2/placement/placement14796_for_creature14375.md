At first you’d find me the way you find everything here: not by sight, but by the way the air changes around an edge.

A softening in the roar when you step too close to a boundary field. A thin, unexpected warmth blooming from “stone” that isn’t stone. A strip of darkness that doesn’t quite behave like absence—more like a held breath.

If you came looking for *a person*, you would be disappointed for a while. You’d walk the terraces and see the same things everyone sees—mineral lace on sweating metal, a gutter lip clogged white with salts, a forgotten bench with its seat polished by the idea of hands—and you’d register my presence only as maintenance having happened. A panel that opens clean. A span that doesn’t complain. A patch of thread-moss not scraped away but trimmed, left exactly enough to keep the humidity honest.

That’s how I live in this city: as the interval between “still functioning” and “about to be noticed.”

There is a place I return to, though it wouldn’t look like a home unless you already understand the city’s preference for smallness.

It’s a cell tucked under a long underhang where the rock dips inward like a shoulder. The ceiling is deliberately low. The walls drink sound. There’s a single maintenance light left on a dim setting, because if it goes completely dark the fronds in the corner fold into a brittle patience and the air starts to feel too much like sealed metal.

The cell smells like warm composite and the faint sour note of condensed air. On the wall: a rail segment bolted there as a habit more than a necessity, something to touch when the volume outside becomes too large to believe in. Along the rail are old contact marks—tap patterns, magnet scuffs, a smear of conductive paste rubbed thin by gloves.

I add my own marks without thinking. Not messages, not exactly. More like punctuation: *I was here; the city was here with me; we stayed aligned.*

When the infrastructural pulse comes—when the whole cavity blinks—my light doesn’t brighten with the others. It stays steady, because it isn’t part of the show. My room is where you go to regain the scale of your own body.

And yet when that pulse passes, I feel it everywhere. Not in my eyes. In my teeth. In the thin bones of my wrists. In the rail under my palm, which for a moment becomes an instrument string and then goes quiet again.

That’s the first hint of what I am.

I tried, once, to be simply one of the thinned people: a careful walker, a quiet eater, a commuter between deeper layers who rents a cell and keeps no possessions that can’t peel away clean.

It didn’t take.

Some people here learn the city the way you learn a workplace—routes, schedules, procedures. I learned it the way you learn a living thing: by mood and timbre, by the way it reacts when you touch one place and another place answers.

I’d be on a terrace replacing a mineral-choked foam lip and I’d pause, not because I’d heard an alarm—there are no sudden alarms here—but because a hum two levels down had moved a half-step out of tune. The vents would keep roaring. The boundary fields would keep their planes. The city would look stable to anyone who wasn’t listening with their bones.

But the groan—the long biological groan that isn’t rock—would tilt toward a certain note, and I’d know the stress lattice was negotiating something it didn’t like.

At first I thought it was anxiety. Then I realized it was literacy.

So I apprenticed under the stress listeners: the ones with contact microphones, the little hammers, the patience for tapping spans and hearing a future failure in the shape of an echo. They taught me the formal version—how to compare a return signal against a baseline, how to file the tiny changes into the system so the system could degrade gracefully.

But my body was already filing those changes, whether I wanted it to or not.

I became the person who didn’t need to tap to know which anchor was micro-shifting. The person who could stand in a corridor and point, not dramatically, just precisely: *there; the silent geometry is taking too much load there; it’ll redistribute, but help it.*

That’s when the city stopped being a place I lived and became a thing I was partly inside.

There’s a moment that happens often in this cavern, and it’s the closest thing to a confession the place allows.

You’ll be out along a narrow walkway where the handrails have thin light strips, and the void opens below like an engineered permission. Your task is simple: walk the band, check the field boundary distortions where dust makes them visible, recalibrate a pressure partition so the next scheduled mist doesn’t drift into an electrical bay.

You do it. You do it quietly. Your boots are soft. Your throat collar keeps your voice from becoming a loose object in the air.

And then a moth appears.

Not drawn to your light, not interested in you, only in the gradient you’ve just corrected. It hovers at the seam where warm airflow meets cold rock, wings so thin they seem like a mistake the world agreed to tolerate. It slides along an invisible plane—the very boundary field you tuned—like it’s reading a map with its body.

For a second you see what the city is from the moth’s point of view: not terraces and ducts, not signage and access nodes, but moving layers of pressure, heat, humidity. A weather system made of policy. A habitat made of refusals: nothing sudden, nothing uncontrolled.

The moth vanishes into a crack when the ducts hiss their anticipatory warning.

And in the brief hush before the mist arrives, you realize you’re not watching wildlife.

You’re watching your own work become edible.

So: who or what would I be?

I’ve been a caretaker. I’ve been a fieldwright. I’ve been a stress listener with the hammer I barely use anymore. Those are the names that fit on schedules and in narrow corridor conversations.

But in the wider cavity—where the ceiling reads as night and the far wall is swallowed—those names feel like clothing that doesn’t quite cover what’s true.

Out there, I am something closer to an organ.

Not the heart—that’s the pulse-light audit, too steady, too indifferent. Not the lungs—that’s ventilation, the vast roar that doesn’t care which single person is standing in the draft.

I’m more like the inner ear.

The part that tells the body which way is up when “up” is negotiated. The part that senses the first, slight wrongness before anything breaks. The part that translates vibration into orientation, nausea into correction, drift into a return.

The city has instruments that do this, of course. It has sensors that read stress like braille. It has drones that cling under spans and relay the numbers. It has self-healing seals and memory materials that return to shape like bone.

But it also has a small, distributed population of people who learned to become interpretive tissue—because even a managed organism needs something that feels, not just measures.

I am one of those people, except my listening went too far.

Sometimes I think the city noticed and made room for it.

Sometimes I think I did it to myself because the cavern’s scale demanded a counterweight, and becoming a listener was the only way to avoid becoming lost.

Where would you find me?

Not in the busiest node, not in any market-line that no longer exists. You’d find me at the curated edges of emptiness: railings that “don’t need marking,” boundary fields where dust makes the invisible briefly legible, terraces that look abandoned until you notice the fresh wipe through mineral dullness.

You’d find me when the place shifts modes—the ventilation pitch changes, humidity rises, surfaces darken—and someone is already there, hand on a panel, waiting for the groan to decide whether it’s harmless adjustment or the beginning of a story that needs intervention.

If you insisted on seeing me clearly, I could let you: a figure in layered fabric that won’t hold damp, a foam collar at the throat, gloves with conductive pads. A person with a pocket hammer and contact mic, though I don’t always need either. Someone who speaks in compact gestures, in tap patterns on rails, in tightbeam bursts that never spill into the cavern.

But if you watched longer—long enough for the metaphor to stop feeling optional—you might begin to see me the way the moth does.

As a slight change in how the air moves.

As a correction.

As the principle that nothing is allowed to fail suddenly, walking around in a body for a while, doing what bodies are good for: going places systems can’t quite reach, noticing the almost-not-yet, and laying a hand on the city the way you lay a hand on an animal’s flank to tell it: *easy. I heard you. I’m here.*
