At first I think I’d choose a hut—anyone dropped into this country of ledges and shorn cliffs learns to crave a corner, a roof-weight, a place where the wind has to go around you instead of through you. I picture myself where the ground is dry enough to keep a fire and the gusts are steady enough to predict; a shoulder of rock with a view that’s too wide to be comforting. I picture hands stained with ash-glaze, wringing fog from a woven screen into a jar that sweats back at me, and I tell myself: *I would be one of the few, a fog-tender, a person of intervals.*

But the longer I stay in the thought of this land, the less I believe I’d last as a person with a single home.

This place doesn’t reward possession. It rewards *attention*—not the sharp, hungry kind, but the patient kind that can sit inside roar and still notice the moment the timbre changes. The land is all thresholds: between wet and dry, between cliff-face sun and cleft-shadow cool, between “the wind is a wall” and “the wind is a corridor.” People here survive by learning those thresholds, and then by letting them change without feeling betrayed.

So I start moving my imagined self downslope, not into danger, but into geometry.

Not the high ridges where the wind flays everything into the same hard honesty, and not the canyon bottoms either, where the ink-colored depth keeps its own damp secrets. Somewhere midway, where a permanent fall makes its own room: a hollow behind water, dark at noon, always breathing mist. The stone there is black with years of slickness. Moss has laid itself like velvet on any ledge that isn’t constantly struck; ferns have taken the reliable spray as permission to become extravagant. The air is never still, but it’s *organized*—broken into currents by the curtain of water, the overhang, the throat of the gorge. Sound behaves differently there. Words have edges. Breath has weight.

And in that particular kind of half-shelter, I don’t imagine myself as the one who speaks.

I imagine myself as what *hears*.

Not an animal—animals here are too wise to be always present. Not a spirit either. Something more local than that. Something with no ambition to move, that nonetheless participates in every passage.

A seam in the rock, perhaps: one of those mineral bands that fog prefers, a line that beads even when everything else stays dusty. A fissure that takes the first condensation at dusk and gives it back as a thin, steady drip just before dawn. People learn such seams the way other people learn wells. They come close, press their palms, feel the cold honesty of it. They speak into it—not to charm it, but to read it.

I can almost feel what it is to be that seam.

To be a narrow place where the land’s restlessness becomes legible. To take the chaos of wind and, by the accident of my shape, turn it into a note. To answer a chant not with meaning but with *response*: a slight thickening of mist along the path of a voice, a visible ribbon for a heartbeat—enough to reveal a hidden void, enough to confirm that beyond this wall is hollow, and hollow means shelter, and shelter means survival.

Then I change again, because the seam alone is too private. Too much of a secret.

Higher up, on a ridge where the standing stones are set—not monuments but calibrations—I find a different version of myself. I stand there in the mind like one of those stones: placed where the strongest winds have to declare themselves. I don’t resist; resisting would be theatrical and short-lived. I simply *take the wind* and make it diagnostic.

A pillar of rock (or a hauled and planted slab) with a particular cut: a bevel that catches the winter stream, a notch that sings only when storms are stacking in the next valley, a face that vibrates when the updrafts begin their late-morning argument. Lichen writes on me in wedges and crescents—an incremental script of seasons that never hurries, even as everything around me does. Moss thickens at my base on the lee side, a small green certainty. My shadow becomes a clock for someone who knows where to place their feet when fog drops and the world shrinks to arm’s length.

If I were that stone, I would be useful without being owned.

People would check me the way they check the sky. They’d put a hand on my windward face and feel the temperature difference that means rain is building from terrain, not horizon. They’d listen for the ridge-flute in my notch and decide whether to cross scree now or wait. In calmer hours—loud, but calmer—they might lean close and speak into the shallow crack near my base, the one that answers with a particular hollow boom, and they’d nod: *the tunnel is clear; the grotto still breathes; the seep is alive.*

And if you asked what I *am*, in the way you ask what lives here, I wouldn’t call myself a person.

I’d call myself an inhabitant the way an arch is an inhabitant.

A fixed thing that has learned one of the three arts by becoming it: not clinging, not hiding, but keeping time for the wind—translating violence into pattern, making the emptiness navigable by offering it a few dependable notes.

You would find me on a ridge that looks empty until it starts to sing. You would find me with lichen-maps on my skin and fog caught briefly against my colder face. You would find me where travelers stop without quite knowing why, listen with their whole ribs, and then choose the moment to move.
