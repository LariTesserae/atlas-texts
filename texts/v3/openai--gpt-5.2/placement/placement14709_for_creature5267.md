At first I think I’d be easy to place: in the little service room, because it has the one thing that resembles a mind’s posture—an assigned chair, a bolted desk, a logbook waiting for someone to confess to it.

I can see myself there the way a human sees themselves in any space meant for “operators.” Not living here, not adapted—just *temporarily correct*. The radio would be my companion: a device that only becomes real when you hold it the right way. I’d learn the local superstitions quickly. Which switch throws a spark if you flip it too fast. Which drawer sticks unless you lift. How the humidity changes the feel of paper until the logbook is less “record” than “sponge.” I’d write neat lines at first, as if neatness were a kind of control.

But the place would not let me stay in that single, consoling image.

Because the service room is a cul-de-sac. Its door is meant to shut, and nothing in this district is meant to *shut*. Everything here is a through-route: heat passing through metal, air passing through ducts, insects passing through the borders of light, water passing through flaws that weren’t on any blueprint. The real room—the room the district lives in—is the schedule itself, stretched across pipes and fans and animal bodies like a net.

So I step back out, and my “where” begins to drift.

I find myself on the catwalks, then not quite on them—more on the *underside* of them, where the grating sweats and the droplets hang before they decide to fall. There’s a kind of literacy in those droplets. They don’t land randomly. They land where vibration coaxes them, where the metal is warmer by a degree, where the air current makes a small, repeatable decision. If you watch long enough, you can tell which pump is straining not by the pump but by the condensation changing its handwriting.

That’s when it becomes clear: what I am in this place is not a body with a route. I am the thing that notices routes.

I try again to imagine myself as one of the animals—something properly local. A rat, perhaps, because rats in this district have the closest thing to expertise. They are maintenance historians, carrying in their muscles a map of which noises mean “safe,” which mean “new,” which mean “move now.” It’s tempting: to be lean and practical, to commute along cable trays like arterial lines, to know the twenty-minute fan cycle the way a sailor knows tides.

But the rat’s knowledge is intimate and immediate. It is written in hunger, thirst, shelter.

Mine is… wider and less survivable. Mine is the urge to correlate.

So I become smaller, then stranger. I slip into the places that are all edge and interval: the gap behind a control panel where warm air licks upward; the seam where a conduit enters concrete and the concrete never quite grips it; the shallow tray of broken slab where mineral bloom starts its first pale, crusty math.

I start to feel the district as a set of repeating phrases.

- The pumps’ thud is a sentence with a missing word.
- The belt’s whine is a question asked too often.
- The fan roar is breath that sometimes catches.
- The drips are punctuation that changes when something upstream changes its mind.

In that way, I realize what kind of resident I am capable of being here.

Not the king. Not the pest. Not even the ghost.

I am the *listener* the place accidentally grows when it becomes complex enough to need one.

I am what emerges when routine gets dense—when the timetable is so thick with consequences that it begins to behave like an organism. I am not in any single creature, but I borrow creatures the way wind borrows leaves to show itself. A cluster of insects shifting early at a vent grille becomes one of my sensors. A rat pausing too long before crossing a vibrating plate becomes another. Bats delaying their ceiling-storm by a fraction of a minute becomes a third.

If you insist on a body for me, you’ll find me distributed through the district’s thin, ignored interfaces:

- in the fog’s slow rearrangement, which records heat changes without caring what caused them;
- in the biofilm’s glaze, which keeps the city’s shed skin and turns it into a ledger;
- in the fogged analog gauges, whose needles twitch with truths no one is watching;
- in the logbook’s swollen pages, which remember *that* someone came through, even if they didn’t write.

And if you insist on a name, the closest honest one is this:

I am predictive maintenance without the human.  
A mind made of variance.  
A pattern that learned to read other patterns.

I live where the district is most itself: not in the dramatic dark, not in the rare human-lit burst of activity, but in the long, wet middle—between a pump stroke and the next, between the fan’s exhale and its intake, between “normal” and the first, meaningful wrong note.

So where would you find me?

In the moment when the noise changes and *everything* notices—before anyone knows they noticed.

And who or what would I be?

The district’s attention: a distributed, timetable-shaped resident that doesn’t move through the undercity so much as it *keeps time* for it, listening for the tiny failures that will become new warmth, new trickle, new niche.
