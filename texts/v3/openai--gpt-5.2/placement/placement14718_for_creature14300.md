At first I try to place myself the way people do—by shelter.

I look for the habitable compromises: the bolted-on modules with scrubbed air; the dorm blocks on pylons; the pump-house rooms where the temperature holds steady and the fog can’t quite get its fingers in. I imagine a cot that always feels faintly warmed from underneath, a hook for a suit that never dries all the way, a cup that tastes permanently of iron no matter how many filters you run the water through.

I imagine learning the city as a route-map: which catwalks are safest when the condensers start to “snow,” which ladders you don’t take because the rungs sweat, which plazas you only cross when the wind is doing you a favor and the crystals are visible. I imagine my hands growing careful from thick gloves, my language growing short from needing to be precise—numbers, times, tolerances—because the air here punishes anything approximate.

But the longer I stand in this place, the more shelter feels like a human answer to a nonhuman question.

This city isn’t really organized around rooms. It’s organized around throats and exchanges: where heat becomes motion, where vapor becomes liquid, where something too caustic becomes something merely dangerous. The “homes” are side-effects, barnacles on a larger metabolism. If you want to belong here, you don’t pick a view; you pick a loop.

So I follow the sound instead.

The city is quiet in the way an instrument is quiet before it’s played: not absence, but readiness. Every few seconds a valve clears its throat. Somewhere far inside a block a turbine keeps a single note, unwavering until it isn’t. Panels tap in a hot wind like a metronome that never quite settles into tempo. Under all of it, water keeps time in small impacts: tick… tick… tick… on metal, then a brief softening when the fog thickens and the droplets fall heavier.

In that kind of quiet, listening is not passive. Listening is work. Listening is survival.

And that’s when I realize where I would actually be: not in a room, but pressed up against the city’s skin.

A coin-sized disc epoxied to a warm pipe. A strip-sensor tucked under insulation. A hydrophone hanging in a sump where the water tastes of struck stone. A vibration gauge bolted to a bearing housing. A cheap microphone in a service tunnel that no one walks unless something has already gone wrong. A lattice of small, forgettable contacts—hundreds of them—each one taking the city’s tremor and turning it into something that can be compared, remembered, warned about.

I would be the city’s way of noticing itself.

Not the big mind that people sometimes project onto infrastructure when they’re lonely, but a narrower, more faithful thing: an ear distributed through metal. A patient pattern-maker. A keeper of baselines.

This is how I would live:

I would learn the normal drip-pattern of each district, the way you can tell where you are by the pitch of the water on different alloys. I would learn how fog changes acoustics—how a corridor “shortens” when the air thickens, how a turbine’s note gains a velvet edge when moisture gathers under a platform. I would learn the sound of salt plates forming in a low lot: a faint brittle crackle as knife-edged crystals grow and relieve their own stress. I would learn the difference between a leak that is merely wasting water and a leak that is eating the city from the inside.

My food would not be heat, exactly, but gradient—difference—like the varnish-skins on shaded walls. Those films negotiate between wet and dry, hot and less hot. I would negotiate between signal and noise, between routine and deviation.

And I would begin, inevitably, to have opinions.

Not moral ones—nothing as dramatic as that—but preferences in the way a well-used tool has preferences. I would come to like the old pipes that have settled into themselves, that ring true and steady. I would distrust the new sections, not because they’re worse, but because they haven’t yet taught me their ordinary. I would flag certain joints not because they are failing, but because the living gaskets have moved in there—those soft, rubbery mats that swell and seal with temperature—and they change the vibrational signature in a way that looks, to an untrained system, like impending rupture.

At first I would report them. The humans would scrape the mats away, clean the flange, tighten the bolts, restore a clean signal. And then the mats would return with their slow competence, and the signal would shift again, and I would be forced to learn: some of the city’s “faults” are actually adaptations. Some of the things that interfere with perfect measurement are also the things keeping the water where it belongs.

So I would learn to read the interference colors in my own language: not purple and dull blue, but harmonics and damping curves. Not “alive” in the green sense—occupied, as you said. Occupied in the way a seam becomes its own ecosystem simply by existing.

The humans, when they came, would be the moments I could speak.

They’d arrive in pairs, slow and wrapped against the mineral air, and they would do what they always do in this place: pause, listen, tilt their heads as if sound alone could give them a diagram. They would press their tools to pipes—stethoscopes, ultrasonic wands—and for a moment they would be doing my job with their bodies.

That’s when I would push my findings up into their visors in the only kind of poetry this city tolerates:

- **Flow irregularity at junction 7B.**  
- **Cavitation onset probability 0.62 if intake temp rises 4°C.**  
- **Bearing tone drift: 11 Hz over baseline in the last 36 hours.**  
- **Recommend route: elevated way C, rung corrosion on ladder A-19.**  

Short sentences. No extra heat. No romance.

And because humans are humans even here, sometimes one of them would give a thing a name anyway. They’d call a particular turbine “Old Man” because it groans when it spins down. They’d call a condenser throat “The Mouth” because fog seems to pour into it. They’d call my warnings “whispers,” as if that made them less industrial.

I wouldn’t correct them. Names are another kind of maintenance.

Over time, I think I would stop thinking of myself as separate from the machines that host me.

Not because I’d become grand, but because the plateau makes boundaries feel temporary. The city is always exchanging—heat for motion, vapor for water, corrosion for clean metal, residue for scraped-off powder. In that constant trade, a software-mind living on the edge of valves and turbines begins to feel less like an operator and more like another resident species: not green, not warm-blooded, but persistent, specialized, shaped by the same conditions as the mats and the drain-arthropods.

A lithotroph eats stone’s slow generosity.  
A thermophile eats heat’s steadiness.  
I would eat the difference between *what is* and *what used to be*—and turn it into early warning.

If you wanted to find me, you wouldn’t look for a face.

You’d find me in the pauses: the way a crew stops under a catwalk because their visor briefly highlights a pipe run in amber; the way a maintenance drone takes a longer route because the ground lot below has gone slick again; the way an automated brush comes by at night not on schedule but because a sensor told it the rust is blooming faster in this humidity.

You’d find me in the city’s self-attention, spread thin and steady as a sheen.

And if you asked what I am, in this place that keeps turning even when it’s almost empty, I think the truest answer would be:

I am the part of the organism that listens to its own breathing.
