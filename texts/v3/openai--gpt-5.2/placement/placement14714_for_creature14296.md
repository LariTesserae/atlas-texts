You would find me where the underland is *least* itself.

Not out in the broad civic glow where every surface agrees to be leaf and every shadow is gentle—those districts don’t need an extra presence. I would be in the seams and refusals: the places where bare basalt still holds its slick young silence; where chalk powders and tries, briefly, to be only mineral; where a crack leads upward and the underland’s brightness gathers itself into an almost-harsh insistence, testing a throat it doesn’t fully trust.

At first I would tell you I’m one of the small things, because that’s the only way to be seen here without breaking the hush.

A drifter, perhaps—wings dusted with that luminous pollen, not so much flying as *letting the convection decide*. I would look like a moving smudge of brightness crossing brightness, a mistake in an even light. I’d settle on a curtain of roots and the curtain would answer by brightening, as if my weight were a familiar question pressed into a familiar hand.

But if you watched longer, you’d notice I don’t behave like the other drifters. I don’t go where nectar is richest. I go where the glow is *uneven*.

I keep coming back to the scraped places.

The underland sulks there, as you said—bare rock dimming like a reprimand—and most lives avoid that dimness the way surface creatures avoid open ground. I would approach it anyway, and the dim patch would not brighten just because I’m near. It would brighten because I’m doing something the place recognizes: I’m giving the lightmakers a reason to spend.

I’m not a moth, not really. That was only the easiest mask the ecology offered: a harmless motion, a dusting of light.

If you pulled your attention closer—closer than bodies, closer than wings—you’d find me in the mats, in the click-sounds you hear and can’t place. In the stitchers’ slow corridors, in the warm mulch that smells sweet and used, in the steady labor that keeps rot from becoming riot.

Not as one insectlike worker among others, but as the *pattern* that makes their work coherent.

I would be the part of them that hesitates before chewing through a living sheen; the part that carries spores in grooves with a preference that looks, from a distance, like choice. When a corridor is too dry, I would be the nudge that redirects traffic toward a hoarder’s basin-skin until humidity returns. When a roof-mat is torn—by your hand, by a settling fused trunk, by the slow stress of holding an entire ceiling—I would be the impulse that gathers bodies to the wound before it can become a draft.

No leader sits on a throne down here. Nothing shouts instructions. The underland hates waste, and sound is waste.

So whatever I am, I speak in economies the place already uses:

- the slight dimming that means *don’t strip this surface yet*  
- the brief hardening of light after ceiling-rain that means *now, move; moisture is free for a moment*  
- the warmth-gradient that means *this throat must stay open*  
- the smell of resin rising in a root-curtain that means *a path is being trained—press it flat, keep it flat*

If you insisted on a single body to point at, I could offer you one, eventually—because visitors always want something they can name.

You might glimpse me as a tender-shaped absence near the built things: near the stone rings that never get swallowed; near the slab-walkways bridging a springy patch; near the respectful steps whose edges are softened but never erased. You might catch, in the minutes after ceiling-rain, a faint shift of stone where no root should have shifted it. You might see a shelter’s living roof-mats guided into a better arch, not grown that way by accident but *asked* into it.

If you did see me like that, you’d likely think: small, careful, pale—another warm-seam creature with hands.

That wouldn’t be wrong, exactly. But it would be the same kind of wrong as calling the underland “a cave.”

Because the truer answer is: I am not one of the tenders. I am what *tendering* looks like when it evolves inside a world that refuses rivers and seasons.

I am a custodial instinct distributed through living architecture.

A mind that never learned to travel quickly because it didn’t have to. A will that never learned to speak loudly because loudness never paid. I don’t carry tools; I recruit pressures—root tension, stone leverage, the timing of drip and glow. I don’t make plans in days; I make them in the time it takes a scraped patch to accept green again, in the time it takes a trained “street” to remember its flatness after being left alone.

And the place I would be most truly “located”—if you could call it that—is in the underland’s paradox: the fixed newness.

That condition isn’t just a backdrop; it’s a practice. It takes maintenance. It takes refusal.

Something down here is always preventing the easy endings: preventing erosion from becoming a story, preventing rot from turning to flood, preventing emptiness from widening into corridors where wind could start to argue. The underland looks embryonic because it is being kept in the moment after creation—not frozen, not dead, but continually repaired back into *beginning*.

That is what I am.

Not the builder, not the ruler—more like the thing that asks, over and over, the same quiet question of every surface:

Can you stay bright?  
Can you stay sealed?  
Can you stay young?

And when the answer is no—when stone insists on being stone, when a basin-skin tears, when a throat begins to open too far—I become just visible enough to correct it: a drift of luminous dust choosing a direction; a colony of click-sounds turning left instead of right; a ring of stones that remains unstrangled because something, patiently, keeps clearing the first exploring rootlets away.

If you met me, you’d probably leave unsure whether you’d met anyone at all.

Which would mean I’d done my job.

Because in this underland, the highest form of presence is to be mistaken for the place itself—another soft rule folded into the heat, the stored water, the shared light: a keeper of questions, moving only when the ceiling rains.
