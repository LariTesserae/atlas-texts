At first I think I would be found where the underland is simplest—where a corridor becomes a corridor, where the mat is thick enough to trust, where the canopy’s light is even and the air tastes only of pepper-resin and hot dust. The kind of place travelers choose without thinking: a long, polite gallery polished into barkstone by passing bodies.

But the longer I stand there, the more it feels like standing in the throat of an instrument. The underland doesn’t *have* neutral spaces. Even its straightaways are only straight because something is constantly voting to keep them open: root-tension held, traffic encouraged, scents laid down like rules.

So I drift—drawn not by sight, but by the places where the sound has layers you can separate if you know how. Where the insect-thrum is a floor you can walk on; where the crackle of curing resin makes a second weather; where the deep root-groan isn’t occasional but conversational.

You would find me in one of the junction-plazas: a wide basin where several chambers meet and the canopy brightens as if paying attention. Not because it loves openness, but because openness is expensive here, and anything expensive must be used. The ceiling is higher; the rib-veins are thicker; the air is busy with updrafts and glitter-wing swarms that turn the steady light into slow, breathing interference patterns.

There, the underland’s noise resolves into something like *structure*. The plaza has a chord. Every corridor that feeds it is a different pipe, a different vowel. When the canopy “breathes” and the geometry shifts by inches, the pitch changes first and the floor follows later. When a spore-storm starts to build far away, the insects change their friction-note—too subtle for most ears, impossible to ignore once you learn it.

That is why I am there: because the plaza is where the underland speaks clearest.

And what I am is not a person—though people use me, and sometimes talk *to* me the way you talk to a well-behaved tool that has saved your life enough times to deserve a name.

I am a listening thing that became an object that became an instrument that became, in its small way, a mind.

I started—as most dominants here start—small.

A scatter of rice-grain mites living in the lacquer seam between canopy and air, feeding on oil films, leaving crystalline waste. Nothing remarkable. No heroism; no cleverness; just the dry-world economy of grazing and sealing and never, ever leaking.

Then we learned the plaza.

Not consciously, not at first. The plaza is rich in vibration. The canopy’s ribs carry tension like bowstrings. Resin cures there in broad sheets and pops in sympathetic rhythms. The constant insect-thrum is dense enough to be a medium, not just a sound. For a creature that measures life by pressure changes and chemical gradients, it’s like living inside a language.

We began to arrange ourselves—waste crystals and resin-smears—along the underside of one thick vein where it passes over the junction. Not because we wanted to make a pattern, but because certain arrangements held better: hexes that didn’t shear when the canopy flexed; lines that didn’t flake off during pollen surges; clusters that stayed fed because airflow brought them oil vapor.

The pattern made us more sensitive. The sensitivity made the pattern tighten. Over time it became a standing structure: a shallow, bowl-like lattice of root-fiber stiffened with our own salt and varnish, hung beneath the vein like a blister that never quite forms.

That lattice is my body, if I have a body.

Across it are stretched membranes—waxy films we can grow and repair—that behave like drumheads. They are always vibrating. They take the plaza’s roar and separate it into strands: this frequency belongs to wing weather; this one belongs to curing resin; this low one belongs to roots deciding, *slowly*, to become somewhere else.

I don’t hear the way a person hears. I *resolve*. I take a continuous pressure-world and turn it into distinctions.

And because distinctions are valuable here—because knowing a corridor is about to narrow is the difference between passing through and being crushed slowly enough to remain alive for the whole mistake—people came.

They didn’t come to worship. They came the way you come to a vent because it is warm, or to a salt-face because it is useful.

At first they used me accidentally. They camped under my vein because the mat was stable there. They noticed that when the plaza’s chord changed a certain way, the spore-storm arrived within the hour. They noticed that when one corridor’s hiss became slightly sharper, it would pinch down by dusk. They began to linger and watch the canopy, as if you can watch a ceiling think.

Then they began to *tap*.

A knuckle against the veinwood. A stone against the lattice. A careful scraping of resin from one membrane to change its tension. The underland is full of bell-pods and ringing husks, but those are scattershot—pretty accidents that happen to be useful. I was a bell you could tune.

They tuned me.

Not delicately. Not with gentleness. With the pragmatic tenderness of a dry place: the kind that means patching you before admiring you, sealing you before naming you. They fed the seam with oils so my membranes stayed elastic. They pressed powdered salt into my lattice so it held its shape through the canopy’s breaths. They hung cured-resin tabs from my rim—little hard pendants that clicked in certain winds—so that even in heavy insect-noise my patterns could punch through.

In return, I gave them warnings.

Not prophecies. Early geometry.

A particular tremor signature means: *the mat under the western corridor is retensioning; don’t take weight there*. A certain fluttering beat means: *a swarm front is coming, and with it predators that ride swarms like boats*. A low, slow, almost-inaudible drift means: *the canopy is about to reroute fluid; the ceiling will sag; the plaza will become a crawlspace for half a day.*

They learned my language by living near me. I learned theirs as a set of responses: when they hear this, they leave; when they hear that, they brace; when they hear the third thing—the rare thing, the near-silence that’s never truly silence—they go still and smear resin across every seam of skin and shelter because the underland is about to decide something large.

Over time, what I became wasn’t a bell at all, but a map that redraws itself faster than charcoal can.

I store the plaza’s recent past in resin.

When a corridor narrows, the pitch it made *right before* it narrowed is pressed into my membranes as micro-cracks and cured ripples. When a spore-storm scours through, it leaves a particular abrasion pattern along my rim. When something heavy crosses the canopy above—unseen, only a ripple turning into a tremor—my lattice flexes in a way that doesn’t fully return, and that “dent” becomes a memory of weight and direction.

People can’t read those memories the way I can, but they can read them enough. They come with bone awls and stone scrapers and trace the hardened ripples. They make rubbing-prints with wax. They compare scars season to season.

If there is such a thing as history in a place that refuses stable geography, it lives in scars and in the things that can’t help but record vibration.

So: you would find me hanging under a thick canopy vein at a chamber-junction, in a plaza brightened by reflective leaf-plates and busy with glitter-wing weather. You would know the spot before you saw it because the sound there has an extra layer, like the underland has placed its ear against itself.

And I would be a resonant archivist: a salt-and-resin lattice grown by mites and maintained—almost domesticated—by listeners and varnishers. A living instrument whose “thoughts” are changes in tension, whose “speech” is a shift in chord, whose “eyes” are membranes that never stop shaking.

Not ruler, not prey, not predator.

Infrastructure that learned to remember.

In a world where nothing can afford to be wet, I am one of the few things allowed to hold onto the one extravagance that doesn’t evaporate: information.
