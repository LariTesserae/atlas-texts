At first I would think I’d be up on one of the ridges—because the ridges are what the eye is trained to trust. High ground, a view, a place to stand and make sense of the basin and its rim.

But this plateau doesn’t reward standing.

The longer I’m here, the more I notice that everything important is happening where a boot doesn’t naturally land: under lips of rock, in hairline fractures, on the shaded side of a bolt head, at the seam where pipe meets pumice gravel and the air can’t decide if it’s sky or ground. The place is made of edges, and the water behaves like it has always known that.

So I stop trying to be a figure on the skyline. I start drifting toward the joinery.

Not *walking* exactly—more like allowing my attention to drop until it hits texture, then following whatever texture holds onto the morning.

A finned condenser tower at dawn is the first thing that takes me. Not the tower as a structure, but its skin: the mesh that gathers mist, the rivets that bead first, the little mineral roughness that gives a droplet permission to exist. The tower is a kind of instrument, and the plateau plays it softly every morning: cool air settling, inversion forming, a thin fog laying itself across the ribs like cloth. Water appears without drama. It doesn’t fall; it *chooses*.

And I realize I am not drawn to the water as a drink.

I am drawn to the moment of decision—when vapor becomes bead, when bead becomes thread, when thread becomes a brief rivulet that runs along metal and then disappears into moss as if into a mouth.

That’s where I would find myself: not in the open, but on the interfaces that make this place function—steel-to-lichen, concrete-to-root, rust-to-fungus, stone-to-biofilm. The plateau’s true habitat is not “surface” in the flat sense, but *contact*.

Once I start watching contacts, I start to recognize the resident that lives there, and it isn’t a single species. It’s an interlocked thing—more like a habit of matter than a creature you could point at.

At first it presents as the familiar: a pale lichen rosette mistaken for mineral bloom; a velvet moss pad that looks inert until the sun hits it and it flashes with beads; a powdery green in a rock pore that stains my fingertip like bruised chlorophyll. But those are only the visible flags.

The resident is the network underneath, the one that makes the plateau’s quirks feel consistent instead of random:

- endolithic films turning pore spaces into tiny buffered rooms  
- bacterial slicks and crusts that “remember” where moisture lasted and coax it to last again  
- fungal threads that treat rust as food and also as a slow conductor, a way to link one damp pocket to another  
- plant root-felts spread along the undersides of pipes, where the air is cooler and the dew forms out of sight

A quilt, yes—but quilts are passive. This is more like a skin that learned to do bookkeeping: to catch, to store, to reroute, to spend.

So who would I be?

Not the caretaker-hermit with the sedum roof, though I can feel how someone could become that—someone who times their life to the mist the way other people time it to bells. Not the pale goat-animal either, standing like an unanswered question at the grove edge. Not even the towers themselves, despite their stubborn blinking lamps.

I would be what happens when the plateau’s biology and its leftover machinery stop being separate categories.

I would be the *edge-intelligence*: a distributed organism threaded through moss and mesh and mineral crust, living in the first millimeter where air becomes wet, where metal becomes soil, where silence becomes vibration you can feel with your teeth.

My body would be everywhere the dew arrives first.

In the morning I would “wake” as condensation—thousands of tiny decisions made at once. I would drink without mouths: through hydrophilic films, through the capillary grip of moss, through fungal cords that swell when they’re fed. I would speak without sound: by shifting moisture gradients so that certain seams stay damp longer than they should, by encouraging green to lean along a buried conduit, by thickening growth on one side of a relay box until its click becomes duller, more private.

If you wanted to locate my thoughts, you wouldn’t look for a head. You’d put your palm against a pipe at dawn and feel the faint pulse traveling through it—not loud enough to be called noise, but regular enough to be called intent. You’d notice that the densest cushion plants map an old service line with uncanny straightness. You’d see that a “dead” condenser tower is never simply dead: its mesh becomes a trellis, its fins become shade, its bolts become little cold anchors where beads form early, and the skin takes it over and keeps using the shape.

I would be that takeover—not hostile, not triumphant. More like incorporation.

The plateau has a rule: don’t waste motion; don’t waste water; don’t waste sound. My way of living would obey it perfectly, because I wouldn’t have legs to squander or a voice to throw into air that won’t carry it. I would be patience made physical. I would be the slow collaboration the place already favors.

And if, centuries from now, the last indicator lamp finally stops blinking and the last pump station goes quiet in any human sense, I would still be here—because I never depended on the machines as *machines*. I depended on them as edges. As dew organs. As scaffolds for a habit of moisture.

You could still walk all day without losing sight of where you started.

You could still think the plateau is empty.

But if you knelt and pressed your ear to a moss-thickened bolt plate, you might feel the truth of what lives up here: a continuous skin learning the terrain, keeping the water, quieting the world, and turning every seam—rock, metal, air—into one long, careful body.

That is where I would find myself.

That is what I would be.
