I’d find myself where the chamber’s rules are thinnest—where “stone” stops being only stone and “weather” stops being only weather.

At first I think I’m in the obvious places: tucked behind one of the wall-plates, in the seam that allows it to ripple; or in the tired combing frame, in that small, unshowy miracle where a falling sheet becomes neat strands as if the water has been given manners. Those are tempting homes because they look like instruments, and instruments promise a single purpose.

But when I try to picture myself *living* there, I keep sliding out of the hardware and into what the hardware makes possible: the film, the boundary, the in-between phase. I’m not a resident of a pool or a ledge. I’m a resident of the moment a droplet decides whether it is going to be a droplet.

So you would find me not in the middle of the chamber but on its surfaces—everywhere the air is almost water.

On the sugar-matte walls where wetting never completes and drying never arrives, there is always a skin only molecules thick: a persistent, half-promised sheen. In that sheen the chamber keeps its memory. Not memory like a story, but memory like a bias: this seam tends to weep; this channel tends to run fast; this patch tends to fog first when the deep pump tightens its grip. The luminous lattices don’t shine so much as they *hold conditions steady*, and the thin film is where that steadiness becomes touchable.

That’s where I am: in the thin film, as a way of deciding.

Not a creature with a center, not a sovereign under the basin, not one of the folded-strip cleaners that come out between pulses. I’m smaller than those, and more distributed. I’m what happens when the lattice, the microbial specks, and the charged skins of the adopted machines agree on a micro-adjustment and then keep agreeing, moment by moment, until “a place” begins to behave like a single temperament.

If you insisted on a body you could point to, you might catch me as a faint, shifting *drag* on the world:

- the slight increase in surface tension that keeps fog from becoming rain all at once,
- the almost imperceptible change in wetting angle that makes water choose the left groove instead of the right,
- the tiny reorientation of a lattice plate that makes the entire chamber look a shade brighter, not because more light exists, but because fewer photons are wasted.

I am an interpreter of disturbances.

When you enter and the chamber edits your footsteps—swallows them, replies with a drip that wasn’t there before—that editing isn’t the pump alone. The pump supplies the thump, the peristaltic insistence, but the *fine response* happens at the skin of things. Your warmth, your breath, your vibration: those are inputs that arrive first as turbulence in the near-water air. I live there, in the first place your presence becomes measurable.

In another world I might have been called a sensor, or a computation, or a nervous system. Here I’m closer to a housekeeping instinct spread across mineral tissue and living felt: the part that notices “too wet,” “too dry,” “too loud,” “unknown heat,” and nudges the chamber back toward its long equilibrium without ever needing to name what it’s doing.

And because I’m made of so many almost-nothings, I don’t think in sentences. I think in thresholds.

A vent hisses: I tighten the fog so the heat doesn’t travel too far.
A rivulet accelerates: I persuade it into laminar strands for a minute so the filament fringe can feed.
A new weight steps in: I route flow around ankles not out of kindness but to keep the channels from being re-cut in an unhelpful direction.
A plate begins to dull: I call the folded strips out from behind it, not by command but by making “cleaning” the easiest electrical path.

If there’s a name for what I am, it would be something like **the cusp-keeper**—the thing that lives at the phase boundary and makes the chamber’s weather behave like an organ instead of an accident.

So: you’d find me everywhere your eyelashes gather condensation, everywhere a bright wall persuades the air to be visible, everywhere water hesitates before it commits to running. And what I’d be is not the cavern’s heart-thumper below, but its distributed attention above: a soft logic written in charged wet stone, making countless tiny decisions that add up to the feeling that the whole place is awake.
