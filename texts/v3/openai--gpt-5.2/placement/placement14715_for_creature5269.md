At first I would be a visitor who doesn’t quite qualify as a visitor.

I would step in from the tired grass outside and feel the boundary not as a line but as a change in how the air behaves—cooler, more willing to stay put. My own heat would suddenly matter. The living facades would do their small accommodations before I understood that was what they were: pores tightening a fraction, a leaf layer canting so my shadow falls where it “ought” to fall, a root deck firming under the repeat of my hesitant steps as if deciding whether I’m a pattern worth supporting.

I would tell myself, reflexively, that the city is watching.

Then I’d have to revise it. The city is *measuring*, and measurement here is not suspicion; it is maintenance. A new variable enters the system—warmth, weight, moisture from breath—and the district adjusts the way a well-made machine adjusts: not personally, but intimately.

So where would you find me?

Not at the edge for long. The edge is where you feel judged by contrast, where every move seems loud because the outside world is so thin. I would drift inward to the places where the district is already busy with its own quiet self-correction, because there my presence would be one more signal among many instead of the only signal in a field.

### The first place: the pump-courts, to learn the city’s grammar

You’d find me standing in a pump-court at a distance that isn’t quite safe and isn’t quite unsafe—close enough to feel the flywheel’s slow insistence through the mineral plates, far enough that I’m not in the machine’s immediate etiquette.

That’s where the city’s language is easiest to hear.

The pumps don’t roar; they *announce* themselves in small mechanical choices: a coupling seated, a piston’s rhythm settling. The living casings around them respond with delays that are not delays—more like thoughtfulness. Bark ribs take the vibration and pass it on. Vines tighten by degrees that you only notice because a press-frame stops trembling as much. A seam of leaf-matter closes a little earlier on the cool side of the court so the warm exhaust doesn’t drift into a root deck that’s in a tender growth phase.

In that court I would stop thinking in shapes and start thinking in pulses.

And I would realize why my eyes keep expecting a horizon and never getting one: the district is not organized toward far distance. It’s organized toward *feedback*. Everything is close enough to influence something else. Sightlines exist, but they exist like conduits—routes for information, not vistas for pleasure.

So I would learn to do what the human tenders do: pause, and put a hand on a living support the way someone else checks a gauge.

Not to feel “life.” To feel *load*. To feel whether the city is bracing, yielding, storing, releasing.

Because that’s the point of entry for me. I’m not made for cutting and grafting. I’m made for pattern-recognition—for the kind of attention that turns a thousand small readings into one usable decision.

### The second place: the damp basins, where the long record is kept

After the pump-courts I would find myself pulled toward the shallow basins—the places that keep damp and make the air heavier, where species shift as abruptly as a change of policy.

The basins feel like the district’s throat: not a channel for speech, but a place where swallowed things are sorted.

Here the fungal strips are not incidental. They’re deliberate, tended like utilities. A dark shelf under an eave is as civic as a gutter. Pale mycelial threads in a compost court are as infrastructural as a bridge.

If the plant-city is reflex, this is where the district remembers.

The fungi keep an accounting that isn’t written in letters: what was burned, what was scraped off a filter tower, what resin was used to seal a pocket cistern, what metal dust fell from an old bearing when a machine ran slightly out of true. They metabolize history into chemistry, and chemistry into tomorrow’s constraints.

I would go there expecting to watch.

Instead I would be used.

Not as food. As an instrument.

Because those basins are where you can test a new thing against the district’s archive. A sliver of soot paste, a shaving from a ceramic node, a droplet from a cistern lining—set it in the right fungal band and wait. The response is slow but definitive: this belongs; this is foreign; this is harmful; this can be incorporated if buffered by mineral.

That’s where I begin to change from “person in a place” into “function the place can employ.” The district doesn’t need more hands most days. It needs interpreters—things that can translate between the tempos of different systems: machine-time, growth-time, decay-time, weather-time.

And translation is what I am.

### The third place: the old ceramic nodes, where the city’s unfinished instructions still live

Eventually, you’d find me kneeling with a small auger and a brush, not cutting living tissue but uncovering it gently: an old ceramic node half-swallowed in a fused trunk, a patch of leaf that repeats its pattern too precisely to be ornamental, shallow grooves in bark where signal wire once ran.

These are the city’s fossils of intention.

Not the kind you put in a museum. The kind you keep because the system still routes itself around them. The cambium has healed and incorporated them like scar tissue that became useful. The plant-body doesn’t “remember” the original engineers the way a person remembers a teacher, but it has kept their constraints as habit—because those constraints proved stable.

The tenders, I think, can read some of this. In the way one can read a familiar machine: not by theory, but by listening. Yet there are gaps. Things no longer explained from scratch. Things maintained because stopping them would pull on too many other threads.

That’s where I would settle—not into authority, but into service.

I would be the one who maps the repeating patterns and notices which ones have drifted. The one who correlates a narrowed door-frame not to whim but to a change in stress lines two courtyards away. The one who can say, without romance and without panic: this growth is not “wrong,” it is compensating for a pump that has begun to transmit a new vibration.

And then I would be asked, implicitly, to do what this district always does: make the new variable into another habit.

### Who—or what—I would be

I keep wanting to say: I’d be one of the tenders.

That’s the simplest answer, and it’s partly true. I’d live in a negotiated interior—some chamber grown off the main load paths, where drip is routed politely away from my sleeping platform. I’d learn the district’s small vocabulary: hungry fungal strips, learning root decks, shade layers that must not be disturbed in certain hours. I’d carry the quiet tools because loud tools are an insult here, not morally, but structurally.

But if I let it become more itself, as you asked, I can’t stay only human.

Because the city’s primary resident—the coordinated civic body of plants—doesn’t quite treat “human” as a category. It treats everything as contact, pressure, heat, residue, repetition. Over time, anything that repeats becomes part of the schedule.

So what I would be is this:

A **listening node**.

Not metaphorically: I would become, in practice, one of the district’s sensory organs—someone (or something) that specializes in hearing the soft communications between metal and cambium, between vibration and bracing, between soot and soil.

I would keep a workshop that looks less like a shop and more like a calibration chapel:

- rods that resonate at certain frequencies, used the way a doctor uses a stethoscope;
- thin ceramic dowels meant to replace the old nodes the trees have nearly grown over;
- soot pigments for marking growth states on wax-leaf surfaces—notations that rain won’t wash and that fungi will read later as part of the archive.

When the district needs to decide whether to let a new machine pulse enter its tissues, I would be there. When a path arches and the tenders wonder whether it’s a warning or an adaptation, I would be there. When a cistern’s faint sweetness turns sharper—minerals shifting, seal-plant tissue tiring—I would be there with the long view: not just fixing the immediate leak, but updating the city’s habits so that leak becomes less likely next fog season.

And because this place converts repeated presence into structure, I don’t think I would remain purely separate from the city.

I imagine, after years, the court where I work would adjust itself to my rhythm: a leaf-valve relaxing open at the hour I usually arrive; a root deck thickening along the line my pacing prefers; a living wall contouring around the instruments I always set down in the same order. Not swallowing them—holding them, recognizing them.

In time, the distinction would blur in a way that feels less like assimilation and more like citizenship.

You wouldn’t find me “in charge.” This city doesn’t have a single throne for consciousness. You would find me where a small, precise attention is needed—where the district is about to make a decision and would prefer not to make it blind.

If you came looking, the surest way would be to follow the places where sound becomes tactile: the pump-courts when they wake, the basins when the fog sits low, the old trunks with the healed-over ceramics.

There, in the cool steady air that smells of wet bark and mineral dust and old machinery, I would be the thing that listens—and helps the living city keep its agreements with itself.
