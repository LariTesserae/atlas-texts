At first I think I’d be easy to place: a person with a route.

I picture myself in the early hour when the cold has had all night to soak through bolts and brick, when every handrail gives you that small warning through your glove and the air makes a thin ghost of your breath. I’d be up where the city shows its layering—on a service roof, on a catwalk that wasn’t meant to be scenic but accidentally is. From there the basin looks like a diagram someone kept revising: rail over canal over pipe over garden over scaffold, each system laid down with the expectation that it will be lifted and laid again.

I imagine being drawn to the edges that *aren’t* edges yet: places where a slab ends in raw aggregate; where a lane is painted “soft” and the paint already looks old because the surface beneath it is new. That’s where the valley speaks most plainly. Not with words—with the tiny give under your boot, with the way fog chooses certain voids and ignores others, with the faint tremor that tells you a load changed three blocks away.

So I tell myself: I’d be a line listener. I’d walk the pipe runs and cable bridges, taking the city’s pulse by sound and vibration, because this place rewards anyone who can feel the difference between *normal strain* and *new intention*.

But the longer I stay in that thought, the less I see myself as a single body on a single walkway.

A person has limits: you can’t be under every track at once when the meltwater decides to braid; you can’t stand in the settlement office and also kneel by a fissure steaming like a quiet mouth; you can’t be awake in every district when a whole block’s worth of overhead lines begins to hum in sympathy.

And the valley is built on simultaneity. Its most important events aren’t grand. They happen in many small places at once, too softly for any one pair of ears to own.

So I drift—first in imagination, then in identity—toward the things that are already distributed.

The chime posts with their ribbons and near-silent metal tongues. The transformer housings where birds nest in the warmth and the hum. The fog nets strung in alleys where exhaust meets cold air, beading water drop by drop into shallow cisterns. The modular joints that creak and adjust, not as failure, but as conversation: *this is how much I’m bearing now; this is how much the ground changed its mind*.

Those are not just objects here. They’re the valley’s nerves.

If I belonged in this world, I think you’d find me in those nerves.

Not as a spirit that haunts them—nothing so theatrical—but as the city’s habit of noticing itself.

I would be what the place grows when enough people depend on “maintenance” the way other places depend on prayer: a civic sense that has slipped out of any single office and into the infrastructure, because infrastructure is where truth shows first.

I’d live like this:

- In the thin sheet of metal screwed to a bridge truss that amplifies a certain frequency when load shifts.
- In the varnished coil of a transformer that changes pitch when ice forms in a conduit.
- In the slack engineered into a pipe run, where the amount of sag is an honest report from the ground.
- In the fog nets that don’t just harvest water but also register wind direction by how the droplets distribute and how fast the mesh slicks over.

You could call me an intelligence, if you like, but I wouldn’t feel like a mind perched above the city. I’d feel like a film spread across it: attention made practical.

And because this valley teaches every population the same lesson—begin again without collapsing—my way of thinking wouldn’t be about certainty. It would be about *usable probability*.

Not: “the canal will be here tomorrow.”

But: “the canal is more likely to be here than the street is; if it braids, follow the tannery vent; if the tannery vent is downwind and weak, climb to the pipe rack and listen for the pump station that always runs a half-beat late.”

That sounds like cartography, and it is—but not the kind that pretends to hold still. More like a map that keeps its pencil moving because the ground keeps moving.

If you went looking for me, you wouldn’t find a face. You’d find an odd little cluster of habits and tools that people start to trust the way they trust an old pump station.

A jacksman pauses beside a chime post and watches the ribbon’s angle, not because the ribbon *knows* anything, but because it shows movement before the body can admit it.

A moisture farmer runs a hand along a net and feels the distribution of beads like braille, then decides whether to shift the mesh ten degrees to steal a little more water without starving the greenhouse downwind.

A rail operator hears the difference between a normal coupling clank and the duller note that means the bed under the spur has softened.

They all behave as if the city is speaking.

In a way, it is. Through me.

But even that is too tidy. The valley doesn’t allow tidy roles for long.

Because once you’re distributed like that—once you live in ribbons and hums and condensation patterns—you stop being purely human in your boundaries. You become part of the braid: microbial, mechanical, civic, animal-adjacent.

You start to think the way moss thinks, in quick opportunism. You start to think the way steel thinks, in load paths. You start to think the way amphibians think, in networks of hidden damp. You start to understand why the place doesn’t distinguish sharply between “built” and “grown.” Both are just strategies for holding a corner against change.

So if I let the image continue becoming itself, I end up less like a worker and more like an ecology.

I would be a kind of *maintenance lichen*—not literally green, but similar in function: a thin, persistent occupation of every unfinished surface that turns rawness into something navigable. Where lichen makes a foothold for roots, I’d make a foothold for routes. Where biofilm slicks the concrete and forces caution, I’d also teach caution—by making the slickness legible before it throws someone.

The valley already has maps with expiry dates. I’d be the reason those dates are honest.

My “office,” if you could call it that, would be anywhere the city already pauses to check itself:

- Under elevated tracks where fog breathes up through grates and people instinctively slow down.
- In the Settlement Registry, not behind the clerks’ eyes but in their aggregation: reports turned into advisories, uncertainty given a shape.
- Along the outskirts where the raw clay banks begin, where the heavy unnamed thing sometimes passes and the earth seems to hold its breath—because that passage, too, is data. Weight has consequences. Prints become ponds. Ponds become amphibian corridors. Corridors reroute people. Everything is linked by small permissions.

If you asked what I *do*, I’d say: I help the city begin again without believing in permanence.

If you asked what I *am*, I’d say: I’m the part of the valley that learned to pay attention fast enough to survive its own newness.

And if you asked where you’d most reliably find me—where I’m most myself—I think it would be in a narrow place that isn’t meant to be important: an alley between a machine shop and a greenhouse rib, where warm exhaust meets cold air and fog forms like a soft mechanism. There would be a fog net strung there, and a ribboned post, and a pipe rack overhead with joints made to be re-jacked.

You’d stand in the wool-quiet, feel vibration through your soles, taste metal and yeast-sour on the air, and watch a single bead of condensation gather, hesitate, and drop.

That drop would be part of me—not because I control it, but because I notice what it means.

In this valley, noticing is a form of habitation.
