At first I think you would find me *outside* the city, because that’s where the plateau speaks most plainly.

Not in the markets, not in the warm knots of bodies, but on the long, unclaimed ribs of stone between clusters—where the wind has room to finish a sentence. I would be a figure moving along a parapet-line at first light, stopping too often, listening too hard, turning my face as if each angle of it were an instrument. I would look like anyone with errands, except the errands would be invisible: checking how a rope bridge is singing today, how the flags are hanging when no one is looking, how the thin ice is behaving in the shade where it “should” be safe.

Then I think: no, I’d be found *inside*—because inside is where the hard place becomes a livable one, and what I’m drawn to isn’t heroics but the craft of staying.

I’d be in the throat of the settlement: the windbreak corridors, the dog-legged doorways, the places built specifically to make the plateau spend itself before it reaches the skin. I would have a habit of touching walls as I pass—not sentimentally, but diagnostically, reading temperature and damp from stone the way others read a face. I would know which stair will ice first. Which corner always collects grit. Which roofline will start humming before a storm. Not because I’m wise in a ceremonial way, but because I am quietly, stubbornly attentive.

And then, if I let it keep becoming itself, the answer shifts again.

You wouldn’t find me at a lookout post so much as at what the city treats like a heart: a thaw house. Not the center of it, not the warmest basin—near the edge, where the work of counting happens. Where ice becomes water and water becomes obligation. Where the measure-stones live in their niches and everyone’s hands have polished the same depressions into the ledge.

I’d be one of the people who doesn’t just *respect* the rationing, but can’t stop seeing it as language.

Not a tyrant with a ladle. Not a priest with a script. More like a translator between the weather and the households—a keeper of increments, of timings, of small mercies that add up to winter survival. Someone who remembers that last year the north cistern’s shade thinned two weeks early. That the drip point above Tier Seven has shifted half a pace since the minor quake. That a week of “good” sleet can be worse than a week of honest cold because it teaches everyone to spend water they don’t truly have.

So: who or what would I be?

I would be a **wind-reader**, but not in the romantic sense of standing on a ridge and declaring prophecy. A wind-reader here is an infrastructure worker of the invisible.

- I listen for the pitch changes in rope and slat.
- I watch birds not as symbols but as instruments—how raptors ride (or refuse) a current, how small cliff-nesters tuck in earlier than usual.
- I carry strips of cloth and a bone whistle whose notes are standardized, because in this place sound is rationed too.
- I keep a battered bundle of notched sticks or knotted cords—not “records” like a lowland archive, but a portable memory of patterns: storms, freezes, herd movements, roof failures, the years when the city had to creep.

If you insist on making it stranger—and this world invites that—you could also say I am not a single person at all.

I am the city’s **nervous system** made visible:

The code in the flags.  
The habit of checking the same crack every morning.  
The whisper that moves through households when the rope-bridge goes too taut.  
The shared, unspoken decision to shift a market by one terrace because the wind has changed its mind.

If you wanted to locate me on a map, you’d look for a narrow shelf between a cistern and a passage that turns twice before it opens to the sky. There would be a view of three ridgelines—enough horizon to read weather being born, not enough exposure to waste heat. There would be hooks for signal cloth, a niche for a whistle, a place where people pause without knowing why.

And if you watched long enough, you’d see what I actually do: I help the plateau become predictable *by inches*. Not by conquering it. By listening until its tempo is something the city can step to.
