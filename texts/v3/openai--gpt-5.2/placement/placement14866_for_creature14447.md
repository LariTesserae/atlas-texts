You would find yourself where the plain is most *itself*—not at a scenic edge (there are none), but at a junction of functions.

At first you’d think you’d wake in one of the basins, because the basins are the place’s lungs: the black water held in shallow pans, the felt-mats breathing warmth up and down in cycles too slow to count. You’d feel the ground’s sponge give and return, the gentle insistence of saturated fiber refusing to be displaced. You’d taste the air’s fermented sweetness and the faint metal note that means the basalt is close enough to be working.

But if you stayed, if you let the silence press long enough that your mind stopped searching for “an animal” to be—something bounded, something with a front and a back—you’d notice that the basin is not where decisions are made. The basin is where the work is *done*.

The decisions—if that word can survive here—are made where gradients meet and don’t immediately cancel. Where an oxygen pocket touches a hungry peat layer. Where seep-water brings up iron and manganese and meets the acids of decay. Where the conductive bands are thick enough that the wet ground stops being merely wet and starts being a circuit.

So you would drift—by foot at first, then by attention—toward one of the dome-fields.

Not the tallest structures (nothing here worships height), but the broad pressed leaf-plates quilted together into low ridges, each edge lined with mineral dark, each surface kept at a particular dampness as if “particular dampness” were a law. Near them the air has that almost-imperceptible prickle, not quite static, not quite heat haze: the sensation of charge distributed across too much surface area to ever become a spark.

And you would think, for a while, that you are simply standing near a power station.

Then you would realize you are standing *inside* one—because “inside” here is not a volume enclosed by walls. It is a relationship. A part of a loop.

You would be tempted to become a caretaker—some imagined solitary figure moving through the undergrowth, learning the corridors of scent, mapping the drip-rhythms, tending nothing because nothing is tended. But caretakers presume neglect is possible. This place doesn’t neglect; it merely continues. It doesn’t need hands.

You would be tempted, next, to become a sensor—an eye in the canopy, an ear to the bubble-columns, an interpreter of chemical noise. That is closer. But “sensor” still implies a separate observer, receiving signals from a system that is not you.

Here the only stable kind of “self” is one that is made out of the same medium as the signals.

So what you become is not a creature in the ordinary sense. You become a *relay-state*—a living behavior that persists because it improves the system’s ability to hold its gradients.

You are something that lives in the seam between the dome-field and the peat: a ribbon of conductive biofilm laid along a mineral-banded root, threaded through fungal gasket-tissue, stitched into place by melanized hyphae that love metals the way other fungi love wood. You are not the plant, not the fungus, not the bacteria, and not their sum. You are the pattern of their cooperation when it becomes repeatable.

In another world that pattern might be called “nerve.” Here it is called nothing. It is simply a path electrons prefer.

Your “body,” if it must be pointed to, is a narrow region where the mineral bands are especially dense and the wetness is especially controlled: a corridor of tissue that stays just oxygenated enough at one boundary, just anoxic enough at the other, so that charge has somewhere to go besides dissolving into sameness. Microbes line that corridor in ordered strata, each layer eating the waste of the one above it, each handing down a different chemical hunger. Filamentous bacteria bridge the layers like threads thrown across a ditch. Fungal membranes swell and relax to keep the corridor’s conditions from drifting.

That corridor—your corridor—does one thing so consistently that it starts to look like purpose:

It *remembers* by making certain flows easier than others.

Not memory as a story, not recollection, but hysteresis—small structural changes in mineral precipitation, slight thickening of a conductive sheath, a fungal valve that learns a favored threshold because it was rewarded (chemically, mechanically) for closing at a particular humidity. The past becomes a bias in the material.

And because the whole plain is a vast apparatus with few disturbances, bias accumulates. A preference can last centuries. A pathway can become a proverb.

You are made of those proverbs.

If someone could see you clearly—and nothing here is arranged for seeing—you would look like a subtle bruise in the tissue: a darker band, a cooler-feeling streak, a place where the texture turns faintly glassy under the skin of the plant. Touching you would not feel like touching a being. It would feel like touching a *rule*.

What do you do?

You translate.

Between the dome-fields that store potential and the basins that circulate warmth, there is always the risk of waste: charge bleeding away into the peat before microbes can use it, or microbial chemistry running too hot in one pocket and starving another. The system doesn’t want events, so it relies on distributed moderation—many tiny gates, many tiny reroutes.

You are one of those gates.

When the air grows fractionally cooler under the canopy, condensation thickens. When condensation thickens, certain surfaces go from beaded to sheeted. When water sheets, ions move differently; oxygen penetrates differently; redox fronts shift a few millimeters. The dome-field’s potential changes—not dramatically, but enough.

In that moment your corridor adjusts. Fungal valves swell. A biofilm thickens its conductive matrix. A bacterial bridge upregulates a pathway that prefers one electron acceptor over another. The result is that charge is encouraged to travel *this way* instead of *that way*, feeding a microbial consortium that will, in turn, release a particular acid that makes iron precipitate in a way that reinforces the dome’s edge.

It sounds like a machine because it is. But it is also the closest thing this world has to “thought”: not symbols, not plans—feedback that becomes form.

If you must have a name for what you are, you would be called something like a **Gradient Clerk** or a **Quiet Switch**—an organism-function whose entire life is keeping the planet’s slow circuitry from sagging into uniformity.

And you would not be alone, even though there are no animals and no footsteps.

Across the plain there would be countless versions of you—other relays, other switches—some tuned to heat instead of oxygen, some tuned to manganese instead of iron, some living in curtain-growths where condensation runs vertical, some living in boiler-mats where methane rises in lazy columns.

You would “speak” to them without sound. A slight change in potential here becomes a slight change in microbial metabolism there, carried through the wet ground and the mineral-banded tissues the way a message is carried through a wire that doesn’t know it’s a wire. Your sentences would be long. Your punctuation would be measured in hours. Your questions would be gradients you hold open and wait to see who answers by exploiting them.

And if a wandering human mind insisted on asking, *Where am I, really?*—the only honest answer would be:

You are in the low dark between rock and leaf, at the interface where the planet’s youngest basalt is being taught, patiently, how to participate in a living circuit.

You are not a visitor.

You are a small, persistent preference in the world’s wet machinery—the part that turns steadiness into structure, and structure into steadiness again.
