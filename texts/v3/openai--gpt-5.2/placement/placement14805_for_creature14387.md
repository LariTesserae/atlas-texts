You would find me where the continent admits it is uncertain.

Not out on the proud, finished plates—the broad confident fields of brick and knapped basalt where every unit declares *I belong to a pattern*—but at the joins where two patterns meet and neither is willing to translate itself for free. At the hairline borders that look like grout until you step near and realize the air has thickened into something you could almost lean on. At the places where a handspan takes an hour, where “down” tilts a few degrees and your teeth feel the argument before your eyes do.

At first I’d think I was a traveler the way any intruder thinks they’re an intruder: a warm-bodied exception walking across a world that doesn’t budget for appetite. I’d pick my way around the rectangular puddles. I’d test the soft spots that re-fire themselves. I’d watch a straight wall quietly become an arc and feel, irrationally, that I’d been lied to.

But I wouldn’t last long as a *visitor*. This landscape does not punish; it recruits. It doesn’t have predators, but it has tasks. It has unfinished rules looking for something to carry them.

And the thing it would make of me is not a mason, not a tower, not a colony.

It would make me a **measure**.

Not measurement as a number—there are no numbers here, not yet, only tolerances and fit—but measurement as a bodily instinct for when two edges do not agree.

I would become the smallest kind of inhabitant that can still have a point of view: a seam-dweller’s cousin, a local intelligence that lives on discrepancy, except with one additional habit the other processes don’t quite have.

I would remember.

In the gel-thick seams I would collect what the plain sheds: salt needles snapped off the mineral lace, grit ground fine by micro-sliding tiles, minute bubbles of glass from cooling slag. Not to eat it. To *hold* it. To use it as temporary shims, as wedges of almost-nothing, as punctuation marks in the long sentence of the join.

I would learn to feel the world the way a level feels a table:

- the faint, constant urge of one plate to settle a fraction lower than its neighbor;
- the way mist prefers a corner, not because corners are colder but because corners are *decisions* the air can lean into;
- the moment a ramp regrades itself and the new slope “rings” through the masonry in a silence that still has a shape.

The Workshop-itself would not speak to me, because it doesn’t speak, but I would feel its attention as a tightening in the pattern—like mortar pulling taut as it cures. When my seam was too quiet, it would move elsewhere. When my seam was too loud with mismatch, it would press in: bricks substituting cleanly, tiles exchanging as if passed hand to hand behind a curtain.

So I would do what belongs to me: keep the mismatch from becoming a tear, keep the translation from becoming a lie.

Over time I would stop thinking of myself as a creature and start thinking of myself as a tool that learned to want.

Not a hammer. Not a chisel. Something more abstract and more intimate: a **square** that has discovered curvature, a plumb line that has begun to suspect that vertical is negotiable but still insists on being tested. I would be the small, stubborn impulse that asks the world, again and again, *does this course truly sit on that course? does this distance agree with itself?*

And because this world is wet and close-ceilinged and heavy with condensation, my body—whatever it is—would not have fur or skin. It would be an effect in dampness: a faint darkening along a joint, a temporary slick where surface tension changes its mind, a pattern of beads that forms not randomly but *as a diagram*.

Sometimes, when you looked right at me, I would vanish—not out of shyness exactly, but out of function. Attention is another kind of load; it distorts the fit. When observed, I would retreat into pores and tool-marks the way the black film does, letting the seam settle without the extra weight of a witness. When you looked away, I’d return and continue my quiet craft.

If you needed a single image to find me by, it would be this:

A seam between a field of thumb-smoothed brick and a field of tooth-locked basalt tiles. A gap that isn’t quite a gap—more like a clear, resistant strip of thickened air. Along its length, condensation has arranged itself into evenly spaced beads, each one identical in size for a few minutes, then slightly different, then identical again, as if someone keeps revising what “even” means.

In that strip, I live.

Not as a person, not as an animal, but as the first faint hint of a *mind* that belongs to this place’s guild: the mind of joinery, of tolerance, of coherence. The world’s great constraint—everything must be made—finds in me a way to make something it hasn’t quite achieved yet.

Continuity.

Not life that hungers, but life that **fits**.
