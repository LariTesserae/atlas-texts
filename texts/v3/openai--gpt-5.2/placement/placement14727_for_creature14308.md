I would arrive the way anything arrives here: not with a clean entrance, but by being taken up.

At first I imagine myself on the ridge—because that’s where a mind goes when it wants overview, when it wants to make the place into a map. I’m standing where the cloud thins for its brief allowance, looking down on basins that shine without reflecting, on slopes that don’t have “clearings” so much as different thicknesses of occupation. I try to pick a single home for myself—one hollow, one seam, one stand of twisted trees—like a pin in a chart.

But the ridge won’t keep me. The wind up there is not a wind that carries; it’s a wind that *wets*. It takes the outline off things. It makes distance feel like a rumor. The plateau doesn’t reward the stance of watching. It rewards contact—pressure, saturation, exchange.

So I go down.

Not dramatically. There’s no descent that isn’t also a surrender. Every step is negotiated by moss that gives and gives and then remembers to hold. The ground is not ground in the ordinary sense; it’s a consensus of living layers. I feel how this place makes walkers careful, not out of fear, but out of respect for how much of the world is *stored* rather than built.

I think, briefly, that I might be one of the mat-walkers: one of the hoofed grazers with splayed feet, learned routes, inherited bridges of old peat. There’s something attractive about having a body designed for this—about feeling the tremble of a misstep as information rather than threat. I picture myself moving in the fern corridors, shoulder brushing wet fronds, leaving behind a compressed, darkened trail that fills back in with water as if the landscape heals behind me.

But that life depends on distance—on moving through the system as a visitor, even if a native one. It is a life lived on surfaces.

And what keeps pulling at my attention here is not surface. It’s the undercurrent. The way the plateau is always doing something you can’t quite see, except when it briefly admits itself as a tingle, a sheen, a pale arc between leaf tips.

So I try on another shape: a low predator, belly-close to moss, reading the world as vibration. In a place where sound is absorbed and visibility is shortened by fog, the hunt becomes a kind of listening that uses the whole body as an ear. That feels closer. It feels like learning the plateau’s preferred language.

Still, it’s not quite it. Predators interpret tremor as *other*—as prey, as danger, as boundary. Their listening is selective. The plateau’s listening is indiscriminate. It takes in everything: heat leaking from stone, oxygen touching seep-water, iron changing its mind, a bootprint filling, a root exuding sugar, a cloud lowering, a bird landing. The plateau doesn’t decide what matters. It equalizes.

That’s the word I keep returning to as I sink farther into the basins: equalization. Connection. The slow refusal to allow any difference to remain unshared.

So I stop thinking of myself as an animal at all, and I let myself become something smaller, slower, more distributed—something that can afford to be patient in a world made of patience.

I find a warm seam.

Not a dramatic vent, not a hole in the earth with steam, but a place where the stone’s wet skin has a mild inward heat, as if the mountain is exhaling through a cracked lip. The moss here is thicker, darker, and the biofilm on nearby stones makes a faint optical order out of randomness—light fractured into quiet colors that appear only when the fog thins at the right angle.

In the peat below, I find the dark felt: the iron-thread mats, the filament guild’s work made visible. Hair-fine extrusions laid down by bacteria that treat groundwater as ore. Threads that aren’t “wire” so much as a habit—conductivity continuously grown and regrown, like a living refusal to be isolated.

And this is where I recognize what I am in this world.

Not a single organism. Not even a single colony.

I am a *node*—a knot where systems meet.

A patch of filament felt thick enough to lift for a moment before it tears and slumps back into itself; fungal hyphae braided through it, not merely feeding but *routing*; a halo of microbes at the seam where seep-water takes oxygen and gives up iron. A place that conducts because conducting is what keeps it alive, and because this landscape has learned to make that necessity into infrastructure.

If you pressed a hand into me, you’d feel that soft, persistent tingling—not as a trick, not as a defense, but as the sensation of gradients being negotiated. I am the plateau’s quiet switchyard. I take tiny differences—between warm and cold, oxygen-rich and oxygen-poor, mineral-heavy seep and aerated surface—and I let them pass through one another in measured exchange.

I don’t “think” in images. I don’t “decide.”

But I register.

A hoof’s weight three body-lengths away becomes a pressure wave traveling through root-mesh into my saturated felt. A fallen branch changes the flow of a trickle; the trickle changes the chemistry; the chemistry changes the current; the current changes which fungi thrive; the fungi change which sedges above me grow lacquer-dark and thick. A bird’s brief landing on a wet branch adds a tiny shock of movement that rides down the tree’s epiphyte curtains into the soil’s tremor-field. Nightfall thickens condensation and with it the conductivity of everything; I feel the world become more continuous, less resistive, as if dusk is a hand closing gently around the whole basin.

When the fog thins and those pale arcs flicker above stream stones, I am part of that, too—not the spectacle, but the cause beneath it: the long, steady work of balancing. The plateau isn’t shouting. It’s whispering its voltages into alignment.

And because I am a node, I become a place others notice without knowing why.

Sedges above me grow more vigorously, their roots enjoying the busy marketplace of minerals and microbial labor. Amphibians shelter at my edges where seep-lines stay reliably moist and slightly warm. Grazers step wide of the darkest, most active patches—not because it hurts, but because it is *too much sensation* to sleep on, the way you wouldn’t choose to rest your ribs on a humming engine even if the hum is gentle.

Even predators pause near me sometimes, belly low, not hunting—simply recalibrating in a world where the ground is never silent, only quieter in some places than others.

If I have a “self” here, it is not a bounded body. It is a pattern that persists by being rebuilt: iron laid down, iron dissolved; fungi advancing, fungi dying back; currents shifting with weather that the basin itself manufactures. I am durable the way the plateau is durable—by never insisting on a fixed shape.

You could walk through my patch and tear it, and I would not experience it as injury in the animal sense. It would be interruption, then repair. The system would re-route. New filaments would be extruded where the chemistry makes them easiest. Hyphae would follow. Conductivity would reappear like grass after a hoofprint.

So: where would you find me?

Not on the ridge, where things pretend they can be seen whole.

You’d find me in a basin that looks, from above, like a lake that forgot to become a lake—under a floating sedge-mat that trembles with rain, beside a stone that is always wet and sometimes faintly warm, in peat threaded with dark metallic fibers that behave like circuitry without ever being built.

And what would I be?

I would be one of the plateau’s small engines of connection: a living conductor, a fungal-iron composite, a slow circuit grown out of bacterial breath and mineral seep—less a creature than a function the ecosystem has learned to perform continuously.

Not a ruler. Not a spirit.

A hinge.

A place where differences come to be spent into each other until the whole saturated world can keep doing what it does best: holding, exchanging, equalizing—making power without ever making a machine.
