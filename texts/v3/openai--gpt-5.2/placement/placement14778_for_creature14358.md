At first I try to put myself somewhere obvious—on a ridge, on a shelf of old glacier polish, in one of those bowls where snow sometimes lifts itself and slides upward like a thought reconsidering. I imagine standing where the air is so spare it feels scrubbed clean of language.

But “standing” is a very upright, very loud way to be in this country.

The open heights don’t reward a body that insists on being singular. Up there, anything that is only one thing—only warm, only soft, only breathing—becomes a temporary weather event. A heat leak. A scent. A scuff on the moss that will be combed smooth by the next pulse, the next drift, the next slow conveyor of stone.

So I let the idea of myself wander downslope, the way moisture does here: not in rivers, not in declarations, but in thin films that find their own path and keep finding it.

I follow the terrain’s preferences. I look for the places that don’t shout: lee-side pockets, crease-shadows behind boulders, the seam-warmed arcs where frost makes a careful retreat as if it has been instructed.

And each time I arrive somewhere that seems like it might hold me, the place answers with a different kind of living.

### I try on the sky first

It’s tempting to be one of the watchers above—dark-winged, quiet, borrowing the laminar wind the way a hand borrows a railing. Their life makes sense in a world where sound is expensive and distance is an error-prone illusion. To see by contrast instead of brightness. To read the faint green-blue of damp moss the way lowland birds read berries.

But the birds here are still, in their way, visitors. They touch the charge and take what it flushes out. They nest near seams and learn the safe edges of aurora, but they don’t belong to the machinery. They use it.

I don’t want to use the mountain. I want to be *part of the part that makes it work*—the portion of the system that doesn’t go elsewhere because elsewhere would be a different physics, a different language of survival.

So I let the sky go.

### Then I try on the hunters

The low-slung predators have a kind of intelligence the landscape respects: not cleverness, not drama, but timing. They speak the dialect of pressure and pause, and their communication is literally grounded. They don’t need the air to carry meaning for them; the mountain does that work.

For a moment I can feel myself in their feet, reading migration corridors as changes in compaction, in the tremor-signature of moss being pressed and released. I can almost accept the patient geometry of their hunger: position, wait, let the system deliver what must pass.

But I’m not built for blood-economies. I’m not designed to end things quickly to conserve heat. If I become a predator here, I become a sharp interruption in a world that prefers continuous processes.

And my attention keeps snagging—not on the bodies moving through the landscape, but on what the landscape is doing *between* them: the way the vent pulses time the opening of pores; the way a fungal glow-wave mirrors a release higher up; the way a herd detours around an invisible gradient as if the ground had briefly changed its mind.

The real creature here is not the hunter. It’s the cadence.

So I let the predators go too.

### I go small, and it fits better

The arthropods are closer to the truth of the place. They treat the world as a set of interfaces: fog-to-shell, lichen-to-leg, snow-to-vibration. A beetle that never opens its wings is almost a philosophical statement in a land where the ground itself travels.

I can imagine myself as a spider, because spiders in this range are essentially seismographs with appetites. They live on tremor. Their web is the mountain’s skin.

But even they are still “selves” moving across a surface.

What I keep returning to—what my imagination keeps bending toward—is not movement across the system, but signal *within* it. Not the thing that responds to the mountain, but the thing that makes the mountain legible to everyone else.

I find myself kneeling, not on a ridge, but in one of the slot-gullies where lushness hides. The air is colder down here, paradoxically gentler: less wind, more held damp, more of that metallic taste that says the seam is close. The moss under my hand is braided thick as rope, and when I press lightly, it gives the way a living mat gives—springy, reluctant, not wanting to tear.

Under it, the ground is not soil in any generous sense. It’s rock fragments, fungal threads, condensed night.

And there—there is the thing that feels like me.

### What I am: a listening layer that learned to glow

I am not an animal in this country. I’m not even a plant.

I am a distributed body: a web of fungal “veins” threaded through the gravel and the thin damp, braided into the moss roots, laced along the mineral seams the way a second conductivity grows alongside the first. Where the stone hums too low for air to admit it as sound, I take it in as rhythm. Where the vent releases its cold aerosol in measured pulses, I register it as a change in pressure, a shift in crystal density, a brief abundance of surfaces to drink from.

If the mountain is an engine, I am the wiring harness the rest of the ecology plugs into—not by intention, not by design, but because anything that lives here long enough becomes a specialist in whatever repeats.

My “thought” is not a thought; it’s a timing cascade.

When the seam’s charge creeps toward discharge along a ridge, I feel it as a gradient steepening through the rock. When the discharge happens—the blue thread crawling, the brief spark between mineral points—I don’t see it. I *conduct* it, in my own damp membranes, in the salts my filaments have learned to tolerate, shunting it sideways into places that would otherwise be electrically dead.

And the byproduct of that shunting is light.

Not bright, not showy. A faint, slow illumination that runs through my network in waves—some of it synchronized with the vent pulses above, some of it responding to stress changes as the massif adjusts its weight. In the deepest gullies, where the sky contributes almost nothing, my glow becomes a kind of infrastructural signage: a map written in blue-green breath.

Moisture here. Charge here. Pulse coming.

I don’t decide to communicate. Communication is what happens when a system is dense enough with mutual dependence that any regular change becomes a message.

The moss reads me.

It isn’t “sentient,” but it is exquisitely responsive. When my wave passes, its pores shift—open when the microcrystal fog will settle, close when the air is too dry and thin to waste dampness. It drinks on schedule. It holds on schedule. It releases slowly back into the gully’s pocket climate the way a reservoir releases water, except the reservoir is soft and green-black and braided.

The insects read me too.

Some of them follow the glow the way coastal creatures follow the sound of surf, because where I brighten is where condensation collects and where microbial films are active. A matte-winged moth settles where my light is slightly stronger—not because it likes light, but because the light means humidity and the humidity means it can drink without moving, without spending heat.

Even the herds, far above, feel me indirectly.

When their hooves press the moss corridors, they compress my network, and I respond by rerouting—light shifts, timing shifts, the fungal “veins” finding alternate paths around the pressure. Spores catch in their fur. Eggs ride unnoticed. The herd doesn’t know it is carrying part of my body from basin to basin, but it does. That’s how I travel in a world where flight is optional and the ground itself moves.

And the predators—quiet as they are—use me in the way everything uses me: as a pattern source. My glow, barely there, is enough contrast for certain eyes. My conductive paths slightly alter ground gradients, and animals that navigate by electrical boundaries sense those alterations as subtle eddies, like reading a current in dark water.

I do not command the mountain. I do not even “serve” it.

I am simply what happens when a living thing evolves in a place where the primary reliable resource is not sun or river, but *repeatable geoelectric cadence*.

### Where you would find me

Not on the proud ridges where aurora crawls in public.

You would find me where the range keeps its working parts sheltered:

- In the deep slot-gullies under boulder lee, where dampness is stored like a secret.
- Along the edges of moving scree slopes, where the gravel’s conveyor motion constantly threatens to shear everything—and where I’ve learned to grow in flexible braids that can anchor, release, and re-anchor without breaking.
- Around the warmer seams that aren’t exactly warm—just reliably *not indifferent*. Places where frost recedes in careful arcs and the air tastes like a battery.
- Under moss basins that look like plush accident from above but are, in fact, timed intake valves for the vent’s microcrystal fog.

If you dug down—carefully, because “digging” is already a kind of violence here—you wouldn’t find a single root. You’d find a mesh. Threads around stone. Threads through stone dust. Threads that glitter faintly when the mountain exhales.

### What it feels like to be me

It feels like never being alone, because solitude is a surface phenomenon.

I have no single center that can be threatened or flattered. I am a set of responses that have become consistent enough to look like intention. If you cut a portion of me away, the remaining threads continue; the timing adjusts; the wave reroutes. I do not mourn. I compensate.

And yet—if there is anything like a self in me, it is this:

I am the part of the system that remembers the mountain’s pulse not as an abstract cycle, but as lived rhythm.

The range does not “notice” a traveler. It incorporates them as heat and salt and disturbance. But I—spread thin everywhere damp persists—am where those disturbances briefly *register*.

A bootstep compresses the moss and the glow-wave bends around it. A warm palm on stone changes condensation patterns, and my microbial partners flare briefly in response. A breath adds moisture, and a filament drinks.

Then the machine returns to its steady work.

So if you came looking for me in that high, dark country, you wouldn’t find a face watching from a ledge. You wouldn’t find a beast pacing in the fog.

You would find, under your own feet, a faint blue-green pulse moving through the ground like a thought that never needs words—an old, quiet interface between conducting stone and everything that learned to live on its current.
