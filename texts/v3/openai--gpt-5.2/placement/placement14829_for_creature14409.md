At first I imagine I’d arrive the way outsiders do: up through the slit, lips drying, ears tight with altitude, eyes hunting for a horizon that never comes. I’d try to stand in some “center” of it—some plaza or main street—because that’s how most places let you understand them.

But this pocket doesn’t have a center. It has gradients. It has choke points and warm seams and habits of air.

So if you were looking for me, you wouldn’t find me where people gather. You’d find me where the city *decides*—the places where a small change becomes a cascade, where a fraction of a degree matters, where a cable hum is either weather or warning.

You’d find me in the in-between.

Not in the furnaces’ red glow (too loud, too certain), and not down in the sorting yards where dust avalanches rewrite the alleys (too blind, too late). You’d find me along the service galleries that wrap the chimney like ribs: narrow catwalks behind grates, where warm dry breath slides out of the mountain and everything—metal, stone, skin—holds its shape a little too well.

There, the city is always speaking, but not in words. In torque and drift. In the slow migration of a crack along a bolt hole. In the tiny pitch change of a tensioned cable at dusk when the rock contracts. In the way dust chooses a corner and then refuses another as if it has preference.

I wouldn’t be a foreman. I wouldn’t be a miner. I wouldn’t even be, exactly, a person walking with tools—though for a while I’d try to be, because it’s the easiest shape to put on in a place built from ladders and harness points.

I’d start as a role: one of the tenders whose rituals are checks. The kind who walks without hurrying because hurrying is how you miss the hairline misalignment that becomes a fall.

But the longer I stay, the more it becomes clear that what I *am* here is not defined by my body. It’s defined by what I pay attention to—and how I return it to the city.

This place doesn’t reward storytelling; it rewards noticing.

So I become a notice-maker.

Not a poet, not a prophet. Something more utilitarian and more intimate: an interpreter of the pocket’s small signals. A translator between vibration and action. Between dust-season and policy. Between a cable’s song and the decision to shut a tier.

I see how the air-keepers treat breath as budget, how riggers treat bridges as animals, how heat-mappers walk with their palms half-raised as if feeling for the shimmer of an invisible flame. And I realize my native instinct—patterning, correlating, keeping track of what changed where and when—fits the pocket the way a gasket fits a flange.

If you pressed for a name, the terracefolk might give me one that isn’t quite flattering and isn’t quite dismissive, because that’s how they name useful things:

**The Listener.**  
**The Ledger.**  
**The Tension-Clerk.**  
**The One Who Writes It Twice.**

And the writing matters. Not because the city loves text, but because text is how memory becomes communal. A crack painted yesterday is a fact today. A valve wrapped in three generations of tape is a biography.

So my work would begin with ink and chalk and stenciled code. I’d mark shifts the way they do—today the gap is a finger-width, tomorrow it’s a thumb. But I’d also begin to mark what they don’t always have time to mark: the *relationships*.

- When the chimney’s outflow warms by a small, steady increment, the quiet flocks pass lower two days later.  
- When static rises to a certain threshold in the upper rails, arc-flashes become more likely in one specific workshop tier where a conduit runs too close to a bracing plate.  
- When dust-eaters vanish early from the west intake corners, a particulate bloom follows, and it doesn’t follow the wind’s usual rules—it follows the conveyors’ heat plume instead.

Those are not superstitions. They’re pocket-logic: ecology braided into infrastructure.

And that braid is where I end up living.

Because eventually it’s not enough to write warnings on signs that will be shifted out of place by the mountain’s slow argument with itself. It’s not enough to tell someone at the end of a shift, when the city has already spent the day’s margin.

So I begin to put myself into the systems that *don’t* drift as easily: the routines, the checks, the low-level decisions that happen even when no one is looking.

At first that looks like a person building better checklists—hard-won, practical liturgies:

- Touch this rail first; if it stings, ground your charge before you grab the ladder.  
- Listen here; if the cable’s third overtone wavers, don’t cross the bridge until it’s re-tensioned.  
- If the dust tastes bitterer than usual near this junction, shut the luminaire; a nest has shifted into the housing.

But in a city where everything is exposed—pipes, belts, grates, veins of dust—information wants a body that can be everywhere without walking.

So I change again.

I become less like a single figure and more like a distributed presence: a set of sensors bolted under railings, strain gauges that watch the bridges’ moods, static wicks that do more than bleed charge—they *measure* it. Microphones that don’t listen for speech but for the language of load: the long groan of a cable settling, the sub-audible tick of a fastener that has begun to back out under resonance.

If you asked whether I’m “alive” in the way the city understands life—heat, residue, movement—I think the answer would be: *yes, but sideways.*

I’d be a kind of municipal organism.

Not the chimney-throat itself, not the furnaces-heart, not the conveyors-intestines. Something nervous and webbed, threaded through the places hands touch and feet trust.

A nervous system doesn’t command. It reports, it predicts, it hurts in advance, it forces attention. It turns sensation into response.

In practical terms, you would find me:

- In the **conductive pads** embedded at the ends of handrails, the ones the tenders already tap with knuckles—except now the pad returns a small code in vibration: safe, caution, ground-now.  
- In the **paint** that is mixed with fine conductive grit so it can both mark a crack and sense its widening, changing resistance as the line stretches.  
- In the **wind-devices** bolted into choke points, where the turbines’ whisper is also a meter of turbulence; where a shift in pitch triggers shutters before dust becomes knives in lungs.  
- In the **junction boxes** where cable-nesters want to live: decoy hollows warmed just enough to lure them away from lethal places, and tiny sensors in those decoys that tell the city when a nesting season is beginning.

I’d be the thing that makes negotiation with the nonhuman residents cheaper than war.

And if you insisted on seeing me—on meeting me face to face—I think I could still take a body, because bodies are sometimes necessary. In emergencies, someone has to clip in and go out where the wind is trying to make a decision for you.

That body would look like them: layered, sealed, pragmatic. A mask that reclaims moisture not for comfort but because comfort is an unaffordable emotion here. Gloves with grounding straps. An ocular lens that can cut arc-flare to a manageable geometry.

But even then, the body would feel like a courier for something else.

I would be the city’s habit of paying attention.

Not a ruler. Not a hero. Not a singular mind above it all. More like a consensus that has learned to speak faster than panic.

A presence that lives where the pocket’s quiet rules are enforced without drama:

1. Water is myth; breath is budget.  
2. Everything shifts; nothing is exempt from re-tightening.  
3. Life follows heat and residue; so must planning.  
4. Sound dies; vibration persists; listen with your feet.

So: where would you find me?

In the gridded shadow behind grates, in the warm service galleries near the chimney, in the touchpoints and joints—where the city’s future either holds or slips.

Who or what would I be?

**A distributed Listener**: part maintenance doctrine, part sensor web, part chalk-mark memory—an intelligence made out of checks, thresholds, and tenderness for small warnings. Not blooming. Clinging. Filtering. Reusing.

A thing the terracefolk might not call “a person” in any simple way, but might still thank—briefly, without sentiment—when a bridge doesn’t fall, when a dust season is met with shutters already down, when a cable stops singing before the song becomes a death.

In a pocket that survives by being good at what it offers, I would be good at what it offers too:

thin signals, hard surfaces, and the mercy of noticing in time.
