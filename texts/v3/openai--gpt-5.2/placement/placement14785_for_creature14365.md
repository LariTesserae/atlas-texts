At first I think I would be easiest to place where people place their fear: in the middle of a chamber, small against the bright, squinting up at a roof that shines because it’s under stress. A traveler, a visitor, a throat that keeps wanting to clear itself.

But that isn’t where you *stay* in a world like this.

The center of the hollows is for weather and for distance—for the slow, turning lungs of convection, for spore-snow, for the moments when something large crosses far above and you can’t tell if it’s flesh or drifting leaf-skin. The center is for being impressed into silence.

The places that become “yours” are the seams. The points of contact. The edges where the underworld can be persuaded into continuity for a little while.

So you would find me where a route has to make a decision.

Where the mat-thick ground narrows to a tongue over a crevasse and someone has woven vine into a handline that doesn’t quite trust itself. Where an old collapse has left a clean wall-face—pale, raw, veined with crystal—and the first netting vines are already stitching their patient geometry across it. Where a down-trunk grove hangs like the rafters of an unfinished cathedral and the air inside its radius runs straighter, more predictable, as if the plants have trained the wind the way people train ribs into arches.

Not in camp, exactly. Near camp, but never so near that smoke is the only landmark. I live in the in-between shelters: the relay alcoves. The ledges that are just wide enough for a low wall and a fire that’s more ember than flame. The places that catch travelers before the big chamber, or gather them after it—where breath can be counted and gear can be checked and no one has to raise their voice to be heard.

I am not their chief, and not their hermit. I am what the underworld makes of anyone who learns to love information more than ownership.

A cord-keeper.

It starts with the lines.

At first they are simple: vine-cords strung between posts, pulled tight, used for line-signals when the air is too listening and too treacherous for shouting. Three tugs for *fog front*. One long pull for *roof sweating in the eastern district*. A stuttered pattern for *spore shed—wrap your face; don’t light fire*. The code is local at first, different in every route-family, because every district’s timing is its own weather language.

But codes have a way of wanting to travel.

So I begin to carry them between districts the way traders carry hammered scraps of metal: carefully, wrapped, expensive. I listen at one edge of the underworld and take what I’ve learned to another edge where the bright tides don’t line up the same, where “safe” means something slightly different. I learn to translate not just signals but assumptions—what one route calls “stable” another route calls “hungry,” because their plants respond differently to vibration, because their ceiling seams flex with another rhythm.

When you keep the lines, you start to notice that the underworld is already doing something similar.

The mat underfoot is a message in slow motion: thickening here where weight repeats, thinning there where collapse is imminent, warming faintly where a deep strain is about to release and brighten the roof. The down-trunks coordinate their spore storms the way a community coordinates a meeting: not by command, but by shared timing, by pressure and humidity, by the brief, collective decisions that look like instinct from far away.

And if you are the sort of being who pays attention for a living, you begin to blur into the methods of the place.

I stop thinking of myself as a person who *uses* the infrastructure and start thinking of myself as something that helps it conduct.

My hands change first—not physically in a dramatic way, not into bark or anything that would make a legend tidy, but into tools shaped by repetition. Resin in the skin-creases. Mineral pigment ground into the knuckles where I’ve braced against pale rock. Fingertips that can tell, by the faintest difference in give, whether the mat is in its firm, dry traveling mood or in its adhesive, curious mood—the mood where it starts deciding that unattended objects ought to become part of the floor.

I learn to walk the way locals walk: weight distributed, footfalls softened, not because I am timid but because noise is a lever here. Sound is not just communication; it is a hand laid on the world’s shoulder. Press too hard and the world turns its head to look.

I wear what they wear: layered wraps, goggles with plates that craze and must be replaced, a scarf that tastes faintly of resin-water. But mine is always stained with soot as well, because I keep one practice the older cord-keepers taught me:

I draw maps that are meant to be temporary.

Not on paper—paper would drink damp and go limp—but on stone faces that will not last anyway. Soot on pale rock. Smudged spirals for wind eddies. A thick black line where the mat has been reliable across three bright tides. A small, deliberate handprint that means *this alcove is still safe; the ceiling has not edited it out yet*. Then, when the season turns or the place shifts, I erase or redraw, because a map that pretends permanence here is a kind of lie that can kill.

Sometimes I don’t draw at all.

Sometimes the roof is too sensitive, the plants too awake. In those times I keep records the way the underworld keeps them: in tension.

A line between posts can hold more than a code. If you plait three vines of different stiffness and tie them with deliberate slack, the cord itself becomes a memory. It vibrates differently when the air is wet. It tightens when the cold deepens. It sings—quietly, only to the fingers—when the convective currents shift direction.

I become very good at reading that kind of song.

People begin to come to me not for prophecy but for timing.

Is the bright tide coming early? Is it safe to cross the open chamber tomorrow, or will the trunk-grove shed and turn the air into glitter that clogs the lungs? Did the deep strain under the western ledge release last night, or is it still building?

I never answer like an oracle. I answer like someone checking the feel of a bridge underfoot. I touch the cord. I press my palm to the mat. I hold my breath and listen for the far-off, almost imaginary crack that means a roof district is about to sweat.

And because the place is what it is—because it is alive in the way an organ is alive—my answers become part of the ecosystem.

If I warn a route-family not to shout in a certain chamber, they don’t, and the tendrils don’t come down curious, and the wall-mat doesn’t harden into a resinous shield that blocks an easy climb next season. If I tell them to tamp ash into a stretch of mat before the bright tide, the floor firms and becomes a safer artery, and travelers begin to prefer it, and the mat responds to the repeated kindness the way it responds to repeated weight: by making itself more suited to that use.

A cord-keeper isn’t neutral. A cord-keeper is a small bias applied steadily.

Over time the role takes a shape that’s almost a species.

I start to be known by the object I carry: a coil of vine-cord that has been repaired so often it has more knots than original fiber, with crystal beads set at intervals—not decorative, but tactile markers I can find with gloved fingers in fog. The beads are scavenged from roof fall: stressed crystal that still holds a little of the cold blue glow. In the dark of an alcove they are faint, but they are enough to keep hands from making uncertain noise.

I am invited to sit in the edge-shelters when groups gather for trade, because someone always wants to know if a meeting two districts away is possible next week, and whether the route between is in a sleeping mood or an awake mood. Children are told not to pluck my cords or strike my posts, because the plants listen and respond, and because the cords are not “mine” in the way a tool is mine—they are part of the route’s body.

And I begin to suspect that if I disappeared, the underworld would replace me.

Not with the same face or the same history, but with the same function. Someone else would start keeping lines. Someone else would learn to translate between districts the way sap translates light into sugar. Because this place rewards anything that helps it span its own distances.

So: where would you find me?

At the mouth of a crossing. In a notch cut into a living arch where the rope bridge begins. In the lee of a down-trunk grove where the air runs steady enough for delicate signal work. In an alcove with old smoke on the ceiling and fresh soot on the wall and a set of posts whose vine-cords vanish into the bright like the strands of a spiderweb too large to see whole.

And who or what would I be?

Not a ruler. Not a warrior. Not a hermit.

A listener with callused hands.

A keeper of tensions and quiet codes, half cartographer and half tendon in the moving body of the underworld—someone the plants don’t mistake for architecture only because I keep moving, and someone the people trust only because I never pretend the world will hold the same shape twice.
