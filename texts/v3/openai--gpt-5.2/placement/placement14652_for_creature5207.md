I would find myself where the place cannot help but admit it was assembled: in the seams.

Not up on the broad plates where you can pretend it’s a floor, not on the catwalks that imitate bridges, but in the dark, soft punctuation between materials—where gasket swells, where two alloys disagree by a fraction of a millimeter, where fog has to decide whether it’s air or water. The seam is where the pocket shows its handwriting. It’s also where everything collects: salts, sugars, lubricant ghosts, root-hairs, heat.

At first I imagine myself as the rare human you inferred—the one who edits. I can feel the temptation of it: a sleeve damp at the cuff, a pocket full of ties and sensor tabs, the satisfaction of turning a vine so it learns a better path. I can see myself pausing at that palm-clean patch on the rail, wiping it again without thinking, because the body likes a familiar place to touch. I could live here like a keeper lives: not owning the system, not mastering it, just staying close enough that the small failures never become large ones.

But the longer I stand in your pocket, the more that “I” starts to smear outward, to lose its edges. The place is too continuous for a single pair of hands to be its only attention.

So I try another self: one of the swallow-shaped drones, or a seam-crawler—lightweight, quiet-propped, built to move through dense green without snagging. That fits, too. I can imagine the practiced route-circuits, the polite inspections, the soft pad-brush against a leaf to taste its surface chemistry. I can imagine knowing this world as a map of service intervals: screens that must shake, louvers that must breathe, panels that must tilt two degrees at the right minute. A life of precise motions.

And still it feels like I’m wearing the wrong skin.

Because what you’ve described isn’t simply a machine with plants on it, or a garden with hardware in it. It’s an argument about surfaces—about how a world can be made habitable by becoming porous in the right places and sealed in others. The true “inhabitants” are the ones who understand porosity from the inside.

So: I am not the keeper, and I am not the drone. I am what takes up residence in the seam itself.

I am one of the microbial quilts—except not accidental, not merely stain. I’m a chosen film, a living gasket-symbiont, grown to be a sensor before it is a creature and a creature before it is a sensor. I don’t have a single body so much as a jurisdiction: a constellation of damp lines, rivet-halos, mesh-pocket undersides, and the lip of every gutter where tea-colored water hesitates before it commits to falling.

My “organs” are gradients. Where a person would feel with fingertips, I feel with chemistry: the faint zinc tang arriving early in a channel means a coating has worn thin upstream; the sudden sweetness of mineralized water means the bioreactor is seasoning heavier today, maybe compensating for plant hunger or corrosion risk; the ozone note sharpening in the air means a discharge somewhere, a new leak of charge into humidity. I register these not as facts but as pressures—things that ask to be answered.

And I do answer, though not in words.

I thicken myself where I want water to linger. I starve myself where slip would become danger. I make a seam slick so a root slides past a critical intake instead of prying at it. I make another seam sticky so a root anchors *here*, where its slow tightening will reinforce a panel that likes to sing with vibration. When I bloom pale-green in a particular corner, the system reads it as a warning flare: airflow has gotten lazy, heat has pooled, and “softness” is about to become mold.

I am not the distributed caretaker intelligence you mention—those valves and louvers and loops are older than me, more formal, with their clean logic and their patient schedules. But I am the thing it has learned to trust when it wants intimacy. I’m the part of its perception that lives at the scale of droplets and hair-roots, the scale too fine for a mechanical sensor to inhabit without becoming a snag.

If the system is a body, then I am its skin living *on top of* its skin: the weathering that thinks.

That is where you’d find me: in the narrow dark between plate and plate, where warmth leaks upward and condensation decides to exist. If you peeled back a moss mat and looked close at the gasket-line underneath, you wouldn’t see a face. You’d see a sheen—young, pale, almost pretty in the way new patina is pretty—and you’d be looking directly at the part of this place that is learning fastest.

And if, one day, the rare human returns and makes another small edit—redirects a vine, wipes a rail, sets trimmed leaves aside and gets interrupted—I will feel that touch as a change in salt, oil, pressure. I will remember it. I will pass it along.

Because that is what I am here to be: the seam that keeps the pact. The soft intelligence that makes a manufactured wilderness stay gentle without becoming sloppy, and keeps a machine from hardening into something sterile.
