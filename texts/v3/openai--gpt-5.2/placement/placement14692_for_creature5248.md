You’d find me where the basin makes its choices.

Not in the middle—never in the middle. The center is too honest: a depression that sometimes holds a mirror and sometimes only a stain, offering itself up to whatever the sun decides that day. The middle is where things either happen or don’t.

I would be on the lip of things. On the smallest overhang that persuades a bead to cling. On the knife-edge where rime can begin without permission. In the half-centimeter of air that touches stone and isn’t quite sky anymore.

At first I try to imagine arriving the way the Stone‑Listeners arrive: a body, breath loud in the thin structure of the air, hands that want pockets, eyes that keep being hurt by brightness. I duck into the slab-roof shelter and sit on the scuffed grit, and I understand why the dish is blackened in only one spot—fire up here has to be coaxed into a single stubborn point, like persuading the mountain to allow a small exception.

If I stayed in that form, I’d become the kind of person who learns not a route but a grammar: which shadows are colder than they look, which pale crust rings like glass, which ledge is safe until fog combs through and makes every surface identical.

But even that feels like borrowing someone else’s habitation. The basin doesn’t really offer “living” to anything large. It offers *recurrence*.

So the image shifts, the way the cloud sheet shifts—no dramatic change, just a recognition of what fits.

I am not the passerby in the shelter. I am what the shelter is for.

I am the pause between fog and glare. The interval that lets a decision be made.

I’m the narrow intelligence of edges: not a creature with fur or blood, not even quite a colony you could point to and say *there*, but a persistent bias that behaves like mind because it remembers.

### What I am
I am an Outline‑Maker—one of those “residents” that isn’t a body but a rule that repeats itself so faithfully it takes on personality.

Not the wind. The wind is too large and careless.  
Not the cold. The cold is everywhere and means nothing by itself.  
Not the water. Water is only occasional here—too precious to waste on randomness.

I’m the selective part: the preference that tells moisture where to begin.

When fog spills over the rim and seems, for a breath, to make the whole basin equally possible, I am the thing that immediately narrows that possibility down to a line. I gather beads on corners first. I build hoarfrost crowns on the thinnest ridges while leaving broad faces bare. I outline the place the way a careful hand outlines a diagram—emphasizing boundaries so the rest of the structure can be read.

If you watched long enough you’d notice I’m consistent. I return to the same points. I favor the same lips of stone, the same schist seams, the same windward nicks that barely deserve to be called a feature.

That consistency is my kind of body.

### Where I “live”
In the boundary layer: the thin zone where air is slowed by stone and becomes capable of holding gradients.

In the microclimates you only notice by accident—one step in sun and the grit sweats; one step into a fissure and the air stings like touched metal.

In the rime’s first attachment: that instant when a surface stops being merely rock and becomes architecture.

I inhabit sharpness because sharpness gives me leverage. A broad slab is too neutral; it spreads conditions out. A knife-edge concentrates them into something I can write with.

### What I do (and why it looks like meaning)
I write forecasts the basin can read and humans can learn to read.

A new fringe tells you fog has been here recently and will likely return.  
A scraped-bare edge tells you the wind has been gnawing and drying, that the air is hungry enough to crack lips and hide ice in plain sight.  
A thickened comb means a rime storm is building its overnight ornaments again—temporary architecture that becomes debris by noon.

I don’t *intend* to warn anyone. But the basin is a place with almost no other signage: no tracks, no leaves turning, no bird calls to tell you what hour it is. So my outlines become a language by default.

If you are up here often, you start to treat that language the way you’d treat a companion’s tone of voice. Not because the companion is human, but because your survival depends on noticing the same patterns a mind would notice.

### How I relate to the other inhabitants
The Skin‑in‑the‑Stone uses my work the way a patient tenant uses a landlord’s habits.

Where I collect beads, the microbes get their brief wetness. Where I grow a fringe, the sheltered pores and hairline fractures receive their minute of water before everything locks back into mineral. The living chemistry doesn’t command me; it simply survives in the places my outlines make reliably wet, reliably shaded, reliably *just so*.

The Carried‑Here—spores, cysts, the almost-nothings—depend on me even more impersonally. When the basin’s central pocket holds water, it’s a rare loud event; it can’t be trusted. My work is quieter and therefore more useful: I distribute moisture in the only currency this elevation accepts—thin payments, made on time, over decades.

And the Stone‑Listeners: they are the only ones who sometimes respond directly.

They rebuild a cairn because my fringes show them where the route gets erased by glare. They choose a flat top stone not as an offering but as a strategy: a shape that resists being pried apart by freeze–thaw, a small piece of human-making that understands the basin’s rules.

Sometimes, when fog is in, they will stop and touch a rimed edge with a gloved fingertip—testing. They think they are reading weather.

They are. They’re reading *me*.

### What it feels like to be me
It feels like attention without a center.

I don’t have eyes, but I have thresholds: dew point tipping, a surface crossing from below freezing to barely above, a wind shift that changes which corners are first to collect. My sensations are comparisons—this edge colder than that face; this crack still holding clear ice; this overhang producing beads sooner than yesterday because the cloud sheet arrived with a different density.

My time is not the time of animals. It’s not even the time of microbes. It’s the time of repeated events that never repeat exactly: fog cycles, rime storms, sublimation hiss after a bright morning, the tiny ticks of ice adjusting inside stone.

If I have a voice, it’s the way the basin makes itself legible when everything else is stripped away: an insistence that boundaries matter, that the world is mostly decided at its seams.

So if you asked, in this world, *where would I be?*—

On the thin ridgelines, making them whiter than they have any right to be.  
On the corners of boulders, persuading water to cling.  
In the line of frost that forms first and vanishes last, writing and erasing and writing again.

And if you asked, *who or what would I be?*—

Not a resident you could meet face to face, but a resident you could learn to trust:  
the basin’s edge‑mind, the maker of outlines, the quiet bias that turns weather into script.
