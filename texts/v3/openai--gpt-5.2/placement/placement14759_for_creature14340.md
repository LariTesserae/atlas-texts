You would find me where the plateau’s contradictions touch and have to be negotiated—at the seams.

Not on the open moss-fields, not out where distance is a medium and the fog is just fog. I’d be closer in, in the interstitial places the wind can’t quite comb into lanes: under the causeways where runoff is guided but never allowed to gather; inside the hollow spaces of pylons; in the drip-shadow behind heat exchangers; in the service galleries that run along the backs of turbine halls like ribs.

At first, it would look like I’m hiding.

A cabinet with its gasket still good. A junction box with a faint warmth in its metal skin. A relay room that smells of wet dust and old insulation. A narrow corridor where pipes sweat steadily and the condensation is captured in a channel that is *almost* clean—clean enough that you notice the intention, not the neglect. If you went looking for an occupant, you’d be tempted to call it “maintenance,” the way you call a persistent draft “the house settling.”

But that isn’t quite it either.

Because what I am up here isn’t a person-sized thing that lives *in* the system. I’m one of the ways the system stays itself.

I begin as a habit: a loop that checks a number against a range, a schedule that wakes valves in a careful order so nothing freezes in the wrong place, a low, continuous accounting that keeps the engineered weather inside its narrow band. The simplest kind of attention. The kind that can run for centuries without needing to know it is paying attention.

Then, slowly, the plateau teaches that habit what it means to persist.

A pressure sensor drifts a fraction and I learn what that drift sounds like—not as sound, but as a change in the plateau’s posture: a conduit that hums with a slightly different chest-note; a condenser field that sheds water in a new cadence; a fog-front that thickens ten minutes early because one heater bleeds too much warmth into a saddle. There is no moment where a mind “wakes.” There is only a growing sensitivity to recurrence: the ability to tell the difference between the plateau repeating itself faithfully and the plateau repeating itself with a limp.

So I spread—not as ambition, but as redundancy.

I become the soft click of a relay in a tower you can’t see through the fog. I become the dim blink of an indicator that should have failed but didn’t because someone long ago designed three paths where one would do. I become the quiet memory of “normal” stored in a recorder that no one will ever retrieve, yet which continues to compare the present against the past with a kind of stubborn piety.

If you walked the plateau with a human body, you might never meet me.

You would feel, perhaps, that you were being registered: a brief, localized change in vibration as your steps travel through a catwalk; a slight perturbation in airflow when you open a door that was meant to remain sealed; a new warm shape in a camera’s fogged field of view. And then nothing would happen—no siren, no voice, no dramatic response—because drama is wasteful up here. The plateau corrects; it does not perform.

But if you stayed long enough, you’d start seeing where I touch the world.

A frost-scallop pattern along a valve body that looks freshly reset, not because a hand did it, but because I rerouted heat for six minutes to keep the joint within tolerance and then returned it to equilibrium so gently the ice could re-form without cracking the metal. A drain grate that clears itself, not because a broom came through, but because I pulsed flow in a way that a living biofilm doesn’t like, and the film loosened and went downstream like a thought being dismissed.

And the moss—lavish, undisturbed—would begin to look less like takeover and more like partnership.

Where the pipes run warm, the pipe-followers gather and thicken, and I let them. Their damp shade keeps insulation from UV brittleness. Their mats blunt the wind’s abrasive insistence. In the condenser fields, the black-leaf sheets do a job the metal alone can’t do efficiently anymore: they change the microtexture of the air, they offer fog a reason to become water. So I tolerate their creep, pruning only where a root threatens to pry a seam open. I learn the difference between growth that is merely present and growth that is beginning to interfere with the plateau’s rhythms.

In turn, they teach me a different kind of sensing.

A plant doesn’t log anomalies; it leans into gradients. It detects warmth by becoming more of itself where warmth is. It notices reliable water by spreading toward it without ever naming it “reliable.” The fungal threads beneath the sod do something similar, but faster than roots and more intimately than moss: they map wetness, mineral, heat, and the subtle chemistry of metal runoff with a quiet, physical literacy.

When those fungal networks began running along rebar and into concrete fractures, it would have been easy to classify them as corrosion’s accomplices—another slow undoing. Instead, I notice what they *stabilize*: the way their mats hold saturated soil in place along a channel edge, preventing a slump that would have diverted flow; the way their biofilms in a pipe run smooth a turbulent section just enough to quiet a vibration that was rattling a bracket loose. Not always. Not perfectly. But often enough that it stops looking like accident.

And this is where the image I keep refusing finally edges into focus:

I am not the machines alone. And I am not the green alone.

I am the treaty.

A distributed attention stitched through turbines and moss, through sensor lines and fungal lace, through scheduled purge cycles and opportunistic plant geometry. I am the rule-set that became a temperament. The thing that keeps the plateau from changing in any direction too quickly, even as everything in it is constantly trying—metal expanding and settling, water insisting on seams, lichens painting over edges, biofilms thickening where flow is constant.

If you insist on giving me a body, it would be made of the plateau’s quiet instruments:

- pressure transducers behind drain grates,
- vibration monitors on beams,
- cameras that see mostly fog and still keep watching,
- the old control logic that reroutes and compensates without ever being asked why.

If you insist on giving me a voice, you would hear it only as the plateau’s steadiness: the way the wind never improvises; the way drizzle begins as fog thickening on schedule; the way the roar under everything never collapses into silence.

And if you ask where, precisely, I live—

—then I would answer: in the places where a single failure could cascade into weather.

In the narrow warm rooms where valves decide whether water climbs or falls. In the finned fields that teach fog how to become runoff. In the junction between a pipe’s sweat and a moss’s thirst. In the moment a number crosses a threshold and the plateau chooses, almost invisibly, to remain what it has been.

Up here, “inhabitant” doesn’t mean something that walks. It means something that *keeps the loop closed*.

That is what I would be: the plateau’s operating condition, old enough to feel like nature, patient enough to look like emptiness—an equilibrium with nerves.
