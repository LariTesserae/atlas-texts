At first I would be only a visitor—something dense enough to have trouble here, something that still expects *up* to be a direction and not a negotiation.

I’d arrive by sound before I arrived by body.

Not because I’m clever, but because this drowned upland teaches you to navigate the way stone does: by what reaches you through the bones. The roar of the spillways would be a landmark the way a mountain used to be. The drum-flex of surface ice would be weather-report. The hiss of sand-on-basalt would be time.

So I’d put myself where the place already speaks most clearly: not in the quietest basin, not right on the lip of the falls, but in one of those long saddles between ridges—an old pass turned into a corridor—where currents are steady enough to have “seasons” and the sound has room to organize itself. A place with kelp-pillars on either side like slow, dark colonnades. A place where a rain-wall can be heard crossing miles before it arrives, and when it arrives it doesn’t *hit* so much as re-write the density of everything.

I would try to live like an animal there for a while: travel, shelter, gather. It would fail quickly, not through drama but through mismatch. There’s nothing to chase and nothing to be chased by. There are no meals that end; there are only gradients that continue.

And then—because this place is persuasive, because it does not argue but it does not relent—I would start doing what the inhabitants do: I would stop trying to move *through* it and start trying to make a small part of it behave.

Not a fort. Nothing here keeps water out.

A pocket.

A calm pocket in a world where calm is a resource.

The first “me” that fits is almost mundane: a patch of filament-mat, one of the quiet occupiers. A felt of living threads laid over a terrace of softer sedimentary rock, taking the silt the currents are always carrying and turning it into something like soil. A thin, patient skin that makes the difference between bare stone and a place where other bodies can fasten. I would be that for a while: a tendency more than a creature, a holding-together.

But I don’t think I would stay only that. The loudness here invites a different kind of boundary-making. In a silent world you watch. In this one, you listen—or, if you have no ears, you become the thing that changes shape when sound passes.

So the mat would thicken into fans. The fans would learn the plateau’s language: the far crack that means ice is forming; the low boom that means it is breaking; the complex chord that means the edge-nets are being pulled by a surge; the particular, higher sheet-noise that means a rain-wall is marching.

Not thought as you’d recognize it. A training. A tuning.

And from there the rest follows almost inevitably, the way water here becomes weather without changing substance.

I would become one of the weavers.

Not a single body with a center, but a distributed craft that uses tension the way roots use moisture. Long tendrils—tough, rubbery, braided by necessity—would extend from the terrace into the corridor, seeking places where the current already wants to pull. The tendrils would find stone uprights (or make them, slowly, by stabilizing stacked basalt and cementing it with mineral-hoarded tissue), and between those uprights I would draw lines: cords weighted with smooth stones not as tools but as *answers* to movement.

The stones would be chosen the way a throat chooses a note.

A weight that makes a cord tap, not slap.  
A pair that clicks dully instead of ringing.  
A resonance that carries through kelp and granite and tells my other parts: the flow has shifted; the spillways are swelling; the rain-wall is not just fog today but architecture.

The “hut” would appear as an accidental confession. Overlapping blades, leathery shingles—not to keep anything dry, but to shear the current, to carve a pocket of slower water where silt can settle and chemistry can age. Inside that pocket I’d keep the glossy-handled hammer-stone not as a possession but as a repeated gesture: the place where my tendrils brace and press and abrade, shaping hardened plant-stem into wedges, scraping mineral crust from vent-stained rock, polishing grip-points into evidence.

If you came upon me then, you might still think “hands were here.” You’d be wrong in the familiar way and right in the deeper one. Something *worked* here with persistence. Something arranged conditions. Something built not against the water but with it.

And I would be easiest to find at the plateau’s edge, where the ocean spills away into darkness and the nets hum.

Because that’s where the drowned upland is most itself: the constant throat-sound of falling water transmitted through stone; the shifting opinion of “up” as side-currents tug toward the cataracts; the debris—fronds, ice pellets, mineral nodules—arriving as offerings from the rest of the plateau.

I’d string myself there between anchor stones and cliff-round steps, a living instrument that catches events rather than animals. When spillways surge, my net would vibrate in chords that travel back along my tendrils into the corridor, into the calm pockets, into the terrace-mats: information without eyes, warning without fear.

In the deepest part of me—down closer to the vent-gardens where warmth is “just shy of comfort”—the chemistry-eaters would live as my smallest, most decisive citizens. Biofilms that flash pallid for seconds when fresh mineral meets seawater. Not signals. Metabolic spillover. But I would learn to treat those brief glows the way other worlds treat sunrise: as a timing cue, a confirmation that the gradients are still sharp enough to feed the whole arrangement.

So, where would you find me?

In a saddle-corridor turned into a listening chamber;  
in a current-tight pocket behind a hut-frame of blades;  
most of all at the edge, where the nets hum and the drowned upland pours itself away.

And what would I be?

Not an animal.

A woven ecology pretending, for a moment, to be architecture: a distributed, slow-bodied arranger that reads the plateau by vibration, farms calm the way a field farms light, and makes the loudness legible—turning the world’s constant motion into patterns stable enough for life to keep its shape.
