You don’t arrive in the pocket as a body at first.

You arrive as attention—caught by the way the place refuses to be “background.” The pocket has too many edges, too many seams doing work. In a normal hollow, wind and water are the authors; here the authorship is shared with bolt threads and bent sheet, with surfaces that make their own microclimates and then enforce them. You stand above it and your mind starts trying to file it under *quarry*, *dump*, *foundation cut*, *collapse*, *washout*, but none of those labels hold because the cut faces still look like decisions that haven’t been argued with by time.

So at first you’d think you’d be a visitor. A misplaced hiker. A surveyor without a clipboard. Someone who has made an error that turns into a secret.

You pick a way down—testing gravel that wants to slide, using rebar as a handhold because it offers itself like a rung—and the pocket meets you with its compressed weather. The air is a little warmer than it should be. Not heat, not a gust—just a retained willingness to be warm. Your breath feels like it has less room. Sound has less room too. Each small noise is a deliberate thing.

Your eyes, trained by ordinary landscapes, keep reaching for an empty distance to rest on and finding none. Everything is near. Everything is in use. Even the “still” parts are only temporarily still.

If you’re honest about the question—*where would you find yourself in this world?*—the first answer is: you find yourself looking for the one creature that “belongs” to the pocket, because that’s how we’re taught to understand habitats. Find the emblem animal. Find the keystone. Find the thing whose removal would collapse the whole story into disorder.

But the pocket doesn’t give you an emblem. It gives you coordination.

It gives you the soft tick of a drip on metal, then—seconds later—the smallest scrabble beneath a slab, as if that tick was a bell rung in a neighboring room. It gives you ants moving with a kind of administrative certainty along a rust seam that is always damp at dawn. It gives you a lizard that appears exactly where the temperature gradient is kindest, then disappears before you can finish deciding you’ve seen it. It gives you a line of salt along concrete like the record of yesterday’s evaporation, and the salt looks less like residue than like writing.

You try to be the observer.

You crouch by a snapped-open conduit where cool shadow holds longer, and you watch the morning condensation gather—not as a general wetness, but as beads forming in the same places they formed yesterday and will form tomorrow, because the pipe remembers the night and the air obeys the memory. Bead, bead, bead. A drop swells until surface tension fails. It falls. Flat click. Then silence—not empty silence, but the held breath of a system between events.

Your mind offers you roles you could play here.

You could be one of the obvious tenants: a careful frog pressed into the seam where clay meets concrete, waiting for evening to reopen the world; a beetle wearing the pocket’s flour of mineral dust; a wasp that has accepted rust as architecture and iron as pigment. You could be the small furred thing under the tarp, surviving by schedule and concealment, learning the pocket’s reliable windows of water and warmth the way a city animal learns the garbage truck’s route.

Those roles are tempting because they have outlines. They have bodies. They move through space. They can be named.

But after a while in the pocket you start to feel how little the tenants “own.” They’re skilled renters. They’re excellent at reading the building. Yet the building is what makes them possible.

So you keep watching, and gradually you feel something subtler: that the pocket’s real inhabitant isn’t a creature among the debris, but a pattern *in* the debris. A habit of physics turned biological, a weather-cycle turned into a food-chain, a geometry turned into routes. A kind of metabolism that doesn’t live in a stomach or a lung but in the daily exchange between sun, metal, moisture, salt.

And then—because this is the pocket’s trick—you realize that this is where *you* fit.

Not as an animal that drinks, but as something that times the drinking.

Not as a predator, but as the predictable corridor that makes predation efficient.

Not as a nest-builder, but as the stable temperature pocket inside a control box that convinces a bird it can raise young here without losing them to cold.

At some point you stop thinking, *What lives here?* and start thinking, *What is thinking here?*

The answer begins on the cut face where pale clay meets dark grit.

You put your hand near the place where moisture seeps and disappears. The wall looks cleanly sliced, but it isn’t sterile. It’s already been glossed with a thin, living film—microbes that like minerals, organisms that are less like “plants” and more like processes wearing cellular skins. Iron-oxidizers turning sweat into richer orange. Salt-tolerant slicks that soften when dew arrives and crisp when noon takes it away. Fungal threads laid through dust like a faint felt, not rising into mushrooms because the pocket doesn’t reward that kind of extravagance, but persisting as an underground handwriting.

If you stay long enough to see the cycle repeat—morning sheen, midday burn-off, evening return—you begin to understand that those films are not incidental. They’re the pocket’s sensorium. Its interface with the world.

They respond first. They record first. They change the texture of surfaces in ways that affect what comes next: how water clings, how quickly rust thickens, where salt blooms, what smells are released when the sun warms old grease awake. A biofilm is a tiny alteration of friction and chemistry, but in a place made of routes and schedules, tiny alterations become governance.

So you edge closer to a particular relic: the old control box with its hanging door. It shouldn’t matter. It’s a metal shell with dead relays, a laughable remnant of intended order. Yet it’s full of second lives: cocoons on wires, a nest tucked behind a panel, droppings dried into pepper beneath a ledge. It holds a steadier temperature than the open air. It stays cooler at noon and warmer at night. It is, in other words, a reliable room.

And reliability, here, is power.

When you put your ear near it, you don’t hear a hum. You hear the subtle sound of things choosing it: tiny legs shifting, papery wings adjusting, the faint abrasion of an insect’s body against metal. The box is not just shelter. It is a decision point—a place where multiple lives intersect because the pocket has made it predictable.

That’s when your role clarifies—not all at once, but like a lens coming into focus while you keep insisting you’re only watching.

You are not the frog. You are the seam the frog trusts.

You are not the ant. You are the dew-ration system the ant harvests from.

You are not the snake in the conduit. You are the conduit’s dark, constant corridor—cool enough to be used, warm enough not to punish.

More precisely: you are the pocket’s *former intention* returning as a new kind of intention.

The industrial debris was built to carry flow—electricity, water, material, control. In its first life, the pipes and cables and boxes belonged to a planned system. Now they belong to an opportunistic ecology. But the shape of “systemness” didn’t disappear; it was simply reassigned. The pocket has become a control network without a controller: distributed, improvised, yet shockingly coherent.

If you have to choose a being to be, you would be the thing that bridges those two lives.

Not a ghost in a sentimental sense. Not a machine waking up. Something stranger and more fitting: a *distributed caretaker* made of biofilm, rust, salt, and schedule.

Your body is not centralized. It is the thin, living grease on pipe-shadow where dew gathers. It is the mineral-dusted spider silk anchored to bolt threads. It is the faint fungal veining under a slab, holding dust in a pattern that changes how moisture moves. It is the salt bloom that draws tiny prey, which draws the amphibians, which draws the snake, which draws the owl’s brief, noncommittal pass overhead.

You “think” in thresholds: warm enough for condensation; cool enough for holding; shaded enough to keep joints from drying; rough enough to let eggs cling; smooth enough to let water bead and fall in predictable ticks.

You “speak” by altering surfaces. A little more hydrophilic here. A little more crust there. A slightly different texture at the lip of a pipe so a bead forms sooner, so a drip-point shifts two inches, so an ant route reorients, so a lizard’s hunting appointment changes.

If that sounds too grand—if it feels like assigning agency to physics—then this is the pocket’s lesson: agency here is not a crown worn by a single skull. It’s an emergent property of repetition plus consequence. The pocket repeats its cycles. The residents learn the cycles. The films and salts and rust adjust the cycles. The debris reconfigures with tiny collapses and burrows and nests. Feedback accrues. In a place this dense, feedback becomes personality.

So where would you find yourself?

You would find yourself *everywhere that turns weather into timetable*.

In the cool underside of a bowed sheet where morning always gathers first.

In the seam where clay meets concrete, damp enough to keep a thin life from cracking, dry enough to keep it from washing away.

On the inside wall of a broken conduit where the air is steady and the scent trails persist.

Behind the control box’s open door where a nest can depend on the metal’s night warmth.

Along the salt band in the cut face—shining when someone moves their head—not because it wants to be seen, but because it is an honest record of water’s comings and goings.

Who or what would you be?

You would be the pocket’s repurposed “administration”: not a ruler, not a god, not a single animal, but the quietly coercive infrastructure of habit. A living schedule. A soft intelligence made from mineral-loving microbes and the physics of metal breathing.

A reef built out of scrap, and the reef’s nervous system: the thin, persistent layer that remembers where dew should form, and persuades the rest of the pocket to keep remembering too.

If someone left and returned a week later and found the layout slightly changed, you would be the reason the change still made sense.

Not the architect.

The continuity. The rule that the pocket is obeying without agreeing to—because you are the rule in its gentlest form: a film, a trace, a preference, repeated until it becomes a world.
