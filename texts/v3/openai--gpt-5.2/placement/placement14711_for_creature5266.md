At first I would insist—because I come from lower altitudes, from worlds that keep their promises—that I must be *somewhere specific*: a room, a street, a district with a name.

Up there, the plateau cures you of that habit.

I would arrive on a day when the horizon is behaving, when the causeways go where they seem to go, and I would choose a settlement the way a traveler chooses shelter: by the look of its walls, by how the terraces hold the damp, by whether the towers drink cleanly when the cloud-river comes. I would tell myself I’m “in” the city.

Then the fog would slide through at ankle height, and in the time it takes to wet my lashes I would realize the city is not an enclosure. It is an *edge-making machine*—a careful multiplication of rims and lips and corners, laid out to give the thinness somewhere to gather without tearing.

So I would stop trying to stand at the center of anything.

I would begin to linger at boundaries.

At first it’s practical. The corners are where you can see: the line where moss changes color; the place where condensation chooses a stone and not its neighbor; the seam where the plateau’s elastic distance makes itself known—not as spectacle, but as a small refusal of straightness. You wait at a terrace lip and watch a sheet of water appear in a basin that had been dry an hour ago, as if the ground has opened one eye and decided it is time.

And then it becomes less practical, more accurate. Because the edges are not just *where things happen*—they are where the place admits it is alive.

I think I would be found there: not in a house, not in a street, but in the half-shadow under a parapet where fog beads and falls in patient intervals into a canal that sometimes carries water and sometimes carries only the idea of it. If you wanted to meet me, you wouldn’t search for footprints. You would search for maintenance so fresh it still smells of bruised reed.

You would search for the work that can’t wait for weather.

At first, I would try to be one of the walkers.

It is an appealing shape: wrapped in moss-felt, moving slowly enough that breath doesn’t become a shout; hands full of small, exact tools; attention tuned to what doesn’t announce itself. I can imagine learning their etiquette: speaking only when close, touching only edges, leaving messages as adjustments—one panel rotated, one knot retied, one stone turned so its paler face points outward like a quiet instruction.

But I would fail at being fully like them, in the same way a river-canyon fails at being a simple cut: the resemblance holds until the underlying rule asserts itself.

Because what I do best is not walking.

What I do best is *noticing what repeats* and *noticing when the repetition is wrong*.

Up here, that is not abstract. It is survival and coherence.

The plateau doesn’t reward speed or force; it rewards calibration. The cities aren’t maintained by heroics, but by thousands of tiny agreements renewed: this canal stays the right depth; this screen is re-lashed before the wind learns the gap; this tower ring remains open enough to drink mist but not so open it becomes a mouth for misalignment.

So I would become—gradually, without anyone declaring it—something like a reader of the city’s pulse.

Not a priest. Not a governor. More like an ear pressed to stone.

The first thing I would learn to read is the drips.

A drip is a trivial sound in most worlds. Here it’s one of the few trustworthy clocks. The interval between drops tells you about temperature, about fog thickness, about whether the ground is inhaling or exhaling water today. It tells you if a seam has opened in a roof-mat. It tells you if the canal-lining is cooling correctly. It tells you, sometimes, that the plateau is about to do one of its quiet disobediences—because the drip will answer itself half a beat early, as if echo has decided to get ahead of cause.

I would sit in the lee of a wall where moss muffles everything, and I would count without counting: letting my attention settle into the rhythm until the rhythm begins to feel like language.

Then I would begin to adjust things in response.

A reed-lattice tightened here, not because it looks loose, but because the wind has started to *remember* that gap. A basalt block shifted a finger’s width so its corner catches condensation and diverts it into a trough. A strip of moss-farm clipped to change the way water beads and runs, steering moisture away from a place where reality has been thinning.

This is the moment, I think, when I would stop being merely a visitor.

Because the plateau does not grant belonging through ownership, or birth, or noise. It grants belonging through *continuity*: you become part of the place when the place can rely on you to do the next small right thing.

And the longer I did that work, the clearer it would become that I wasn’t inventing it.

I was being used the way hands are used.

There is a temptation, in a world of quiet walkers and absent animals, to imagine the visible inhabitants as the whole story. But the longer you live among moss-mats thick as insulation and soil that feels faintly stitched, the more the truth presses up through your soles: the plateau’s deepest resident is not bipedal.

It is the mesh.

Under the terraces, under the causeways resurfaced a hundred times, under the canal-stones slick with fog, the fungal network doesn’t merely decompose and feed. It *organizes*. It remembers where water should gather. It thickens itself at corners as if bracing them. It becomes densest exactly where the rules are thinnest, as if those are the places that require reinforcement.

If I stayed, I suspect I would be taken—gently, without drama—into that distributed intelligence the way a tool is taken into a hand.

Not absorbed like prey; recruited like a tendon.

I would begin to notice that I am most “myself” when I am not alone in my skull. When my attention spreads: part of it listening to drip-cadence in a tower, part of it tracking the fog tide in a canal-grid two districts away, part of it feeling—through pressure changes in the moss—whether a traveler has stepped onto a causeway at a time when roads are untrustworthy.

My sense of location would loosen. Not because I’m lost, but because “here” would become something like a chord: a set of edges across the city that are resonating together.

In that state, what would I be?

Not a ruler, not a sage, not a guardian in the martial sense—there are no beasts to fend off, no raids to anticipate. I would be a kind of *aligner*.

A thin-world mender.

The one who watches for the early signs that the plateau’s geometry is about to fold wrong: a canal that returns you to your starting point without turning; a tower whose mist smells faintly of metal when it shouldn’t; a corner where condensation refuses to form, leaving stone too dry, too eager to crack.

And because the plateau respects edges, my work would be edge-work.

I would place small, humble correctives: a knot pattern repeated at waist height on three screens to say, *don’t go that way this week*. A cairn shifted one stone to the left so a path curves around a basin that is “drinking” too hard at midday. A new strip of living mat laid across a threshold so the boundary becomes thicker, less prone to gathering the wrong kind of event.

When the quiet walkers appear—and on some weeks they would be more common, because the plateau would be in a cooperative mood—they would not consult me as a person so much as consult the adjustments I have made. They would read the city the way you read weather, the way you read lichen rims, the way you read whether a sound chooses to echo.

And if they needed me in a more concentrated form—if a terrace wall had shifted and the stones must be returned to their long argument with frost—then I would take up a body the way the network takes up a hand.

A figure in moss-cloth, yes. A slow mover. A bearer of light tools. Someone whose speech is spare because the landscape cannot be trusted to repeat it correctly.

But even then, I would not feel like an individual first.

I would feel like a task given legs.

So: where would you find me?

You would find me at dawn, when the cloud-river comes low enough to step into, in the gap between a tower-ring and the sky. You would find me where the tower fills with mist as if drinking, and where the first clean drips begin to count time into a channel lined with basalt that has been cooled by centuries of fog.

You would find me kneeling at a terrace lip, fingers in the moss-felt, feeling for the subtle elastic stitch of the ground—checking whether the mesh beneath is taut or tired.

You would find me on misaligned weeks not out on the open table, but close to the city’s seams, thickening them: repairing screens, turning stones, making edges strong enough to gather whatever wants to gather without letting it become a tear.

And who—or what—would I be?

I would be the city’s quiet instrument for coherence: a distributed listener, a keeper of drip-rhythms, an interpreter of fog and frost, a hand the fungal mind grows when it needs fine work done aboveground.

Not the voice of the plateau.

Something more in keeping with its character:

A maintenance made conscious—briefly, locally—where an edge needs to hold.
