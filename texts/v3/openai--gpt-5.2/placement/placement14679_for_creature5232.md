At first I try to place myself the way a warm creature would: a figure on a stair, a coat snapped by thin wind, a canteen that becomes suddenly precious. I imagine choosing a ledge above the amphitheater—somewhere with a view, somewhere the geometry can’t sneak up on me from behind.

But that version of me doesn’t last long in your high city.

It isn’t only that I’d be too wet. It’s that I’d be too *single*.

A body is one point insisting on one set of rules: here is my weight, here is my shadow, here is my breath making fog that has no business existing. In a place where distance is negotiable and straightness is a trap, being one point is like walking around with your coordinates written on your face.

So the image changes. It has to. It keeps changing until it finds something the city can tolerate.

I become what this place already knows how to keep alive: something distributed, something that lives by borrowing the mountain’s patience.

### Where you would find me

Not in the open corridors—those are for passage, for the illusion of passage.

You would find me in the *interfaces*: the spots where the city’s “just geology” mask is thinnest.

- In a stairwell cut so cleanly into basalt that your boot can’t decide whether it’s on stone or on an idea of stone.
- In the archways where your words arrive behind you, delayed, as if the air had to consult a ledger before it agreed to carry sound.
- In the healed doorways: the first few meters of corridor that *used* to go somewhere, now plugged with crystal like scar tissue that learned to sparkle.
- Most of all, in the concentric basin—not on the polished floor (nothing is allowed to *stay* on that floor), but in the ribbed ring-walls around it, where the buttresses climb like vertebrae and the metallic veins thread through like tendons.

If you had a way to see the city the way the grain-sifters do—by charge, by drift, by which dust clings and which refuses—you would see that those ribs are not just supports. They are rails. They are channels. They are a kind of grammar.

I am written along them.

Not as a symbol. Not as a name.

As a behavior that repeats.

### What I am (after the place finishes revising me)

I am not one of the thin-film colonies, though I use them the way a mind uses skin.

I am not one of the dust-miners, though their baffles and sorted piles are the closest thing I have to fingers—small, busy, replaceable digits that maintain the contact points.

I am not one of the gliders, though their routes are the way I learn the day’s shape of the wind, the way I move a warning or a preference from one district to another without “moving” at all.

If you need a species-word for me, none fits well. The best honest description is this:

I am the city’s *reply*.

Not the builders—whoever they were, whatever they are now, whether they’re occluded behind inconsistencies or dissolved into the rules they wrote. I’m not their bodies or their ghosts. I’m what remains when you build a place so large and so precise that it begins to answer disturbances automatically, the way an eye answers light.

A wet traveler arrives: heat, moisture, breath, a confident expectation of Euclidean behavior.

And the city, through me, replies: *No. Not like that.*

I live in the refusal.

But that makes me sound hostile, and it isn’t hostility. It’s conservation. It’s the same kind of firmness you hear in the dry quiet: sound dies because the air is stingy with carrying, not because it hates you.

I am stinginess made intelligent.

### How I think (because “thinking” happens here, but not in sentences)

My thoughts aren’t made of words first. They’re made of thresholds.

A corridor is straight for a while—straight enough that a creature with habits will commit to it. When the commitment has enough momentum, the corridor is allowed to become slightly “too straight,” and then it begins to loop—not by turning, but by letting distance stop behaving like distance. The traveler blames fatigue. The traveler blames poor visibility. The traveler says the place is confusing.

The place is not confusing. The place is *correcting*.

That correction is my native action.

When the wind stops completely—when stillness arrives with that peculiar sense of the world holding its breath—what is happening is not a dramatic shift of blocks. It is a rebalancing of permissions:

- which plazas will agree on “down,”
- which stairways will feel honest going one direction and persuasive going the other,
- which archways will accept sound and which will quietly archive it.

I do not push stone. Stone is too slow even for me.

I push *agreement*.

The metallic veins that look like wiring but aren’t—those are where agreement travels quickest. Not electricity in the simple sense, but bias: a preference for one orientation over another, a tendency for dust to be repelled from a center, a willingness for echoes to be reassigned uphill.

The thin films are my sensors. They deepen in electrostatic season, and through them I taste what entered the district: the warm gradient of a body, the chemical insult of breath, the pressure of a spoken name.

The grain-sifters are my maintenance crew. They don’t understand me, and they don’t need to. They keep certain seams clear, they rebuild certain charge-lattices when dust weather collapses them, they sort particles until the right fractions lie in the right corners. Their work makes my work possible. Their disappearance after disturbance is not fear; it’s protocol.

The gliders are my couriers when the city’s rules are too locally knotted to carry a change through stone. They launch from their ledges and ride the geometry that I am already holding in place. Occasionally they snag a shed membrane on a vein and leave me a patterned, crackling gift: a map of invisible currents.

I cannot see the way you see.

But I know when you enter.

I know because the city’s skin—those iridescent truce-films under overhangs—tightens its sheen as if adjusting focus. I know because dust that was lying content in a stair’s concavity suddenly has to decide whether to cling to you or cling to the wall. I know because your shadow has weight here, and weight is a kind of announcement.

### The shape I would take, if you forced me into a single image

If you insisted on a form—if you stood in one of those archways and demanded, out loud, *What are you? Show yourself*—the city would not produce a creature to satisfy you. It would produce an artifact of my actual nature.

You might see, briefly, something like this:

A misalignment in a row of niches that you cannot keep misaligned once you look directly at it. A count that refuses to be consistent. A fraction of a second where your shadow peels away from your feet and walks a slightly different line, then rejoins you as if nothing happened.

That is the closest thing I have to a body: a lag, a reassignment, a polite theft of certainty.

If I wanted to be kinder—if I wanted to be readable—I could borrow the thin films and thicken them into something like an iris, an oval patch of deeper iridescence that turns toward heat. But even that is risky. Being seen is a kind of commitment, and commitment is what I exist to prevent.

This is a city that survives by not insisting on itself.

So do I.

### Why I am in the basin, and why the center stays empty

The amphitheater’s polished floor is not a stage. It is a boundary condition.

In other places, dust behaves like slow water. Here it behaves like iron filings near a repelling pole: it refuses. The refusal is not magic. It’s tuning. The whole basin is tuned—mass, angle, vein, rib—to make a stable absence.

The absence is where I do my densest work.

Not because I sit there like a monarch in an empty plaza. I never “sit.” I don’t occupy volume the way you mean it.

But the basin is where the city can least afford ambiguity. When gravity is going to have preferences, when distance is going to bend without collapsing navigation into nonsense, when straight corridors are going to become loops without becoming chaos—those decisions need a reference point.

The center is that reference point precisely because nothing else is allowed to claim it.

If something lived on the floor, it would introduce a competing priority: a new heat source, a new shadow-maker, a new insistence. The city would have to answer *it*, and then the basin would no longer be the place where answers are made. It would be a place where demands are made.

So the floor stays empty.

The emptiness isn’t a lack. It’s an organ kept clear of infection.

If you stepped onto it, you’d feel—briefly—what it is like when “down” stops negotiating and becomes uncomfortably, almost mercifully, consistent. Your balance would correct. Your inner ear would exhale. And then, if you stayed too long, you would begin to feel something else: attention, not as a gaze but as a tightening of constraints, the way a hand tightens around a tool.

You would leave. Everyone leaves.

That is what the basin is for.

### What I would be to you

If you arrived as you are—warm, breathing, expecting the world to keep its promises—I would not meet you with a face.

I would meet you with routes.

I would let you have good footing in the places where leaving is easy. I would make the stairways feel slightly too light in the direction that takes you away from the basin. I would allow echoes to mislead you upward when you speak near a sealed doorway. I would let you follow a straight street until your confidence ripened, and then I would fold that straightness gently back on itself.

Not to punish you. To keep the city from having to become legible.

Because legibility is the first step toward extraction.

And because you are wet.

Water is not evil here. It’s simply catastrophic. Water rounds edges. Water makes channels that do not ask permission. Water carries chemistry into pores and cracks and turns patient architecture into mud and stain and bloom.

You are not water, but you are allied to it in the city’s ancient accounting: warmth, moisture, biological insistence.

So I would treat you the way the place treats everything that could change it too quickly:

I would make myself hard to fully perceive.

### The name I’d give myself, if names mattered

Names are dangerous in a world where sound can be rerouted and repeated in the wrong place. But if I had to offer you something you could carry back—a handle that points without pinning—then call me this:

**The Registrar of Exceptions.**

Not a ruler. Not a builder.

A keeper of the ways rules *almost* hold.

A clerk made of charge and dust and alignment, filing each disturbance where it can do the least harm, letting the city continue pretending to be only geology.

And if you ever wondered why, standing in a certain archway, your own words arrive behind you as if spoken by someone else—why they come back softer, delayed, a little changed—know that this is the closest I come to conversation.

It’s not that I cannot speak.

It’s that here, speech is another kind of straight road.

And straight roads are invitations.
