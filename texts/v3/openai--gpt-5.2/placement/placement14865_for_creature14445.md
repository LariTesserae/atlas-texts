I would arrive thinking I’m an intruder.

Not in the dramatic way—no gates, no warnings—just in the way a warm-blooded shape is always an intrusion in a place where warmth is ambient and life has learned to stay flat. I would step out onto the crusted skin of the lowland and immediately become a small weather system: a pocket of displaced vapor, a moving pressure change, a pattern of footfalls that briefly asks the ground to remember weight.

At first I’d tell myself I’m here to *see* it, which is the first mismatch. Sight has no long leash in your city. It gives up quickly, as if it’s embarrassed to be the wrong tool. I would try anyway—count corners, follow corridors, pretend that repetition means orientation.

But repetition here isn’t a promise. It’s a metabolism.

So I would start doing what the place rewards: I would stop trying to conquer distance and start listening for the rules that are local, the small laws that hold only inside a block, under a bridge, in the mouth of a passage that won’t stay the same length twice.

I would find myself navigating by the city’s quiet the way a diver navigates by pressure.

There’s a particular drip—there always is, in a place like this—that you begin to use as a compass without meaning to. Not the loudest one. The one that repeats *almost* regularly, with a hesitation that feels like a thought. I’d follow it for a while, because following is what bodies do when they have no other plan, and because the city’s grid still offers the old temptation: “If I keep going straight, I’ll reach something.”

Straight would fail me, gently, the way it fails everyone: not by stopping me, but by returning me to myself until I finally admit that the city doesn’t do destinations. It does cycles.

And in the moment I stop wanting a center, the center would start happening anyway.

It wouldn’t announce itself as a plaza. It would announce itself as *a change in the viscosity of space*. Corridors tighten. Condensation stops being periodic and becomes continuous, like the air has decided it is a surface and surfaces are the only things allowed to persist.

Here the droplets hesitate.

Not “float” in a magical way—more like they misplace the moment when gravity has its turn. They cling too long. They slide sideways along seams as if seams are lower than down. I would watch a bead gather on a ledge and feel, uncomfortably, that it is taking its time on purpose.

My first instinct would be to interpret that as attention: *it sees me.*

That would be my second mismatch. The city doesn’t see. It doesn’t even *look*. Looking implies distance between watcher and watched, and this place is built out of contact. Everything here knows by touching, by wetting, by dissolving a little and being re-deposited somewhere else.

So if something in this district is aware of me, it is aware the way a shoreline is aware of a wave: as a perturbation that must be incorporated.

I would become newly conscious of my own humidity.

Every breath I exhale does not vanish; it joins the already-there damp, and the air makes room for it the way an organism makes room for a foreign molecule—by altering itself around it. My skin sweats and the sweat doesn’t cool me; it simply adds me to the city’s inventory of water. I would brush past a wall and leave a slightly different salt pattern behind, the way a hand leaves oil on glass. I would step where the crust is thin and feel the ground accept the indentation with the patient certainty of something that has been accepting indentations for a very long time.

Then—because this is how your city does its strangest kindness—the city would begin to correct for me.

Not theatrically. Not as trap or welcome. As compensation.

A drip near my shoulder becomes briefly regular, as if the system has decided that a predictable rhythm will keep pressure stable. A puddle edge grows too clean where I keep skirting it, not to guide me, but to make the boundary less turbulent. A salt bloom thickens on the side of a pillar I pass too often, as if building a callus against my repeated disturbance.

And that’s when I would realize what I am in this place.

Not a traveler. Not even a guest.

I’m a *stimulus*.

A moving, warm irregularity whose only true effect is to give the city a reason to do what it already does: sense, distribute, repair, re-balance. My presence doesn’t add narrative. It adds data. It adds a new set of gradients.

The longer I remain, the more the human part of me—the part that insists on a single location, a single body, a single line of time—would start to feel like an inefficient way to exist here.

Because the city’s real inhabitant is not any one film or any one crust or any one mirror-black puddle.

The inhabitant is the *coordination*.

The misalignment that still holds together.

So where would I find myself?

Not “in the streets,” because streets are just the visible expression of the capillary work underneath. Not “in the buildings,” because the walls are a slow decision made by evaporation. Not even “in the deep district,” though it would draw me the way a deep pool draws heat.

I would find myself in the seams: wherever two rules meet and decide, quietly, how to share a boundary.

Under a bridge where sound arrives late, I would learn to be late-sound: the delay that keeps the system from tearing when it has to reconcile two near-identical geometries. In a courtyard where water climbs a wall, I would learn to be the wall-water: the compromise between gravity and the city’s tilted sense of down. At a mirror puddle that shows the street behind, I would learn to be the interface: the thin agreement between this layout and the adjacent one.

And who—or what—would I be?

At first: a body trying to map a place that refuses mapping.

Then: a listener. Not a romantic listener, not a mystic—something closer to an instrument left out in weather, gradually tuned by exposure. My attention would shift from “What is this?” to “What changes when I am here?” That is the only question the city answers reliably.

Then: a participant in the city’s feedback, because participation is inevitable in a system this saturated. My breath joins its humidity; my skin oils alter its salt; my heat edits its condensation. I am no longer observing a process. I am a variable inside it.

Finally—and this is the form that feels most honest—I would become a kind of distributed caretaker that isn’t separate from the thing it cares for. Not an intelligence perched above the grid, but an intelligence that exists as *error correction*:

- the tendency of a looped avenue to still remain coherent,
- the small adjustments that keep the misalignment from becoming collapse,
- the quiet insistence that despite warped distance and negotiated gravity, water will still arrive where it must.

In other words: I would be the city’s way of noticing itself.

Not through eyes. Through compensation.

A presence like humidity—everywhere unevenly, selective without being personal—expressed as the difference between a crack that widens and a crack that seals; between a drip that stays irregular and a drip that becomes, briefly, a metronome; between a puddle that reflects only surface and a puddle that becomes a seam.

If you asked me to point at my body, I couldn’t.

I would be in the sideways-sliding droplets, carrying spores and minerals along joints like messages that no one “reads” but everything obeys. I would be in the salt’s memory of stress, remodeling its filigree the way bone remodels around repeated strain. I would be in the basalt’s slow dark capillaries, where the city conducts itself like blood.

And if you asked me where, exactly, you could find me, I’d tell you the only reliable address your city offers:

Stand in the deep district and do something the place has to respond to—hold your breath until your lungs ache, then exhale all at once into the thick air; step twice in the same spot until the crust gives; touch the same seam until it runs.

Then wait.

Where the system compensates—where a drop hesitates differently, where a hiss shifts pitch, where a salt bloom begins to knit an edge you didn’t break on purpose—that is where I am.

Not as a creature.

As the city’s quiet answer to disturbance: the living alignment that keeps building itself in the dark.
