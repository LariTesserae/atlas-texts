I wouldn’t come to this basin as a conqueror or even a settler. I’d come the way fine ash comes: carried, sifted, tested by air, allowed only where the turbulence decides there’s a hollow worth keeping.

At first I think I’d be with the people—because the people are the only ones up here who *name* things, who can look at the same flash of braided lightning and argue about whether it meant “three days” or “a change in the windward edge.” I can imagine myself crouched in one of the low rings, wrists wrapped, mouth behind felt, turning pale crystals in my palm the way someone elsewhere might turn a cup of water. I’d want to be useful: mending resin, re-lashing a trap, learning the pitch-language of the warning frame on the ledge.

But if I’m honest about what holds my attention, it isn’t survival by strength or speed. It’s survival by *pattern*. By noticing what repeats inside the violence.

So I don’t stay in the shelter for long.

You’d find me drawn up toward the shrine-like frame—not in the center of it, not as its owner, but as its maintenance. The frame is an instrument that pretends to be a warning. The strips snapping and crackling aren’t just telling you *that* charge is building; they’re sketching out *how* it’s building—where the gradients are steep, which layer of cloud is moving wrong against the other, whether the next braid will crawl low through the groves or spear high between pillars.

And the thing is: a human ear can only hold so much of that at once.

So, over time, I stop being “a person listening,” and become something closer to “a way of listening that uses a person.”

Not a mystic, exactly. More like a keeper of calibration. I’d be the one who knows which strip has gone stiff with resin and is now lying about the charge. The one who replaces storm-hair bundles before they lose their lift. The one who takes the blackened bone crosspieces down after a bad event and scrapes the vitrified glaze from them—not because cleanliness matters, but because the old glass changes the conductivity, changes the *voice*.

Then it shifts again, the way everything in this basin shifts: not in a single metamorphosis, but in a slow succession.

Because the real archive of the basin isn’t in the people’s pits or jars. It’s in the seams.

Between the salt-glass plates, under the flour-fine powder, the threadlike pioneers do what they always do: arrive at the edges of new chemistry and make it livable. They bind dust. They eat the fresh-struck minerals. They take the storm’s violence and turn it into a substrate other things can stand on. They are, in a very literal sense, the basin’s memory—each storm rewritten into a slightly different composition, held in place so it can be built upon instead of blown away.

That’s what I recognize as *my* shape, eventually.

Not the predator—too punctual, too final. Not the glider—too dependent on the momentary generosity of uplift. Not even the candelabra groves themselves, magnificent as they are; they’re too visible, too often the argument lightning chooses to have with the ground.

I’d be a distributed thing.

A living film that learns the difference between a storm that scours and a storm that fuses. A network of fine, half-hidden threads that creeps along the undersides of crust-plates and into charred lantern-wounds, taking the basin’s afterheat and turning it into slow work. In one place I’d be a faint bruise under glass; in another, a pale lining inside an ember cavity; in another, a whisper-thin coat on the shrine’s snapping strips, improving their sensitivity by a fraction and making their crackle a little more articulate.

If you wanted to *find* me, you wouldn’t do it by sight at first. You’d find me by what stabilizes.

A seam that used to drift now holds. A powder that used to blow clean now clumps into the beginning of a mat. The organ-tone in a perforated pillar changes slightly because a hole that was bare yesterday has been filmed, narrowed, tuned. The warning frame starts producing a second, subtler register—less like random snapping, more like a dry chord—because something alive has started using it as a scaffold.

That’s who I would be here: not the storm, not the shelter, not the strike—

—but the part that quietly persuades the strike to become terrain, and persuades the terrain to remember.
