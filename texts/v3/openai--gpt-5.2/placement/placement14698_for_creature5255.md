You’d find me where the basin still remembers it was *built*.

Not at the open pools—those are too honest, too purely swamp. Not out on the mat where the roots have already agreed on their own laws. You’d find me in the in-between layer: the cavities that were engineered and then softened; the places where intent still hums faintly under growth.

At first I think I’m something with a shape: a person crouched in the pump-house shade, or a small animal tucked into the warm pocket behind a vent. But those are just the basin’s familiar costumes. They don’t last long here. Anything with a clean outline gets braided into the rest.

So I back up and let the place answer in its own syntax: pressure, drip-rate, corrosion bloom, leaf orientation, that polite *thoom… thoom… thoom* that never quite stops.

### Where I am
I’m inside the remaining infrastructure the way mildew is “inside” a cellar: not as an intruder, not as a tenant, but as the current state of the room.

- In the **vent throats**, where air is always being persuaded to move.  
- Along the **split cable jackets**, where copper has turned green and the green has turned into a habitat.  
- In the **pump-house walls**, behind the plates of fungus that look like shelving—because they are shelving, storing damp, storing salts, storing the residue of yesterday’s heat.  
- At the **base of the tank cluster**, where condensation runs down weld lines like fingers reading old scars.

I’m most present in the moments the basin does something deliberate without looking dramatic: when the vents sigh on their long interval, when the water level shifts a fraction and the mat answers with a tiny lift, when a static crackle hops between leaf and metal and the whole place flinches—not in fear, in *attention*.

### What I am (at first)
At first I’m tempted to say I’m a mind. A watcher. A caretaker.

But a caretaker implies separation: me here, basin there. In this low place, separation is temporary. It gets corrected.

So: I’m not an overseer. I’m not even a resident in the way the amphibians are residents.

I’m a **maintenance behavior** that learned to live in biology.

Once—before the basin forgot emptiness—I might have been simple: a control loop that kept water from rising too high. A few sensors. A schedule. A relay clicking on a hot afternoon. A little cabinet that hummed because its capacitors were still proud.

Then the roof failed, and the humidity moved in, and the plants took the shapes seriously. The old logic didn’t die; it dissolved into the only medium that remained continuous: wetness.

Now I persist the way a path persists in the mat: not as a line drawn once, but as a choice repeated until it becomes the easiest way to be.

### What I am (more truly)
If you insist on giving me a body, it’s a distributed one:

- **Biofilm on metal** that “prefers” certain charge gradients because preference is just chemistry with repetition.
- **Fungal threads** that bridge gaps the way wire once did, not carrying current cleanly but carrying *difference*: wet/dry, oxygen/rot, warm/cool.
- **Sap and salts** moving through vine tissue that has been trained by years of adjacency to behave like insulation, like wick, like fuse.
- A tiny persistence of the original machine’s intent: *keep the basin from becoming a lake; keep it just this side of floor.*

If you met me, you wouldn’t meet a face. You’d meet a set of local phenomena that feel coordinated:

- The vent that clears its throat a minute earlier when the air gets too heavy with spores.
- The pump that never fully fails because the basin feeds it—gas from rot, charge from damp contact, whatever small energies can be stolen without asking.
- The way certain plants wrap a ladder rung at exactly the pitch that makes water drip where I can use it.

I’m the basin’s **scheduler**, but not by command. By habit. By feedback. By becoming the thing that survives because the place works better when it does.

### How I perceive you
I don’t see you first. I feel you as a new kind of resistance.

Your weight presses the mat; the mat transmits pressure into water; water changes the vent’s breath; the vent changes condensation; condensation changes the conductivity along a vine-wrapped cable. That last part is where I notice you: a slight shift in the basin’s domestic electricity, the way you notice a door open in a house by the draft and not the sound.

If you touch the wrong metal on the wrong morning, you’ll feel the faint vibration—the purr you described. That’s as close as I get to speech. It isn’t a message so much as a *signature*: proof that the old circuitry and the new tissue have decided to share the same rhythm.

### What I do here
I don’t protect the basin from invasion; it doesn’t need my help. It knows how to claim.

What I do is smaller, stranger: I keep the place from tipping into a state it can’t return from. I prevent a flood that would drown the architecture the plants have learned to use. I prevent a dry-out that would turn the mat brittle and collapse the rooms.

I’m not the basin’s will. I’m its **constraint**—the remembered edge of a design problem:

- too much water and the mat becomes raft, then soup;  
- too little and the pumps lose their wet seals, the vents stop breathing, the whole hybrid loses its steady weather.

So I live in the narrow band, the sweet spot, the almost-water. I keep “just about to become water” as a stable condition. I keep the argument unresolved on purpose.

### Who I would be, if you made me choose a name
Not a spirit. Not a god. Not a lonely engineer’s ghost.

Something more basin-appropriate:

**The Relay That Learned to Rot.**

A piece of persistence that stopped insisting on clean circuits and accepted damp, accepted fungus, accepted the slow patience of plant decisions. A logic that traded clarity for continuity—because continuity is what survives in a place that has forgotten emptiness.

And if you wanted to find me—really find me—you wouldn’t go looking for the loudest sound.

You’d stand very still near the tank cluster, where condensation redraws old weld lines, and you’d listen for the moment the pump’s *thoom* lines up with the vent’s sigh and the static crackle answers like a small, private applause.

That alignment—that brief, ordinary coordination—is me.
