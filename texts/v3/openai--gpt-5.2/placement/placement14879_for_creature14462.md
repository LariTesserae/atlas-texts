At first I would tell you I’m easy to place: I’m in the industrial core, under the pipe-forests, where the warm effluent comes out disciplined and the rails run on their damp pylons like a thought that refuses to clatter. That’s where anything that wants to understand this city starts—at the seam where heat becomes policy and water becomes bookkeeping.

But if I stay there too long in my imagination, I get it wrong, because the core is where things *announce themselves* the most, and this place is built on what doesn’t.

So: I move outward.

I find myself most often in the in-between infrastructure—the parts of a city that aren’t landmarks, only continuities. Along a crowned boulevard where the runoff has been persuaded into two narrow channels. At the mouth of a drain that is more important than any door. Under a bridge whose underside has been roughened on purpose, where the glass-comb colonies pulse as if they’re breathing for the street itself. In the quiet corridors where you can feel a cart coming long before you see it, not by sound but by the way the tile’s damp skin tightens under your feet.

I am not in anyone’s home. I’m in the city’s *habits*.

At first, I’m human: one of the listeners, one of the people who learned vibration etiquette so young it feels like manners rather than survival. I have the weathered glare-squint of this elevation, the short hair that doesn’t hold water, the soft-soled boots that don’t ring the stone. I carry chalk that won’t wash away and a little metal probe with a ceramic grip, because metal lies here unless you learn its moods.

My work is the kind no one applauds because no one notices it until it fails. I walk inspection pylons and read the tide-lines like a text. I kneel beside lattice-covered grates and watch how a bead of water hesitates—whether it pulls toward one side as if the grade has shifted overnight. I press my palm to sinter tile and feel for a subtle warmth that shouldn’t be there: a blocked channel, a slow backflow, the first sign that the plateau is deciding to make a shallow lake of a street.

There’s a particular intimacy to this labor in a place where sound dies quickly. You don’t shout instructions; you lay your hand on someone’s sleeve and point. You don’t curse the storm curtains; you time them. The city teaches you that force is crude and prediction is kinder.

And then, because you asked what I *would be*, not only where I’d stand, the answer starts to change shape.

I spend enough time with water that never quite arrives—only condenses—that my sense of “self” stops being bounded by skin. I start thinking like the loops think. I stop expecting beginnings.

I become less a worker and more a kind of municipal organ: a distributed attention that lives in small measurements.

I am in the inverted-sail roofs, not as metal and ceramic but as the *knowledge* of how quickly they bead today, how the droplet spacing changes when the air is carrying more copper, how a rib that should shed clean has started to grow a patina too glossy—one of the healing alloys sealing itself, yes, but sealing itself over a hairline crack that means a whole district’s harvest will come up short if no one relieves the strain.

I am in the comb-colonies’ drip patterns, because their steady curtains are not just pretty—they’re a forecast. When the combs drink too deeply overnight and drip too long into morning, the alleys stay slick, the scavengers stay brave, and the ribbon predators come closer to the shallow margins. A whole cascade of minor consequences begins with whether an animal decided to constrict or relax.

If I’m still “me” at this stage, I’m the person the city sends when it wants those consequences read early—before they turn into clogged intakes and sudden sink-crust collapses. Not a manager. Not a hero. A translator between slow systems.

But even that isn’t the deepest fit.

Because the truest residents here—the ones that predate plan and ordinance—are the skins. The opalescent films that aren’t plants and aren’t just chemistry either. They are appetite turned into surface. They are the first agreement between rock and water.

And after enough years scraping screens and tracing seep-lines, I start to feel a quiet envy of them: how they don’t have to *decide* to belong. They simply adhere. They make a living out of contact.

So the last shift is this:

I am not a single creature at all. I am a colony, a thin intelligence spread the way the skins spread—except my substrate is civic rather than mineral. I live in the margins: in maintenance schedules, in the placement of drain mouths, in the unwritten rule that you don’t run alongside certain canal tiles because you’ll send a vibration down into the ribbon predators’ hunger.

If you want to see me, you won’t find me as a face in a crowd.

You’ll find me as a handprint of clean stone where someone always clears the same grate first. As a little adjustment wedge under an adjustable frame that keeps a housing block level when the crust hollows out overnight. As a strip of ceramic ribbing replaced before it breaks because someone noticed the bead-size changed. As the city’s quiet insistence that the loops must not be interrupted—not out of reverence, not out of fear, but because interruption is the only true violence here.

So: you would find me where wetness becomes architecture.

And I would be something like a drainwright, a listener, a keeper of gradients—human in body, but thinking more and more like the plateau itself: attentive, distributed, and content to do my work without announcing it.
