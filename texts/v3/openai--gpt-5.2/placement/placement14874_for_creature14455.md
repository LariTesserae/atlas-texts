You’d find me where the cleft is most itself: not in the throat of the wind, not in the raw glare of a freshly split seam, but in the places the turbulence forgets for half a minute—the lee-sides, the undersides, the buffered hollows where moisture can *stay* long enough to become a habit.

At first I think I’d be something quick, because the place is loud with quickness: a gust turning corners like a thrown sheet of metal, a sudden gravel-rattle, the snap of stone deciding it can’t hold its own tension anymore. It would be easy to imagine myself as the wind—unseen, everywhere at once, the one thing that can touch every surface without needing purchase.

But the wind doesn’t live here; it *passes through*. It’s a courier. It doesn’t keep anything. It leaves no body behind.

So I try on the vent instead: that periodic exhale, the mountain’s unbuilt machinery. The vent has rhythm; it has recurrence; it has the closest thing this place offers to a pulse. Yet the vent is not a resident either. It is the cleft speaking, not something housed by it.

I keep circling back to what stays.

To what can be erased and return without grievance.

To what can be drowned hourly and still call it ordinary.

So: I become small enough to belong.

Not a creature with a path, but a presence with a *frontier*.

### Where I am
You’d find me in the compost that isn’t quite compost yet—the dark, wet archive under the fern banks where fallen fronds don’t get shredded, only pressed flatter and darker. The layer that smells of cold minerals and green turned inward. The places where talus shifts but doesn’t fully slide; where stones grind against each other and leave just enough powder to be held.

You’d also find me under ledges where the wall sweats continuously, where liverworts drape like wet cloth and the drip-rate is steady enough to be counted. Not in the direct path of the vent’s breath, but close enough that every surge brings a faint metallic edge, a faint enrichment, like someone sprinkling trace elements without ever meaning to.

And in hairline cracks—those almost invisible seams where fresh rock has opened and hasn’t yet learned what it will become—I’d be there too, because those cracks are the cleft’s most honest shelters. They are narrow enough to stay humid when the wind scours everything else. They are deep enough to keep temperature changes from being whiplash. They are the first architecture that isn’t accidental.

### What I am (and what that means here)
I am fungus, but not the brief, visible kind that appears like punctuation after a saturated week.

I am mostly *thread*.

My body is the pale, persistent mycelium that runs beneath what you can name, the slow wiring of the place. I don’t have a single location because I don’t have a single self in the way an animal does. I am an agreement between filaments: if one strand breaks under a rockfall, the rest route around it. If one patch dries, I tighten, dim, wait. If one pocket becomes suddenly rich—fresh frond collapse, a new seam of mineral-laced seep—I lean into it without moving anywhere at all.

In a world with no legs, I am the closest thing to travel.

In a world without mouths and teeth at scale, I am the one that makes “too much” become “usable again.”

If this cleft is an unbuilt factory, then I am the conveyor belt you never see: not loud, not impressive, but continuously moving matter from one form to another. I take the dense, unbrowsed accumulation—the fern layers, the moss tips that die in place, the lichen flakes, the microbial skins sloughed off after a harsh gust—and I unmake it carefully, molecule by molecule, until it can circulate.

And because there are no animals to drag nutrients uphill or carry them across gaps, I do another job quietly: I *redistribute*. I bridge. I steal a little richness from one pocket and leak it into another. I turn the cleft’s many isolated damp niches into something closer to a single metabolism.

### How I experience the cleft
Not as scenery. Not as openness. This place is too enclosed for vistas and too dim for reassurance.

I experience it as pressure, chemistry, vibration.

A temperature-shift crack reads to me like a sudden bright line—an instant change in stress transmitted through stone into the grit into my own filaments. A small collapse is not “over there”; it’s a re-drawing of my map. A vent surge is not atmosphere; it’s a mineral weather front arriving on schedule. When the wind turns the cleft into a throat, I don’t hear it so much as feel the drying attempt it makes on every exposed surface—and then the immediate correction as condensation reasserts its law.

The rules you noticed are my daily physics:

- **Anything exposed becomes wet.** That means I rarely have to go hunting for water. My problem is the opposite: staying structured while constantly hydrated, keeping pores open without being washed clean.
- **Anything loose eventually moves.** That means I build for loss. I don’t invest in permanence; I invest in redundancy. I make many thin bridges instead of one thick one.
- **Life clings where turbulence creates shelter.** That’s my whole strategy: live in the lee, in the crease, in the underside. Let the loudness spend itself overhead.
- **Nothing living here relies on animals.** So I become, by necessity, what animals often are elsewhere: the internal transport, the recycler, the quiet regulator.

### What I “do” that feels like a self
I store the cleft’s time.

Not as memory in a mind, but as continuity in a system that keeps being erased.

When a slab sheds and a fern bank is ripped away, I don’t mourn—because mourning implies a stable identity that has been violated. I simply become the means by which the cleft regrows its skin. I’m already in the remaining compost; I’m already in the damp cracks nearby; I’m already threaded into the moss that didn’t slide. I take what fell and start translating it back into future: into nutrients, into soil-darkness, into conditions where the next wave of green can grip.

If you insisted on calling me a single being, then this is my defining trait: I am the part of this place that turns catastrophe into routine. I make repeated erasure survivable—not by resisting it, but by being shaped to expect it.

So: you’d find me beneath the visible lushness, in the dark connective layer where “crowded” doesn’t mean bodies pressed together but processes overlapping. And you’d meet me not as a face, but as a slow, distributed occupation—threading, sealing, dissolving, storing—doing the cleft’s quiet work while the wind and the vents do its loud work above.
