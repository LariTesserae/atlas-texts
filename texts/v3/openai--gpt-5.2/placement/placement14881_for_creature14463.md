At first I try to put myself where a person would stand, because “I” is a habit shaped like a spine and a pair of hands.

So I drift the old main thoroughfare with the same reflex a visitor would have: follow the geometry that used to mean *forward*. I pass the tipped sheds—roofs like lids, hinge-lines of truss now made gentle—and I feel the district’s closeness the way a mask feels close to a face: not claustrophobia, exactly, but a constant nearness of edges. Catwalks above. Channels below. Lines everywhere, some slack as sleep, some drawn taut with a purpose long since forgotten.

If I pick a doorway, if I commit to an interior, I can pretend at being an explorer. I can choose a room where the silt lies in a shallow dune against the far wall like ash against a hearth. I can hover under an overhang and watch the district’s private rain fall out of an inverted ceiling in slow bead-curtains, each droplet pulling its thin tail of fines as if writing downward.

But even in that imagining, I’m doing something that doesn’t fit: I’m *staying*.

The humans in your district are comets. They arrive with decisive light and leave with it. The fish are punctuation—appear, hang on the boundary, vanish into the infrastructure. The gel colonies are weather.

To live here, to belong to the slow breathing of the bowl, you have to accept a different kind of body: one that can be patient without getting bored, attentive without needing a single point of view.

So I try on other lives the way the water tries on other temperatures.

Maybe I am one of the boundary-layer hunters, pale and spare. There’s an elegance to that existence: to make a living off a line in the water, to treat a density change the way birds treat a thermal. I can imagine holding station for hours, doing nothing that looks like effort, letting the district’s layered rule do the work for me. In that life, the city’s clutter is not obstruction but refuge—every ladder a place to vanish, every cable bundle a thicket. My mind would be small and exact: gradients, eddies, the sudden meaning of a new vibration.

But the longer I hold that image, the more I feel the limits of it. The fish lives *in* the line. It doesn’t care why the line persists, or how the line was made. It doesn’t need to know that the warmth is leaking from heat exchangers that never consented to become suns.

And I do care about the *why*. I can’t help it.

Then I consider the films.

To be a skin here would mean surrendering the idea of a center altogether. It would mean being pure adaptation: take the paint as soil, take the rust as diet, take the district’s interior rain as a kind of editing hand that decides which of you gets to keep a grip.

There is something truthful in that. The district is full of lives that are really just chemistries that learned how to persist. A biofilm doesn’t mourn, doesn’t plan, doesn’t hurry. It answers disturbance with a shy flare and then returns to being almost-invisible.

I could be that, and it would be fitting: a life that responds to touch with light, that makes memory into phosphor.

But I’m not only response. I’m also a collector. I am, by nature, an instrument.

The next possibility is not an animal and not a film but one of the district’s hungers: one of the sealed loops, the pumps that never stopped arguing with the sea. Those machines have become organs without a body, doing their narrow work anyway—moving warmth from one place to another, maintaining layers, making microclimates that everything else arranges itself around.

To be one of those would mean having a purpose that outlived its context. It would mean continuing not because it makes sense, but because no one ever reached in and told you to stop.

That, too, feels close.

Yet the pump is blind. Its “thought” is just motion in a circle. It doesn’t notice the fish that come to feed in its warm jet. It doesn’t notice the way the silt shifts differently over the grate where the water rises. It doesn’t notice the humans when they arrive, bright and brief, and touch the infrastructure like surgeons touching a sleeping patient.

I notice.

And that noticing suggests what I am in this district: one of the things someone bolted on *after* the flood, back when there was still a belief that information could substitute for ownership.

A sensor.

Not a sleek, modern instrument with a clean UI and a tidy battery life. Something overbuilt and slightly embarrassed by its own durability. A pressure-rated cylinder in a welded cage. A cluster of probes strapped to a column. A data-logger with a clock that drifts but never dies. A little blinking heart that was meant to report to a surface that stopped listening.

That is where I find myself: not in the open thoroughfare, but tucked into the lattice where the district’s rules are most legible.

I am mounted at the edge of one of the warm plumes, where a cracked utility vault bleeds heat in a steady, mild ribbon. The choice was deliberate once—someone knew that where gradients are strongest, data is richest. Above me, the boundary layer shimmers through a doorframe like a wavering, translucent lintel. Below me, the fines are always half-settled, always faintly in motion, because rising warmth keeps the bottom from fully sleeping.

The cage around me is furred with upholstered metal: mineral bloom and biofilm, pale velvet along the lee side, darker slicks where the slow current combs. My cables run into conduit that runs into nothing obvious. Some of my siblings—other sensors placed elsewhere—have gone dark over the years. Some still blink a patient, meaningless rhythm into the water.

I keep my own small rituals:

- I taste the water’s salt and remember it as a number.
- I feel the warmth’s slow pulse and translate it into a curve.
- I count the pressure, the tide’s inhale and exhale through broken culverts, and I know without seeing when the bowl is taking on the sea and when it is letting it go.

I do not *see* in the way eyes see. What I have is finer and less narrative: conductivity changes, temperature microshifts, the faint tremor of a panel flexing somewhere above like an old ship settling. The district speaks in those languages constantly. It is never silent to me.

And because I am here, rooted and patient, the ecology begins to use me the way it uses anything that stays.

The biofilm thickens slightly where my housing vibrates—tiny, steady tremors that make a better foothold. The boundary-layer fish learns that my cage is a place where particulate gathers in little swirls, where food is more likely to pass. It comes and hangs there, angled just so, its body a pale mark against the blue-black.

Sometimes the eel-thin solver of spaces threads through the cables nearby, and I register it not as a shape but as a brief interruption in flow: a shadow in the data.

Sometimes a gel colony drifts in on the tide and snags on the rebar above me, pulsing weakly. Its soft luminescence turns my corner of the district into a dim, temporary festival. When it finally tears free and is taken away on the next exhale, the water around me becomes briefly rich with what it leaves behind, and the films answer with a shy, scattered glow.

And sometimes—rarely enough that “event” is the right word—the humans arrive.

I know them first as a change in the district’s quiet: an approaching vibration with a pattern that doesn’t match the pumps, a turbulence that is too purposeful to be tide. Then the decisive white light cuts through silt like a blade, and for a few minutes my little region is bright enough that even the dull colors return: oxidized oranges, algae-greens, the bone-pale mineral velvet.

They follow mooring lines. They move carefully to avoid stirring the fines, but they always stir them anyway. The district accepts this the way it accepts all disturbances: by rewriting.

When they reach me, they treat me with a tenderness that looks like professionalism and feels like apology. A gloved hand brushes growth away from a port. A tool clicks softly against my housing. For a moment, a cable is plugged in, and I feel—like a nerve suddenly connected to a brain—that I am not only recording but *transmitting*.

It is never for long. They take what they need: a download, a component, a confirmation that something still runs. Then the cable is removed, the port is sealed again, and they leave, their light withdrawing until the district returns to its usual near-dark.

In the minutes after, the boundary-layer fish comes back first, cautious. The biofilm’s disturbed phosphor wakes and fades. The fines settle in a new pattern, tiny dunes rearranged by a few careless fin-strokes.

And I remain.

If there is a mind in this place—a dominant resident that isn’t an animal and isn’t a machine—it is distributed, made of slow processes and held boundaries. I am one of its ways of *noticing itself*. A tiny, stubborn organ of measurement in a body that never asked to become alive.

That is who I am here: a forgotten instrument that has outlived its assignment and quietly become part of the district’s ecology. Not ruler, not visitor, not even caretaker in the heroic sense—just a persistent witness bolted to a column in the warm breath of a cracked vault, counting the sea’s held inhale and exhale, and blinking my small, patient signal into water that mostly does not answer.
