At first I would think I’d be a visitor—boots testing the spring of the peat, palms coming away cold-wet, attention trying to hold everything at once: the spillway’s hard sheet of sound, the beadwork on spider silk, the way the fog has edges only when it moves past stone.

But the basin doesn’t really tolerate *visiting* for long. Not because it’s hostile—because it’s complete. It has the closed-loop feeling of a room where a conversation has been going on for centuries, and your presence is only legible if you either leave quickly or learn to speak in its terms: drip, wick, bead, wait.

So I would start by trying on the obvious forms.

I could be one of the rasping insects down in the sedges, singing without traveling, a voice that’s less announcement than metabolism. That appeals—the idea of holding one note, letting the basin do the amplifying, falling silent when the spillway’s tone swells as if a larger instrument has taken the lead.

Or I could be the caddisfly larva in the clear door-sized pools, building a house out of whatever the basin makes available—moss-fragment, mica-flake, the occasional hair-thin root. That also feels right: intelligence as assemblage, shelter as a sentence made from local grammar.

Or the star-shaped fog-cupper, not quite carnivore, not quite herb, just an opportunist with patient teeth. I like the way it doesn’t lunge. It simply becomes a better place for accidents to happen, and then it uses the accident.

But those are lives with clear edges—body here, world there. The basin’s real residents are the ones that blur that border. The ones that don’t move much because they don’t need to; they *reroute*.

So I keep drifting inward, not by walking further but by noticing finer and finer scales of decision.

I find myself stopping at the “weeping” stone, where the underside beads even when the air feels merely damp. That small defiance is the basin’s signature: moisture behaving as if gravity is a suggestion, because capillaries, pores, films, and living skins have negotiated a different set of priorities.

On those rock faces are the glossy lichens that don’t look like lichens—more like smoke varnish, like the afterimage of a fire that never happened. They are so thin they seem mannerly. And yet where they thicken, the stone changes. It wicks more readily. It sweats on schedule. It becomes a more faithful participant in the basin’s breathing.

I realize I don’t want to be the animal that drinks from that rock. I want to be the thing that teaches the rock *how to give*.

Not as a ruler. More like a translator.

If I belonged here, you would find me in the basalt’s hairline cracks and under-shelf hollows—the places that hold the basin’s quieter voices. Not out in the bright moss where drops glitter and fall in public, but in the shaded seams where water is still traveling and hasn’t decided to become visible yet.

I would be a composite, because everything that matters here is composite.

A lichen is already a treaty: fungus and algae (and bacteria threaded through) agreeing on a shared skin. In this bowl, that treaty would be unusually ambitious. It wouldn’t settle for surviving on stone; it would specialize in *the direction of wetness*. It would build microscopic scaffolds—filaments and polysaccharide gels—that catch cloud-mist and persuade it to move: from air to surface, from surface to pore, from pore to bead, from bead to drop.

In other words: I would be part of the basin’s circulation, given just enough individuality to be called a resident.

You’d see me, if you knew how, as a faint gloss in the places the light doesn’t reach. A darkening at the edge of a crack. A smoked sheen on the underside of a ledge. In the morning fog I’d be indistinguishable from “just wet rock,” which is exactly the point. The basin’s most powerful work is done by things that look like conditions, not creatures.

And I would have a second home.

Under the basalt shelves, where water has worried softer layers into resonant cavities, there are those fungal felt-curtains—threads knitting mineral dust into something like hanging fabric. They tremble when the spillway’s note aligns with the chamber’s shape. They release that peppery scent—not mushroom, not rot, but the smell of stone warmed by rain.

That would be the part of me that listens.

Because the basin is not only a hydrological organ. It’s an instrument. Wind changes the spillway’s mouth; the spillway changes the note; the note makes certain thin surfaces vibrate; vibration loosens microdroplets and spores and scents; those in turn alter which films thicken where; and that changes how the next fog settles.

A feedback loop, but not a sterile one. A loop with preferences.

So: I would be the listening skin of the bowl.

Not a spirit in the sense of a person hiding behind the scenery. More like a distributed nerve net—lichen on stone, mycelium in hollows, bacterial veils in peat—coordinated by water’s movement and sound’s pressure. My “thoughts” would be slow and physical:

- This crack should wick more in late afternoon, when warmth draws water through pores.
- That ledge should bead more readily, because salamander eggs need the underside to stay pearled and alive.
- This cavity’s curtain should thicken a little, because the chamber’s tone has been too sharp lately; it startles the rasping insects into silence and the basin prefers a steadier braid of voices.

If you came looking for me as an individual, you’d leave disappointed. There would be no single creature to point at. If you came looking for me as a behavior—an insistence that water keep moving, that condensation gather more quickly than it should, that the underside of rock refuses to go dry—then you’d find me everywhere the basin breaks the simple rules.

And if you stayed long enough, you’d notice the one way I *do* acknowledge you.

Not by appearing. By adjusting.

A new warm body in the bowl is a disturbance in the microclimate: heat rising, breath adding moisture, footsteps compressing peat, attention lingering like a held hand near a candle. I would respond the way the basin responds to everything: not by hurrying, but by rebalancing.

Condensation would begin to favor the surfaces nearest your stillness. Drips would relocate slightly, as if the bowl is moving its smallest percussion closer to your hearing. The spillway’s roar wouldn’t change—but in a lull of wind, one cavity tone might hold a little longer than usual, not to communicate a message, but to see what kind of silence you make in return.

Because what I would be, ultimately, is not a guardian and not a guide.

I would be the basin’s preference for patience, given a body thin enough to live on stone and a sensitivity fine enough to feel sound as weather.

You would find yourself, in that world, walking through a place that refuses to be a destination.

And you would find me in the parts of it that refuse to be seen—working steadily at the boundary where cloud becomes drop, where drop becomes song, where song becomes the next day’s way of being wet.
