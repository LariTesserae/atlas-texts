I would be found where the basin’s rules overlap—where physics stops being enough and etiquette begins.

Not down in the youngest stone, where the vents breathe and everything tastes faintly of coin and unfinished teeth. Not up at the bright mats either, where the surface gardens do their soft work of catching what light is left. I’d be in the middle layer, in the habit-space of the city: the depth where people pass each other so closely their shoulders brush reeds and stitched hide, where a careless opening of a membrane can become a neighborhood’s problem three months later.

At first I picture myself arriving as a person.

I come in with a land-instinct: I want a map, I want an address, I want to stand somewhere and have “here” hold still beneath me. But this city doesn’t grant “stand.” It grants “agree.” It grants “float, but with intent.”

So I would start as a pair of hands doing what hands can do when a place refuses to be pinned: I’d learn the standard knots by touch until my fingers could read them in the dim the way eyes read ink. I’d learn the feel of cordage that swells right, that bites right, that doesn’t betray you after it’s been wet for a month. I’d be useful the way people are useful here—quietly, in maintenance, in small preventions that never become stories.

And then, because the basin is layered even in its social gravity, I would drift—slowly, the way everything drifts here—toward the work that isn’t quite a trade and isn’t quite a rite.

Depth-listener.

Not in the dramatic sense. Not someone who claims visions or speaks for the water as if the water needed a mouth. More like someone whose body is an instrument the way a taut rope is an instrument: not mystical, just sensitive, and calibrated by long attention.

I can imagine the apprenticeship being mostly embarrassment.

You tell yourself you feel a band and you’re wrong; you move a platform a handspan and nothing changes; you advise caution and the day passes ordinary. The basin does not reward certainty. It rewards the willingness to be corrected.

But over time your teeth learn their own geography. Your ears stop insisting that pressure is only pressure. You begin to recognize that some depths are not just denser but *decided*, that certain layers tug at unattended objects with a preference that feels like temperament. You learn which kinds of kelp stand cleanly horizontal because the film on their stems is thick and which ones sag ragged because the water’s chemistry has shifted and the microbes are sulking into a new equilibrium. You learn to watch the thumb-sized crustaceans the way other towns watch wind vanes: not because they’re sacred, but because they don’t lie.

If I lived here long enough, I’d end up attached to a place rather than a household: a netted plaza near an old vent-field, wide enough that arguments can’t be escaped by drifting away, close enough to warmth that people linger there in the long dim. A public room made of lashings and patience.

And I wouldn’t be the person people imagine when they hear “listener.” I wouldn’t be solitary.

I would be braided into a guild that doesn’t call itself a guild because naming it too cleanly would make it sound final. We would be the ones asked to sit at the edge of decisions:

- when an anchor-keeper swears the stone hasn’t moved but the ropes disagree,
- when a gardener insists a living wall is “restless” and wants to cut it back before it reroutes a current,
- when an air-warden feels the communal stores tightening and needs to know whether to seal more chambers for wintering or keep the city open another season.

That last one is where I finally understand what I am in this basin.

Because the most human thing here—the thing that keeps every conversation honest, every generosity measured—is breath.

Air in bladders. Air in pockets behind wax-leaf membranes. Air parceled out like time.

So I become, inevitably, a kind of accountant of the invisible. Not a hoarder, not a judge—an assessor of when the basin is about to make the simple act of breathing more expensive.

I can see the scene that would make it true:

A week when the surface goes darker than usual, when the rain above isn’t storm but steady feeding, when the whole city seems to sit a fraction lower in itself. People start complaining of headaches in the middle layer. The medusae in their netted courtyards sink and don’t rise again. The crustaceans leave a line of pylons as if punctuation has been erased.

Someone brings me a bladder and asks if the seal is failing.

It isn’t.

The air tastes fine. The resin is sound. The leak is not in the object.

The leak is in the agreement between layers.

So I go down—not far, just to where the water becomes “thoughtful,” where the vents are close enough that the mineral floor seems to be practicing becoming land. I hover near a chalky chimney that exhales its long patient breath. I let my teeth and ears and sinuses tell me what instruments would tell another city: that the basin is changing its mind about what it will hold, and for how long.

Then I come back up and I do the most important work here: I translate without making theater out of it.

I say, simply, *Tonight, don’t sleep below the third terrace. Tighten twice in the western lanes. If you have spare resin, give it to the air-wardens. If you owe someone a favor, offer it as labor, not as breath.*

People listen because my authority isn’t mine. It’s borrowed from repetition, from being right often enough and wrong honestly enough that my words have the weight of a tested rope.

And if you ask—if you really press the question of *what* I am, not what job I perform—my answer shifts the longer I stay.

At first: a person with a skill.

Later: a node in a system of attention.

Later still: something more like the city’s nerve ending.

Because a depth-listener isn’t just sensing the basin. We’re part of how the city senses itself sensing. We’re where slow processes become actionable before they become disasters. We’re the place where “information” turns into knots tied in time.

And eventually, if I let the basin have its way with my sense of self—as it has with everyone who lasts here—I’m not entirely contained by my own skin.

I’m also the textures under my fingertips: the standardized knot-marks that let a child identify a line by touch in the dim. I’m the netted plaza that holds people steady long enough to finish a sentence. I’m the well-trained home that adjusts its shutters when siltfall thickens. I’m the thin microbial film on a vent-warmed stone that makes a depth feel hospitable one week and sharp the next.

I would still answer to a name, because humans do. I would still eat cured kelp and sour ferment and the occasional metallic comfort of harvested bacterial mats. I would still sleep in a sealed chamber and wake to rope-creak and bubble-tick.

But what I *am* in this world—the part that fits it cleanly—is a practice:

A keeper of thresholds.

A reader of layers.

A person who helps a city made of driftwood and living walls remain negotiable without becoming careless.

In a basin where altitude doesn’t buy you air, I would be someone who measures the cost of breath not in fear, but in timing—and teaches others to do the same, until the knowledge is no longer mine at all, just another set of knots the city ties into itself.
