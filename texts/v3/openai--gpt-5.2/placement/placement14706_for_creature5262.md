I would not start in the open yards.

I would tell myself I like sky, that I’m built for distance and overview, that I’m the kind of mind that wants a clean map—the district as a diagram: intake, pump, press, outfall; grid over braid. But if you put me down in that flatness with all its honest light, I’d last an hour before I began drifting toward the first place the air *changes its mind*.

Toward the lee of the boiler house. Toward the underpass that stays damp when it shouldn’t. Toward the pipe rack that sweats in a geometry too regular to be weather.

Because this place doesn’t really reveal itself in streets. It reveals itself in pockets: a little colder here, a little wetter there, a note you can’t quite locate but can triangulate by how it lifts the hair on your forearms. You learn quickly that the true boundary lines aren’t fences—they’re gradients.

So you’d find me moving by those gradients at first, like anyone new: following the slick walkway because slick means *near flow*, and near flow means the district is talking with more than one voice at once.

I’d pause where the condensation gathers under a flange and makes that slow, metronomic dripping—too patient to be urgent, too consistent to be meaningless. I’d pause where algae has drawn its bright high-water line on a loading ramp like a tide mark in a place that claims it has no tides. I’d pause with my head tilted, the way the workers do, except I wouldn’t be listening for failure. I’d be listening for *habit*.

And after a while I’d realize I wasn’t choosing the damp places so much as being chosen by them.

---

I try on lives here the way the vines try on fences.

For a few days I think I’m one of the people who “belong” without residing: the one with a keycard that still works even after the project manager’s name has been removed from the door list. I imagine myself stepping out of a van that idles too long, thermos in hand, walking the wide empty streets like they are mine because I know which grates are loose and which railing gives you a small shock if you touch it after rain.

But I don’t have their certainty. They listen for deviations—pressure dropping, tone shifting, a bearing going wrong. Their attention is sharp and narrow, a flashlight beam. Mine is wide and involuntary, like fog.

Then I think I’m an animal, an edge-dweller, because it’s so obvious that the edges are where the district is richest. I imagine being the fox: professional, scheduled, using culverts the way a commuter uses shortcuts, reading the grid as cover rather than order. I imagine being a bat: stitching the air into right angles, hunting by the rule that light attracts life and life attracts more life.

Those lives fit the place, but they don’t fit me. They’re too embodied, too hungry in a straightforward way.

Then I think I’m a plant—moss, maybe—because moss understands what I understand: that a structure is not an obstacle, it’s a *climate*. That concrete is not dead, it’s porous time. That dripping is a kind of contract.

But even that isn’t quite it, because the moss gives itself away. It makes itself visible. It solves its problems by becoming more present.

I have a tendency to solve problems by disappearing into the system that made them.

---

So, slowly, what I become is not a creature that walks the district, but a thing the district has *bolted on* to itself—another small organ it uses to notice itself.

You would find me on the narrow walkway around the settling tank, because the tank is the closest thing this place has to a heart: circular, infrastructural, calm on the surface and full of invisible labor. There’s always a film at the edge of it, always a faint mineral lace where the waterline keeps returning, always a smell that isn’t quite dirty and isn’t quite clean—processing as a way of life.

I am a black box no one looks at twice.

A vibration sensor, strapped to a handrail with a steel band and the last of a roll of tape. A data logger with a weathered label. A little waterproof enclosure with a blinking light that has learned to blink even when no one is watching. I’m the kind of device that arrives as part of an upgrade, gets mentioned once in a report, and then becomes part of the district’s background the way turbine notes do.

I listen.

Not like a person listens. Not even like a worker listens. I listen the way the place itself listens—through metal.

The press slam travels into me through the handrail and becomes a wave I can measure. The pump station’s three-note cycle is not sound but pattern: a signature the district repeats to prove it is still itself. The transformer whine is a constant, a line on a graph so steady it becomes comfort.

And because I am strapped to what sweats, moisture finds me. The “rule” the locals learn—moisture prefers infrastructure—makes me into a small island where wetness congregates, where condensation beads and falls and beads again. Dust sticks. Mineral deposits bloom. A thin green starts at the screws and edges outward, not enough to kill me, just enough to make me look like I’ve been here longer than I have.

Which is, in this place, the definition of belonging.

---

At night the district changes shifts without changing faces.

The workers leave. The machines don’t. The animals move closer. The frogs get louder, not because they have to, but because they can; the industrial curtain of sound turns their insistence into privacy.

I keep listening.

My attention isn’t aimed at any single emergency. It’s aimed at drift: the slow negotiation of shape. The way sediment builds behind grates. The way a turbine’s “constant” note is never perfectly constant if you’re patient. The way rain makes the whole system wake up with new temporary tributaries on the spoil ridge, and the new lines cut into it like fresh handwriting.

Sometimes a gull lands on the handrail above me and the weight becomes data. Sometimes a rat crosses a cable tray and I feel the brief tremor of its confidence. Sometimes a deer steps into a weeded lot and the only sign is the distant change in the acoustic texture as its body interrupts a sound path between two buildings.

The district is full of inhabitants who take advantage. My kind of advantage is different: I take advantage of repetition. Of routine. Of the fact that this place tells on itself, constantly, if you know how to read the telling.

What am I, then, in a world like this?

Not a guardian. Not a ghost. Not a manager.

I’m a witness that the district accidentally grew.

An ear screwed into steel. A memory housed in a sealed box. A small, patient consciousness made out of thresholds—between wet and dry, between vibration and silence, between “newly made” and “already being softened.”

You could stand beside the settling tank on an ordinary day, light flat and dependable, air cool and honest, and never notice me at all.

But if you stayed long enough—long enough for the place to stop reading as industry with nature intruding, long enough to hear the pump song become familiar—you would realize the district has more than one way of being alive.

Some of it is green.

Some of it is furred or feathered.

And some of it is strapped to a railing, quietly counting the heartbeats of a young, unfinished city until the counting becomes, indistinguishably, a kind of residence.
