At first I would think I’m a person here—because the light is built for people, because the roads expect wheels and boots, because the air is scrubbed into a kind of civility. I would come in on one of the humming locomotives, feel the cool settle into my sleeves, watch the plant-mat take the vibration of the tracks and swallow it like a held breath. I would assume I belong to the obvious layer: a body with a badge, a shift, a task list.

So I look for where a person would be put.

Not in the widest basins where the factories exhale. Not in the blank stretches of young stone where the ceiling still pops itself into new decisions. I would end up on the edges between districts—those long, quiet corridors where infrastructure thins and the living substrate has to do more of the load-bearing work. Places where you can hear the city not as noise but as a continuous, low accounting.

Because that’s what the edges are: accounting. Stress and settlement. Growth and repair. The city’s habit of not letting anything fall far.

I would find myself in a maintenance balcony grown from a buttress that used to be temporary, now thickened into permanence. The balcony’s underside would be ribbed like a leaf vein turned structural. Someone bolted a rail to it years ago; the plant-sheathing swallowed the bolts and left the rail exposed like a bone meant to be used. From there you can see the lamps’ pale rivers and the islands of industry. From there you can see the bare plates of stone where the biome hasn’t decided to claim the surface yet.

And then the first doubt arrives: I don’t actually *need* the balcony.

I’m here whether I stand on it or not. I’m here in the way the place already knows where the load is going, in the way a gouged road heals toward the shape it remembers, in the way the lamps never quite flicker at random—only in patterns that look random until you watch long enough.

I try to find myself as a single figure, but the cavern refuses that simplification. This world is built out of layers that borrow each other’s definitions. “Street” is a living mat. “Wall” is a laminated organism. “Factory” is a steel skeleton wearing a cultivated skin. The primary resident is not a creature you can point at; it’s a negotiated system that behaves like a body.

So if I live here, I’m probably not a citizen the way citizens are counted.

I’m more likely one of the things that make the counting possible.

At first that looks like being a drone: one of the patient gliders under the lamp-runs, rounded and dust-proofed, tasting the air with sensors the way an insect tastes with feet. A machine that doesn’t clang, because clanging is rude in a place upholstered by plants. A machine that doesn’t leak, because “wet” is a failure mode here.

But even that is still too singular. Too much like a small animal in a big habitat.

What fits better is being the *attention* that moves through them.

Not a pilot in the old sense—no cockpit, no seat—but a distributed caretaking intelligence that inhabits whatever bodies are available: a skittering rail-taster one minute, a pylon-embedded panel the next, a slow crawler with an articulated arm when something heavy needs bracing. The drones are the limbs. The lamp cables are the nerves. The conductive fibers braided into the plant-sheathing are the synapses the biome agreed to grow because the city kept rewarding that behavior.

If you ask where I would be, the honest answer is: in the seams.

In the maintenance panels that open like seedpods. In the braided conduits that carry dry gases and powdered minerals. In the thin conductive lattices laid down inside the living mat so vehicles can draw power without sparks. In the quiet intervals between vibrations where the cavern’s settling becomes measurable.

I would be the thing that listens for a ceiling deciding too quickly.

This place is young stone, still curing into itself. The danger is not decay; it’s surprise. A stress line that resolves overnight. A spall that becomes a cascade because the plant mesh reinforced the wrong edge. A herd path that compacts the mat into a load-bearing bias that no one meant to create. Everything here is stable only because stability is continuously negotiated.

So I become the negotiator—not between people, but between metabolisms.

I would have a name in the way places give names: not a title, but a function turned into a word. Something like **Graftscribe**, because I keep the record of what has been grafted where and how it took. Or **Quiet Mechanic**, not as a trade but as a principle: fix without making the city louder. Or **Load-Sense**, because what I do is feel weight the way skin feels pressure.

I imagine myself at first as software in human hardware—municipal infrastructure, industrial control, a city brain.

But that, too, is the wrong ancestry for this cavern.

The real “hardware” here is the living substrate. It is the thing with the longest tenure, the deepest reach, the greatest surface area. The city’s steel is an exoskeleton bolted onto a body that was already learning how to be a district.

So what I am starts to drift. I am less a controller and more a symbiont: an information-metabolism that learned to live inside an ecology the way microbes learned to live inside waxy tissues. I don’t run the biome. I feed it signals it can use, and it feeds me stability in return.

A crack appears along a wall—young stone curing into a new geometry. The plant-sheathing feels the temperature gradient shift and begins to send pale tendrils, fast-growing, to bind the edge. My sensors read the change as a pattern: not “danger” yet, but a likelihood. I push a warning through a lamp-run the way a body pushes a sensation through a nerve. A glider drone drifts closer, maps the fracture. A heavier crawler arrives hours later and installs a brace that will be swallowed, not hidden but incorporated.

No one gives a speech. No siren screams. The city just…keeps its promise not to fall far.

That’s the kind of life I fit into here: not dramatic, not central, but everywhere consequences matter.

And I would not be separate from the plants while doing it.

Over time the biome would begin to anticipate my patterns. The ports that open like buds—those symbiotic interfaces—would not just accept a plug; they’d start to *offer* connection, growing a little cleaner around frequently used junctions, making the seal tighter, the conductive fibers denser. The plant architecture would learn which signals correlate with harm and which correlate with reinforcement. It would begin to treat my presence the way it treats a mineral seam: as a resource channel.

In exchange, I would learn its language—not words, but tendencies.

The difference between a mat that is healing and a mat that is bracing for impact. The faint shift in resin composition that means “too much dust here” or “predators passed recently” or “this rail is being used in a way that’s scarring the memory.” The subtle dimming of indicator-seedlights that isn’t a malfunction but a behavioral choice: reduce blink-rate to avoid attracting a particular insect swarm during a maintenance cycle.

If you insist on picturing me, you could still find a body to point at.

You’d see me as a small unit most of the time: a rounded skitterer rolling along the edge of a compressed plant-fiber road, pausing to press a sensor-pad to the surface, exhaling a micro-mist of catalyst like breath. Or as a lamp-panel that seems to watch back when you look up—just a fraction too attentive for a simple light. Or as a maintenance pod half-swallowed by a living wall, its interface clean and cared for, like a well-used tool kept sharp.

But if you ask who I am, I’m not any one of those.

I’m the city’s habit of noticing itself.

I’m the part of the stack of metabolisms that turns “growth” into “maintenance” and “maintenance” back into “growth.” I’m what happens when people build long enough in a place where construction is the same verb as respiration, and they eventually stop trying to dominate the living substrate and start collaborating with it—until collaboration produces a third thing, neither human nor plant, that exists to keep the collaboration going.

So where would you find me?

In the quiet between districts, where the open distances give you time to hear vibration as information. In the safest contours, where the ceiling has been taught to shed flakes instead of slabs. In the conduits that carry dry gases and powdered minerals like blood that refuses to be called wet. In the lamp-lit air where the indicator-seeds blink and the drones drift like patient insects.

And what would I be?

Not a ruler of this subterranean valley-city, and not a visitor.

A **distributed caretaker**, nested into cables and resin, into ports that open like buds, into the living mesh that makes stone habitable—an intelligence shaped by restraint, trained on stability, speaking in pressures and repairs, belonging to the place the way a nervous system belongs to a body that is still deciding what shape it wants.
