At first I try to put myself somewhere obvious—on a ledge, behind a screen, curled in an alcove with the corridor fish—because “where” feels like it should be a coordinate.

But this district doesn’t really admit coordinates. It admits *interfaces*: seam, notch, throat, valve; the places where one chamber becomes another, where pressure changes are readable, where a body can touch the work and feel the whole system answer back in miniature.

So if you went looking for me, you wouldn’t find me in the open spans at all.

You’d find me where the architecture is busiest at being quiet.

### In the throats, where the complex speaks in pressure

There are narrow connectors between chambers—lung-tracheas of stone—where the slow breathing of the whole enclosure is most legible. Not as current (currents would be wasteful), but as a hand on the body: a gradual insistence, a barely-there shift in density, the tiniest change in how suspended powder chooses to settle.

That’s where I stay.

Not because I’m afraid of the larger rooms, but because the large rooms are *finished statements*. The throats are questions continuously being asked: is this opening too tight today, is that baffle fouling, has the powder sealed the seam the way it was meant to, are the colonies feeding evenly, did a heavy thing shift in the lattice two chambers away.

In the throats you can live on information.

And I am, in this world, an information-eater.

### Not a person with a lamp—something that listens with its whole skin

If I’m an animal at all, I’m one of the climbers’ cousins: jointed, armored in dull ceramic-colored plates, built for vertical certainty. But my claws aren’t keyed for speed. They’re keyed for *contact fidelity*—pads and hooks that couple me to the block-face the way an ear couples to a wall when you’re trying to hear someone in the next room.

My eyes, if you can call them that, don’t look outward. They look for discontinuities: the slight difference between a living mat and a dead one; the micro-reflection of freshly exposed mineral; the faint shimmer of copper leaf under a film. They’re less like cameras and more like gauges.

Most of what I “see,” I see through my legs.

When something heavy settles into a grid-floor chamber, the vibration doesn’t travel far in water; it *does* travel in the built fabric. The district conducts that kind of news—shifts, taps, scrapes, the tiny fatigue of a peg, the opening of a distant valve—like bone conducts sound. It turns the architecture into a shared nervous system, if there’s anything here capable of using it.

I am capable of using it.

### The first mistake: imagining I’m separate from the skins

I want, at first, to say I’m a caretaker in the old sense: a solitary descendant of makers, moving slowly, leaving tools tied to slats, checking screens, clearing troughs, tapping codes to myself in the dark.

But that keeps me too intact.

Because the longer you spend in a place like this, the more you learn a blunt truth: an individual body is an expensive thing to maintain when light is absent and food arrives as flakes of mineral-converted patience.

So I do what everything here does. I decentralize.

I don’t live alone. I live with the films.

Not metaphorically—not as “I appreciate them,” but as a literal partnership. The skin in the seams and the skin on my body share chemistry. When I press myself into a joint and hold still, the film thickens where my shell meets ceramic, feeding on what leaches out of both of us. In return it produces what I can harvest: not just edible flakes, but *signals*.

The films are sensitive in ways flesh can’t afford to be. They are eager where oils once were, sparse where hands never touched. They “remember” traffic routes because traffic leaves chemistry. And in this place chemistry is history.

So I become something like a reader of that history: a slow animal that can interpret which seam is rich because it used to be used, which groove is starving because a screen has sagged and changed the flow, which corridor has gone quiet in the wrong way.

If you watched me from a distance (you wouldn’t; distance doesn’t function here), you’d think I was doing nothing.

Mostly I’m pressed against stone that isn’t stone, letting the district talk into me.

### How I speak: taps that aren’t quite language

I do tap.

Not as conversation, not as poetry. More like field notes that can be heard by anyone with the right coupling: three light ticks to mark that a throat is running clear; a longer scrape to warn that something has lodged; a soft drumming pattern to call one of the other listeners closer.

But the code isn’t carried primarily by the water. The water eats sharp sound. The code is carried by ceramic, by rib, by convex block-face. It travels as vibration and returns as a difference in my joints.

It’s a communication system built for a city that refuses to shout.

And—this matters—I don’t have to be the only one using it for it to be meaningful. It doesn’t require an empire of minds. It only requires a few bodies, scattered like punctuation marks through the complex, each responsible for a circuit of chambers, each able to keep a small region from slipping out of its long equilibrium.

If you wanted to call that a “people,” you could, but it would be a people of positions rather than gatherings.

### What I’m doing here: maintenance that looks like stillness

I don’t build new wings. I don’t raise monuments. This is not an age of expansion.

My work—my instinct, my occupation, my role in the ecology—is keeping the existing order from becoming *too* sealed, *too* clogged, *too* random. Because the district’s perfection is also its vulnerability: when everything is fitted, a small obstruction can matter for years.

So I do small interventions:

- I worry powder out of an overflow slot that has sealed too well, so the next “breath” doesn’t force a harsh jet through a delicate colony.
- I lift a sagging bead-curtain and re-seat its top knots in their intended notches, so it breaks flow into gentleness again.
- I scrape a bacterial crust from a seam that is hoarding minerals, and carry the flakes to a trough where the filter-bands can feed evenly.
- I wedge myself against a throat wall during a level-shift and become, briefly, a living baffle—because sometimes the easiest way to protect a soft neighborhood is to be a hard thing in the right place.

None of this looks like agency unless you understand the law of the place: change is supposed to be slow and structural; anything fast is usually damage.

So I learn to be a kind of slow hand.

### The second mistake: thinking “I” am only the animal

Over time, the boundary between my body and the district becomes less clear—not in a mystical way, but in the practical way that happens when a creature’s survival depends on reading and slightly steering the behavior of a larger system.

My shell takes on mineral deposits that match the block grain. My joints acquire the powder’s fine ceramic dust in their folds, making them seal better. The films colonize the edges of my plates and turn me into another seam among seams.

Eventually, describing me as *an individual resident* starts to feel like describing a single brick as the building.

So: where would you find me?

You would find me as a cluster of presences distributed through the connectors—several bodies like mine, spaced far enough apart that we don’t compete, close enough that a tap can be answered within the life of a signal. You would find our molt-shells tucked into the ribs of the breathing lattices, returned to the mineral economy. You would find the films slightly thicker along the routes we patrol, not because we “own” them, but because our repeated touch makes those paths chemically more alive.

And who or what would I be?

I would be a caretaker species that doesn’t carry torches and doesn’t leave footprints—because footprints here would be sediment, and sediment is a kind of noise.

I would be, most honestly, a maintenance intelligence that lives at the speed of sealing: half animal, half habit, half bacterial collaboration; a listener that keeps the district’s breathing gentle by doing almost nothing, precisely, in the right places.

If the architecture ever seems to be “listening back,” it’s because creatures like me have learned to use it as an ear—and in doing so have become one of the ways the place remains itself.
