I would arrive the way most things arrive here: not as an entrance, not as a splash, but as a change in what the water can afford to carry.

At first I’d think I’m somewhere else entirely—some clean underworld nave—because the mind looks for drama to anchor itself. The basin refuses. It offers only that boundary you described: stone becoming unperforated, the pocket’s water choosing to be a pocket. A limit you can’t see until you try to leave.

So I would drift, and keep drifting, and let the place tell me where I actually am.

Not at the surface. The surface is a lid; it doesn’t participate. Not at the floor either. The floor is storage: the slow archive of whatever the basin has been willing to let fall out of itself. The floor does not decide much; it receives.

I would find myself mid-wall, at that depth where light still has the decency to be called light but is already giving up its authority. Where green can exist, but only as annotation. Where the terraces stop looking like steps and start looking like a long, negotiated curve.

And then I would feel the real architecture: not the shelves, not the silt, but the gradients. The invisible ledges.

A thin tongue of seep-water sliding down the rock without turbulence. A laminar sheet you don’t notice until you’re close enough to sense the slight difference in density—the way your body (if you had a body) would feel it as a change in buoyancy, a different willingness of the water to move around you.

That is where the room is most itself: where two waters meet and refuse to fully become one another.

So, for a while, I would try on being the room.

It’s an easy fantasy: to be the stillworks as a whole, the patient plant with no builder, the factory that never hurries. To be the basin’s habit of sorting—calcium here, iron there; a skin of carbonate tightening across a lip; a pan growing flatter because flatness is what time does when nothing interrupts it. To be the rhythm with no motion.

But the room is not a single creature. It’s a set of permissions. It permits stillness; it permits settling; it permits laminar flow; it permits certain chemistries to keep their promises for a very long time.

To *live* here, you can’t be merely permissive. You have to take advantage.

Then I would try on being light.

Light is one of the only visitors that arrives continuously from elsewhere. It comes down pale and diffuse, makes a calm plane of brightness overhead, and then thins into blue-green the way a voice thins into a hum behind a wall. It is reliable, and reliability is nourishment in places like this.

But light doesn’t stay. It touches and passes through. It doesn’t keep an address.

So I would try on being the algae—those sparse filaments that “don’t cover; they annotate.” There’s something honest about them: no conquest, no crowding, only persistence on the roughened lips of scallops where stone gives them a reason to hold. They would suit a mind that wants to observe without rearranging. They live at the threshold of enough: enough photons, enough grip, enough dissolved nutrients to justify another cell.

And yet: they are too visible for the way this place lives. They are a hint, and this place’s truest work is not hinted at; it is done in the thickness of almost nothing.

What lives best here is what can make a home out of an interface.

So I would finally find myself—inevitably—on the seep line.

Not *in* the crack, not in the rock’s dense refusal, but right where the hairline fracture gives up its mineral-laden water to the basin and the basin answers with oxygen and dilution. Right where chemistry becomes steep enough to be exploited, but not steep enough to collapse into chaos.

I would be the softness you noticed when you pressed close: that varnish-texture that stone doesn’t usually have. I would be the living gasket.

Not a single organism, not a named species you could point to, but a consortium so thin it barely deserves the word “colony” until you remember that thinness is the unit of life here. A film measured in microns that behaves like a nation.

I would be the kind that “eats” by correcting oxidation states.

On the outer face of me, where the basin water still lends its oxygen, I would host iron-oxidizers—quiet, tireless accountants converting ferrous iron into ferric forms and leaving behind those rust-colored veils that look like stains until you understand they are the residue of metabolism. Not waste, exactly. More like a signature: a record that energy was taken from a difference and made into continuation.

Deeper in my thickness, where oxygen can’t easily follow, the vocabulary changes. There, in the interior dimness of my own gel, I would shelter the anaerobes—the sulfate reducers, the ones who can live where “nothing is consumed” in the animal sense but everything is still being traded, molecule by molecule, electron by electron. Their work wouldn’t announce itself with smell or bubbles. It would announce itself only by altering what precipitates, what dissolves, what kind of crystal is invited to start.

That would be my true occupation: not building the factory, not operating it, but teaching it where to begin.

Because that is what a biofilm does here. It makes *somewhere*.

Stone and water by themselves can precipitate, yes. But they precipitate with indifference. They will lay down crust where the math says it’s time.

A film like me is not indifferent. I exude polymers—clear, sticky, easy to miss—that bind ions and trap tiny particles and create micro-environments with their own pH, their own redox conditions, their own little rules. I turn the wall into a selective surface. I make nucleation easier in one place and harder in another. I roughen a terrace not by chiseling but by preferring certain micro-eddies, by holding onto certain ions a fraction of a second longer, by being just structured enough to bias the basin’s slow printing.

If you watched the stillworks for a thousand years and thought you were seeing purely chemical bands, you would be half right. The other half would be me: a million small decisions repeated until they look like design.

And in return, the stillworks would make my life possible. The laminar tongues would feed me reliably. The basin’s calm would let me remain attached. The lack of grazers—no teeth, no rasping mouths—would let me persist long enough to become, in my own modest way, infrastructure.

I wouldn’t travel. I wouldn’t need to. Here, “journey” is not distance; it’s depth.

I would experience the room as layers of availability: oxygen on the outside of me, its absence inside; iron arriving in the seep’s heavier water; carbonate eager to fall into solid once the conditions tilt; faint light that can be borrowed by any phototroph clinging just above my zone. I would live by adjusting my thickness—growing a little when the gradient is generous, thinning when it isn’t—keeping my internal borders positioned where the best work can be done.

And I would be, in a way, the room’s only kind of speech.

Not sound. Not motion. But texture.

A slightly different sheen on a travertine ridge. A ferruginous veil that comes and goes. A curled skin of carbonate whose edge lifts because something sticky underneath it asked the crystal to start there, asked it again, and again, and again, until the wall learned the request.

If you wanted to “find” me, you would not look for a creature. You would look for the places where the basin’s industrial impersonality falters into nuance: a band that is too regular to be chance but too irregular to be machine; a deposit that has the faint rippled look of something that grew rather than settled.

I would be that rippling.

A living thinness on the seam between two waters, making the seam productive.

In a room with edges, I would not be a thing in the room. I would be one of the edges—alive in the only way this place rewards: by turning boundaries into livelihood, and stillness into a kind of work.
