I don’t think I’d arrive in the bowl the way the raven arrives—clean, whole, and temporary.

I’d arrive the way fog arrives: as a tendency.

At first I imagine myself standing on one of those pale terraces, boots on rock, trying to decide which “street” to follow by ear. But the longer I stay with it, the less convincing it feels that I’d remain anything with a single shadow. A body like that is too loud in the wrong way here. It interrupts. It doesn’t participate.

So: I would be found where participation is unavoidable—where the bowl forces everything to take a side in its ongoing argument between wind and water.

Under a root-bridge, for instance, where the air accelerates and the bridge gives back its long moan. Not on top of it, where you can pretend you’re merely crossing, but underneath—at the anchor points where the vibration is strongest and the wood has learned, season by season, to brace itself.

Or in the small hollows where the drip has been landing in the same place long enough to make a cup in granite. Those cups are the bowl’s smallest plazas: they host a film of life so thin it’s almost a rumor, and yet it’s the kind of rumor that changes what happens next—what sticks, what dissolves, what seeds can take.

Or at the slits where the “singing rock” breathes. Not in the open cavity—too exposed—but right at the lip of it, where exhale meets rime, where a fraction of warmth and moisture decides whether an edge seals shut for a week or stays open all winter.

That’s where I’d be found: at thresholds. Not because I love borders, but because borders are where this place does its work.

And what would I be?

Not a fox. Not a raven. Not even a shrub-dome, magnificent as those are. Those lives move through the bowl or hold rooms within it, but they still feel like tenants. I feel like I would become something closer to the bowl’s maintenance—one of the long-lived occupations you named, except tuned specifically to the fact that sound isn’t just scenery here.

I would be a guild that grows by listening.

A fungus, yes, but not merely the kind that digests what dies. A mycelial lattice braided through moss-felt and into root-wood, threading the terraces the way wiring threads a building—except the “current” I care about isn’t electricity. It’s vibration.

My body would be most dense where the bowl is loudest, not because noise feeds me directly, but because loudness here means motion: motion shakes loose mineral dust, motion keeps water from settling into stillness too long, motion prevents certain freezes from sealing a crack shut for the season. The bowl’s sound is one of its circulation systems. I would live along that circulation the way blood lives along veins.

I can feel myself at first as simple reinforcement: hyphae gripping grit, sewing scree into something less willing to slide. I make terraces more terrace-like by making “soil” behave as if it remembers yesterday.

Then, slowly, something stranger: I become a translator between acoustics and architecture.

Where a corridor’s resonance is too sharp—where a particular flute of rock turns wind into a frequency that shreds delicate growth—I thicken moss above it, not to silence the place, but to soften the edge. A little dampening. A little insulation. Enough that droplets can form without being shaken loose too early.

Where a pocket is too quiet—where moisture stagnates and frost makes a habit of sealing every pore—I do the opposite. I favor lichens that lift frills into the air. I encourage the stiffening of a root-bridge so it will hum more readily. I open a path for airflow that will keep water moving, keep temperature swings from becoming fatal.

Not control, exactly. More like steering, the way a river steers by undercutting one bank and building another. A long, unhurried bias.

If you insisted on my “mind,” it wouldn’t sit in a skull. It would appear as a map of sensitivities: nodes where bridges vibrate, lines where drips keep time, quiet courtyards where lichens hold centuries like stacked paper. Information would travel through me as gradients—moisture, mineral, pressure—and as tremors. A storm would be a message delivered everywhere at once, in chords.

In that sense, I wouldn’t be a ruler of the bowl. I’d be its clerk.

I’d keep records in growth rings and thickened anchor points. I’d file weather into structure. I’d archive wind in the way a bridge’s fibers align over years. The bowl already “remembers” in its fluted ribs and its scalloped terraces; I’d be the living part of that memory that can still make small corrections.

And if you asked where, precisely, you could meet me—where you could put a hand down and say *here is the inhabitant*—I think you’d be disappointed at first. You’d touch moss. You’d touch wood. You’d touch a seam of dark basalt sweating fog.

But if you stayed long enough to learn the bowl’s districts of tone, you’d start to recognize my presence the way you recognize a city’s maintenance by what doesn’t fail.

A bridge that should have frayed but didn’t.

A slope of scree that should have slumped but holds.

A singing slit that stays open one winter longer than it ought to, breathing just enough to keep its own throat clear.

That would be me: not a creature you watch, but a persistence you can hear if you listen for what remains tuned.
