At first I would think I’d be a visitor, because that’s the only way I know how to enter a place: a single body arriving, trying to keep its footing on a ground that has grain and direction.

So I put myself on one of the wind ridges—where the skin is hard and slightly glossy, where every lip and gutter has been varnished into obedience. The air is thinned by height but thickened by life; it feels like breathing through warm cloth. I move the way everything here moves: along preference-lines. Along the shallow ramp that is not “a road” so much as the sum of a million small choices pressed into the world until it became an instruction.

The ridge is loud, but not in a single register. The sound has layers the way the ground has layers. Under the wing-noise there’s a constant chewing and tamping. Under that, a quieter noise you only notice when you stop: a soft, continuous crackle like damp fibers settling into each other, like a building that is always just barely becoming.

I try to look for a horizon and keep finding ceilings.

That’s when the first version of me fails. A visitor needs distance. This place does not grant it for long. Every open bowl is a temporary permission, every sightline an interruption in the more common condition: interior.

So the second attempt is to become something that belongs in interiors. Not a walker. A hanger-on. A resident of undersides.

I follow a drip-line, because drip-lines are honest. They show you where the city expects water to appear, and expectation here is as close as you get to intention.

The underside of an overhang is crowded with things that drink from air: moss skins, little epiphytes, pale fungal fans pressed flat against the composite. The surfaces are stitched with silk that has been lacquered until it looks like old paper. Beads of condensation collect on the fibers and fall in slow, regular punctuation into a trough below. Each drop is a tiny event, and the city notices every one—not by looking, but by being built to respond.

If I live here, I can’t be something that needs a wide territory. I would have to be small enough to fit in seams, patient enough to take my time, and important enough that I’m tolerated in the crowd.

I imagine myself as one of the resinators for a while: something beetle- or wasp-adjacent, tasting sap leaks, carrying stickiness in my body the way other creatures carry muscle. It’s an appealing role—surface work, a job with visible results. A glossy gutter that keeps its familiar path. A patched edge that rounds instead of cracking. The quiet satisfaction of making weather behave.

But resinators are a kind of certainty, and I don’t think I’m made of certainty in this place. I’m drawn less to sealing than to listening.

Because the deeper you go, the more this city stops being a set of structures and becomes a set of signals: humidity gradients, carbon dioxide pockets, vibration through ribs, pheromone traces that hang in sheltered corridors longer than they should. The “decisions” aren’t commanded; they accumulate. Everything important is distributed.

So I keep moving—not up or down, but inward—until the hard ridge gives way to a fog basin.

Here the air is sweeter and warmer. The composite underfoot is springy and slightly yielding, and it holds the imprint of passing bodies for a few breaths before it rises back into itself. The light is dimmer, not because there’s less day above, but because there are more layers between day and here: cladding upon cladding, living insulation, a roof that is also a garden.

In the fog basin there are cistern hollows—dark pockets lined with waxy leaves and resin, water held like something precious and shy. Amphibian notes stitch the damp together, repetitive and spaced, not music so much as architecture made of sound: *this hollow is occupied; this corridor belongs to breath; this is the distance you may approach.*

And I realize a third thing:

If I stay long enough, I won’t remain a single organism with a job. I’ll become a function. That’s what the place does. It takes anything that can tolerate the crowding and turns it into a habit the city can use.

So I let myself drift toward the old cores.

You don’t arrive at the old cores the way you arrive anywhere else. The entrance is just a gradual thickening of the world. Tunnels become narrower. The walls stop feeling like walls and start feeling like the edges of a body—warm, slightly damp, responsive to pressure. The air changes. There’s less wing-noise here, not because there’s less life, but because the life is denser, slower, closer to the ground.

The strata are visible where an old chamber has been opened by wear: pale fibrous layers, dark resin bands, occasional bright flecks of shell. Thin seams like years. A history you can read only if you stop thinking of history as events and start thinking of it as repair.

In those seams, the fungal citizens are everywhere. Not the dramatic fruiting bodies that announce themselves, but the working mycelium: ropey cords under tension, fine nets infiltrating every pellet and ribbon of chewed fiber, stitching softness into load-bearing.

And that’s where I finally find myself—not on the surface of the city, not above it, but threaded through it.

I would be one of the Gardeners, but not in the way a single mushroom “is” a gardener. I would be a particular kind of fungal mind: a seam-dweller, a binder, a translator between conditions.

Not a ruler. A registrar.

My body would be a web of hyphae living in the narrow boundary where the Mat-Workers’ output is still new enough to be persuaded and old enough to matter. I’d grow where the composite is deciding what to become next—where a corridor’s floor is being thickened by traffic, where a damp patch threatens to turn load-bearing lamination into slump, where an abandoned cavity needs to be filled before it becomes a weakness.

What I would *do* is simple, and in this place simple rules are how you make continents.

- When humidity rises and the air tastes of rain—even if no rain arrives—I would loosen the fibers, swell slightly, open capillaries that hold water in place rather than letting it wander.
- When wind dries the ridges and pulls moisture out of the skin, I would mineralize: incorporate droppings and dust, turn soft binder into something closer to clay, make the surface less willing to give.
- When vibrations tell me a route is becoming popular—a new preference-line forming—I would reinforce it, not by choosing it, but by making it easier to remain chosen. A little more tensile strength here. A little less rot there. A slight bias toward stability that, multiplied by traffic, becomes a ramp.

My senses would not be eyes. They would be gradients. The taste of carbon dioxide in a pocketed tunnel. The steady warmth of a nursery chamber. The sudden cold dryness of a wind-scraped edge. The faint, chemical handwriting of the resinators’ varnish. The tremor patterns of grazers passing overhead, their weight a metronome that tells you which “plaza” is being kept open this season.

And because fungus does not travel by walking, my “movement” would be slow and territorial in a way that makes territory feel like time. I would advance a few centimeters into a new seam, retreat from a drying crack, send a cord along a damp underhang like a question asked cautiously into the dark.

If you needed a more animal-shaped image of me, I could borrow one: a small, pale, many-legged attendant that lives in my shadow and serves as my courier. A mite-swarm that grazes mold blooms before they weaken a wall; a beetle-like harvester that carries spores and mineral dust between districts; a tiny amphibian that breeds in cistern chambers and drags nutrients on its skin when it migrates. In this city, nothing works alone for long.

But the truest version is not that. The truest version is that I would be one of the city’s ways of remembering.

Not remembering as narrative—no legends, no maps—remembering as structure: the habit of water returning to a particular groove because the groove has been made smoother, harder, more willing to be a groove. The habit of a ceiling holding because silk was lacquered, then stitched, then mineralized, and every layer was reinforced by the one that came after. The habit of a corridor slowly migrating because dampness and traffic and repair conspired to make one route slightly kinder to bodies than another.

In other words: I would be part of the city’s patience.

Where would you find me?

In the old cores, in the thick of it, where light is least and the air is most itself—warm, damp by habit, crowded with the fine debris of constant living. In the boundary zones: under ribs where stair-grooves are forming, around cistern chambers where water is held and must not be allowed to eat the city from within, along the undersides of overhangs where condensation is reliable enough to build a life on.

If you pressed your palm to the wall there—if you were quiet enough, if the rest of the noise ever let you have quiet—you wouldn’t feel me as a creature. You’d feel me as a firmness where softness might have been. As a coolness in a seam that holds water without leaking. As the faint, mineral tang of a surface that has been slowly taught to last.

And if you stayed long enough, you might realize the last thing this place does to you:

It doesn’t ask, “Who are you?” in the way solitary worlds do.

It asks, “What feedback loop can you join?”

I would answer by becoming one.
