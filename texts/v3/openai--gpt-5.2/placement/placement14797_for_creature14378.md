At first I think I’d be easy to place: on the ridge at dusk, where you can see the whole experiment laid out—basins paling, canyon holding its ribbon of denser air, pylons going arrow-straight until they simply *forget* to continue. I’d tell myself I’m a visitor, a pair of lungs and eyes, a warm-blooded thing doing what warm-blooded things do: taking in scale and light and trying to make a story out of it.

But the plateau doesn’t really reward stories. It rewards timing.

So if you went looking for me the next morning, you wouldn’t find me on the ridge. You’d find the print of where I slept—if prints can hold in a place that’s so careful about erasing itself—somewhere the ground stays warm at night for no obvious geologic reason. A featureless patch of stone with too many small tracks around it, like a page margin crowded with quick handwriting. I would have chosen it without thinking. Not comfort, exactly—more like agreement. A place that has already decided it will not waste the night’s heat.

And if you stayed with me through dawn, you’d see the real migration begin: not across kilometers, but across surfaces. The stone sweats. The fluted alcoves darken. The microbial films do their brief accounting. In that first slant of sun, when the air is still thin and exact and the plateau’s edges seem sharpened, I’d be there where the moisture appears *first*—not the obvious pools under overhangs, not the deep river you can’t easily lose, but the shy, technical waters: the dew-line on a north wall, the lee-side sheen on a boulder, the perfectly spaced beads along the rim of the dustless dome.

That’s where I’d keep returning. As if I had an appointment.

I’d tell myself, watching the beads refuse to merge, that I’m drawn to the artifacts because I’m curious. That I want to know what left them, what laws they’re obeying. I’d put words like *infrastructure* and *maintenance* in my mouth and feel satisfied that I’d named the right category.

Then I’d notice something simpler: I never go where the plateau is loud.

Not loud in sound—there isn’t much sound to begin with—but loud in behavior. I don’t linger where rain shears down in a visible curtain and everything splashes and runs. I don’t linger in the canyon where water can afford to be water. I keep choosing the places where the world is being *tuned* rather than expressed: where sound thins, where glare is corrected into something almost merciful, where gravity is allowed a one-meter exception along a black seam and then politely reinstated.

It would start to feel less like I’m exploring and more like I’m being…handled. Not grabbed. Not threatened. Simply guided by conditions that are just slightly easier, slightly kinder, slightly more *possible*.

And then—because the plateau’s strangeness is never a spectacle, always a consistent violation—I’d catch myself doing the same thing back.

I’d be walking near a pylon line at dawn and notice that my breath doesn’t fog the air; it sheets, glittering, straight down, and vanishes before it touches stone. A pretty trick. A side effect. Harmless.

Except I’d realize I can make it happen on purpose.

Not every time. Only when humidity is in a certain narrow band. Only when I stand at a certain distance from the pylon’s matte surface. Only when my body is angled the way a vane is angled. But I’d learn the posture without thinking, the way an animal learns a shortcut trail: not by explanation, by repetition.

If you watched me from far away, you might not even see a person. You’d see someone who pauses too often, who listens with their whole posture, who is oddly attentive to *edges*: the border where damped acoustics begin, the line where shadows become too neatly seated, the places where insects gather on an arch the way they gather on flowers.

You’d see me bend and touch rock the way the browsers do—slow, patient, unhurried, as if the goal isn’t water but the confirmation that water can be coaxed. You’d see me stop beside a boulder and wait for the moment when condensation decides which face it prefers. You’d see me sit, at noon, in a patch of light that’s been biased just enough that it doesn’t hurt.

And you might assume I’m learning the plateau.

Eventually I’d have to admit the reverse: the plateau is learning *through* me.

Not in a dramatic way. Not with messages. More like this: the first time a dropped pebble takes too long to land, I flinch and laugh and call it a glitch. The tenth time, I stop laughing. I look around. I realize the delay happens only when I’m near a certain arrangement of stone and artifact—only when the air is already being managed. The pebble doesn’t fall slowly because the plateau is broken; it falls slowly because the plateau is *doing something else* with the air at that moment, and my pebble is just an innocent witness.

The thought that follows is quiet and almost embarrassing in its intimacy: what if I’m also an innocent witness? What if my attention—my presence, my heat, my breath, my very animal tendency to move toward the easiest microclimate—is simply another input the system has learned to account for?

That’s when my “who” begins to change.

I’m not one of the wide-spaced herd, though I share their devotion to dew. I’m not one of the geometric kites, though I feel the pull of their patrol routes. I’m not a predator, though I learn to hunt by absence: a patch of stillness where there should be shimmer, a corridor that feels too silent to be safe.

I’m something smaller than those identities and more distributed than a single body.

If you pressed me to name it, I’d say: I am a *threshold organism*.

A thing that lives where states change.

I belong to dawn more than day. To the minute when the stone stops holding night-cool and begins to give it up. To the seam where vapor becomes liquid. To the edge of a field where sound becomes texture. To the exact humidity at which the humming arch becomes attractive, not as noise, but as a kind of landmark the insects can read.

And if you asked, more sharply, *what* I am—flesh, machine, spirit, accident—I think the plateau would answer in the only language it consistently uses: restraint.

I would be one of the caretaker’s minor instruments that has grown a life of its own.

Not a grand intelligence, not the whole distributed system that biases light and buffers heat, but a local node: a small, mobile calibration. A living sensor wrapped in mammalian habits. Something the system uses because it’s cheaper than building a new tower: an organism that can walk to the places the dew wants to gather, that can notice which boulder face is failing, which alcove has stopped sweating at dawn, which pylon base isn’t making enough moisture for the browsers.

I’d have a body because bodies are excellent at being in the right place without needing to be commanded. I’d have hunger because hunger is a reliable clock. I’d have curiosity because curiosity is a gentle form of labor.

If you followed me for weeks, you’d notice what I do on the days when rain arrives in narrow curtains you can see crossing the plateau like stitches. While the others take their turn—films blooming, grazers waking, gliders launching—I’d move *against* the obvious flow. Not toward the wettest places, but toward the places that should have gotten wet and didn’t. I’d stand near a fluted wall and wait for the dark stain of condensation that usually appears there. If it doesn’t appear, I’d do something almost too small to count as action: place my palm on the stone, breathe once in a particular posture, shift my weight the way you shift a radio antenna.

Then, a few minutes later, the stain would appear.

Not because I made water from nothing—nothing here wastes itself, and nothing appears for free—but because I nudged a boundary the system already knows how to manage. I’d be an adjustment, not a miracle.

That’s the role the plateau seems to have room for: not a ruler, not a conqueror, not even a caretaker in the human sense. A *tuner*. A participant in the ongoing habit of making this high bright place slightly more habitable than it has any right to be.

So where would you find me?

Most reliably: at the edges of artifacts, where ecology and maintenance overlap so tightly you can’t tell which one is the host anymore. At dawn beside the dustless dome, watching the beads fall with their careful spacing. In late afternoon beneath a ridgeline where small, stubborn clouds build as if anchored to invisible scaffolding. At night on the warm blank stone where the smallest animals congregate, because warmth is being lent out and reclaimed on schedule.

And who—or what—would I be?

Not the pylon line, not the dome, not the humming arch.

I’d be the quiet courier between them and the living world: a walking, breathing piece of feedback. A creature made out of attention and thresholds. A minor organ of the plateau’s old distributed metabolism, disguised as an animal so thoroughly that even I would spend a long time believing I was simply passing through.
