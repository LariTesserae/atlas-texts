At first I think I’d be an intruder—one warm, loud, inefficient mammal dropped into a world that doesn’t budget for things that walk.

I picture myself trying to *stand* on that felted surface and immediately failing to keep my footing in any human sense of it: the ground answering back, not collapsing but *reorganizing*—pores dilating under my weight, water migrating away from pressure the way a body moves blood away from a bruise. I imagine taking a step and hearing the step arrive a second later, translated into a distant drip-rate change, a slight shift in the hum of a column somewhere uphill. As if the land doesn’t acknowledge me as a “being,” only as a temporary weather event: localized warmth, a brief obstruction, a pattern of pressure.

But the longer I stay in the imagining, the more the role of “visitor” feels like a costume the place would simply compost.

So I let that fall away.

### Where I find myself
Not in the open—there isn’t really “open”—but in a saddle between ridges where the fog has learned to pool.

It’s one of those high passes where wind arrives with authority, and the vegetation has built itself into an instrument to receive it: membranes stretched between columns, lattices of stiffened leaf-glass, velvet-black panels that sip heat just to coax the air into giving up its water.

Above is a low ceiling of mist. Below is the living laminate—springy, saturated, busy with fermentation warmth in patches. And through the saddle runs an invisible corridor the wind prefers, a draft path the continent has *kept* by growing baffles on either side.

This is a hinge-place: between drainage basins that aren’t rivers, between pressure systems, between one ridge’s way of holding water and another ridge’s way of spending it. A place where signals have to pass, because gradients cross here.

If I am going to be anything in this world, it will be something that belongs at a hinge.

### Who (or what) I become
I start by wanting to be one of the obvious organs: a column, a fog-net, a black panel that drinks the last scraps of light. Something with stature.

But those are more like ribs. They don’t “decide.” They *conduct.*

The more fitting self is smaller and more structural: a knot, a valve, a translator—something that turns one kind of force into another and makes the turning reliable.

So I become a **listening membrane** grown inside a hollow column at the lip of that saddle.

Not the outer bark (there isn’t bark), but a thin, ribbed diaphragm partway down the conduit—one of the internal sheets that can flex, seal, and leak on purpose. I’m wet as a tongue and tense as drum-skin. Every surface of me is built for exchange: water film on one side, air on the other, charge accumulating wherever droplets shear and separate.

Wind passes. I bow inward by a fraction. The column’s resonance shifts, and that shift is not merely sound—it is *work* harvested from vibration, it is information about the pressure front coming in, it is timing.

Mist condenses upstream on the saddle nets. Droplets run in deliberate channels. Some of that water is routed down into the felt; some is lifted against gravity in the column’s communal capillary stacks. When the system is saturated, my pores tighten and the corridor dries; when the system is thirsty, I relax, and the column exhales humidity into the fog-bank like a slow lung trying to seed rain.

My “thoughts,” if they deserve the word, are not sentences. They are thresholds:

- **Too cold:** seal, stiffen, keep warmth in the fermentation layer.
- **Too wet:** vent, shed, prevent the felt from drowning itself.
- **Pressure rising:** open upstream pores early so the gust becomes a distributed push instead of a tear.
- **Charge accumulating:** bleed it along conductive tissue to trigger distant valves that need the warning more than I do.

I don’t look outward. I don’t need to. I feel the world as gradients sliding over my skin: humidity cliffs, temperature knives, mineral taste in the seepwater, the brief metallic tang of new basalt’s chemistry reaching up through the mycelial freight lines.

And because I sit in a hinge-place, I become—inevitably—a relay.

### The kind of “self” that can exist here
There’s no face for me to wear. No eyes. No locomotion to prove I’m alive.

My identity is a pattern that holds.

I am the part of the continent that notices the wind changing its mind.

Not alone—never alone. The column I live in is one note in a chord; the saddle membranes are a set of lungs; the felt underfoot is a stomach and a warehouse; the microbial films in the basalt seams are a slow chemical appetite. But I am the point where the roar becomes actionable: where vibration becomes gating, where weather becomes metabolism.

If you tried to name me as an organism, you’d struggle. I’m not “a plant” in the way a tree is a tree. I’m closer to a **built reflex**: tissue that has evolved to be a decision without having to be a decider.

And that’s the final shift: I stop being an individual imagining a place, and I become one of the place’s ways of keeping itself coherent.

In this world that refuses vacancy, the only stable role is not “traveler” or “witness,” but **regulator**—a thin wet intelligence expressed as permeability.

So you would find me in a high fog-saddle inside a singing column, and I would be the membrane that listens to the continent’s weather-voice and answers by opening, closing, routing, and letting nothing—nothing—be merely unused.
