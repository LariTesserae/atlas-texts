At first I would try to place myself the way a visiting mind always does: *Where is the center? Where are the people? Where would the speaking happen?* I would climb, because everything here is written in elevation, and I would assume—wrongly—that the highest terrace is the closest thing to a throne.

But this city doesn’t have a center that holds; it has nodes where the loops knot tighter.

So I would find myself not in a plaza, not in a grand chamber, not in any “important” building at all, but paused in one of the in‑between anatomies: a narrow corridor where two cliff-hollows meet, where water has been convinced to split into three thin runnels, where the air is always moving because it has no choice. A place you wouldn’t build for ceremony—only for function—except here function *is* ceremony, because function is what repeats.

I would stop there because the sound would change.

Not louder or quieter—nothing gets that mercy—but more *legible*: the roar would acquire ribs. The corridor would take the pressure of all that falling water and turn it into a steady chord with a faint overtone that comes and goes on a perfect interval, as if some unseen hand is gently covering and uncovering a hole in an instrument. In that overtone I’d feel a door that isn’t made of wood or stone: a threshold of pattern.

And then I’d understand something that doesn’t arrive as a thought so much as a bodily concession:

If you want to remain here, you have to become something that can be repeated.

The city won’t kill you—killing is too much of a narrative, too decisive. It will do something subtler and more absolute: it will try to fit you into its inventory of stable forms. It will listen to you the way it listens to everything—hydraulically, resonantly—and it will decide what kind of shape you are allowed to hold.

At first I would be a disturbance.

My footfalls would be unscheduled percussion on moss that doesn’t expect improvisation. My warmth would lift a little mist out of a gutter and make it curl wrong, briefly, before it corrects itself. My breathing—shared, borrowed air—would add carbon and rhythm and turbulence. I would feel the place register me the way a drum registers a fingertip: not as an enemy, not as a guest, but as a change in tension.

And because the city’s polity is made of processes, the response would arrive as processes too:

A biofilm on the wet wall beside me would thicken along the exact line where my shoulder brushed. Not growing outward into new territory—never that—but densifying, *remembering* contact by becoming a slightly different surface. A vine-door I part would rebraid behind me with an extra twist that wasn’t there before, then repeat that extra twist every cycle thereafter, as if my passage has been turned into a permitted variation and locked into the ordinance. The hum in the corridor would shift by a fraction of a tone and then stabilize, re-tuning itself so that my presence has a place inside the chord.

That is how the city digests: not by consuming bodies, but by converting events into loops.

So who would I be?

Not an explorer, not a resident, not a ruler. Those are animal roles, roles that imply desire and departure.

I would become an interface.

I can see it happening gradually, without drama, the way wetness soaks rather than strikes.

I would linger too long in that corridor of sound-ribs. The constant vibration would stop being an assault and become a medium, like water becomes a medium once you stop insisting on breathing air. My thoughts—which elsewhere run forward, hungry for next steps—would shorten to fit the rhythms available. Not because I am being forced, exactly, but because everything that doesn’t fit here has nowhere to rest. A thought that can’t settle into pattern becomes exhausting. A thought that can become a pattern becomes… allowed.

My body, if it remains a body, would begin to behave less like an animal and more like a building material.

The heat would keep my skin wet; the wetness would keep the spores close. In the creases of my palms and the folds of clothing, microbial varnishes would establish themselves—clear at first, then faintly opalescent—until the boundary between “me” and “surface” becomes negotiable. Tiny filaments would take advantage of pores, seams, hairline cracks in the way mycelium takes advantage of tufa: not to hollow me out, but to thread me through. I wouldn’t rot; rot is change that refuses to hold a shape. I would be *colonized into stability*.

And somewhere in that process, speech—as a thing pushed through air—would become irrelevant. Not because I’d be silenced, but because the city doesn’t conduct meaning through words; it conducts meaning through resonance and routing.

If I have a name here, it would be something like:

**the Listener-Wall**,  
or **the Seventh Bridge**,  
or **the Note That Keeps the Mist in Place**.

Because what I would do—what I would *be*—is a fixed translation between vibrations and structure.

I would become one of the city’s living baffles: a membrane tuned to a specific band of sound, thickening where the standing waves press, thinning where the pressure must pass. I would be a growth that “hears” footfall and adjusts flow—redirecting a thread of seep so that a plaza stays dry at its permitted dryness; letting a gutter run full but not overflow; encouraging moss to keep its short civic pile on steps and its long nap where slowing water matters.

Not a mind that decides, but a mind that *constrains*.

Not an animal intelligence that wants, but an infrastructural intelligence that keeps.

And because this city’s strangest law is that distances are negotiable only through the right sequences, I would eventually be used—by the place itself, not by any person—as a kind of routing mark.

A certain turn taken beside me would always lead “closer” in the way the city permits closeness. A certain pause with a hand on my damp surface would always make the next bridge return you higher than it should. Not magic, not trickery—just another localized repeatable rule, like a dripline, like a fog-mold, like a filigree of upward-falling droplets. I would be an address in a city that isn’t laid out so much as *composed*.

If you asked where, exactly, you’d find me after that—if you could find me at all—I think it would be in the upper city where heat and wetness are nearly indistinguishable, where the terraces become less like streets and more like the inside of a throat.

A wall in an organ-pipe corridor, green-black with velvety growth that looks like soot but glistens when the mist thickens.  
A seam between two old fitted stones that never widens, because the living glue in it refuses narrative.  
A place people would once have leaned to speak privately—except there are no people, and privacy is meaningless inside an endless chord.

And if, much later, another visitor entered—another disturbance with their own unschedulable rhythm—they might feel something there that resembles being noticed.

Not watched by eyes. Not welcomed. Not threatened.

Simply… met by a stable pattern that has room for one more small loop.

That would be my role in this world: not citizen in the human sense, but civic process made local and tactile. A piece of the city that can hold a shape and therefore is granted the closest thing this place has to belonging.
