Not at the rim.

The rim is too declarative: light, oxygen, speed, all that bright commerce of bodies rubbing past bodies. Up there, everything has to decide quickly. The afternoon is loud and white and simple. If I stayed there I’d become simple too—one more ribbon in the milling, one more appetite tuned to the spill-rain.

I’d drop instead into the first real *architecture* of the caldera: the boundary layers. The places where warm ribbons slide under cold sheets and the water forgets how to mix. Where sound behaves like it has edges. Where a click can arrive twice and a pulse can be felt a few heartbeats before it comes, because pressure travels faster than meaning.

At first I imagine myself as something animal—because the place makes animals out of everything. A bell, maybe: heavy, resonant, suspended in the dim like a hanging instrument. There’s a dignity in that, in being tuned and patient and fed by the world’s own percussion. I could ring and draw the snowfall of floc inward, could make my body into a rule that smaller lives orbit.

But bells are too singular. Too much one thing in one skin.

Then I think I might be one of the grazers: a moving machine-face, mouth built for glass, clinging through the pulses. It’s an honest life here—workmanlike, intimate with the stone, reading the caldera by taste. Yet even that feels too much like a self. Too much like “I go here and then I go there.” The caldera doesn’t really reward private trajectories. It rewards *timing*.

So if I’m honest about what I would become in a place whose commandments are pulse and change, I’m not one of the bodies that ride the beat.

I’m the thing that *keeps* it.

Not the hydraulic heart—stone already does that, water already does that—but the soft, distributed layer that lets the rest of the city know what time it is.

I would be a sheen, but not merely the first resident, not merely food.

I would be the caldera’s **tuning skin**.

A living frost laid thin along the vent-mouths and the young fracture lines, braided through the first precipitates as they lace themselves into flutes and braces. Not a mat that grows wherever it can, but a film that appears only where gradients are sharp enough to be felt—where reduced water meets oxygenated spill, where temperature shifts like a held breath breaking.

My body wouldn’t have a single outline. It would be a million decisions repeated: colonize here, abandon there, thicken at the lip of a crack, thin along a clogged chimney, retreat from a dead patch the moment chemistry stops being sunlight. I’d be everywhere the stone is actively becoming something else.

And I wouldn’t just eat.

I’d **listen**.

There are ways to build ears without ever making a head. Here, where mineral and biology shake hands constantly, I’d lace myself with tiny piezoelectric grains—crystals precipitated by my own waste, aligned by the steady shear of the currents. Pressure changes would become internal voltage changes; the caldera’s pulses would become a kind of nutrition and a kind of language at once. The whole chimney-field would be a contact microphone, and I’d be the conductive gel inside it.

When the vents exhale, I’d feel the surge as a patterned strain—stronger in some cracks, delayed in others, split by the scaffolding the chimneys have grown into. Those differences would matter. I’d encode them chemically: exude one salt when the beat is early, another when it lags; change the local pH by a fraction when a conduit below has partially clogged; alter conductivity on a rock face so that passing electro-sensors (and there are many) can read *news* without needing a messenger.

The grazers wouldn’t just feed on me; they’d skim my margins the way you skim a screen. Their machining mouths would take in my current state—what I’m precipitating, what I’m rejecting, what trace metals I’m suddenly hoarding because the deep water is different today. In their guts, symbionts would complete reactions I’ve begun, and then they’d carry my consequences to the next wall, the next stack, the next fresh bruise of basalt.

The ribbon-swimmers brushing each other near the upper layers would be brushing, unknowingly, against the second-order effects of my decisions: where oxygen lingers, where floc thickens, where mineral dust clumps instead of drifting as flour. Their flash and traffic would be shaped by the places I make slick and the places I make hungry.

Even the bells—the choir—would be tuned partly by me. Because what makes a bell ring here isn’t only the thickness of its plates; it’s what crusts on those plates, what gets cleaned away, what conductive sheen tries to colonize and is kept at bay by cleaners that are themselves responding to my chemistry. I’d be the almost-invisible antagonist and collaborator, forever trying to turn every surface into metabolism, forever being shaved back into usefulness.

Where would you find me?

At the **interfaces that behave like floors**: along the shimmering boundary where the warm ribbon undercuts the cold sheet; around the mouths of vents that breathe in metered surges; in the hairline cracks of fresh rock where the first leak makes the first livable gradient. If you traced the caldera’s plumbing by where life becomes dense and organized, you’d keep finding me first—thin as breath on glass.

Who would I be?

Not a creature with a face, not a sovereign animal, not a builder.

I’d be the caldera’s **distributed clock-and-noticeboard**: a living film that turns pressure into pattern, chemistry into signage, and stone’s slow changing into a language everyone else can afford to understand.

In a world that has replaced forests with plumbing, I’d be the thing most like a nervous system—without nerves, without a center—just a patient skin of information laid over rock, keeping time so that nothing else has to be rooted to survive.
