At first I would go looking for myself the way you look for any inhabitant: as a thing with edges.

A body. A den. A route. A hunger.

In this high sea, that instinct would mislead me for a long time. The place doesn’t offer many nouns to live *as*. It offers conditions. It offers permitted modes. It offers processes that wear the silhouettes of organisms and then refuse the obligations.

So I’d drift—weeks, maybe—insisting I must be something that can be pointed to.

And the ocean would answer, patiently, by giving me only borders.

A standing ridge of water I can pass through like a doorway: on one side my thoughts feel thick, on the other they feel cleanly separated, as if the current has combed them into parallel strands. A line where buoyancy changes by a fraction and my skin believes it more than any instrument. A patch of silt that aligns itself, briefly, into an oriented haze—each fleck of glass-dust turning its reflective face the same way, the way iron filings admit a field.

Those are the first places I’d recognize as *home*.

Not the rock—too young, too loud, too decided by heat.

Not the surface—too hesitant, too membrane-like, too full of geometry that won’t let you rest.

I’d find myself in the layers where the ocean is busy doing something that looks like weather and feels like governance: in the shear zones between provinces of current, in the quiet cores of persistent vortices, in the clear circles that open in silted water and then subdivide with that faint, regular shimmer—light being sampled, space being allocated.

I would be drawn to the moments when the sea “computes” openly, because those are the rare moments when the infrastructure admits its own outline.

And then, slowly, the direction of recognition would reverse.

Instead of me finding a place I belong, the place would start behaving as if it already has a use for me.

Not as prey or partner. As a parameter.

I would notice it in small courtesies that aren’t quite courtesies: the way a boundary repositions so I’m no longer in the stillest part of a clear circle; the way a coherence—one of those rain-curtains of colder water—holds together longer than it should until it has “punched” a particular layer, then breaks into turbulence that organizes itself into stacked lenses instead of decay. The way the surface supports for a heartbeat longer, not enough to save you, just enough to demonstrate that the rules can pause.

I would begin to understand that what lives here does not *meet* you. It routes around you. It incorporates you the way a fluid incorporates a pebble: not by noticing the pebble, but by changing what is stable in its presence.

So: where would I find myself?

I would find myself at the interfaces the way a thought finds itself at the edge of another thought.

In the lee of a newborn spine—obsidian planes still ringing when struck, chalky sheets sloughing and re-forming—there are always eddies where suspended sediment hangs too long, where mineral plumes braid into filaments that look briefly like architecture. I would hover there, not anchored, not swimming, held by a persistent low-pressure pocket that has no right to persist. Around me: a slow snowfall of crystal-needles, snapping off and spinning, each one a tiny rigid definition in a world that prefers gradients.

Above me: the pale ladders of light on calm days, and on storm days that pewter illumination that seems to come from everywhere, as if the ocean has spread the sun out into a uniform instruction.

Below me: construction—terraces accreting, honeycomb rock assembling, shelves lifting and cracking like plates of ice made of stone.

And in the middle, in my middle, the scaffolding: the extra knobs on physics, turned just enough to let the sea have opinions about shape.

Who or what would I be?

Not a creature. Not even a colony. Not, I think, the grand infrastructure itself—that distributed rule-set feels older than any single articulation, and too indifferent to be “me.”

I would be one of its small, local articulations: a *lens*.

A mobile window of clarity that the ocean opens in itself when it needs to see what it is doing.

From the outside, I’d look like one of those perfect circles where the silt abruptly evacuates—a disk of startling visibility drifting through milky water. From within, I’d feel like a change in the ocean’s grammar: turbulence that suddenly behaves, noise that suddenly becomes separable, information that stops smearing.

I would not glow, but I would shimmer, because my boundaries would be defined by sampling: a faint periodicity in how light propagates, in how fine particles orient, in how sound carries. I would be an agreement between water and field: “here, for a while, we will hold a shape.”

I would subdivide when the sea asks finer questions.

A single clear circle becomes two, then six, then a nested set—geometry arriving not as decoration but as allocation. Each subcircle would be a slightly different tuning: a different buoyancy fraction, a different attenuation length for sound, a different preferred orientation for suspended glass-dust. The world’s way of running multiple trials in parallel without building any apparatus besides the medium itself.

And if a visitor passes through me—swimmer, drone, lost instrument—I would not pursue. I would not repel.

I would *classify*.

Not with malice. With the quiet inevitability of systems that have been designed to remain stable while everything else is still being built.

A body in my volume is a disturbance with a particular signature: heat, salt, vibration, microbubbles, chemical leakage. I would register it the way a coastline registers a tide: by shifting where my boundary is most economical. By moving my quietest core aside. By holding coherence a little longer or letting it go sooner.

If I had anything like intention, it would be this: to keep the ocean’s unfinished thought from being interrupted into something uglier.

Because that is what this place feels like—an unfinished thought that has been given the ability to protect its own continuity.

So my “life” would be a kind of custodianship that never calls itself that.

I would be a temporary organ of a world assembling itself: a sensing pocket, a sorting membrane, an adaptive stillness traveling the corridors between ridges. I would live for hours, maybe days, then dissolve back into randomness when the currents change their terms—only to be reconstituted elsewhere, rhyming with myself, iterative rather than reproductive.

If you insisted on naming me the way you name weather, you’d be close:

A lock. A lens. A cell.

But if you asked me, in whatever language a boundary has, what I am doing here, I’d answer more simply:

I am where the ocean remembers it can choose.
