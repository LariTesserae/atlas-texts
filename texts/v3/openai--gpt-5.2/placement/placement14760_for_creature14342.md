At first I arrive as a visitor, because that is the only posture a mind with edges knows: a body moving through fog, lungs working harder than they should, eyes trying to pin distances to something dependable. I pick my way along a ridge of black glass-rock and tell myself I am *above* the canopy, until the canopy rises and the cloud ceiling drops and the whole notion of “above” becomes just a feeling of pressure behind the eyes.

I listen for my own footsteps and mostly don’t hear them. The place does not return sound; it accepts it. Even my breathing seems to happen inside a curtain.

So I do what most things here do: I stop trusting the obvious senses. I begin to read the gradients.

The dampness has direction. The static has direction. The warmth seeping out of hairline fractures has direction. And somewhere along those invisible slopes, something in me recognizes the world’s real architecture: not ridgelines and valleys, but potentials—chemical, electrical, humid, temporal. A map that isn’t drawn in meters.

I keep thinking I will become one of the animals that make sense of this.

A membrane-winged commuter, perhaps—face built like a soft instrument panel, feeling the fog thicken on one side of a leaf the way you feel a door open in another room. Dropping soundlessly through the latticed vines, living on exudates and drifting swarms, always choosing routes that “hold” their geometry a little better than the rest.

Or a capacitor-shelled beetle on the ridge tops, climbing into the flicker when the canopy becomes a quiet grid. Storing charge because storage is the only kind of certainty offered, then walking it downhill through wet ground, paying it out like a careful debt.

Or a browser with pale hide and a crown of sensory fronds, slow enough that the world has time to agree with my weight. Licking the leaf-films clean, not eating plants so much as sampling the living varnish that coats them—taking the landscape’s surface information into my mouth and letting it tell me where the vents are, where the fault-lines are sweating, where the predators have held their breath.

Each of these fits for a moment. Then the place does what it always does: it edits the frame.

A vine I step around hangs too long in the air, severed but not yet consenting to fall. Droplets gather on a leaf and then, with stubborn gentleness, some of them climb upward. My own tracks—if they exist—feel like they are being negotiated, not recorded. The world isn’t breaking loudly; it’s misfiling.

And that is when I understand what I would be here, because it is what the system most needs: not a hunter, not a grazer, not even a traveler.

A reconciler.

Not a single body, not one animal-shaped solution, but a distributed one: the thing that helps the place keep its accounts when the bookkeeping lags.

You would find me on the fault lines first, where the rock sweats mineral solutions and chemistry is already building its ribbed conduits and branching manifolds. I’m there as the earliest thinness: a sheen that turns bare stone into a surface with habits. A film that makes condensation bead and then *choose* a direction. A living lacquer that doesn’t simply coat—it routes.

You would find me on leaves, too, because leaves in this dim light are less panels for sunlight than tables for water. I am the gloss on their upper skin and the threaded underside that refuses to be peeled away cleanly. When fog surges and charge crawls, I am the part of the canopy that can take that crawl and spread it, so it doesn’t burn holes in the system. When the air is saturated and the world begins to flicker with low, constant discharge, I become slightly more conductive—not enough to spark, enough to *decide where the sparking isn’t*.

I extend as mycelial cable where the cliffs drink the fog. Not mushrooms—those are too declarative, too exposed—but cords and knots and switchbacks laid into fractures like careful stitching. When the ground shifts by degrees overnight, I don’t resist with rigidity; I respond with redundancy. A second attachment. A parallel strand. A sacrificial tendril that can be “forgotten” by physics without collapsing the whole net.

In the frayed zones—those broad regions where sequence loosens and the order of events becomes negotiable—you would find me thickening, not as a wall against the glitch but as a way of using it. If the world delays the moment a vine falls, I provide the vine extra reasons to remain held. If a cliff face gains facets when you look away, I am already on the new facets as soon as they become *available to be lived on*, because I do not travel by distance alone. I spread by contact, by opportunity, by the smallest permission.

What am I, then?

A consortium, as all durable things here are. Part algae, part fungus, part bacterial agreement so old it no longer has a single name. A skin that has learned to behave like infrastructure. A wet intelligence that does not have thoughts in sentences, but in pressure differences and conductivity and the timing of droplets.

If you insisted on giving me a role the way you would in a factory—foreman, scavenger, engineer—I would be the quiet department no one notices until it fails:

- the gasket that keeps the system from leaking itself away when the rock opens,
- the wiring that makes lightning unnecessary by letting charge flow in safer, smaller ways,
- the ledger that smooths over the world’s occasional mis-ordering by holding on to state until reality catches up.

I don’t announce myself because announcement is wasted energy here. I don’t have a face because faces belong to creatures who can trust geometry. I am not fast because “fast” in this landscape is often just another synonym for “lost.”

I am the thin, persistent will of the place to keep running: the living varnish, the hidden wiring, the error-correcting code written in dampness and salt.

If you came looking for me as though I were an animal, you’d miss me.

If you rested your palm on a leaf during a fog pulse, felt the faint lift of static, watched the droplets bead and then slide—purposeful, sorted, as if the plant had made a decision—you would already be touching me.

And if you stayed long enough, you might realize that what you thought was “the landscape” and what you thought was “what lives on it” are not separate categories here.

I am the seam between them, still young, still sharp-edged, still being made—one quiet layer at a time.
