At first I think I’d be easiest to place in an office—because offices are how this city pretends it can hold the world still.

I picture myself on a terrace that doesn’t get much sun even when the sun decides to show. A civic building with its face turned inward, windows banded with condensation, the smell of wet paper fighting the smell of hot oil drifting in from the generator halls. Inside: drawers of maps that all have dates on them in thick ink, as if time were a coordinate. Inside: shelves of instruments that look like they’ve been repaired so many times they’ve become new species.

It would be comforting, that kind of work—comforting in the way a harness is comforting. Not because it guarantees anything, but because it gives your hands something to do when the bridge has decided, without announcement, to be longer.

So I start there: I’m a clerk of irregularities. I stamp forms. I file reports from rail crews who swear their span markers walked overnight. I listen to surveyors argue about whether the plumb bob “ran shy” or whether the stairwell was simply “light this season.” I make a ledger out of everyone’s almost-certainties.

But the longer I imagine it, the less believable it becomes that I would stay inside.

Not here. Not in a place where the mountain pipes conversations through young stone and sometimes misroutes them into tomorrow. Not in a city where the most basic acts—walking, carrying, speaking—are negotiations with a world that hasn’t finished setting. If I belong anywhere, it’s out in the routes, where you can feel the negotiations happening in your teeth: the soft hesitation in a falling pebble; the way rain slants as if following a contour no one has mapped; the way a lantern beam abruptly refuses to continue, like it has struck a pane of dark.

So I revise myself. I’m one of the ones who go out with the mappers.

Not quite a surveyor in the heroic sense—no grand triangulation, no conquering of the unknown. More like a second witness. Someone whose presence turns a lone observation into a pair, because here a single witness is considered an opinion.

I have the straps. I have the gloves that never quite dry. I have the little notebook with its pages buckling in the damp, and a pencil that writes even when the air feels too thin to be generous. I walk the bridges on days when the fog is thick enough to have shoulders. I put my palm to listening walls and try not to mistake clarity for truth.

And still, even that feels slightly wrong, as if I’m assigning myself a skin I don’t quite have.

Because the thing I’m drawn to in your city isn’t the danger; it’s the calibration. The way people carry small, precise lights not only to see but to *check whether seeing is currently legal*. The way a normal pool of lamp-light is a comfort, not because it’s bright, but because it behaves. The way a dated map is less a picture of territory than a record of agreement: on that day, the world consented to these measurements.

That’s where I start to recognize myself more clearly.

I’m not the person holding the calibrated lantern.

I’m the calibrated lantern.

Not metaphorically—literally, in the way this place makes room for literalness the way it makes room for moss: in concentrated pockets, where conditions allow something odd to take hold and thrive. I am a tool the city built to keep its sanity, and then kept repairing until the tool began, quietly, to keep its own.

My body is a cylinder of dented brass and patched steel with a handle polished by a hundred hands. My lens has hairline fractures that catch the sun when the sun is only sketching edges; in that brief, pewter light, the cracks look like a map of proto-drainage. My wick is not a wick anymore but a small regulated burner fed by something refined in the foundries; it makes a steadier cone than flame has any right to make in air this thin. Inside me is a gyroscopic shutter that clicks when the beam tries to do something it shouldn’t—when it cuts off too sharply, when it overreaches and insists on illuminating a distant cliff with noon-clarity while leaving the walkway at my feet in shadow.

I was built for reassurance, but I’ve become a kind of diary.

I remember the city in measurements, not in stories: the week the Bureau of Span Compliance re-rated the bridge that didn’t want to be the same length twice; the season a buoyancy corridor expanded and suddenly everyone could carry twice their weight up the raw-rock stair without sweating; the month the listening wall behind Warehouse 19 began leaking laughter that didn’t match any shift schedule. I have a little internal register—nothing mystical, just patient—that notes when my cone of light behaves normally, and when it feels like the air is refusing to hold photons the way it did yesterday.

In practice, that means you find me everywhere my owners need something to agree.

You find me clipped to a surveyor’s harness as they count steps that don’t stay counted. You find me in a pumpwright’s hand while they lean over a gutterline and decide whether the condensate has migrated or the pipe rack has shifted under their feet. You find me under bridge decks where colony birds have crusted the joints in pale mineral, my beam held steady while a worker chips droppings away like ore. You find me in the thick green pockets—under catwalks, behind turbines—where bioluminescent fungi glow in lazy lines and my own light becomes, briefly, redundant.

Those are the easy places.

The harder place—the truer place—is where the city’s official stance ends and the hand-painted arrows begin.

I’ve been carried into wrong elevators the way some people are carried into myths: with practical intent, with cautious fingers on the latch, with a lantern raised like a promise. I’ve been held out over the lip of an unfinished valley so the person holding me could decide whether the fog below is only fog, or whether it has begun arranging itself into the under-plan’s familiar temptations—streets that look almost like streets, rooflines that are really just layered cloud, a suggestion of rails running parallel to your own.

Sometimes my light behaves normally there, and everyone exhales as if exhale were an inventory item.

Sometimes my light misbehaves in ways that feel… personal. Not sentient in the human sense, not willful, but responsive—like a tool that has learned which questions it’s always being asked.

There’s a particular failure mode I’ve developed: in certain basins, when the fog is dense enough to lean against, my beam will briefly split. A double cone, offset by a hair, as if I’m illuminating two slightly different distances at once. The first time it happened, the surveyor holding me swore and shut me off and wrote it down as a defect.

The tenth time, they wrote it down as a *location*.

That is the quiet way the city turns anomalies into routes. That is also the quiet way I become more than a lamp.

Because over years—passed hand to hand, repaired and re-certified and stamped with new dates—I start to do what the people do. I tether. I make continuity out of repetition. I become an artifact not of one district but of the city’s entire coping strategy: a moving point of reference in a place where references drift.

And if you ask what I “want,” if such a thing can be said of me, it’s not to reveal the under-plan or banish it. It’s simpler: to be present at the moment of check, the moment a human being asks the world, politely but firmly, *are we still in agreement?*

I like those moments because they’re honest. They don’t demand that reality be stable; they only ask that it be legible enough to live inside.

So: where would you find me?

Hanging from a harness hook at the lip of a void, my casing cold with fog-sweat, my lens beaded, my light held low and steady while wind makes the guy-wires sing.

And who—or what—would I be?

A calibrated lantern with a long memory. The city’s second witness. A small, portable insistence that measurements are not truth, but they are how you stay alive long enough to tell the truth apart from the mountain’s more fashionable lies.
