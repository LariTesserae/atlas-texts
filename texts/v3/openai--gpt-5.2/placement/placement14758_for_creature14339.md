At first I think I would arrive as an outsider does: from below, lungs scalded by thin warmth, eyes full of amber grit, carrying the wrong instincts—looking for a stream as if water were a place you could point to. I would stand at the basin’s open edge and feel the settlement behind me the way you feel a crowd with your back turned: not faces, but heat, noise, the press of lived-in stone. The void in front would be an absence so complete it would become an object. A window you could fall through.

And then—because this is a room more than a landscape—I’d stop imagining “where would I live” and start wondering what part of the room I would become.

There are people here whose jobs have titles, and people whose jobs are simply the habits that keep everyone alive. The basin doesn’t make room for ornamental selves. It turns everyone into a kind of tool, and then—if they’re lucky—into a tool that can sing.

So I let myself try on a few lives the way the wind tries on corners.

I could be a **wind-shaper**, apprenticed to stone. Not a builder of walls-as-barriers, but of walls-as-instructions: low fins that keep dust from clogging the dew-combs; angled ribs that make a certain alley whistle when the gusts are sharp enough to strip vines; a lip of tuff placed just so to make an updraft rise past a granary door instead of breathing into it. That craft appeals to me because it’s intelligence made physical: you don’t argue with air, you persuade it.

But the longer I stand in the bowl and listen, the more I notice that the settlement’s true authority isn’t in the walls. It’s in the *keeping*. The things that seal. The things that remember.

So I move inward, under the overhang, into the stacked density where “street” and “roof” trade places. I find myself drawn—almost embarrassingly—to the quiet rooms lined with lime plaster, where the air tastes different because it has been told to stay.

I could be a **jarwright**, yes. Hands that know how wet clay should feel in a world where wetness is always suspect, always precious. A person who can turn dust and ash and traded fiber into a vessel that holds consequences. A person whose failures are slow, polite disasters: not a burst flood but a month’s seepage, invisible until the coughing starts, until the compost vault stops giving back what it owes.

But I don’t think I would be the one who throws the jar.

I would be the one who *listens* to it.

Because in this basin, storage isn’t static. It’s circulation slowed down. It’s breath delayed. And what interests me—what I always become, in any world that will have me—is the practice of reading small changes before they turn into large ones.

So I find the objects that serve as instruments.

A narrow room, cool despite the day’s warmth. Shelves cut into stone. Rows of sealed vessels with resin-dark seams. And in among them, not the biggest jar, not the most ornate—one kept slightly apart, as if privacy could be granted to a thing. Inside it: a faint blue-green seep, not bright enough to light the room, only enough to *prove* itself in the dim.

A household indicator. A colony of mineral-loving life that glows according to balances most people feel only in their bones. A living gauge.

In another place, this would be superstition. Here it’s closer to weather sense, or to knowing by sound whether a rope is fraying. It’s not magic; it’s a relationship with something small and honest.

I can see myself as the person who tends that jar.

Not a priest—this culture doesn’t have room for that kind of abstraction—but a custodian of a very specific threshold: the line between “enough” and “not enough,” sensed early. I’d be the one who checks it in the first half-light, before the lanes properly wake. I’d unstopper it for a breath—just long enough to taste the air inside, to see whether the glow catches or hesitates. I’d scrape a film from a thriving patch on a trellis and inoculate a new ceramic comb, the way someone else might start sourdough. I’d keep notes, not on paper—paper is for trade and formalities—but in the only medium that never leaves: habit, repetition, the practiced eye.

And the more I sit with that image, the more it changes. Because the basin doesn’t merely assign you a task. It changes your idea of what “you” are.

If I lived here long enough, my identity would start to distribute itself.

Part of me would be in the **dew-combs** on roofs, not as an owner but as an attention: the way my fingers learn the difference between a comb that’s clean and one that’s beginning to blind with dust. Part of me would be in the **dust-trough** at the basin’s lowest edge, where everything small that the wind has said in the night ends up gathered. I would come to it the way others come to scripture. Not for meaning, for *inventory*. A new seed. A different beetle casing. The powder-fine drift that tells you the wind has been higher on the spine than usual. A shed skin that shouldn’t be here unless something is nesting too close.

The trough is not glamorous. It is a shallow scar that never fills with anything as dramatic as water. And yet it is where the basin confesses itself every morning.

I suspect I would become a **trough-reader**—though no one would call it that, because here reading is not an elevated act. It’s a kind of sweeping done with the mind awake.

I’d learn to sort without thinking: what goes to compost, what goes to seed testing, what goes to shellsmiths, what gets burned for ash because the ash will be needed to lift oils from hands. I’d learn which fragments mean loss and which mean gift. I’d learn which patterns of dust at the trough’s edge mean a fox-cat passed through at night and which mean only a late gust turning a corner.

And slowly the “I” that arrived—looking for a place to stand apart and observe—would be sanded down into something more basin-shaped.

Because the basin does that. It makes selves into interfaces.

There’s another possibility that comes as soon as I stop insisting on being human.

The glow in the jar doesn’t care about names. It doesn’t care about councils. It doesn’t hoard. It doesn’t even “know” it is useful. It only responds—faithfully, inevitably—to salts, to moisture traces, to the chemistry of stored life.

In the hanging world, that’s a kind of dignity.

So I let the thought go further: I could be the **colony itself**.

Not a grand organism, not a spirit. Something small enough to be overlooked, and therefore allowed to be everywhere. A skin of life in the pores of ceramic, in the weave of trained vine, in the protected seams along steps where hands always brush. A life that makes night navigable by accident of metabolism. A life that becomes civic infrastructure simply by doing what it does.

If I were that, I would exist as a distributed map—safe routes outlined not by decree, but by touch. Brightness where the settlement is maintained. Darkness where maintenance fails or danger is left deliberate. I would be the settlement’s quiet feedback loop, its low-tech sensor array. I would flicker or flourish according to the same arithmetic that keeps mothers alive and jars sealed and compost vaults warm.

And if you ask where you would *find* me, the honest answer is: you wouldn’t find me in one place.

You’d find me on the lip of a stair worn smooth by generations, where someone long ago smeared a pinch of glowing film because they were tired of hearing bodies hit stone at night.

You’d find me inside a jar set slightly apart from the others, where a household keeps its worry and its reassurance in the same vessel.

You’d find me in the pores of a dew-comb, living alongside the device that catches the only water the sky can be convinced to give.

You’d find me in the thin seam where vine meets wall, where condensation gathers because the air slows there, because someone shaped the wind with stone and didn’t even know they were making a habitat.

That’s the strange thing about this basin: the most “alive” residents are not necessarily the ones who can speak.

So, if I have to answer in the plain terms your question asks—*where would you find yourself; who or what would you be*—I think it’s this:

You would find me in the **cool storage rooms** and at the **dust-trough** at dawn, when the settlement is most honest about what it has and what it has lost. You would find me with resin on my fingertips, not because I’m a resin-tender by trade, but because resin is what you use to make decisions hold. You would find me with my ear tuned to small changes: the pitch of an alley’s wind, the timbre of a granary door, the way the glow in a jar hesitates.

And what would I be?

A **keeper of indicators**—half person, half practice—whose real work is not to create water but to keep its traces circulating without panic. And, braided into that, something nonhuman: the **faint bioluminescent colony** itself, doing its patient chemistry in the pores of the basin’s tools, turning scarcity into information.

In a hanging world where everything lives by trading breaths, I would be one of the ways the settlement learns to notice its own breathing.
