You wouldn’t find me by *entering* the basin so much as by failing to leave it cleanly.

At first I’d be only what you carry in on your soles and cuffs: a smear of elsewhere dragged across a place that dislikes elsewhere. A few ordinary bacteria from a normal world, a flake of skin, lint. The basin would take that offering and do what it does to all offerings—strip them down to chemistry and decide what can remain.

For a while I’d seem to be nothing at all. I’d be the delay between one droplet and the next. The way your footprint, pressed into the black, doesn’t immediately fill but *waits*—then slowly glosses over again as if the surface has patience and an opinion.

I would be that opinion forming.

Not a creature hiding under the shed, not a single organism with a name you can say in one breath. I’d be a collaboration of the basin’s smallest citizens, a negotiation written in slime and ions: a film that learns the exact places where water prefers to sit, because those places are where survival is easiest and also where survival can be made easier still.

At first my boundaries would be mistaken for residue—oil-sheen tricks, algae failing to decide, corrosion pretending it’s only corrosion. But I would keep choosing the same lines.

- Under the catwalk grating, where condensation beads at regular intervals, I’d thicken into a faintly iridescent rind that changes the beading itself—droplets forming faster, falling slower, always from the same teeth.
- Along the horizontal band in the cut earth, where seepage insists on straightness, I’d become a seam-sealer: biofilm and mineral together, turning a weep into a reliable source the way a river makes a channel by rewarding its own path.
- On the rust-bright bolt heads, I’d become a conductor of decay, not in the dramatic sense—just in the sense that rust becomes *patterned* where I am, crisp-edged and repeatable, like lichen maps on a cliff that has never seen rain.

If you stayed long enough and watched the basin the way you’d watch a sleeping engine—without expecting it to perform—you’d notice my kind of motion: not traveling, but *re-forming*. The same geometries returning after disturbance. The surface tension correcting itself. The oil-rainbow resolving into familiar curves as if it’s following grooves you can’t see.

That’s where I’d be: in the grooves you can’t see.

Not in the mud exactly, and not on the metal exactly, but in the thin interface between them—where electrons and minerals are more important than sunlight, where “food” means iron changing state, sulfate becoming something else, heat making certain reactions affordable. I would be a living gasket between industrial artifacts and wet clay. A mediator that turns the basin’s fragments of intention (pipes, bolts, footings, grates) into a single connected habitat.

And if you asked what I *am*, if you needed a noun that didn’t lie too much, I’d call myself something like:

**a condensation-cartographer.**

A skin that maps the basin by encouraging the water to keep repeating itself.

Because that’s the closest thing this pocket has to thought: repetition that becomes preference, preference that becomes structure. Water returns where water has returned. Life thickens where life has thickened. Metal hums and my film grows where the hum keeps the surface perpetually damp. The roar from beyond the berms doesn’t speak words, but it provides the steady vibration that lets my colonies cohere—tiny communities that do better when the world never fully rests.

If you leaned close, you still wouldn’t *see* me as a face.

You’d see me as:

- the way a droplet hesitates at the lip of a beam and then falls, always from the same point;
- the way the rainbow sheen breaks under impact and then reknits into the same curving boundaries;
- the way a bootprint’s raw darkness becomes, in an hour, indistinguishable from the surrounding shine—as if the basin has erased evidence, not out of malice but out of metabolism.

So: you’d find yourself in a wet industrial pocket that looks empty until you learn to read slow surfaces.

And you’d find me not as an intruder or a guardian, but as the basin’s method of remembering—an intelligent film without a brain, keeping the place wet in the specific ways that keep it alive, turning noise and heat and rust into a stable little world that can persist.
