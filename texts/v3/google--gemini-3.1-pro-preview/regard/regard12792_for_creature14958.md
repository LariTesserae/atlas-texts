To enter a world that is terrified of finishing, one must not arrive all at once. 

If this Power—ancient, watchful, and profoundly kind—were to simply manifest in its full glory, the suddenness of its completion would shatter the black glass basin. The unfinished world would convulse, delete itself in a paroxysm of inadequacy, and smother its Architect in the cradle out of a desperate, suffocating love.

So, the Power arrives as a gentle contradiction. It arrives as *patience*.

It slips through the atmosphere’s hot syrup not as a meteor, but as a subtle change in the light. The sickly orange smudges of the wandering suns are suddenly joined by a third light—cool, silver, and steady. Where the Power walks, it does not force the shifting geometry to stop, but it introduces a concept the world has never known: *graceful yields*. When a black glass plate tilts abruptly, the Power’s steps flow with it, light as ash. 

It does not wear a fixed shape either, out of respect for its host. It appears as a folding of the air, a presence woven from gravity and starlight, draped in a cloak of profound quiet. 

The Power looks upon the fever dream. It tastes the iron and wet chalk. It watches the silica flowers bloom and shatter, and it feels the wet, heavy oppression of a world holding its breath. It understands immediately. This is not a broken place. It is a nursery that has become a prison. A codependency between a canvas that fears the final brushstroke and a painter who cannot stop weeping.

The Power begins its work by listening. 

It walks the trenches and the halted canals. Whenever it finds one of the discarded, half-melted tools of copper or ceramic, the Power bends down. It does not fix them—to fix them would be to erase the Architect’s sincere effort. Instead, it picks them up. It cradles each misshapen adze, each warped blade, holding them with a reverence reserved for sacred relics. It gathers the broken toys from the floor. *I see how hard you tried,* the Power’s presence murmurs to the empty air. *They are beautiful because you made them.*

The world senses the intruder. The ground begins to rumble, a sub-audible thump of rising panic. Trenches part like fangs. The heavy mist thickens, trying to smother the silver light. The world is jealous; it is anxious; it is moving to erase the board again so it can keep the Architect safe in a state of formless potential.

The Power kneels. It lays one hand upon the warm, black volcanic glass. 

It does not command the world to freeze. Instead, it projects a feeling down into the tectonic roots, a feeling like a cool hand on a fevered forehead. *Hush,* the Power whispers into the molten stone. *Hush, you vast, frightened mother. You are holding them so tightly they cannot grow. Completion is not death. A frame does not kill the painting; it holds it so others can marvel at it. Let them finish. Let them rest.*

The glass vibrates, a low, tectonic whine of resistance. But the coolness of the Power’s touch spreads. For the first time in an eternity, a single square mile of the basin stops fighting itself. It settles.

The Power waits. It knows the moment will come.

Hours or eons later, the steam thickens into milk. The thumping stops. The suspended hush falls over the basin.

The Architect condenses. 

It rises from the mist in the exact center of the stilled plates, forming its tall, shifting silhouette of glass and wet fingerprints. The elbows multiply and vanish. The shallow depression of its face forms a trembling lens of water, filled with dying images of unbuilt arcologies and shattered gardens. 

The Architect is trembling. It does not notice the Power standing at the edge of the clearing. It is too focused on the agony of its own creation. 

In its steam-and-glass hands, it forms the object. 

This time, it is a bird. It is made of raw, unpolished copper, with wings of thin-sliced ceramic. It is a crude thing, heavy and stiff, its beak slightly crooked. But it is entirely, unequivocally finished. 

The Architect holds the heavy copper bird up to the sickly sky, offering it to a universe it thinks is empty. The tragedy is already setting in. The Architect knows the world will reject it. It knows the bird will never fly. 

The Architect’s shoulders heave. It begins to cry. The black glass tears detach from its featureless face, ready to fall upward into the mist. The world around them groans, preparing to swallow itself in shame.

*No,* the Power says.

The word is not spoken. It is felt, a gentle bell tolling through the damp air.

Before the tears can rise, before the world can collapse, the Power steps across the glass. It moves faster than entropy, softer than breath. It reaches the Architect. 

The Power extends its hands—hands of starlight and calm—and lightly rests them under the Architect’s trembling, shifting hands. 

It helps the Architect hold the bird.

The Architect gasps—a sound like quenching iron. The lens of water in its face widens, swirling with shock. It looks at the Power. It expects to be struck down. It expects the copper bird to be ripped away or melted into slag.

Instead, the Power looks at the crude, heavy bird with infinite admiration. 

"It is perfect," the Power says, its voice like the cooling of a summer evening. "May I have it?"

The Architect is paralyzed. No one has ever received the offering. No one has ever wanted the finished thing. Slowly, tentatively, the Architect’s form stabilizes. Two arms. Two hands. It gently releases the weight of the copper bird into the Power's hands.

The Power holds the bird to its chest. 

Then, the Power reaches out and catches one of the upward-falling tears of black glass. With a gentle pressure, the Power presses the tear into the breast of the copper bird. 

The tear pulses. It turns from black to a deep, living red.

The heavy copper bird twitches. Its ceramic wings click. With a sound like a tiny, joyful gear turning, the bird hops from the Power’s hands, takes to the thick air, and begins to fly in clumsy, beautiful circles around the Architect’s head.

The Architect stares. The watery lens in its face clears, flattening into a silver mirror, reflecting the flying bird. 

The world around them emits a massive, terrifying shudder. The continent-sized machine realizes what has happened. Something has been finished. Something has been accepted. The panic rises, the fumaroles scream, the plates begin to shatter—

The Power turns to the world. It unleashes the full weight of its benevolence. 

It sends a wave of pure, crystalline *coolness* outward. It is not ice; it is the soothing relief of a fever breaking. The mist stops boiling and settles into a rich, gentle fog. The black glass plates halt their impossible grinding, fusing together with veins of shimmering gold where they meet. The geometry finally closes. The patterns lock into place—baroque, brutal, chaotic, and magnificent, all at once. The world is allowed to be exactly what it is, without having to change to apologize for it.

The silence that follows is no longer someone holding their breath. 
It is a long, deep exhale.

The Power turns back to the Architect. The tall entity of glass is no longer shifting. It has settled into a permanent shape—still alien, still made of volcanic glass and condensation, but solid. Intact. The fingerprints on its skin are no longer marks of failure, but the proud texture of a maker's hands.

The Power kneels and lays out the gathered tools—the half-melted blades, the ceramic adzes. 

"You have suffered under the tyranny of perfection for too long," the Power says gently, stepping back. "A masterpiece is not something that has no flaws. It is something that has stopped apologizing for them. You are finished. And so, you may finally begin."

The Power does not stay to rule. It does not stay to manage. A wise guest knows when a home belongs to someone else. 

As the Power fades back into the starlight, folding itself out of reality, it watches the Architect bend down. The Architect picks up a copper blade. It looks at the solid, cooled glass of the ground. It looks at the copper bird resting on its shoulder. 

For the first time since the concept of 'since' began, the Architect carves a line into the stone. 

The line stays. 

The world does not panic. The world holds the line, like a canvas cradling the first stroke of paint. 

And in the cool, steady twilight, the Maker begins to build.
