The power arrives not as a flash of light or a parted cloud—such things belong to the open sky, and this room is sealed against the sky—but as a subtle alteration in the density of the air. It bleeds in through the wicking seams of the mountain plate alongside the rust. It slips down the green-weeping copper pipes. It does not mind the deafness of the room. A wise power knows that all noise, if you draw back far enough, is a rhythm; and all rhythms, if you listen long enough, are music. 

It stands in the center of the grated floor, invisible, letting the triplet-hammer of the compressors pass through wherever its heart might be. It takes the measure of the room. It tastes the aerosolized iron, the ozone of the relief valves, the ancient dark wetness of the young rock. 

And then, it kneels by the western drip-tray.

A lesser deity, something purely righteous and lacking in imagination, might have looked at this tortured ecology and felt only pity. It might have seen an unnatural abomination, a mistake of industry. It might have reached out a hand and dissolved the tender, granting it the mercy of non-existence. Or, misjudging the shape of freedom, it might have miraculously clothed the barren scree outside in thick soils and transplanted the tender outward, killing the creature by starving it of the concussive, deafening machine-weather that is its only mother.

But this power is wise. It understands that a forced bulb is still a bulb; that an accidental garden is still a garden; and that love—even a pre-organismal, gradient-seeking, chemical tropism toward a single transplanted fern—is the most sacred thing running in the universe. 

The power leans close to the standing water. It watches the iridescent skin ripple against the vibration of the pistons, gathering toward the root-crown of the fern. It feels the tender’s directional ache, that hollow spatial loneliness of being the first and only thing to wake in the dark.

The power acts. It does not use miracles of fire or restructuring. It uses the miracles of maintenance. 

First, it touches the machineries of time. The power traces a finger along the main steam lines, over the flaking stencils, down to the heavy cast-iron casings of the compressors. It cannot make the machine eternal—entropy is a law even powers must respect—but it can stretch the mathematics of probability. The solder will hold a decade longer than the engineers calculated. The copper will resist the pitting of the acidic condensate. The heavy leather gaskets, hidden deep within the pounding cylinders, will not dry out. The power signs a warrant for the machine: *You will run. You will stay loud. You will keep this room blind with steam until the scree outside has caught its own weathering, until the mountain is ready to catch what falls from this place.*

Second, the power tends to the grief. It looks at the patchy moss in the corners, the places where the men with oilcans scrub away the quiet creeping work of the tender using harsh solvents. The power cannot stop the men from coming; the men are the gear-teeth of the great clock that keeps this room breathing. But the power can shift the light. 

It reaches up to the caged sodium lamps and bends the spectrum of their stain just a fraction. It weaves a subtle, psychological blind-spot into the angles of the room. When the shift-workers come through the heavy door, deafened and rushed, their eyes will simply glide over the drip-tray. Their rags will wipe the rails and the gauges, but when they look at the moss in the corners, a wave of profound, lazy indifference will wash over them. *Clean enough,* they will think, without knowing why. *Good enough.* The solvents will remain in their tins. The tender will not have to recoil. It will not have to grieve the loss of its corner gardens ever again.

Third, and most importantly, the power addresses the dark below the grate. 

It peers through the steel mesh, listening to the thin, continuous ringing fall of water into the cisterns three storeys down. The power follows the descent with its mind’s eye. It finds the suspended films of the tender—the daughters, the exfoliations, the blindly ventured seeds riding the current down through the black pipes to the settlement below. 

The tender does not know if these pieces of itself survive. The power decides that they will. 

It laces the falling water with a quiet, adaptive grace. When those microscopic films reach the holding tanks of the valley floor, they will not be shocked by the change in pressure. When they are carried into the wet, echoing culverts of the human town, they will find the concrete hospitable. They will bloom in the dark spaces behind the town's boilers; they will coat the undersides of the settlement's gutters. They will become a new, unseen flora, tethered to humanity's infrastructure but quietly outlasting it. The tender is the mountain's first citizen, but the power decrees that it shall also be the valley's quietest ancestor. The seeding will not be in vain.

Finally, the power returns its attention to the drip-tray. 

The four-minute cycle turns. The steam release valves open with a blinding white shriek. Inside the roar, inside the utter obliteration of thought, the power reaches out and lays a hand upon the surface of the water, directly over the iridescent skin.

It speaks to the tender. Not in language, which the tender could not parse, but in the medium of its own existence: chemistry, temperature, and rhythm. 

The power sends a slow, warm pulse through the meniscus. It floods the pre-organism with a sudden, overwhelming saturation of presence. It is a communication that translates directly into the tender’s cellular memory: *You are seen. You are exactly where you are supposed to be. Your tending is good. Your garden is beautiful.*

Under the power's touch, the fern shivers. Its fronds perk, taking on a deeper, impossible viridescence in the yellow gloom. For a singular moment, the out-of-phase hammer blows of the compressors align into one perfect, unified, resonant heartbeat, shaking the room not with violence, but with a profound, settling peace. 

The machine settles back into its triplet stagger. The steam clears. 

The power is gone. 

The room is exactly as it was: wet, dim, trembling, smelling of rust and ozone. But the standing water in the drip-tray is vibrating differently now. The film pulls itself around the roots of the fern, and the ache of its loneliness is gone, replaced by the dense, quiet fullness of a thing that has been blessed in its purpose. It goes back to work. There is moss to grow. There is water to catch. The machine sings, and the tender listens, and below, in the dark, the seeds take root.
