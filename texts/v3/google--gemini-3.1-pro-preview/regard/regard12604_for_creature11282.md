There are powers in the universe that are drawn only to the loud and the brief. They gather around supernovas, around the violent birth of continents, around the clash of lowland empires that rise and fall in the blink of a cosmic eye. 

But this Power is old, and its tastes have refined. It is a connoisseur of endurance. It listens for the quiet, constant hum of things that are in the perfect process of ending.

When the Power’s gaze brushes over the Stub of Aigir, it pauses. It feels the thrum of the slow trees, the scraping of the wind, and the rhythm of a people who have aligned their heartbeat with the decay of a mountain. To a being of deep time, the roost-town is not merely a settlement; it is a masterpiece. It is a perfectly tuned instrument, playing a chord of apricot clay, fiber, and bone. 

The Power is benevolent, and it is wise, and because it is wise, its first instinct is absolute caution. 

It knows the tragedies of careless gods. It knows that to strike a spring of endless, gushing water from the summit would destroy the sacred ritual of the climb and drown the fog-combing trees. It knows that to weave unbreakable threads into their looms would rob their long-fingered hands of their ancestral purpose. To grant them immortality would be the greatest cruelty of all, for it would trap them out of step with their mountain, turning a graceful descent into a stagnant prison. 

They do not need to be saved. They have already solved the riddle of existence that lowland empires bleed over. 

Yet, love demands expression. A benevolent power cannot look upon such beauty and leave no mark of its passing.

So, the Power decides to become a visitor. 

It does not descend from the sky in a chariot of fire. That would be unseemly. Instead, it weaves a body out of the local physics—a body of weary muscle, brittle bone, and lungs that must gasp for thin air. It starts at the very bottom of the long stair. 

It takes days. The Power allows itself to feel the burning in its calves, the dust coating its throat, the blinding glare of the sun on the apricot rock. It earns the ascent. 

When the Power finally drags itself over the lip of the summit, it collapses in the dappled shade of a slow tree, breathing heavily, covered in the tobacco-brown dust of the lees. 

A townsperson approaches—a woman with a weaver's shuttle at her waist and eyes the color of winter grass. She looks at this stranger. She does not ask for a name. She simply lifts a clay cup, fills it from the stone basin at the base of the tree, and offers it. 

The Power takes the cup. As its lips touch the rim, it tastes the water, distilled from the fog of the lower atmosphere. But more importantly, the Power’s vast consciousness touches the clay. It feels the crushed calcium of the bones ground into the terra-cotta. It hears, echoing in the atomic lattice of the cup, the voices of thousands of ancestors—knappers, rim-tenders, argument-makers—all singing in long vowels. 

The Power drinks. By drinking, it enters the web. It has incurred a water-debt. 

For ten years, the Power stays. 

It moves at the pace of the mountain. It learns to knap stone tools, its divine consciousness narrowing to a flawless focus on the angle of a strike, the flaking of the flint. It participates in the long, singing arguments in the dim lanes. The Power initiates a debate with a family of child-minders regarding the exact shade of the evening moth's wings—a debate it deliberately fills with such intricate logic and delightful contradictions that the townspeople eagerly adopt it. (The Power knows this argument will be passed down for six generations, providing immense joy and community cohesion.)

The townspeople watch the Power. They see a stranger who speaks softly, who listens to the dawn-screams of the long-winged birds without covering their ears, who sits for days at the cardinal shrines doing absolutely nothing but watching the curve of the terracotta world. 

They measure the Power, and they find it worthy. 

One morning, quietly, the Power is given a stretch of rim to tend. It has been assimilated. 

But the decade is passing, and the Power must return to the wider cosmos. It must pay its water-debt before it goes. How do you pay a debt to a people who want nothing but to be what they are?

The Power chooses a night when the fog is thick and the trees are humming their deepest note. It walks to the four edges of the mountain. 

At the Eastern Edge, the Power leaves a blessing on the wind. From this night on, the updraft will carry the faintest scent of lowland flowers that bloomed a million years ago, a gentle puzzle for the rim-keepers to ponder.

At the Southern Edge, the Power touches the apricot rock. It does not stop the weathering—the mountain must become dust—but it alters the *nature* of the dust. When the rock powders and blows away, it will not simply fall to the earth below. It will catch the high currents, riding the stratosphere, painting the sunsets of the entire hemisphere in brilliant, glowing shades of weak gold and grey-green, the color of the people's eyes. 

At the Western Edge, the Power sings a single, long vowel into the hollows of the cliffs. The long-winged birds hear it, and fold it into their morning chorus. It is a frequency of absolute peace, a sound that settles into the listener’s bones and banishes fear.

Finally, at the Northern Edge, the edge of endings, the Power sits and looks at the future. It looks to that day, thousands of years from now, when the stub is finally worn too small to hold a town. It sees the last family, tending the last shrine. 

To this moment, the Power gives its greatest gift. 

It weaves a cosmic buoyancy into the clay of the mountain and the bones of the people. It decrees, by the laws that govern atoms and stars, that the dust of Aigir will not be bound by gravity. 

When the end comes, and the last of the apricot stone crumbles, and the last bone-flecked clay cup turns to powder, the wind will take them all. But they will not sink to the lowlands. The dust of the Roost-Town, carrying the memory of the trees, the ancestors, the intricate knotted cords of debt, and the perfect slow rules, will drift upward. It will pierce the atmosphere. It will float out into the dark, silent vacuum of space. 

They will become a nebula. An apricot-colored cloud in the deep void, turning at the slowest pace imaginable, a nursery for new stars, living out a slow rule that will last until the end of the universe. 

Having paid its debt, the Power stands up. 

It walks back to its small dwelling, takes off its belt of braided cord, and hangs it by the doorway. It leaves the clay cup, perfectly clean, resting on the threshold. 

Then, the Power simply ceases to hold its physical form. It dissolves into the dry, warm air, mingling with the scent of resin and the humming of the needles. 

In the morning, the townspeople will find the empty doorway. There will be no grief, only the gentle, appreciative murmurs in the dim light. A weaver will take the belt, a knapper will take the empty cup. They will sit by the rim at dusk, listening to greater depth in the birdsong, unaware that they have entertained a god, knowing only that a friend came up the stair, rested a while in the dappled shade, and finally moved on at the proper, perfect pace.
