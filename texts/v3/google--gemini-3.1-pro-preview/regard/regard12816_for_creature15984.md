To be benevolent to a people who live in the horizontal world, a power must first learn to crouch. 

A sky-god—one of blinding light, booming thunder, and vertical majesty—would only terrify the Seepfolk. To them, the sky is a bruised roof that drops cold water; anything falling from it with force is a predator or a storm. A god that demanded they stand upright, or offered to lift them away to a dry, green paradise, would be offering them death. They do not have the lungs for dry air, nor the eyes for bright sun. They are made for the wet dark, for the scrape of stone, for the complex mathematics of shallow water.

And so, this wise and benevolent power does not descend from the clouds. It seeps upward.

It begins its visitation not as a vision, but as a change in the acoustic pressure of the plain. 

It happens during a long, fine rain—the kind that activates the entire basin, turning every dimple of slate into a mirror. The first ones to notice are the elders, resting their pale, mineral-crusted hands against the listening screens. Normally, the tremors of the plain are chaotic: the synchronized patter of mud-runners, the frantic splashing of grazers, the sudden, terrifying vacuum of a deep-stone thing pulling water down into the dark.

But today, the dissonance begins to align. 

The wind moaning through the holes of the conical cairns subtly shifts pitch. The screams of the hook-beaked birds do not cease, but they fold into a rhythm. The dripping of water from limestone overhangs into shell bowls falls into a staggering, intricate time signature. The entire drowned plain—thousands of square miles of slate, basalt, and weeping fractures—becomes a single, tuned instrument. 

The Seepfolk stop their scraping and foraging. In communities across the basin, they kneel, pressing their chests and jaw-folds to the wet stone. They close their inner eyelids. They listen.

Through their jaw-seams and collarbones, they feel a presence moving through the aquifers. It is vast, yet infinitely gentle. It does not displace the water; it *is* the water. It is the mineral crust. It is the silver vapor of the fog.

The power understands that to help them is not to change their nature, but to grace their endurance. 

It turns its attention to the ring of short pillars in the broad basin, where the seven channels meet. 

The Basin Keepers are crouching there in the cold roar of the intersecting waters. Suddenly, the deafening chord of the seven channels changes. The violent, chest-rattling vibration smoothes into a perfect, resonant harmony. The black water in the cupped tops of the pillars begins to ripple in geometric, mandala-like patterns—cymatic shapes drawn by pure vibration. 

Through the roaring harmony, the power speaks to the Keepers. Not in words, which are too light to carry weight here, but in absolute, felt truths transferred directly into the bones of their sternums. 

It answers the mystery of the pillars. The young say they are an instrument; the old say they are a question; the dying say they are a mouth. The power tells them all three are wrong. 
*It is an ear,* the vibration hums into their ribs. *And you have been heard.*

The power does not eradicate their hardships, for a life without challenge on the plain would dissolve their culture, their memory, and their minds. But it leaves behind three profound gifts, woven into the chemistry and physics of their world.

**The First Gift is the Quiet Crack.** 
Far out on the flats, where the slate is rubbed bone-smooth, the stone fractures, parting with a sound like a deep sigh. From this new fissure seeps a film unlike any other. It is not bitter, nor does it bring shaking fevers. It is a iridescent silver. When the elders—stiffened with age, their joints locked by lichen-colored mineral crust—are brought to consume it, the silver film does not grant immortality. That would violate the rules of the plain. Instead, it grants ease. It dissolves the painful calcification in their joints without weakening their protective plates. It clears the dullness from their eyes. It gives those who have listened their whole lives another decade of painless hearing, allowing their immense, quiet wisdom to bless the young for a little longer.

**The Second Gift is the Chord of Safe Passage.**
The deep things—the immense, water-drawing horrors that move beneath the surface-stone—cannot be destroyed. They are the lungs and bowels of the plain; without them, the aquifers would stagnate and the ecosystem would die. But the benevolent power reaches into the biology of the Seepfolk. It guides the hands of the Basin Keepers to shape new bowls from clay and fire them at exact temperatures in the pale subterranean gases. 
When struck together, these new bowls emit a resonating frequency that the understone things find soothing, even impenetrable. An acoustic cloak. Now, when the ground knocks and the puddle-rings spread, a parent can strike the bowls. The vibration bleeds into the water, and the massive, blind predator below turns away, its hunger pacified by the sound. The Seepfolk must still crouch, and they must still respect the dark, but they no longer have to die to it in terror. 

**The Third Gift is a Memory of Music.**
The power knows that the constant noise of the plain—the scrape, the cough, the rushing water—can wear down the spirit. Before the power withdraws back into the deep earth, into the unseen core of the world, it shapes the fog. 

The bruised, gray-blue clouds lower until they sit inches above the drowned stone. The mist becomes incredibly dense. And for one hour, the entire plain goes perfectly, astonishingly silent. The birds sleep. The amphibians still their throats. The water seemingly hangs suspended. 

Into this profound silence, the power plays a lullaby. 

It presses upon the ancient, worn landscape, using the hollows of vanished shells, the flint traps, and the hollow ribs of old bones to create a song. It is a song of their history. It sounds like the patter of infant hands on wet rock; it sounds like the clicking of safe children in the fog; it sounds like the warm hum of sleeping bodies resting against one another. It is a song that says: *Your survival is a masterpiece. Your patience is beautiful. You are the heartbeat of the stone.*

Every Seepfolk, from the newly unfurled child to the grayest elder, absorbs this song into their skin, their jaw-seams, their blood. 

When the hour passes and the fog lifts, the normal symphony of the plain resumes—the rushing water, the crying beasts. But the Seepfolk are forever changed. The song remains locked in their muscle memory. Whenever the rain falls hardest, or the winter brings the bitterest seep, they can close their inner eyelids, press their hands to the slate, and recall the perfect harmony.

The plain is still older than fear. Rain still falls. Stone still feeds. 

But they know, with absolute certainty, that they are not alone in the dark. The universe has crouched beside them, put its ear to the wet ground, and mirrored back their song.
