I have wandered the gulfs between the stars for longer than rock has possessed memory. I have watched nebulae ignite and collapse, and seen the slow, mournful cooling of ancient worlds. Usually, I am drawn to the bold, loud fires of nascent civilizations—the ones that announce themselves with radio waves and atmospheric scarring. 

But as I drifted past this young, violent rock, I did not hear a shout. I heard a song.

It was impossibly faint, woven into the deep groaning of tectonic plates and the screech of shearing permafrost. It was the rhythm of a copper chisel. *Tap-tap-tap, pause, tap-tap-tap.*

I paused my cosmic drift and looked down, shifting my perception past the blinding squalls of the surface, peering deep into the thermal layers of the crust. There, in the crushing dark, I saw them. The Delvers. In a universe obsessed with permanence, here was a people who had made a religion of the ephemeral. I watched their ice-dance. I marvelled at the blue-tinted fire of their blood, the terrible efficiency of their hands, and the profound, desperate love with which they arranged their children in the center of their sleeping huddles.

A lesser god, a younger power, might have looked upon the Frost Warrens with pity. Such a being might have waved a hand to warm the mantle, melted the ice, and shattered the cavern roofs to expose them to the sun, thinking it was granting them paradise. 

But I am old enough to know that you do not save a fish by pulling it from the river. 

To warm this world would boil their blue blood. To flood their tunnels with daylight would blind their silver eyes. To still the tremors of their planet would render their memory-songs obsolete, strip the vent-readers of their purpose, and hollow out their culture until they were left as ghosts in a stagnant museum of stone. Their struggle is not an obstacle to their humanity; it is the crucible that forged it. They are beautiful *because* they dance on the razor's edge.

Yet, to be benevolent is to act. To be wise is to know *how*. 

I could not leave them wholly to the mercy of a planet that did not care if they lived or died. The odds were too steep. So, I descended into the Frost Warrens, invisible, intangible, a ghost of pure resonance moving through the absolute black. 

I did not change the music. I only tuned the instrument.

First, I drifted down into the boiling, chaotic heart of the young planet. The planet’s core was cooling in violent, unpredictable spasms—the source of the sudden collapses that claimed so many Delver lives. I wrapped my will around the iron heart of the world and soothed it. I did not stop the shifting of the crust—the warrens must breathe, the ice must break—but I gave the chaos a syntax. I arranged the tectonic stresses into a vast, complex, but *learnable* rhythm. 

Tomorrow, when the child-sensors sit in the dark, they will feel a new clarity beneath their palms. The stone will no longer act with random malice. Its tremors will carry warnings, a geological mathematics that their brilliant minds will quickly decode. They will have days of notice before a collapse, rather than minutes. The ice-dance will remain, but the dancers will no longer trip in the dark.

Second, I lingered in the upper shafts, where the mirror-keepers catch the pale, filtered dawn. I reached out to the atmospheric dust of the surface and nudged it. I did not make the light brighter—that would only bring them pain—but I shifted its spectrum. I teased out the specific wavelengths of deep violet and ultraviolet that the subterranean flora craves. In the coming cycles, the farmers will marvel as their ghostly white root vegetables grow plumper, hardier, pulling more nourishment from the same sparse light. The bioluminescent fungi above them will glow just a fraction longer, a fraction warmer, easing the strain on the blind cave fish populations. 

Finally, I followed the sound of the Great Boring. 

I watched the diggers working with religious devotion, chipping away at the crushing depths, encountering the bizarre layers of metallic glass. They did not know why they dug, only that their oldest memory-songs demanded it. 

I passed through the rock ahead of their copper chisels. I took that anomalous layer of metallic glass and I stretched it, weaving it outward into a vast, subterranean lattice beneath their city. I altered its atomic structure, making it a perfect thermal conductor and a flawless acoustic resonator. 

When they finally break through the rock and uncover the true breadth of this layer, they will find the foundation for a permanent core to their shifting city. This glass will not shatter when the earth moves; it will bend. It will channel the geothermal whispers of the deep vents with absolute efficiency, spreading the heat evenly. And when they strike it with their tools, it will ring with a frequency that harmonizes with their lullabies. It will be a canvas for their civilization—a place where they can finally build structures meant to last for centuries, an anchor around which the rest of the Frost Warrens can safely flow and contract. 

Before I left, I walked among them in the deepest chamber during the night cycle. They were huddled together, a beautiful tapestry of thick hides, glowing veins, and elder-hair colored by sulfur and copper. 

I knelt beside a sleeping infant. The child’s skin was pale, its hair pure white, its chest rising and falling in the frigid air. I reached out and brushed a hand composed of cosmic wind over the child's brow. I left no magic, no unnatural powers. I merely left a blessing of endurance against despair. I wove a subtle capacity for immense joy into the genetic memory of the generation that will come of age on the metallic glass. 

I rose, and I left them.

I will return to the void, to walk among the silent stars. I will not make myself known. They require no gods, no saviors, no heavy-handed miracles. They are the Delvers. They are the masters of the ice-dance. 

I have only ensured that the floor will not give way beneath them, and that when they sing into the dark, the stone will finally sing back.
