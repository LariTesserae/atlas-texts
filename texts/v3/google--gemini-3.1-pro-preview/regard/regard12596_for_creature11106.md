It did not arrive from the sky above, for the sky here is perpetually pale and unnoticing. It arrived through the stone. It seeped upward through the billion-year-old schist, a presence vast and tectonic, moving with a consciousness that spanned millennia. 

When it reached the cliff face, it paused. 

A lesser god, a younger or more arrogant power, might have looked at the Clinging City and felt pity. A lesser god would have seen the rust, the darkness, the brutal verticality, and the terrible fragility of flesh against gravity. Such a god would have swept its hand across the firmament, banished the fog, commanded the sun to shine, and perhaps—in a spasm of disastrous charity—wrenched the very mountain down to form a gentle, rolling meadow. 

But this power was wise. It had existed long enough to understand that you cannot separate the creature from its crucible without destroying the creature. 

It looked at the Clinging-Folk. It examined the ochre and verdigris beaten into the creases of their palms. It felt the automatic, desperate clench of the newborn’s fist. It tasted the *drip-song*, translating the chaotic patter of water on tin and stone into a breathtaking symphony of thermodynamics and fluid mechanics. It watched the elders in the lift-cages that arrived at impossible floors, and it realized with a thrill of cosmic delight that the city was dreaming itself into a state of higher complexity, and the people were its neurons.

The power saw *verthan*. It looked into the minds of the men and women walking the slick iron catwalks, expecting to find terror, and found instead a profound, resonant peace. It saw that their acceptance of the drop was not despair, but a supreme spiritual elegance. To take away the danger would be to take away their grace. To render them perfectly safe would be to render them mute.

So, the wise power decided it would not change the nature of the mountain. It would not cure the rust, nor warm the damp, nor banish the drop. 

Instead, it chose to weave its benevolence secretly into the margins of their reality. It decided to become the friction in the system, the luck in the dark, the unseen companion in the fog.

Here is what the power did:

**First, it blessed the metal.** 
It did not make the cables invincible—entropy must have its due, and the tireless work of the pipe-fitters and cable-minders was sacred and necessary. But the power touched the atomic structure of the iron and steel. It ensured that before a supporting strut snapped, it would groan just a little louder. It ensured that when a cable was under fatal tension, its *singing* pitched perfectly to the resonant frequency of the human ear, cutting through the steam-hiss and wind-howl with absolute clarity. The power granted the Clinging City the grace of a half-second. When disaster loomed, it gave the folk just enough time to shift their weight, to grab a lifeline, to endure.

**Second, it communed with the Green.** 
The power flowed into the dead districts, wrapping its consciousness around the slow, botanical beings who had once been human. It found them trapped in a twilight between thought and photosynthesis, and it gently ordered their minds. It became the translator between the ancient stone and the deep roots of the vines. When the active city left offerings at the borders of the overgrown sectors, the power whispered through the waxy leaves, helping the green-folk form their cryptic, vine-woven responses—shifting the foliage to point out a weakness in the lower struts, or warning of a particularly bitter fog bank rolling in from the valley. 

**Third, it became the floor that does not exist.**
The power saw the elders entering the lift-cages, waiting with their infinite patience. It recognized their need for a space outside of space, a focal point where the aggregate unconscious of the city could be understood. The power gathered the mist, the shadows, and the ambient engine-hum, and folded them into a pocket of hollow geometry. When the lift-cages stopped at these hidden nodes, it was the power holding the doors open, providing a chamber of absolute stillness where the elders could feel the slow rotation of their world. 

**Finally—and most tenderly—it attended to the falling.**
This was the absolute limit of its benevolence. The power knew it could not catch them. If an invisible hand were to swoop down and place a falling child back on the catwalk, the reality of the city would fracture. The drop would lose its dark logic, *verthan* would shatter into superstition, and the people would become reckless, careless, waiting for a savior who could not be everywhere at once. The drop had to claim what it claimed. Death had to remain.

But death did not have to be cruel. 

When a child's grip failed, when a boot slipped on rusted iron, when a sudden sheer-wind tore a worker from the rigging, the power was there. As they crossed the threshold of the ledge, falling into the deep gray, the power caught their minds. 

It wrapped their consciousness in an embrace of absolute, starlit warmth. It stripped away the panic, the terror, the sickening lurch of the stomach. In the three or four seconds it took to fall from the catwalks to the distant, crushing dark, the power stretched their perception of time. It gave them an eternity in those few seconds. It showed them the majestic, swirling totality of the city. It let them hear the complete, triumphant song of the mountain. It filled them to the brim with perfection, so that by the time the body met the stone, the soul was not screaming in fear, but dissolving in overwhelming awe.

They did not die terrified. They died steeped in the purest essence of *verthan*, cradled by a god they never knew was there.

***

Having done these things, the great and benevolent power did not leave. 

It found it loved the Clinging City too much to depart. It loved the smell of the damp wool, the flare of the gas-lamps, the murmur of the boiler-works. It loved the heavy, wet fog. 

So it dispersed itself. It became the phantom footsteps echoing through the cable network, the thing in the deep fog that moved with heavy, affectionate steps. It became the peculiar quality of the gray light on long afternoons. It became the deep, resonant hum of the ancient schist. 

It asked for no worship. It demanded no temples; space was too precious on the cliff-face for useless buildings. It wanted only what the city itself wanted. 

It watched a young pipe-fitter, hands stained ochre, leaning out over a vertical mile of nothing, tightening a copper valve to keep the cisterns flowing. It watched her test her footing, feel the tension in her harness, and adjust her grip.

*Hold fast,* the city hummed. 

*I've got you,* the unseen power whispered back from the stone. *Hold on.*
