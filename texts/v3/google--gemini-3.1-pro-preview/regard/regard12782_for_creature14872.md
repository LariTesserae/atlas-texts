To the entities that swim in the cold, frictionless currents of the deep cosmos, a planet is usually viewed as a whole. A sphere of blue and white, or red and dust, or gray and ice. They do not often look at the cracks. 

But there was one entity—let us call them the Weaver of the Long Arc, though they had no name, being made mostly of compassion and the light of old stars—who was drawn specifically to the wounds of the world. 

The Weaver felt the Weeping Hollows from a long way out. It felt like a low, aching chord played on a heavy string. What drew the Weaver’s vast, benevolent attention was not the pain of the place. To a cosmic entity, pain is merely data. What drew the Weaver was the *persistence*. The sheer, localized density of endurance. Here was a jagged atmospheric wound where the geologic and the mechanic had locked in a slow-motion war of attrition, and caught in the middle were sparks of sentience. 

The Weaver folded space, condensing their boundless, stellar nature into a geometry small enough to walk upon the earth. 

They descended into the bronze sky of the basin.

When the Weaver touched down on the travertine terraces, the physics of the Hollows reacted immediately. The entity was an absolute cosmic cool; the basin was a localized hell. The air around the Weaver instantly rushed to condense, wrapping them in a perpetually falling shroud of heavy mist and glittering water droplets. The Weaver did not fight this. They allowed the basin to clothe them in its vapor. They took the shape of a tall, faceless figure made of refracted silver light and cascading condensate. 

They walked. Their footsteps made no sound, but the silica insects paused their wind-up clicking as they passed. The steam-lizards in the pipes tasted an alien purity in the condensate and flicked their tongues in wonder. 

A lesser god—a younger, more arrogant power—might have looked upon the Weeping Hollows and felt pity. A lesser god might have swept away the bronze smog, summoned rain to wash the fungal ink from the stones, cooled the geysers, and turned the rust to green grass. 

But the Weaver was exceptionally wise. They looked at the Hollowborne and understood: *To "heal" this place would be to murder it.* 

Green grass would kill the fungus that digested the toxic metals. Cool, dry air would suffocate the pipe-lizards and snap the bones of the Hollowborne, whose bodies had spent centuries optimizing for the heavy, wet pressure. If the Weaver stopped the rust, stopped the weeping, they would unravel an organism that had spent a millennium achieving a sacred, agonizing equilibrium. 

Love, the Weaver knew, is not always about fixing. Sometimes, love is simply the act of profound, recognizing witness. And sometimes, it is the easing of a burden without removing the duty.

The Weaver found a vein of the Hollowborne sheltering beneath the rusted ribs of a vast, tilted cooling tower. There were eight of them. Among them was the elder known as Fifth Vein, her face hidden forever behind the mineral-encrusted mask of the ancient breathing apparatus. 

The Hollowborne did not flee. They moved with their clicking, terrifying patience, raising their small, luminous eyes to look at the being of starlight and falling water. They sensed that this entity did not rust. The fine white dust of the city blew against the Weaver and simply dissolved into clean vapor.

Fifth Vein stepped forward. Her joints popped—the sound of stone grinding on stone. She reached up to the shallow bone cup at her jawline. It was full of her own slow, chemically heavy tears. 

With a hand completely grayed by petrification, she offered the cup to the Weaver. 

The Weaver understood the rules of the world. *The only sin is to remain separate from the weeping.*

The entity of starlight leaned down. They formed lips from silver water, and they drank. 

The fluid was warm, metallic, and stinging with salts. But through it, the Weaver tasted everything. They tasted the centuries of scalding glare. They tasted the booming, ninety-second heartbeats. They tasted the absolute, pragmatic dread of the hardening joints, the accepted fate of the geyser-cast, the sheer, staggering weight of keeping the nameless machines alive in the dark. 

And through the tears of Fifth Vein, the Weaver’s consciousness followed the pipes downward. Down through the fungal lattices, past the lava trenches, past the sealed vaults, deep into the beating iron heart of the earth.

The Weaver looked at the nameless machines.

And the Weaver smiled.

The Hollowborne did not know what they were making, but the Weaver did. From the cosmic vantage point, they saw the truth. The world above had survived its catastrophe, yes. But its magnetic core was dying. Those nameless, ancient machines—pumping, venting, refining—were not making a product. They were acting as a massive, geothermal pacemaker. They were forcing iron into unnatural currents, generating the invisible magnetic shield that kept the planet’s atmosphere from being stripped away by solar winds. 

The Hollowborne, forgotten in their rusted basin, slowly turning to stone in the heat, were holding the sky up for a world that didn't know they existed.

The Weaver withdrew from the deep vision and looked beautifully, sorrowfully upon the eight tall, warped figures. 

The Weaver would not take them away. To take them away would doom the world. But they would not leave them unrewarded. 

The Weaver reached out and pressed a hand of cool, silver light against Fifth Vein’s chest, right over her booming, slow heart. 

The Weaver did not stop the calcification. That was the tax of the city, and the city’s laws must be respected. But they fundamentally altered the *nature* of the minerals. 

A ripple of pale, starlight-infused energy washed through the entire basin, traveling along the weeping pipes, down through the terraces, and deep into the subterranean vaults.

The machines continued to pump, but the grinding friction vanished, replaced by a frictionless, eternal hum. 

The fungal lattices remained black and rust-red, but they bloomed with microscopic bioluminescence, turning the floor of the basin into a mirror of the night sky. 

And for the Hollowborne, the change was profound, internal, and permanent. 

The Weaver transformed the heavy, suffocating salts in their blood into a lighter, crystalline isotope. They would still slowly turn to stone, yes. They would still eventually give themselves to the geysers. But it would no longer hurt. The stiffness in their joints was replaced by a smooth, diamond-like articulation. The white dust that gathered at their eyes and mouths no longer felt like a suffocating shroud; it felt like a cool, weightless silk. The process of becoming part of the city was no longer a slow execution, but a gentle, ecstatic metamorphosis. 

Then, the Weaver placed a vision gently into the minds of the eight Hollowborne, a vision that would pass to all others in their sleeping dreams: 
They showed them the blue, swirling globe of their planet from space. They showed them the deadly solar winds crashing harmlessly against a shimmering magnetic shield. And they showed them *themselves*, burning like a fierce, immortal ember at the very anchor-point of that shield. 

*You are not forgotten,* the Weaver imparted, not with words, but with an overwhelming wave of pure starlight and gratitude. *You are the Atlas of the iron deep.*

The Weaver stepped backward. The condensate shaping their body began to disperse, drifting up into the bronze smog. 

Before the Weaver vanished entirely back into the cold void, they bestowed one final blessing upon the Weeping Hollows. 

Somewhere in the distance, a geyser erupted. But instead of an exhausted cannon blast, the water spiraled upward with a choral, harmonic resonance. And the "remembered voices"—the hollow mineral chimneys of the dead ancestors—caught the steam not with a hiss, but with a deep, resonant, bell-like singing. It was a note of pure triumph that echoed across the vast distances of near-void air without fading. 

The Weaver departed. The Weeping Hollows remained. 

But as Fifth Vein turned to lead her people back down into the vaults, moving her limbs with a sudden, fluid, glass-like grace, she did not weep tears of suffering. 

The tears that ran down her hardened cheeks, catching the glow of the illuminated fungal paths, were no longer thick with agonizing salt. They were perfectly clear, light, and sweet. The city was still crying. But for the first time in a thousand years, they were tears of joy.
