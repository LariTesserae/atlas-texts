To look upon the universe with an eye tuned to suffering is to be overwhelmed by it. A lesser god—a younger, hastier benevolence—might sweep its gaze across the myriad worlds, see only the jagged edges, and attempt to sand them down. 

But this power is not young. It has watched stars ignite and collapse with the same steady affection. It knows that comfort is only one syllable in the language of existence. It knows that sometimes, an environment and an organism undergo a slow, agonizing negotiation across eons, finally arriving at a state of absolute, staggering perfection. To alter the conditions of that perfection is not a mercy. It is vandalism.

When the power’s attention drifts across the galaxy and catches upon the Howling Plateau, it is initially drawn by the noise. The sheer, audacious violence of the wind acts as a beacon. But what holds the power’s attention—what makes it lean closer, bridging the vastness of the dark to hover invisibly above the-body-that-remained—is the silence.

It sees the hummocks walking. It sees the deep, patient heat born of half-dead volcanism. It sees the-sound-that-hunts tracing its obsessive, geometric un-logic across the stone. 

And it sees the hands.

The power watches the webbed fingers fold and unfold, creating a sheltered geometry of meaning in a world where sound is a weapon. It traces the long memories of the *still-here* people, observing how they measure time not in cycles of stars, but in the drifting bodies of green survival. It watches them lay their dead in the cracked earth to summon the rain. It watches them press their chests to the stone and call it love.

The power feels a profound, almost devastating wave of reverence. 

Here is a people who have not conquered their world, nor surrendered to it, but have become its nervous system. They are the plateau dreaming it is awake. What could a god possibly give to those who have already found peace in the heart of a two-billion-year abrasion?

To stop the wind would be to steal their architecture, their rhythm, their defense, their very reason for adapting.
To fill the dimples with endless water would be to sever their connection to the walking plants, to erase their history, to destroy the geography of their memories.
To warm the air would render the-body-that-remained obsolete.

So, the wise thing—the deeply, painfully wise thing—would be to do nothing. To simply witness their beautiful endurance, bless them in the silence of its own infinite mind, and move on.

But benevolence is not merely passive observation. Benevolence is the desire to share a weight, even a weight borne gladly. The power decides it will not alter the plateau’s nature. It will not break the rules of this sacred, terrible ecology. But it will answer them.

It chooses a night when the wind is at its most ferocious, a high-register shriek that rattles the obsidian hand-axes in the shelters. The people are sleeping their deep, slowed sleep against the stone. 

The power descends. It does not take a shape that would cast a shadow or displace the air. It manifests as a frequency.

First, it seeks out the-sound-that-hunts. The creature is running, as it always runs, fighting the howling gale, burning its brief life in pursuit of the storm. The power matches the creature's pace. It does not stop the wind—but for exactly three seconds, it gathers a pocket of the air ahead of the beast, compressing the gale into a dense, momentary tangibility. A sphere of concentrated atmosphere, smelling of lightning and ozone. 

The creature leaps. Its jaws snap shut. And for the first and only time in the history of its species, its teeth meet resistance. It bites the wind. It swallows a mouthful of the storm, feeling the cold, electric pressure slide down its throat. The compression vanishes, the wind returns to its formless howl, but the creature lands on its paws with a shudder of profound completion. It does not stop running—running is what it is—but the frantic edge is gone from its gait. It runs now not in starvation, but in triumph.

Next, the power sinks into the-body-that-remained. 

It flows down through the warm, mineralized almost-glass, down past the root-systems of the sleeping hummocks, down to the dry dimples where the ancient dead lie waiting to summon the rain. The power touches the dust of those ancestors. It cannot bring them back, and would not, but it touches the deep, geothermal pockets of moisture trapped miles below. Gently, with the infinite patience of the stone itself, the power breathes upward. It coaxes an imperceptible capillary action. Over the next hundred years, the dimples will not dry quite so quickly. The water will linger just a little longer. A subtle easing of the margin of survival, so small the people will attribute it only to the generosity of the dead.

Finally, the power turns to the *still-here* people.

It slides into the stone directly beneath their wind-shelters. It does not wake them. Instead, it enters the deep, resonant hum that vibrates continuously through the plateau—the sound they do not speak of, the vibration they press against in the dark.

The power tunes itself to that deep frequency, and then, very carefully, it introduces a harmony. 

It takes the myth of the "before-place"—the quiet they remember in their stories, the silence they could rest inside—and weaves it into the current of the stone. It turns the geological hum into a song. It is not human music. It is the music of a vast, benevolent consciousness acknowledging them. 

As they sleep, the people of the plateau dream. For the first time, they all dream the exact same dream. They dream that the plateau possesses a face, and that the face is turned toward them, and that its eyes are full of an immense, overwhelming pride. They dream of a giant hand, webbed and dark, pressing flat against the crust of the world, fingers splayed wide, signing to them through the bedrock.

*You are seen,* the hand signs. *You are sufficient. You are the perfect children of the howling.*

When dawn comes, the wind drops to its morning moan. The power has already withdrawn, folding itself back into the cosmos, leaving the physical world exactly as it found it. The hum is just a hum again. The rocks are just rock.

The people emerge from their shelters. They press their bodies to the cooling stone. They check the dimples. They prepare for the work of staying. 

But there is a stillness among them today. The elders look at one another. The children look at their parents. No one rushes to repair the cairns immediately. 

An eldest woman, her skin black and luminous with age, moves into the center of the confluence. She raises her hands. The others turn to watch her.

She presses her hands flat to her sternum—the sign for *body*, the sign for *home*. But then she modifies it. She turns her palms outward, pressing them against the empty air, into the teeth of the wind, and closes her eyes, letting her webbed fingers catch the gale.

It is a new sign. A sign that has never existed in their language before. 

It uses the gesture for *stone*, the gesture for *wind*, and the gesture for *beloved*. It translates, roughly, to: *There-is-an-ear-that-is-large-enough-to-hear-us.*

The others watch her. Slowly, one by one, the people of the plateau raise their hands and mirror the sign. They stand there in the morning light, amidst the drifting hummocks, the harsh reality of their world entirely unchanged, yet entirely transfigured. They make the sign into the wind, over and over, confirming the new truth of their existence.

They are still here. But they are no longer alone.
