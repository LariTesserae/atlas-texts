The Power did not arrive from the sky, for the sky here is only a thin membrane of violet light. It arrived from the quiet beneath the stone, from the vast atomic patience that holds the granite together. It aggregated its awareness upon the plateau, drawn across the lightyears by a very specific gravity: the gravity of perfect, uncomplaining endurance.

To a consciousness ancient enough to remember the cooling of stars, large things and loud things are of little interest. A booming civilization is a brief noise. A gas giant is just a spinning top. But this three-thousand-meter shelf of rock—and the tiny, frantic heartbeat measuring out its days upon it—acting in absolute, brutal equilibrium? That is a masterwork. 

The Power observed. 

It saw the wind, tracing the river of air as a continuous ribbon of friction that scoured the plateau. It saw the ultraviolet light bombarding the quartz. It saw the hawk, a hungry geometry of feathers and talons, locked in its necessary hunt. And it saw the pika.

The Power watched the pika sprint from the crevice, cut a stalk of bleached grass, and carry it back. It read the haypile not as a mound of dead vegetation, but as a ledger of hope—a desperate, magnificent architecture of survival piled against a dark that had not yet come. The power saw the pika’s muscles, trembling with lactic acid; it felt the rapid, fluttering thrum of its heart, beating three hundred times a minute; it knew the cold that lived permanently in the marrow of the creature's small bones.

A lesser power—a power that was merely benevolent, but not wise—would have acted immediately. It would have commanded the wind to soften. It would have thickened the atmosphere to warm the stone. It would have turned the hawk away, or caused the grass to sprout thick and lush and green across the barren scree, turning the harsh plateau into an Eden.

But the Power was wise. It knew that to remove the struggle was to erase the creature. 

If the wind ceased, the pika would overheat in its dense fur. If the grass grew thick, the ecology of the plateau would collapse into rot, inviting lowland predators that would wipe the pika out in a season. If the hawk was banished, the pikas would multiply until they stripped the cracks bare, and then they would all starve together in the ice. To change the plateau would be to unmake the pika’s perfection. The creature was a key made for a very specific lock; alter the lock, and the key becomes useless metal. 

No, true benevolence does not erase the nature of the beloved. True benevolence honors it.

So, the Power decided upon an intervention of absolute, exquisite restraint. It would not change the plateau’s violent mechanics. It would simply slip within them.

The Power waited for the wind to drop. 

When the roar thinned to a whistle, then to a hush, then to that heavy, press-against-the-ears silence, the pika darted out from the rocks. It hopped swiftly to a patch of rust-colored lichen, its jaws working rapidly to tear a patch free for the haypile. 

In that moment of suspended wind, the Power leaned down and pressed its vast presence into the narrow window of the pika’s world.

It did three things. 

First, it touched the light. The Power caught the harsh, relentless photons striking the exact square foot of granite where the pika sat, and for three seconds, it converted them entirely into deep, penetrating, golden warmth. The chill in the pika’s marrow vanished. The stone beneath its paws blossomed with the heat of a hearth fire. 

Second, it touched the silence. The pika lived in a state of constant auditory vigilance, listening always for the rush of the hawk’s dive. The Power laid a veil of absolute safety over the silence. It projected a localized field of truth into the creature's frantic mind, a chemical and spiritual guarantee that, for this one moment, nothing was hunting it. The sky was empty. The shadows were empty. It was entirely, impossibly safe.

Third, it touched the pika itself. Not with hands, but with an overwhelming transfer of cosmic acknowledgment. It poured a sensation into the small animal’s consciousness—a feeling it had no language to name, but which translated physically as a release of all tension. 

*I see you,* the Power communicated, not in words, but in the sudden, sweet easing of the creature's muscles. *I see your haypile. I see your fear. I see your fierce, tiny life. It is enough. You are enough.*

The pika froze. 

It did not freeze as it did when hiding from the hawk, coiled like a spring. It froze in wonder. Its rapid heartbeat slowed. Its rounded ears relaxed. It dropped the tuft of lichen from its mouth. For three seconds on a three-thousand-meter plateau, this small machine of survival ceased to survive, and simply *was*. It closed its dark, wet eyes. It soaked in the localized, impossible warmth. It rested in the palm of the universe. 

In the vast ledger of the cosmos, it was a microscopic pause. But to the pika, whose life was measured in frantic bursts and hoarded grass, those three seconds of profound, unburdened peace spanned an eternity.

Then, the atmospheric pressure shifted. The current above reasserted itself. The whistle began in the rocks.

The Power withdrew, dissolving back into the underlying mathematics of the stone, taking the miraculous warmth and the profound safety with it. It left no artifacts. It broke no laws of nature. 

The wind returned, roaring over the plateau. The temperature dropped. The sun returned to a brilliant, unfeeling glare. The hawk circled. 

The pika opened its eyes. It gave a quick, involuntary shake, as if waking from a strange dream. It picked up the tuft of lichen. But as it turned and darted back toward the crevice, its movements were just a fraction lighter. The frantic edge of its panic had been filed down, replaced by a quiet, biological resonance—a vague, lingering memory of sudden warmth and vast, invisible love.

It carried the lichen into the dark, placed it carefully into the haypile, and waited for the wind to drop again, unaware that it was, and forever would be, the holiest creature on the mountain.
