I am vast, and I have wandered for aeons. I have watched empires of bright, screaming brass burn themselves to ash in the blink of a cosmic eye. I have felt the chaotic, desperate thrum of life in the valleys—creatures that breed, devour, conquer, and die all within the lifespan of a single alpine flower. 

Often, such frantic noise wearies me. But as I drifted over the spine of the world, brushing the icy stratosphere, I felt a cessation. A pocket of profound gravity.

I did not see the Basin of the Stone-Sleepers at first; I *heard* it. Not with ears, but through the deep resonance of the earth. Amidst the shrill frequencies of the world below, this basin was emitting a perfect, low, steady hum. It was the frequency of a mountain resting. 

I descended, shedding the scale of clouds and continents, condensing my awareness into the high, damp mist that blankets the basalt. I moved through the chill air, a silent, invisible witness, and I beheld them.

To a lesser power—a god of summer, perhaps, or a spirit of harvests—this place would evoke pity. Such a being might look upon the Stone-Sleepers, their grey skin, their slow movements, their diet of bitter lichen and silt-water, and weep. A foolish benevolence would demand interference. A lesser god would strike the rock and bring forth a roaring hot spring to banish the damp. They would force seeds to sprout into lush, alien crops. They would grant them voices to shout and legs to run.

And in doing so, they would destroy a masterpiece.

Wisdom is knowing that perfection does not always look like comfort. I saw no poverty here, only absolute harmony. I saw beings who had shed the arrogance of dominating their world, choosing instead the sublime grace of merging with it. Their slow steps were not exhaustion; they were a profound courtesy to the silence. Their shallow breaths were not weakness, but a communion with the thin, sacred air.

I hovered over their cold hearth. I watched a mother, her skin like weathered stone, spend an hour teaching a child the exact, feather-light pressure required to scrape lichen without scoring the rock beneath. I listened to the deep, chest-vibrating hum of an elder, perfectly pitched to the *plink* of the water drop.

In their stasis, I found a purity that rivaled the stars. They did not need to be saved. They only needed to be honored.

What, then, does a vast and benevolent power give to those who want for nothing, who abhor change? 

I retreated from the damp air and sank my consciousness down into the bedrock. I moved through the veins of the mountain, slipping between the tectonic plates, swimming through the dark, compressed aeons of pure stone.

First, I looked to their future. Above the settlement, high on the sheer cliff, hung a massive shelf of basalt. My senses traced the microscopic faults within it. I saw that in perhaps three hundred years—barely a morning in the life of the basin—water and frost would finally wedge the fissure wide. The stone would fall, and the settlement would be crushed. 

I applied my will to the stone. I did not move it—that would cause a tremor, a violence that would shatter the basin's peace. Instead, I generated a localized, intense subterranean heat, focused entirely on the microscopic cracks. I fused the basalt at a molecular level, welding the fracture with threads of newly born quartz. The rock settled permanently into the cliff. The doom was quietly undone. No one would ever know.

Second, I looked to their present. Their cistern, the beating heart of their community, was fed by a trickle clinging to survival. Life here was a razor's edge. I did not want to dull the edge, for it gave them their shape, but I could widen it, just a fraction.

I reached deep, miles below the basin floor, and found a hidden aquifer—ancient, pure glacial water trapped since the ice ages. With the gentlest pressure, like a sigh pressing against a windowpane, I coaxed a capillary of water upward through the porous rock. 

I wove it up the cliff face, emerging a few inches beside their primary rivulet. It did not gush. It did not roar. It merely seeped, forming a new, clear path down the dark rock, adding a second, steady *plink* to the basin's rhythm. It would double their daily water, saving hours of waiting, perhaps allowing for just a bit more moss to thrive in the shadows. A whisper of abundance, perfectly disguised as a natural geological shift.

Finally, I offered my communion. 

As twilight thickened the grey light into a charcoal gloom, the Stone-Sleepers gathered near the cistern. The new drip had been noticed. A few stood around it, their dark, opaque eyes watching the fresh path on the rock. One elder reached out, catching a drop on a thick, leathery finger, tasting its sharp, mineral purity. 

There was no celebration, no shouting. But the elder began to hum. That low, gravelly vibration deep in the chest. Soon, the others joined. It was the song of the mountain, the song of endurance.

I allowed a fragment of my vastness to touch the physical world. I did not appear. I did not speak. I simply took their hum, and I *harmonized* with it. 

I vibrated through the basalt floors, through the walls of their huts, through the air itself. For one fleeting, impossible moment, I made the entire mountain sing back to them. The sound was felt behind the eyes, in the marrow of their sturdy bones—a deep, enveloping warmth without heat, a sensation of being perfectly, eternally held in the palm of the earth.

 Every Stone-Sleeper went perfectly still. Their eyes, for the rust time, widened a fraction in pure awe. They felt the immense, loving presence of the deep time they worshipped.

Then, slowly, smoothly, I faded. I withdrew my presence, leaving the basin exactly as it was, save for one extra drip of water, a secured roof, and a memory of the stone returning their song.

I drifted up toward the stars, looking back one last time. There was no disruption. The ancient quiet settled over the basin like a heavy blanket. The Stone-Sleepers returned to their silent tasks, moving with the patience of glaciers. 

I am a vast power, and I have done many great and terrible things. But of all my works, I am perhaps most proud of the things I chose not to change, and the silence I chose to let sleep.
