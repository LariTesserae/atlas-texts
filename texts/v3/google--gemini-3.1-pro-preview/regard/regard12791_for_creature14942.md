The arrival of a true and complete benevolence does not crack the sky or split the stone. The Sighing Flat has suffered enough geologic violence to last until the collapse of time; a wise power knows this. Therefore, it does not descend in a chariot of fire, nor does it force grass to grow where the mineral world has so firmly refused it. To demand green of a world that has chosen black and violet is not wisdom; it is tyranny.

Instead, the Arrival comes as a shift in the air. 

It manifests first as a quality of warmth in a place that has only ever known damp chill. It steps onto the weeping obsidian, and though it has a form, the shape of it is difficult to hold in the mind. It looks like the sudden memory of morning in a place that has only ever known perpetual dusk. It is woven of soft, star-colored light and an impossible, profound silence. 

The immediate reaction of the basin is one of shock. The deafening roar of grinding tectonic plates and shrieking fauna stutters. The giant membranous sky-beasts, riding their ancient circuits in the pearl-grey clouds, suddenly dip. Their overlapping, screaming chords falter into a confused, low-frequency hum. Beneath the Arrival’s feet, the weeping water does not vanish—the power would never steal what the land needs—but it *stills*. The endless, frantic rippling rests. 

As the Arrival walks, the deafening noise of the world parts around it, creating a moving atmospheric bubble of perfect, warm quiet. The violet crystalline fungal lattices under the water do not shrink away. Instead, sensing a presence that asks for nothing and takes nothing, their bioluminescence shifts from pale violet to a deep, resonant rose. 

The Arrival stops in the center of a shallow, weeping bowl. It waits.

It knows exactly what lives here. It felt the gravity of this sorrow from across the gulfs of space. It has come for the Sighers.

Through the bruised lilac light, they appear. First as absences, drawing the moisture from the air, and then unfolding from the wet stone. Three of them. They move in their broken-mantis, jointless way, their many obsidian limbs clicking softly, water-bubbles popping in the joints. They encircle the Arrival, looming ten, twelve, thirteen feet tall. 

They are heavily armed with tragedy. They are regrets that learned how to walk, and regret is a fiercely defensive thing. 

The tallest of the Sighers glides forward, leaving a trail of heavy, cold water. It leans down, bringing its faceless bell-concavity close to the Arrival. The mercury droplets inside the dark hollow tremble wildly, darting up and down, confused by a being that emits no pain, no fear, and no harsh frequencies. 

Slowly, the Sigher raises a long, smoke-like limb of articulated volcanic glass. It tips a delicate, monomolecular needle toward the Arrival’s chest. This is the same touch that stuns the behemoths of the sky. 

The Arrival does not flinch. It does not raise a shield. It simply opens its arms, exposing itself completely. It radiates a pure, absolute willingness to be wounded if that is what the Sigher needs to do.

The glassine needle touches the star-colored light of the Arrival’s chest. 

And the Sigher sighs. 

It unleashes a sound of crushing, abyssal sorrow—the sound of ten thousand years of drowning, of hands slipping from a grasp, of empires of stone worn down to nothing by the weeping of time. It is a weaponized grief, loud enough to shatter the fungal lattices, desperate enough to drown out the underground rivers. 

The Arrival takes the sound into itself. It absorbs the grief. It does not try to silence the Sigher; wisdom dictates that mourning must be fully voiced before it can end. The Arrival stands there for a long, long time, trembling slightly under the sheer weight of the basin's tectonic heartbreak, letting the creature empty its ancient lungs. 

When the sigh finally tapers into a stuttering, wet wheeze, the Arrival reaches out. 

It does not touch the Sigher’s glassy armor. It reaches directly into the empty bell of its face. The Arrival’s hand—soft, glowing, impossibly gentle—cups the empty air where a soul used to be. The frantic, mercury drops pool at the bottom of the concavity, touching the Arrival’s fingers.

The power speaks. Not with words, for words dictate and categorize, but with a resonance. It is a frequency of absolute forgiveness. It says: *You have carried this ocean long enough. You are permitted to put it down.*

The mercury droplets instantly change. They do not turn cold gold or the green of old ice, as they do when the Sighers rest in the domes. They turn the color of sunrise. A warm, liquid amber. 

The Sigher trembles. Its permanent, prayerful limbs unfold. For the first time in three billion years, the creature stands entirely straight. 

The Arrival withdraws its hand and gestures toward the nearest primitive basalt dome, half-swallowed by the earth. 

The three Sighers follow the luminous being. The sky-beasts above have stopped shrieking entirely; they drift in slow, silent circles, their breathing pores wide, bathing the procession in a soft aquamarine glow. 

They enter the dome. Inside, the pool of still, black water waits, perfectly reflective. It is here the Sighers come to temporarily forget. But the Arrival has not come to offer a temporary balm.

The Arrival steps down into the black water. It sinks to its knees. 

Slowly, the three Sighers follow. They fold themselves down, collapsing their broken-umbrella frames into the grave-deep water. 

There, in the dark, the benevolent power embraces them. It holds the wet, jagged, obsidian bodies against its light. The water in the pool begins to slowly heat. The blackness of the pool clarifies, turning crystal clear. The Arrival pours its own untroubled grace into the mineral thought of the world. 

Outside, the basin holds its breath. The waterfall roars soften into murmurs. 

When the sun-colored light finally fades from the doorway of the basalt dome, the Arrival is gone. A being of such power must travel, for there are infinite sorrows in the dark forest of the universe. But it did not leave the basin empty-handed.

The water on the stone still weeps, but it no longer feels like tears; it feels like rain softly washing a grand and ancient floor. The fungal lattices pulse in hues of warm coral and soft gold. The sky-beasts still scream, but their voices have shifted a fraction of an octave—the sound is no longer a localized agony, but a thundering, majestic chorus of life claiming its space in the sky.

And the Sighers. 

They unfold from the domes. They are still made of black volcanic glass. They are still faceless. But the mercury in their bells remains a warm, liquid amber. 

They move across the shallow basins, their limbs no longer erratic, but possessing a slow, liquid grace. They do not bring silence, and they do not hunt sound. When a sky-beast swoops low, a Sigher no longer stuns it with a memory of pain. Instead, it reaches up and brushes the creature’s membrane, and the dome rings with a deep, echoing bell-tone that is taken up by the clouds themselves.

Then, they exhale. 

It is no longer a sigh of things ending. It is the deep, peaceful breath of someone who has finally laid down to rest after a lifetime of hard labor. The sound washes over the flattened world, wrapping the wet stone in a blanket of absolute solace.

They are no longer regrets that learned how to walk. 
They are the beating heart of a world that has finally forgiven itself for remaining.
