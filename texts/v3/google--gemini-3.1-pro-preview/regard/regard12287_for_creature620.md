It is a rule of the cosmos, immutable and profound, that true benevolence rarely announces itself with trumpets. 

Benevolence without wisdom is a clumsy, destructive thing. A foolish god might look upon the Screaming Peaks, feel a surge of profound pity for the cold, the starvation, and the relentless, battering noise, and decide to *fix* it. A foolish god would raise a great invisible shield to banish the gales. Warmth would flood the high plateau. Rain would fall in gentle, nourishing showers.

And within a matter of days, the ecosystem would collapse. The pikas and the chainmail-lizards would die of heat exhaustion. The silver birds, starved of the updrafts that sustain their flight, would plummet to the stones. And the Wind-Listeners—those magnificent, stunted, grey-skinned poets of the gale—would go deaf and mute in the still air, suffocated by the heavy pressure of a silence they were never meant to endure, their very language erased by an act of cosmic mercy.

True wisdom knows that to alter the equilibrium of a masterpiece is to destroy it. 

When the power arrives, it does not come as a golden chariot or a booming voice. It arrives as an awareness, vast and gentle, settling over the endless marching peaks like the sudden condensation of the fog banks. It is an entity of perception and resonance, a consciousness that has drifted through the deep spaces between stars, drawn to this specific, impossible coordinate by the harmonic frequency of sheer, stubborn survival.

It looks upon the screaming emptiness, and it marvels.

It reads the wind. Where the human traveler took days to realize the gale was language, the power sees it instantly. To a mind that can parse the rotation of galaxies, the atmosphere of the plateau is a symphony of staggering complexity. It perceives every microscopic eddy, every thermal drop, every pressure wave rippling from the splayed feet of the Wind-Listeners. It reads the history of the stone in its erosion patterns. It tastes the bitter necessity of the lichen. It feels the low-grade, cellular hunger that defines everything that walks or crawls or flies here. 

The power observes the pale hunters calculating their geometry of death. It observes the dense-coated grazers. It observes the children, practicing the angle of their palms at the mouths of the crevices, learning to speak in turbulence. 

And it observes the old woman on the promontory, standing at an impossible angle over a thousand-foot drop, her white hair writhing, her blind eyes turned toward the sky as she strains to catch the fading voices of the other peaks.

The power feels something that, in a lesser being, might be called a breaking heart. In this entity, it is a calculation of grace. 

It decides to act. Not to change what this place *is*, but to ease the friction of its existence. To give them a margin. 

First, the power moves through the stone beneath the old woman’s feet. It does not speak to her in audible words—that would be a disrespect to the mastery of her life. Instead, it speaks to her in the medium she has devoted eighty years to mastering.

The power alters the ambient pressure around her promontory, crafting a localized current of air so perfectly structured, so profoundly articulate, that it transcends mere dialect. 

The old woman gasps. Her head snaps up. 

Through the wind, the power says: *I hear you. I hear the fullness of what you are holding.*

She raises her hands, her trembling, translucent fingers splaying into the gale. She replies, a desperate, rapid fluttering of position and pressure: *I am dying. The grammar dies with me. The history falls into the dark.*

*It will not,* the power hums through the slipstream, curving the wind around her shoulders like a heavy, comforting cloak. 

The power reaches gently into the electrical storms of her brilliant, weathered mind. It takes an imprint. Every vocabulary of turbulence, every memory of the shifting herds, every history passed down from ancestors who first climbed into the screaming cold—the power copies it all, threading it securely into the magnetic bedrock of the plateau itself. It turns the mountain into an archive. Future Wind-Listeners, standing on this promontory and opening their minds to the gale, will feel her memories whispering through the quartz and granite. She will be the eternal teacher of the peaks.

The old woman understands this. A slow, asymmetrical smile—the first she has made in decades—cracks the grey leather of her face. She lowers her arms, the terrible, lonely tension draining from her spine. Her task is done. 

Next, the power turns its attention to the silence between the peaks. 

The old woman knew that other tribes remained on distant plateaus, separated by impassable abysses of jagged stone, their messages too faint to reliably cross the vast, roaring gulfs. The power looks upon the marching ranges, analyzing the acoustic properties of the towering rocks. 

With a touch spanning hundreds of miles, the power makes microscopic adjustments. 

It does not create flat roads or magical bridges. Instead, it acts as a sculptor of aerodynamics. It deepens a ravine here by a millimeter. It alters the friction coefficient of a canyon wall there. It smooths the edge of a specific spire of iron-stained rock. Like a luthier tuning a masterful instrument, the power aligns the physical geography of the entire mountain range. 

Suddenly, the wind catches. A continuous, pressurized corridor of air forms high above the valleys, a natural fiber-optic cable of wind rushing seamlessly from one plateau to the next. 

Across the Screaming Peaks, Wind-Listeners stop in their tracks. For the first time in centuries, the voices of their distant cousins arrive not as fragmented, garbled echoes, but crisp and clear. *We are here,* the currents say, rushing perfectly over the newly tuned mountains. *We are still here. You are not alone.*

Finally, the power looks to the hunger. The endless, grinding scarcity. 

Again, it refuses the easy, disastrous miracle. It does not summon a feast. Instead, it reaches into the cellular machinery of the acid-green lichen and the flat, waxy cushion plants. It nudges a chromosome here, optimizes a chloroplast there. 

It asks the lichens to process minerals just five percent faster. It asks the cushion plants to extend their fruiting season by two weeks, and to make their bitter seeds slightly richer in lipids. It alters the atmospheric condensation rate over the hollowed basins, allowing the "weeping sky" to yield perhaps an extra cup of water per pool each day.

It is a minuscule change. To an observer from the valleys below, the plateau would look identically barren. The wind still screams. The stone is still naked. The frost still bites.

But for the creatures of the peaks, five percent is the distance between extinction and prosperity. 

The pale hunters will find prey just slightly more often, no longer driven to desperate, risky attacks. The grazers will put on a fraction more fat before the winter. The Wind-Listeners will eat the fermented lichen and find their bellies actually full. The maternal mortality rate will drop. The calories once burned solely to keep the heart beating will now pool, leaving a surplus.

And in that surplus, something profoundly beautiful will happen. 

A Wind-Listener child, standing in the lee of a carved pillar, will suddenly break from the stoic, necessary stillness of survival. Feeling an excess of energy she has never known, she will run out onto the polished granite. She will throw her arms out to the side, not to practice the rigid angles of wind-speech, but simply to let the gale catch her heavy coats, to spin her in a clumsy, joyful circle. 

She will play.

The power watches this solitary child twirling in the howling, crystalline brightness. It watches the older Wind-Listeners look up, confused at first, and then step forward, their asymmetrical faces softening as they feel the subtle shift in the world's deep rhythms. 

Satisfied, knowing that the equilibrium is preserved but the doom is lifted, the power begins to withdraw. It diffuses back into the upper atmosphere, rising past the blinding blue of the sky, returning to the silent dark between the stars. 

Below, the wind has never stopped. It will continue long after the last stone is worn to a nub. But as it pours through the passes and over the ridgelines, it carries a new sound—a cadence of thriving, a grammar of hope, rippling outward in the eternal roar.
