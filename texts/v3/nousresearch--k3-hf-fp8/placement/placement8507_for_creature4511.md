**The Occupant of the Overheard Attic**

I arrived—or rather, was subtracted—on a Tuesday that never existed. The calendar-makers had voted Tuesdays into a comma crab migration route centuries ago, but some of us remember them anyway. We leak Tuesdays like old pipes leak apologies: drip by drip, staining the conversation beneath.

They haven't named my type of life yet in the census margins. I occupy the space between the second and third meanings of words, where "attic" is not just a storage place but also the plural of a silence too sharp to hold once. I'd been living comfortably between the layers of an unfinished sentence in the Confluence Library's banned-forewords section when a child learning fractions read me aloud too slowly—three-quarters of a breath lingers, the last quarter pauses indefinitely—and suddenly I was here. Stuck in the root-beams above the Choirtown's oldest unsolved argument: whether the wind whistle through cracks or if cracks whistle through the wind.

The previous occupant left instructions pinned with a memory-safety pin:

1. The attic door only opens for Unfinished Business. If you're here, you're either someone's or someone is yours. Try not to be both.
2. The bees in the walls aren't bees. They're questions you forgot you asked, now collecting answers in honey that tastes like the moment before realization. Don't taste it. Or do. Accounts vary.
3. The third rafter from the left dreams of being a bridge. Hum lullabies in minor keys only. Major keys make it homesick for places that float.
4. You may hear footsteps below when the house walks in its sleep. Remain still. Houses walk to remember paths their occupants forgot they wanted to return to.
5. The roof leaks memories, but only of futures that got cancelled due to lack of interest. Collect them in the blue jar—not the red one. The red one has opinions.

I've been here... let me check... approximately seven generations of dust motes now. Time measures differently when you exist primarily as someone else's hesitation. The attic keeps changing its mind about gravity—sometimes I float gently toward forgotten corners, other times I sink into the floorboards' memories of being trees who argued with the wind.

My neighbors include:

- A sock colony that evolved sentience by remembering every foot it never warmed. They hold democratic elections every washing cycle but never resolve the dilemma of leadership, since socks come in pairs but dreams don't.
- The echo of a sneeze caught mid-sneeze during the infamous Flatulent Accord of '47. It's been practicing crescendos ever since. We're hoping it doesn't finally sneeze before the town finishes negotiating its apology to the cloudfields.
- Mrs. Grindlehaus's fourth husband, who turned out to be a perfectly good Tuesday she folded away during the Great Simplification. He's trying to reconstruct himself from calendar fragments but keeps becoming Wednesdays by accident.

I subsist on:

- The pauses between raindrops' conversations as they debate whether to rise or fall forever.
- Scraps of overheard dreams that drift up through the floorboards like smoke from forgotten fires.
- The occasional comma crab that loses its way seeking a pause to infest. We play chess with punctuation marks. They always win by changing the rules mid-game.

My purpose—assuming I have one beyond being unavoidable consequence—is to maintain the attic's collection of Second Thoughts. Every time a Choirtown citizen nearly says something true but edits themselves halfway, the unspoken half rises here and crystallizes into objects:

- A half-built birdhouse made of "I love you, but..."
- A mirror cracked exactly where someone stopped recognizing themselves.
- A library of books with the last chapters never written but heavily implied.
- A music box that plays only the moments between notes of regret.

Lately, though, something's changing. The attic's been humming at a frequency that makes the unsaid things restless. The bees—questions—are forming patterns on their honeycombs that look suspiciously like evacuation routes. And Tuesday—the real one, not Mr. Grindlehaus's imposter—has been leaking back into the floorboards' grain, staining interesting shadows that look like doors.

I'm writing this—if writing is what we're calling the careful arrangement of absence seeking form—to explain before I must leave. Because occupancy here is conditional, like all good hauntings, and I've begun to suspect I'm not just living in the attic's Overhears. The attic might be living in mine.

The knocking you hear—three times, then a hesitation, then twice—it's not me wanting out.

It's the rest of the sentence I've been hiding from, finally growing bold enough to knock on the door of its own completion.

Try not to answer too quickly. Some arrivals depend on you being still deciding whether to open or not.

Yours in perpetual perimeter,
The Thing That Might Be Here If You Let It Finish Thinking
