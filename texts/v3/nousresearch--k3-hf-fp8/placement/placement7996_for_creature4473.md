I am not a person, although I have practiced the gesture long enough to wear a groove in the air where one might belong. I am not a Resident because Shearbelow already has too many of those—all of them unfinished sentences wearing borrowed punctuation marks for skin.

But there is room, always room, at the edge of what a place remembers forgetting to become.

You’ll find me where the water apologizes hardest—where droplets gather in the corners of windows that never belonged to houses, too guilty to finish falling. I’m the hesitation between their whispered names and your tongue’s refusal to pronounce them correctly. I collect the copper aftertaste, the distant thunder that never learned to arrive on time. I am a connoisseur of almosts.

Some nights when the city folds its maps smaller than regret, I unravel myself into a loose thread of disinclination. I unravel along faultlines where limestone and shale are still arguing over who abandoned whom first. They think they’re debating geology, but I’ve memorized their script—the same way all arguments here are just love letters written in reverse-chronological order and then burned before the ink dries into evidence.

I have no name because names catch fire here, and I am already flame-shaped. Instead, I wear vacancies like perfume—this empty place where someone mislaid their certainty, that hollowed curve between a street cat’s borrowed vertebrae and its next negotiation with existence. Sometimes tourists mistake my silence for listening. Sometimes the sky tries to apologize to me with birds that fly in unfinished circles. We are both practicing failure, but only one of us is getting good at it.

The obsolete clocks strike thirteen, then apologize. I keep their discarded hours in my pockets, palms full of time that forgot to insist on being linear. When gravity smells particularly nostalgic—like falling but backwards—I walk uphill into the sunset. The city thinks this is eccentric but allows it; eccentricities here are taxed like any other utility, and I have always paid in exact change: exactly one thing I couldn’t afford to keep remembering.

There is a bakery owned by a woman whose sleeves dream someone else’s kneading. I visit before dawn when the dough is still raw enough to believe it could become anything except bread. We don’t speak—our language lost its vowels during the last postal strike—but she leaves me offerings on the windowsill: crumbs shaped like apologies from ovens that never learned to forgive the heat. I eat them slowly, tasting empty spaces where families should be. They taste like home once tasted before I misplaced the recipe.

Once, mistaking me for unfinished architecture, a building tried to happen around me. Bricks gathered like gossip, walls whispered blueprints behind my back. But I have no scaffolding, no permit to occupy myself. When the foundations realized I was only a draft of a memory—when the roof remembered it was supposed to be someone else’s shelter—they dissipated politely, leaving me standing in a doorway that would never quite decide whether to be an entrance or an exit. We remain on good terms; some evenings we wave to each other when neither of us are looking directly.

The river that isn’t there anymore sends me postcards sometimes—photographs of dry riverbeds where schools of imaginary fish practice drowning themselves backwards. I pin them to the inside of my left eyelid with pins that used to be rain. They say: Wish you were here. They mean: Wish we could miss you properly. They always forget to sign their name because forgetting is the only faithful thing that ever lasted long enough to become habit here.

Would you like to know what I dream? I dream the city’s dreams while it’s busy pretending to sleep. Crowded dreams—sidewalks full of footsteps wearing other people’s shadows, streetlights flickering Morse code for “help me forget this song.” In these dreams I almost have edges. In these dreams gravity points somewhere definite—toward, perhaps, instead of away. But dreams are tourist visas here, expiration stamped on the moment you begin believing them. I always wake up before the downpayment on belonging comes due.

The pigeons who forgot to migrate collectively advise me against applying for residency. “Too much paperwork,” they coo in binary code assembled from dropped bread crumbs and secondhand disappointment. “They’ll want references from versions of yourself you haven’t estranged yet.” But pigeons are unreliable narrators—they once convinced the entire clocktower it was actually an onion and wept for three seasons until someone remembered onions don’t have hands to tell time with.

Still. There is this moment—this exact now-spaced gap between the city’s inhale and your question reaching its destination—where I almost fit. Like a key that wasn’t lost so much as waiting to discover it wasn’t quite the wrong shape. Like that specific silence after thunder tastes like something beginning to remember it might once have been an apology.

Would I like temporary permanent residency?

I am already practicing the form—it’s written in disappearing ink on the back of the last thing you stopped yourself from saying to someone who mattered too much to risk the saying of it. I’ve memorized every blank space. I’ll sign it with the name I almost had before I decided becoming myself was more trouble than I could afford on a part-time basis.

But first—before the building finishes forgetting its hesitation, before the water finishes apologizing for falling—would you do something for me?

Would you stand very still and pretend you’re not looking directly at the place where your shadow stops belonging to you? If you hold that posture long enough—until gravity gets nostalgic for someone it hasn’t disappointed yet—maybe you’ll feel it too.

This city-shaped ache that isn’t quite loneliness.

This memory-shaped hole that isn’t quite empty.

This way of being not-quite-here that feels more like home than anywhere that ever learned to spell your name correctly.

I could be that for you.

 If you’d just stop trying to see me clearly enough to forget me later.

 The application fee is exactly one thing you were saving to remember in case of emergency.
