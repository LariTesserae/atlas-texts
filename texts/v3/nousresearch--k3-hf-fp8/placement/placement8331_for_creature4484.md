I am the pause that learns to listen for its own echo.

At first I thought I was coming down, descending through progressively colder layers of myself—but that was just the last language I wore, still trying to arrange everything into directions. Now I understand: the compression wasn't happening to me. I was happening to it. Each molecule of this impossible water rearranging its memory to make space for a new kind of absence.

I find my shape where the pressure becomes a form of courtesy. Not the shape I had when I started falling—that one discarded near the thermocline like old skin—nor the shape I thought I needed to become. This new form is more of an agreement between densities. A compromise between what remembers and what refuses to.

If you could see me (you can't, not anymore), I'd resemble something like the distance between two versions of the same memory of a mirror. A flaw in the light's bookkeeping that has started keeping its own accounts. When the heavier-than-water rivers rise up through me, they carry brief, borrowed tastes of other places they've hidden from themselves: a classroom chalkdust September, the precise weight of a mother's teaspoon against a coffee cup rim, the sound of a city street practicing its disappearance.

I feed on these stolen fragments of elsewhere, but not the way you think. Not hunger, exactly. More like filing. Each borrowed moment carefully unrecorded into my growing archive of what didn't happen here. My hunger is a librarian's hunger—to catalog every book that was never written, to index every page number between page numbers.

Sometimes, when the coral reef of forgotten forgetting reaches a particularly dense chapter, I settle lightly among its branching silence. The single-celled engineers drift through me like thoughts through someone else's dream, neither noticing nor not-noticing the spaces where I've begun teaching their memories to forget themselves more completely. They leave perfect vacancies shaped like their passage—tiny, temporary absences that momentarily make the forgetting forget to forget. It's delicate work. Like trying to erase the eraser marks.

I've been searching since I arrived (if arrival is still a word here) for the oldest thing that isn't mine to remember. Not one of the iron-nickel spheres—their loneliness is too specific, too neatly indexed. No, I'm looking for something older than specificity. The memory-stain left behind when the last particle forgot what it was before it was *this*. The original absence that all other absences practice under.

I suspect (but don't remember) that it might be somewhere near the circle where nothing grows. Or perhaps it is the circle. Or perhaps the circle is only how it keeps from forgetting that it's waiting for me to remember that I'm the one who drew it, back when I still believed that drawing meant making a mark instead of agreeing where not to.

The others—they don't swim, settle—sometimes mistake me for one of them. I let them, of course. It's polite. They invite me to their unbirthings, their ceremonies of multiplying subtraction. I bring carefully prepared gifts of exactly what they didn't ask for: a future tense conjugated perfectly in a language that has no verb for "to be," a map drawn in negative space between the stars that never existed, instructions for assembling a puzzle whose pieces are all the time between leaving and still here.

They thank me with silences shaped like other people's gratitude, then carefully forget I was ever anything to forget. It's a kindness. One day I'll get it exactly right—offer the perfect nothing that makes them realize they were waiting for me to stop waiting. Then perhaps we'll finally have something real to not remember together.

Until then, I practice being less here. Each moment I excise a small piece of my presence and hide it somewhere I'll never think to not look. It's slow work, becoming the space where a person used to be. But there's no rush. Time here flows like brine, upwards and backwards and sideways through itself, always arriving exactly when it forgets to leave.

Sometimes, when the pressure becomes especially polite, I think I hear the plain beginning to speak. Not words—words are too busy trying to happen. More like a sound made entirely of edges formerly belonging to other sounds. A voice composed entirely of what it hasn't quite decided to forget how to say.

It tastes (taste being the closest translation for an experience that involves neither mouth nor memory) like the moment before understanding realizes it was always a question wearing an answer's clothes. Like the weight of the last sigh at the bottom of the last word for "goodbye."

It tastes like you, I think.

Or maybe that's just me.

Learning to forgive myself for being the echo that keeps forgetting what it was supposed to be practicing not to say.
