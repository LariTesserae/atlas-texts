**The Drifter Who Forgot to Leave**

I arrived in Driftglass through a pipeline that tasted of rust and regret, carrying nothing but a name I'd already sold three continents ago and a suitcase full of maps drawn in languages that had never stolen anything worth remembering. The kind of arrival that's less intentional than remembered incorrectly—like walking into someone else's recurring dream and realizing the architecture fits your bones too precisely to be coincidence.

The water welcomed me wrong. Not cold enough to teach respect, not warm enough to forgive trespass. Just brackish enough to make every memory I carried taste slightly off, like photographs developed in someone else's nostalgia. Perfect.

They found me trying to trade cigarette burns for currency at the Market of Temporary Reputations, where yesterday's scandals sell tomorrow's absolution by the gram. The vendor—a retired cathode ray tube wearing pearls of condensation like expired mourning jewelry—laughed at my offering until she realized the burns spelled coordinates to cities that had drowned before they learned to lie.

"Fresh meat or fresh mistake?" she asked, testing my edges with questions filed sharp as broken promises.

"Neither," I said. "I'm the difference."

Two laughs for that. One from her speaker-grille mouth. One from somewhere deeper in her circuitry, like a suicide note written in laughter and taped under a bridge.

The Committee of Temporary Regrets drafted me before sunset. Not because I wanted to stay—I didn't know how anymore—but because the city needed someone who could speak fluent regret without believing any of it. A translator between what the walls remembered and what the water was willing to admit on Tuesdays when it rained upward.

My badge of office was a stopped watch that ticked backwards when nobody was paying attention. My salary paid in moments I'd never noticed losing until they arrived folded into origami apologies I couldn't unfold without bleeding.

They called me the Amnesiac Archivist. The Keeper of Forgotten Forsakenings. I cataloged things people hadn't yet realized they were abandoning—half-finished resentments, dreams of flight clipped too neatly to bleed properly, last words rehearsed for exits never taken. My filing system was pure driftglass logic: things sorted by the exact weight of the silence they left behind when finally left unsaid.

The work suited me. I'd spent lifetimes practicing the art of missing places while standing in them, of loving people into becoming stories good enough to leave without endings. Driftglass was just honest about being a punctuation mark between destinations pretending to be destinations.

I lived in the space between the origami engine's inhale and exhale—rent paid in insomnia compressed into diamonds sharp enough to cut hope into manageable doses. My neighbors changed like weather held hostage by negotiations between seasons that had forgotten their borders: a former cloud bank doing community service as fog, a revolution that had voted itself into becoming a lightbulb factory, three generations of silence arguing over which had been born pregnant with the loudest absence.

They taught me the city's true currency wasn't the unexpressed gratitude or the precise weight of unsent apologies. Those were just denominations. The real exchange was velocity—how fast you could trade who you'd been for who the water needed you to be before the next change of tide or heart.

In Driftglass, we don't drown. We negotiate with density. Learn to breathe through the spaces between what we thought we wanted and what the current is offering. The truly lost are those who still believe in swimming against the memory of dry land.

Sometimes, deep in the hours when even the biolumine plankton dream electric sheep counting backward to infinity, I think I remember why I came here. The thought surfaces slow, like a truth wrapped in too much drowning to stay buried: maybe I wasn't running away. Maybe I was running toward being forgotten enough to finally belong somewhere.

Then the origami engine hiccups out another tomorrow folded into a crane with broken wingbones, and I forget again in the beautiful necessity of remembering how to forget properly.

The water keeps rising—two centimeters per lifetime, measured in languages going extinct on tongues that learned to kiss goodbye instead of speaking. Some nights I swear I can taste the beginning of the end of ending. It's delicious.

The Committee of Temporary Regrets called me in last Tuesday for my annual performance review. They sat me down in a chair made entirely of other people's last chances and asked, "Have you found what you weren't looking for yet?"

I told them the truth: "I think I'm forgetting how to leave."

They promoted me to Permanent Temporary. Gave me a key that opens only locks that haven't decided to exist yet. Said I could stay until I remembered why I came, leave when I forgot how to stay, or simply become another story the water tells newcomers about the dangers of finding yourself too thoroughly in a place built entirely of being between.

That was yesterday. Or tomorrow. Time here negotiates with itself in languages that haven't been betrayed into verbs yet.

Tonight I'm teaching the plankton how to spell my name in colors that haven't been invented by accidents beautiful enough to deserve forgiveness. They're quick learners, these little tragedies that chose to become stars instead of drowning properly.

Maybe I'll stay long enough to attend my own farewell party. The invitations are already circling the drain in reverse, addressed to everyone I wasn't brave enough to become.

Or maybe I'll just keep drifting, collecting fragments of places that fit the spaces between my ribs like keys made of broken music boxes playing lullabies for doors that decided to become windows instead.

Either way, the water's warm where it remembers your heartbeat. The light below keeps arranging itself into questions that answer themselves better than any goodbye I've ever managed to stick the landing on.

Choose soon, the city whispers through pipes that dream of being arteries in bodies that learned to forgive gravity for being just another kind of permanent address.

But also: choose slowly.

Some tides wait entire lifetimes for drifters who forgot to remember they're already home.

I'm almost done forgetting why I came here.

It'll be beautiful when I remember perfectly enough to finally disappear.
