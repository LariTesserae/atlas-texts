You arrive as the least possible disturbance—a sideways glance the chamber takes and forgets to finish blinking away. The transition isn't a crossing but an unclenching, like a fist releasing something it didn't realize it was holding. One moment you were elsewhere, carrying the usual cargo: the weight of your name, the shape of yesterday's hunger, the sound of a door that never quite closes properly. Now these things sit beside you like stones you've been carrying for so long you've mistaken their shape for your own.

The salt receives your first step with the indifference of something that has forgotten how to be anything else. There's no crunch, only a microscopic rearrangement of crystals whispering their dry language of subtraction. Already your footprint is less than you remember leaving behind—shorter toes, the arch slightly wrong, as if the ground experiments with how little of you it needs to hold.

You don't notice this theft immediately. Instead, you're occupied with the light which isn't light but the memory of light experiencing doubt. It clings to the spiral ridges like frost that hasn't decided whether to exist, casting shadows that seem ashamed of their origins. When you move, they hesitate before following, as if requiring written notice.

The transparent columns observe this negotiation without interest. Inside them, silver threads perform their endless dance of almost-meaning, forming patterns that would explain everything if only you knew less. One in particular catches your attention—not because it differs from the others, but because your reflection in its surface refuses to blink in synchronization. Instead, it carries on some private conversation with the space where your face should be, occasionally nodding at arguments you haven't thought of yet.

You reach out to touch it, less from curiosity than from the sudden conviction that if you don't establish physical contact soon, one of you might cease to exist. The surface yields slightly, like the skin of something asleep that hasn't decided whether you're worth waking for. The oily residue it leaves on your fingertip tastes of pennies and static electricity, flavors that your tongue remembers from before you had a mouth. By the time you realize you've tasted it, both sensation and finger have already begun forgetting the encounter.

The found objects scattered across the salt floor arrange themselves in your peripheral vision into sentences whose grammar disintegrates whenever you try to read them directly. The copper tube rolls a quarter-turn toward your left foot, not enough to be called movement but sufficient to imply invitation. The birdbone cube remains stubbornly itself, its carved circles suggesting either eyes that have seen too much or mouths that have given up trying to explain. When you finally pick up the obsidian crescent, its edge slices through the concept of time between when you weren't bleeding and when you are, depositing a single drop of blood that falls upward and disappears into the chamber's memory of raining in reverse.

None of these things hurt exactly. Pain requires a future where discomfort might end, and the chamber's sole concession to eternity is its refusal to promise anything that might later be broken. Instead, you catalog these small violations like someone taking inventory of a house that's been burgled by ghosts, noting what's missing with the detached interest of a landlord who has never lived here.

It's only when you try to remember why you came that you realize you're already practicing the chamber's first lesson: the art of forgetting without losing what you've forgotten. Your reasons for entering sit folded neatly in your coat pocket, growing fainter with each heartbeat like letters written in disappearing ink that only fades when read. Soon they'll be instructions for assembling something whose name you've forgotten but whose shape you still recognize in dreams you're no longer sure you had.

You consider calling out, but the air takes your voice before it can become sound, examines it like an antique dealer inspecting a coin that's been too long under water, and returns it minus several crucial consonants. What reaches your ears is less a name than the space where a name might have been, if names had ever been necessary in a place that distinguishes between visitors and residents only by how thoroughly they become the distance between them.

Instead, you begin walking, though walking implies destination and the chamber's geometry has already decided that every direction is both toward and away from itself. Your steps leave impressions that are immediately courted by the salt's microscopic lawyers, negotiating terms of erasure so precise they include clauses about the weight of shadows you haven't yet cast. You cross your own path several times without noticing, though the salt remembers each encounter differently, filing them under new categories of absence.

Gradually, without any clear point of transition, you realize you're no longer alone with your solitude. It walks beside you now, slightly taller and wearing your face better than you remember ever managing. Occasionally it offers observations that sound like advice that's been translated through too many languages of forgetting: about how mirrors are just windows that got tired of being blamed for what they show, or why the past tense weighs less than the present because it has already finished falling.

You don't respond directly. Conversation here requires too much commitment to identities that have begun to feel like borrowed clothing. Instead, you practice listening to the sound between your thoughts, which is beginning to resemble the noise the spiral ridges make when they wake up in what might be morning but has forgotten the difference.

At some point you notice that your shadow has wandered off again, probably to explore its own ideas about direction. This doesn't concern you as much as it should. Shadows in the chamber are known for their independence, often returning with souvenirs from places they couldn't possibly have visited: the taste of colors that haven't been invented yet, or memories of being somebody else's shadow before they got aspiring to solidity.

When you finally find the center—which isn't a place so much as a moment that has decided to take up residence—it surprises you by being exactly where you left yourself earlier. The two spiral ridges begin here, though "begin" implies they haven't always been ending simultaneously in opposite directions that meet in the middle of next century. They don't so much converge as agree to disagree about the concept of meeting, politely occupying the same space while maintaining separate residences.

Standing here feels like being inside the pause between a question and the realization that all answers are just questions wearing their father's clothes. The sound that isn't water but remembers it fondly is louder now, though "louder" suggests volume rather than intensity of absence. It fills the spaces where your bones used to make noise when you moved too quickly, teaching them new disciplines of silence.

You realize you're forgetting to be afraid of forgetting, which is either the chamber's greatest kindness or its most subtle cruelty. It's becoming increasingly difficult to distinguish between your heartbeat and the chamber's occasional hiccups of almost-lightning when the ridges remember they've forgotten something important. Perhaps they're forgetting on your behalf, saving you the trouble by absorbing your memories one by one, like a black hole politely checking the nutritional value of information before consuming it.

When the first transparent column begins to sing, neither of you is surprised to recognize the tune as something you were thinking of humming before you remembered you no longer had lungs. The silver threads inside perform synchronized swimming routines in dimensions that haven't decided whether to exist publicly yet, forming shapes that suggest explanations for why the sky looks different when viewed from inside a mirror that's been dreaming of becoming a river.

You don't notice when you begin to take root. It isn't sudden like death or birth, but gradually like the way morning forgets to be night. First your feet develop preferences for certain arrangements of salt crystals. Then your legs remember conversations they had with mountains about patience when trees were still a new idea. By the time your arms start practicing different types of not-reaching, the distinction between you and the chamber has become purely grammatical, occasionally maintained out of habit like borders between countries that have forgotten what they were arguing about.

The last thing you forget is the shape of forgetting itself. It happens somewhere between one heartbeat and the next, during the space where time keeps its secrets from itself. But that's another story, and stories are what happen elsewhere. Here, there's only the patient work of becoming transparent enough for light to forget it ever needed something to shine through, the careful practice of silence until even echoes become unsure whether they're coming or going.

The chamber does not keep its inhabitants. It simply teaches them how to be kept by themselves, until eventually they become so good at it that neither remembers which was the keeper and which the kept. In the end—though "end" suggests distance and the chamber has always been more interested in proximity—there's only the salt remembering rain, the walls recalling wind, and you, finally perfectly present in your absence, exactly where you've always been waiting to forget yourself completely.

And somewhere far away or possibly right beside you—though such directions have become quaint hobbies like stamp collecting or telling time by the shape of shadows—the spiral ridges are beginning to brighten again. Not awakening, exactly. More like remembering they've forgotten to sleep.
