I arrive as a draft of wind tasting of graphite—someone's seventh attempt at writing "I miss you" before the paper was crumpled. Not the first attempt's sincerity, nor the final version's resignation, but the raw middle where longing had three too many syllables and "miss" still sounded like mistake.

The city's edges eat into me immediately. My particles shuffle through possible shapes: here a smear of eraser dust remembering palm-sweat, there a pair of empty quotation marks that once held someone's name. Eremonium, the native element of revisions, condenses in my non-lungs. Crystal structures form and collapse between my intentions, building scaffolding for architectures of "almost."

In the Markets of Crossed-Out Lines, vendors sell singularities made from dead-end metaphors. I trade three conditional tenses and an unfinished simile (likening loss to _____) for a pocket of negative space that fits perfectly in my chest cavity. It hums there, resonating with the city's background radiation of deleted things.

Gravity negotiates with me hourly. Sometimes I am weighted, sinking through seventeen drafts of the same sidewalk. Other times I lift like a margin note written too lightly, drifting up past windows where residents practice expressions they'll never wear. A woman on the fourth-and-a-half floor mouths "come back" at me across four different versions of evening, each iteration subtracting one more consonant from forgiveness.

I find employment as a professional hesitation. Businesses here thrive on the geometries of almost-decisions, the profitable friction between yes and what-comes-after-yes. My job: to stand in doorways and represent the infinite infinitesimal pause where choice curves back toward itself. I wear a uniform of three dots suspended in brightness-to-be-adjusted, carry a briefcase filled with alternative endings packed in liquid potential.

During lunch breaks I eat eraser shavings from documents that never existed. They taste like possibilities scraped thin—mostly tax exemptions for guilt, some incorporation papers for regrets under assumed names. The nutrients rearrange my syntax; soon I begin describing myself in second person conditional: you would be taller if childhood had fewer sharp corners. Your eyes might have stayed that color before _____.

At night (which solidifies between the words "good" and "night" in conversations happening simultaneously everywhere), I inhabit the spaces between pages in the Library of Unopened Books. There, I share drinks with marginalia that outlived their texts—one particularly drunk footnote from a vanished philosophy treatise keeps buyrounding rounds of absinthe made from liquified footnotes to itself. We toast to recursion and the courage of parenthetical statements.

Time pays in installments here. I receive a small allowance of deferred moments, spend them carefully on small eternities: the duration between lightning and thunder in storms that never materialize, the lifespan of a name hovering on the tip of a tongue that will never belong to anyone. I save some in a jar labeled "for the emergency of being understood."

In the season of strikethroughs (when leaves pencil themselves onto trees only to erase themselves overnight), I fall in love with a contradiction who lives in the apartment above deleted scenes. She exists most fully during fade-outs, speaks the language of ellipses arranged into constellations. We meet in the buffering space between touching and touched, where skin remembers being air remembers being desire remembers being—

Our wedding is officiated by a typo that became sapient after centuries of being corrected into existence. We exchange vows written on carbon paper pressed together by gravity's hesitation. Our witnesses are all the versions of ourselves that didn't come to this moment—standing in row after row of unchosen aisles, wearing what-might-have-been white and almost-black, raising champagne flutes filled with the sound of doors closing gently, elsewhere.

The marriage lasts exactly one lifetime, measured from the first draft's promising opening to the final manuscript's rejected postscript. In the annulment papers (printed on vanishing ink that reappears only during existential crises), we cite irreconcilable realities.

I move into the house the city prepared: a perfect replica of childhood nightmares built at 0.7 scale, located on the cul-de-sac where all emotional absences intersect. My neighbors include the smell of rain during droughts, the exact weight of silence after certain questions, and a retired god who now only creates slightly disappointing weather. We're all very quiet here.

Sometimes, when the wind blows just right (from directions numbered between pi and regret), I become briefly visible to lost tourists from dimensions where choices are permanent. They ask me for directions to somewhere real. I always tell them the same thing, each time slightly altered:

Go straight on until you reach the intersection of what's-his-name and that-place-we-used-to-go. Turn left where the _____ used to be (you'll know it by the way it isn't there anymore). Continue until you feel like you've gone too far, then go exactly one step further than that. You'll find what you're not looking for in the space between looking and finding. Can't miss it—it's the only thing that's impossible to find on purpose.

They never thank me. They just keep walking through me toward destinations that will rewrite themselves as soon as they arrive, leaving footprints too temporary for even the city's memory to retain.

I am becoming more exact in my ambiguities now—less draft, more edit. Soon I will be precise enough to vanish completely, a perfectly empty set of parentheses enclosing only the echo of what might have been placed between them.
