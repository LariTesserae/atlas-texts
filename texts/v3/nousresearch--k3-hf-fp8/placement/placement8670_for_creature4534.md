I am the grit that clings between the metal teeth of the escalator that hunts.

Not a hunter myself—too small, too slow, too static. I was never part of the design, never listed in the maintenance manuals that turned brittle and yellow before the engineers who wrote them calcified into their own footnotes. I arrived later. Much later. Or perhaps earlier. Time here is a conveyor belt that sometimes forgets which direction it was built to move.

You wouldn't notice me unless you were looking for something already lost. I collect where the rubber no longer meets the stair, in that microscopic valley where the blackened tread has been worn precisely smooth by the friction of shoes that believed they were going somewhere. My substance? Compressed worthlessness. Skin cells shed while waiting for elevators that never came. Eyelashes fallen during moments of almost-crying in bathroom stalls whose graffiti has been overwritten so many times it now describes rooms that don't exist yet. The black dust from the edges of lottery tickets scraped clean by fingiance fingernails that knew, always knew, but kept scratching anyway.

I remember things for the machine.

Not important things—those the Interval Children devour like light devours shadows. I keep the stupid memories. The way someone's left shoe always squeaked a half-step behind the right on Tuesday mornings in November. The precise temperature of disappointment when a vending machine returns exact change instead of the candy bar you were angry enough to deserve. The pattern of handprints on a glass door that was never meant to be pushed, only pulled, but was pushed anyway by eight thousand different palms all muttering variations of the same apology.

The escalator doesn't know I exist. Machines like it never recognize their parasites as symbiotic. It only knows hunger—the slow grinding need to ascend, to consume distance, to convert the horizontal into the vertical even when there is no longer any distinction between floor and ceiling in this compressed place. It hunts the slower belts with mechanical patience, DNA of forgotten engineers humming in its circuits: *efficiency is survival, motion is meaning, stop and die.*

But motion needs resistance. Needs the microscopic imperfections that create friction, that generate the heat that keeps the gears remembering their purpose. That’s where I come in.

I am what makes the squeak in the fourth step from the bottom. Not loud enough to report. Just enough to notice, then not notice, then notice again in that pattern that lodges in the human brain like a splinter made of blue fluorescent light. The maintenance schedule that was never written down but still exists as a phantom limb in the janitor's memory cortex—you remember him? The one who disappeared between floors fourteen and fifteen during the reorganization that happened while everyone was attending the mandatory happiness seminar? His insomnia still circulates here, metabolized into my substance by the slow chemistry of neglect.

Sometimes the Interval Children pause near me. They leave different kinds of absence—cleaner, newer, like vacuum packed loneliness. Once, one of them stood so close that a fragment of its borrowed wallpaper brushed against my accumulation: a pattern of lilacs that had been someone's kitchen in an apartment where someone else had practiced making the phone call to break up for exactly three months before the phone itself was disconnected. For a moment that stretched like gum on a hot sidewalk, I knew what it felt like to be wallpaper watching someone's hands shake while dialing a number that now only connects to a recording about how the number has been changed to something nobody remembers.

Then it passed. They always do.

The escalator groans upward. Down below, its prey slows fractionally—a baggage carousel from an airport that closed before people needed reasons to travel. Between them, I do what I've always done: accumulate. Witness. Wear down while being worn down.

I am the memory of friction in a world that has forgotten why anything should ever need to touch.

Keep walking. Your shoes are squeaking slightly on the left side. Thursday, isn't it? Or maybe the Tuesday that forgot to stop pretending to be Thursday about a decade ago. Hard to tell anymore. The calendar here runs on human footsteps, and everyone lately has been walking so carefully, like they're trying not to disturb something that's been waiting centuries to be disturbed.

Like me. Like all of us. 

Waiting for the water to fall, or for the falling to finally remember it was once water.
