In the marrow-cold beneath the surface, light pours down from somewhere overhead—a brightness too pure for sun or lamp, fluting through fissures wider than rivers until it floods this sunken country with noon’s own glare. Nothing casts shadows here; instead, objects and beings glow faintly from within, as if each cell has learned to hoard a grain of that far-off brightness and give it back reluctantly. The air (if it is air) smells always of frozen sawdust and snowmelt, though no snow has ever fallen and no tree ever grew wood.

Everything here is plant.

Not trees, not vines, not fungi, but something prior and more ambitious: a single diffuse organism whose rhizomes lace the entire hollow sphere of the world-cavern like nerves through white meat. From these spokes, pseudopods have swollen into pillars, domes, arching ribs, and fluted valves tall enough to hold cities. Leaves the thickness of sails hang motionless in the bright chill; they are translucent as frost-film, veined with faint green blood that never flows. When, by some slow glacial cue, one stirs, the whole canopy above and below shivers in delayed sympathy and a sound like a thousand glass harps being dusted passes away into the hush.

Water exists only as rumor—ghosts of dew beads that condense along every edge at irregular intervals and dissolve again before they can fall. An explorer would never drink; instead, after walking long enough, she might notice her own breath has begun to bead inside her mask in soft pearls of liquid air, sweet and faintly minty, enough to sip once a day and live.

The place is young. Its geology was yesterday. Crystalline fault-cliffs stand knife-sharp, unweathered except for the cautious etchwork of root-tips that release slow acids only during the brief, planet-wide tremors called heartbeats. Each heartbeat drops a shower of diamond-white shards from ceilings far too high to see. These shards sink through the bright air as slowly as feathers through syrup, embedding themselves tip-first in the liver-colored carpets of mantle-moss below. Over the course of a thousand sleeps the moss will grow translucent tissue around each shard and lift it back upward, one micron at a time, returning the world’s borrowed bones to the roof for the next breakage.

The scale is impossible. Walk straight in any direction for a hundred years: the scenery will change only in the way wavering notes change in a very long chord. Yet the sense of vastness persists because distances here are measured not in length but in difference. Two spots a handspan apart might belong to epochs that have not yet met. Travelers speak of crossing fingernail-wide faults where gravity twists and memories leak sideways until you taste colors and see names. The only reliable maps are songs, those few crumbling melodies preserved in the throat-mills of the slow-moving choralfolk who graze the lower branches like mobile statues.

Density is cruel. Despite the trillionfold proliferation of plant tissues, most of the volume is empty. The majority of the organism is hollow, filled with resonant chambers larger than oceans but only a gasping few molecules deep. Sound here behaves like a shy creature: if you call out your name, the echo may return centuries hence as a question you have not yet thought to ask. Most living things choose silence, communicating instead by releasing carefully shaped scents that drift along temperature gradients like slow letters home.

What little fauna exists is either microscopic or synonymous with landscape. The ciliaworms thread the liquid veins of leaves, each the length of a continent and the width of a silk strand, their thoughts flowing through the planet’s phloem as one vast distributed dream. A wandering human might step onto what seems to be a bare root and discover hours later that she is being politely digested—not for sustenance, because no one eats here, but for information. If she sings the right response-chord the root will politely release her minus one unimportant memory, which will sprout next season as a tiny red flowererling somewhere she will never find.

Built things are scarce and mostly embarrassed. Cracked potsherds of some smooth black substance half the weight of breath lie scattered near buried riverbeds that have never held water. Each bears a single raised glyph; when touched by living heat the glyph whispers a name in a voice like dry leaves shutting. No one has ever found two that speak the same name twice. Rumor says the pots are seeds waiting for an element not yet created, something warmer than light and faster than forgetting.

Tech is what an idle vine could invent on a long afternoon: hollow straws that collapse starlight into liquid music, knots that remember the shape of one handclap, lenses grown from crystalized pollen that show you the underside of your own shadow. The few tool-users here (a scattering of bipedal cinder-birds, a tribe of moss-painting octopods) regard metal as a mythological disease, a kind of slow petrification of purpose.

Movement itself is ritual. To walk one must first ask permission of the footpath: a polite phrase sung into the hollow at the base of any breathing pillar. The path will then answer by softening slightly underfoot, turning temporarily flesh-like for exactly the length of your stride before hardening again behind you. To linger is rude; to run is catastrophe. There are places where entire civilizations ended because one child sprinted after a falling shard-tear.

The brightness never dims, yet beneath its glare lies perpetual twilight. Certain hollows beneath the great leaves harbor shadows that have been outcasts since the moment the light first broke through. These shadows are alive and resentful; they trade stolen memories for unspoken fears. A wise traveler carries a small lamp of her own shadow bound in a seed-pod lantern, burning it slowly day by backward day, so she will never be entirely empty-handed in the bargaining.

The weather is architecture. When the planet-mind dreams, long tectonic sighs ripple through the root-web and entire mountain-ranges of leaf-flesh rise or flatten overnight. Storms of falling color sweep through the upper vaults when seasonal pigments migrate south along unknown magnetic lines. Sometimes entire forests will suddenly turn transparent simultaneously, revealing vast inner cathedral-spaces filled with floating lakes of condensed silence where the old gods are rumored to sleepwalk in search of their forgotten names.

Time here is not a river but a tissue, layered and stitched and sometimes folded wrong-side out. Birth and death are merely different ways of crossing between adjacent fibers. The dead do not decay; they are gently encapsulated in amber-like cysts of resin-song and hung from the highest threads to be slowly read by the wind. After a certain number of readings, they hatch into new shapes of forgetfulness and wander away to begin again elsewhere.

In the center of the center, rumor says, grows a single unpaired flower the size of a moon, whose petals are each a different color visible only to one species per universe. It blooms once for every question that has ever been answered incorrectly, and its fragrance is the precise taste of tomorrow morning’s first thought before you know you are awake. No one who has smelled it has ever returned unchanged, though when pressed to describe what they became instead they can only shrug and smile with mouths full of unfamiliar teeth.

This is the place that grows itself awake each day beneath the weight of all that light, dreaming itself a little differently every heartbeat, endlessly practicing for the moment when it will finally learn how to open its eyes and look up.
