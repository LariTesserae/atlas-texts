The place begins as a shallow bowl in the earth's face, vast enough that its rim merges with the horizon on every side. A crater, perhaps, though no scarred rock or fused glass recalls violence: only soft clay, warm to the touch, kneaded once and left to rest.  
From this depression the land rises—a series of gentle terraces like frozen ripples, each step narrower than the last until the final rim stands barely a body’s height above the surrounding plain. Between terraces run narrow channels, dry and smooth, half a thumb’s width apart and perfectly parallel as if combed by an absent-minded giant.  
The plain itself is pale yellow, coarse as shredded silk no microscope could weave—pure silica shells of microscopic creatures long since dust. Nothing grows there; nothing rots. Footprints fill slowly behind themselves, the air too still to remember disturbance.

Drop into the bowl. The air thickens, becomes almost humid though no water gleams. Sound stops short here too, muffled like breath inside a clasped hand.  
Turn slowly. Every surface of every terrace is occupied.

It is a forest without trees, a city without streets.  
Each wide ledge is paved from edge to edge with flat, leaf-shaped plates of living matter: overlapping wings of dull green, russet, ochre, charcoal, veined in patterns that shimmer faintly then forget their order before the eye can hold it.  
From seams between plates rise towers no taller than a child—slender hollow cylinders whorled like cowries, capped with swollen bulbs that swell and shrink in slow rhythm, inhaling nothing, exhaling nothing visible. Between them humps of softer tissue swell into domed rooms, their translucent skins alive with sluggish currents of darker, lighter, darker again.

This is both animal and architecture.  
The forest does not feed on light: beneath each plate lie patches of silvery crystal that absorb heat directly from the warm soil and kindle slow chemical marriages—sulfur with sulfur, metal with halogen, carbon copied in rings and chains that serve instead of watery blood.  
Nor does it drink. Instead, at precise hours that can be felt rather than seen, the outermost ring of the lowest terrace all swell together until something cracks and a mist of fine droplets hisses out into the central air. That mist falls as black rain, invisible on black skin, and an instant later every tower and every dome is humming softly like fingers rubbed on wet glass. Then quiet returns. The tags of moisture are harvested again by microscopic valves before they reach ground level.  
The community thirsts, sips, and is content.

There is no empty space.  
There are only passages: tunnels a wrist deep between plates where footless bodies like flattened slugs glide on belts of bristles, ferrying packets of powdered shell, fragments of crystal, or tiny sealed buds wrapped in membrane.  
From below come faint clicks as plates contract to let them pass, then click again to seal the seam as if nothing had ever moved.  
The air smells faintly of hot stone, faintly of copper, faintly of something like dry grass after rain that never fell.

Walk carefully.  
At the second terrace the plates become tougher, cooled slightly by offset ducts that throat upward from below; here the towers branch now and then into triple crowns, each bearing at its apex an eyeless cup lined with hairs that tremble at your passing breath but never turn to follow.  
Between cups hang threads of spun glass no thicker than thought, woven into nets that tremble ceaselessly though no wind stirs them. Something like meaning travels along those nets—a pulse of heat, a silence that is the absence of heat—sentences of nothing written in the language of thermals.  
The slugs avoid stepping over the threads; they take detours measured in millimeters, yet never collide.  
This is the grid that tells the whole living city when a fragment of sky-grown meteorite seeds the edge, or when an intrusion from the plain brings too much unknown chemistry, or when simply it time to breathe again.

The third terrace is darker. The plates slope inward now; the towers open their sides into latticeworks that fold and unfold like slow origami fans, exposing and hiding interior chambers whose walls are lined with rows of lenses—each lens black, each pupil shuttered by a pupil by a pupil to infinity.  
Here the density becomes almost unbearable. You must crawl. The air presses warmer, tastes of iron.  
Beneath every surface surge rivers of liquid metal—tiny slow rivulets of mercury-silver moving through channels carved not by hand but by centuries of selective pressure favoring the crystals that dissolved just so much rock and no more.  
In the greatest domes stand objects like statues: towering columns of stacked rings, each ring another living plate grown along a fault line of impossible geometry. At the base of one such pillar a slug has stopped. It presses its blunt head into a crevice, trembles violently, and bursts.  
From its ruptured skin pours a cloud of motes finer than dust: spores, seeds, fragments of instructions.  
The pillar drinks them through a thousand mouths.  
Far away, on the plain beyond sight, something that will never be either slug or tower shudders awake and begins to secrete its first tiny collar of silica.

You have reached the center.

Here is not a plaza but an eye.  
An emptiness perfectly round, ten strides wide, rimmed by the tallest towers yet—each a single spiral climbing until it too bends inward and becomes part of the roof overhead, weaving above you the living dome of this hollow world.  
The floor of the eye is naked clay, untouched except for one thing: a shallow depression exactly the shape of a kneeling human body.  
There is no invitation; no prohibition.  
Above you, in the dome’s apex, a final bulb swells larger than all others, translucent enough to show the slow churn within of colorless liquids dividing and rejoining in patterns like cities seen from night heights.  
The bulb pulses once.  
Something in the clay remembers hoof beats, remembers rain, remembers voices chanting numbers to a drumbeat of hands on hide.  
Nothing answers.

This is the Library.  
Not of books or bones, but of contingencies: every possible arrangement of crystals that might, given heat and time and absence of water, dream of becoming alive again.  
The slugs bring fragments of the world’s accidents—meteor dust, a fleck from a traveler’s boot, even the shed scales of your own skin already drifting unnoticed in the still air.  
The Library dissolves them, plays every game that chemistry allows, and discards all outcomes except those that continue to play themselves.  
Then it waits.

The plain outside forgets. This bowl remembers.  
The plain grows colder, night by night.  
Within, the dance never stops; it only slows, until a breath drawn here takes longer than a star’s death elsewhere.  
But the rules remain constant:

Feed on difference.  
Breathe only once in a thousand years.  
And never, ever, open the eye except in the presence of one who has already seen what waits behind it.

The silence here is not empty. It is packed as tight as the living stone, endlessly rehearsing every sound that could ever break it.  
And somewhere among those rehearsals—so faint that even the Library’s most delicate threads have not yet woven it into certainty—  
there is a word for water.
