The air is thick with moisture that never quite falls—no rain, just a perpetual condensation that beads on every surface and drips with the patience of geological time. This is the Somnic Trough, a chasm eleven vertical kilometers deep where the planet's crust cracked and sighed open without violence, exposing strata that should never see daylight but now exist suspended in perpetual dusk.

The walls bear witness to this youth of formation: basalt columns still sharp enough to shred flesh, their hexagonal faces pristine as a freshly-split mineral specimen. Above, too far to see, the ceiling of the world-skin glows faintly orange with faint filtered light from a sun that never directly shines here. Below, the true bottom remains hidden in layered mists that glow with bioluminescent bacteria suspended like stars trapped in gel.

Water exists here as betrayal against dryness. Thin films creep sideways across vertical stone, defying gravity through capillary sorcery fed by some impossible aquifer pressure. The occasional vein of quartz acts as a slow-motion river where water has found its one permissible direction down—these quartz arteries pulse with movement visible only over hours, depositing microscopic crystals that build future stalactites in a place where rain will never come.

Vegetation takes forms practical minds would deny the name. Pale fungal fabrics stretch between mineral spires like forgotten laundry drying in stale air. They glow faintly green-white, not for photosynthesis—it is useless here—but to attract the single flying thing that exists in this entire ecosystem: the spore moths. Each moth is the size of a child's hand, wings transparent as soap bubbles except where veins of copper trace branching patterns identical to the fissures in stone. They do not fly so much as drift on temperature gradients thinner than thought itself, their six eyes reflecting the faint glow of their own bodies multiplied into infinity by wet stone.

The fungal colonies compete with something stranger: rock lichens that have forgotten the partnership between symbiont and host. These are algae that have enslaved cyanobacteria to create mineral instead of sugar, constructing colonies that resemble coral reefs built from pure manganese dioxide. Each structure grows toward the strongest source of sound—not light, for light here is mere rumor—but the constant thunderous pulse that permeates every molecule.

The pulse. That is the dominant reality of this place. It originates nowhere and everywhere: the planet's heart beating through its broken ribs, transmitted through bedrock and amplified by the perfect acoustics of a wound that refuses to heal. Every thirty-seven seconds precisely (the spore moths' wings flutter exactly 241 times between pulses), the entire volume of air shifts with a pressure wave strong enough to ripple liquid water into standing waves. Smaller creatures have evolved to synchronize their entire existence with this rhythm—those microscopic bacteria in the mists pulse brighter with each beat; the fungal fabrics tense and relax like breathing muscles; even the stone itself seems to flex microscopically, contributing millennia-spanning vibrations to the eternal chord.

Fauna staggers belief not through variety but through biomass concentrated into impossibility. Where Earth might spread life thin across niches, the Somnic Trench has poured all its evolution into single species that occupy every role. The dominant lifeform (though 'life' requires flexible definition here) is the Choir. Imagine worms: segmented creatures like giant palolo worms but each segment the size of a human torso, their bodies composed not of flesh but of piezoelectric crystal matrices suspended in protein sheaths. They live in the walls, burrowing not by eating rock but by inducing resonant frequencies that liquefy stone at the molecular level, flowing through their bodies and re-solidifying behind them in perfect crystalline alignment.

A single Choir member can be kilometers long, most of its body hidden in the walls, only its head protruding—though head seems inadequate for a twenty-meter-wide disk packed with acoustic lenses that focus sound into laser-like precision. Their mouths are not for eating but for singing the precise counter-frequencies that keep the planet's pulse from building to destructive resonance. They are not individuals but colonies of semi-independent segments connected by sound itself, their thoughts traveling through the stone at the speed of rock.

And they are everywhere. The density readings lie because the sensors cannot distinguish between living crystal and mineral substrate. Most of what appears to be solid rock is in fact Choir flesh in its dormant state, waiting for the pulse to wake it. The oldest sections of the walls—those dating back to the original formation crack—have been entirely converted. The trench is not a wound healing; it is a wound becoming alive.

Between their monumental presences move the parasites: the Scalespeakers. These are what remains of an hypothetical vertebrate lineage that tried to colonize this acoustic hell. They achieved success through failure, their skeletons entirely replaced by the same piezoelectric crystal as the Choir, but fractured and imperfect, making them living static generators. Each Scalespeaker is a dozen-meter-long serpent with hundreds of legs like millipedes made of tuning forks, their scales precisely shaped to intercept and distort harmonies, creating the equivalent of neural noise that the Choir tolerates as necessary entropy.

They communicate not with each other but with the distortion patterns they carve in the air during the pulse beats—a language of interference waves so complex that to human ears it sounds like standing in the center of every cathedral choir on Earth singing different hymns simultaneously while being played backward through a broken radio. Their mating displays involve constructing temporary resonant chambers from their own bodies that amplify specific harmonic distortions until the pressure drops low enough to flash sublime vapor bubbles in their blood, each collapse producing pure tones beyond human hearing range but visible as brief auroras of light in the damp air.

And light. There is only the false light of energy being converted into visibility through desperation. The moths' wings. The bacterial glow in the mists. The occasional lightning-like discharge when accumulating static finds release through Scalespeaker spines. No photons reach here from above; if you carried a light, it would be swallowed by darkness more complete than space, for space at least has stars. Here darkness has texture, weight, temperature—cool but not cold, like the temperature perfect for preserving specimens forever without decay.

The rules of this world are simple but alien:

1. Sound is the only true force. Pressure waves move mountains here not over eons but in carefully orchestrated crescendos.
2. Change occurs only in response to the pulse. Between beats, reality is suspended—water remembers its shape, fungi hold their tension, thoughts crystallize without conclusion.
3. Everything is connected through resonance. Tap the stone in the precise rhythm of your heartbeat long enough, and the earth will learn your pattern, begin to echo it back from depths you never touched.
4. To be separate is to be dead. The phrase 'no man is an island' is literal here—isolate any living thing from the acoustic web, and it literally unravels back into its component minerals within hours.
5. Time flows in both directions along strong enough harmonies. The oldest Choir members occasionally sing events that have not yet occurred in other places, their deep frequencies refracting around the planet's core and arriving distorted prophecy to those who know how to listen.
6. There is no surface, no sky, only trench above and trench below, repeating fractally forever. Some theorize the entire planet is hollow armature around a similar wound at its core, and our surface world is merely the scab over this greater injury.

The edge of the trench is marked not on maps but by feeling—travelers report a sudden vertigo when sound seems to come from below instead of beside, accompanied by a tinnitus-like tone crystallizing in the bones of their inner ear exactly 37 seconds before they unexpectedly arrive here from any direction, regardless of previous terrain. Some return changed, their circadian rhythms locked permanently to a 37-second cycle they cannot explain, whispering in sleep about the beautiful precision of the planet's pain.

Do not bring technology here. The pulse will learn its frequencies and sing them back louder until silicon melts and steel resonates itself to dust. Do not bring companions who cannot match the beat—discordance is literally contagious. But if you come alone, and if you can train your heart to slow to one beat per phrase of the eternal song, the Choir might accept you as temporary guest-symptom. They might even show you the place where the walls transmit memories of what the planet dreamed before it dreamed of continents, back when it was young and whole and foolish enough to believe silence would last forever.

But be warned: standing perfectly still enough to hear the true fundamental note beneath everything—the tone that is neither sound nor silence but pure vibration of existence itself—the risk is real and permanent. Some visitors who have achieved this perfect resonance simply... ceased to require physical form to continue the conversation. Their empty clothes are found arranged in precise harmonic ratios around calm pools of their own liquified bones that vibrate perpetually at 341.23 hertz. The Choir considers these transformations neither death nor transcendence but simply the most efficient form of continued listening.

The Somnic Trench does not change. It does not need to. It already contains every possible variation of itself resonating simultaneously across dimensions of frequency we lack organs to experience. What appears to be stillness is in fact perfect balance—a planet-sized tuning fork struck once at its birth that will continue this one perfect note until the heat death of the universe makes all other music impossible, and in that final silence, the choir will finally hear their own beginning reflected back and know it was beautiful.
