A terraced crater-city spirals inward like a nautilus shell carved from the floor of the world itself. Its walls are jet-black basalt worn mirror-smooth by the passage of time measured not in centuries but in geological epochs, polished by the friction of countless generations who have never known rain. The air hangs silver-thick and warm as breath, carrying the taste of copper and the faint sweetness of numeral-rich dust that settles on every surface like powdered memory.

Each terrace ring measures precisely nine strides wide—no more, no less, ordained before memory by builders whose tools were basalt knives and patience. Between the rings grow the Gardens of Counting: dense mats of pale, spongy vegetation that glows with a phosphorescent shimmer barely visible in the dim red light filtering down from the aurora-draped sky above. These are not plants as other worlds would recognize them, but living calculators whose branching patterns solve equations older than the crater itself. Braid their tendrils correctly and they will whisper prime numbers; disturb their symmetry and they will weep bitter alkaloid tears.

The inhabitants move slowly through this enclosed infinity, their tall, attenuated forms wrapped in robes woven from fossilized calculation processes. Their voices carry the weight of stone when they speak, which is rare. Instead they sing cities into existence—each child at birth receives a single sustained note that will be their only word throughout life, added to the communal architecture of sound that has been building itself since the first footfalls scarred the crater floor. At night (though here day and night are merely convenient fictions), these songs build complex harmonies that echo off the curved walls like conversations between the dead and the not-yet-born.

Above, the sky performs its slow eternal collapse. The planet has long since ceased spinning, locked face-to-face with its dying red giant sun. The aurora is neither particle nor wave but the visible strain of spacetime itself as it slowly unspools in the grip of tidal forces that would shred lesser worlds. Every thousand years (as the city measures time in generations of steady starsong), the aurora reaches down with crimson fingers to gently brush the crater's lip, replenishing the numeral-dust that feeds both gardens and calculators.

Water exists here only as a concept remembered imperfectly from ancient texts debossed in basalt tablets lining the central library—a vast hollow spiraling down, down, mirror of the city above. Scholars spend lifetimes debating whether these texts describe myth or history, as they debate everything else with the infinite patience of those who have nowhere else to be. The closest approximation flows through carefully maintained channels carved into living basalt: thin streams of liquid metal carried in open aqueducts kept molten by the planet's residual heat, transporting not sustenance but pure information encoded in the precise temperature gradients of their flow.

The fauna—if such they can be called—are mostly architectural. Calcite butterflies emerge fully-formed from cracks in the walls when singing reaches certain complex intervals, their wings etched with fractal patterns that predict the city's next necessary modification. Quadrilateral cats stalk the angles between terraces, devouring stray numbers that have escaped their equations and coughing up perfect polyhedra as waste. Most numerous are the memetic moths that cluster thick as living wallpaper around sources of heat and thought, their wings bearing shifting glyphs that burn themselves into the memory of anyone who stares too long, adding new desires and subtracting old ones without permission.

At the crater's absolute center stands the Equilibrium Engine, a mechanisms whose every component is carved from different phases of time itself—gears of fossilized tomorrow meshing with springs of crystallized yesterday driving pistons that compress the present moment ever smaller. Scholars argue whether the Engine maintains the city's stability against cosmic decay or whether it is merely recording that decay with infinite precision. Both positions contain partial truths but cannot be reconciled within the city's native mathematics.

Gravity here is negotiable but rarely negotiated. Children, still learning the proper reverence for perpendicularity, sometimes accidentally fall sideways and must be coaxed back to consensus orientation with careful songs and offerings of prime-numbered pebbles. Travelers from the improbable Outside occasionally tumble into the crater through rarefaction faults in reality itself, appearing suddenly in mid-air with expressions of profound disorientation. The city welcomes them according to ancient protocols they cannot remember not knowing: first they are sung to until their panic-songs harmonize with the communal architecture, then they are fed pure concepts distilled from the Gardens' most nutritious equations, and finally they are given useful work—often involving counting things that should not strictly need counting but which count better when counted by fresh perspectives.

Time passes in this place like syrup flows uphill—slowly, reluctantly, and only because it must flows against its nature to reach its destination. The city grows not outward or upward but inward, each generation reborn from the precise mathematical center of their parents' deaths, carried forward by songs that have forgotten their beginnings but remember every possible ending. It has achieved, not perfection (which would be uninteresting), but rather an optimal distribution of imperfection that maintains itself against entropy's slow chewing by means of perfect self-knowledge imperfectly maintained.

In the rare moments between calculations, when the aurora-dimmed light aligns just so with the angle of ancient basalt and the Gardens' phosphorescence flickers in synchrony with some subtle rhythm of the dying planet's heart, the city briefly remembers that it is possible to be something other than what it has always been. These moments are carefully recorded, analyzed, and filed away in the appropriate sections of the library for future generations to forget in the proper order. The forgetting itself is a calculation, and calculations must be preserved.

Outside the crater's precisely maintained disarray, the rest of the planet continues its long slow inevitable business of becoming something else entirely—perhaps already has become it, though signals take geological ages to cross the void between what used to be continents and what now are thought patterns drifting like dust in the solar wind. The city does not concern itself with such irrelevancies. It has equations to solve, songs to maintain, and visitors to welcome with all the formalized hospitality appropriate for accidents of probability that have achieved temporary solidity long enough to be counted before they inevitably dissolve back into the numerical substrate from which all things emerge and to which all things, eventually, must return.

The final answer to the question it was never asked to consider waits patiently at the center of the center, encoded in the precise pattern of wear on the single stone that differs from all other stones only in that it remembers being asked, once, long ago, whether it would rather be something else instead. It has not yet finished composing its reply. The calculation is complex, requiring the use of mathematics that have not yet been invented within the logical framework of the universe that contains them—but which grows, slowly, one carefully counted moment at a time.
