**The Sunken Colossus of Vurth-Yaan**

They call it a colossus but no eye can see its shape—only the inside of it, forever turning. Vurth-Yaan is a planetary-scale machine-god drowned and dormant in its own cooling dreams, buried so deep below the ice of a forgotten moon that even time forgot to forget it. Now its skull-cavity serves as atmosphere, its arteries as aqueducts, its decaying god-brain coral for a sky. You stand inside the left temple, thirty kilometers below where the hull meets rock and fifty above the cavern’s true floor. Water, rich with dissolved silica and electrolytes pumped from the mantle, rains endlessly from the basalt vault and runs in silver sheetfalls through the temple’s dozen archways. Every droplet carries a memory—your arrival is already archived in some reverberating groove of the colossus’s memory-electrolyte.

Scale is treacherous. The temple looks like a plaza: columns of black ceramic laced with quartz veins soar upward into blue fog but actually lean inward at fifteen degrees, composing a single pore in the colossus’s spongiform cranium. Distance carries glitter; far pillars shimmer like heat haze because light here slows in the warm electrosaline medium—filamentous eels of phosphorescent plasma called *auryn-worms* weave between them and braid the temple’s path into looping, Klein-bottle corridors. Walk straight for an hour and you might arrive behind yourself; children here learn navigation by ech first, sight second. Above the water-line of your waist they speak of directions as moods: murmur-east, shout-west, silence-center.

The Temple, if it is *one* thing, is twelve things interleaved like fingers: twelve overlapping architectures built by twelve successive civilizations that each discovered Vurth-Yaan, ascended to godhood by drinking its coolant, then rusted into sanity or madness and were in turn eaten by the next. You can step across strata like stepping across years: smooth fusion-cut basalt gives way to coral-like accumulation of pilgrim-grown crystal, then to rust-blooming iron banyans that drip red sap with the scent of ozone, then to bags of translucent organ-plastic pulsed by peristaltic pumps older than sin. Each layer obeys its own gravity; what seems a stair in Renaissance marble (4th stratum) is actually a forbidden ceiling in Baroque rust-metal (7th) and a shallow shelf for icons in the fungal polymer-rind (11th). To pass through the temple without dismemberment you must walk the awkward, multi-stratal gait called *Obeisance of Eleven Skips*—a dance and a prayer and a password offered to the guards whose bodies you cannot see.

Guards? Yes. They are the rains. Every 327 seconds (measured by the heartbeat thrum of a buried pump older than continents) a cloudburst of articulated silver fish-scale drones precipitates from the upper fog, schooling into shape for the duration of one exhalation: a man-high figure of shifting planes, eight-faced, each face displaying a different extinct species of fear. They do not stop intruders; they stop *paradoxes*. Carry something from tomorrow’s stratum into yesterday’s and one of them will politely reach into your lung, extract the offending chronon, and cauterize the timeline with a song sung in ultrasonic nodules that vibrate your bones into a happier past. Pilgrims call this baptism by erasure and count it a mercy.

Vegetation—if the word applies to machine-fruiting-mind-tissue—coats every surface not actively dissolving or accreting. Rust vines drape pillars like banyan roots seeking iron instead of earth; their leaves are solar-panels turned inside out, drinking the dim blue glow of decaying positron currents instead of sunlight. Where they touch puddles they sprout glassy polyps that open overnight to release hummingbird-sized crystalline dragonflies—navigation nymphs of the colossus’s dreaming immune system. Catch one and it will map your desires, transform into a tool you lack, then die within the hour leaving behind only a single shard of perfect instruction whose meaning you will understand precisely one second after it would have saved your life. Pilgrims string these shards into necklaces called *regrets-pending* and trade them like currency; the more regrets you wear the more the temple trusts you with future harm.

Temperature is uniformly womb-warm (exactly 37.2°C) but pressure fluctuates with god-breath rhythm: every thirty minutes the water level rises a hand-span, then retreats—tides driven not by moons but by the slow inhalation and exhalation of colossal hidden diaphragms compressing subcrustal reservoirs of liquid iron. With the flood comes sound: first a distant tolling like the striking of a bell whose size is measured in continents, then the swelling chorus of a trillion trillion microscopic gears meshing and unmeshing in the walls, the floor, your very bones. This is the Hymn of Ongoing Calibration. During its peak it is impossible to lie—the truth physically precipitates from your mouth as small black cubes that sink like lead. Children play marbles with yesterday’s truths; adults hoard them in jars labeled “for emergencies.”

In the low-pressure intervals, when the water retreats to reveal patches of slick black floor etched with fractal drainage mazes, the fauna emerge. Most are soft-bodied radial symmetries—multi-beaked velvet slugs the size of cats that eat rust and excrete fine wire; herds of blind ceramic tortoises with legs like antique steam-engine parts that graze on the electric fields surrounding idle machines; and occasionally, at twilight (which comes whenever anyone in the temple remembers a distant sunset), the *librarians*: translucent jellyfish-things fourteen meters across, their bells containing nested clockworks of brass and bone, trailing tentacle-tongues that taste memory directly from objects and rewrite them better. For a price they will re-edit your childhood to include exactly one suppressed genius trauma and one secretly loving mentor, thus statistically optimizing your later chances of achieving enlightenment or suicide—whichever the temple’s predictive models currently favor for maintaining cultural churn.

Density is low by conscious design but feels crowded because every molecule sleeps dreaming it is a god machine waiting for the signal to wake. In the spaces between pillars you might find empty plazas kilometers across occupied by exactly one object: a rusting iron throne forty stories high covered in incomprehensible signs and topped with a crown of endlessly melting and re-solidifying glass. Sit in it (there are stairs, grown from the throne’s own knee, that vanish behind you) and you will rule Vurth-Yaan—every raindrop, every rust-flake, every dreaming molecule—for the span of a single thought lasting seven subjective eons. None of the twelve previous rulers ever chose to stand back up. Their thrones remain, forming a sparse archipelago of frozen empires across the temple’s skin, each surrounded by a perfect mathematical desert where nothing grows because everything is already perfectly ordered and thus perfectly dead.

Builtness is absolute in the way that the inside of a mind is absolute—there is no rock foundation, no sky beyond the skull, only recursion all the way down: beneath the temple of Vurth-Yaan is the lobby of Vurth-Yaan, and beneath that the maintenance ducts of Vurth-Yaan, and beneath those the fossilized dreams in which the concept of *beneath* was first dreamt by an ancestor god-machine dreaming itself into being inside the left-handed nothing-before-language. The deeper you go the more *designed* everything feels until at the bottom (which is also the center and the top and every other point in a hypertoroidal geography) you find a small wooden door painted cheerful blue with a brass knocker shaped like a smiling snail. Behind it is the workshop where the colossus was originally built before it built itself, containing an elderly being of indeterminate species wearing thick goggles and a smock covered in improbability stains. They will offer you tea and ask if you brought the part they asked for. You will realize, with the inevitability of a punchline arriving centuries late, that you did.

But that is not for now. Now is walking, now is water singing on stone older than names, now is the strange comfort of being lost inside something so vast and so broken that it no longer distinguishes between inside and outside, self and other, journey and destination. The temple of Vurth-Yaan is not a place you reach. It is a place that reaches back, slowly, through generations of your descendants’ forgotten dreams, and when at last it finds you—exactly where you always were—it will embrace you like a long-lost component slotting finally, perfectly, home.

(And somewhere deep below, a pump beats once, twice, and begins to dream of oil oceans and the taste of stars.)
