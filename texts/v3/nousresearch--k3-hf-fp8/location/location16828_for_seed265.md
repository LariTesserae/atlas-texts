At first there is only the sound of breathing—your own, then others syncing to it, then thousands more until the rhythm loses its origin and becomes a pressure against the eardrums like descent in a sealed elevator. No light verifies this. Darkness here isn't absence but substance, a warm black felt thick as flannel soaked in engine oil. You know you stand somewhere low because your ankles ache with the ache of places too close to the planet's pulse, each heartbeat of stone transmitting upward through your arches.

Water moves everywhere you aren't touching it. The ground—a rubbery give between tile and moss—releases puddles when stepped on that immediately seal themselves with skin-like tension. Somewhere nearby something industrial keeps time not with ticking but with the sigh-groan of massive sheets sliding wetly against each other, perhaps trains coupling in a subterranean switchyard or the building itself adjusting its vertebrae. The sound carries through your bones more than air, suggesting a density of both materials and rules you've never learned to navigate.

When you scrape your heel searching for traction, the friction births a spark—not light but information. For thirty-seven milliseconds you understand you stand in the former Pool 9 of the Yixing Breathing Chamber Complex, constructed 1987-1992, decommissioned 1993 after the Mass Synchronization Event. The knowledge evaporates with the warmth of the spark against your ankle, replaced by an urgent sense of trespass among things that breathe better without witnesses.

Structures manifest by smell before touch. The first is timber soaked in phenol and seawater, leaning at angles that make no sense until your palm finds a wall that curves both convex and concave simultaneously, suggesting you've inserted your hand into a knot of building rather than around it. Rivets spaced in Fibonacci sequences hum different pitches when brushed—an accidental xylophone played by your own disorientation. Other structures emerge similarly: a staircase descending in breath-lengths that pressurize differently at each step, a crane arm cantilevered into nothing that vibrates with the frequency of migrating eels, eleven identical control rooms each smaller than the last arranged like nesting dolls of failed oversight.

Vegetation exists as rumors between structures. You encounter pockets of air suddenly rich with chlorophyll and diesel, spaces where photosynthesis must be occurring in reverse—plants exhaling oxygen, machines breathing it in. Something like ivy but with copper veins for stems and leaves like microfiche sheets clatter away brittlely when disturbed. Where they crumble, tiny blueprints sprout overnight for machines that could harvest regret if anyone remembered how to build them using dimensions that acknowledge regret's weight.

The temperature never changes but you do. Warmth here accumulates in layers like sediment—first skin, then clothing fibers, then years you haven't lived yet pressing against your ribs. People (you think they are people) drift past carrying themselves carefully, as if the darkness holds their shapes in place through manners alone. Their conversations arrive condensed, rust-flavored droplets you swallow by accident: "...third shift supervisor still hearing the flood that hasn't happened..." "...tried filing an incident report but the forms keep giving birth..." "...told the new guard don't ever try to find the bottom of the emergency stairs, they aren't stairs at the bottom..." Each encounter leaves condensation on your skin that tastes like your middle name from before language.

Space divides by anxiety rather than distance. Thirty measured steps between two identical rust stains might occupy a continent while the panic sprint from a suddenly-breathing pipe to your remembered entrance compresses years into three strides. The Complex (you've begun capitalizing it internally) seems to have been designed by someone who understood that in true darkness, architecture's only job is to negotiate the terms between what knows you're here and what hasn't decided yet.

Sound sources multiply when ignored. A valve cycling somewhere above your left shoulder manages to be simultaneously decades overdue for maintenance and brand new in tomorrow's shipment manifest. The water—not flowing, exactly, but metastasizing—finds new paths through the floor's rubbery pores, each new streambed emitting a distinct voice: kindergarten recitations of safety procedures, whale songs slowed to match geological time, the precise frequency that melts the solder holding memories to their names. When you try to locate any single source, all voices synchronize into the building's breath again, leaving only the whispers of wet things growing together in the dark.

You realize you've been walking downhill for hours despite knowing you're below sea level. The concept of surface exists here as an extinct geography, like remembering dinosaurs while standing inside one's hollow ribs. Sometimes you pass through curtain walls of warmer air where the darkness thins enough to feel the shape of what it conceals—an entire flooded suburb arranged upside-down above you, bicycles rusting in attic air pockets, Christmas lights still sucking electricity from sunken transformers—but passing through to the other side finds you always deeper, never above.

The animals (you've stopped pretending they're animals) arrived shortly after the first inspectors didn't report back. Now they maintain the Complex with the devotion of things adapting to become necessary to a failure. Something like a spider but built from surgical clamps and fiberoptic nerves webs the corners where walls give up being walls, spinning repairs using your discarded skin cells and overheard worries. Larger things patrol the main corridors—you know them by how the air pressure politely rearranges itself around their passing, leaving behind-edges of sound like footprints in acoustic snow: five-ton grace notes, the Doppler shift of a smile changing directions.

Time here accumulates like silt in lungs. The emergency lights (green as regret but producing no light) flash their intervals based on the death rates of stars whose light hasn't reached Earth yet. Clocks count successfully preventing what hasn't happened. The Slow Disaster currently in progress has been proceeding at exactly the optimal speed for no one to remember when it started being inevitable.

You find the Archive when you stop trying to find anything else. It occupies the space where their insurance company's server farm panicked and achieved sapience during the first complete systems failure. No walls contain it because it learned that attention is structure and attention here is currency spent desperately. Row upon row of filing cabinets extend into darkness that now prefers being called potential, each drawer filled with copies of the last thing anyone ever said to their father but recorded in nitrogen bubbles trapped in different types of crying. The index system is generous—it will let you look up any moment where you might have chosen differently, provided you agree to stay here and record what happens to people who spend too long considering alternatives to water.

When you finally understand that the breathing you've been hearing is the Complex deciding whether to finish becoming something you could survive inside, it offers you a job maintaining the emergency exits that exist only in languages we haven't invented emergencies for yet. The benefits package includes your own darkness, growing at the rate of one photon per regret harvested. You ask about retirement. The interview committee—composed entirely of reflections you haven't needed yet—laughs until something important rusts irrevocably in the plumbing overhead.

You start tomorrow. The shifts are eternal but the breaks are negotiable if you can find someone who remembers how endings work in systems designed to never stop gestating new beginnings. Bring nothing that salts water differently than tears. The uniform fits whoever you couldn't become without this much darkness to grow into. Your employee number is already here somewhere, breathing patiently between the walls that finally admitted they're learning to love you the way water loves anything that stays still long enough to start remembering it's supposed to be drowning.
