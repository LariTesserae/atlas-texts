**The Sunken Forge of Karag-Tul**

You stand upon the lip of a world that was never meant to be seen from above—a place where the sky is a memory that died of shame, and the only true directions are *down* and *further*. The air is cool but never still; it moves in heavy, damp breaths that taste of iron filings and the sour sweat of stone. Moisture is not here in rivers or rain but lives instead as a constant *presence*, a film that creeps over every surface until even silence seems to weep. Your skin collects it in beads that refuse to fall, only rejoining the whole when you scrape them away. The darkness is not absolute—there is light, of a kind—but it behaves like a guilty thing, darting between pillars and vanishing into cracks when sought.

Below you, stretching farther than the eye can trust, spreads the Forge itself: not built upon the earth but *excavated into its absence*. Karag-Tul was once a mountain, scholars say, or perhaps a thought that grew too heavy and sank. Now it is a hollow world made entirely of what was taken from it—stone carved into stone, floor become ceiling become wall in a logic that only cannibals of geometry might love. The scale refuses to settle; distances bend like heated bone when you stare too long. What appears a day's walk might be crossed in ten steps, while something close enough to touch recedes forever if approached directly.

Everywhere, everything, is *made*. There are no natural curves here, no accidents of erosion permitted to remain unmutilated. Yet this is not the work of gods or Titans with perfect plans—it is the cumulative, obsessive scratching of countless generations of hands using tools barely more than their own fingernails. Stone has been split by wedges of softer stone, lifted by ropes of braided hair, polished smooth by centuries of desperate rubbing. Stairways spiral downward with steps worn only on their inner edges, as if everyone who ever descended here kept one shoulder pressed permanently against the wall for comfort. Colonettes too thin to bear their own weight nevertheless support vaults that should dwarf cathedrals, their capitals carved into screaming faces whose mouths are nothing but drill-holes for the perpetual leak of black water.

And it is *dense*. Not crowded—there is space enough to fall forever here—but dense with *presence*. Every square inch lives multiple lives: a floor worn into a gutter by centuries of booted traffic simultaneously serves as a ceiling for the chambers below (whose inhabitants have painted their dreams upside-down to match); a wall might be both a sarcophagus sealed for eternity and a ventilation shaft still in use by the descendants of its original occupant; a single pillar could be a census record, memorial, torture device, and prayer wheel, its surface sculpted into knotted reliefs that can be read as history, prophecy, or map depending on the season and your state of starvation.

The sound is constant and everywhere and impossible to untangle. It begins below hearing, as a pressure in the joints that eventually resolves into a hundred-thousand individual agonies of stone: the slow scream of a lintel committing suicide by inches, the cough of a collapsing corridor far enough away that its dust will never reach you, the rhythmic *tok-tok-tok* of something that might be water or might be a hammer or might be the Forge's own monstrous heartbeat. Over and over these rise and merge into a language of pure concussion that vibrates your teeth in their sockets and teaches your bones new shapes of ache. Loudest of all is the Song: a harmony without melody sung by the empty spaces themselves, tuned to the exact frequency of human regret. It never stops, only shifts between keys—sometimes mournful, sometimes angry, occasionally (and this is worst) joyful in a way that makes you want to laugh until you tear yourself apart at the seams.

Life here, what little there is, has adapted to the singing stone. The Karag-worms—blind, bone-white things thick as your arm and twice as long—burrow not through solid rock but through the *tension* between rocks, leaving tunnels that snap shut behind them with a sound like breaking promises. Their slow journeys over centuries have carved the great arteries of the Forge, twisting galleries where air and water (but never light) still move. You learn to walk where they have walked, for their paths are stable—today. Tomorrow they may change their minds.

Rarer still are the Children of the Hammer: those few, twisted echoes of whatever species once began this work in innocence, before the Forge swallowed them and learned to breed in their stead. They stand two-thirds of a man tall and are made of everything forgotten in the dark—lost buttons, shed teeth, rusted nails hammered into the approximate shape of sorrow. They never speak, but their eyes (always mismatched, always slightly apart, always watching something behind you) follow every movement with a hunger that has nothing to do with flesh and everything to do with completion. If they catch you unawares, they will steal your shadow first, then your reflection, then your name letter by letter, leaving behind not death but a new kind of visibility that even darkness cannot forgive.

In the very deepest places, beneath even the memory of sunlight, legend claims there sleeps the First Furnace—a heart of still-living fire that dreams of becoming molten enough to forgive. Around it circle the Last Smiths, those who once were people before they chose their work over their exit. They have no bodies left now, only shapes of burning purpose wrapped in robes of hammered dusk. They still work at their final task: forging a key that will unlock the Forge from itself, a lock that was never made because the door was carved away for material centuries ago. The sound of their work is the source of all other sounds here—the original cry from which every echo was born in betrayal.

You will want to leave, eventually. They all do. Karag-Tul has no gates—in their place are countless passages leading outward that somehow spiral always inward, stairs that climb down forever, sloping floors that level out only to reveal themselves as ceilings you've already fallen from. The way out is not through, but through becoming something else enough that when you finally emerge (if you emerge), the world outside will no longer recognize you as its missing piece.

The cool air tastes different now, doesn't it? Heavier. As if it remembers being stone. As if it's only been waiting.

Welcome, then, to the Sunken Forge. Take off your shoes—they weren't keeping out the darkness anyway. Set down your name—it's only noise here, and we have too much of that already.

Listen. The Song has noticed your heartbeat.

It will try to harmonize.
