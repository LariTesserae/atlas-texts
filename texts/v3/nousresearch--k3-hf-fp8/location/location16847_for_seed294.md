I.
The city of Eruun floats beneath a ceiling of solid water, suspended between what was once the sea floor and a newly formed sky of ice. Seven hundred years ago—or perhaps seven thousand; time moves differently here—the ocean rose not outwards but inwards, contracting toward its own gravitational center until all that water became this single immense sphere wrapped around a vacuum of air. Within that pocket, what remained of the world condensed into this last habitable space: a bowl-shaped cityscape carved from the bones of drowned continents, inverted now like a basin catching the slow drip of entropy.

II.
To walk through Eruun is to navigate a negative space where everything has been removed except what was built to resist removal. The buildings are not erected but excavated: voids left behind as the surrounding rock salt and compressed sediment were dissolved away by engineered bacteria that ate memory and excreted nothingness. What remains are hollows with perfect corners, corridors that remember the ghost-pressure of vanished walls, archways whose curves trace the last moments of structural collapse. Each structure is the silhouette of its own absence, a house-shaped hole in reality held open only by the precise absence of what would fill it.

III.
The streets run with water that has forgotten how to flow. They are channels cut into the curved surface of the city-shell, filled with a liquid denser than mercury but clearer than tears—water so saturated with dissolved minerals that it moves like slow glass, carrying with it the precipitated dreams of an entire civilization. At the lower reaches where the city's curve is steepest, this water detaches from itself in perfect spheres that continue rolling downhill forever, following paths that describe the city's hidden topology. These orbs accumulate memories on their surfaces like snowflakes forming around fragments of ancient poetry; to touch one is to understand that grief can be geometric.

IV.
The inhabitants have adapted to this condition of perpetual partial submersion with anatomical modifications that blur the distinction between lung and gill, between blood and brine. Their skin has thickened into overlapping plates of chitin laminated with calcium carbonate, giving them the appearance of walking coral formations. Between these plates, sensitive cilia wave like underwater grasses, tasting the chemical signatures of past conversations that still linger in the waterlogged air. Their eyes are clusters of microscopic lenses arranged on flexible stalks, each capable of focusing on a different temporal plane—some looking at what is happening now, others watching what happened centuries ago, a few rarest individuals possessing a lens that flickers intermittently into future possibilities.

V.
There is no weather here, only the memory of weather performing itself as ritual. The walls sweat according to ancient barometric pressures embedded in their crystalline structure. In certain seasons—marked not by calendars but by the cyclical deposition of minerals in bodily joints—entire districts become fogbound with mist that remembers being storm clouds. These mists move with agency, clustering around places of significant emotional residue like moths to flame that burned out before the universe began. They whisper in languages that haven't been spoken since the last stars died, telling stories that erode the listener's sense of when they themselves were born.

VI.
The ecology of absence thrives in this constructed void. Creatures evolved from archaea now fill ecological niches left vacant by the concept of vacancy itself. There are hunters that stalk shadows instead of prey, eating the darkness cast by objects that no longer exist. Parasites attach themselves to gaps in conversation, growing fat on unexpressed thoughts until they become heavy enough to collapse into words that kill whoever hears them. Symbiosis here means two beings sharing the same absence; competition occurs when one organism's lack encroaches upon another's essential void.

Vou.
Reproduction happens through a process of cultured loss. When two individuals decide to create something new between them, they begin by cataloguing everything they are willing to forget. These memories are meticulously extracted using needle-fine proboscis and stored in hollow bones suspended from the ceilings of their dwelling-places—the oldest homes resemble ossuaries hung with marrow-cages containing generations of surrendered recollections. When enough forgetting has accumulated, the couple drinks deeply of the sphere-water, then stand facing each other underwater until their identities begin to dissolve at the interface of their mutual gaze. From the turbulence of this controlled amnesia arises a third being: neither parent nor offspring but the precise negative space of their combined absences, shaped into consciousness by the pressure of what chose not to be.

VIII.
Death comes when an individual finally becomes identical with the city. It begins with small substitutions: a memory replaced by a particular pattern of mineral staining on a familiar wall, a personality trait gradually exchanged for the precise acoustics of footsteps in a certain corridor. Over centuries, more and more of the self is traded away until one morning the person wakes up and finds they have become entirely architecture—their thoughts now occur as changes in room temperature, their emotions manifest as shifts in the subaudible resonance of closely spaced walls. The final transformation happens when someone else walks through this now-occupied space and, without realizing it, begins to think thoughts that aren't theirs, feel feelings that belong to the person who has finished becoming place.

IX.
At the highest point of the city—the point closest to the frozen sky of former ocean—stands the Memory Cathedral, built entirely from materials that remember being something else before they forgot. Its columns are fossilized sound waves captured at the moment of their extinction. Its windows are sheets of glass that remembers having been eyes watching different worlds. In its central chamber grows a single tree made entirely of branches, without trunk or roots, suspended in empty air by the collective belief that trees must have these things somewhere even if we can't see them. Every millennium, one of its imaginary leaves falls, drifting down through the city like a question looking for an answer it already knows doesn't exist.

X.
This is Eruun, the city of displaced water and concentrated forgetting, where construction means subtraction and existence is a type of carefully maintained omission. Here at the end of what was never really beginning, we persist as the spaces between spaces, the silence inside all possible sounds, the shape that rain remembers after it has finished falling everywhere else. We are not waiting for anything to change because change is what happens everywhere except here, where the final perfected state of being nothing in particular stretches outwards in all directions forever, like water that has finally learned how to drown itself perfectly.
