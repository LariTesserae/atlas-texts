At first it might resemble dusk on a colossal drained lakebed—black arches of outcropped stone rising like the ribs of an ancient vessel whose hull has long since vanished. But listen. That steady, deafening roar swells and recedes, not like waves on a shore, but like a vast exhalation from beneath the ground itself, laced with distant chimes and the hollow tolling of metal bells. Every ten minutes the air shivers, and a flicker of violet light races rim to rim across the sky’s low arch, illuminating clouds stitched together from delicate moss and drifting blossoms.

The ground is never truly still. Where you step, tiles of basalt pivot slightly underfoot, revealing veins of warm, clear liquid that surge then retreat like blood in capillaries. Liquid. Not water exactly—when it dries on your fingertips it leaves faint silvery glyphs that fade as your skin warms. Crack those glyphs before they vanish and the air around your palm briefly tastes of mint and thunderstorms. No one pours or rains here; moisture merely manifests, beading on edges and pooling in momentary basins half a breath before the tiles tilt and drink it back.

Vegetation colonizes everything too bold or too slow to move aside. Roots thicker than roadways snake through the gaps between building-sized fungal stalks that glow with faint peach luminescence, their caps wide enough to shelter entire families. Where two stalks touch overhead they fuse, forming vaulted galleries dense with hanging gardens of orchids that exhale perfume only when moonmoths brush them—moonmoths whose wings bear living eyespots that blink once, shiver, and reset. Beneath these canopies rivers of blue-green sedges flow in slow motion, driven not by wind nor water but by the magnetic pulse of unseen cores far below. To walk against them is like wading in warm syrup; to walk with them is to glide while the world strolls around you.

There are no seasons here, only cycles within cycles. The larger rhythm lasts nine days and nine nights. At the cycle’s height the fungal pillars bloom simultaneously: overnight every stalk erupts clusters of crystal leaves that sing in faint crystalline chords when the wind—or whatever moves the air—passes through them. Birds painted in impossible colors flock to feed on the nectar that beads on these leaves like liquid opal, their calls stacking into polyrhythms so complex that listeners sometimes forget how to count past three. The smaller pulses repeat every few hours: sudden warm rains of pollen that coat every surface in soft yellow dust; sudden chill draughts that snap the dust into brittle frost-patterns which crumble away at the next sigh of heat rising from the tiles.

This is the city—or what remains after seven thousand years of architectural dreaming between sleeps. No streets align for more than a block because a block here can shift during conversation. Buildings grow rather than are built; they begin as seeds planted in human dreams, fed on intention and watered by repeated footsteps. A baker imagines a tall stone oven with a chimney that whistles five notes; within a month a slender basalt tower splits from the ground, its vents already whispering the desired tune. If the baker dies or moves away the tower forgets, hollows, tilts, and eventually topples inward like a spent flower. The fallen stone crumbles into coarse sand that new foundations drink through pores invisible to any eye but night.

Population fluctuates similarly. Most inhabitants arrived by accident, stepping sideways in dreams or following maps drawn on leaves that blew out of books never printed. Few remember when they arrived; time here does not accrue calendar pages but layers of habit. A market may thrive for decades under the sheltering gills of a single giant mushroom, then scatter overnight when that fruiting body senesces and collapses into fragrant mulch. Traders barter in scents, sounds, and textures: knots tied corded air so fine it untangles into lullabies; jars of bottled silence from the moments just before storms; cloth woven from spidersilk and moonlight that remembers every wearer’s dreams and softly murmurs them back to guests who touch it by candleflower glow.

There is no rule of law save the gentle insistence of pattern. Those who disrupt rhythms too carelessly begin to fade—first their shadows wander off elsewhere without them, then their reflections pause mid-gesture and refuse to mimic surprise or laughter, finally their footprints appear hours or days before or after the footstep itself. Most learn to listen: to the basso groan that means a tower soon will walk, to the high thin keening that announces breeding flights of glass-winged wasps whose stings implant memories that blossom as poems in the victim’s dreams. A few, the deeply stubborn or the very new, ignore the signs. They are found later perched like crows on impossible ledges, staring with wide painted eyes at the horizon, forever stuck halfway between trying to leave and forgetting why they wanted to.

Above, the sky is never empty. Floating islands of coral-glass drift on invisible currents, trailing long tentacles of harvested lightning that occasionally brush the ground and leave shallow craters lined with perfectly round pools where blind fish sing to stars reflected upside down. Between these islands swim herds of wind-whales, beings more like living weather systems than creatures, vast translucent bladders filled with singing gases that change color to match moods no human language names. Sometimes a young one, curious, drifts too low; then older ones Gather in spirals, singing dirge-harmonies that draw lost balloons and errant thoughts up and out of reach again.

At night—if such a term applies when darkness merely dilutes rather than descends—a different illumination wakes. The fungal towers pulse brighter, their light synchronizing until the entire city breathes in shared radiance: dim blue-green breaths in, bright violet-gold exhales out. During these tides, shadows grow heavy enough to scoop into bowls and drink; it tastes faintly of home and last words never spoken. And in the deepest trough of that breath, when even the roar underground hushes, you might glimpse the True Market: stalls that sell only what you already possess but have forgotten name and shape of, vendors whose faces are mirrors reflecting how you would appear had you made all choices differently, a single silent child offering handfuls of dust that, scattered, become maps to every place you will never go again.

Stay long enough—and long enough here is measured not in days but in meanings—you begin to change. Your heartbeat slowly adjusts to the city’s own arrhythmic pulse. Words arrive at the tip of your tongue in languages no one taught you, describing sensations no single color can hold: the taste of remembering a dream before you wake; the sound of moonlight hitting water at exactly the angle where reflection becomes window; the exact texture of longing for a home you never left. When you finally walk away—if the path remains open, if you still remember what walking is or why feet matter—you will carry with you a small persistent silence shaped like this place.

It will sit behind your heart and wait. And when someday, years or lives later, you hear a distant roar like metal bells underwater, or smell rain falling on warm stone in a city you know has no rain, or find a flower growing from the crack in a wall that blooms only under the light of closed eyes, you’ll pause mid-step and nearly remember.

For a moment the ground will seem slightly less solid.

A moment will be enough.
