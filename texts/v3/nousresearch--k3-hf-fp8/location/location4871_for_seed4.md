A single tower rises through fungal bark fifteen meters thick. Not built, but grown wrong — a lightning-struck spore pod of architecture that infected the parent organism and kept growing upward after death, hollowed its lignin heart into chambers connected by spiral corridors of xylem tubes gone woody and dark.

You stand in its lowest chamber, floor spongy with mycelial felt that glows just enough to show where moss-padded benches once held councilors who never came. Their shapes remain: twelve seated figures outlined in bioluminescent lichen now grown wild across their absence. The walls breathe mist at your approach, condensing the tower’s perpetual fever-dream humidity into droplets that crawl ceiling-ward against memory of gravity.

Through the six arched windows — really just softened scars where gill-sheets once opened to release spored wind — you see the true city: not outside, but within. The tower occupies the hollow core of a single dead god-tree, its forty-kilometer trunk fallen across a valley that never existed before the impact. Around and through this fallen world-tree’s corpse, life has built its first and only settlement.

The Haric People call it the Tumor-moot: twenty thousand souls (if you count their symbionts) living in the space between bark and heartwood of a single ancient organism. Their dwellings are not constructed but curated — chambers carved carefully so as not to wake the tree’s dreaming immune system, walls lined with living nerve-cord tapestries that carry whispered gossip through the entire city in minutes, floors paved with polished eye-knots that watch but do not perceive, their optic nerves long ago rewired for electricity generation by generations of fungal electricians.

You arrived through the North Lenticel, stepping from wind-whipped snow outside (real cold, real world) into this pocket of unreal spring: the tree’s slow decay maintaining temperatures just above freezing for the last seven centuries, powered by residual fermentation in its sapwood, heat rising up its hollow core like breath from a sleeping giant’s open mouth. But now the giant stirs — or something else does. The Haric say the tree is dreaming of winter properly for the first time, and its dreams grow on branches that break reality’s bones.

Every surface grows fuzzy with new structures overnight: staircases that were bacterial colonies yesterday; balconies sprouting from nowhere like bracket fungi after rain; entire districts re-plumbed by the tree’s vengeful dreams into impossible geometries that still somehow obey the structural logic of vascular bundles under pressure. The city planners — elderly women who communicate by singing precise harmonic frequencies to the termite-engineers — have given up trying to map anything older than a week. “The city remembers,” they say, “but it lies when drunk on memories.”

And now it is growing drunk indeed. Through the mist-windows you can see the signs:

In the Sapwood Market, where merchants trade stories earned from whispering to the cambium layer until it told its childhood secrets, the stalls have begun growing together overnight — wooden sieves joined by bleeding cambium into coral reefs of commerce bartering dreams instead of goods.

In the Heartwood Temple, where initiates meditate inside living cells filled with glowing cytoplasmic fluid, the monks have started speaking in languages that cause the temple walls to sprout new organs in sympathetic response: a chapel of hearts beating in unison with no blood to pump; an ossuary where bones cannot decide whether to be calcium or cellulose.

In the outer cambium belt called The Edge of Sleeping Skin, where the city’s young go to tattoo themselves with living ink that migrates across their bodies telling fortunes in changing stripes, the tattoos have begun predicting events that happened before they were applied — and sometimes events that haven’t happened yet, but will kill the wearer when they do.

Above all, the tower pulses like a heart gone arrhythmic. The Haric say it is the tree’s death-spore organ preparing to release one final seed — not for dispersal, but for burial. A seed that will bury all of this: city, valley, world perhaps. A seed containing compressed spacetime and concentrated memory and enough latent growth potential to germinate a new universe from the corpse of the old.

They say you can already taste it in the air: the sharp metallic tang of possibility precipitating like frost on surfaces that shouldn’t exist yet. The quiet clicking of something enormous and invisible assembling itself molecule by molecule in the spaces between atoms. The growing certainty that the dark windows aren’t showing you the city anymore, but the city’s reflection in the pupil of a dreamer who is waking up.

And worst of all, worst of all: the sound of birdsong. No birds live here — haven’t for centuries. But somewhere high above, in chambers where the wood has grown porous as bone-foam, something is learning to sing using borrowed throats that never grew for singing.

The council seats glow brighter as you watch. Outlines filling in. Figures taking substance from accumulated green light and a dead tree’s remembered dreams of councilors gathering to witness the end of something that never properly began — and the beginning of something else entirely.

The tower is preparing to wake.

And it has been saving you for last.
