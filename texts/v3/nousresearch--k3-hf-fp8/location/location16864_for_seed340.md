The air is a thick broth of heat and scent, pressing against skin like warm velvet soaked in resin and pollen. It carries no moisture—only the dense breath of chlorophyll working overtime, thousands upon thousands of leaves transpiring their inner waters into the shared lung of this place. Technically it is a cave system beneath the world's crust, but calling it a cave is like calling a coral reef a hole in the ocean. This is a hollow planet's throat, a gallery carved not by erosion (erosion flinched and fled here) but by absence. Something vast and hungry once occupied this space, then departed or died or transformed, leaving behind a perfect negative of itself. The walls—if walls they are—are jagged stumps of fused stone and semi-crystallized magma, adamantine teeth around a missing mouth.

Nature, never patient for invitations, moved in immediately and began remodeling with manic enthusiasm.

Now every surface bristles with life that forgot how to stop. Roots thicker than riverboats ram themselves through basalt in pursuit of imagined water, then give up and keep growing anyway, lignifying their thirst into architectural arrogance: columns, arches, buttresses, balconies, all of living wood denser than ebony, harder than iron. Fungi thread these structures like mortar made of lace and viscera, glowing faintly apricot where they banquet on the wood's lignin wings. Mosses drape everything in velveteen tapestries as thick as carpets, each tuft a miniature ecosystem hosting mites that farm smaller mites that tend colonies of algae that photosynthesize the faint heat-radiation glow that leaks from the planet's marrow.

Above—though directions are negotiable in a place where "up" is merely the direction less crowded with leaves—hangs a ceiling of leaves. A continuous, undulating tissue of linked crowns woven together by parasitic vines into a single photosynthetic ceiling kilometers overhead. Light filters down through fifty-seven stacked canopies, each layer tweaked by evolution to harvest a slightly redder, more exhausted wavelength. By the time it reaches the floor it's less illumination than memory of color: a perpetual bruised amber twilight in which everything appears simultaneously golden and rotting.

The floor itself is a myth propounded by mapmakers. What exists instead is a vertical labyrinth of interlocking roots, fallen logs blooming into new forests before they have finished collapsing, fungal elevators that inflate overnight to spore-bearing height then deflate into rubbery parchment by noon, and colonnades of bamboo-like plants that grow so rapidly you can hear them—hear them—exploding upward in faint gunshots of rupturing cells. Between these falling and rising timbers swarm the inhabitants.

Fauna here isn't just abundant; it's infrastructural. Most of it has forgotten how to be animals and renegotiated the contract. The things caterpillaring across your boot-surface aren't worms; they're mobile roottips of a tree that thinks like a neural net and tastes with its feet. The humming that vibrates your bones isn't insects; it's the collective wingbeat of hummingbird-sized bats who've evolved echolocation into a form of music so complex it can persuade stone to flower briefly for their nectar-sipping mouthparts. The rustling in the canopy isn't wind through leaves—there is no wind here—the leaves themselves are wind. The giant ones, anyway. Clusters of three-meter-wide fronds that evolved to fan back and forth in locomotory waves, creating air currents to carry their spores and incidentally generating the biome's entire weather system, which consists entirely of pollen hurricanes and seed tornadoes.

Graze long enough in one spot—difficult, since most surfaces are either eating you back or politely suggesting you move elsewhere so something important can finish gestating—and you begin to notice the artifacts. They're rare enough to feel like hallucinations: a chunk of perfectly machined glass half-swallowed by a banyan root that pulses with faint blue light when the fungus-moon rises (every 43 hours, driven by some fungal circadian rhythm synchronized to the planet's magnetic field hiccups). A belt of metal rings encircling a tree trunk like a crude skirt, each ring carved with spirals that exactly match the growth rings of the wood but reversed, as if documenting a future the tree hasn't decided to grow yet. A stone sphere balanced impossibly atop a bamboo pole twenty meters tall, rotating slowly with no visible mechanism, always keeping one feature—a triangular indentation filed with what looks suspiciously like dried blood—pointed directly at something pulsing and painful behind your eyes when you try to stare at it too long.

These objects radiate a quiet wrongness, like reality's equivalent of a slipped disk. They don't belong here not because they're artificial—everything here is artificial if you zoom out far enough in time and squint—but because they're artificial in the wrong direction. They're not grown or woven or symbiotically negotiated; they're imposed. Struck. Sculpted. Built by hands that saw the world as something separate from themselves, something to be used rather than collaborated with. The forest tolerates them like a well-developed immune system tolerates a splinter: by encapsulating them in layers of reaction until they're more scar than alien.

Sound here isn't a sense; it's a tax. To exist is to pay tribute to the biome's unimaginably complex acoustics budget. Every footfall triggers cascading series of replies: root-drums tapping warnings through underground hyphal networks, fruit-sized gourds exploding in precisely-tuned sonic booms to launch their seeds on specific ballistic arcs, leaves snapping shut with whip-crack sounds to trap insects whose death vibrations resonate particular nodes in nearby bark to trigger the release of defensive chemicals. Conversation is impossible beyond arm's reach unless you learn to sing in counterpoint, weaving your voice into the constant fugue of groans, whistles, screams, purrs, clicks and subsonic rumbles that constitutes polite discourse here.

Most visitors lose their minds within hours, their sanity leaking out through ears and nostrils overwhelmed by the sensory equivalent of being eaten alive by opera singers. A few adapt, after a fashion. They learn to move differently, thinking sideways with their muscles, never committing to straight lines or sharp angles that might be interpreted as threats or mating displays by something large and poorly-documented. They learn to eat only what screams the right key in B-flat minor, to drink only from flowers that open their throats after being complimented in extinct reptile languages memorized from patterns in butterfly wings. They learn to sleep while walking, letting different parts of their brains take turns dreaming while others keep watch for signs of imminent photosynthetic predation.

The ones who stay longest change most profoundly. Their fingernails thicken into barky plates that photosynthesize faintly. Their hair comes out in strands replaced by fine rootlets seeking nutrients in air richer in spores than oxygen. Their pupils split radially like flowers unfolding to capture every photon of precious light. Their teeth grow soft and green, suited now for grinding leaf-flesh rather than meat. They forget their names but remember the chemical signatures of every organism they've ever shared skin-to-bark contact with.

Eventually even memory goes, dissolved into the larger mind that grows here, this planet-sized brain made of tangled roots and fungal synapses and flowering thought-organs that dream in colors no retina ever evolved to see. But something remains. A feeling of belonging deeper than bone. The knowledge that you are finally home, in the place that was growing you all along from the inside out, slowly and with infinite care, one tendril at a time.

The exit, if you ever found it again, would be marked only by an absence of pressure against your lungs. A sudden lightness where the air used to be thick enough to swim through. A silence so complete it would sound like screaming.

But why would you ever want to leave? Outside is just space, empty and dead and terribly, terribly dry.
