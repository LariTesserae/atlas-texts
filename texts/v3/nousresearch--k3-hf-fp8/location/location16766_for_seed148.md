The air itself is a lung.

Not metaphorically. Not poetically. Not even religiously. It expands. It contracts. On a three-minute cycle, exactly, and you can feel it in your teeth. The pressure drops, the hush widens, and then the soft exhale returns — breath flavored with copper and wet slate and the round, bruised scent of pear flesh left too long in the dark.

You are inside something.

You are deep inside something.

The ground beneath you is not ground. It is a pad of spongy, dead-green tissue pocked with tiny pores that ooze a colorless fluid when you step. Not water. Not oil. Something that evaporates before it hits anything else, leaving behind only the faint taste of pennies and the memory of a bell ringing once, somewhere far below you, and then again, answering.

The walls—if you can call them that—are vanes.

Ribbed. Fluted. Sixty meters tall at their tallest, but bending overhead like cathedral arches built by a species that never heard of stone. They are not trees. They are not bone. They are not muscle, exactly, though they flex when the lung-air breathes. They are amber-green and semitranslucent, lit from inside by slow pulses of cold blue light that move like blood in a fever dream of veins. Each vane is furred along its length with smaller fronds — fractal hairs finer than spider silk — and every frond holds at its tip a tiny droplet of water, perfectly round, perfectly still, never falling.

The dew does not fall here because down has not been decided yet.

Everything is damp. Nothing is wet.

The gentle leak of water is everywhere. It condenses on nothing and is never absorbed. It beads on your sleeves without touching them. It rolls off your breath and hangs there, glittering, then drifts sideways like lazy smoke and joins a slow-moving river of other droplets sliding through the air five feet above your head, east to west, on no wind you can feel.

Occasionally two rivers collide. They merge without sound. They birth a single larger drop that immediately splits into two smaller ones moving opposite directions and you think—wrongly—you have understood something important.

The place is not empty.

It is nearly empty.

It is empty in the way a skull is empty.

Open volume held taut by invisible necessities.

Once in a long while you glimpse the inhabitants.

They move purposefully between the vanes. Some are human-shaped. Most are not. None are alive in any way your biology would recognize as life. They are made of knots of coppery wire wrapped around cores of wet clay. They do not walk. They unspool themselves into the holes between moments, pour through, and re-coalesce somewhere else. Their bodies hum at the exact frequency of the lung-air inhalation, so that when they pass close you experience a sudden vertigo: a feeling that you have forgotten how to breathe for a hundred years and now, suddenly, remember.

They do not speak to you. They do not speak to each other. They inspect the fronds. They harvest the dew that does not fall using bowls woven from their own unbraided hair. They carry the bowls to machines that hang suspended in empty space between nothing and nothing else — squat iron cylinders aged green with verdigris and throbbing with an oily heartbeat. The machines drink the dew and belch steam that smells faintly of winter and burnt sugar. The steam rises. The steam condenses on the vanes. The cycle continues.

This is a city.

This is the city.

This is the only city.

There are no streets. There are no doors. There is no inside or outside except where you happen to be standing and how you choose to pretend. The density of built things is exactly equal to the density of unbuilt things, so that every house is also a hole and every tower is also a tunnel leading back to itself. The buildings — if you insist on calling them that — are living scaffoldings of green-black ivy that grows clockwise around the vanes and blooms once every seven exhalations with flowers shaped like tiny screaming faces made of porcelain. The faces open their mouths. The mouths sing numbers in an extinct dialect of mathematics. Then the petals close, and turn inside-out, and become something else entirely: an iron nail, a child's marble, a human tooth with a cavity shaped like a perfect chess knight. The inhabitants collect these gifts and wear them around their necks like holy medals. You can hear them clink when they move.

The light is darkness.

It is not the absence of light. It is a positive substance, thick and granular, like very fine soot suspended in oil. It pools in the hollows. It drips from the edges of things. It collects in the corners of your eyes and when you blink you see afterimages that are not backwards versions of what you saw but sideways ones—colors that do not exist, angles that do not meet. When you try to focus on them they slide away, like trying to pick up mercury with a magnet.

The temperature is cool.

It is exactly the temperature of a cellar where apples have slept through December and are just beginning to dream of rot. There is no wind. There is no movement except the breathing and the drip of the undripping dew and the slow, ceaseless rearrangement of the city itself as the vanes flex incrementally wider each cycle and the lung-air fills them and they sigh back again a little less narrow than before, growing slowly, imperceptibly, like frost feathers on a windowpane you forgot to clean last winter and remembered this spring only half too late.

The scale is small.

You can walk from one side of the world to the other in twenty minutes if you do not pause to marvel. If you pause — and you will — the world stretches under your attention like warm taffy. A doorway that was three steps away becomes three hundred. A vane that brushed your shoulder shrinks to a distant needle on a horizon that never moves because there is no horizon, only the slow curve of the inside of the lung turning endlessly into itself like a mobius strip made of inhale.

The erosion is nascent.

Nothing here has been broken down yet because nothing has ever fully existed. Everything is in the process of becoming something else. The ground underfoot is still trying to decide whether it wants to be soil or silence. The vanes waver between being trees and being questions. The inhabitants are halfway between people and punctuation marks. Even time here is not finished; it stutters and repeats like a child learning to read aloud. Sometimes you hear the same breath twice. Sometimes you turn around and find an earlier version of yourself standing there, looking confused, before he notices you noticing him and politely unhappens.

The fauna is abundant.

There are more creatures here than there is space to contain them. They live in the gaps between molecules, between thoughts, between one heartbeat and the next. You do not see them. You do not hear them. You feel them as soft pressures on the edge of your skin, like the memory of a touch from someone you loved before you were born. When you sleep—if you sleep, which is not recommended—they come out and rearrange your dreams into more efficient configurations: shorter, sweeter, ending sooner. You wake up tasting unfamiliar names and the faint regret of betrayals you have not yet committed but will, now, inevitably, because they are more aerodynamic this way.

The tech is industrial.

It is the industry of things that do not yet need to exist but already remember the taste of exhaustion. The machines run on the difference between what is and what was almost but not quite. Their gears are the teeth of extinct emotions: longing, pity, the precise shape of the sound a mother makes when she finally stops calling your name because she knows you are never coming home. Their fuel is the smell of libraries burning in timelines where paper was never invented. Their waste product is silence, distilled to 99.999% purity and bottled in atom-sized vials worn as earrings by the wire-and-clay people who govern without governing because here there is nothing to govern except the endless becoming of more and less and exactly the same.

The weirdness is absolute.

It is not weird because it is strange. It is weird because it is correct. Every atom in your body recognizes it as homecoming. Every law of physics you ever memorized in school is here displayed as a polite fiction, like the tooth fairy, like democracy, like the idea that you are separate from the things that hurt you. When you breathe in the lung-air you are not breathing in. You are being breathed. When you walk forward you are not walking. You are being walked upon by places that have missed you without knowing your name. When you speak your voice does not travel outward. It circles back and enters your ear from inside your skull, whispering secrets you have been keeping from yourself for longer than this universe has had stars.

You are not lost.

You have never been lost.

You are exactly where you left yourself the first time you decided to become something else.

Stay awhile.

The next breath is beginning.

It is always beginning.

You will not be harmed.

You will be changed.

That is not the same thing.
