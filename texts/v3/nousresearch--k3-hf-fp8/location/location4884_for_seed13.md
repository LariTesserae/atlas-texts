The city of Ascensus rests on a plateau that shouldn't exist—lifted from the ocean floor a thousand years ago, but the textbooks say a thousand years ago was last Tuesday. Everything here is new and freezing.

You arrive by climbing. There's no other way. The cliffs are vertical sandstone still dripping with compression water, fossilized bubbles of ancient atmosphere popping against your gloves as you scramble up bulges of half-formed strata. At the top: the Shelf. Two kilometers of perfectly flat basalt sloping imperceptibly westward, grooved by the single-minded determination of retreating ice sheets that have already finished their work elsewhere. The air is thin enough that thoughts need to settle before they soak in.

The city spreads like lichen across the shelf, built entirely from what was available:
- Houses carved into standing sea stacks, their former wave-cut notches now serving as lintels
- Streets that are simply the spaces between submarine volcanoes, their basalt columns hacked to different heights for stepping
- A market square occupying the negative space of a vanished submarine canyon, roofs spanning nothing anchored to nothing
- Everything held together with ice mortar that refreezes stronger than steel in these perpetual-just-below temperatures

The water here is architectural. It drips from every overhang into stone troughs carved by the first settlers who built backwards from the drip patterns, letting potential canals choose their own courses. The rich live upstream of their own water supply—a status symbol indicating their houses are cold enough to keep their streams solid. The poor live downstream where meltwater trickles even in winter, pooling in communal cisterns where children catch blind cave fish that somehow learned to love sunlight at 2,300 meters.

Vegetation clings to impossible surfaces with impossible vigor, feeding on something that isn't photosynthesis. The trees are all bonsaied giants—kauri and sequoia ancestors arrested at seedling size but retaining adult bark patterns, growing horizontally from vertical rock faces whose orientation hasn't changed since the seabed uplift. Their leaves are primitive needles flattened into solar panels that rust instead of wither. In spring (a Tuesday term, meaningless here), they release spores that float upward like snow in reverse, building temporary cloud forests over the city that collapse into mulch by evening feeding the lichens that digest the stone beneath.

The fauna is abundant because nothing ever dies properly. Creatures accumulate here like sediment: flightless albatrosses walking in mating circles for centuries, their feathers turned to asbestos from volcanic ash; eyeless whales adapted to the crushing depth of air, navigating by the pressure differential between light and shadow; beetles evolved from lost ship's biscuits that learned to roll themselves up basalt columns using stored yeast farts as propulsion. The people are just another species in this catalog of leftovers—descendants of sailors who mistook the rising plateau for salvation and climbed so high they forgot the ocean existed below them.

Technology is what survives the night. Metal rusts into useful shapes while you sleep, tools grow like oysters around irritants of purpose. The city's greatest engine is a maintained avalanche—a carefully calibrated landslide of copper ore rolling eternally downhill for 400 years now, its track repaved daily by falling copper. The waterfall at the plateau's edge is actually this engine continuous output, a solid river of oxidized metal pouring into clouds that don't exist, powering mills below by the theory that something so pointless must be useful.

Time works sideways here. The sun circles at the wrong speed, completing its arc between breakfast and lunch every day for the last three centuries according to sundials that cast shadows in both directions simultaneously. Children are born knowing languages their parents never spoke, forget them at puberty, spend their lives carving dictionaries into walls for future children to puzzle over. There are exactly 144 words for rock but none for tomorrow because the concept refused to climb this high.

The sky above is a historical document, stars frozen in positions that predate most of them existing. Astronomers here are archaeologists who dig downward into future time, carefully peeling back layers of unformed constellations while avoiding the anachronistic meteors that sometimes land years before their comet was discovered.

The city has no walls because it has no outside—step beyond the last house any direction and you merely enter an earlier part of Ascensus, younger and less explained. The oldest district is built entirely from materials not yet carved from the youngest quarry. The king (elected monthly by rainfall measurement) rules from a palace whose foundations will be laid next century according to blueprints found yesterday in a cave that appeared when nobody was looking directly at it.

Everything here is temporary except the temporary things that have become permanent by accident. The ice sculpture that was the city gate rusted into iron oxide and couldn't be moved, now it's sacred. The wind is still learning how to blow across this new geometry of land, sometimes gets confused and circles a street corner for weeks whispering secrets in languages only the moss understands. The moss remembers everything but only speaks in chemical graffiti that appears on walls during certain shades of twilight.

And yet—it works. The city functions by refusing to admit it shouldn't. Food comes from somewhere, usually downward via trade with better-placed geological strata. People marry, argue, die except for the ones who think of something better to do, their bodies carefully positioned on north-facing slopes to fossilize into useful object classes for future generations. Children play games predicting which adult will erode into what utility, elders take bets on which mountain will decide to be a mountain tomorrow.

Ascensus is what happens when a civilization climbs too high too fast trying to escape something that was only ever rising with them, and instead of admitting the mistake, decided to build walls against the concept of falling. Now we live here. We are all new arrivals, even those born yesterday which was a thousand years ago by any other calendar. The air is thin, the stone is fresh, the water remembers being ocean, and the only direction left to grow is inward toward the center that hasn't been built yet but already has a waiting list centuries long.

Welcome to the plateau. You can't go back down because down is still up here. But don't worry—the edges are still growing outward at the rate of one anxious thought per night, so eventually there'll be room for everyone who hasn't arrived yet. The view is spectacular if you remember to look inward at the right angle, preferably during the eclipse that happens every noon when yesterday's sun catches up with itself.

Mind the drip edges. Everything here is still settling into what it will eventually decide to have been.
