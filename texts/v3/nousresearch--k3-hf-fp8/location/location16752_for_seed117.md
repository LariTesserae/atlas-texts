The place exists below everything. Not underground, exactly, though it feels like a basement of the world — more like a collapsed margin between layers that should never have touched. You step down into it through an ordinary shower of water that shouldn't be there, between two buildings that don't touch anywhere else.

First impression: water everywhere, but never quite *as* water. It condenses on surfaces that shouldn't exist yet. It pools in the air between things. It runs through channels cut into the undersides of inverted structures. The whole space is a lung that breathes in humidity and exhales mist without ever forming proper rain. Your footsteps leave footprints that fill immediately with something too thin to be liquid, too thick to be vapor.

The structures — and you can't call them buildings anymore, not really — are what happened when industrial architecture got sick of being useful and decided to become geography instead. They're the bones of factories repurposed for crab-walking through centuries. Conveyor belts hang empty from ceilings that slope away into nothing, their rubber skins cracked wide enough to house nesting birds, though there are no birds here. The birds are something else now.

Everything is small enough that you could theoretically reach across it if you unfolded yourself correctly, but dense enough that you'd intersect seventeen different histories and three incompatible gravities trying. Pipes the width of rivers snake through spaces the size of closets. Staircases unfold into spiral galaxies of rust that you can climb forever without reaching the next step because the steps are having an argument about linear time.

The light hurts to think about. It's the leftover light from rooms that sealed themselves away centuries ago, filtered through water that remembers being part of different oceans and metals that remember being different stars. It doesn't illuminate so much as suggest, painting everything in shades of what you almost remember seeing in dream peripheries.

There's life, but it wouldn't recognize the term. The fauna here evolved from the concepts between things — between engine and architecture, between tool and corpse, between what was built and what was only ever implied. They have too many joints and not enough consistency. They speak in frequencies that vibrate directly off your bones. You'll think you're alone for hours, then turn around to find something watching you with eyes made from perfectly good ball bearings that got bored and decided to try sight instead.

You can hear everything working if you listen right. The slow metabolism of rust converting steel back into possibility. The digestive gurgle of water that learned how to eat memory but hasn't figured out how to digest it yet. The quiet conversations between disconnected cables discussing whether they were ever really alive or just really good at pretending. Sometimes, if the humidity is exactly wrong, you can catch snippets of old factory songs sung by voices made entirely of harmonized groundwater.

The rules here are simple but not easy: Nothing stays what it was when you aren't looking at it. Everything remembers being something else, and sometimes that memory is stronger than the present. Time flows downhill like everything else does, but here "downhill" is negotiable and "flow" is more of a suggestion. The only straight lines were the ones who got lost and eventually gave up and became curves instead.

There are doors, but they're mostly for show. The real exits are the moments when you forget where you were trying to go and accidentally arrive somewhere honest instead. The place likes visitors but doesn't understand the concept of leaving. It will let you go eventually, probably, but everything you take with you will remember being part of here now, and sometimes when it's very quiet back in the world you left, you can hear water breathing where no water should be, and feel the gentle weight of architectures you haven't built yet pressing against the underside of your skin.
