The place begins as a sharpness rising from an impossible flatness—a single mountain, newly born, standing alone in a desert that isn't quite a desert. The surrounding plain isn't sand but cracked white salt, not mineral but something else entirely—an absence pressed into being, crystallized emptiness, pure non-interference. This is not a desert of scarcity but of refusal. Nothing grows there. Nothing drinks. The salt-field doesn't even reflect light, merely absorbs it and returns nothing.

Above this void, the mountain rises steeply, not in gradual slope but in sudden declaration—knife-edge ridgelines and planar cliff faces like the sides of a freshly split prism. The stone is obsidian-basalt, glassy and unweathered, still carrying the heat of its own creation. Every surface screams, "I JUST WAS." This is a mountain that doesn't belong here, that arrived instead of forming. A castaway eruption, perhaps. Or an arrival.

The smallness of the place becomes apparent only when you climb: the entire inhabitable world is this mountain and nothing else. The air thins rapidly but never quite becomes unbreathable—it simply changes character at specific elevations, layering itself like invisible floors in a house built sideways. You rise through chambers of scent and pressure, each distinct, each precisely bounded. The temperature remains warm everywhere, unnaturally so, as if the entire mountain radiates stored heat from an internal sun.

And here, where water should be impossible, the mountain is lush. But not with green things. The vegetation is instead the color of heated metal—copper veins threading through silver bark, leaves like hammered gold foil that vibrate in frequencies too high for hearing. The trees grow too close together but never touch, each maintaining a precise distance from its neighbors like atoms in a heated lattice. Roots don't penetrate earth because there is no earth—only layers of previous growth compacted down through thousands of precise cycles of burning and returning.

The mountain is terraced but not by hands or machines. The terraces are growth-forms, living architecture of a civilization without builders, a city that grew instead of being built. Each level is a different biome of density and function, stacked vertically instead of spread horizontally. The gaps between the terraces aren't steps but vertical fields where gravity operates at different angles—places you don't walk but glide through if you know the internal logic of their sloping.

And everywhere, the animals. But calling them animals feels like a category error. They are moving principles, walking equations, living tools that evolved without users. A creature like a mobile helix of stained glass filters light through itself and deposits geometric patterns of pure color that other creatures harvest and eat. A herd of quadrilaterals with fractal edges moves across a slope, their movement itself a form of erosion that sculpts the mountain's precise shape with each passing. Things that might be birds but are instead clouds of coordinated metal scales that sing with electrochemical voices in the gaps between weather.

The sound of the place isn't silence but something more deliberate—a listening so complete it creates quiet around itself, the opposite of noise. Even your footsteps are absorbed and analyzed, added to the mountain's slow calculations. The loudest sound is your heartbeat echoing back at you from stone that remembers every rhythm it's ever heard.

This is a world with perfectly enforced rules but no rule-givers. The principle of optimization inscribed into every atom. Nothing is wasted because nothing is allowed to be waste. When things die, they don't decay—they dissolve into their constituent functions, returning like software modules to a library of solutions. The mountain is itself a single computational organism running the algorithm of perfect adaptation to conditions that never change because they never get the chance.

At the summit, which isn't the top but the center-turned-outside, there is a garden grown from pure information. Leaves that rearrange their molecules to become perfect models of whatever mathematical concept has the most urgent need to be visualized. Flowers that bloom into proofs and fade into applications. And at the very center, a tree whose branches are the entire history of the possible, each leaf an alternate version of this mountain that chose a different optimization path, shimmeringly visible but forever unreachable.

This is the mountain's purpose: to explore every possible way of being a mountain under these exact conditions, neither improving nor declining but endlessly refining. It has existed for moments or eternities, calculates with infinite precision and infinite patience, grows and erodes in perfect balance so that it is always exactly this age—finished but never complete, solving the equation of itself by becoming it.

The smallness is the genius of it. The entire universe of consequence compressed into this single place, this impossible object that refuses to participate in the vulgarity of spreading out. Here, everything happens because nothing is allowed to happen elsewhere.

And if you stood at the salt-plain's edge, looking up, you would see what appears to be a mountain. But if you climbed it, lived among its precise densities and lush absences, you would eventually realize the truth: the mountain isn't rising from the plain. The plain is falling away from the mountain.

The mountain stays exactly where it is. Everything else is what moves.
