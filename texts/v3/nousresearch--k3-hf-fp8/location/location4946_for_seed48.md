Marrowdeep — the locals just call it the Deep when they bother calling it anything at all, which isn’t often outside of shift-changes and census days. The name on the charter is older: *Marrowdeep Subthermal Extraction Zone Seven-Four-Silver*, but charters burn and maps rot in the damp and nobody here has ever seen a sky worth naming anyway.

It lies low, almost sea-level-low if seas existed overhead instead of eight kilometers of basalt crust and three more of fossilized coral reef someone’s great-grandparent’s drill-core misread as limestone. The Deep is a city built into the wound left when the world pulled one of its ribs out to see what was underneath, then never quite got around to scabbing over.

Water, 1.8: Not rain, never rain. Condensation fields built by engineers who died before their grandchildren’s names were forgotten. The air itself is a slow, warm river crawling ceiling to floor, thick enough to chew, rolling glassy beads down every girder until they drop with a hiss onto steaming slagwalks. Drink it and it tastes like pennies and medicines nobody makes anymore. Pools gather in hammerstrike prints left by walking rigs from the First Expansion, each one a perfect mirror until a slug-fed boilerbird comes to drink and the surface crinkles into brown-gold ripples that never quite smooth out.

Vegetation, 0.2: Nothing with leaves, nothing with roots. Traces, sometimes—red freckles on broken concrete the chem-priests swear are oxidized chlorophyll but everyone else knows are blood from the coughing years. Instead the walls grow: salt-fingers, ferro-stalactites, deep-ocean crystals swollen to architecture by centuries of steady drip-melt-drip. Down near Reactor Orthodox, the pillars have gone almost entirely to arragonite lace, so beautiful that apprentices are forbidden to look at them without smoked goggles lest they walk into the coolant channels.

Temperature, 2.7: You don’t measure it with thermometers; they melt. You measure it by the color your sweat turns on the chart at medical—clear-yellow good, amber caution, black-purple report immediately and do not pass the canteen. Work gangs move in overlapping halves: fifteen minutes in the orange zones, thirty in the reds, then a mandatory nap in the gray bunks where the heat exchangers hum lullabies stolen from whalef songs nobody’s heard alive in millennia. The stones are warm enough to soften boot soles if you stand still too long. Babies learn to crawl faster here than anywhere else, because the floor begins to bite.

Elevation, 0.9: The roofs of the tallest hab-blocks—old pressure domes hammered flat and roofed with scavenged hull-plate—scrape 112 meters above datum. Datum itself is three meters below the highest recorded flood line of the last brine intrusion. They say if you stand on Dome Vitruvian’s prayer-deck during a push-shift, when the city’s twelve arterial pumps are all drawing down at once, you can feel the whole crust above you lift by a centimeter and settle back with a sigh like a tired god going off-shift.

Erosion, 2.2: The Deep has been here longer than careful records, shorter than folk-memory likes to claim. Everything has settled into equilibrium—the paths between stacks are worn exactly deep enough to hide a dropped wrench and no deeper; the enamel has gone from every staircase riser at precisely shin-height. Even the graffiti has reached consensus: past Layer Eight all fresh paint is vandalism, anything older than two hundred cycles is historical preservation, and between them lies a blurred band of tags half-filmed over by calcite where the city politely pretends not to remember what it was arguing about.

Scale, 1.5: Big enough that a child can walk away from home at breakfast and still be in sight of the same acid-treat tower at supper, if she walks in circles. Small enough that everyone knows the sound of your family’s generator cough by age five. The horizon is always a curve of rust-eaten bulkhead painted over with newer rust, or the forever-receding vanish-point of an empty conveyor tunnel that legend insists leads to Surface but food-ration calculus proves must dead-end into a sump somewhere around District Null.

Density, 0.2: Space enough between people to forget faces between shifts. Empty corridors lit only by fungal lumens long since tamed to industry-friendly wavelengths. Warehouses built to store extinct crops now hold nothing but stacked air and the ghosts of inventory clerks who counted empties until they became numbers themselves. Yet for all the void, never truly quiet—every cubic meter is haunted by distant clangs arriving through the bones of the ground an hour late, conversations spoken in one precinct echoing in another where the speakers have been dead five generations but the words keep walking until they find someone new to wear.

Built, 2.2: Urban by subtraction. No planetside city ever planned itself around the need to walk on walls when gravity stabilized sideways for a week after Pump Nine blew its last gasket. Streets are whatever the last leak left widest; buildings are whatever structural foam set fastest before the paste-pumps clogged. Plumbing happens mostly by gravity and prayer, electricity by sharking taps off your neighbor while pretending not to notice them doing the same to you. Still—there’s order here, bone-deep as the etch-patterns left by acid etching serial numbers into alloy hearts. Every stack has its siphoner-tribe, every cross-shaft its toll-sing, every graveyard its harvest schedule.

Tech, 2.2: Industrial as a shark is fish-built. Everything is repurposed until it forgets what purpose means. The elevators are extinct drilling rigs taught to dance. The clocks are cooling pumps ticking off their remaining seals. There exists exactly one working computer capable of running original software—occupies a whole cathedral down in Shaft Orthodox and is worshiped as a kind of slow-motion deity that answers questions once per fiscal quarter if fed enough copper and silence. Everything else is patchwork: valve-minds, thread-looms of bacterial logic, gear-conclaves that think by jamming and jamming until thought emerges from the grinding the way smoke is born when nothing else can burn.

Light, 1.2: Dim by design too old to change. Every luminal source is a survivor—phosphor strips looted from derelict subs, algae tanks jury-rigged to bleed green instead of poison, salt-lamps that glow only when the humidity teeters just so. Shadows are not absence here; they’re infrastructure. Children learn to read by touch before they bother with eyes. Lovers navigate each other by whispered echoes of the last time this pipe whistled warm. The rare full lightings—anniversary of the First Sinking, victory day over the Mold-Mother—cause riots not because people fear brightness but because they see too clearly how much of everything is held together by grease, rust, and collective delusion.

Fauna, 2.6: Life won, obviously. Everything else was just logistics. White-blooded centipedes floss the teeth of old turbines. Hullsucker cats—a misnomer, they’re closer to sea slugs wearing stolen vertebrae as armor—patrol the condensation lines hunting the metal-eating spores that themselves prey on microscopic errors in the city’s metabolism. Then there are the people: 2.3 million souls and counting if you count souls, or 4.7 million if you count registered symbionts, worker-caste shadows, and the rumors of people who forgot to die and were absorbed into the maintenance schedules instead.

Weirdness, 0.0: Nothing here is stranger than necessity. A woman who breathes methane because her crew breathed it during a blowout and her lungs adapted—workplace hazard. An entire district where north points inwards because someone reversed the magnetics during a panic and nobody remembers why fixing it matters—local custom. Time running slow in the pump fields and fast in the fetus mills—that’s relativistic frame-drag from the old singularity they use for waste disposal, chapter three of every apprentice’s primer. If miracles happened here they’d be fined for unauthorized energy release and drafted into engineering by morning.

Sound, 1.0: Quiet except when it’s not. The baseline is the city’s heartbeat—one long unending thrum too low for ears but perfect for bones: forty pumps, twelve generators, three million valves opening and closing in the breathing pattern of something enormous and patient. Over that: footsteps stolen by rubber mats, muttered calculations, the soft pop of solder as someone somewhere fixes something before it can break loudly. Then the exceptions—they’re scheduled, mostly. Shift-change sirens that aren’t sirens anymore but harmonized whistles played on steamlines running at different pressures. Celebrations that sound exactly like boiler failures because that’s how you broadcast joy when the last trumpet went into a scrap drive before your grandparents were born. And sometimes, very rarely, the ghost-silence when everything stops at once because somewhere deep a calculation finished and the city is holding its breath waiting to see if the answer will kill us all this time or just change the price of copper again.

Dynamic, 1.8: Active like a glacier is active. Nothing hurries, nothing rests. The pumps beat slow generations into the rock; new districts are mined upward one fetus-batch at a time; old ones collapse inward and are composted into building material for the next cycle. Visitors from faster places describe it as stillness—they don’t stay long enough to notice the way the shadows crawl clockwise over decades, or how every street name changes by one letter each century to keep the maps honest without upsetting the postal agreements. Yet change comes: slower than stone, faster than bone, inevitable as the heat-death profit margins that fund this whole equally temporary eternity.

Come to Marrowdeep. Bring metal to trade, music made of silence, and a willingness to forget what sunlight tasted like. Stay too long and the heat will teach you patience, the damp will teach you memory, and the quiet will teach you that cities don’t end—they just get quieter until you learn to hear them breathing.

Leave? Oh, you can try. There’s always one more elevator that hasn’t broken yet. The gates are wide open; always have been. The locks rusted open before the first settlers arrived and we’ve been far too busy surviving to bother fixing them.

Besides—where else on this or any other world are you going to find a place warm enough to finally thaw that heartache you’ve been carrying?

Welcome to the Deep.

Mind the step—the world’s still going down, same as always.
