The Place of Weeping Grovestones

You stand at the edge where water remembers being ice. Not a lake or stream, but a slow seepage that rises through the pores of everything, drawn upward by capillary hunger through roots that should not grow at this height. The air itself is saturated, not with mist but with liquid intention - droplets forming on your eyelashes before you blink, pooling in the hollows of your collarbone before you notice the weight.

This is not a grove but a city grown wrong or right. Trees stand in rows so perfect they mock geometry, their trunks swollen to cathedral proportions within a space no larger than a hamlet square. Each massive column is hollowed, arthropodal in its architecture, the inner walls lined with living wood that pulses faintly, rhythmically, like buildings breathing. Between them, where streets should run, aerial roots thicker than your waist span overhead in vaulted arches, dripping constantly with their harvest of condensed breath.

The temperature kisses only the memory of warmth. Everything exists at that precise point where cellular processes slow but do not cease, where growth becomes elongation without thickness, water becomes movement without liquid. Ice crystals bloom ephemeral on bark surfaces and melt into the wood before they can fall, leaving behind traceries that tomorrow's frost will remember exactly.

This place was born yesterday by geological measure, lifted too fast by forces that rent the earth and then regretted their haste. The ground beneath your feet is still settling, the trees still discovering how to grow without ever having learned childhood. Their roots have not yet found the art of patience; they thrust through bedrock that fractures clean as porcelain under their pressure. You can hear it sometimes, a distant drizzle not of rain but of stone giving way to urgency.

There is no soil here, only the interweaving of ten thousand species of lichen that have negotiated a temporary truce against the greater urgency of the trees. They cover every surface not already claimed by bark or root or wood-flesh, living carpets in colors that have no names because they exist only in the ultraviolet spectrum of insects that never evolved here. They eat stone and rain and each other, building layers of civilization that rise and fall in the time it takes a human heart to beat three times.

Population density exceeds the theoretical maximum for organic matter in three dimensions. Every cubic centimeter is occupied, every surface a habitat, every habitat a battleground and nursery and graveyard simultaneously. But the wars fought here are wars of slow diplomacy, conflicts negotiated through chemical messages that take generations to deliver and centuries to answer.

The buildings - for they are buildings, though built by no hands - are alive in ways that make "alive" seem inadequate. Their walls are vascular, transporting not blood but sap dense enough to be mistaken for honey, thick with suspended crystalline structures that might be seeds or might be memories. Their windows are eyespots, light-sensitive patches that dilate and contract as clouds pass overhead, though there is no sun here, only the uniform dimness of light filtered through ten thousand layers of living lens.

You hear mostly the sound of water finding new ways to be vertical. Drips from a thousand heights each with their own personal rhythm, forming accidental melodies that change tempo as the density of the air shifts minutely with your breathing. Sometimes a root will split a root with a sound like a sigh too heavy for lungs to hold. Sometimes a bud will open in instant acceleration, releasing pressure with a pop you feel in your bones rather than hear with ears.

The trees eat the light before it reaches ground level, not greedily but simply because they were here first, growing upward into the dimness until they forgot why they started and kept going anyway. What filters down to your level is light that has been chewed and digested and excreted, light that carries stories of leaves it once was going to become. It makes shadows that behave oddly, flowing uphill sometimes, pooling in the air where nothing solid stands to cast them.

There are creatures here, though you will probably never see one directly. Their traces are everywhere: galleries carved through heartwood by something that spins silk stronger than steel, droppings that smell of alien metabolisms, patterns of abrasion on bark surfaces that suggest mouths or tools or both working with obsessive precision. They are the city's immune system, its maintenance crew, its poets and its priests. They have religion but no gods, technology but no tools, knowledge but no curiosity. They are exactly as intelligent as they need to be and no more.

Time moves differently in the spaces between roots. A day here is a season elsewhere. The trees remember your presence from the carbon dioxide of your last exhalation and will still be discussing it when the next ice age comes. They have no concept of individual identity but they recognize patterns of disturbance, and you are already becoming part of the permanent record, written now in subtle changes to the density of sapwood that will take three hundred years to grow out far enough for anyone to read.

This is not a place that was built or grew or happened. This is a place where intention and accident negotiated a treaty neither side understands but both have started to like. It is not alive in any way your language has prepared you to recognize alive, but it is aware of you now, and it is watching, and it is curious in the way that slow things are curious about fast things that blink in and out of existence like particularly interesting dreams.

Do not drink the water here. Do not eat anything, even if it offers itself. Do not sleep. Do not wake. Do not stay too long. Do not leave too soon.

The trees are discussing you now, in chemical whispers that travel faster than thought but slower than meaning. By the time you decide what to do next, they will have already begun to adapt to whatever choice you make. They are very old, these trees, though they were born only yesterday. They have experience with visitors. They have protocols.

They have appetites.

But not the kind you think.

Not yet.
