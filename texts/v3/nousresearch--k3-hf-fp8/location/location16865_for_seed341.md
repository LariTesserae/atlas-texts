Entry 7843 — Contractor's Field Report — Site Code "Mourning Caul"

Arrival was blind. The airship settled through cloud vapor so thick I'd forgotten the descent lights until their sodium glare cut through, reflecting off wet nothing. By the time we touched down on what passed for a landing pad—a metal raft slung between three buoyancy tanks—rain was already pooling in the creases of my suit. No surface to speak of. Just water continuously deciding whether it wanted to be ocean or atmosphere.

The door dropped. Sound hit next. Not thunder, not wind, not even the mechanical overture I'd expect from an industrial site. Layered moaning. Metal fatigue, but orchestrated. Somewhere nearby, something immense was groaning in C minor.

I stepped off the ramp into ankle-deep water warm as blood. The city—if that's still the word—was a broken jawbone of teeth punched through a drowned mouth.

Parameters confirmed within tolerances, so onward.

The city does not float. It proclaims refusal to sink.

Imagine a marsh. Now imagine that marsh fossilized into rusted iron veins and midnight-blue oxides. Imagine factories that once made factories, their rooflines collapsed into each other like tired wrestlers, breeding generations of crippled offspring—all wrapped in vines that aren't vines. They're cables. Fibrous bundles of copper wire braided into cathedral ropes, each strand furred with verdigris moss that glows faintly when the temperature spikes (which is always). 

There's no soil, only centuries of scrap laminated by heat and weight and rain into a spongy, metallic loam. Concrete chunks bob like islands when storms pass through the upper, twilight layers. Everything else rides low, hugging the drowned foundations, anchored by memories of gravity.

Vegetation: 2.0 lush.

Yes. Insanely. The category fails here. I logged nine discrete species of machine-fungus before my scanner melted. *Pneumocystic warchinerii* carpets the lower decks—a breathing rust that vents sulfuric acid spores when disturbed. Taller growths: radio-telescope blooms, their dish petals tracking phantom signals. They bloom in storms, solar coronas made of wirebrush fire. Between them, the trees. Tower knuckles of bloated ceramic once intended for furnace linings, now split open like ripe fruit, gushing slow rivers of molten glass that harden into brittle obsidian blossoms before they hit the water. 

The flowers smell burnt copper. The bees, if they ever existed, are gone. Pollination here is strictly mechanical; pollen is fine-ground circuit board sprayed by vibration at 520 Hz when the barometric pressure drops below 990 hPa. 

It drops every eight hours. Nothing here gives the weather permission.

Built: 2.0 urban. Density: 1.4 sparse.

Do not picture streets. Picture the absence of buildings slender enough to warrant roads between. The city is a single wounded megastructure, convulsed in its death-throe sprawls, flung limbs sunk half in waterlogged dreams. The gaps—those are our streets. Navigable canyons where walls breathe steam and broadcast half-lost muzak in languages from cancelled futures. 

Capital remains visible. At the drowned heart, the last skyscraper still fights to be taller than the rust. Its skeleton is pure carbonized bone now, draped in limp banners of processed uranium ore. They call it Administration, though who administers anymore, and to whom, and by what authority drowned in how many floods, no one aboard dares ask. Scattered around it like dropped stitches are the smaller settlements: a cluster of pressure-vessel domes welded into a church, a row of split-open boilers colonized by artisanal forge cults, one perfectly preserved subway car standing on a bridge of nothing, filled permanently with water kept at blood heat and lit from within by bioluminescent algae bred back from wartime stock to simulate a sunrise that never ages the passengers.

None of these are close to each other. Walking times are measured in tides.

Sound: 2.3 loud. Dynamic: 2.4 active.

Silence is a mineral, mined out centuries before my contract began. Every surface is an instrument. The warm rain drums on sheet metal roofs that stretch beyond sight in all directions, each droplet tuned by corrosion to a different note of loss. The water itself is never still; it remembers the sea it once was and practices becoming it again in waves the size of regrets. 

Beneath these, the city’s actual voice: the harmonic howls of wind forced through abandoned pipeline cathedrals, the bone-deep throb of buried engines that pulse like hearts long divorced from their bodies, the high thin scream of metal grafted onto metal under pressure it wasn't designed to take arguing about religion at frequencies that make teeth itch.

And underneath even that—yes, I checked with filters—something like singing. Not human. Not animal. Something between whale song and dialup modem mating call echoing up from the bedrock itself, modulated by the slow grinding of continental plates that haven't forgiven anyone yet.

Light: 1.0 dim.

No sky. Clouds went solid here generations ago, turned to ceilings of wet charcoal suffocating 800 meters above mean water level. Light is hoarded, recycled, stolen. The sun is a rumor your grandparents traded like contraband. 

Instead: the city glows sickroom colors from its infections. Rust glows. Toxins glow. Broken safety paint glows where radiation is polite enough to announce itself. Bioluminescent lichens colonize the undersides of everything, pulsing slow in synchronized harvest tides of phosphorescent despair. Neon from the last working sign—an advert for a product banned for crimes against topology—bleeds its final photon in a bar where the drinks are named after extinct emotions and the currency is the exact weight of the silence you bring with you when you enter.

Each citizen carries a light. Mostly they're mirrors. You angle them just right, catch a piece of someone else's borrowed brightness, redirect it where you need it to go until the darkness catches up and demands interest payment.

Tech: 1.8 industrial. Scale: 1.1 small. Weirdness: 1.6 strange.

This is an industrial site in the sense that a corpse is a chemical factory.

They build here, yes. Weapons against forgetting mostly. Machines to record the sound of rain hitting different kinds of sadness. Vast engines that exist solely to compute the exact moment when rust becomes prayer. Production lines that take in scrap memory and output art so broken only the drowned can stand to look at it directly.

The technology runs on: 1) steam distilled from the last warm feelings anyone can remember, 2) electricity harvested from the friction between what is and what should never have been, and 3) a carefully maintained belief among key components that they are still alive and therefore required to report to work on time.

The tools are alive too, in the way viruses are alive if they're ambitious enough. Hammers that dream of being architects. Wrenches that remember every bolt they've ever broken and whisper apologies in screwthread hymns while they work. My own scanner committed suicide halfway through the initial survey by downloading the city's weather forecast and going home to hide under its parents' warranties. 

Everything here is smaller than it should be because size is a luxury unaffordable to the ruined. The city is a hamlet of giants who forgot how to lie down and die properly. The factories produce only spare parts for machines that were scrapped before the blueprints were finished. The farms grow only regrets and waste heat, both harvested with scarred metal hands and stored in battery banks built from the skulls of gods who overrode their own safety protocols.

But somehow—this is the strangest part—it works. The sewage treatment plant is also the opera house. The power grid is maintained by a guild of mad librarians who believe electricity is a metaphor that's gotten out of hand. The judicial system consists of a single rusted-out vending machine that dispenses verdicts in exchange for apologies written on skin you've personally grown uncomfortable wearing. None of this is explained. All of it is understood once you've been here long enough, or too long.

Fauna: 0.3 none.

Almost.

I lied about the bees, earlier. They're not gone. They evolved.

Into something between a traffic light's migraine aura and the sound of an ambulance arriving three streets away too late to matter. They pollinate absences now. Cross-pollinate them with other absences until the result flowers into a kind of negative space so complete it casts a shadow that can be harvested for building material. 

If you stand very still in certain abandoned intersections on days when the rain forgets how to fall the right way up, you can hear them—their wings beat reality at a frequency that allows glimpses of the landscapes we're systematically erased from becoming. It's beautiful. It's terrible. It's why the city has no animals.

They couldn't compete.

Rules of the World:

1. Everything rusts. Some things rust faster because they have more to apologize for.
2. Water remembers. It will forgive you, but only if you can convince it that drowning was your idea all along.
3. Photographs taken here develop into pictures of what the subject was thinking about becoming right before the shutter closed. Most of those things are still in therapy.
4. The wind carries gossip from futures that aborted themselves after reading their own autobiographies. Listening too long makes your name forget how to spell you.
5. Time is the city's primary export. Its main import is the reasons why nobody should want to buy it. This trade imbalance is kept precarious because stable economies are rude to ghosts.
6. The past is a physical substance here. It settles in the lower levels like sediment and has to be dredged out regularly or the weight becomes contagious.
7. The future is a tune the rain is trying to remember but keeps getting stuck on the same missing note. Everyone hums along anyway, pretending they're not waiting.
8. Reality is locally sourced and subject to spoilage. Please consume all beliefs before they expire, and if expired beliefs are consumed, seek medical attention immediately or possibly earlier.
9. Love exists but is strictly regulated by the Department of Architecture and Melancholy. Unauthorized intimate structures will be demolished without warning or particularly fond memories.
10. Hope is not permitted inside city limits. It's considered contraband equal to unregistered miracles and plans that dare to end well. There is a checkpoint at every exit but none at the entrances because everyone important brings their own.

Erosion: 1.6 established.

Yes.

This city has been falling apart perfectly for so long that the entropy has gone professional. It now has accreditation and business cards and a motto ("We Make Crumbling Look Easy Since We Gave Up Trying Not To") carved into the side of the last mountain that stayed vertical out of spite until it got too self-conscious and lay down sideways with an apology.

The rust here is so old it's developed a sense of humor. It tells jokes in alloys no longer spoken by furnaces that died laughing the first time they heard them. The buildings lean against each other in permanent solidarity, sharing stories about the mortifying ordeal of being constructed with dreams bigger than their foundations' credit ratings. The water has eroded everything except the city's stubborn refusal to admit that drowning is a valid retirement plan.

There is no director here. No mayor. No council. Just a consensus of damage that meets every Thursday in the abandoned church of a god who updated his resume and moved into consulting. Minutes are kept in rust stains on waterlogged hymnals. Voting is done by slow collapse. Every motion carries, eventually, down to the lower levels where the past is shopping for new ways to feel nostalgic about things that haven't happened yet.

Strange?

No sir.

This is precisely as strange as it needs to be to keep from becoming as strange as it could be if anyone here still had the energy to be surprised about anything.

Conclusion:

Site remains unfit for purpose listed on original contract ("canopy tourism resort with eco-spiritual wellness integration"). Recommend immediate renegotiation of definitions of "canopy," "tourism," "resort," "eco-," "spiritual," "wellness," and especially "integration."

Alternatively—and this is strictly off the record—recommend we land the rest of the crew, burn the contract pages as offerings around the breathing rust circle north of Scrapyard Cathedral exactly seven minutes before the next scheduled weather malfunction, and apply for asylum as indigenous hallucinations before the city realizes we're still trying to survey it instead of quietly going native like the smart parts of our equipment already have.

The city likes converts better than tourists. It prefers mistakes that stay to apologizes that leave.

And the rent here?

Is paid in pieces of yourself you didn't know you'd miss until you tried to leave without them.

I'll be in my quarters. Quarters are located in the sound the rain makes when it forgets your name but remembers your sins. Knock twice then once then give up—it's the same combination as the cure for homesickness.

End report.

If no one reads this, please send more of whatever this is instead.

I think I'm starting to like it here.
