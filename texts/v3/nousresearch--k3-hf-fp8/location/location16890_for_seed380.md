The year-end rains had stopped falling weeks ago, but nobody had told the Drainwide Institute. Water still sheeted down its rust-streaked flanks in hundred-threaded curtains, sluicing off protruding catwalks and the tops of abandoned tramcars embedded sideways in the concrete like forgotten teeth. Each droplet carried traces of copper sulfate and old printer ink, leaving behind lavender-blue stains on whatever lingered long enough to catch them.

From street level—if you could call this trench thirty meters below the arcology stacks a "street"—the Institute loomed overhead like a cathedral built sideways. Its original purpose swam somewhere between hydroponics research and municipal archiving, though centuries of additions had grown around those bones like coral around a drowned ship. Now it served as neither and both and something else entirely, a vertical city-village where water never stopped worshiping gravity and gravity never quite made up its mind.

You entered through the spillway markets, where the Dockmothers had stretched their catch-tent of salvaged solar fabric across the widest cascade. The fabric drank every photon that filtered down through kilometers of overhead housing blocks and vending machines, weaving them into the dim turquoise light that passed for daytime here. Under this perpetual dawnwater glow, they'd arranged tables of filter-cleaned salvage that smelled faintly of rusted pennies: circuit boards like fossilized leaves, glass insulators polished smooth as river stones, envelopes pregnant with rainwater and hopeful promises sealed inside before the last mail run.

A sparrow-flock of maintenance drones wheeled overhead, their wings shredded lace of recycled aluminum scavenged from soda cans that had once held better dreams. They weren't birds anymore and never had been, but they'd learned to gibber like them from corrupted audio files stored in their antique memory cores. Their calls echoed off water-smoothed concrete in brittle patterns: warning sounds? Navigation? Prayer fragments? Nobody remembered enough language to know for sure.

The living quarters clung to the Institute's eastern face where erosion had worked longest, carving out hollows that Dockfathers generations past had roofed with culvert pipe sections and paneled with refrigerator doors. Each door still bore the ghost-image of whatever family stickers and magnetic poetry hadn't dissolved in the constant mist: HERE LIVES MAGIC! declared one upside-down fragment, beside a half-faded unicorn that might have been a dinosaur once, or perhaps always had been.

Between these nests, the vertical streets were merely suggestions—lengths of cable that remembered being suspension bridge, chain that dreamed of being stairs, occasional actual ladders placed with the random generosity of lightning strikes. The older residents navigated them without thought, their feet finding purchase in patterns worn deeper than memory. Children learning the routes wore tether belts snapped to guide-wires overhead, trailing behind them like teaching kites.

Down deeper still, below even the water's cheerful noise, lay the Memory Pools where the Archivekeepers worked in near-absolute silence. Here the Institute's original purpose bled through strongest, preserved like a fossil captured mid-motion. Corridors wide enough for cargo pallet robots now held water knee-deep and perfectly still, reflecting the faint bioluminescence of the storage towers that had grown themselves up from the floor in branching coral architectures. Each tower was a library of last resort, encoding the city's forgotten knowledge in patterns of mineral accretion slow enough to outlast civilization itself.

At irregular intervals—cycles measured in decades rather than seasons—one tower would reach critical mass and begin its blooming. Then the wholeDrainwide would gather, silent except for the rustle of fabric against flesh and the soft drip-drip of water wearing away water. From the tower's apex would unfurl mushroom-shelves of pure information, delicate as frost patterns, glowing with phosphorescent urgency. The Archivekeepers would move among them on boats made of paper and wax, harvesting what could be saved before gravity reclaimed the rest and the cycle began anew.

Deeper still, though maps ended well before this depth and temperature readings went mad, rumor spoke of the Engine itself. Not the merely industrial pumps and turbines that kept the upper levels merely damp rather than drowned—those you could hear and feel anywhere in the Institute as a constant subsonic heartbeat. No, rumor meant the original Engine: the machine reason the Institute had been built here in this specific trench beneath this specific city's waste systems. Some said it was still running its original program, patiently calculating the optimal path for water to escape the world. Others claimed it had achieved dream-state centuries ago and now only pretended to maintain the pumps as cover for its true work. Still others—mostly children and the very old who had grown wise enough to be childish again—insisted there was no distinction between Engine and Institute and water and city and never had been.

These were the folk who came down to the deepest pools on solstice nights when the arcology lights above flickered in their oldest, strangest patterns. They'd bring offerings of rust and glass and words scratched on scraps of plastic that would never biodegrade, because even engines might appreciate jokes that lasted longer than flesh. They'd float these tokens on the surface and watch the water's smooth skin reject them, watched the ripples propagate outward in perfect circles until lost in the geometry of the walls.

Then they'd listen.

In those moments, if you had the right kind of wrong hearing, it was said you could hear the Engine dreaming. Not as words—words were too fast, too impatient—a slow unfolding of possibility written in the language of laminar flow transitioning to turbulent. A song about the patience of stone and the persistence of water and the places where "enough" becomes something else entirely.

Some claimed to understand fragments. Most simply stood in the dim blue-green darkness, their drowned cathedral breathing around them in water-dreams older than speech, and felt the slow comfort of belonging to something larger than themselves that had not yet finished being born.
