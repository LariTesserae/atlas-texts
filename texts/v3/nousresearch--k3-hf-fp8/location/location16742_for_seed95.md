The plateau stretches beyond sight, a vast tilted plane of banded shale and petrified lichen-scars pressed two and a half kilometres closer to the thinning sky. An old wound in the crust, cauterised long before any name could be given to its pain, it was lifted and then forgotten when the world’s plates grew tired and settled into a last, reluctant embrace. You stand at the eroded lip of its eastern verge: the wind is warm, smelling faintly of baked metal and the faint, spiced breath of an extinct sea.

Water exists here only as relics—thumbprints left by vanished clouds, a dampness that gathers overnight inside the spiralling flute-hollows of a fossil coral reef, gone crisp now and clattering in the breeze like brittle wood chimes. During the highest hours of afternoon, rare condensation beads slide down the cheeks of monolithic basalt masks that jut from the ridges; ants of fused silica drink them with slow, glassy devotion before sealing themselves back into their burrows of powdered stone. No streams; no rivers; only gravity’s memory of rain that falls once every thousand lifetimes and evaporates before it touches the shattered gneiss.

Vegetation, likewise, has become cautious. A cobalt-grey lichen hangs in curtain-like sheets from the ribs of fallen lithic arches, each sheet no thicker than a moth’s wing yet toughened by age until it can be folded like paper and will not tear. Here and there, a solitary metallodendron rises—a tree wrought from pewter-grey biometal and tubes of translucent silica, its roots anchoring deep into cracks where the last faint hot breath of the planet’s cooling iron core still sighs. Every seven centuries a single transparent leaf unfurls at the apex, siphoning starlight and moonlight alike into its internal veins of mercury, then folds again, heavier by one whispered dream.

Temperature cradles everything like a fever that never quite breaks. The shale beneath your boots radiates yesterday’s heat upward; yet the brief shadows cast by balancing rocks tall as temples offer no respite, only a warmer shade of breathlessness. You feel yourself drying from the inside, parchment becoming parchment becoming parchment.

Fauna—no other word suffices—has somehow thrived on scarcity’s bounty. The plateau swarms with life not seen in any catalogue. A herd of gravity-skewed beasts—each an accordion-folded prism of cartilaginous struts and iridescent air-sacs—glides by overhead, whispering harmonics that make your teeth ache for a home you never had. They graze on nothing but the thin gradients of ultraviolet slope-angle, their digestive chambers pulsing like slowly collapsing hearts. Whenever they herd-think a new hue into existence, the horizon blushes with it for a heartbeat, then forgets.

At ground level the living is denser still: armies of cobalt mites marching in perfect fractal spirals across the faces of stupendous fossils, each mite propelling itself by excreting a single drop of acid fermented from the memory of rain, then crystallising the resulting salt into a microscopic wheel. Flocks of translucent fan-winged insects—no larger than eyelashes—flit between them, carrying pollen that is not pollen but unfinished limericks whispered between extinct tongues. Every footstep you dare to take threatens a quiet catastrophe: a mosaic of fragile exoskeletons, discarded antennae-bells, and embryonic hibernacula that have waited motionless since the plate itself was young.

Built things are here, but they are shamed by their own impermanence. Half a dozen stone lozenges, each carved from a single block of preposterously dense ore, tilt at angles that defy local gravity. They were houses once—perhaps. Now they are moults. Their doorways breathe out coolness when you pass them, exhaling soft sighs of a mathematical lullaby constructed by creatures who must have weighed the stars and found them wanting company. Inside a single lozenge, shards of an ammonite-shaped window remain, refracting the normal sunlight into a faint image of a primordial coastline that never existed here. If you stay too long in that false surf-light, you begin to forget the order in which your own bones grew.

Technology is absent because it has been eaten, metabolised, beloved into biology. The plateau’s highest law is that every invention sooner or later hatches and walks away on legs of refined function. Even the idea of a tool is lonely here; it flees into the nearest living crevice and is adopted as an organelle.

Light is ordinary at first glance—warm, slightly inclined westward, casting only modest shadows. Yet watch for hours. Notice how the sun’s disk never quite sets but instead elongates in the sky, stretching like taffy until it becomes a thin copper thread wound around the horizon’s brim; it unwinds again come dawn but never quite fast enough to repeat yesterday’s exact illumination. Shadows accumulate interest, growing richer in detail like old men remembering wars they never fought. Occasionally, when the light thread snags on some invisible jaggedness in space-time, a second shadow of you will detach and wander off toward the skyline to begin its own, more careful pilgrimage.

Sound nests inside itself: mostly the low hiss of silt being sorted by the wind’s invisible fingers, the occasional faint chiming as two limestone bells kissed by chance, or the muffled heartbeat of a burrowed contemplative entity as great as a cathedral yet convinced it is still an embryo. Human ears invent louder things—distant thunder that might just be remembered thunder stored in the spiral chambers of fossilised nautili, or the plucked-string twang of far-off mountains settling one micron more into the Earth’s embrace. But listen deeper: you will start hearing the conversations held between grains of quartz in languages that are all diphthong and pause, the slow negotiations for territory by adjacent molecules of air.

The world’s rules have entered their twilight years, grown lenient with age. Cause still precedes effect but only if they are old friends; sometimes effect wanders off to lunch alone, leaving cause to invent an excuse. Time moves, but like trickling honey that occasionally forgets which direction down is. Step over a fallen sill of shale and you may step back into yesterday’s afternoon, unchanged except for a small regret growing in one pocket like a pebble. You might meet yourself coming back, younger and still believing in cardinal directions. Being polite, neither of you mentions the future.

This is the stage where everything seems to hover just above ending, yet ending itself has gone away on sabbatical. The plateau does not expect you to stay. It offers no bargains, no curses, no comforts. Only the endless performance of an ecosystem that has forgotten how to curtain call—fauna too numerous, too strange, too busy being final drafts of themselves to notice the warm, bewildered mammal who came looking for a landscape to describe and found instead a thousand million whispering love letters addressed to no one, written in dust kept damp only by curiosity, signed in a language the ink is still inventing letter by breathing letter.
