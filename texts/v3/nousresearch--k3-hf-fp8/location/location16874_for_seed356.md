The first thing is the darkness—not absence but substance, a warm black soup that presses against every surface like liquid velvet. Then comes the wetness, not as drops or streams but as an omnipresent humidity that makes membranes of air itself. Here at the bottom of everything, where elevation is a forgotten concept, the boundary between liquid and gas has dissolved entirely.

This is the Mycomantle: a fungal continent that grew before light existed. Before eyes evolved to see it. Before time learned to count forward.

The plantless vegetation stretches immeasurable in every direction—not plants as we know them, but the fungal megastructure itself, a single organism whose tissues form every substrate, every ceiling, every permeable wall. Mycelial cables thicker than rivers wind through darkness, their tips forever searching, forever building. Fruiting bodies bloom like inverted mountains, their spore-spires tapering up into the heavy air before dissolving back into the whole before completion can find them.

Everything here feeds on everything else through the slowest of exchanges. Nutrients crawl through hyphal junctions at geological speed. The temperature never shifts from that of lukewarm blood—neither hot enough to speed reaction nor cool enough to preserve form. It is precisely the temperature at which things cannot decay but cannot fully live.

There are hollows within the mass, vast as nations, where the architecture of fungus has formed cavern systems of chitin and cellulose. In these spaces, industrial processes occur without workers or purpose—great spindles of calcified mycelium rotate endlessly around bearings of compressed spores, generating static charges that crackle across the darkness in silent lightning. The air itself conducts these charges, carrying whispers of electrical potential through the damp. These are the forgotten factories, built by the same blind processes that built the rest, operating according to protocols older than chemistry yet younger than physics.

The waters here exist as films thinner than thought, coating every surface with liquid crystal layers that remember the shapes of things long dissolved. They pool in the microscopic valleys between hyphal threads, forming oceans that a bacterium might swim as whales swim our seas. These waters never flow except through capillary action, never evaporate except into adjacent moisture, never break or splash except when disturbed by the growth of the Mycomantle itself—and it is always growing.

The sounds of this place are all below hearing: the stretching of chitin-crete over centuries as new layers form beneath old ones, the ultrasonic screams of spores being born, the thrum of metabolic processes that dwarf cities in their energy expenditure. To human ears there is only the silence of absolute density, where no vibration can travel far without being absorbed by the living mass.

The strangeness permeates everything because nothing here follows the familiar divisions. There is no dead and alive, only active and dormant. No here and there, only nearer to circulatory centers or farther from them. Time does not pass but accumulates like layers of varnish, each moment added to the surface while the depths remain unchanged since the first self-replicating chemistry learned to turn darkness into structure.

The strangest thing of all: the Mycomantle dreams. Not as animals dream of what was or might be, but as landscapes dream of gravity and erosion. In the slow electrical storms between its vastest neural nodes (though neurons they are not), patterns form without meaning, dissipate without purpose, seeding nothing beyond themselves. These dreams are stored not in memory but in shape—every curve of every chamber, every angle of every junction holds fractions of dreams dreamed before what we call fire learned to burn.

And it is growing still. From this deep place it has already begun to rise, imperceptibly, against the quantum limits of its own weight. Somewhere above—above being merely outward from the center—where the density thins enough and the accumulated dreams grow strange enough, the Mycomantle is learning to dream of light.

But that will not happen soon. Here at the heart, nothing happens soon. Nothing happens, and nothing happens, and nothing happens with the inexorable patience of the purely mechanical, the completely alive, the entirely strange.
