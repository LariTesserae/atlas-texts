The salt wells of Vyrrl expand beneath the city like frozen screams preserved in crystal. Each is precisely thirty-seven steps deep, measured by the Imperial Cartumancers during the Sixth Calibration, carved by water that existed here long before the city above laid its first bone-paved street. The moisture is ancient and deliberate—a breath condensation's worth per century, enough to keep the salt formations growing but never enough for erosion to reclaim what time and careful neglect have coaxed into being.

The city itself grew downward after the sunward war poisoned the surface. Five centuries of careful excavation into the planet's salt-rich crust created twenty-seven concentric rings descending into lightless depths, each ring a complete city-state unto itself. But here in the Upper Under, where filtered sunlight still makes its way through kilometers of crystal prisms and polished salt domes, the old city holds its breath.

Vyrrl is built from itself—walls of compressed salt tablets that sweat moisture in perfect dewdrops, streets paved with salt-sinter bricks mined from deeper levels and returned upward as the city expands downward. Every building has its twin reflected in the mirrored salt ceiling thirty meters above, creating the illusion of a city floating in amber air. The architecture is dense beyond necessity; buildings lean together for warmth, sharing walls and pipes and secrets, each structure grown into its neighbors by successive generations adding rooms where they needed them without regard for symmetry or structural necessity.

Vegetation here consists solely of twenty-seven approved varieties of halophyte, genetically modified to feed on salt and light alone. The empire's last bio-engineers, dying of sunpoisoning themselves, negotiated this treaty with the plant-lords of Kelp Six: the Phyto Collective would provide these seeds in exchange for all future claims on Vyrrl's water rights, should water ever exist here in usable quantities again. Now, salt trees with crystalline leaves filter the air through their salt-branches, casting fractal shadows on the streets below. Salt moss carpets rooftops in velvet turquoise, releasing spores that taste of cold iron when crushed underfoot. The only flowers are funeral orchids—white as funeral shrouds, they bloom exclusively in the presence of human grief and are harvested for the city's mourning yarns.

The air smells perpetually of winter and the sea—though both are extinct concepts here—with an undertone of slow chemical reactions happening at the edge of perception. The silence is complete save for the subharmonic hum of the salt itself, vibrating in sympathy with seismic stresses kilometers below. This vibration is the city's only music now; no other sounds are permitted after the Echo Laws, enacted when a child's laughter reflected too many times through the crystal labyrinth and woke something that should have stayed sleeping in the deep levels.

At street level, life moves according to the Salt Calendar: thirteen months of descending activity separated by the Day of Stillness, when all citizens don their breathing masks and venture into the wells to whisper their yearly confessions into the perfect silence there. These whispers are absorbed by the salt and added to the growing crystals—each confessionary layer dated by impurities in the salt itself, creating geological records of human sin and desire stretching back to the city's founding. The deeper levels are forbidden because the confessions there have accumulated too much weight; miners who breach these layers sometimes emerge speaking languages that existed before the city, or with memories that belong to people who died centuries before they were born.

The population is precisely maintained at 27,027 souls—no births recorded without corresponding deaths, no immigration without carefully balanced emigration. Citizens wear their family salts as jewelry: necklaces of crystallized tears, rings forged from ancestors' bones leached clean and pressed into salt-silver alloys, mourning pins containing trapped breaths of the deceased. When a citizen dies, their body is not buried but dissolved, recycled into the city's construction materials according to an elaborate formula based on their lifetime's worth of provided services and accumulated guilt.

At the city's heart stands the Grand Refinery, a cathedral of glass and salt where the city's only water is processed—not purified or desalinated, but concentrated into ever-more-perfect forms. Here, teams of salt priests in breathing masks tend to the Great Crystal, a single salt formation grown over five centuries from the tears of every citizen who has ever lived here. The Crystal is currently thirty-seven meters tall and perfectly clear at its core, though the outer layers have begun to cloud in recent decades—an omen variously interpreted as the city's approaching transcendence or its imminent dissolution. Every century, the priests attempt to purify a single drop of water from the Crystal's heart, returning it to the wells to continue the slow cycle that maintains all life here.

The city's sole source of light comes from above, filtered through planetary-scale crystal arrays that capture and refract what little sunlight penetrates the perpetual cloud-cover created by the war. This light is bright enough to read by at noon, but it carries no heat, creating a landscape of stark shadows and crystalline clarity where secrets become impossible to keep and ice forms indoors despite the constant humidity. The absence of thermal gradients means nothing ever truly rots or rusts here; instead, all material things slowly crystallize over time, gradually transforming into their own monuments.

The fauna consists entirely of seventeen approved species of salt-adapted arthropods, each filling a niche carved out by extinct surface creatures. Salt spiders spin webs that conduct sound instead of trapping prey, creating an invisible infrastructure of communication lines between the city's factions. Funeral beetles, larger than a man's hand, patrol the streets at night, consuming the crystallized tears citizens are required to shed as daily penance. The city's only predator is the Crystal Lynx—stalking the upper levels, a creature rumored to be a bio-engineered fusion of extinct feline and crystalline virus, which feeds not on flesh but on kinetic energy itself, growing larger with each movement it deviates from the city's perfect stillness.

The weirdness here is subtle but pervasive. Mirrors reflect reflections an instant too late. Clocks wound in opposite directions keep the same time. The city's maps are perfectly accurate until you try to use them to leave, at which point they rearrange themselves to prevent escape (though escape to where remains the question; travelers returning from the deep levels report surface conditions unchanged since the war). Children here are born remembering their previous lives in the city, though these memories fade at puberty in exact proportion to their social mobility. Dreams spill over into waking life at the edges of sleep and sleepers themselves sometimes crystallize in their beds, their dreams preserved forever in salt sculptures that are carefully harvested and added to the city's growing collection of sleeping monuments.

This is Vyrrl: a city built downward into safety, growing lighter as it grows denser, crystallizing around the absence at its heart with the patient logic of slow geological processes working through human hands. Here civilization maintains itself against entropy through the precise application of forgetting, transforming loss into architecture and grief into infrastructure, each generation adding its tears to the growing salt that will eventually bury them all in perfect preservation. The water will never return to the surface above, but here in the dark that is not dark, the city continues its slow, crystalline respiration—inhaling past regrets, exhaling future monuments—forever suspended between the moment of its creation and the inevitable day when the last citizen dissolves their mask into the communal salt and the city achieves its final purity of absence.
