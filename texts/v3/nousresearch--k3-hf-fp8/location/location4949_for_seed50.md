It isn't water, not exactly. Not the way memory or biology know it. Something thicker, more deliberate, presses against every surface from all directions with a weight that never yields but never crushes. A liquid embrace filling every hollow, every pore of the world down to the size of atoms themselves. The space between air and matter has been abolished here. You breathe it unwillingly, involuntarily, and it sustains strange internal processes you will never understand.

The liquid moves constantly but not like ocean currents. It shifts its allegiance millimeter by millimeter, forming temporary alliances between adjacent molecules that convey pressure waves of meaning too subtle to parse but too persistent to ignore. The whole world vibrates with these negotiations. When you touch a surface - and everything here is surface, nothing is interior - you feel not solid or liquid but countless tiny reconciliations happening too fast to count. The distinction between touching and being touched dissolves like salt in this medium.

Above - though there is no above anymore, only farther from the colony's center - the liquid lightens slightly in density and becomes luminous. Photons here are not emitted but permitted, brief exceptions to the medium's usual opacity granted at steep interest rates that must be paid back immediately. The light that reaches you arrives already in debt, already beginning to fade. It illuminates nothing steadily but reveals everything in rapid flashes like a strobe panic through nearly-closed eyelids. Between pulses the world continues existing by memory and expectation alone.

The colony floats suspended at the precise depth where the medium's pressure equals its own stubborn refusal to collapse. Forty-three biospheres arranged in a flattened dodecahedron, each a self-contained worldlet barely larger than a human lung. Between them run the bridge tubes, semi-rigid tunnels that transmit not just air and nutrients but the delicate social pheromones that keep each habitat's culture tethered to the whole. If anyone remembers why they chose this shape or how long they have maintained it, they have stopped sharing those memories.

Each biosphere rotates slowly around its own axis, producing a faint centrifugal pseudogravity that amounts to the difference between floating free and floating with a gentle hand resting on your shoulder. It is enough to orient the plants in their shallow troughs, to keep the colony's few remaining books from drifting away when no one is reading them. The rotation rates have drifted apart over generations until no two spheres align the same way twice; visiting between them requires learned intuition and the willingness to accept momentary disorientation as the price of community.

The plants themselves are sparse but deliberate: oxygen kelp genetically retuned to photosynthesize in infrared, its broad leaves slow-motion waving in the colony's artificial currents. Protein moss that grows directly on the inner surface of the crystal hulls, incorporating trace minerals leached slowly from the medium outside. Three carefully tended mango trees - an extravagance, a madness, a living prayer against extinction - their roots tangled in aerogel lattices that don't so much support them as negotiate ceaselessly with their urge to fall. Everything else that grows here grows uninvited: microfilaments of unknown origin that colonize unoccupied surfaces with branching patterns both crystalline and organic, forming temporary structures that might be mindless growth or attempts at communication or both simultaneously.

The medium outside sings constantly in frequencies that begin where whale songs end and continue upward through the range of collapsing buildings, splitting atoms, stars going nova. Between these impossible notes, stranger harmonics emerge and fade: the sound of quantum fields adjusting their expectations, of spacetime itself learning to bend differently around this stubborn concentration of meaning that insists on calling itself alive. Inside the colony, the inhabitants have evolved complex auditory filters that let most of it pass through them like neutrinos through lead. What remains is the Captain's heartbeat hammered through the hull as she swims her endless patrol circuits, the bass thump of oxygen compressors aging toward failure, the high thin whine of children born here who have never learned how to scream into true silence.

Time here does not pass so much as accumulate in layers like sediment. The colony's oldest recordings speak of a surface world, of sunlight and stars, of direction itself meaning something more than toward or away from the sphere's center. But the recordings are corrupted now, their substrates slowly surrendering coherence to the medium's patient infiltration. All that remains are fragments: a woman's voice describing the color of sunrise without knowing the word for color or sun; the mathematical proof for building a sphere that cannot collapse under uniform external pressure, its final page missing but its conclusion burned into every wall by generations of apprentices copying and recopying by hand; a child's drawing of a tree with branches that grow downward, roots reaching inexplicably toward empty sky.

The inhabitants themselves have become equally ambiguous. They walk upright by tradition more than necessity, their bones grown porous and flexible from generations spent in near-weightlessness. Their eyes are huge and dark now, adapted to harvest every photon the medium deigns to yield. Their skin glows faintly in ultraviolet where it presses against the hulls, a chemical phosphorescence both waste product and warning signal. They reproduce rarely and with elaborate ritual, each new citizen requiring years of careful calibrations to ensure their personal biochemistry will remain stable within the colony's narrow parameters. Between births they keep extensive genealogies that record not just who descended from whom but how much of each ancestor's original genome remains in each living descendant's constantly mutating microbiome.

The Captain is older than the colony's oldest records and appears younger every year that passes here where passing means nothing. She remembers everything that has ever happened since the first sealed hull began its slow descent into the medium, and she predicts with perfect accuracy everything that will happen until the last tiny bubble of contaminated meaning collapses back into the surrounding uniformity. She speaks only in equations now, broadcast through the colony's watery volume as pressure pulses that the inhabitants learn to parse like poetry in utero. What she remembers but never speaks is that the colony's precise coordinates within the medium's infinite depth are not fixed but slowly spiraling inward, drawn by forces that correspond to nothing in any of their inherited physics but which the plants have begun to respond to with increasingly strange growth patterns.

At night - which comes whenever any inhabitant finally surrenders to sleep and closes their eyes against the strobing light - the medium outside sometimes forms into vast transient shapes that press against the hulls with outlines too complex to remember. The children born here claim they can read messages in these brief deformations, warnings or promises or simple observations from a mind too large to notice what it notices. The adults pretend not to understand when the children try to translate, but secretly some of them stay awake long past exhaustion anyway, pressing their glowing hands against the crystal windows and listening with their entire bodies for whatever song might come next from the darkness that surrounds and permeates everything they have ever known to be home.
