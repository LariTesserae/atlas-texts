They call it the Scaffolded Garden, though it has grown far beyond anyone’s original intention. From orbit, it looks like an ancient copper filament nest, spun around a dead world’s spine—an open framework of lattice towers, buttresses, and crisscrossing catwalks that rise from the planet’s equatorial glacier all the way into the 180-kilometer bands of surfer air where sub-synchronous storms still remember oceanic names. Nothing here is untouched. Every pebble, drop, and petal once passed through a crucible or a stamping press. This is a place where the line between bedrock and scaffolding finally gave up and decided to have children.

The Structure

Begin at the lowest habitable level: the Rootworks. They are dimly lit now—only emergency phosphors and glow moss irrigation lines—yet still warm from the last cycle of molten-metal flows that poured new bone into the world’s fractures three centuries ago. Here, in a labyrinth of corrosion-resistant pillars encrusted with knots of calcite cables and drip-ferns, water condenses daily from high-altitude ducts and flakes off as an acidic drizzle. The rain is soft because it has been softened—passing through miles of silk-weave filters and bone-char beds harvested from the Garden’s own extinct megafauna. It pools briefly in ceramic sump-forms shaped like giant lotus leaves. Then tanks the size of cathedrals sip it up and use the pressure to launch it skyward again.

Climb any vertical conduit, riding an antique chain-lift driven by braided muscle-wire furnaces, and hatches open into the New Vestibule—bright, loud, and smelling of citrus oil and hot lemonade. Light pours down in beams thick enough to swim in: atmo-refracted sunlight caught by mirrors larger than continents, bounced once off a belt of satellites coated in herringbone gold, then decanted into 600-meter-wide lightwells lined with silvered ivy. Here the noise begins properly—turbine song layered over nursery chimes, the perpetual hammering of nutrient pumps, and the low, reassuring heartbeat of gravity-tethered arrhythmia benches where old machines dream their maintenance cycles out loud.

Above you, for 40 kilometers straight up, the Garden proper unrolls in terraces called Boughs. Each Bough is a flattened doughnut world: a strip of gravity about two Roman miles wide, its sky the underside of the next Bough above. Between them circulate airships built from beetle-wing laminate and spun sugar steel, trailing seed-bags like blimp-sized fruit. Everything grows toward the light and sideways toward company: reed forests that double as wind-bracing cables, redwood-sized bamboos whose hollows are tunnel-busses, and cypresses whose bark exudes calming pheromones to soothe the passenger pigeons that police the pest-eating drones. Leaves the color of molten glass photosynthesize in infrared and serve as Wi-Fi antennas. Roots reach downward not for soil but to plug into municipal nutrient stacks. The rain cycles back up as mist through the hollow veins of chandeliers made of living linden.

Ecology of Re-use

Fauna here are plentiful but careful. Most are neopliable—designed to be dismantled politely. Corpus deer shed antler-tools annually scraped up by swarm-shears; lantern bears hibernate inside their own gut-garages until maintenance schedules summon them. Even the insects follow protocols: pollenator bees encode delivery slips in their dance, and cockroaches—never quite eradicated—have unionized. They repair micro-cracks in plumbing with self-ossifying saliva and receive tiny ration tokens in return.

Predation still exists, but by appointment. The apex predators now are Architectural Wolves. Imagine wolf-shapes braided of wind-harvested carbon fiber muscle and ceramic chassis. Their eyes are phased-array lidar clusters. Their howls are evacuation tones modulated into baroque fugues. They patrol erosion boundaries—the places where old layering languages wrestle with new patch code—and cull any growth that forgets its place in load-bearing hierarchy. When a wolf takes a tree, that tree’s memory-core receives full archival honors. Its wood becomes corridor paneling; its sap is distilled into ceremonial ink for the next generation of blueprints.

Atmosphere & Society

The air beneath the topmost leaf-canopy is warm, humid, slightly sweet from continuous sugar-factory photosynthesis. It tastes faintly of solder flux on the east wind, vanilla on the west. Ventilation is democratic: anyone may propose a new breeze route on the civic mesh; if enough neighbors approve, drone-flocks splice new ductwork overnight. Clouds form at mezzanine levels as deliberate artworks—thermocline sculptures that condense into music when the afternoon heating ducts chant hymns through their puckered brass mouths. Occasionally a cloud will declare independence, accreting illegal rogue drones and rogue poets, wandering the heights until a wolf parliament negotiates its incorporation or dispersal in exchange for novel wind patterns.

Settlements

People live wherever cables fattened with centuries of repair cables become thick enough to hollow out. Homes are grown-cast cavities inside transport hubs, water towers repurposed into theaters, the hollow hearts of waterfalls that also serve as vertical farms. Buildings breathe and sleep. Walls carry warm blood in summer and chilled glycerin in winter. Streets are conveyor belts of slow moss that carry conversations and packages at exactly the same stately pace. If you hurry, you take the kites.

Government is entangled: a symbiosis of tree-ring referendums (each growth layer encodes budget allocations in xylem density), fungal consensus networks (spore polls conducted every rainy day at noon), and a single immortal AI built from the concatenated memory loops of every elevator that ever died of boredom. Together they negotiate something similar to peace. Resolutions are printed on leaves which fall seasonally and must be raked up and composted within a week or become law permanently. Most citizens prefer raking.

The Edge & Beyond

Higher still, beyond the thickest greenery where light unravels into ultraviolet and silence begins to win against the hum of machinery, lies the Peroxidic Lenticel—the skin of the world-garden’s lung. Here towers stretch so thin their shadows are whip-crack whispers. Air thins to a dangerous perfume. Temperature drops sharply; metal whines in the night-long breath of the planet turning under its scaffolded jacket. Only the maintenance monks of the High Repair Orders live here permanently. They walk in pressure suits painted with living lichen that generate oxygen and poetry in equal measure. Their task is to listen for the faraway groan of the last true seam—the place where the purchased rock of the planet remembers it was once raw magma not yet asked to dream of leaves.

When that seam finally speaks its cracking note, the Garden will either close itself into a seed and launch toward another star, or it will choose to bloom entirely into structure, converting every last gram of water and bone into beam-floral perfection and folding existence into a single everlasting blueprint sung by light alone.

Until then, every dawn is a rehearsal, every dusk a committee meeting, and every raindrop carries within it the bouncing, boisterous, entirely unnatural promise: the world is not built to last.

It’s built to grow.
