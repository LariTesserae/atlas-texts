Bright water-sound, a constant hissing roar, shimmers up from everywhere. There are no single sources. Instead the cliff itself weeps, overhangs drip, and every vertical face leaks in thin silver sheets. The air feels like glass under your face.

You stand on a ledge almost too narrow for the feet, halfway up a gorge eight thousand metres deep. Opposite you another wall repeats the same banding, the same stains, as if one slice of bedrock had been doubled and set back-to-back. Between them pours not a river but a fog of mist so dense it reflects nearly all light. Sunlight, from a sun that never moves, strikes the cliffs above and bounces down through galleries and tunnels until the whole canyon glows like a kiln of pure daylight. Shadows exist only as faint blue cooler spots in the dazzle.

Nothing grows. The rock, a pale tuff turned hard as chert, shows no grain for roots, no pockets for soil. Yet the surfaces are not bare. Near every seepage, microcrystals of a dozen different salts have settled out in overlapping crusts: purple at the freshest leaks, pink where the water has begun to prefer one mineral, mustard yellow where another starts to win. Lower down, where evaporation overtakes input, the colors darken through wine into metallic black. There walk ridges only a knife-edge wide where blood-red and iridescent green meet without mixing, each crystal face angled precisely to catch the light and throw it into your eyes.

The ledge beneath you is not natural. Some agency long ago hammered it flat along a single bedding plane, then incised shallow gutters to carry runoff away from the walking surface. The tool marks are visible: thousands of overlapping crescents of hard-stone striking softer, every strike aimed diagonally downward to limit the chance of a flake lifting. No statue, no inscription, no sign of dwelling-place accompanies this careful work. Only the ledge itself, and at intervals of a hundred steps one of the small cairns that mark where the path continues around a spur or across a collapsed section. Each cairn is built of three flat slabs and a rounded capstone that balances without weighting. Touch one and it rings faintly, a note that lasts longer than stone should permit.

Ahead the path narrows further, then vanishes into a slit where two cliff faces slipped past one another and sealed the gap to a crack no wider than a hand. Water streams from the top of the crack in a thin, rigid curve, like glass rod bending under its own weight but never breaking. If you put your ear to the rock you hear voices inside that sound like words spoken underwater, syllables too slow to recognize.

Behind you the ledge widens briefly into a shallow scoop walled on three sides. Here four such cracks converge, each spilling its own arched water vein that crosses the others without touching. Where they meet in the air they form a hollow sphere of spinning mist, perfectly clear at the center, edged with rainbow fringes where sunlight fractures through droplets moving at different speeds. The sphere is about the height of a person and it vibrates slightly, humming a chord that seems to come from both behind and inside your forehead.

You cannot see the bottom of the gorge. Radar and light fail in the mist after a kilometre or so. But sometimes something rises from below large enough to displace that fog like smoke torn by a wind. Then for a few seconds you glimpse structures too regular for geology: overlapping arches of stone too thin to stand, linked bridges that branch but never fall, spirals that widen upward like giant snail shells built from the inside out. They appear briefly, shimmer, collapse back into the white roar. Then the sound of falling water returns, louder than before, as if the lower world has noticed that you saw.

Touch the mist sphere with a fingernail. The vibration stops. The cracks stop leaking. The ledge underfoot ceases to hum.

Now the silence is louder than water had ever been. High above, tiny flakes of salt begin to detach from their nests and drift downward like snowflakes that never reach the ledge. They catch the light and flicker out, each one a brief white star against sky impossibly bright and empty of everything except the taste of iron in your mouth.

After a while you hear a new sound: very far below and approaching, a slow, deliberate scraping, as of a stone the size of a city waking and remembering it is alive.
