The place exists at the edge where heat meets height: a caldera terrace newly cracked open on the flank of a sleeping shield volcano, where last century's lava plateau has begun to sag under its own weight. Everything here has happened too recently for settling, too suddenly for grace.

Water leaks from every seam. Not rain - the sky remains clear and staring white above - but groundwater forced upward by geothermal pressure, seeping through fractures in black glass and grey basalt at precise thirty-meter intervals. Each fissure births a column of mist that rises exactly shoulder-height before condensing into liquid again, collapsing back as warm rain that never quite reaches the ground. The air itself drinks these offerings, perpetually saturated with moisture that cannot decide its state.

At this altitude - five thousand meters above a distant sea that exists only in geological memory - the landscape should be sterile gravel and ice pockets. Instead, a garden of impossibility thrives in the boundary zone between the lava's residual heat and the water's constant gift. The vegetation here is not rooted but riveted: tough-fibred mosses that anchor themselves with copper-alloy tendrils grown from trace metals in the rock, drinking directly from the vapor columns. Between them stand miniature forests of silicate trees, their transparent trunks grown crystal-by-crystal over fifty years from dissolved volcanic glass, each precisely calibrated to bend light toward the photosynthetic mats that carpet their upper branches like living snow.

The scale is wrong for everything. What appears from a distance as a rolling meadow resolves into a field of stone towers each no taller than a human, their tops flattened into platforms where water pools mirror-perfect before overflowing into delicate spillways carved by acid rain alone. These spillways cross and recross in three dimensions like a child's drawing of water lost in perspective, connecting pools to pools in a gravity-defying network where water flows uphill for meters at a stretch before plunging down sudden, sculpted falls.

Between the towers and terraces, the ground moves. Not quickly - this landscape's days are measured in generations - but continuously, with that slow, viscous rhythm of cooling stone. New cracks open with a sound like distant thunderclaps compressed into seconds, releasing fresh vapor and sometimes, rarely, the sulfur stench of deeper layers disturbed. Older fractures sigh closed over decades, their edges grinding together night after night with a sound that local legend calls the teeth of the mountain remembering something sweet.

The industrial presence here is older than the garden but younger than the lava. Six concrete pads remain from the geothermal survey of '87, each supporting a corroded tower of scaffolding and rusted pipework that once reached down past the water table toward magma's heat. Now these towers have become part of the ecology - their depths filled with mineral deposits that glow faintly radioactive green in moonlight, their upper works transformed into condensation chambers where rare microbes cultivate themselves in corrosive pools of concentrated brine. A single generator still functions at the center of the complex, a diesel relic converted to run on methane seeps, turning over once every six hundred seconds with a sound like a giant's heartbeat heard through a bad connection.

The generator powers nothing visible, but its rhythm shapes the place. Every tenth pulse triggers a release valve somewhere deep below, sending a surge of hot water through hidden channels that cause certain trees to bloom sudden flowers of living glass - transparent petals that refract light into patterns that move across the stones like messages written in a forgotten code. Every hundredth pulse activates something more complex in the abandoned control shed: computers older than human memory waking for microseconds to trade packets of corrupted data across copper lines grown brittle with mineral intrusion.

Fauna exists here but must be measured carefully. No birds circle overhead - the air is too thin and strange. No insects buzz between flowers - the blooms offer no nectar, only light. But in the deepest pools where water collects without boiling despite the heat, colonies of shrimp-like creatures live their entire lives in darkness, their bodies transparent except for bones grown of pure copper from the leached pipes below. They filter-feed on the microscopic glass fragments shed by the trees overhead, and in dying add their bodies to the slowly accreting mineral reefs that will, in another million years, become this place's first proper soil.

The rules here are simple but hard to learn. Water follows the paths of greatest former resistance. Heat remembers pressure long after pressure is gone. Life takes whatever gradient remains and makes geometry of it. Time moves differently at different depths - a centimeter of stone growth equals a century of moss evolution equals a day in the lives of the copper shrimp. Nothing is wasted, but everything is transformed through transformations that themselves become permanent features of the changing landscape.

At sunset - which happens suddenly at this latitude, the sun dropping straight down through clear air like a coin through water - the entire place briefly doubles. Each steam column casts a shadow of absence in the already shadowless light, while every pool of water catches and holds a perfect inverted image of the sky above. For seven minutes exactly, visitors can walk between worlds if they know the right sequence of pools to step into, each footfall breaking a reflection that reforms behind them slightly different, until they emerge - if they emerge - at the same coordinates but different elevation, having gained or lost exactly one vertical meter for every horizontal kilometer walked.

This phenomenon, recorded but never explained in the surviving survey logs, is marked on no maps except those drawn in the memory patterns of the mosses themselves. They remember every walker who ever passed this way, encoding their steps in subtle shifts of growth direction visible only to those who know how to read the living stones. The mosses do not judge these passages - they merely record them, building their slow history one molecule of memory at a time, waiting for the day when someone will return who understands what the mountain is trying to say in its long, slow language of heat and water and time made solid, then liquid, then solid again.
