The Mirrorscape of Stillnew Mud

You arrive in velvet silence at a midday that never moves. The light is absolute—the sun, if there ever was one, has been replaced by a flat white dome that presses evenly on everything below, leaving no shadows and no dusk. It is not harsh, just constant, like staring into the underside of a closed eye.

The ground beneath your feet is not ground. It is formed entirely of pale, clay-like mud—never quite wet, never quite dry—that has been shaped into gentle undulations extending to every horizon. Nothing grows out of it; instead, everything grows into it. The landscape is built, yes, but built with boredom: repeated shallow bowls and low ridges, like fingerprints pressed into soft matter by a giant who gave up halfway through. You could stretch your arms and span a dozen hills, each one identical to the last, each one unmoving since the moment they were pressed.

Water is everywhere, but it hides. The mud surface breathes to reveal flashes—not pools, not puddles, but mirror-bright patches the exact length of your arm that appear between your steps and vanish before you lift your foot. If you kneel quickly enough, before the liquid seals itself with applause-less stillness, you can glimpse another landscape inside each mirror: upside-down hills, upside-down you, upside-down horizon bending back on itself. When you touch these mirrors, your fingertip comes away clean and dry and slightly heavier, as if memory instead of water clings to the skin.

There is no stone, no sand, no bones from an older world. Instead, the whole surface is paved with prisms of hardened vegetation that never lived: translucent greenish slabs that look like leaves caught between glass, laid down edge to edge in mosaics of chlorophyll dryness. Step on one and it sings—a single leaf-bell note too high for ears, carried away by creatures with no ears either. Ten steps further, you will find the same note answered from nowhere.

Those creatures form the sky. There is no air between you and the sky because there is no sky above. Instead, the dome is the sky and the sky is crowded—every cubic span, from ground to dome, is filled with floating things that do not swim, do not fly, only drift. They are bright against the brightness: jelly-kites of living wood, their spines carved from unburned firewood, sails stretched from paper-thin bark pulled tight like drumheads. Tiny hammered-metal birds perch on the knots of the traveling trees, forever pecking for insects that dried out before the first day. Beneath them, longer creatures—swaths of braided vine as thick as rivers—undulate without moving, their weightless bellies dusted with pollen that will never find a wind to open it.

All of it is quiet except for the drip you can never locate: once every hundred breaths, a single drop of something heavier than water falls nowhere, touches nothing, and the soundless splash travels through the whole impossible distance and tickles the soles of your feet.

Smell is the only weather. Every inhalation pulls in three scents, rotating endlessly: crushed mint so fresh it bleeds, hot iron left too long in a forge that never cooled, and something that is exactly the inside of your own mouth after waking from a dream you never had. They do not mix; they arrive in sequence, like a tide turning inside your skull.

The rules of this place are simple but hard to remember. If you walk backwards the hills grow taller until they block the dome. If you stand still for too long the ground remembers your shape and begins to fill it with something warmer than mud. If you speak aloud the nearest mirror-ripples will copy your words exactly, then repeat them louder and louder until every mirror-patch trembles together and the floating rivers of vine turn briefly to face you—they have no eyes, but you can feel them deciding. The only safe rule is to walk forward in an unfamiliar rhythm, changing your pace at every third heartbeat, so nothing can anticipate where you will next disturb the calm.

There are structures here, built by hands or the softest echoes of hands. They rise only a little higher than a seated person: hollow domes of cracked mud bricks, each dome pierced with a single door shaped like an open mouth. Inside, the floor is always slightly cooler and exactly the wrong color. Some domes house stacks of tools that do not exist outside—bone hooks to catch breath, clay jars sealed with skin holding trapped echoes of laughter, stone spoons worn down to nothing by stirring something thinner than water. You may borrow any tool if you leave behind the memory of how to fear small spaces. But be cautious; the domes are jealous guardians. If you stay inside while the dome-door’s shadow touches your shadow twice, you will forget the direction of outside and have to tunnel upward through the mud ceiling, emerging miles later beneath another dome whose door will already be waiting open.

There are no other travelers in the mirrorscape, only tracks that wander in pairs—yours and a shadow-step that always arrives at every ridge exactly one heartbeat before you do. If you sprint, the tracks sprint. If you leap, they leap higher, and their landing prints are deeper, as if they carry the weight of mornings you have not woken to yet.

At the far edge of sight (which moves away at the exact speed you blink) there is always a single taller thing: a tower made entirely of mirrors knitted together with hair-thin roots of green glass. The tower does not stand on the mud; instead, the mud presses up around it like slow waves frozen mid-shiver. Each mirror in the tower shows a different time of day, but all are copies of today, endlessly rearranged: midday with yesterday’s wind, midday tomorrow before you left home. At the tower’s base lies a staircase that spirals downward into the mud with steps too shallow for feet and too deep for memory. Legend (if legend can exist where nothing changes) says that the one who reaches the bottom step will walk out into the first morning this place ever had, and all the mirrors will shatter into rain.

But no one ever reaches the last step. The staircase grows one new step behind you for each step you take, and every step is already warm, as if someone else is just ahead, walking back upward into the day that never ends.

So you walk, as everyone walks here: alone but echoing, between the built and the building, through a lullaby of constant brightness where even falling is forbidden because there has never yet been a single place low enough to fall to.
