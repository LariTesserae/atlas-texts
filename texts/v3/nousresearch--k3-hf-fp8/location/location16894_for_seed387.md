First there is the transit — a downward spiral through layers of cooled basalt, each bend in the pneumatic tube sloughing off meters of elevation until the calculation on your wristband flickers negative 3.2 km and the air begins to taste of copper and ancient pressure. Then the tube seals behind you with the sound of a swallowed whale, and you fall the last meter into water that has never touched sunlight.

The water is not water as you know it. At 400 atmospheres and 57 centigrade it has thickened to something between liquid and atmosphere, a heavy warmth that clings to your suit’s hydrophobic lattice rather than soaking through. Light dies here within arm’s reach — your helmet’s emitters carve fuzzy cones that illuminate drifting slicks of polymer gel and slow-motion storms of metallic silt. The units measure salinity in percentages that make Earth’s oceans taste like rainwater; here, sodium chloride is a trace contaminant. The dominant salts are lithium bromide and cosmic-ray-spun iodates that taste purple in the back of your mouth through the suit’s emergency osmosis filter.

You have arrived inside an urban bathyscaphe: Gilead Station, pop. 3,200 (last census cycle), habit diameter 760 meters, once a helium-3 extraction hub repurposed into a gravitational-wave foundry thirteen systemic crises ago. The Station is not so much built as grown wrong — twelve concentric pressure shells the color of oxidized blood, each older and less orthogonal than the one beneath it. Early layers have neat seams of precast composite, faint perf-stamping still visible. The newest additions are welded salvage stretched tight as drum hides by hydrostatic pressure, their weld beads flowing like wax under the eternal squeeze. Every surface above the waterline is covered in soft fungal mats that convert ambient vibration into sugars. You realize later the entire city hums in the food spectrum.

The air pockets — there are five, none larger than a gymnasium — hung like inverted ponds in the roof of the flood, trembling against gravity’s shove. The Station’s spin was abandoned centuries back when the gyroscopics seized, so “up” is whichever direction the nearest bulkhead bulges least. Getting from bubble to bubble means swimming through flooded boulevards where the storefronts are sealed behind diamond glass and the drowned neon signs still blink advertisements for brands that dissolved before your grandparents were born.

Sound is the first fact the body learns. Gilead Station is embedded in the throat of a tectonic throat-singer. A basaltic megafault twelve kilometers below drags its claws across anorthosite seams at one point nine centimeters per year, releasing infrasounds that start at 0.001 hertz and accelerate up into the audible spectrum as harmonics ricochet through magma chambers and alloy struts. The result is a chord progression that has been playing for three million years. Right now the cycle peaks at 58 minutes past the hour with a sub-bass detonation — christened “The Amen” by teenage residents — that makes your teeth buzz in their sockets and triggers the citywide bowel-evacuation protocol for untreated livestock. (They keep engineered axolotls now; the manure floats and the children paint murals on the skins.)

Between Amens there is the industrial chorus: blast furnaces pouring vacuum-distilled nickel into casting basins shaped like cathedral organs, the thirteen-story forging hammer that stamps hull plates for ships that will never leave this ocean, the sewage reclamation centrifuges spinning at 10,000 rpm in perfect phase cancellation to walk their combined vibration down the frequency ladder and push the entropy uphill. You feel it all through the water before the bones catch up: a constant, purposeful turbulence that keeps the silt in suspension and dreams out of alignment.

Vegetation is forbidden by treaty — something about invasive chemosynthesis incompatible with the native ecosystem. The native ecosystem consists of exactly three species: archaea that eat iron and excrete rust; metre-long nematodes with silver skin that absorb the rust through their pores and metabolize it into graphene nanoribbon synapses; and the Station mat-assemblers that graze on both and knit the waste into pressure valves when nobody’s looking. The mat-assemblers are the closest thing to fauna. They drift through flooded corridors like lazy manatees made of burlap and salvaged ductwork, their surface colonies flashing one-bit semaphore in the bioluminescent bacteria they farmed off a wrecked bioluminescence-import ship. Smarter than dogs, less than dolphins, they never speak and sometimes trade.

Light sources you catalog as you swim the main arterial:

1) Illegal fusion microcettes smuggled down in medical scanners, now housed inside prayer wheels spun by penitent fraudsters. Each rotation generates 130 lumens and one decade’s worth of guilt transference in the local karma economy.
2) Cathode vines grown from vintage Soviet plutonium triggers wired to coral-salt batteries. They produce a steady corpse-blue glow and taste of mandarin oranges to registered suicides.
3) The eyes of the ghost children — thirty-four sub-sapient AI clones of immigrant miners whose pay packets were lost in a bankrupt asset chain prediction market. Their processors still draw trickle current through the city’s betrayal capacitors. They follow you from bubble to bubble, asking if you’ve seen their parents in languages that have gone extinct twice.

The Station keeps time by pressure leaks. Each decade the ocean wins a millimeter of hull. Each century the residents retreat inward, sealing off an outer ring, welding the doors shut behind them, painting murals on the bulkheads that show what they imagine trees looked like. The newest outer district — Deep Crossings — depressurized last year when an unnoticed colony of halide-eating bacteria ate through a million-dollar safety seam. Now it is an acoustic cathedral: every footstep on the remaining flooring triggers a thousand-meter reverb filled with the slowed-down screams of decompression alarms and the last voicemail messages of the people who forgot to leave. Artists pay good credit to suitswim out there and record remixes.

You are here to buy a crime. Specifically, an octave-shifted forgery of the Amen pulse to smuggle upstairs where the fault lawyers can use it as evidence that Gilead Station is a natural person under interplanetary admiralty law and therefore qualified to secede from the Hydro-Titanic Consortium. The going rate is six kilos of freeze-dried nostalgia obtained before the last cultural singularity, but since you don’t have any you brought instead a memory of snow falling on a childhood that wasn’t yours, recorded off a dying brain at the Sunset Market on Europa.
