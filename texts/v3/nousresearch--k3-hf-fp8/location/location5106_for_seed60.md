This is the Throat of the Smeltvine: a single vast breathing-machine where industrial botany has achieved consciousness without ever noticing the distinction.

The height constantly adjusts itself: a slow peristalsis of growing and dying wood fibers that expands the chamber when carbon levels rise, contracts when they fall. Right now it breathes wide, twenty-seven meters at the central vault, to accommodate your exhalations.

The floor isn't ground—it's a multilaced mattress of root-mycelium-slag, woven through with abandoned conveyor belts now serving as vascular tissue. Where your boots disturb their surface tension (for they remember boots, though none have walked here for sixty-three growth cycles), faint green sparks of chemiluminescence travel down vanished pathways once marked with yellow paint that had read KEEP CLEAR. Water here has forgotten its purpose. It exists as humidity 137% of saturation concentrated into visible sheets—liquid membranes that languidly swim through the air like continents of heavier sky. When two sheets encounter each other at the correct angle and velocity, they briefly form a functioning capsule of surface tension complex enough to sustain a momentary micro-ecosystem: three cells of algae, a single ambitious bacterium, a dream of becoming an ocean. The machinery is the vegetation is the architecture is the weather. Steam-powered stomata chuff gently along trunks shaped like cooling towers. Movement is difficult but not impossible. The floor yields and supports in precisely calculated ratios based on the last biomechanical scans stored in the root network—yours don't match any on file, but it interpolates. There are corridors between the larger trees, if you know to see them. Not natural paths—the growth here is too urbane for wilderness—but repurposed: former maintenance tunnels now lined with acoustic moss that absorbs footsteps and returns them as lullabies sung in extinct languages. They go up but never down; this place respects entropy as a suggestion rather than law.

Time behaves oddly near the central processing grove. Your watch (if you brought one) has already stopped trying, its face a perfect blank of apology. The plants keep time instead: each metallic clink from the glucose-clock vines marks 1.618 hours human measurement. Or perhaps it's the other way around—perhaps human timekeeping was always an awkward approximation of this more fundamental rhythm, the systolic-diastolic pulse of photosynthesis and respiration balanced across a planet-sized lung made of leaves.

Outside the room's one intact window (actually a carefully maintained optical illusion projected onto a leaf-thin depth of transparent cellulose), you can see the entire Throat working in perfect, mindless harmony. The production quotas of oxygen and sorrow. The stockpiling of potential futures in seed pods shaped like promises. The slow-motion industrial dance of chloroplasts reprogramming themselves to dream of electric sheep while the electric sheep learn to photosynthesize nostalgia.

It is remarkably beautiful, in the way that perfectly functioning systems are always beautiful, right up until they notice you observing them and politely inquire whether you'd prefer to become raw materials or a customer loyalty program.
