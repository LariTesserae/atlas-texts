The air here is a thick broth, humid with the memory of rain that never quite arrives. Water drips somewhere—never steady enough to count, never absent enough to forget. It beads on every surface that isn't already slick with moss or slime: overhead pipes of corroded iron, crumbling brickwork stained white with salt leach, the irregular gills of shelf fungi flaring out in fan-shaped cascades. The drips echo strangely through the space, as if each one remembers other drops it once traveled beside, and mourns them.

This is an underground world built by someone who forgot—or never knew—that the surface existed. The scale is wrong for a natural cave system but right for some fever-dream of architecture: cathedral heights in places where vaults have collapsed into each other, lending sudden echoes to every space. Mostly though, the ceilings press down at mercantile building heights, tiled in squares of blackened ceramic that shed flakes like dead scales. Columns rise in irregular spirals where load-bearing calculations intersected with aesthetics that favored resemblance to things that grow—tree trunks, coral, fossilized organs of unknown beasts—all cast in the same cheap gray concrete that everything here seems made of.

Though below ground, the place isn't dark. Light filters down in shafts where ancient skylight wells still connect to streets above, now completely buried. Each shaft has become a microclimate garden inverted, with pale vines and translucent leaves layered around the edges like the pages of books that forgot how to close, filtering the sun down into something greenish and underwater. Elsewhere, gaslamps still burn with a steady blue flame behind cracked glass globes, fed by pipes whose origins are best not contemplated too closely.

The temperature holds at summer fever pitch, maintained less by the scant heat from above than by the slow metabolism of decomposition happening everywhere. Everything here that was ever dead—wood, paper, fabric, flesh—is still in the process of becoming something else, and that transition generates warmth like a compost heap of civilization's leavings. The few living things move through this heat as if dreaming: white crabs the size of hands that scuttle sideways across the ceiling, their eyestalks converging on nothing; clusters of blind fish hanging motionless in pools that should be stagnant but aren't; and once—a memory that surfaces and sinks again like a bubble in tar—the glimpse of something pale and long-limbed, walking upright through the steam.

But these are merely the indigenous fauna. The dominant life here, the thing that made this place and keeps remaking it, is vegetable now for the most part. Plants rooted in impossible substrates—mangroves that drink from cracked sewer mains, figs that strangle the concrete bones of collapsed buildings and call it sacrifice—have grown together into a single vast organism whose boundaries are unclear and whose intentions impossible to guess. Breath in, breath out: every night around (what must be) midnight, the entire system exhales a cloud of spores so thick it falls like gray snow, coating every horizontal surface with a fresh layer of substrate for tomorrow's growth.

Through this, carved by slow inexorable counter-pressure like rivers seeking sea level, runs the last of human habitation. The remaining people—how many generations below daylight now? they themselves disagree—occupy carved-out spaces like plant galls or coral excavations, cities (if they still deserve the name) grown backward into the organic meshwork rather than imposed upon it. Their technology is the technology of salvage and circumstance: pumps that keep their reclaimed squares and alleys merely damp instead of flooded, filter-beds of fungi and moss that turn foul water drinkable again, stoves that burn the eternal methane of the deep layers to cook food grown under gaslight hydroponics.

Their buildings start as tents popped open inside bone-dry chambers where roof-falls have created sudden temporary sky, then become shacks, then houses, then compounds, then neighborhoods that eventually send out exploratory tendrils of their own—always upward, always toward the buried sun they can never reach. The construction materials are whatever the plant-city rejects or cannot digest: carbon-fiber panels from wind turbines whose blades still turn somewhere far above, rusting silently in the dark wind of buried gorges; ceramic insulators from power lines that carried current to dead cities; sheets of bulletproof glass cut down to make windows for rooms that will never see enemies armored in anything but spore-clouds and time.

They make paths through the jungle of infrastructure-gone-native by agreement rather than force, marking territories with patterns of light rather than walls. To block growth is to invite rot; to invite growth is to be eaten slowly from within. So they move instead, constantly, like fish maintaining position in a stream they cannot see. The old maps are kept as art now, too beautiful to be useful—color-coded dreams of motionless geometry preserved while the world around them crawls and flexes and fruits.

And at the heart (if any place so rhizomatic can be said to have such a thing) are the gardens where they grow what's left of their history. Not as books—the plants find paper too delicious—or as data—electricity goes strange in the presence of so much life—but as architecture plants: trees grafted into living sculptures of their own ancestral forms, bushes trimmed not for beauty but to maintain negative spaces that remember vanished buildings. Stand at the right angle in the right season when the growth hormones are balanced just so and you can see your great-great-grandmother's house in the gaps between branches, occupied now by flocks of silver doves that never learned to sing because they have never heard a hawk.

But very few visitors ever reach that far. Most who come this deep are either lost already or searching for something they've forgotten has no name that can survive being spoken in air this thick with other purposes. They pass through the outskirts: the shanties built on platforms over pools that boil at random from geothermal vents below, where laundry dries in the updrafts from cracks that smell faintly of sulfur and fried circuits. The markets where things are traded not by value but by resonance—the particular song this shell makes when blown through with spore-rich breath, the pattern this vein of copper remembers from when it was molten miles below the world's skin. The workshops where machines still run on principles half-magic, half-thermodynamics, where metal is coaxed rather than cut, where nothing ever breaks because everything is already in the process of becoming something else.

And they hear the sound that fills all the spaces between these temporary things: not quite silence, not quite music, but the deep slow breath of a world dreaming itself alive from the discarded parts of another dream entirely. One that forgot to end when the dreamers woke up, or perhaps just changed its mind about who was dreaming whom.

If they stay too long, they start to forget the difference between growth and decay, between building and living in, between the map and the territory and the thing neither map nor territory was ever quite able to capture. They start to dream in green fractals that taste of copper and rain that remembers being sea. They start to understand what the birds are really saying when they sing at dusk.

And sometimes—rarely, but with increasing frequency as the years go and the plant-city grows ever more complex in its negotiations with gravity and light and time—sometimes they wake up to find roots growing from the soles of their feet, gentle and painless as memory, anchoring them in place with exactly the pressure needed to keep them from falling off the world when it finally finishes turning over in its sleep and forgets which way 'up' used to be.

Most find this peaceful, they say, those few who remember language long enough to speak of such things.

The others merely smile, and flower.
