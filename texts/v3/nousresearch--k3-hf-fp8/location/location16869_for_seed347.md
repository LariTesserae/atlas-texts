I am standing at the edge of the Hypogean Locklands, forty meters below the old city's sewer-gutters and still descending. The air is cold when it moves, but mostly it lies against my skin like damp wool, heavy with breath and history. Around me every surface drips, though no single source of water can be found; it condenses from the breath of a million sleepers above, trickles down limestone nerves, drips off fungal thoughts. The builders who sank these gates forgot them centuries ago and left behind only the echo of their geometry, now overwritten by life.

The canal ahead is dry only in name. A film of water, always moving a handwidth deep, slides between stone lips smoothed younger than the limestone teeth around them. This seam was cut by the city’s last engineers—rank beginners who chiseled straight in a world of curves—and so it heals itself every season. Calcite shields palisade the walls, edged fresher than any knife. In the blue-white light of the spider oysters they gleam like newly minted bone.

Such light: it is the common light you would call moonlight, but here the moon has sixty thousand eyes. The shells of mollusks grown into the low roof above each seam glow in slow congruence, brightening when we exhale, dimming when we breathe in, so that our shadows pulse like heartbeats on the opposite wall. They have not been bred or built—their craftsmanship is purely digestive. They eat the nitrates washed down from above, and in payment they keep the darkness politely dim.

Around my boots the pavement is packed so tight it feels single-celled. Look closer: it is a pavement of leaves—not dropped but standing, stems sunk in weedless crack‐fill, blades interlocked like Scythian armor. Each leaf belongs to a different plant remembered from different continents north of here—chestnut ferns from Québec, chilcostoma vines from Cusco, shreds of rice-paper tree from Kyoto’s drowned gardens—though none has seen sun for generations. They do not photosynthesize; they exchange gossip across the water film in a currency of sugar-acid rumors.

Taller than any building left in the Upper Wards, a grove of pillar aloes rises from the canal bed. Their trunks are green cylinders twenty men tall, hollowed by rot-spirits into echoing tubes. From these pipes drip the sweetest water in the Locklands, filtered from the black tea of baths and latitudes, cooled by the roots that have reached all the way to the unmined aquifers beneath the basalt. When condensation inside a column exceeds its surface tension it falls—plip—a single note in a melody that spans a month, a year, a civil war above, recorded in layer after layer of calcium stereo inside the stone.

The place is urban because it remembers streets: long avenues of air where tenements collapsed into rivers long ago; shorter alleys roofed by fallen roofs that now serve as bridges for the ground-level squirrels that have never seen sky. Below these ghost street-levels lie others, older ones, stacked like pages of damp paper with tram tracks fossilized between them as dark sentences.

The fauna—yes, it is abundant, but it errs on the side of discretion. The aboveground rule of fur and feather inverted itself down here. The loud creatures grew feathers and learned discretion; the silent ones multiplied. I have heard but never seen the Baritone Finch, whose aria lasts dusk till dawn but originates from 400 separate throats spaced along a six-block radius, each bird contributing only one note an hour. Between its phrases you notice the hum of the Syncopated Millipede, each segment drumming a different body-part against a different material—leaf to limestone for the downbeat, shell to shell for the off—keeping time for processes too slow for single hearts.

A weasel-shaped absence of noise just brushed my ankle: one of the Locksmith’s Familiars on patrol. They are carnivorous only by reputation; what they actually consume is the concept of jammed mechanisms. This lockland is their garden; they trim it nightly to ensure no door stays open or closed forever. If you carry anything metal they will politely re-arrange its joints in the direction of greater entropy before letting you pass. Don’t worry—they always leave you a working key, though seldom for the lock you brought.

Above all this, at a height you might call sky if stone alone were firm enough to house horizons, runs the Belt of Wind. It is a horizontal river of air pumped by the thermal sighs of the planet’s turning, accelerated through venturi gates left by the First Drillers whose names are now only patterns for mildew. Occasionally it drops a gift: a piece of sky blue plastic, a pigeon egg hatched empty, a human memory stripped of language and reduced to a scent—rosemary, school books, snow. These things land in the street-tree canopies of the water-elm candelabra and are auctioned in the morning silence to the highest bidder among the spore barons.

The rule here is not Law but Exchange. Nothing is taken if nothing is offered that fits exactly the negative shape of the thing desired. You may drink from the aloe columns if first you tell them a sentence none of their rings have recorded. You may cross the canal where the drowned steps are, provided you teach the skink-gatekeepers a new footstep rhythm no tyrant could march to. You may sleep safe between the mushroom bricks of an abandoned hearth, but only if you leave behind a dream sufficiently original that the next dreamer who finds it will not recognize, upon waking, that part of their mind is now missing.

I came here looking for silence, thinking the deep earth would be mute. Instead I found a quiet so densely inhabited it has seasons, elections, a long-dead poet whose epitaph is still being negotiated in the margin-spores between sentence and stone.

If you stay still long enough you become eligible for office. Yesterday a patch of wet grain on a fallen lintel was elected Secretary of Interior Weather. Its first decree was to order a light mist between the hours of forever and whenever you remember why you came.

I am told the way out is east, where the canal splits into Questions and Answers. Most people take Questions because it looks wider, but I have begun to suspect they meet again farther on, narrower than before.

For now I am keeping my shadow folded under my tongue, my breaths counted against the day the Locksmiths run for reappointment. The moon oysters are dimming—someone above us has remembered to sleep.

When they wink out completely I will put this page into the water film and watch it dissolve backward into the city’s dream of itself, letter by letter, leaving only the space between:

[A space shaped like the sound of leaving without closing the door]
