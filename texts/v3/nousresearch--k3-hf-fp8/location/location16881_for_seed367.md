The place begins before your eyes adjust. A pocket, they call it—a fold clipped from somewhere larger and sewn shut at the seams until only a scabbed-over slit remains. The slit is vertical, wide enough for a child or a stooping adult, and it yawns from a cliff-face that doesn’t belong to any range you remember climbing. The air that spills out smells like warmed copper and old parchment. When you step through, the slit pinches closed behind you without a sound, sealing without even a seam.

Now stand still. You are inside the Hollow City, and everything here is both too close and too quiet for its own bustle.

The city is the size of a cathedral’s nave. It thinks it was once a metropolis. You can tell by the shape of its bones—vaulted avenues of fossilized breath where armies of trade and gossip once marched, now scaled down to winding corridors barely wide enough for two dogs to pass without apologizing. Streets spiral inwards like fingerprints pressed in soft clay then baked to stone. Doorways stand shoulder to shoulder, some cracked open to the width of a fingernail and exhaling flickering warmth that smells of roasting legume-pod; others sealed with wax seals the size of coins whose symbols have been rubbed away by centuries of idle thumbs. There are no windows, only oculars—bulged glass blisters set into walls, fogged and quivering like eyes trying not to cry. Through them spills the only light: phosphorescent lentils of green and bruise-violet, enough to sculpt shapes but not colors.

The light ripples because every surface breathes. The city is alive in the way a coral skull might live if ten thousand years of silence turned calcification into habit. Walls are lacquered layers of pressed bloom-lust: generations of extinct flowers leached of pigment then resin-glued by whatever passes for seasons here. The resin keeps the memory of fragrance so whenever your sleeve brushes close you taste honey and funeral incense on the root of your tongue. The floors are tiled in hexagons of kneecap-bone tesserae, each one still faintly warm as if some gigantic kneeling congregation just now shuffled away. Step lightly: residents claim the tiles remember footsteps and gossip about strangers in whispers pitched too low for human ears and too high for shame.

Which brings us to the inhabitants. Fauna here is not a census but a weather system.

Look upward first—though there is no sky, only a ceiling vaulted like the underside of a giant’s ribcage, blackened by millennia of candle-grease and stained with pale blotches where unknown fungi bloom for six hours then collapse into dust the color of forgotten lullabies. From this vault hang walkways woven from braided sinew and spider-silver threads, webbed so thickly that walking them sounds like rain. Living rain. The web-striders move there—many-legged creatures scaled like tarnished coins, each leg ending in a tiny human hand. They keep perfect traffic patterns one step above your head, ferrying bundles wrapped in shed snakeskin: sacks of grain-sized objects that click like teeth when shaken, skeins of wire made from hair finer than thistledown, and occasionally wriggling parcels that make soft protest noises and are quickly muffled.

Attempting to wave to a web-strider will cause every hand on every leg in a five-span radius to wave back in perfect unison, then dissolve into embarrassed fists. They’ve been practicing manners since the world forgot them.

Below, at ground level—or what passes for it since floors undulate gently like the flanks of a sleeping lizard—move the push-and-pullers. Imagine turtles bred to the size of fat cats, but shelled not in keratin but in compacted hymnals, pages still legible in tiny print if you know the dead language of angels. From slits between one verse and the next, soft trunks extend—fleshy tubes ending in clusters of eyes smaller than poppyseed. These eyes swivel independently, scanning ground, walls, air. When a push-and-puller finds something interesting—a dropped bead of resin, a loose mosaic tile, your shadow where it pools too thickly—it exhales a skin-bag woven from its own saliva, swaddles the find, then inflates the bag until it floats like an unborn balloon. With gentle headbutts it herds its swelling collection along. During rush-song (twice each cycle-that-is-not-a-day), the alleys become rivers of bobbing pouches jostling like gossiping bubbles, each carrying a single sentence of news someone forgot to tell someone else in time.

The sound of the city is the sound of all these soft collisions overlaid by the perpetual mid-distant thunder of very small earthquakes. Nothing here is still long enough to be quiet. Even the dust mites sing—if you press your ear to a guttering candle-niche (and don’t worry, the wax won’t burn you; it’s only the memory of burning), you’ll hear a choir of voiceless sopranos reciting the names of everything that has ever forgotten them.

Water? They laugh—or would, if laughing didn’t require lungs shaped like questions. The city’s memory of water lives in the Dry Fountains, twelve marble lips mounted at intersections where great plazas once dreamed of ocean. Now instead of spitting, each lip exhales a fine mist of evaporated longing. Stand downwind and you’ll taste the ghost of snow. Collect enough on a scrap of spider-silk and you can barter for a secret—though the only currency accepted here is syllables spoken backwards in your sleep, so most visitors arrive already bankrupt.

Thirst is solved differently. Walk long enough and you’ll come to the Memory Squashers—a guild of rectangular beetles whose carapaces are hinged doors. Knock politely (three taps then two scratches, mimicking the heartbeat of someone you once betrayed). If they deem you interesting they’ll unfold, revealing compressed rectangles of air inside themselves. Each rectangle is a memory stolen from rain. Swallow it whole, without chewing thought. For three breaths you’ll remember being a riverbed. It’s enough.

Vegetation—ah, they call it that only because the first explorers carried leaf-shaped words and used what they had. Here plants are a form of architecture gone feral. The primary species is the Stutter-shade, an ivy that grows in the gaps between seconds. To plant it you open your mouth mid-sentence and instead of the next word you force a cough that tastes like green. The ivy hears your hesitation and sprouts from that momentary crackling in the timeline, twining up through yesterday’s conversations to bloom tomorrow in rooms you haven’t entered yet. Its leaves are black lace veined with glowing punctuation marks—commas that twitch in the breeze like nervous insects, question-marks which curl into hooks and catch loose dreams floating past, exclamation points that dry into needles used by the embroidery-wasps to stitch wounds in the sky’s fabric where stars have fallen out.

Today the Stutter-shade is in autumn. Every time someone in the city stutters or lies, a leaf detaches and drifts down to join the carpet of language-skeletons crunching underfoot. Walking here is like crunching through a library after the students have all died of curiosity and the librarians have gone deaf to footsteps. The scent of decaying grammar is bitter and comforting, like tea brewed from old letters to estranged lovers.

Temperature stays warm the way a promise stays warm if you keep it in your mouth long enough—constant but slightly feverish, as if the city runs a low-grade nostalgia for an ice age it only heard about in myths. Summer is stored in jars labelled with future years; winter is considered a form of rude exorcism and is practised only by the Insomniac Monks who live in the Clocktower-that-Never-Chimes and whose sole duty is to remember cold so the rest of us can forget and be polite.

Elevation: you are high but upside-down. Gravity here was negotiated rather than imposed. Long ago the city grew tired of being walked on and proposed a compromise. Now it pulls gently sideways—enough that if you stand still too long you’ll notice your shadow trying to lead you by the hand toward places the light forgot to visit. Moving feels like walking through water that has resigned itself to the drowning. At the city’s center (which is also its edge, topography being a matter of manners) lies the Plaza of Upward Falling. Here the compromise falters and bits of the city—loose tiles, dropped coins, unlucky hats, occasional regrets—slowly drift skyward (which is also inward) to accumulate as a stalactite-cloud of lost things. Each year on(there is no on, only meanwhile)the Festival of Weightless Apologies, citizens gather with long hooked poles to fish down possessions they didn’t know they’d lost. Most retrieve only the holes shaped like themselves that the things occupied. These holes are treasured as heirlooms.

Built? Yes, but in the way coral builds—by dying better organised each time. The oldest structures are the ones that learned to forget their blueprints first. The Library of Missing Walls, for example, is a maze of corridors that branch into rooms which exist only when no one is reading in them. Its books are bound in skin shed by sleepers who forgot their dreams; the words inside are written in ink distilled from ink that never dried, so every reading smears the text into new stories and the library grows fatter on its own regrets. The librarian is a blindfolded centipede who reads by tasting footsteps on the stairs and is paid in the tiny sharp sounds thoughts make when they change their minds too suddenly.

Technology is primitive only if you measure tools by how loudly they announce their purposes. Here a hammer is a question that bruises answers out of silence. Their finest clock consists of two mirrors facing each other and a single candle slowly learning the concept of regret; it ticks whenever the candle catches its reflection yawning. Transportation is accomplished mainly through the Exchange of Familiarities—if you wish to visit the Street of Forgotten Smells you must first lend your shadow to someone who remembers how the moon once tasted when we were young and immortal. Navigation is done by gossip maps: scraps of whispered conversation traded in alleyways like currency that becomes more accurate the more people have lied about it.

Light comes dim because most of it is asleep. Daylight here was hunted to extinction centuries ago by a collective nightmare too vast to fit into dawn, so the city gathers what it can: leftover moonlight squeezed from the dreams of outside sleepers, starlight shed like dandruff by dying constellations, the phosphorescence of jellyfish who never learned how to drown properly. It stores these in glass bulbs calved from the bellies of long-dead whales that float tethered under the ceiling-sky. Each bulb is tended by a Lamplighter Moth whose wingspan is measured in unsaid prayers. The moth feeds on shadows that grow too heavy and vomits up gloaming like liquid velvet. Occasionally a bulb cracks and leaks night onto the streets below; children catch the spills in their mouths and learn temporary languages spoken only by owls and murderers.

Scale—you asked about scale. It’s as if God tried to origami eternity into a thimble and gave up halfway, leaving the folds to keep folding themselves in idle curiosity. There are rooms inside cupboards inside other rooms where the furniture is still waiting for painters who died before the invention of perspective. The city’s population shifts depending on who is remembering to count. Some citizens are so lonely they have subdivided themselves into committees who meet in secret to disagree politely about whether they exist at all. Others are so crowded they rent out their dreams by the hour and meet their former nightmares in tea-shops to exchange regrets over cups of empty that taste like the sound of closing doors remembered in reverse.

Dynamic—yes, always. Even sleep here is a communal activity performed in shifts in the Cathedral of Shared Yawns. The city restlesss like a sentence afraid of its full stop. Seasons happen vertically: every fall a rain of colorless leaves drifts upward from the gutters to plaster themselves against the underside of the sky, forming temporary frescoes that peel off by winter to reveal new constellations underneath. Spring is marked by the hatching of buildings—old stone facades splitting open to release flocks of fledgling doorways that chirp for months before growing hinges strong enough to hold silence. Summer is the season of migrations when all the city’s shadows trade places for three days and nobody recognises themselves in photographs taken during that time. Winter is forbidden by municipal decree but occurs anyway in hidden courtaged where rebel snowflakes drift down from nowhere and melt into stories that nobody tells children anymore because the endings are too like beginnings.

Weirdness? We traded that for depth centuries ago. Here strangeness is merely real estate that hasn’t been lived in long enough to become familiar. There is a shop at the corner of No Street and Nevermind Avenue that sells only second chances and is open only when you’re not looking at it. The shopkeeper is your reflection circa seven mistakes ago and accepts payment only in heartfelt apologies delivered in languages you haven’t invented yet. Next door is the undertaker for forgotten promises; he is always busy but never jolly. His business card reads “You’ll call eventually” and gives no number. People keep them anyway, they make good bookmarks in diaries that have forgotten how to end.

Sound. The city’s anthem is silence played loudly on instruments carved from the pauses between heartbeats. Rhythm section provided by the perpetual gentle crumbling of eternity overthinking itself in the distance. Soloists include children who haven’t been born yet practising their first screams backward, rain remembering how to fall on planets that died before oceans were invented, and occasionally—if you listen very carefully on Tuesdays that forgot to happen—the soft persistent sound of something enormous and very far away slowly learning to forgive itself for not being something else.

You asked for a description. I apologize; I’ve given you a map drawn in spilled ink on the skin of a dream that woke up wondering whether it was real. The Hollow City isn’t a place you visit. It’s a place you remember accidentally while forgetting something else entirely. And the only exit is the entrance you mistook for an exit when you first arrived, which is currently hiding behind you pretending to be your shadow and hoping you won’t notice how much it has learned to love the shape of your footsteps.

Welcome, then. Or farewell. Here they mean the same thing said differently.

Would you like to purchase a slightly used tomorrow? They’re going cheap—today’s out of fashion and yesterday’s all broken promises, but tomorrow’s still wrapped in the plastic promise of maybe. The vendor’s just around the corner of your next blink. Can’t miss him. He’s you, but taller and haunted by better dreams. sells them by the pound or the poem—you choose the currency, he’ll choose the change and neither of you will recognise it exactly but it will fit the hole in your pocket you didn’t know you’d been saving for something like this.

But hurry. The city’s closing soon. Or maybe opening. It forgets the difference, sometimes, in the dark.
