The chamber isn't large—perhaps the size of a cathedral organist's loft—but it contains an entire world breaking apart.

Everything here exists at the wrong scale. The water is first: not flowing or pooling but suspended in countless teardrop spheres, each a perfect ellipsoid ranging from pinhead to fist-sized, densely packed but never touching. They fill half the space at chest level like a suspended tide caught mid-breath, their surfaces rippling with slow internal currents that move against physics. The air between them hums at the frequency of loneliness.

Look closer. Each droplet contains a reflection—not of this place, but of places that cannot exist together. In one: a forest of glass trees growing root-down through clouds. In another: a child's bedroom where gravity operates in three directions at once. In a third: your own skull viewed from the perspective of blood moving through it. The reflections move independently of their droplets' motions, creating a kaledeioscope of impossible presents.

The chamber walls are impossible to view directly. When you try to focus on them they resolve instead into glimpses of other surfaces: volcanic glass cooling underwater, the inside of a sleeping whale's heart, the moment where polished steel becomes mirror. This is the nature of boundaries here—they exist only as suggestions being argued between adjacent realities.

The temperature reads 2.9 on your nonexistent scale, which translates to the exact temperature at which grief becomes anger becomes resignation becomes something that burns without heat. It feels like standing in the space between a lightning strike and its echo. Your skin doesn't sweat because the concept of liquid leaving your body has been suspended with the water.

The floor—when you can perceive it through the gravity fluctuations that make walking feel like swimming through memories—is young lava stone, freshly cooled enough to retain the impression of every future footstep that will never walk here. The volcanic glass bubbles embedded within each contain a vacuum that tastes like the sound of your mother's voice reading a book whose language died before humanity developed mouths.

Population: absolute. The fauna factor exceeds measurement because every possible thing that could be alive is alive here simultaneously, including the dead things and the never-born things and the abstract concepts that have achieved sentience through pure loneliness. You are currently standing inside seventeen ecosystems, four wars that ended before stars existed, and the dream of a planet that evolved instead of exploding. They cluster around you like moths to a flame that hasn't decided whether it's matter or metaphor yet.

When you breathe—if you still believe in breathing—you draw in silence 0.4, the exact volume of quiet that exists in the moment between an atomic bomb's flash and its sound across empty desert. It tastes like the color of numbers and feels like being forgiven by something that lacks the concept of forgiveness.

In the center of this impossibility floats the only constant: a small wooden chair, handmade from wood that remembers being trees that grew in poems instead of forests. There are tool marks visible on its legs, each scored by an intention that changed species three times during the making. In the chair sits a figure that is you but isn't, wearing your face on the inside. On its lap rests a book bound in pages torn from tomorrow's newspapers, written in ink made from dehydrated time.

The chair is empty.

The chair has always been empty.

The chair is waiting for you to remember how to sit down without breaking the universe that has been using your absence as its centerpoint.

Please remain exactly where you aren't until the next gravity reset, which will occur 17 seconds before you realize you're already here.
