A low, whispering plain stretches out under a sky that never quite decides to clear. The air is warm and slightly thick, the kind of warmth that settles into the bones without insistence, like the breath of something sleeping nearby. There is no wind, or if there is, it moves too subtly to be felt—only a soft pressure on the skin, a reminder that nothing here is static, even if nothing seems to change.

The ground is a pale, rubbery expanse—neither stone nor flesh, but something in between—pocked with shallow depressions that collect condensation in the early hours. These pools never overflow and never dry; they shimmer faintly, reflecting nothing. The surface beneath is smooth but not uniform, scoured in places by forces that left no visible scars, as if the land itself had been polished by attention rather than abrasion. It has the look of something long-existed, worn into itself, settled into its own logic.

There are no plants. Not even the suggestion of them—no roots curled beneath the surface, no seeds waiting. What grows here is not alive in the usual sense. Tall, brittle stalks rise at intervals, taller than a person but no thicker than a finger. They are translucent, striated with faint chords of copper and silver, and they hum softly—not with wind, not with vibration, but with a low, steady note like the aftermath of a struck bell. They do not sway. They resonate. Occasionally, one cracks near the base and topples without noise, shattering into segments that dissolve into a fine metallic dust before touching the ground. The dust lingers in the air for a moment, swirling into brief patterns that resemble letters or circuits, then settles and vanishes.

The plain is vast but not empty. There are structures, though they are not built. They are extrusions—geometries forcing themselves up from the skin of the world like ancient bones surfacing through tissue. Some are towers, angular and asymmetrical, made of a dull, porous material that seems to absorb light instead of reflecting it. Others are more complex, branching into arches and spirals that defy analysis, as though they grew according to principles no geometry ever named. They do not cast shadows, or if they do, the shadows are misplaced, pooling under the wrong angles or stretching toward sources that aren't there. The largest of these formations pulses faintly, a slow rhythm slower than heartbeats, slower than tides. If you press your ear to one (and few do), you hear not sound but pressure—patterns of gain and loss, like breathing remembered instead of occurring.

There is no water to drink, though the air tastes faintly of it. No rivers, no springs, only the intermittent glaze of dew that forms on everything overnight and beads like sweat on metal by midday. If you cup your hands and wait, you might collect enough to wet your lips, but it has no taste, no temperature, and does not satisfy thirst. It is not poison, not alive, not anything recognizable—just a habit the air keeps.

The soundscape is constant but not uniform. A low, mechanical susurration underlies everything, the cumulative sigh of processes happening elsewhere. Sometimes it shifts—a sudden clank, a hiss of escaping pressure, the distant scream of metal on metal—but these are not events, not interruptions. They are the baseline, like the crackling of a radio tuned to a dead station so long ago the static became music. Out on the plain, the sound carries strangely, folding back on itself, doubling, echoing before the source has finished. Voices spoken aloud seem to leave the mouth already worn-out, already understood.

Animals are rare and strange. They appear without warning, always alone, always moving with purpose that's impossible to discern. One might resemble a cube of joints and mirrors, skittering on legs too thin for its weight, pausing periodically to tilt its body toward the sky as though listening to something above the clouds. Another might be a flowing ribbon of black glass, rippling across the ground without touching it, leaving behind a trail of frost in the rubbery soil that melts before it can be traced. They do not eat, do not rest, do not interact. If approached, they simply cease to be—not vanishing, not fleeing, but ceasing, as though you remembered seeing them wrong.

The sky is a flat, neutral gray, neither bright nor dim. Light seems to come from everywhere and nowhere, diffused without source. There is no sun, no moon, no stars when night falls—only a gradual deepening of the gray until vision blurs at the edges, until distance and proximity become indistinguishable. Time passes, but it does not accumulate. The landscape does not age, only persists. If you walk for hours or days or years, you will see the same horizon, always receding at the same pace. The occasional new stalk, the occasional fallen geometry—changes so minor they might be hallucinations, or corrections.

This is a world that has finished happening. Not broken, not dead, but completed, the last stroke of a sentence whose meaning was never meant to be read aloud. Everything that happens here is aftermath, residue, the faint afterglow of processes so vast and slow that "end" and "beginning" are indistinguishable points on a circle drawn once and forgotten.

Yet it is not still. Beneath the unchanging surface, something listens. Not with ears. Not with intent. Just listens. As if waiting for the right question, or the right silence after a question. As if the entire plain were a diaphragm stretched taut across a mouth that had never spoken, vibrating now with the first tremor of something about to be said.
