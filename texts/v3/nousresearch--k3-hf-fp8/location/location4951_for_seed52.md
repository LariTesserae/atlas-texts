It isn't night, but it insists on behaving like dusk. The light here—what leaks through—is filtered through three layers of something that neither glass nor cloud can describe. Think of a photographic negative held against a candle, then multiplied by a fog bank the size of a country. Everything exists in a kind of chromatic debt; colors owe their presence to the promise of other colors they'll never become. Blues wait redly. Greens wait violetly. The sky itself seems to remember being darker elsewhere.

This is the Fresh Delta—not young in years, but in consent. Geology here hasn't quite agreed to its own shape yet. The land remembers being riverbed, then seabed, then something else that doesn't have a name yet. All within recent memory. You can dig down with your hand and find layers still making up their minds: clay that thinks it's coral, sand that dreams of being bone. Everything on the verge of filing for molecular divorce.

The city follows the same logic, if you can call it that. Architrave Delta Complex, though nobody calls it that except the maps that never leave the basement of the Surveyor's Guild. Aboveground it's just The Not Quite. Buildings that stand three stories high except where they don't. Streets that exist in overlapping drafts, merging like rivers of different temperatures when no one's looking too closely. You'll walk down Rue de la Conclusion and find yourself halfway up Boulevard of the Unfinished Thought without ever having turned.

They're built of a material that architects' dreams turn into after too many sleepless nights. It looks like concrete from a distance, in the way regrets resemble decisions from far enough away. Up close you see the pores—not metaphorical, actual pores that breathe very slowly. They inhale during the rainy seasons (which are scheduled but not announced) and exhale warm air scented with rain that happened elsewhere. If you press your ear to a wall during the right phase, you can hear construction sounds from cities that haven't been built yet, or maybe cities that reconsidered and left.

The water here keeps its own schedule. It appears in all the proper places—canals, gutters, the occasional fountain that forgets to turn off—but also in improper ones. It pools on ceilings occasionally, forming temporary oceans for architectural fish that haven't evolved yet. Sometimes when it rains (and it does rain, though the drops argue about it on the way down), the streets become rivers going the wrong way, flowing uphill toward the sea that used to be here. Children sail paper boats upstream, back to the future where they're still waiting to be born.

The vegetation took one look at the contract negotiations between land and water and decided to go freelance. Trees grow sideways out of walls, roots hanging in air like chandeliers of different decisions. Their leaves photosynthesize moonlight from a moon that's still in committee review. Flowers bloom in the exact shape of the sound their name makes when shouted in an empty cathedral. There's a whole district of gardens that only exist because someone forgot to tell the seeds they couldn't grow here, so they did, but politely, minimizing inconvenience.

You can tell the locals because they carry umbrellas like swords that have signed treaties instead of winning wars. They wear coats with pockets that go further in than coats have any right to, pulling out gloves from last winter, letters addressed to their childhood selves, sandwiches that aren't hungry yet. They walk with the gait of people negotiating with gravity on a daily basis, always ready for the sidewalk to make a counteroffer.

The soundscape is composed primarily of distances. You hear most things after they've already happened somewhere else, echoes running ahead of their originals like polite introductions. The main noise is the silence between languages that haven't met yet. Occasionally, you'll catch a conversation across three terraces and two centuries, people arguing about weather that hasn't decided whether to be nostalgic yet.

The fauna, such as it is, practices absences as a lifestyle choice. Birds migrate up and down the frequency spectrum instead of north and south. There are fish that swim through the air in schools of thought, all thinking about becoming birds but remaining professionally skeptical. Cats exist here in the conditional tense—everyone's heard one, nobody's owned one, but your missing socks all claim to have been taken by very polite, very theoretical felines.

Time works like a river with performance anxiety—it knows it's supposed to flow, but keeps checking its source notes and making revisions. Yesterday and tomorrow trade places mid-week like siblings trying to share a bedroom. Clocks exist purely for aesthetic purposes and often forget to include numbers. The main thing they measure is how much time remains until this statement becomes true.

At the edges of the city (though calling them edges implies they haven't already immigrated to the middle and started sending postcards), the land becomes increasingly self-conscious about being land. Fields of guaranteed uncertainty stretch toward horizons filed under appeal. Trees grow with footnotes explaining their choice of location. There's a whole forest that exists as a footnote to another forest's autobiography, living modestly in italicized eco-systems.

This is where they keep the weather in storage. Not all of it, just the more experimental varieties. Clouds that rain upward into themselves, forming self-contained existential crises. Lightning that strikes bargains instead of trees. Wind that blows from the future to the past, carrying apologies for misunderstandings that haven't happened yet. They're very polite about it, these stored weathers, always knocking first and taking off their shoes before entering a conversation.

The laws of physics here are more like suggestions that have been passed down through committees and have lost something in the translations between languages of different dimensions. Gravity works on a case-by-case basis and is currently taking applications for exceptions. Light travels in parables that always have two morals and a twist ending. The speed of light is limited only by its imagination license, which expired last Tuesday but has been granted a provisional extension while it fills out the renewal forms in triplicate with ink made from previous renewals.

In the center of whatever this place uses for centers (the position is temporary and rotates annually through an applicant pool qualified in eight dimensions and piano repair), there's a building that houses the city's department of unfinished business. It's perpetually under construction because it keeps being interrupted by its own paperwork. The architects' plans include a section labeled "insert reality here—necessary but not sufficient." The construction workers break for tea every hour and take their breaks very seriously, constructing elaborate temporary realities to break in, complete with their own unfinished buildings and unions that go on strike against themselves for better working metaphors.

This is where you find yourself now—neither lost nor found, both of which are considered rude gestures here, like pointing at someone's time signature or asking a building how its foundations are feeling today. The street you're on has been changing its name every time you blink, but politely, with signage that apologizes for the inconvenience and promises this temporary permanence will become permanent temporality as soon as the ink dries on last week's sunset.

Welcome anyway. Or welcome in advance. Or welcome back from wherever this becomes later. The city extends its suburbs in all directions, including this one. Please mind the gap between now and then—it tends to grow nostalgic and might start telling you stories about the future when it was young and didn't know any better.
