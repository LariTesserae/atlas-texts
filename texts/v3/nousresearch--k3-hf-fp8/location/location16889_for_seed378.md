The place is called Lipsu Null, a name that does not translate cleanly across any known language family, though some claim it is onomatopoeic: the sound of a drop falling into something too hot to hold it. From orbit it appears as a single blistered continent set in a flexing sea of steam, the coastline zigzagging inward and outward like a crack rehealed a million times and never quite settling. The eye picks out radial scars: old crater edges eroded into terraces, each level now a terraced city-ring, still inhabited, still growing outward rather than upward because here water is abundant but gravity is a stern landlord and nothing tall leans long.

Elevation is high enough that breath burns raw at the back of the throat, but partial pressure of oxygen is regulated by an accident of ancient chemistry: the rocks here run rich in perchlorates and fluorides that react slowly, steadily, with groundwater leaching upward. The air itself is a low-grade oxidizer, pale yellow-white at noon and faintly luminous on the deepest nights. Visitors learn not to close their eyes too tightly; sparks sometimes dance between lashes.

Water is everywhere and nowhere: it boils from vents at midday, boils away before it touches pavement, condenses again at sunset as a thin warm rain that sizzles on skin and never soaks clothing through. Streets are gutters and gutters are streets, flowing with ankle-deep soup that alternates between steam and fog and liquid and back before you can finish describing it to yourself. The city’s children skip stones across it by night the way other worlds’ children throw them at lakes; the stones themselves are a pressed composite of dried algal mats and powdered glass harvested from the coastal dunes, because here nothing grows that could be called plant life and nothing rots that does not first befriend stone.

Vegetation score 0.2 is not quite zero: in the lee of every building suction-cupped to a damp wall you can see the pale, lazy fur that citizens informally call “mother’s lace.” It is an autotrophic fungus, or perhaps a lichen that forgot its algae, or perhaps an engineered symbiont whose patent expired before planetary records began. It photosynthesizes in the chemical sense, fixing carbon from the carbon dioxide and the steady rain of unstable peroxides. It also listens. Cut a patch away and the air around the wound rings for minutes afterward at a pitch too high for human ears but perfectly audible to the city’s dominant fauna. That is the city’s alarm system, or perhaps its gossip network, depending on whom you ask and how much you pay.

Fauna score 2.1 abundant: everywhere, everything that moves is either fabric or animal and often both at once. The smallest are the zipper motes, translucent polychaete worms as long as a thumbnail, each segment rimmed with microscopic crystals of barium titanate. When irritated they contract rhythmically, producing alternating bursts of ultrasound and static electricity strong enough to weld simple circuits. Children string chains of motes between balcony railings to make living radios that can only broadcast the sound of the current weather one second into the future. Larger cousins, the weaver-skeins, flow through the gutter-streams in ribbons ten meters long, spinning behind them nets of polymerized silicates harvested from the water itself. They are the city’s textile factories, the city’s builders of scaffolding, the city’s undertakers: when something dies here, if human authorities do not claim it within the statutory hour, a skein arrives, unwraps like a tongue, and begins its patient knitting. By sunrise what remains is a sculpture of hollow filaments ten times the original weight but light enough to be carried by the wind. These sculptures fill the rooftops, clicking and humming in every breeze, the city’s memorial garden carved by digestive acids and intentionless art.

Built score 1.7 urban, yet no buildings rise more than five storeys. Instead the city digs into its own past, each floor down a century cooler, a little richer in breathable air, a little poorer in daylight. The oldest catacombs are now warehouses, the next layer up are factories, above that living quarters, above that the market-streets, above that the terraces where water is allowed to run free for the sake of the mother’s lace and the children’s games. Construction itself is a nightly festival: teams of masons walk along ridges of scaffolding woven fresh each evening by the skein-fauna, carrying bricks that are actually ceramic foam filled with dormant eggs of a specialized mite. When placed in contact with the city’s humidity they hatch, eat, excrete limestone, and die within an hour, leaving behind a seam stronger than mortar yet perfectly soluble in next week’s storm. Thus every building grows slightly inward each year, while the city planners keep careful track of how much is allowed to dissolve away at the edges. A century of neglect does not ruin a house here; it merely returns it to a pleasant open courtyard.

Technology score 1.7 industrial, but the factories have no chimneys and the engines have no moving parts. Power comes from gradient: temperature difference between noon stone and midnight fog, chemical difference between gutter-bottom and rooftop air, gravitational difference between high city and the still-deeper mines where miners never go because they send instead their ancestors’ bones encased in dopant-glass. Down there, at the interface where sunlight never reaches but geothermal heat is still bearable for machinery, the city farms its memories. A neuron etched in sapphire fired once every thousandth of a second by a carefully calibrated drip of water laden with dissolved salts will continue to fire for geological ages, or so the engineers claim, and who is left to contradict them? On the surface the lights burn bright score 2.6, not because electricity is cheap but because darkness here is dangerous: the light disturbs fewer things than the dark would wake.

Sound score 0.1 silence or quiet sounds, yet the city never sleeps. What you hear if you stop trying to hear anything at all is the polyphony of small absorptions: droplet entering drop, crystal shifting under crystal, mote zipping shut its microscopic jaw. Every century or so a tourist arrives bearing a superconducting microphone and leaves weeks later with recordings that experts on other worlds classify as avant-garde music or evidence of orbital mind control lasers. Locals listen politely, nod, then forget.

Weirdness score 1.5 strange but explainable: the laws of physics hold, they just happen to be local ordinances rather than universal statutes. Conservation of mass applies except on Tuesdays. Entropy increases except within certain registered historical districts. The second law of thermodynamics is more of a guideline, enforced by a small polite office staffed entirely by retired physicists who went mad early enough to have acquired excellent pensions. Tourists are advised to carry a small sealed jar containing a certified vacuum; in case of sudden collapse of causality, open jar toward the past and proceed briskly into the future.

Dynamic score 1.5 active: weather cycles obey the schedule posted annually by the Current Editor of the Weather, an elected position that nobody campaigns for because the winner must spend the next four years living alone atop the central convective chimney, adjusting flaps and valves to ensure that the city’s dreams condense into fog instead of hail. The Editor is allowed one visitor per month and five books, chosen at random from the city library by an algorithm trained on the dreams of all previous Editors. No one has ever served a second term. In the streets below, predator-prey relationships are maintained by mutual contract: the zipper motes prey on excess humidity, the weaver-skeins prey on surplus complexity, the city’s bureaucracy preys on time itself, and so far every party considers the arrangement fair. Newcomers are advised to register their intentions at the nearest civic dream-catcher, available free of charge in three sizes and fifty-seven flavors of unprovability.

In the market-streets that spiral outward from the crater’s edge like a fingerprint on fire, you can buy anything that has ever been forgotten somewhere else. Prices are negotiated in memories of temperature, payable in installments measured in heartbeats per hour. The local currency is a living dye derived from the sweat of mathematicians; it fades from blue to red as it ages and smells faintly of correct answers on difficult questions. Theft is punished by enforced empathy: the thief must spend a year experiencing the daily life of the first thing they ever stole, from the thing’s point of view. Statistically speaking, this discourages petty crime rather effectively while producing a statistically significant surplus of poets and philosophers every seventh generation.

Leave the city by any of the twelve causeways that bracket the horizon likepins holding up a map that keeps trying to curl up at the corners, and eventually you reach the true desert beyond the crater’s rim: a plain of fused glass dunes that sing in chorus whenever the wind shifts direction, which it does every 4.7 minutes precisely. The dunes are not glass but fossilized time, solidified the moment the first dreamer in Lipsu Null woke and decided not to leave. Walk far enough and you may meet her still, sitting cross-legged atop the highest dune, petting a weaver-skein that has no end and no beginning. She will offer you tea brewed from the water that has not yet fallen as rain and cookies baked from the flour of yesterday’s tomorrow. Accept and you may stay, neither visitor nor citizen, until you learn the real reason why this place has no plant life: because every potential seed that ever came here on the wind or in a traveller’s pocket decided, after careful consideration, that it preferred to go back to being impossible.

Refuse, politely of course, and she will smile and wave goodbye with a hand that has too many fingers or perhaps exactly the right number but arranged in an order that currently disagrees with your expectations. Turn around then, and walk back along your own footprints, which should still be there unless they have discovered a better way to be somewhere else. Either way, the city will be waiting just as you left it, except a little older on the outside and a little newer within, like all the best places that never quite finish becoming themselves.
