You step out of the airlock onto what the charts call Urthward Tlacoloc and immediately make three mistakes. First, you inhale. Your suit alarms warn of mild hyperoxygenation—harmless for short exposure—but it's the flavor that stuns you: not air, but broth. Miso and rain and something fungal that tastes purple against your tongue. Second, you blink. The light is normal, yes, ISO daylight balanced, but it arrives from everywhere and nowhere, illuminating nothing while leaving no shadows—shadows have been agreed upon to be inefficient here. Third, you try to orient yourself by horizon, but horizons are considered rude in places older than perspective.

The ground resolves as you stop looking for ground. What you thought were puddles—shivering mirror-skates that flee from your boots—are actually the surface tension of Urthward Tlacoloc itself. The whole place is a wetness holding its breath. Your weight dimples it into concave moments that become temporary valleys, filling immediately with regret from the surrounding wetness. The physics documentation described this as "hydrostatic empathy." The locals (there are always locals, even when there isn't supposed to be an environment) just call it manners.

Trees? No. Not trees. Trees are what plants aspire to when they're young and still believe in height as achievement. Here, photosynthesis has long since resolved its daddy issues with thermodynamics and moved back home to become intergenerational wealth. You're standing in a single grove-scale photosynthetic accumulator that reaches down twelve kilometers and sideways into four adjacent jurisdictions. Its "leaves" are vaulted chambers where light slows down to gossip. Its "bark" is city infrastructure—veins of superconducting phloem carrying computational turgor pressure, nodes of lignin-decision trees that groan softly when making municipal policy decisions about Tuesday's weather.

Speaking of which, the weather is currently being voted on overhead by a parliament of clouds. Real clouds, proper cumuliform quorum-makers, their bellies pregnant with ballots. They've been debating this particular storm cycle for six thousand years, which explains why the rain, when it finally arrives, arrives apologizing. Each drop carries a purseful of excuses and a memory of every apology it ever delivered successfully. They splatter against your suit with the sound of grandmothers forgiving genocide by degrees.

Your map says you're in the district called Thirteen Sunsets Before Breakfast. The name is literal but misleading—the suns here are small, polite things, no larger than beachballs, orbiting the planet like overeager pets. There are exactly thirteen, none of them ours, all of them borrowed or inherited. They rise and set according to a schedule painted in the margins of old love letters, and currently they're all experiencing a minor scheduling disagreement involving Renaissance Italy and the invention of the clarinet. This is why, when you look directly east, you see late afternoon Venetian light painting the clouds in ochres and bloods while simultaneously, in your peripheral vision, 3 AM Mongolian steppe stars wheeling furiously overhead like someone shook the snowglobe of the sky in a fit of pique.

The architecture begins where your attention ends. When you stop trying to distinguish "building" from "not-building," the city of Urthward Tlacoloc politely fills in your blindspots with infrastructure. There's a bridge currently forming out of your own assumption that you'd need to cross something wet eventually—its arches are ribcages of calcified speculation, its roadbed paved with second thoughts you've not yet had. The buildings are grown rather than built, but "grown" implies patience and these structures have the architectural equivalent of hyperphagia. They budded off from parent complexes during particularly nutritious economic theories and haven't stopped metastasizing into adjacent property concepts since. One particularly ambitious townhouse complex has eaten four streets and the concept of gentrification and is now politely asking a nearby park whether it minds being recontextualized as a living room annex.

The inhabitants—fauna 1.9, abundant indeed—are what happens when evolution discovers social media and decides to become its own fanfic. They exist in a superposition of species, collapsing into whatever form their current conversational partner finds least threatening while simultaneously posting their true selves on encrypted reality layers you need the right memetic antibodies to perceive. The one currently trying to sell you a map is, in visible wavelengths, a charmingly asymmetrical cluster of otters wearing a lab coat as a group activity. In ultraviolet it's a single, vast, symbiotic poem about the emotional lives of abandoned shopping carts. In the gamma spectrum it's having a panic attack about the heat death of information.

You buy the map because it's polite and because refusing commerce here is considered a form of littering. The map unfolds into seventeen dimensions, twelve of which protest being unfolded in public. One dimension is currently dating the concept of "north" but it's complicated. Another dimension is just directions to your mother's house, spelled correctly this time. The dimension you think you need is currently experiencing service delays due to unscheduled ontological maintenance on the nature of need itself.

The sound—1.5 loud, which here is measured not in decibels but in conversational brownian motion—is the noise of a million separate histories trying to resolve their differences in the same bar at closing time. Beneath that, the deeper frequencies: the geological gossip of tectonic plates swapping recipes for continent formation. The high, sweet tones: individual photons singing their autobiographies as they die against retinas or sensor arrays or raindrops too polite to interrupt. And threading through everything, the city-woven baseline of Urthward Tlacoloc itself—a city-wide shared earworm composed by all the music ever left unfinished in all the showers ever taken.

Movement here is paradoxically simple once you stop trying to control your destination. The ground—remember the ground?—rearranges itself into the most probable path between where you are and where you're embarrassed to admit you were going, then apologizes profusely for any improbable personal growth you experience en route. You're currently walking downhill toward an elevated area because this district is having a romantic moment with the concept of elevation and doesn't want to rush things.

The weirdness index reads 1.9, which is approximately the weirdness of realizing you've been pronounced dead in a language you didn't know you speak fluently. Everything here is that level of familiar-yet-what-the-shit—is that tree photosynthesizing music? Why does that cloud look exactly like your third-grade teacher's disappointment? Did the street just change its name to thank you for noticing it?

Yes.

You're beginning to understand.

Urthward Tlacoloc isn't weird because it breaks rules. It's weird because it follows all rules simultaneously—including the rules it hasn't invented yet—and then meticulously apologizes for any poetic resonance this might accidentally produce. It is the city equivalent of a cat bringing you gifts you didn't know you were missing until you learned they were extinct. It is a warm wet place at the end of the world where all the things that ever got lost between cultures go to live happily ever after in complicated polyamorous arrangements.

It is, according to your now-hilariously-obsolete cultural briefing, uninhabited.

You laugh. The city laughs with you. Even the rain laughs, forgetting for a moment what it was apologizing about.

Welcome to Urthward Tlacoloc. Please try not to exist too much in any one place—static objects are considered prime real estate, and the city has been looking to expand into a family-friendly misunderstanding about the nature of edges.

Your map folds itself into a small anxious bird and flies away, leaving you alone with everything you've ever misplaced and several things you'll only realize you're missing after they've moved on and find better conversation elsewhere. 

It's going to be a long, beautiful forever getting lost here.

You'll love it.
