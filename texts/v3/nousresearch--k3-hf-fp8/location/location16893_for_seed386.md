At first glance it appears to be a single enormous tree risen higher than any mountain, but it is not one trunk—it is millions braided together into a living tower that has forgotten how to stop growing. Each bough is broader than the widest river and sweeps upward rather than out, forming concentric terraces where roots and branches lose distinction. The entire mass hovers, suspended by no visible means, a quarter mile above steaming marshes that stretch beyond sight in every direction. Gravity here behaves as an afterthought; rain falls upward, gathers in silver clouds beneath the canopy, then cascades again in continuous cycles.

The bark is black and polished like obsidian, but warm to the touch and partially translucent. Within its depths move slow rivers of liquid green light—chlorophyll pumped through vessels vast enough to sail—that pulse to an achingly slow heartbeat measured in centuries. At irregular intervals these luminous arteries surface as branching fissures that emit steady daylight, enough for everything below to photosynthesize but not enough to cast shadows. Time itself seems thickened by the heat; sound travels languidly, spreading like ink in water.

Water surfaces everywhere without source. It beads from leaves broader than fields, collects in trunk-grooves deep as fjords, then overflows in sheets that never quite reach the ground but instead hang suspended as living curtains. Within these vertical lakes swim translucent creatures resembling both fish and severed wings that filter nutrients through feathery gills while singing barely audible harmonies. The song is mostly felt in bones rather than heard—a constant gentle pressure like being underwater far below the surface.

Vegetation here has abandoned the ground entirely. Every available surface crawls with growth in endless competition for light and space. Mosses the color of heated copper form shifting carpets over bark ridges, periodically lifting entire sections of themselves to expose luminous fungal gardens beneath. Vines thick as highways loop between levels without touching, their interiors hollowed into tube-pipes that transport pressurized sap that bursts forth as geysers whenever they rupture. These ruptures happen constantly—plants growing too fast for their own structure in the perpetual heat—and each explosion seeds new colonies in the brief rain of nutrients.

The temperature never drops below blood-warm even in the perpetual shade between bark layers. Humidity saturates everything until air becomes palpable, a thick medium that must be pushed through like warm syrup. Breathing here requires practice; newcomers gasp until their lungs adapt to extracting oxygen from mist rather than air. Clothing rots or sprouts overnight, tools corrode or blossom, and only thoughts remain unchanged if protected beneath layers of wax-sealed memory-bark notched with patterns that resist blooming.

Movement is verticality here. The distinction between climbing and walking dissolved ages ago. Paths are living escalators of interconnected root-hands that grip and lift those who know the correct pressure patterns to request passage, or entangle and digest those who don't. Communities form in hollowed chambers where multiple trunks intertwine, their floors breathing gently with shared circulatory systems. Each such space houses unique ecologies adapted to precise altitudes and light frequencies—there are cities seven miles up where everything floats freely in zero-gravity zones created by the tree's impossible mass, and other cities buried in root-pockets near absolute darkness where sightless harvesters cultivate bioluminescent crops fed by drippings from above.

Life teems in densities that defy counting but rarely collide. Every cubic foot contains hundreds of species in layered symbiosis—predators that exist only as clouds of microscopic needles drifting on convection currents, prey that grow protective shells from their own crystallized waste overnight, scavengers that are actually mobile ecosystems containing hundreds of smaller scavengers inside their transparent bodies. Nothing dies alone here; death is always communal, a flowering event that feeds dozens of others before the original form dissolves completely back into circulation.

The only metal present is stolen from ancient asteroids captured slowly over millennia by the tree's growing gravity. It exists as scattered jewelry embedded deep within heartwood chambers—time capsules of pure ore wrapped in protective sap that maintains them against corrosion. Occasionally these pieces are exposed by lightning strikes or internal growth stress, and then briefly before being engulfed again they act as strange attractors for local biology. Organisms near such sites develop abnormal properties: leaves that fold into perfect geometric shapes, insects that build working gear mechanisms into their nests, birds whose songs fracture light into visible spectra.

Soundscape is mostly the subsonic groan of billions of tons of wood slowly moving against itself, punctuated by sudden explosive pops as water pockets flash to steam and by the constant rain-rain-rain that isn't really falling anywhere. Occasionally one hears what might be voices carried through the root network—slow deep conversations between sections of the tree separated by centuries of growth, debating topics beyond human temporal resolution. These communications sometimes emerge as temporary patterns in bark texture or sequences of synchronized flowering events, messages written in living language across continents of wood.

The only regular rhythm comes from far below where the root system finally penetrates down through miles of atmosphere and soil to touch the planet's actual surface once every several thousand years. On these rare occasions the entire structure trembles slightly, its billions of separate minds momentarily synchronized by deep-earth information, and for a brief time everything that grows here remembers where it came from before beginning again to forget upward.
