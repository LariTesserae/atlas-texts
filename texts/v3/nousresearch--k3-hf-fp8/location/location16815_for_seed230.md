The place is a hanging town, ancient and speechless, built into the underside of a fluted limestone plate that once curved above a river canyon. Centuries of slow, upward water-sculpting reversed the order of things: the river plunged into darkness below, the sky receded above a roof of rock, and the plate drifted free, buoyed on unknown forces. Now it floats motionless in a cavernous hollow whose walls, two bow-shots apart, are windowless basalt mirrors reflecting nothing but the soft, steady glow that fills the void.

The plateau’s underside—what the inhabitants call the sky—starts smooth as pottery, then breaks into rounded bosses and dripstone arrows pointing downward into void. From these anchorages stone-walled dwellings hang like inverted barns, each whole village built of the leavings of gravity: lintels that once were lintels but now serve as beams, beams turned posts, doorways pivoted sideways to become hearths. Streets are open ladders of ironwood bolted between houses; bridges are single planks lashed with braided gut-sling; stairways dissolve into knotted ropes downward. Everything is joined by peg and rawhide peg, for metal is rarely mined and never melted here. What little copper the people possess came up with the cataclysm’s refugees in their pockets and pouches, and it lies hidden in shrine-chests beneath the floors.

Dampness rules—not from rain, for no clouds exist so deep in stone, but from the exhalation of the rock itself and the perpetual mist that rises from the unseen river in the abyss. Lime-rich water beads on every surface like sweat and drips in mock-rainfall, collecting in turquoise pans set under each roof-peak (which are really troughs) and draining through hollowed quills to shared cisterns carved beneath every plaza. Clothing must be tanned twice and smoked every moonrise to keep it supple; wood—mostly drift-tamarack fallen from the upperworld before the Sundering—steams in damp piles beneath oilcloths weighted with stones of remembrance. Yet nothing rots quickly. The cool air, uniform as the light, has no seasons to rot in.

That light comes equally from everywhere and has no source the oldest songs remember. It is the color of candle-wick under thin porridge, bright enough to distinguish thread from thread but never to cast true shadow. Mornings and nights are matters of habit: shutters open, shutters close; fires lit for company, not warmth; prayers spoken toward the Compass-Bosses—North Boss, South Boss, East Boss, West Boss—that are merely the largest of the upward bulges in the ceiling-cliff. Between these marker dints lie smaller shrines: the Nestling Bosses where babies’ cords are buried, the Speaking Boss where disputes are settled by each party shouting truth and untruth into the stone until one voice cracks with honesty.

Vegetation clings where it can: pale lichens spread like spilled paint across the vertical walls; methva reeds sprout in nooks where damp gathers and are gathered in turn for cooking baskets and the plating of ropes; in a few blessed scree-pockets where house-heat leaks through vents, careful gardeners coax out bitterleaves and rusty-thyme whose leaves are chewed to ward off the dreaming-sickness of too much stillness. The richest plots are the hanging gardens: barrels of cliff-soil suspended beneath balconied houses, fed by drip-strings siphoning from the roof-troughs above. From these swaying parcels the townsfolk cut scant handfuls of leaves, tiny bulbs, pods no bigger than fingernails—enough to remind the tongue, never to fill the belly.

The belly is filled elsewhere. Once a year—or once in what memory calls a year—slow mighty currents carry an upward flood of tiny silver fish called blinkers out of the abyss. Nets of nettle-thread are cast from the lower balconies at the appointed hour-spans; lights of polished mica are shaken underwater to dazzle them into rising. The catch lasts weeks, smoked on racks that hang beneath the smokehouses’ floors and drip grease into waiting pans. The rest of the diet is what falls or what climbs: lichens boiled into paste, mosses pressed into cakes, cave-crabs clawed from crevices high above the inhabited strip, and the eggs of cliff-swifts who somehow still remember the vanished wind and lay coin-shaped eggs in sandstone cups glued upside-down under the unreachable overhangs. A child climbs for these on ropes tied waist-to-waist with the gonder, the blind old man whose sole remaining sense is balance and whose sole duty is to shout “Hold!” if ever the rope slackens in the wrong way.

Animals are few and regarded as ghosts. The chill eye of a whitewhisk mink staring from a hole is cause for a Feast of Ancestors’ Remembrance. Once in seven or thirty-seven lives, a bird appears—a speck against the quartz veins of the north wall—circles, sings a single terrified note, vanishes. Its feather, if found, is sewn into the cap of the Finder of Wonder and worn until it crumbles.

The people themselves are small, pale as cave-mushroom, slow-moving as rainwater across marble. They speak softly because all sound travels far and returns unkindly from the gripping stone. Their words are almost entirely nouns leaning against each other in subtle tilt to show motion or desire: “rope-down-pathward-quick” means “bring the ladder quickly because the child is wandering toward the edge again.” Their names are likewise phrases woven from the first notable things seen from the birthing-window: Grief-of-the-Dripping-Stone, Seed-in-Dark-Corner, Song-Caught-Between-Ridges. No one remembers the old single-name tongue of the upperworld, if ever such existed.

Rules of existence are few but absolute:

1. Do not forget to set the drip-pots nightly under the sleeping-cots lest your dreams overflow and become waking visions that walk the bridges and confuse the goats.

2. The lower balcony nets are communal. Whoever lifts fish must share eyes and tail with the carpenter who braids the reeds, the dye-woman who soaks the dyes, and the silence-keeper who records each catch in knots upon the Great Cord.

3. Never climb alone. If you must, leave your name-puzzle lying broken upon the floor so the house knows whom to mourn.

4. The Bosses are listening. Speak truth to stone, for stone remembers and in time repeats all things in voices of falling water.

5. The town never moves, nor rock beneath, nor river below. Time is a circle drawn once with a charred reed and then erased. What was, will be again exactly. Therefore be kind even to your enemies for the next time they may wear your face.

This last rule is the hardest to believe in youth and the easiest in age. Here nothing changes except the slow growth of stalactite from roof to eventual grasp of floor—a reunion that in all remembered spans has advanced less than the width of a child’s hand. Seasons are recounted not by weather but by the twelve-thousand-and-one drip-rhythms of the Great Calendar-Stone in the silence-house at the town’s heart. When all rhythms coincide in perfect unison—which happened once to the grandmother of the eldest grandmother’s grandmother—then and only then does the silence-keeper add a single new knot to the end of the rope that began in the hand of the First Builder who first climbed up from the Sundering. The rope now loops twelve times around the central pillar and trails away into darkness, endlessly growing, endlessly the same.

Visitors from elsewhere—if ever such come—are greeted with offerings of water caught in cupped hands and the softest questions repeated thrice: “What falls in your place? What climbs? What never changes?” Their answers, whispered into the Bosses’ listening cracks, dissolve overnight into new dreams for the whole town to share, dreams utterly forgotten upon waking except for one word left hanging in each mouth like the taste of stone itself.

That taste is home. It is the flavor of being always half-drowned and half-drowning in the same cool breath, beneath the same unmoving light, in the same circle of quiet that has never broken and never will.
