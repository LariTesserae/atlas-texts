The place is called the Lantern Barrens, though the name comes from old maps drawn before the machines came. It sits high above the treeline on the eastern limb of an unnamed range, a plateau of black basalt cloven by deep fissures that collect mist from below. The elevation numbs fingertips and slows thought; visitors often dream they are underwater here, weightless but cold.

Despite the name, nothing burns on the Lantern Barrens anymore. Instead they glow.

You'll see it first at dusk: pale blue light seeping from every crack and crevice, brightening as true night falls until the entire plateau resembles a fallen piece of moon blanching the clouds beneath it. Dig anywhere into the porous rock and you uncover veins of living phosphor - not mineral but organism, bacterial colonies that metabolize trace uranium in the basalt itself. Their waste product is light. Bright light. Enough to photograph by at midnight, enough to bleach shadows from the writhing shapes that cover every surface.

When the Mining Syndicate discovered this accidental illumination forty years ago, they came with bore drills and conveyor belts, excavators that walked on six legs like drunk spiders. Now three generations of abandoned machinery rusts across the plateau in perfect concentric rings, each layer representing a larger radius than they could economically exploit before the colonies died back too deep to reach. The deepest holes have become lakes now, filled with condensation and stormwater, their surfaces mirror-bright with floating colonies of light-drinkers that turn bright orange nightly when the bacteria below them flare brightest.

These machine-lakes fascinate biologists because they shouldn't exist at this elevation or temperature. Each excavator's rusting carcass has become an island ecosystem, its hollowed belly sprouting gardens of fifteen-foot ferns and violet mosses that only grow within six meters of artificial metal. The mosses are carnivorous; they lure rare moths up from the valleys below with bioluminescent patterns that mimic the bacteria's mating displays, then slowly dissolve them into nutrients the ferns can't extract from rock alone.

The plateau breathes slowly through these lakes. In summer (though summer here means only four weeks when rain falls as liquid rather than needles of ice) the bioluminescent bacteria bloom exponentially, driving their phosphor output high enough to give sunburn to unprotected skin. The mosses respond by flowering in synchronized waves, releasing clouds of spores so dense they form visible rippling curtains in the air. The ferns unfurl curled fronds that drip nectar sweeter than honeydew, attracting every moth and butterfly within fifty kilometers altitude - though precious few make the journey alive.

Then autumn comes in a single day, heralded by the sudden silence of all insect life. Temperature drops ten degrees by noon. The bacteria retreat deep into their rock, dimming until only the faintest ghost-lights flicker in the deepest cracks. The mosses collapse into themselves like dying stars, their brilliant violet fading to ash gray overnight. Only the ferns remain upright, slowly transmuting their summer bounty into fist-sized seed pods armored with iron-hard scales.

These pods are the reason scientists still come despite the Syndicate's abandonment. When driven mad by cold or hunger, they sometimes drop their seeds not into soil - there is none here deeper than a finger-width of lichen - but directly onto exposed metal. Those rare seeds that strike iron or steel rather than stone germinate overnight into something new. The first sign is always the shedding: all the fern's leaves fall simultaneously, each leaflet curling into a perfect gear or spring or piston rod before fossilizing into actual metal. Within three days, the entire plant has reassembled itself into a miniature clockwork machine that crawls mineshaft walls on root-legs of braided cable, searching for more metal to eat.

The Syndicate's final report - classified until leaked last year - named them Fernacities. They grow by adding new components taken from any available source: a bolt here, a circuit board there, occasionally entire abandoned generators if they can coordinate enough individual ferns into working together. The largest known specimen occupies the hollowed-out shell of the original Surveyor Prime drilling rig, now transformed over twenty years into a twelve-story tower of interlocking brass and copper bones wrapped in living fern fronds that change color to match the bacteria's nightly flares.

The villagers who live in the biomechanical huts at the plateau's only trailhead (built from patched-together excavator cabins and insulated with fern-fiber) claim the Fernacities are building something within Surveyor Prime's corpse. They whisper of hearing music on nights when the auroras dance low enough to touch the peaks; complex harmonic progressions played on windpipes of hollow bone - but not any animal's bone - that echo down abandoned ventilation shafts to emerge from the rig's cracked bellmouth as songs that make dogs howl and humans weep without knowing why.

No scientist who has descended into Surveyor Prime's transformed depths has returned to confirm or deny these stories. Their final radio transmissions always begin the same way: reporting normal machinery sounds, pumps and gears and the click-click-click of counting mechanisms. Then, always after exactly 42 minutes regardless of daylight or weather, their voices change. They start speaking in perfect unison, describing something they can see but cannot name.

The last words, transmitted simultaneously by three different expeditions over fifteen years, were identical down to the inflection:

"It's turning the sky inside out through the holes where the stars used to be. The moons are leaking light through their cracks like broken lanterns. We can see the sea we left behind us, but it is made of metal now, and the waves are singing our names..."

Then silence, except for the faint echo of distant machinery continuing to count in base perfect silence. Dawn finds only their footprints leading down into the machine's open mouth, already filling with drifting fern-seeds that sparkle violet against the blue-glowing rock like rain falling upward into night.

The villagers have learned not to follow. They harvest their summer nectar with metal tongs and speak only in daylight now. When children ask why they never look south toward Surveyor Prime's light, they tell the old story about the world before this one, swallowed by its own hunger for light.

But sometimes - just sometimes - when the auroras flare brightest and the Fernacities' nightly song swells to thunder you can feel in your bones rather than hear - even the oldest grandmother will pause in her work and tilt her head toward the silent south, listening for something that might be an answer to a question nobody remembers asking.
