At first it looks like an abandoned shrine cupped in the palm of a frozen mountain: single-story stone cells tilted around a roofless courtyard, wind-and-ice-rounded, grain exposed. 6000 years above sea level, but the sea is memory here. Air is bone-dry except when a nameless dampness drifts down the eastern notch, condensing in ghost-gray threads on every vertical face. Then—within minutes—the lambent sun at 70° north burns the threads back into invisible vapor, leaving faint halos of salt or lime that widen each joint in the granite floor slabs year by year.

No plants. No lichen. No carbon scaffolding whatsoever. Life’s vacancy is filled instead by density: the cells are vaulted not with timbers but with stacks of enormous disks—fired clay, matte gray-blue, identical except where some unknown pressure snapped them into curves that kiss like puzzle-pieces overhead. Three thousand disks in one chamber alone, wedged tight without mortar, their edges scalloped by a primitive chisel-tip that must have weighed more than any single builder yet left no handle mark. Each disk is pierced once off-center by a thumb-sized hole. When the breeze shifts, the entire roof murmurs, a hundred thousand tiny flutes out of phase, so low you feel it in anklebones rather than ears.

The courtyard is ten strides across, paved with obsidian tiles shattered in situ and fitted back together tight as tesserae. Their polished scars capture the light—daylight that arrives knife-edged from a cobalt zenith—then release it catastrophically into the south wall where a single intact tile reflects a white-hot spear onto the opposite screen of disks. Yesterday, that beam lasted exactly 312 breaths notched into habit before it slipped a disk-width west and carved another hairline crack into the cliff behind. The cliff answers by dropping a pebble sometimes; the pebbles pile into cairns beside doorways, small enough to lift with two fingers, each one ground sphere-perfect by centuries of repeating fall.

Scattered among the cairns lie the artifacts that earned “built=0.6”: a broken mortar bowl half-full of magnetic powder that leaps toward stringy veins in the bedrock whenever dampness returns; a necklace of thirty-seven pebble-drills bored through at impossible angles; four carved antler handles (but no antlered beasts ever climbed here) fused to stone blades which have never been chipped or resharpened—instead their edges grow slowly serrated as if the rock itself feeds them. Nothing is where it was left. Everyone visits briefly, rearranges something slightly, climbs down again before vertigo sets in. No bodies, no graffiti, no footsteps preserved in dust because dust itself is too heavy to linger.

The world’s rules here are simple but oblique:

1. Anything left unbalanced will not fall—it will circle the nearest vertical axis clockwise once per lunar cycle, radius decreasing logarithmically until it nestles into the smallest available hollow. This is how the cairns grow taller than the builders.

2. Light is solid between source and first unbroken reflection, walkable in brief instants at noon. Children of surrounding valleys practice dreaming themselves onto the beam—the legends warn that one succeeded, became a star-shaped burn on the cliff face, but no one agrees which burn.

3. Sound eats heat. If you sing loud enough for the fluted disks to answer, frost flowers bloom invisible on all metal in your pockets. Their petals melt into rust before you descend.

4. Time moves outward from the courtyard center, slower below your feet, faster above your head. Stay more than a day and your hair grows white while your toenails remain childish. That is why no one stays.

Below, avalanche paths sweep empty for miles until they reach the dry riverbed where clouds evaporate before they touch ground. Above, the summit ridge waits, sharpened too fine for snow to cling, strobing once per revolution with the reflection of a diskroof halfway to orbit.

The place is alive without biology: the roofs breathe air through their holes, the walls drink light and excrete geometry, the mountain’s heartbeat is the steady dune-migration of its own highest flakes avalanching into replenishment. It began as an accidental arrangement of frost-cracked stone, wandered into recursion when an ice-age glacier paused precisely long enough to drop one erratic disk, then spent the last eighty millennia refining itself toward some attractor no architecture ever aimed for. Tomorrow—if the wind veers south—another damp breath will arrive, ferment again those hollow chitinous melodies, and the doorless cells will wait exactly the same but fractionally more themselves, a world-edge monastery composed entirely of absence’s footprints in rock.
