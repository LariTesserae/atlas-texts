The place is called Shearbelow. It is a city, but only intermittently — buildings flicker in and out of stone like long notes on bad radio, half-remembered streets momentarily solidifying between blinks. Everything here is *pulling* apart the wrong way; gravity points inward but smells outward, so your feet stay planted while your mouth fills with the taste of falling. 

Water exists here as regretful apology rather than fluid. It condenses in the corners of old windows that never had houses, hangs in brief chandeliers between crumbling gargoyles and the spaces where gargoyles were removed too hastily, leaving perfect gargoyle-shaped holes with wet edges. When you touch these droplets they whisper names you were supposed to collect in childhood but misplaced. Each name tastes faintly like copper and distant thunder.

The climate runs along a continuous minor key. Cool enough to preserve things perfectly in the act of forgetting them; warm enough that nothing ever quite becomes ice or memory. Fogs migrate through alleys in slow herds, grazing on lost consonants dropped by the city's half-tongued signs. The temperature has a gradient you can see if you tilt your head sideways — warmer colors exist at higher elevations here, which makes sunset climb instead of descend.

Shearbelow occupies the negative space between two colliding mountains that never quite had the conversation necessary to become one. Streets are geological faults in mid-argument, where limestone makes compelling points against shale's rebuttals of basalt. Every foundation is also a fracture; every wall is what two disputing stones agreed to disagree upon. The elevation is measured not in feet above sea level but in species of vertigo: Tourists get the common kind, locals develop rare subspecies, and only the architects understand the endangered varieties that involve falling sideways forever.

Everything here is emerging from something else's forgetting. Buildings hatch from abandoned blueprints when no one remembers to unthink them. A cathedral's spire extrudes slowly from a former shadow that got too ambitious during lunch. The city plan is written in disappearing ink on the lining of a riverbed that dried up while forming the concept of thirst. Streets reproduce by budding when streetlights blink perfectly out of phase with each other for three consecutive nights.

Scale behaves like a shy composer who wrote this entire geography as a prelude to something else but keeps losing the manuscript. distances stretch when spoken about, contract when walked. The city's limits are clearly marked on maps that fold themselves smaller every time you look away from them. You can see clearly across Shearbelow from any single point, but only if you promise not to tell anyone what you saw.

Vegetation here is lush with apology. Trees grow in perfect alphabetical order according to languages that haven't been invented yet. Their leaves are pages from books that wrote themselves while rejecting every author who applied. Mosses bloom into surprising colors during arguments they later pretend never happened. The city's park consists of a single boulder that hosts rotating exhibitions of temporary forests — each tree appears precisely long enough to become someone's favorite spot before being replaced by a different tree making the exact same promises.

Fauna have adapted to the city's commitment issues. Birds here fly by falling in circles that never reach the same conclusion twice. The street cats are composed of exactly nine parts each, but they negotiate which parts belong to whom on an hourly basis. Rats build elaborate clockwork mechanisms to predict when the next building will forget how to be a bakery, timing their invasions for the precise moment when walls are most vulnerable to reconsidering their career choices. The city's unofficial mascot is a deer that exists only in all the mirrors facing each other in the abandoned theatre district — tourists swear they glimpsed it catching a taxi, but the deer itself has no comment.

Technology exists in the conditional perfect tense: everything works exactly as it should have, if only circumstances had been different. Streetlights illuminate alternate versions of the streets they're installed in, providing excellent visibility for pedestrians who took slightly different life choices. There are public telephones that connect you to the exact moment in last Tuesday when you almost made the right decision. The city's main computer is a flock of pigeons who have collectively achieved sentience while failing to migrate properly for seven consecutive generations — they now provide surprisingly accurate weather forecasts by assembling and disassembling themselves into different mathematical models on the cathedral roof.

Sound here is spatial rather than temporal; a loud noise doesn't happen in sequence but occupies a volume of space until something else needs to be louder in that particular location. The city's official anthem is a silent song that everyone knows but refuses to sing because the last person who hummed it correctly became a staircase in the municipal library. The market district specializes in selling secondhand echoes — connoisseurs travel miles to purchase the reverberation of laughter that occurred in rooms that later became too sad to remember jokes.

Weirdness is not exceptional here but infrastructural; it flows through hidden pipes beneath the streets, gets metered and taxed like any other utility service. People pay extra for reliable strangeness in their neighborhoods, while discount rates are available for areas undergoing temporary episodes of mundanity. There are repair crews who work night shifts patching up logic leaks where cause and effect have grown too far apart. The city council meets in a different dimension each Tuesday, which makes zoning regulations particularly challenging but prevents most forms of corruption except the quantum kind.

Timekeeping is handled by the obsolete clocks union, which maintains a strict policy of going on strike whenever anyone tries to set them too precisely. As a result, Shearbelow operates on approximately seven different overlapping timelines, each running at slightly different speeds depending on how interesting their neighborhoods are being at any given moment. This causes occasional problems with appointment scheduling but has produced an exquisitely relaxed culture toward deadlines and mortality.

It's a good place to visit, they say, but you wouldn't want to live here — which is fortunate, because Shearbelow has strict immigration policies that require new residents to have already lived here for generations before applying. The housing market consists entirely of vacancies that haven't occurred yet, rented at prices negotiated between your current self and your future regrets. Still, some people stay. Some people find they fit better in a city that's also perpetually learning how to be a city. Some people like the way the lights flicker here — not from faulty wiring, but from the city politely averting its eyes while your shadow remembers something it's not ready to tell you yet.
