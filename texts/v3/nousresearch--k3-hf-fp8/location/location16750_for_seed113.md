The water isn't where it should be.

The city of Nisak lies eleven meters below the original seabed, encased in a sphere of stabilized air that hums dissonantly against your teeth. Outside the pressure would crush granite to flour; inside, warm saltwater rivers run inverted in mid-air, arcing between buildings like liquid bridges that defy every treaty Bernoulli ever signed.

Descended through the last intact airlock, you stand on what was once the floor of the Philippine Abyss. The soil crunches beneath your boots—not rock, not coral, but the calcified dreams of architects who learned to negotiate with physics rather than obey it. Each step sends ripples through the groundcover: some kind of engineered lichen that remembers being coral, that remembers being circuit board. Tiny bioluminescent glyphs flare at your passing, spelling out density calculations in obsolete units.

Above, or perhaps below—the geometry here hasn't decided—the replacement sun runs its twelve-hour cycle through simulated cloud chambers. It's sunrise now, if that word retains meaning in a place where gravity exhales rather than pulls. Pale amber light filters through the water-rivers, refracted into impossible spectra where chlorophyll never evolved to follow. The illumination carries weight. You can feel it pooling in your lungs with each breath of the compression-adjusted air.

The buildings arrange themselves according to principles of negative space that would give Escher migraines. They grow upward and inward simultaneously, their surfaces rippling between basalt and mother-of-pearl at the speed of architectural thought. No joints, no seams—just continuous transitions where material properties phase-shift to meet structural demands that shift faster than observation. Between them flow the city’s true arteries: not roads but probability gradients, alleys that exist only when your destination requires them, squares that condense from empty space when enough people simultaneously imagine gathering.

There should be noise. An urban space this dense should deafen with life—the susurrus of millions negotiating coexistence at close quarters. Instead you hear only water talking to itself in hundreds of languages it shouldn't speak. The rivers sing equations that describe their own bending. Falls of saltwater cascade up cathedral faces, exploding into fractal spray that recomposes itself into fish-shapes of pure mathematics before dissolving again. At intersections, opposing streams pass through each other without mixing, trading qualities like secrets in the dark.

Between the empty doorways—empty because doors themselves became obsolete when walls learned to phase-shift on demand—tendrils of something that wants to be vine but hasn't committed to the career path explore the edges of where structure meets possibility. Green, yes, but green like copper verdigris dreams of forests. They grow by feeding on architectural uncertainty, wrapping twined hypotheses around columns that might be load-bearing or might be purely decorative or might be both in superposition until someone observes them critically.

The city breathes around you, exhales warmth that tastes faintly of metabolized plastic and ancient whale song. Every surface here is engaged in slow conversation with every other, negotiating revisions to local reality too subtle for human senses to parse but impossible to ignore at the level of blood chemistry. Your heartbeat syncopates against the city's pulse despite your best efforts. Denial becomes increasingly oxygen-expensive.

In the central plaza—or what serves for central on this particular Tuesday, before the morning's topological referendum migrates it elsewhere—stands the Memory Fountain. Not built, exactly; more like remembered into solidity by consensus of the city's distributed consciousness. It flows neither up nor down but outward through dimensions that hurt to look at directly. The water (but again, not water) carries fragments: here a shard of mosaic from drowned Pompeii, there a fragment of song sung in a language whose last speaker died before fire was discovered. Visitors—though visitor implies someone native to somewhere else, and where would that be, exactly?—come to drink remembering and forgetting in carefully measured ratios.

But there are no visitors today. No residents. No one at all except you and the city learning your shape by the negative space you occupy in its awareness. The anthropocentric error dies hard here, surrounded by evidence of its irrelevance. Nisak wasn't built for people. It was built by people who finally understood themselves as temporary configurations in processes too vast for individual identity to survive participation. What remains is architecture that remembers walking by the sea on legs it no longer possesses, dreaming in wavelengths no retina ever evolved to capture.

The water remembers you, though. As you move through the empty city beneath the absent sea, the rivers shift their courses to flow around where you will be three steps from now. Not accommodating you. Adjusting for local turbulence in patterns that will continue long after your atoms have forgotten they ever pretended to be a person.

The air tastes like coming home to a place that only existed in the instant before waking. The strangest thing isn't how alien everything is. The strangest thing is how much it doesn't matter.
