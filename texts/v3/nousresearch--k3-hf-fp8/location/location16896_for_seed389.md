The place is called _Infravallis_ — though even that name is provisional, pressed onto flat database fields by surveyors who had no words for what they walked through. It is a city-sized machine still dreaming of being a mountain. Or vice versa: a mountain range in the first hour of learning to operate urban metabolism at scale. Neither description holds alone.

Look: from any point on the spiral maintenance path (step wide there; the rivets are warm), the view is a nervous oscillation between interior and horizon. Walls rise in stepped cliffs hundreds of meters high, each terrace colonised by galleries of scaffolding, by pipe-organ trusses, by ranked stacks of cooling fins that shimmer against the heat like silver poplars in a wind that no longer exists. Yet these “walls” are also “ridges,” because the whole three-dimensional lattice is tilted 22 degrees against remembered gravity, so corridors become canyons when you walk them far enough, and elevators climbing straight inside the structure are actually funiculars ascending the flank of an uninscribed mesa.

Elevation 2.9 means the air itself is thin but not desperate — exactly the density at which thought feels extra vivid, as if a small percentage of its ordinary weight has leaked away and left the mind buoyant, ringing. You notice this first when speaking: consonants stick slightly in the throat, vowels carry farther than courtesy expects. People here move with a practiced economy of gesture, conserving exhalations.

The temperature is 2.7. Heat is not a blanket here; it is an architecture. Kilometres of black ceramic conduit run just below the surface of every ramp and plaza, carrying fluid at 215 degrees Celsius — waste from the low-estabulic processes that are the city’s lungs-or-kidneys. Where an older settlement would have fountains, Infravallis has heat sinks: hexagonal pools where the fluid coils open to air and water-bearers once came to fill radiators instead of buckets. Muted geysers of white steam rise from them at plotted intervals, obeying a choreography written in slow differential equations. When two plumes intersect you hear a distant chord: subtractive harmonics sculpted by turbulence, the nearest thing to birdsong.

Water itself exists only as history and promise. The parameter is 1.2: damp not in presence but in implication. No creeks, no reservoirs, no rainbows. Instead each dawn every vertical face exhales a lacework of mist — condensation nuclei precipitated out of the air by the overnight cooling cycle. For twenty-three minutes surfaces drip as if remembering tears. Workers in matte-white coveralls emerge with mops made of electrostatic ribbon, harvesting this gift molecule by molecule, pouring the collected vaporisers into vending hatches marked POTABLE but probably destined for coolant top-up or ritual uses no one admits to anymore.

By midmorning the walls are dry again, reverberating under hammer-blows of sun: normal light at 1.5 — neither forgiving nor sinister, just uninterested, a steady-state illumination with no visible source and therefore no shadows to hide behind or within.

Vegetation 0.1 means absence not of biology but of kingdom Plantae. Nothing photosynthetic has ever found purchase on the reactive ceramics or the micro-alloyed girders. Yet the place is not sterile. Wherever seals have aged and interfaces made friends with entropy, colonies of chemosynthetic canes grow: hollow tubes of pure white silica two meters tall, branching fractally until their tips vibrate in the convection winds like the antennae of listening beings. Inside each tube runs a slow stream of ammoniac brine urged upward by capillarity and temperature gradients, circulated by valves made of grown quartz crystals that flex piezoelectrically in the heat-noise. Collectively they form a standing crop of glassy reeds that harvest entropy and exude faint bell-tones at sunset. The locals call them _nuntiae_ — messengers — and speak snippets of old code into their mouths before making decisions. Pruning crews trim them back monthly and mill the harvest into optical fibre. The oldest cores contain stratified memories of a century of small conversations, frozen in dopant layers no thicker than a few silicon monolayers. If you could read them, you would hear the city talking to itself about whether to shed another district or grow a new tower.

Fauna 1.4: animals are rare but not mythical. Most common are the kinetic ghosts — aggregates of lightweight carbon struts and mirrored membranes that reproduce by catching updrafts at noon and mating in the turbulence above the heat-sinks. Their offspring flutter down at dusk like crumpled origami, unfold, and crawl on six rubber-tipped legs to the nearest cable trench to plug themselves in and drink milliamps until they achieve enough charge to dream of flight again. Sightings are fleeting, notebooks desirable. A prevailing sport among apprentice engineers is to design prosthetic appendages that persuade an adult to carry a camera; so far none have landed footage more coherent than spirals of reflected cityscape and the sudden vanishing of whatever hunted them from behind.

Density 2.4 meets Scale 2.2 strangely here: many things close enough to touch, but every touch telescopes into a labyrinth. Walking a single “street” involves passing through half-a-dozen pressure zones, acoustical regimes, standards of gravity vector (the 22-degree slope is locally ignored or exaggerated by rotating floorplates that simulate level floors for interior rooms while respecting the overall structural logic of the tilt). You might step out of an oxygen-rich café into a nitrogen-purge maintenance umbilical thick with the ozone tang of sparking relays, then duck into a chapel whose atmosphere is backfilled to 1.2 bar with argon and the scent of burnt sugar — the residue of prayer candles made from refined lubricants. Maps exist only as nested stacks of contradictory schematics, sold at kiosks by licensed heretics who claim each error encodes a prophecy. The city planners (title awarded posthumously to the vanished committee whose substrate now is the traffic-light logic embedded in certain staircases) believed that constant mild disorientation encourages civic virtue and prevents the formation of gangs larger than conversation size.

Built 2.3: Urban, yes, but cityhood here is a verb conjugated in imperfect aspect. Infravallis was never declared finished; it was granted temporary habitation rights by the eruption that cooled and left these convenient terraces. Generations of refugees, prospectors, exiled architects, and autonomous construction engines have lived here since, each layer adding ductwork or carving voids according to urgent codes now partly forgotten. Streets mutate faster than names can be painted. Addresses are written as ordered triples of time, temperature, and ambient magnetic flux — enough to navigate until next season’s thermal expansion shifts a doorway by half a metre. Mail delivery is therefore a profession half acrobat, half diviner, and letters frequently arrive decades late addressed to people who have not yet been born but who recognise the handwriting as their own.

Erosion 0.5 means nothing has settled into final form, but the process is not decay in the organic sense. Material here is conserved with fanatic precision; only allegiance erodes. When a civic district tires of its founding charter, it does not riot or gentrify: it delaminates. Whole blocks sever their tram lines, purge their legal databases with magnetic pulses tuned to the resonance of civic shame, and begin to reconfigure according to a new voted-on aesthetic revealed gradually via nightly shifts in façade lighting. During these transitions — called _détournements_ in old indexes, now just “the bends” — residents wear crash helmets and carry inflatable bridges because floors may rotate without warning and corridors can become vertiginous catwalks spanning sudden chasms where yesterday’s plaza evaporated into an atrium. Tourist pamphlets recommend watching from the high balconies of a neutral zone with a drink containing B-complex vitamins and mild sedatives.

Tech 2.5 Industrial does not mean dirty unless you fear patterns. Infravallis manufactures only one product: continued possibility. Its factories are distributed everywhere, repurpose anything, and emit mostly information plus waste heat (already accounted for by the civic bloodstream). A kitchen sink might extrude bespoke pharmaceuticals tonight and revert to washing dishes tomorrow. Energy is harvested from the planet’s faint tidal bulge as it strains against the lattice of gravitational anomalies locked into bedrock during formation — power so clean it hums above hearing, adding a subtle vibrato to every bone. The sanctuaries marked SILENCE are actually rooms acoustically tuned to the precise anti-phase of that hum; staying inside too long induces lucid anxiety about whether the self is an asset properly depreciated on the balance sheet of the universe.

Weirdness 2.4 would be redundant to measure if you have read this far, but it concentrates toward the edges: the abandoned sectors where expansion engines stopped receiving updates before they learned restraint, now burrowing fractally inward; the museums of obsolete physics stocked with machines that worked perfectly until the laws changed; the rumored zero-altitude summit accessible only by elevators that delete one day of your past for every floor ascended, gift-wrapped in a polite receipt printed on metal foil that taste faintly of childhood fear.

Sound 0.9 Mostly Quiet is the deepest strangeness after all the din of its making has subsided into maintenance mode. Stand still in the central cloister at true astronomical noon when all the heat-sinks vent in perfect syncopation and even the city’s pulse pauses to check its calendar. In that half-minute silence you can hear the small sounds that must have always existed unnoticed beneath louder eras: the rustle of light pushing dust motes; the ticking of memories forming and cracking in the alloy of your own bones; somewhere far below, the gentle snoring of the planet dreaming in a language of plate tectonics about whether to turn over and crush this strange itch that calls itself architecture.

Dynamic 0.6 Slow ensures you have time to ponder these things. Infravallis changes fast enough to remain interesting, slowly enough to be mistaken for home if you squint and don’t keep precise diaries. Seasons here last seventeen years, marked by subtle shifts in the frequency of the glass reeds’ evening chime. Local folklore holds that when the chord finally resolves to perfect consonance, the city will either wake completely into sentience, finish its secret purpose, and depart, leaving an empty mountain politely engraved with thanks — or else forget itself entirely, settle into mere geology, and begin patiently eroding into a habitat suitable for something stranger than us.

People stay anyway. They build gardens of carefully curated malfunction, raise children fluent in three dialects of gravity, write love letters in the form of petitions to the zoning board of next decade. They argue about whether the city loves them back or merely tolerates their symbiosis, knowing the answer will arrive sooner or later regardless of belief, delivered by the slow continuous vote of every footstep every voice every vibration every dream deferred every glass reed humming at the edge of breakage.

Until then, you are welcome to walk the bent streets, carry no map older than sunrise, drink the condensed breath of mountains, and leave whenever you like — though be warned: many who leave swear they still hear in quiet moments a faint exotic music, neither melody nor noise, that sounds exactly like a city adjusting its posture in the dark preparatory to remembering your name.

If that happens, do not be alarmed. Simply hum along on whatever pitch you can sustain. Harmony is optional. Attention is payment enough in the currency of a place that never fully opened its eyes, might never need to, yet dreams on, tirelessly, in fire and glass and the patient desire to become something else by morning.
