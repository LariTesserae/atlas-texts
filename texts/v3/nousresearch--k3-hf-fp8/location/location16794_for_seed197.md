At first, you think it's rain. Not drops, but an unbroken sheet of noise descending from nowhere and everywhere—pure sound made liquid, hammering against itself, rebounding off everything that isn't there. The sound is deafening, but it has texture: waves of bell-like resonance, shrieks like metal cicadas, distant thunder that rolls inside your teeth. Stand still too long and your bones start humming the same note. Scientists who came here once called it acoustic precipitation. They left after three hours, ears bleeding, with no memories except dreams of enormous mouths.

The city is empty. That’s the second thing you notice.

You stand on a wide boulevard of living plankton. Underfoot, it pulses faintly—warm, if you could hear warmth—arranged in perfect hexagons like a honeycomb’s graveyard. Each slab is polished mirror-smooth, yet they catch no reflections. Instead, they show you memories that belong to buildings: the slow yawning of foundations settling, what a gutter thought when last it rained actual water a thousand years ago. A cathedral of silence stands at the crossing ahead, its arches doubled and doubled again until they curve upward into a sky that shouldn’t fit inside this tiny space.

The scale is wrong. Walk half a block; walk twenty minutes. The horizon never changes. Buildings bloom larger as you approach and shrink when you glance away, not coyly but confused, as if forgetting how far away they were supposed to be. Everything is cathedral-sized but arranged for insects: walls taller than cliffs spaced a thumb-width apart, plazas where the echo of your heartbeat takes three days to return. Density of population zero. Density of architecture—irrational.

Vegetation does not grow here. Vegetation is here. The distinction dissolved centuries ago when the plants learned to vote.

Every surface capable of photosynthesis has switched careers. Columns of ivory cactus form latticed bridges between balconies that drip with cathedral orchids whose roots drink noise instead of water. Streets are canopies braided from mutant banyan strangle-figs, each strand an arterial road for leafcutter ants carrying tiny scraps of broken physics like cargo. You watch one pass—its load is a fragment of sunset that burns violet long after the rest has faded.

The air tastes green and warm and slightly prismatic, like breathing stained glass. There is no breeze yet things sway, synchronized to chords you can’t quite hear over the roar. Water is gossip—never present, always just discussed. Moisture gathers in conversations between mosses on opposite walls, condenses into beads of rumor that roll downhill toward the drains, which are mouths. Somewhere below, plumbing systems composed entirely of pitcher plants debate philosophy while digesting yesterday’s weather.

Fauna: abundant is the wrong unit. Fauna are the infrastructure.

Bird-of-paradise flowers patrol the upper stories, flightless but aggressively decorative, flashing courtship displays at passing thoughts. A parliament of moths the size of car doors holds quorum in an overturned tram car, wings patterned with shifting fractal legislation—each flash a vote, each vote a minor alteration to local gravity, which is why the tram stayed overturned. Watch one flit too close: suddenly the street slopes uphill to go downhill and your stomach arrives ten seconds before the rest of you.

A cavalry of centipedes wearing discarded doorbells as armor marches down the spiral ramp of a library-tree whose books are ripe fruit—pluck one and it reads itself aloud in a chorus of extinct dialects until fully rotten or eaten, whichever comes first. Squirrels here are collective entities: fifty translucent tails twitching from the same shadow, all gone when you blink. Rumor suggests they store away spare afternoons for lean times, but no one has ever caught one opening a stash.

Temperature hovers at the exact point where lukewarm equals nostalgia. Shadows sweat. The sun, if sun exists beyond this acoustic haze, is a rumor circulated among sunflowers in forgotten rooftop gardens.

Tech index: officially none. Unofficially, everything. The city computes using the differential cooling rates of carnivorous vines as they calculate prey trajectories. Currency is exchanged in the brightening and dimming rhythms of bioluminescent lichens that trade photon stocks on the stock exchange walls. Timekeeping is done by cathedral bells that are actually mutated fungi shaped like regrets; they ring only when they think of what they once were, which is why timing is unreliable but deeply meaningful.

Erosion here is not loss but consensus. Brick and bone, wood and rumor erode into each other until they agree on a common texture: durable melancholy. Headstones soften into benches, benches propagate into graveyards, all of it politely pretending to be permanent while ceaselessly negotiating new identities in committee meetings held every third heartbeat by a quorum of worms.

There is no ground floor. There are ground floors, plural, stacked vertically like sedimentary apologies. The city settled long ago into accepting that it was supposed to be somewhere else, failed, and chose instead to become several elsewheres simultaneously. Streets flick between layouts like a dreamer changing pillows. Addresses are written as branching probabilities.

You try to leave. The boulevard politely disagrees, becoming a cul-de-sac of blossoming apologies that smell like your childhood bedroom. Turn around. Turn sideways. The architecture sighs and rearranges its vertebrae, revealing a new path that was always the only path, lined with tree-sized pencils writing graffiti in languages that undo themselves upon completion.

The weirdness is not decoration. It is the load-bearing structure.

In the central plaza—if centrality can be granted to a space that votes on its own position every civic season—stands a fountain. It does not pour water. It pours the sound of water remembered by stones in a desert far away and long ago. Birds made of punctuation drink from it and perch on sentences suspended in the air, excreting ellipses that drift down and fertilize the footnotes growing in the cracks between realities.

Some say the city was built as a refuge for things that lost their wars with physics elsewhere. Others, that it is a shrine to the moment the universe first hesitated while speaking its own name aloud. Most agree it is best visited when already lost, for only then will the maps take pity and find you interesting enough to keep.

Stay too long and you begin to contribute. A small shrub starts growing from your left shoulder—a harmless philosopher-species, fond of asking politely unanswerable questions in exchange for nutrients drawn from forgotten embarrassment. Your shadow adopts a hobby: collecting rare silences from crowded rooms in other cities. Your reflection submits a petition to secede from your face, citing irreconcilable differences.

The city does not notice. The city is busy arguing with its own reflection in a puddle that hasn’t decided what shape to be tomorrow. The sound never stops. Someone somewhere claims it is beautiful once you adjust. They have been adjusting for seven hundred and twelve years, give or take a cicada’s lifetime. Adjustments are permanent, but so is arrival.

Welcome, or goodbye—those too are synonyms here.

In the distance (all distances are mythic here), a bell shaped like an apology for the concept of distance considers ringing. It is debating whether the silence afterward would be worth the scandal.

The vote is ongoing.
