At first touch it is only a hollow, a recession in the deeper strata where warmth never lingered. The rock around you is gray-blue gneiss, banded in pale quartz like frozen lightning caught mid-flash. No passage leads here directly; you arrive by forgetting the surface, by lingering in spiraled fractures until stone gives way to memory of emptiness—an inversion chamber compressed under three hundred meters of unyielding weight.

Air is present but reluctant. It sits against your skin like chilled silk, tasting faintly of iron and the dim memory of rain. Moisture beads on every mineral face: not dripping, not flowing, but condensing out of darkness itself, gathering into droplets that hesitate, swell, silver-bright, then surrender back to the rock without a sound. The humidity is a whispered secret between solids, nothing so bold as rain, nothing so alive as breath.

Light has not simply vanished; it has been systematically uninvited. Occasionally, a filament of phosphorescence flexes between crystal grains—an electric fish impulse lasting less than thought—illuminating nothing except the brief fact of its own refusal to stay. Afterward, the darkness feels denser, as if annoyed by the attempt.

Scale is intimate yet duplicitous. Your extended hand finds walls within reach; turn twice and the same ridges repeat at twice the distance. The void is folded, cloth stitched into itself by invisible seams. Perhaps it occupies the space inside a single boulder’s shadow stretched down through epochs. Perhaps you have entered the interstice between two chronotactic grains in the world’s mantle. Both possibilities feel equally plausible and equally irrelevant.

Against one curve of gneiss, vegetation—if such a word applies—clings in dark velvet strata. Begoniomycota interlacing with lichenized sphenopsids: stems as thin as capillary thread, leaves that are not leaves but overlapping bruise-colored scales quilted over swollen pneumatophores. No chlorophyll stains them; they photosynthesize instead from half-decay radioisotopes seeping through oligoclase veins, life feeding on the faint slow fire of uranium’s sleep. Where two fronds cross they solder together with a line of pale metal until the distinction of plant and plant dissolves into shared osmotic tissue. The smallest patch is a city of interconnected chambers, yet the whole grove could be carried in a teacup.

They grow very slowly, adding perhaps one cell-layer per century. Their metabolism pulses to the rhythm of geothermal tides ten kilometers below; you feel the effect as a faint arrhythmia in your own heartbeat, a sympathetic lagging.

Scattered through this living felt lie remnants built by someone else’s boredom or obsession: fragments too precise to be accidental, too pointless to be useful. A hemisphere of glassy carbon, perfectly smooth except for the single flake missing from its rim, reflecting nothing because it curves inward rather than out. Half a dozen rings of silver-white metal stacked vertically like the ghost of a column—each ring rotates independently without friction, bearings lubricated by vacuum itself, powered by differences in local time dilation too subtle to measure. A cluster of rods the length of metacarpals, each rod composed of alternating segments of quartz and pure vacuum frozen into solid geometry. Touch one and it vibrates at a frequency that makes the bones in your wrist remember being liquid magma.

None of these artifacts communicate purpose, only persistence. They are punctuation marks in a conversation that ended before teeth evolved to shape words. They dimly, politely, ignore your trespass.

Sound is mostly the faint, wet breathing of the walls as mineral-laden water migrates grain by grain through pressure gradients you can hear but not feel. Occasionally, deeper, a wordless resonance like someone plucking a string suspended between planets in orbit around dwarf suns—an echo filtered up through thousands of cubic kilometers of silicate sponge, arriving here stretched thinner than a photon’s patience, recognizable only as the shape of absence after it fades.

No animals have ever found or fertilized this place. There is nothing to hunt, nothing to scavenge except entropy itself, too cold to eat. Yet the geometry of niche persists: hollows in the fungal matting shaped exactly for curled creatures the size of fists, tunnels through the quartz-rods that spiral like empty cochlea. Awaiting tenants that evolved somewhere else, somewhen else—perhaps waiting still for evolution to invent backwards and fill them from the future.

The rules here are simple but odd: Time dilates toward stillness at the center of each artifact cluster, accelerates slightly in the condensing zones between droplets. Gravity remains obedient to general relativity but grows politely indifferent at distances less than a handspan, allowing pebbles to orbit your thumb in lazy ellipses if you are patient enough to wait an hour for a single revolution. Light cannot accumulate; darkness accumulates instead, pooling in corners like liquid shadow that flows syrup-slow when you disturb the air.

It is a pocket that has forgotten it is part of a larger garment, a single paragraph torn from a geology textbook written in the language of dreams and archived between the pages of a crystalline library that stands on the event horizon of a quiet black hole somewhere else entirely.

You are not supposed to be here. Somehow, you are not leaving.

You sit down—not because you are tired, but because the floor welcomes the curve of your hip exactly, as if someone measured you from inside your bones in a childhood you never lived. The air folds around you. A droplet condenses on the ceiling far above, begins its silver pilgrimage downward, attracted less by gravity than by the slow growing certainty of your attention.

It will reach you in approximately eleven thousand years. You find yourself willing to wait.
