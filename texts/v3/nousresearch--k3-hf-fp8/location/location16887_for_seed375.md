The city stands unfinished on the salt flats where no rain has ever fallen. Kilometers-wide hexagonal tiles of pale sodium chloride stretch away from its lower districts, cooled relics of some lapsed ocean now pressed thin as paper by the world's own weight. The tiles whisper against each other when season-shifts pass overhead, producing a choral shivering too low for ears, felt instead in the marrow of those rare animals that venture out from the city-bones at twilight.

Atmosphere here has weight but no moisture. Thin and metallic, it pulls heat from lungs with each breath, growing colder the deeper you descend into the city's strata. Mornings begin with frost forming on metal teeth that protrude from walls - alloy cooling fins meant for a different climate system that never arrived. By midday, the same frost sublimates directly into pale vapor that drifts like ghosts through empty thoroughfares, seeking condensation points that do not exist.

The city itself was built downward through eighteen known levels, each more massive and more incomplete than the last. Construction follows geometries that frustrate surveyors - hexagonal foundations supporting octagonal frameworks that terminate in star-shaped apertures opening onto voids filled with scaffolding. No two levels share the same gravity orientation. Staircases spiral against walls that later become floors. Elevator shafts terminate in mid-air over plazas where water fountains were planned ("water" chiseled into original blueprints in a forgotten language, alongside diagrams of impossible fluid dynamics.)

Materials change as you descend. Upper levels: poured concrete mixed with ground bones of the salt flats' extinct megafauna, the aggregate fossil teeth occasionally gleaming white in dim light. Middle levels: ceramic composites fired in shapes that suggest melted circuit boards, their surfaces etched with patterns that capacitance meters read as functional but disconnected. Lower levels: metals that cannot exist at standard pressure - cubic iron, hexagonal copper, aluminum with the crystal structure of diamond. The deepest accessible chambers are lined with mirror-smooth panels of single-crystal salt grown from solutions saturated with heavy elements that decay slowly, casting their own faint radiation glow.

Light arrives fractured. The sky is a permanent dusk-purple vault without sun or stars, illuminated from within by some phosphorescence of the atmosphere itself perhaps, or by the sum total of the city's aborted radiance reflected back from distant salt horizons. Within the city, light behaves differently on each level. Some districts are lit by bioluminescent algae engineered into the walls centuries ago, colonies that pulse irregularly in cycles unrelated to any known biology. Others are permeated with Cherenkov-blue illumination from the decaying cores of machines that were never completed enough to activate. The deepest levels require no external light - the eyes of those who descend far enough adapt to see by the faint radio glow of potassium-40 decaying in their own bones.

Vegetation exists in fifteen documented forms, all descended from one original species of extremophilic lichen that learned to metabolize concrete. They grow only on the surfaces where different construction eras intersect - pale branching networks that excrete acids to dissolve boundary layers between materials, extracting trace nutrients from the resulting chemical slurry. During rare convergence events when multiple levels' ecosystem cycles align, these lichens bloom simultaneously across kilometers of surface area, producing spores that drift like snowflakes through the city's empty spaces. The few remaining seed-archives (glass cylinders smashed open centuries ago by expanding freeze-thaw cycles) suggest the original colonists intended to terraform with these organisms first, creating soil before introducing water, air, trees, animals, people.

People never arrived. Or they arrived and departed. Or they are still arriving, one by one, through processes too gradual to notice. The evidence conflicted: quarters sized for humanoids but with furniture bolted to multiple walls simultaneously. Kitchens equipped with appliances that process rock into nutrient pellets metabolizable by no known biochemistry. A single complete suit of sealed environmental armor found in Level Seven's central plaza, perfectly preserved except for the visor scratched from inside by something that left marks resembling both fingerprints and geometric proofs.

Those who live here now (if "live" applies) came later. They move through the city's abandoned circulatory systems: the air-handling ducts that terminated in solid walls (the designers expected atmosphere to be different), the water mains that were never connected to source or sink (they terminate in specialized gravity wells where nonexistent fluid was meant to endlessly circulate), the neuroelectric cable bundles that drape between levels like abandoned synapses transmitting static patterns of nothing. They trade in artifacts from the city's own incomplete metabolism: gears milled to impossible tolerances that rotate forever in evacuated chambers with no axes; books written in languages where each translation into any other language including itself produces new, contradictory meanings; seeds frozen mid-germination in cryogenic chambers that cycle between temperatures without external power.

The city grows now by accretion and subtraction simultaneously. Upper levels erode slowly as salt winds abrade exposed surfaces, returning concrete to dust that blows out across the flats where it might someday nucleate new tiles. Lower levels grow as the planet's own heat and pressure continue the construction processes abandoned millennia ago: mantle convection slowly folding new chambers into existence, mineral solutions precipitating into crystalline forms that resolve gradually into familiar architecture - a doorway here, half of a window there, the suggestion of a stair spiraling down into rock that will become emptiness given enough geological time.

Through the center of everything runs the Great Shaft, a vertical borehole kilometers in diameter that passes through all known levels and continues deeper, its walls smooth as glass and scored with helical grooves too regular to be natural. No one knows who or what made it. Dropped objects accelerate downward at rates that decrease smoothly rather than increasing, as if gravity itself grows weaker with depth. Light shone into its depths returns after measurable delays but always Doppler-shifted toward lower frequencies, as if the shaft is growing longer while you watch it. Strange sounds sometimes drift up from far below: choral harmonies in frequencies that resonate with the calcium in bones, mathematical proofs spoken in voices that make sense only when you understand that they are arguments against their own existence.

At irregular intervals measured in centuries, everything changes. The salt tiles shift position relative to each other across the flats, forming new patterns that spell out messages in languages not yet invented. The city's internal gravity fields reconfigure, causing rivers of abandoned furniture and machinery to flow uphill through previously horizontal streets. Lake-sized chambers fill from bottom to top with air that precipitates out of the rock itself, linger for days or years, then drain away equally suddenly into fractures that seal themselves as if embarrassed. During these events (called "corrections" in the fragmented literature left by previous inhabitants, or "beauties" by those who worship them), the city approaches completion for brief moments before diverging again into new forms of incompleteness.

It is said that at the bottom of everything, beyond the deepest accessible levels where the city transitions smoothly into unmodified planetary crust, lies the Central Chamber where the original blueprints are stored. Not paper or digital copies, but the Platonic forms themselves: perfect geometries of space and time and possibility that leaked through from some adjacent dimension where the city was built correctly, with all systems integrated and all inhabitants present and accounted for. Those who seek it never return, or return changed in subtle ways - their shadows now cast in directions different from the light sources that should create them, their memories arranged in patterns that predict rather than record events, their bodies slowly crystallizing from the inside out into shapes that match empty spaces in the city's architecture where statues of the city's true builders were meant to stand.

The city is called Absentia by those who know it exists at all. It was built to be inhabited but never completed enough for inhabitation to begin. It maintains itself in the precise state of near-completion where all possibilities remain simultaneously true and false: the doors that open onto rooms that may or may not contain what you're looking for, the elevators that might take you up or down or sideways into versions of yourself that made different choices, the mirrors that reflect not what is but what was meant to be before reality diverged from intention.

You stand now at one of its thousand unmanned gates, where massive doors carved from single crystals of rose quartz hang suspended between open and closed on hinges that have never been used. Behind you, the salt flats stretch away to geometric horizons under the dim purple sky.     * **可能性**：标准模型（目前最成功的粒子物理理论）假设质子是稳定的，即寿命无限长，因为它们是最轻的强相互作用粒子（重子），没有任何允许的衰变途径来保持所有守恒定律（特别是重子数守恒）。但是，许多试图超越标准模型的理论（例如大统一理论）预言了质子应该是不稳定的，可以在极长的时间尺度上衰变。
* **中子**：
    * **自由中子**（不在原子核内）：平均寿命约为 **880 秒**（约 14.8 分钟）。这意味着如果你孤立一个自由中子，平均等待约 15 分钟，它就会通过弱相互作用衰变成一个质子、一个电子和一个反电子中微子。
    * **束缚中子**（在原子核内）：在大多数稳定的原子核中，中子被认为是**稳定**的（理论寿命可与质子衰变寿命相比拟，极其漫长），因为它们处于能量最低的状态，没有足够的能量通过衰变变成质子并满足所有守恒定律（例如质量守恒、能量守恒、电荷守恒、重子数守恒等）。只有在某些放射性原子核中，中子才能通过 β 衰变等方式转变为质子，但这并非中子本身的固有属性，而是整个原子核不稳定性的表现。

* 质子的寿命目前被认为要么无限长（标准模型观点），要么极长（超过 10^34 年，这远超宇宙当前年龄）。
* 自由中子的寿命很短，只有约 15 分钟。
