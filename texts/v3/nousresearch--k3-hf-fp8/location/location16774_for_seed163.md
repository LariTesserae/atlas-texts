The landscape begins where the last stone of the old crater rim gives way to living slope—a transition so gradual it might be mistaken for shadow if your eyes weren't adjusting to the brightness here. Titaniocereus towers lean over everything at impossible angles, their trunks grown thick as cathedral columns but hollowed into resonance chambers. Each ribbed column carves the wind into a different tone until the entire basin hums a single sustained chord, deafening but not unpleasant, like being inside a struck bell that never decays.

The air itself is wet enough to drink. It condenses constantly on your exposed skin, collects on every leaf edge as perfect spheres that swell until they snap upward as microspheres, defying gravity's remainder here. These walking-water pearls roll uphill along preprogrammed friction slopes, joining streams that climb the towers' bark to feed the photosynthetic crowns where atmosphere ends. You can follow one for kilometers as it audibly murmurs its mineral content to the symbiotic fungi lining its path.

Nothing here is soil yet. The groundscape lies only a few centuries exposed—raw impact breccia still warm enough in places to steam when touched, overlaid with the first living carpet. Glass-fiber lichens weave their filaments across basalt shards, dissolving rock with embedded acid vesicles while simultaneously depositing silicon threads stronger than steel. Between them grow the precursors of forests: stonebreaker corms splitting boulders from within, their root-pressures measured in kilotons per square millimeter but applied so slowly the fracture lines are clean enough to be musical instruments when struck.

The trees—if they are trees—remember what they ate. Embedded in translucent bark you can read the geologic autobiography of each tower: here the iron-rich layer from the deep mantle plume, here the platinum signature of the comet that seeded these waters, here the unexpected strontium spikes that suggests something stranger. Their growth rings are not annual but epochal, each storing centuries of computation in the quantum spin states of their lignin crystals. When two towers touch cortex to cortex, spark-bridges arc between them carrying entire planetary histories in flashes too brief to see but bright enough to leave retinal fossils.

Atmosphere pools in the lowest hundred meters like a colder, heavier sea. Below this boundary, your breath fogs and speech turns sluggish with increased nitrogen viscosity. Above it, the air thins rapidly to vacuum clarity but remains breathable thanks to the oxygen fountains—biomechanical gills the size of stadiums that pump from the planet's saturated lithosphere. Between these layers swim the first tentative fauna: silicate blimpfish filled with hydrogen peroxide compartments, their skins photovoltaic cells that crack when their photon tanks overfill, allowing controlled explosive bursts of migration. They graze on airborne pollen reefs that bloom and shrink with the day-bright light cycle, which here lasts 168 Earth hours due to the tidally locked moon's slow dance.

The built structures—if you can call them that when they grow themselves—are scattered like seeds thrown carelessly uphill. Each begins as a crystalline seed dropped by the dying towers: a single self-ordering molecule complex containing instructions for a ceramics factory, a bioreactor, a memory palace, or something else entirely. Those that find suitable niches in the still-cooling rock extrude themselves upward in slow-motion eruptions, building hexagonal vaults from waste oxygen and cellulose nanofiber reinforced with extracted metals. Most fail, collapsing into rubble gardens that become nucleation points for new ecosystems. The few that mature stand empty and humming, their doors too wide or too narrow for any known body-shape, their windows opening onto views impossible in this geometry.

There is one persistent sound beneath the towers' drone: a deep, slow heartbeat measured in pressure waves that travel through bedrock faster than sound through air. It originates from below the still-molten core, where something planet-sized and embryonic is beginning to dream. The trees are its nervous system's first tentative synapses. The walking waters are its blood plasma beginning circulation. The factories are organelles assembling themselves according to instructions written in the genetic code of entire biospheres, crashed here and reassembled from fragments.

None of this is normal or weird—it simply is, at this particular moment when a world long dead has begun to reconsider the question of life and arrived at a different answer. By the time the first spore-ships from distant stars arrive to catalog this world, the towers will have grown into space elevators and walked away across the stars themselves. The factories will have manufactured their last products and gone to seed. The heartbeat will have quickened to a child's pulse.

But that is still millions of heartbeats in the future. For now, you can sit on this ledge of newborn stone still warm from yesterday's growth spurt, watching water climb uphill toward the sun that never sets here at the terminator line between night and day eternal, and listen to a planet learning how to sing itself awake.
