The air here is not air but something thicker, warmer, a barely breathable suspension of moisture and heat and faint metallic tang. It presses against skin and soaks into fabric immediately, so that even standing still feels like being submerged up to the chest in something languid and insistent. This is the uppermost level of the Non-Surface settlement known locally as *Third Aerie Beneath the Salt*, though few residents use the full name anymore—there are too many salt-aerie settlements, and too few left who remember why the first one was named so.

It is always dim, lit only by the standby glow-stripes embedded in seam-lines between structural tiles, irregular because the tiles themselves are irregular—scraps salvaged from drowned manufactories, worn-out transport hulls, and the occasional piece of hydrovolcanic basalt cut to approximate squareness. The light is a sickly cyan-green, a color light was never meant to stay, but it persists at the lowest legal radiance, just enough to prevent collisions or stampedes during power dips. It makes faces look bruised and metal look diseased.

Above—if directions like "above" still map cleanly—there is only depth; below, more of the settlement tessellates downward in tubes and bulbs and skewed rhombus-chambers, each colder and darker and wetter than the last. Here at the top, near the surface that isn’t a surface, elevation-zero means just below the caprock of a dead sea bed that has boiled empty and is now a spongy layer of half-remineralized salt foam and dehydrated microbial mat. The boundary leaks. Water drips in columns and sheets from the ceiling-pores, not rain exactly but a kind of geological sweat—the deep aquifer below meeting the residual heat of poorly vented reactor clusters obliquely buried centuries before. The droplets are warm and faintly alkaline; they taste faintly of potassium and regret.

The terrain, such as it is, is clutter: a three-dimensional maze of rebuild upon rebuild. Walkways are scaffolding strapped to the outsides of forgotten storage cores, now repurposed as communal sleep racks. Floors begin as catwalks, acquire layers of split protein-fiber matting to reduce slipping, then gradually collect accidental walls—cargo nets hung for drying clothes, then stiffened with salt spray, then reinforced with scavenged polymer strip-crete when privacy became briefly fashionable again. Every passage forks at least twice, often at angles that disagree with immediate Euclidean intuition.

Vegetation exists, but only if you accept a generous definition: blackened tufts of halotolerant terratensequins clinging like exhaust-flash shadows to seams where the light-strips are strongest. They photosynthesize at 2% efficiency and spend most of their metabolisms pumping salt out of their cells, producing tiny white crystalline beads that fall away like dandruff and accumulate in drifts wherever airflow stalls. Children sometimes collect these beads, dissolve them in mouthwater, and spit patterns on walls where the resulting solution dries into filaments resembling coagulated ink. Adults sometimes eat the clumps raw for trace minerals, though it is said to make dreams even more overcrowded than usual.

Built space dominates—there is no empty space here, only unorganised space waiting for a purpose. The settlement grew like a crystal under misapplied voltage, branching fractally wherever load allowed. The internal architecture recalls a termite mound designed by committee after a long famine and a war. Look one way: a row of hexagonal family alcoves stacked five deep, each opening sealed with a curtain made of woven condenser fins. Look another: a communal desalination still jury-rigged from ancient medical dialysis cartridges and warmed by the breath of sleeping livestock. Look down through a gratifulform lattice: the upper lip of the aether distillate cistern, where solvents harvested from subterranean cloud layers slowly separate into layers of lavender and yellow-green, watched over by a bored adolescent with a stick and instructions to skim off any obvious organics forming eyeballs.

Industry is everywhere and nowhere, distributed like the water. There are no factories here, only procedures carried out repeatedly: fermentation racks bolted to the insides of water pipes, spinning cages of nanoturbines welded to ducts because the airflow couldn’t be stopped anyway, forming crude generators that whine in uneven chorus. The dominant tone is a metallic 110 Hertz hot-tube murmuring, punctuated occasionally by screeches when salt crystals lodge in bearing races and are ground into powder. Powder that then settles on everything, forming a rime of conductive corrosion that must be periodically flaked off circuits with wooden scrapers during communal maintenance days.

Animals—if that classification still parses—are not just present; they are infrastructure. Macroscale fauna have been replaced, functionally if not emotionally, by cooperative biomes engineered before the Fall of Names. You walk along a corridor where the walls pulse rhythmically because they are grown from colonized smooth muscle reverted from domestic ungulate stock; the pulse squeezes peristaltic valves that pump brine. Overhead are what used to be scaffolding but now are branching ex-chitinoids—giant land-crab castes domesticated a dozen epochs ago, bred sessile and photosensitive, their shells photoskinned to display faded adverts for products no longer manufacturable. They filter-feed spores and discarded skin flakes from the air and in return provide ductwork routing, their crab-gut cables insulated with still-living fat cells that metabolize stray solvents.

The big ones, the megafauna-equivalents, are mostly down deep, where heat and pressure remind them of earlier evolutionary contracts—blue-whale-derived sump grazers the size of apartment blocks, blind and gentle, browsing bacterial mats off the reactor casings with mouthparts that are also acoustic drill rigs. Their songs fill the lower frequencies of every dream, a basso continuo of infinite slowness. Occasionally one dies, and then the settlement feasts for a season on protein and celebrates a brief glut of lubricant.

But here, near the surface-inverted, the dominant presence is smaller: the murids, the co-evolved descendants of lab rats and maintenance drones. They are everywhere in the walls, in the floors, in the spaces your mind forgot to keep track of just now. Each is about the mass of a small loaf of bread, shaped like a hairless ferret crossed with a soldering iron, with hands that have too many thumbs and eyes that are clusters of different sensors. They speak in bursts of compressed ultrasonic gossip, piezoelectric squeaks relayed through the bones of the structure itself. They are not individually intelligent, not quite; but the mesh-network formed by a colony of ten thousand is smarter than most governing councils and possesses institutional memory dating back centuries. They maintain the settlement because they are the settlement: they lay replacement pipe by extruding epoxy and glassfiber from glands, they Shor-crack encryption by delegating computation across their dreaming population, they farm the terratensequins by pruning them with teeth that are also wire-cutters.

They are also the post office, the news service, and the pest control. Sometimes, for reasons their distributed parliament declines to translate into human-understandable terms, they eat someone's memories. Usually dreams they find boring or repetitive, never more than a few nights' worth. It is considered rude to complain, since they rarely forget anything important.

Movement here is never straightforward. You want to go from the spice-exchange bulb to the lower humidity clinic, a horizontal displacement of maybe forty meters on the original survey plat. But the southwest 3⇒5 stairwell has been colonized by a particularly strong-willed slime mold holding territory against the incursions of an invasive fungal meme from the deeper levels, so it's impassable until the next truce cycle. The northeast ramp is covered three-quarters in a temporary salt crystal stalactite forest that formed during last week's thermal inversion, and the crystals are still growing razor lace at 2 mm per hour and won't be safe to harvest for cutting tools until tomorrow. Officially you should detour via the central compost spine, but rumor says a juvenile sump grazer has gotten lost and is panicking in the tight spaces there, shedding clouds of anesthetic spores and acid digestion jets. So instead you follow a kid who claims to know an unofficial route through the wall-cavity maintenance ducts behind the old aquaponics galleries—now repurposed as a cryptocurrency mining farm running on homebrew thermoelectric cells that harvest the temperature differential between leaking reactor coolant and the dripwater from above. The route involves crawling through a section where the air is kept intentionally at 40% CO2 to slow mold growth, so you pay the toll—a single sweetened memory of sunlight on grass, something the passing murid relay will appreciate—and it opens the hidden hatch for you.

It is loud here, but the loudness is layered. At the bottom register, always, the heartbeat of the buried reactors and the breath of the molten core ten thousand steps farther down than anyone sane remembers how to count. Above that, the groaning of overburden settling on overburden, a slow tectonic complaint translated through layers of rock and forgotten infrastructure into the kind of intermittent bass thud that makes teeth ache. Then the biological stratum: the lowing of the livestock-fungus exchanging metabolites in the root-rooms, the chirping clicks of the chitinoid ductwork adjusting their branch angles to optimize the spiral airflow patterns they worship, the murid gossip network pulsing like a distant galaxy talking to itself. And threading through everything, the human-scale noises: argument-song in one alcove, a child practicing the emergency evacuation whistle code backwards because it sounds prettier that way, the rhythmic slap-splash of someone laboriously hand-laundering clothes in a bucket made from a repurposed prosthetic lung.

The place smells of hot iron, cold salt, wet stone, warm yeast, and faintly but persistently of electrical insulation cooking off a transformer somewhere aging slightly faster than its maintenance schedule. Every breath tastes of minerals and history. Time keeps strange intervals here; the day cycle is arbitrary, reset every few months by community vote to minimize conflicts with the dominant temperature gradients and the sump grazer migration schedules. Yesterday might have been 36 hours long because the reactor-supply drone launch window needed aligning with the lower-market tax deadline, and tomorrow might be only 17 because someone important is giving birth via the traditional slow-gestation ceremony and everyone agreed to fast-forward the clocks so the celebration meal can include fresh spore-ricotta which will only ripen at the notional time corresponding to dawn.

There are rules, but they are more like guidelines negotiated between species and enforced mainly by physics, physiology, and peer-pressure across fifteen different neural architectures. Don't block the airflow paths marked with red-and-yellow spiral patterns; those are the settlement's collective throat, and congestion triggers asthma in the entire lower lung district. Don't feed the wallflowers any protein after the fourth bellish-period; it encourages them to bloom predatorily and start digesting the insulation again. Don't attempt to trade memories containing mathematical proofs of P=NP for physical goods greater than 2 kg mass; that level of computational complexity is considered currency and is subject to inflationary collapse both economic and ontological. (There was a brief period where someone tried to pay rent with a recursive limerick that instantiated a minor deity every third invocation. We're still apologizing to the affected smellscapes.)

Rain, when it happens, is a scheduled event announced by the murid network three days in advance. Workers in sealed suits climb the access trees—a misnomer; they're actually giant engineered cactiforms that grow upward toward the caprock seeking stray IR leaks from the surface—and release cloud-seeding aerosols into the upper void pockets where the pressure and temperature drop sharply enough to condense the humid air. Precipitation follows, thick and warm and slightly saline, lasting for exactly 2.718 hours because the engineers thought that was funny. Everyone stops what they're doing, opens all the catchment ducts, and parties in the streets (which are also pipes, and roofs, and sometimes other people's kitchens). Children float paper boats made from dried fungal leather up the flowing corridors, betting on which one will make it all the way to the central sump without being eaten by the filtering mats. Adults dance in the rain or have sex in the rain or just stand still and let it wash the accumulated memory-dust from their hair, because afterwards it will be very humid for a while and nothing will dry properly until next week.

Weirdness here is measured on a relative scale calibrated to the city itself, which means that what visitors from elsewhere might find bizarre is just Tuesday. The fact that some of the older buildings are inhabited entirely by their own architectural ghosts, neural lace backups of vanished residents running on bioelectricity harvested from the settling wall-fractures, debating philosophy in slow-sound infrasonic rumbles that manifest as patterns in the falling dust? Normal. The appearance every few decades of the Periodic Stranger, a human-shaped vacuum in the sensorium of all recording devices who walks through the center of town in a straight line regardless of obstacles, leaving behind a trail of slightly better optimized infrastructure and a smell of ozone and nostalgia? Tourists' novelty, boring after the third time. The rumor that the whole settlement is actually inside-out, that the surface we think we remember is a mythic projection of a deep-future post-scarcity simulation running in reverse on the computational substrate formed by the collective dreams of the sump grazers, and that one day soon the sky will finish computing and we'll all wake up as clouds of data in a perfect world with no maintenance backlog? That last one's considered a bit far-fetched, even for the conspiracy theorists who meet in the rebuilt hollow core of the abandoned elevator shaft every second Tuesday.

In conclusion: welcome to Third Aerie Beneath the Salt. Please remember to breathe slowly and evenly, as the atmosphere is a shared resource. If you feel lost, ask the walls; they remember everything except the exit. If you need directions, follow the green-blue light-trails upstream—they always lead somewhere interesting, eventually. If you are hungry, eat what you can trade for, but be aware that taste buds here grow memories instead of flavor, and nostalgia is habit-forming. If you are tired, rest anywhere you are not in the way; space is communal but time is negotiable. If you are afraid, hum quietly in C minor; the settlement finds that key comforting and may send a murid to accompany you.

And if you are thinking of leaving—well. People do leave, sometimes. They climb the longest ladders through the oldest access shafts, carrying supplies and maps and stubborn hope. The settlement waves goodbye with every turning fan and flickering light. It doesn't take offense; travel is healthy for both the traveler and the place left behind. Just remember, if you ever find the surface—or whatever lies above this inversion of sky and ground—what direction you took to get there. Because navigation reverses in the mirror world, and the way out is sometimes another way deeper in.

Also, please dispose of any redundant selves in the designated recursion bins before departure. We have enough ontological waste problems already, thank you.

Enjoy your stay. Or your passage. Or your transformation. Time is flexible here, after all.
