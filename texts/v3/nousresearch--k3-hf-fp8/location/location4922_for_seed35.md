A single chamber, hollowed from the planet’s dry interior long before its oceans evaporated. The ceiling is bedrock, unevenly fused and still glowing faintly orange from whatever ancient heat-boring tool carved it; veins of extinguished metal streak across like petrified lightning strikes fifteen paces overhead. No natural sky ever looked down here. Scale is deceptive: a cathedral might fit, but only if its vaulting were compressed by the weight of entire mountain ranges. Every footstep echoes once—a stone slap—and then nothing, as if the walls agree to forget ripples as soon as they are born.

Temperature pools at skin level like stalled breath, neither comfort nor threat, leaving sweat to bead and evaporate halfway down forearms. Moisture is only memory: salt blossoms crust the lower walls in faint white petals that flake if touched, tasting of bitter iron instead of sea. The air itself is almost too thick to breathe, packed with suspended dust and fine alchemical powders that catch the throat. Filtering masks hang like discarded skins from ceiling hooks made of rail bolts and duct-tape loops—none intact, none needed anymore.

Out of that stillness vegetation grips life with absurd tenacity. Dozens of candelabra-shaped pipes burst through cracks in the floor, each rupture crowned by tufts of jet-black moss. Not green, not chlorophyll-leaning, but something that metabolizes stray voltage instead of sunlight. Every hour (if hours still passed here) they sprout slender glassy stalks five centimeters high, tremble in unison like nerve endings sensing a heartbeat that never comes, then retract and melt back into metallic fuzz. Nothing ever flowers; nothing ever grows taller.

Fauna fills the gaps those minimal plants leave undoing. The largest creatures are glove-sized: fat flattened disks of laminated bone and copper wire that drift along invisible temperature gradients, spinning slowly, flicking antennae that spark blue micro-arcs against stone. A continuous metallic chirp rises from their wing-case quills rubbing—ten thousand crickets tuned to a steel saw. Scuttling beneath them, mites composed entirely of corroded screws and cable insulation nibble the salt-lichen rounds and shed rust flakes like dandruff. Occasionally one of the disks will fold inward, flash bright white-hot, and split into two smaller versions; the room’s sound spikes for a split-second whistle then settles back to baseline. Birth and death here are only rearrangements of debris.

Built things lie scattered without routine. Halfway along the east arc stands an upright loom as tall as a doorframe, its frame cast from solidified glass slag and threaded with hundreds of hanging strands—each strand a different metal wire, from hair-fine gold to thumb-thick iron rebar braided with silk-thin fiber-optic threads that glow with dull pulsing colors decades after their last data pulse died. No weaver sits at it, but the warp keeps tightening by microscopic degrees day after millennia day, driven by a buried counterweight locked into the slow contraction of cooling rock beneath the floor. When the tension reaches symmetry it will snap everything at once; the room has been listening toward that chord-shift for a geological epoch.

Beside the loom rests what was once a locomotive cab, stripped to ribs and bellows, now occupied by a colony of air-bladder plants that inflate translucent sacs from steam-leak valves and deflate them again with sighs like distant horns—breathing industrial lullabies to no sleeper. Brass gauges on the sarcophagus-like boiler still needle-read permanent fractions of pressure that never quite reach zero, endlessly seeking atmospheric balance that will never grant it rest.

Light filters down from nowhere obvious but everywhere available—slits where imperfect welds in the roof slab admit faint glow from molten layers kilometres above, scattered into cave-cat gloom by airborne metal dust. The shadows cast are not shadows of things present but overlays of every previous shadow that ever danced across chemically-photochromic paint still clinging in flakes to ceiling girders. Touch a dark patch and your hand leaves a pale imprint that fades back over quiet centuries; the room remembers silhouettes better than shapes.

There is a softest of rhythms underfoot: once every dozen heartbeats the stone floor flexes downward the width of a sheet of paper, then rises again, as if some immense buried lung beneath this pocket of lungless air sighs in sleep. No earthquake, no settling load, no thermal pulse explains it. The motion has never altered amplitude or interval since the first intruder scratched a tally mark on a pillar and realized each mark matched exactly twenty-seven thousand others already scratched there before him. Time here is not frozen; it erases the distinction between change and stillness.

Weirdness saturates without spectacle. Gravity is locally inconsistent: a pebble rolled along the floor will curve gently toward the loom on Tuesdays (if days were numbered), toward the locomotive on days that feel prime-numbered in the marrow, and roll true only when left alone. If two people stand facing each other and trade places without turning, each arrives dressed in a single item belonging to the other—button, ring, shoelace—but neither remembers the exchange. Machines brought in stop functioning predictably: a watch ticks backwards only when forgotten, a radio tunes to broadcasts transmitted tomorrow by stations that never existed, a flashlight beam solidifies into a glass rod that shatters into music notes if snapped.

Narratives accumulate like dust but never cohere. Geologists argue this cavity was drilled by vanished megafauna seeking minerals; archaeologists catalogue the artifacts as remnants of an underground factory sealed during a forgotten war; theologians claim it is a sarcophagus chamber for a sleeping god whose dreams filter reality through random holes. None agree on entrance or exit because there may be none: corridors visible at dusk leading forward are blank wall at dawn, and when you turn around the way you came the landmarks have rearranged themselves into plausible new orders.

You can live here indefinitely if you do not thirst, sleep, or question too sharply. The air sustains, the biomass is edible if you pry apart copper from protein, and the ambient thrum of unresolved tension eventually soothes the biological urge for novelty. Settlements have coalesced and evaporated: twenty silhouettes pressed into the salt floor where a camp once held nightly councils; a rusted perimeter of filing cabinets ringed round a dry fountain where someone tried to legislate laws of passage. Always, after generations or weeks, the final inhabitants rise one morning, take nothing, walk single-file into the direction that gravity favors that hour—and no new footprints ever lead back.

The place is loud because it is full of conversations interrupted mid-syllable, engines stalled between strokes, music stuck on notes held forever. The loudest silence is the absence of water drip, wind moan, heartbeat ticking towards eventual silence. Here, every clock is stopped at the same moment—though none agree what moment, and none ever will. Here, a world ended by forgetting to end. Here, the landscape continues composing itself in layers too dense to peel apart, too small to escape, warm with the leftover heat of creation that never quite decided whether to begin or to pause indefinitely.

And in the center, equidistant from wall and loom and locomotive and absent water, stands a simple metal chair bolted to the floor facing nowhere in particular. Its seat cushion is worn smooth by countless liftings-off that were never completed. On its backrest, someone long dissolved into component ions engraved one word with a stylus made of hope: “Wait.”

The chair waits. The room waits. The parameters hold steady. The next arrival is already overdue.
