The City of Calmaris stands submerged beneath itself, layer upon layer of failing infrastructure sinking slowly into the liquid earth.

What was once seabed rose during an engineering project gone divine, lifting this metropolis of a million souls to 1,800 meters above its original shore. The city planners celebrated their victory over nature with champagne and bronze plaques, but they forgot that water never forgets where it belongs. Now it returns, not as ocean but as everything else—condensing on every surface, dripping from ceilings that were once other cities’ floors, seeping through waterproof membranes turned porous with age.

You stand on West Berthold Promenade, theoretically the 43rd floor of what your map calls the Entertainment District. The guidebook claims this was open air once, rooftop gardens and observation decks, but ten generations of desperate expansion have filled every cubic meter between here and the original ground level with more city. Apartments burrow into the walls of abandoned factories. Stairwells spiral through abandoned stairwells. Elevators built to carry people now carry smaller elevators that carry cargo pods that carry components for building more elevators.

The air tastes like old pennies and newer rust. Every breath carries dissolved traces of the fourteen industrial processes happening somewhere nearby—plating chromium, distilling solvents, recycling the recycling systems. Seventy-two percent humidity doesn’t begin to capture it. Water isn’t content to stay vapor here; it colonizes surfaces as dew, gathers as beads, joins into ropes that crawl down walls like slow-motion snakes. The city’s engineers call it capillary action. The city’s priests call it pilgrimage.

The vegetation has adapted beautifully. Down in the pressures where sunlight never reaches anymore, engineered mosses convert rust into oxygen while flowering vines drink microplastics and bloom colors no pollinator ever evolved to see. Up here in the dim middle layers—what passes for surface now—grow trees that were planters that were sculptures that were infrastructure. Their roots have found the ancient sewage mains and nutrient-rich time capsules of forgotten landfills. They fruit year-round now, producing apples that taste faintly of antifreeze, pears that glow softly in the presence of electromagnetic fields.

The soundscape compresses distance into texture. Nothing echoes properly in this environment of infinite acoustic crannies. A factory whistle three kilometers away and two hundred meters down arrives as a vibration in your teeth. Conversation happens at murmur level or through expensive noise-canceling implants. Occasionally a building somewhere reaches its engineered limit and begins to sing—a deep harmonic groan that lasts for weeks as stresses redistribute through the city’s connected bones.

Movement requires planning here. The original street grid exists only as a legend on old maps and a ghostly signal in outdated navigation systems. Instead, you follow the codes spray-painted by different transit guilds—blue spirals for water-secure routes, red triangles for areas where electrical systems are currently stable, yellow chevrals that change meaning daily depending on which maintenance collective last updated them. Children learn to read the graffiti before they learn to read words.

The fauna that remains has gone either tiny or slow. Spiders weave webs across elevator shafts that function as literal food webs, catching the insects that catch the dust that used to be documents. On the upper floors—what pass for upper floors this decade—breeding populations of pigeons have evolved denser bones and more efficient metabolism, living their entire lives without ever experiencing true flight, hopping instead from ledge to ledge in patterns that now appear in mathematical papers about optimization algorithms. Rumors persist of rats somewhere below that have grown intelligent, but then rumors always persist about something growing intelligent somewhere below.

Temperature stays remarkably consistent at 13°C year-round, thanks to the combined thermal inertia of billions of tons of concrete, steel, and human ambition soaked through with water’s extraordinary heat capacity. Seasons manifest instead in the schedule of manipulations performed by the hydraulic engineers of the Bureau of Climatological Stability—opening certain valves in the deepest basements to release carefully calculated pulses of warmer water from the geothermal taps, closing others to redirect cooler currents from the ancient aqueduct network that still somehow carries glacial melt from mountains that no one has seen in generations.

Light operates under similar bureaucratic authority. The original architect of Calmaris had grand visions of artificial suns, massive orbital mirrors bringing endless daylight to their creation. What remains are thousands of aging LED arrays mounted on the ceilings of what were once plazas, now programmed by competing algorithms to simulate everything from tropical noon to Arctic twilight to marketing campaigns. Shadows behave unpredictably here, sometimes running in multiple directions at once, occasionally pooling in corners and becoming solid enough to trip over.

The city builds itself constantly, but not always on purpose. The official Construction Administration issues plans and permits, schedules deliveries of rubble and reinforcement, commissions new districts in the theoretical spaces between existing ones. Meanwhile, the unofficial architecture—the architecture of water finding joints, of roots finding cracks, of people finding empty corners—works faster and more inventively. Every citizen learns to distinguish between load-bearing ornamentation and ornamental load-bearing structures, between stairs that lead somewhere and stairs that lead to lawsuits waiting to happen.

At night—when the dim light dims further according to ancient municipal codes designed to save electricity during wars that ended centuries ago—you can sometimes see the city’s true shape revealed in the patterns of failing lights and newly formed waterfalls. Calmaris isn’t dying, exactly. It’s more accurate to say that cities and water are finally negotiating the terms of their long engagement here, and water has centuries of experience in these matters.

The treaty process looks like collapse from inside the timeframe of individual humans, but geological patience makes different judgments. To the limestone slowly precipitating in abandoned subway tunnels, this is the beginning of a beautiful cathedral. To the mercury collecting in pools at the bottom of forgotten elevator shafts, this is finally coming home. To the tiny, adaptable organisms colonizing both, this is the Garden of Eden with industrial lighting and excellent transport links.

Stand here long enough—long enough to let your sense of up and down adjust, long enough to stop interpreting every drip as danger, every rust-colored stain as evidence of structural failure—and you might begin to see it too. Not the city that was built, nor the city that is falling apart, but the city that is becoming something else entirely. Something that belongs to itself again, at last.

But for now, you have appointments to keep in districts that may or may not still exist by the time you reach them. The concierge has arranged a guide—one of the amphibious children who navigate both dry codes and wet spaces with equal fluency, who will take your payment in currency that hasn’t been officially minted yet and doesn’t mind if parts of your itinerary require scuba gear.

Welcome to Calmaris. Mind the gap between floors. Mind the floors between gaps. Mind everything, actually. The city certainly does.
