The place begins as a single thought, then spills out like water from a cracked bowl.

It is a settlement without a name, built into the hollowed-out ribs of something vast and dead — perhaps a hill-sized beast, though no one remembers with certainty. Its bones are blackened wood or fossilized cartilage, and they arch overhead like the ribs of an inverted ship, nearly touching at their center. Water drips through cracks between them, not from rain — there is no sky here, only a ceiling of damp, breathing darkness — but from some internal source. Condensation, or memory.

The air is cool, always cool, thick enough to drink. It smells of iron and wet wool and something faintly fungal, like bread left too long under cloth. The water pools in shallow, uneven channels carved by decades of footsteps. People walk in zigzags to avoid them, but never succeed entirely. Their feet are always slightly wet.

There are no trees, no grass, no moss even — only stone, bone, and the things people have brought or made. The ground is packed earth layered over older floors: tile, then wood, then something smooth like porcelain, none matching. The walls are not walls, but constructions: woven mats hung from pegged vertebrae, walls of stacked pottery shards mortared with boiled hoof-glue, curtains of dried reeds threaded with hair.

The place is small — no larger than a wide valley turned on its side, then compressed. You can walk from edge to edge in an hour if you know the way. But few ever do. The paths twist and double back, squeezed between stalls and sleeping cubbies and communal hearths without chimneys, their smoke rising in lazy coils until it joins the damp overhead. Everything is close enough to touch: the smell of another person's breath, the warmth of their cooking pot, the murmur of dreams leaking through walls too thin to be called such.

The people — and they are people, mostly — live stacked like bees in cells too small for lying flat. Most slept curled long before they learned to crawl. They speak a language with no word for alone and twenty-seven for “the space between two sleeping bodies.” Children learn to walk by gripping the hems of older children's skirts. Everyone knows everyone's scars by shape if not by story.

They eat what they can cultivate between the drip-lines: pale mushrooms that grow in darkness, algae scraped from wall cracks and pressed into sheets, small white fish caught in the pools with nets made of hair and bone. Meat is rare and never wasted; every kill is communal, every death a feast, every feast a funeral. They do not bury their dead. They sing them into the walls, pressing their bones into gaps where the structure groans loudest, whispering their names so the house remembers.

They have tools but no machines: knives chipped from flint scavenged from deeper layers, needles carved from fish spine, lamps that burn fat in shallow stone bowls. Fire is precious and kept small, tended like an invalid child. They have no metal, though sometimes someone digs up a rusted thing — a hinge, a buckle, half a blade — and everyone gathers to stare at it like prophecy.

The animals are few and strange: white toads that blink sideways, translucent worms that glow faintly when hungry, beetles that roll balls of waste larger than themselves and sculpt them into shapes resembling tiny, perfect houses (no one knows why). Birds never come here. There were bats once, the elders say, but they left when the walls grew too crowded.

The place is always changing, but slowly, like a thought forming. New corridors appear when old ones collapse. Rooms shrink overnight, growing warmer as they do, until families wake pressed together like spoons and take it as a gift. Sometimes a wall will begin to whisper — syllables without meaning, rhythm without words — and children will gather to listen until their parents pull them away. The whispers never repeat.

There is no day or night, only the slow pulse of communal sleep and waking. Time is measured by hunger, by water levels, by the growth of a certain fungus that blooms once every sixty-three sleep cycles and tastes faintly of regret.

The most sacred place is the highest point — a platform built into the place where the ribs curve closest together, reachable only by climbing a ladder made of living children who stand unmoving, hands linked, until the one at the top has prayed and climbed down again to take their place at the bottom. There, if you press your ear to the bone, some say you can hear the place breathing.

They do not leave. There is no stable outside. There are only stories: of emptiness too wide to walk, of light too sharp to look at, of hunger that walks on two legs and speaks in your own voice. They tell these stories to their children at the edge of sleep, the same way they tell stories of warm fields and open sky — as fairy tales, not warnings.

And yet sometimes, rarely, someone does leave. They slip away during the communal dream, when the walls are thickest and the water loudest. They take nothing but a bone knife and a handful of dried fish. They walk until the paths end, then keep walking through places where the floor gives way to nothing, where the air turns cold and thin, where even the water stops dripping.

Sometimes, much later, their bones come back. Always clean, always arranged in the shape of something familiar — a house, a hearth, a hand reaching up. No one knows how. No one asks.

The place accepts them. The place remembers.

Everything else is just water, and time, and the slow, patient sound of walls learning to speak.
