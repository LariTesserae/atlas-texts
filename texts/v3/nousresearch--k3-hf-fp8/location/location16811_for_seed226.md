Midway up the thousand-fluted cliffs of the Lachrymal range lies the Carven Quarter, a city that began yesterday and will be finished tomorrow. Clouds walk here at eye level, their cool bellies dragging moisture across raw basalt terraces where no moss has yet found purchase. Below, the world drops into mist and rumor; above, only the wind’s hammer and the faint echo of tomorrow’s carvers shaping the next tier of dwellings.

Water rules here in negotiations rather than dominion. From the cliff’s porous bones seep slow tears—mineral-rich rivulets barely thicker than wire—that form silver hieroglyphs across virgin stone before plunging into the emptiness. From these slender streams the first artificial things were made: hollow reed gutters braided with copper hair from lightning-struck birds, guiding the cliff’s grief into communal basins carved before the carving of floors or walls. Every morning children walk these gutter-lines playing bone flutes to plead for generous weeping; when the wind is right, the entire city sings in mournful harmony with itself.

The vegetation comes careful-brave up here where soil fears to settle. Between mattress-thick lichens in maroon and umber spread networks of rootless vineglass—transparent tubular plants that filter minerals from mist and glow faint lavender at dusk when full. They cluster around the hundred doorless doorways of the Quarter, each entrance a negative space still being argued between stone and sculptor, outlined only by these living ropes that knot and unknot daily as the community debates the proper threshold ritual for spaces not yet conceived. No leaf touches leaf unless driven by storms; each plant maintains its own exact sphere of atmospheric propriety, creating an invisible garden more ordered than any planted one.

Coolness here has texture and agency. It pools in the hollow quarry-squares where tomorrow’s market will manifest, sits on benches that exist as carved intentions rather than objects. Locals wear it draped around shoulders like living cloaks—actually colonies of thermophobic microbes traded between families like heirlooms, each symbiote-cloud producing faint music when temperature gradients increase. Walk too quickly through these cool spots and your passage will scream brief arpeggios that the city elders record for weather prophecies.

Scale plays tricks married to honesty. Though you stand in a settlement—hear now the morning’s communal stone-song rising from nine hundred throats directing nine hundred chisels in the northern depression—no two buildings allow themselves to be fully present at once. The carvers work in quantum phases: the smithy exists fully only between hammer strikes, its chimney’s smoke solid enough to climb only while someone watches it with belief. Your rented room is currently represented by a carved outline on the cliff face, a pitcher drop-carving itself into existence within its implied window at the geological pace of one crystal layer per breath you take standing here reading its sign. This is not illusion but etiquette; architecture must never presume upon the future’s consent.

Sound builds the city more than hands. The relentless loudness isn’t chaotic but choral—every strike of chisel answers across stone mouths shaped precisely to catch and return specific frequencies. A child’s novice tap in the apprentice canyon becomes, after twenty-seven planned reflections, the bass undertone shaping the archbishop’s private chapel three cliffs north. Conversations must be sung in counterpoint to the ambient construction fugue; misunderstandings manifest physically as small avalanches of misformed plinths that devotees collect to carve into prayer beads.

Below you in the fog-vaulted streets—streets being carved upward toward your position at the rate of one handspan per lunar cycle—move the rare punctuations of fauna. Here comes the parchment mule, its hide tattooed with city ordinances that flake off as it walks, leaving legal trails for scavenger birds too theoretical to exist yet. Listen: those aren’t hoofbeats but its hollow bones ringing against cobblestones still being debated by the Civic Texture Committee. The only other regular animal is the echo-eater, a blind mole-dolphin hybrid that swims through rock using acoustic pressure, surfacing in public squares to devour accumulated sound pollution and excrete fresh silence that potters mold into temporary dishes.

Technology here peaks at the complexity of questions. A physician might prescribe hours of listening to a particular waterfall in a specific posture, letting the water’s mineral song dissolve illness crystals in your bones. Farmers plant fields of echoing shapes that grow neither grain nor fruit but the memory of them—food for a later season when hunger itself has evolved into something different. All tools are borrowed from tomorrow’s possibilities; a hammerhead today might be a baby’s rattle yesterday, will be part of a cathedral bell tomorrow. The only permanent invention is the City Not Yet Carved, its blueprints encoded in the way dust never quite settles here.

For newcomers, the strangest law is that of personal erosion. Stand too long admiring the view (which doesn’t exist yet but is being vigorously carved into existence behind your back) and you will find your shadow beginning to adhere to the stone. With sufficient inattention, tourists become minor features—useful statues marking good contemplation spots, slowly incorporated into the municipal design. The city catalog lists twelve thousand such volunteer columns, each plaque bearing only the inscription “They came to see what wasn’t ready.”

At sunset, if suns set here, all work ceases for the Hour of Apology. Every carver faces their in-progress stone and bows three times: once for presumption, once for what has been taken away, once for what has not yet been given back. In the hush that follows, you might hear it—the faintest sound beneath sounds—tomorrow’s city clearing its throat in preparation for tonight’s dreams of itself.

Would you like to rent an empty space with excellent potential for views? The price is one small regret, carved into this blank stone. No promise it’ll still be here when you return to regret it. Payment accepted in memories of rain, tomorrow’s words for today’s emotions, or the exact sound of your heart learning to sing in time with something that doesn’t exist yet.
