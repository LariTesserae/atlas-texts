The city sits at the edge of a high plateau, where water breaks from the earth in hundreds of small springs and runs down fresh-cut channels in the stone. The plateau itself is newly risen — geologically speaking, a sudden child — and its edges still bear the sharpness of recent birth: cliffs of raw sandstone and basalt dropping sheer into mist-filled valleys miles below.

Water is everywhere here. It beads on every surface, collects in the hollows your feet make on the path, drips constantly from the edges of roofs and doorways. The air drinks it in and gives it back as perpetual mist and gentle rain, never enough to drown, always enough to notice. Stone steps worn already in their youth are slick with moss that shouldn't have time to grow here. The city's builders — whoever they were, whoever they are — shaped their settlement around each spring and seep, coaxing the water into stone gutters that sing with quiet voices as they carry it down through the terraces.

The buildings themselves are strange in their simplicity. Monolithic blocks of pale yellow stone quarried from the plateau's heart, each one hollowed by patient hands using only tools of water and stone against stone. There is no mortar, no metal, no sharp corners still sharp. Every edge has been softened already by water's patient work. The shapes are basic geometries repeated with small variations: cubes and cylinders and half-ovals stacked or joined with spaces left between for the wind and water to flow through.

Nothing here seems finished, yet nothing seems incomplete.

The vegetation is sparse but deliberate. Small trees grow in the inevitable cracks where stone has already begun its long return to grains — thin-leaved things with silver bark and roots that grip like fingers. Between the buildings and along the water channels are beds of plants that don't quite resemble ferns or grasses or succulents but seem borrowed from all three: low, tough growths with roots submerged always in moving water and leaves that catch light and moisture alike. Each leaf holds at its center a tiny pool, and in these pools grow smaller plants still, green islands on green islands.

There are no animals. No birds wheel in the mist overhead, no insects drone by the water, no creatures great or small disturb the dust that shouldn't exist here — ground stone fine as flour that gathers in corners and circles out again on drafts of air. The city makes its own sounds: water speaking to stone in a thousand languages, the occasional distant note like stone chime or wooden bell responding in tones too deep to feel, the soft hiss of mist condensing and falling.

The scale of the place is difficult to grasp. From any single point within it, you can see only a few buildings in any direction before mist and water's curtain intervene. But walk in any direction and the city continues, following the plateau's edge but never turning inward, never ending. Sometimes a street opens suddenly onto empty space, no railing or edge marking where stone ends and sky begins. The mist hides what lies below, but sometimes it clears for a breath and you glimpse distant peaks rising through clouds like other, older plateaus far below.

Everything here is in motion and everything remains the same. Water flows down but never runs dry. Plants grow but never seed. Stone wears away but never disappears. The mist lifts and falls but never clears or thickens. Time moves differently here — not stopped, exactly, but caught in eddies and pools like water itself, flowing but never arriving anywhere new.

The city's people — if they are people, if they were ever here — have left their marks but not themselves. Works half-completed in every dwelling: stone tools laid carefully beside unfinished blocks of stone; molds for things that might be bowls or might be buildings; drawings etched onto floors that show the city from above, but with towers and bridges and shapes that don't exist in the city you see. In one room a fire pit holds charcoal that should be cold ash but still gives off gentle warmth when you pass your hand near. In another, water drips into a stone basin with perfect regularity, each drop raising its tiny wavelet that crosses the surface and dies against the opposite side exactly as the next drop falls.

Some have tried to stay here. Their traces remain in small shelters added onto stone walls, in attempts to cultivate familiar plants that died or changed into forms that grew only water and mist. In the books and records they left behind — paper turned to leather, ink turned to rust — they write of dreams that grew too vivid, of water that tasted like memory, of time that pooled in the hollows of morning until it became impossible to remember what needed doing next, or why it mattered to do anything at all.

Now only the water comes and goes freely, rising eternal from the stone's young heart, carving its slow path to the edge's sudden drop, carrying nothing away but the sound of its own flowing.

And in the quiet between these sounds, the city waits — not old enough yet to be ruins, too young to be finished, caught perfectly between the taking shapes and the giving way, teaching water how to build and stone how to fall with infinite patience and no haste at all.
