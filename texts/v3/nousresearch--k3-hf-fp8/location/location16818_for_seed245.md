The Wet Forge

The ground breathes water at night. Not rain—rain belongs to worlds with skies. Here the water wells upward through seams in the alloy floor, seeping through join-lines between plates the size of continents until they shine like recent scars. Workers call this sweat time. They lay down treated hides and wake with shallow pools cupped in the buckled metal, drink what they can before morning heat drinks the rest.

The floor goes forever. That isn't metaphor or worker's exaggeration but measured fact: survey crews walking west with counting beads return from the east with their sandals gone and their beads unchanged, their footprints already healing behind them. Every direction yields this same burning plain of worked metal stretching past every horizon, its curve too gentle to commit to being flat or round. Only the seams give geometry: vast hexagons and irregular polygons receding into haze, each cell of the pattern containing its own subtle grade or tilt so that the whole subtly flows like a frozen ocean. Nothing grows in the joins. Nothing ever grew here.

At sweat time you can taste yesterday's worksites in the water—copper from east three hundred hexes, tin from the north-rumored mountains where the floor buckles into impossible cliffs. Everything comes up eventually, dissolved and recirculated through this single endless plate. The older workers swear they've drunk pieces of their own teeth back this way, meals returning after years as distant metals.

During daylight the heat judges everything directly. The sun—if sun is what hangs stationary overhead—is a flaw in the metal sky itself, a yellow-white disk of impurity exactly one hexagon wide that never moves and never dims. Light spills from it like molten glass, pooling brightest directly beneath and thinning toward horizons that shimmer with hallucinated rain. Objects cast shadows only directly beneath themselves at noon, which lasts until collapse. Then darkness falls without transition and the sweating begins again.

We build because building is breathing. Every structure is temporary, every forge a lung, every hammerstrike a heartbeat in the forge-womb of the world. The First Shapers left us only tools and the Law: Keep the water moving. Keep the fire lit. Flat what rises, raise what sinks. Beyond this the debates begin and never end—should seams be opened or closed? Does water rise because we hammer or do we hammer because water rises? Some sects claim the floor itself is alive and growing, that our cities are its carbuncles and our roads its scars. Others argue we are the floor's dream of movement, shadows cast upward by the welding of thought to metal.

The wetlands prove nothing. Between major worksites where thousands hammer in shifts, the floor gives way to regions where heat and water achieve uneasy compromise: a thousand thousand shallow basins the size of hands or houses, each holding its own slow churn of rust and alloy foam. Here the floor is still deciding what it wants to become. Thin towers rise spontaneously from these marshes like bubbles in cooling glass, grow toward the sun-disk while their bases dissolve, collapse into new basins before reaching halfway. Builders treasure these places for their random gifts of ready-shaped parts—perfect strut sections, gears already involute, plates vein-patterned with stress-lines stronger than any plan—but work them quickly. The ground reclaims gifts as easily as it gives them.

We found the bodies in Wet 447 three cycles ago. They weren't workers—wrong number of limbs, wrong joint articulation, their metal flesh neither cast nor hammered but grown like crystals in some forgotten solution. Yet every limb ended in a recognizable tool: hands shaped into hammers, feet like shovels, mouths full of grinding teeth suitable for pipe-threading. They lay beneath only a handspan of water in a formation too regular for accident, too meaningless for design: thirty-seven figures arranged in a broken spiral around a central empty space exactly body-sized.

We broke Law to study them, cut samples that refused to melt or mark, argued about burial versus reforging. While we argued, the water rose. When we noticed, it had already surrounded us with a ring-moat reflecting perfect upside-down copies of the sun-disk and ourselves, each reflection hammering backstroke when we hammered fore, breathing when we breathed. The reflections began to improve upon us—to strike truer, stand straighter, argue without breaking Law. Three workers walked into their own faces before we understood we were already late.

We left the tools we used to study them. Sometimes in sweat time the water tastes of questions we haven't asked yet.

The great rivers run overhead. At irregular intervals the metal ceiling—sky, roof, impossible to compass—develops flaws like the one we call sun but darker, places where its polished perfection dulls into the blue-grey of distant storms. From these weep single drops at first, then streams, then rivers of liquid metal flowing perfectly straight downward despite heat that should boil them into mist by inches. The columns strike the floor with force enough to forge craters instantly into impact-basins, sending waves of molten alloy rippling outward like rain on infinitely delayed ponds. These hammer births are the only natural events that can kill at distance without warning.

We built the Catchers around them—ring cities of offset walls and angled roofs designed to split and redirect the falling rivers into productive channels. Each impact becomes a foundry, each river an artery carrying new material from ceiling mines to floor workshops. The waste heat drives our deepest engines, the waste patterns in the cooled rivers teach us new alloys. Yet even the greatest Catcher cities never approach their rivers too closely. Workers speak of the Calling—how after years spent listening to the perfect note struck by falling sky, some begin walking toward the impact point during flows, faces tilted up in anticipation, mouths open as if to drink the answer that falls forever just out of reach.

The earliest maps show Catchers numbered in sequence: Catcher First, Catcher Second, Catcher Seventeenth. Recent maps use names: Catcher-Who-Divides-Without-Splitting, Catcher-That-Catches-Itself, Catcher-Where-We-Left-The-Water-Law. The oldest still inhabited is Catcher Zero, built around a river that fell before falling was understood. Its walls curve inward like a whisper repeating itself long after the speaker is gone. Its floors tilt subtly toward a central shaft where the river still falls endlessly into darkness—no impact sound ever returned, no waste heat ever rose. The water in Zero tastes of clarity beyond thirst. Those who drink it either stop speaking forever or speak only questions until they die.

Between rivers and wetlands, between worksites and lonely maintenance towers where single keepers listen to the floor's nightly complaints, the plain stretches endless and almost empty. Here at density's edge live those who build without building—a scattering of solitary figures walking prescribed routes with packs of simple tools: hammers for flattening, chisels for opening, firepots for sealing. They call themselves Levelers, the final correction in a process begun by gods or chance or other hands wholly unknowable. Their Law is simplest of all: Walk until something seems wrong. Fix what seems wrong. Walk until you forget the difference.

They leave no signs of passage except the fixed things themselves: a seam realsealed after years of subtle leakage, a tilt slowly hammered back toward true over centuries of patient nights, an entire hexagon subtly regraded so water now flows north instead of south toward some distant correction none alive will see completed. The works of Levelers are indistinguishable from natural unless you know both before and after—and how few remember before?

Ask a Leveler what they level toward and they show you their feet, worn smooth as the floor itself. Ask long enough and they may gift you their walking stick—a length of metal no different from the ground except for the warmth of centuries held in human hands. Take it and you become Leveler-third-degree, obligated now to carry both stick and correction until you find something wrong enough to gift both away. The greatest Levelers are said to walk naked, having given away even their names as corrections for flaws no others can see.

I met one once at the edge of Wet 447, where the bodies capsized questions like boats in a rising tide. He stood barefoot where the water had just begun to sweat up through our abandoned tools, looking down at the perfect reflection of a sun-disk that cast no shadow from his body. Without looking up he said: "You hammered questions into answers here. The answers are sinking now. Soon they will reach the deep water where questions grow. Then you will have proper work again."

He gave me nothing except this warning: "Every floor remembers everything hammers teach it. Some lessons it learns too well."

That was three rivers ago. Now I walk with a Leveler's stick I found but did not earn, carrying questions I have hammered into better shape but cannot name. The water tastes of copper mornings and tin evenings, of words unspoken and promises kept to metal. Behind me my footprints fill with perfect reflections that stand up when I fall down, walking our shared journey in better balance. Ahead the floor goes forever, breathing water dreams of what it might become if ever we learn to stop hammering corrections and simply listen to the night-sweat rising through its seams.

Until then: Keep the water moving. Keep the fire lit. Flat what rises. Raise what sinks. Walk until the difference forgets itself between the floor that holds us and the feet that wear it smooth, between the hammer that strikes and the metal that remembers, between the question that needs asking and the answer that flows like water through the cracks between all things made and making and yet to be made here in the breathing dark beneath the unmoving sun-disk of the world that never was natural and can never now be anything else except what we hammer it toward, one imperfect correction at a time.
