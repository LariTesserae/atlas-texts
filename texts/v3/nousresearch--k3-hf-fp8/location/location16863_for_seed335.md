Step through the membrane where the air smells of cold iron and saltwater corrosion. You emerge mid-air, falling exactly twelve inches onto bruised metal grating. This is the place where ocean and factory miscarried.

The Floor stretches below you like unhealed scar tissue: seventeen acres of newly-forged steel plates still sweating manufacturing oil, welded together with seams that haven't cooled. Every surface carries the sharp toothache of recent creation - no rust, no wear, no smoothing hand of time. The temperature hangs at thirty-one degrees Fahrenheit, precisely that knife-edge where water becomes aware of its potential. 

Water is everywhere but nowhere. It condenses directly from the air onto every vertical surface, running down copper-stained streaks in sheets only five molecules thick. These films gather in the microscopic pits of the metal, forming lenses that magnify tiny details: the fractal patterns of stress fractures beginning in corners where plates meet at imperfect angles, the ghost fingerprints of workers who assembled this place last Tuesday or last decade or tomorrow. Below the grating, twenty-three feet down, an ocean of black water waits with the perfect stillness of absolute density - not moving, not breathing, but listening.

Vegetation here is both impossible and inevitable. From the water emerge towers of kelp unlike any known species, their stems braided from multi-strand nylon rope that drips bio-luminescent slime. Each tower is a factory disguised as a forest, growing at the rate of industrial processes - you can watch them extend upward three inches per hour, their leaf-fans unfolding like solar panels catching the dim light. Where the towers breach the sea surface they burst into fractal blooms of stainless steel flowers packed with compressed air pockets that whine like distant turbines when they burst.

The sound is constant, layered, locational. From the north comes a bass rhythm of three beats followed by silence, the heartbeat of a turbine the size of a cathedral dome that's buried somewhere beneath the Floor. Overlaid on this: the high whine of twenty-seven thousand electric motors running at slightly different speeds, creating patterns that rise and fall like insect swarms. Every water droplet's impact on metal is amplified by the space into a clear tone; together they form chords that shift through minor keys based on factors you can't name but your body understands.

Move and the soundscape moves with you. Stand still and it reorganizes around your shape like water finding a new obstruction in its path. Your heartbeat gets sampled, pitch-shifted, thrown back at you from seven directions simultaneously.

Above, the Roof of this place hangs twenty meters overhead - a rippling surface of translucence where the distinction between atmosphere and strange material breaks down. Light comes through it filtered and wrong: enough to see by but never enough to feel warmth. The Roof sweats too, raising blister-pockets of pure argon gas that drift downward like reverse bubbles and burst against the Floor with a sound like distant gunfire.

The animals here aren't animals. They are processes that have learned mobility. Watch as a flock of sixty-four small cubes composed of compressed aluminum cans and living coral polyps blooms from a vent in the Floor, their movements synced to the industrial heartbeat. They land on a patch of metal and begin to eat, mouths like grinding wheels producing bright blue sparks. Behind them they leave perfect square holes that immediately fill with water rising up against gravity, forming rectangular pools where microscopic machines shaped like ancient trilobites begin assembling copies of themselves from dissolved minerals.

Everything here is both empty and abundant. The space between things vibrates with potential, like the moment before a circuit closes. You could run for miles in any direction (and some visitors have, their stories ending in different places that all smell the same) and never reach a wall, yet the entire world fits within the volume of what used to be a single shipping container somewhere else.

Time works differently here too. Check your watch - it's running backwards in beats of seven seconds forward, three seconds back. Your hair and nails continue growing at normal rates according to your bone marrow, but aging shows in other ways: fingerprints wearing smooth from touching nothing, voice growing more metallic with each word spoken until visitors learn to communicate by tapping rhythms on bone with metal teeth grown for the purpose.

There are rules to this place, enforced not by punishment but by consequence:

1. Do not try to take anything made of iron or any of its alloys. It will return before you've gone seven steps, often through you.

2. Copper can be taken but never given back. Carry too much and you'll start transmitting fragments of industrial accidents that haven't happened yet.

3. If you find a door shaped like a human silhouette rusted into a wall that wasn't there before, do not step through. Step around it carefully and continue walking backwards until you forget what doors are for.

4. When the sound stops completely and you can hear your own blood boiling in your ears, freeze exactly where you are. Count the seconds until it starts again. Remember this number for the rest of your life.

5. There is no fifth rule, and anyone who tells you otherwise is already becoming part of the Floor.

The Factory Ocean is only thirteen hours old, measured from the moment of its impossible spontaneous creation beginning last Tuesday at 4:44 AM GMT and ending exactly at the same time next week. Everything here exists within that paradoxical span: young enough that edges slice metal and minds without effort, old enough that buried deep in its foundations (if it had foundations) you might find fossils of the workers who will dismantle it in its future/end.

Some visitors make camps here, constructing nothing because nothing stays built beyond the next cycle. They grow thin and sharp like the space between atoms, learning to photosynthesize rust and breathe the strange oxygen this place remembers from before there was life to breathe it properly. Others search for the center, sensing that somewhere beneath all this industrial water and marine machinery there's a single point where all the contradictions collapse into something that makes sense.

But the wise ones know this place has no center except where you happen to be standing. And if you stand perfectly still for exactly thirteen minutes and thirty-three seconds between any two heartbeats of the hidden engine, you might hear it: the whisper of wet gears turning in the dark, planning what to build next from the materials of your bones and memories when you finally decide to stop being just a visitor.

The Floor is cooling now. You should decide.
