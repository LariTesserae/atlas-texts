The Balcony Boroughs of Mal-Kreekt

Water everywhere, yet none to drink. The city clings to verticality like moss on a gravestone, nine hundred balcony-houses stacked on a granite cliffside so tall its base dissolves into myth. Every surface weeps. Moisture pours from porous stone, from carved gargoyles whose mouths never closed, from the thousand hollow spines of giant fossilized sea-urchins repurposed as downspouts. Water falls upward too, caught in great nets of copper wire strung between buildings to harvest the inverse rain that rises each noon when the sun hits the cliff-face warms the waterlogged stone wrong somehow.

Vegetation holds conference only in crevices. Black orchids with roots made of copper wire coil around rusted drainpipes. Mosses grow backwards, starting as luminous green clouds that condense overnight into tiny stone-hard nodules by dawn. Children collect these, trade them like marbles, smash them open to find impossible fossils inside: tiny fish with human fingers, dragonfly wings etched with maps of cities that haven't been built yet.

Temperature hovers at the exact point where breath becomes architecture. Every exhale freezes instantly into delicate lattices that shatter against the next breath, creating perpetually churning clouds of ice-sharp confetti around every living thing. People wear masks not for warmth but to prevent accidental self-mutilation from breathing. The air itself is a slow-motion knife fight.

Elevation defines everything. Your address is your vertigo score. The rich live where oxygen grows thick enough to swim in, their houses inverted chandeliers hanging down into breathable pockets. The poor cling to the exalt-zone where every lungful feels like inhaling knives made of fog. Time moves slower up here, or perhaps just feels that way when every heartbeat has to fight gravity and cold and thin air and the constant soundless roar of water falling in all directions.

Erosion is civic policy. Buildings are not maintained but carefully allowed to collapse according to ancient algorithms carved on tablets too heavy to lift. Each district appoints a Decay Warden who oversees the precise rate of collapse, ensures that when your neighbor's left wall falls it will become your new roof exactly when needed. The city is a conversation between gravity and patience that has been ongoing since before language. Streets are vertical scars left by particularly eloquent arguments between stone and time.

Scale warps at the edges. A single room might contain whole families through clever use of perspective mirrors and gravity wells. The Grand Market fits inside a teacup, but only if you approach it from the south-east corner while thinking about betrayal. Conversely, some closets contain entire forgotten districts where roads loop back on themselves in Möbius strips and the sun is just a rumor passed down through increasingly skeptical generations. Children know these shortcuts instinctively, forget them upon adulthood like dreams.

Density of life defies both measurement and sanity. Every cubic inch teems: microscopic water bears in the ice crystals of your eyelashes; lichen colonies spelling out prophecies in dead languages across your coat buttons; tiny blind birds nesting in the marrow of your bones (you can hear them singing when you stand perfectly still between breaths); shadow-puppet civilizations reenacting the city's entire history on the backs of your retinas every time you blink. To be alone here requires surgical intervention and a special license from the Department of Solitude.

The central aqueduct cries every anniversary of the day it carried poison instead of water, its tears forming the tiny river called Regret that powers the city's only clock.
