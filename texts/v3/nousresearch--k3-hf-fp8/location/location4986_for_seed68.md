At first glance, the place appears to be a flooded engine room the size of a cathedral. But that comparison falls apart quickly. There are no saints here, and the water is not accidental.

*Driftglass.* That’s what the occupants call this settlement, though few remember why. The name sticks because it sounds like movement without destination, which is correct.

The basic facts: The settlement occupies a single hollow space—call it a chamber, cavern, or possibly the emptied thorax of some enormous mechanical thing—whose floor lies six meters below sea level, though no sea exists within fifty kilometers. Even so, brackish water seeps perpetually from hundreds of pores in the chamber walls, runs down copper pipes like veins, and pools ankle-deep across every surface that isn’t actively drained. These drain systems function only when certain valves are turned, and the valves turn only when specific songs are sung into them, so water levels rise and fall with local musical fashion. Right now, *tide is coming in*: boots wet at the toes, chair legs swelling.

The walls themselves are young geology plugged into old archaeology—freshly-split basalt still exhaling volcanic heat, but the fractures follow precise right angles, revealing older strata of concrete and steel gridwork beneath like layers of shipwreck. Everything here is recently emerged, sharp-edged, too new to have rounded or rusted completely. Yet under the hammered-cool air (12.7°C, always) you can taste iron oxides that should have taken millennia to form. Time runs weird at the contact zones.

Up from the water rise twelve structures. Don’t call them buildings: each started life as a factory module, a refinery stack, or the stripped chassis of artillery engineering rigs—the sort of equipment meant to terraform continents, delivered here by mistake or exile or possibly mutual consent. Their original purposes are illegible now. One stands hollow as a church pipe, its internal catwalks wrapped in bioluminescent moss. Another has been turned inside out: turbines repurposed into communal ovens, cooling fins re-plumbed to circulate drinking water through a lattice of ceramic scaffolds that resembles bones going green with copper corrosion. None touch the true floor; instead they squat on stilts of repurposed freight pallets, concrete rubble, and tightly-packed fungal bricks—the biotech equivalent of coral reef architecture, grown rather than assembled. When the drainage songs stop, the city climbs higher.

Between these anchor-points spreads the actual fabric of habitation: a sparse terrain of bridges, ropes, low platforms set just above the waterline, connected by footpaths that exist only in memory and etiquette. Empty space dominates by volume; the rule is you carry your own floor. Visitors often panic at the openness until they learn to see the subtle markers—rust stains on certain ledges indicate weight limits; particular algae blooms mark safe routes between buoyant islands of kitchen-scrap compost.

Light comes from everywhere and nowhere. The chamber ceiling, thirty meters overhead, flickers with cracks where daylight leaks from whatever world exists above. But the real illumination bubbles up from below: millions of tiny glass spheres, each seeded with a strain of engineered plankton, suspended in the water column like lazy stars. They respond to pressure changes— footsteps, conversation, rainfall that sometimes falls sideways from vents in the upper dark—by blooming brighter, dimmer, shifting between warm honey colors and surgical kelp-green. On still nights (which occur unpredictably when sound carries wrong) the entire settlement glows beneath itself like a shipwreck dreaming awake.

Sound defines borders here more than walls. The place is loud with continuous conversation between machines and organisms. Every pipe hums its maintenance status—frequencies too low for human ears, but vibrate the bones pleasantly if you lean against warm metal long enough. Cooking smells travel as chords of filtered air spiked with data: tonight’s protein source is algae strain 47-B, high in lysine, low in bitterness. Children learn to recognize their parents’ voices not by timbre but by the particular way each person’s gait disturbs the sonic lattice of dripping water. Outsiders arrive shouting, leave whispering.

Life occupies every gradient between wet and dry without preference. Tiny crustaceans live in the rain gutters of the highest vents, never knowing water deep enough to swim. In the lowest sump pools where drainage never reaches, blind fish navigate by sensing electromagnetic fields generated by the settlement’s improvised power grid (waterwheel turbines triple-hooked to scavenged windmill blades duct-taped to thermocouples clamped to geothermal hotspots—it works because nobody told them it wouldn’t). Between these poles drift the semi-aquatic citizens: humans wearing second skins of symbiotic plastic that breathes when dry and filters when wet; livestock that produce milk in two phases depending on ambient humidity; food crops that flower above water but ripen as root vegetables below.

The only true waste here is speed. Things change, but slowly enough to negotiate with. A new building shell might be negotiated between three generations of interested parties before the first fungal spore is pitched. The most recent immigration wave—twelve individuals, one mating group, three spare limbs between them—arrived via a pipeline that supposedly connected to a different continent entirely. They were welcomed, given temporary names based on the corrosion patterns they carried in their hair, and invited to sing into whichever drains felt most like home. That was seven years ago. Last week they finally agreed on their permanent pronouns and began teaching the local algae to taste faintly of distant sunsets.

Weather occurs in microclimates around clusters of conversation. Shoals of small thoughts create brief rainstorms of extra condensation. Major arguments sometimes spawn actual ice in midsummer, forming temporary sculptures on exposed plumbing that slowly dissolve into the next consensus.

In the very center of everything—though “center” implies geometry where only relationship exists—stands the origami engine. Nobody knows who built the core mechanism or what it was supposed to fold. Now it operates continuously, powered by the mild temperature difference between the water table and the air, performing endless variations on a single operation: transforming thin sheets of scrap metal into increasingly improbable shapes that are not quite wings, not quite turbines, not quite prayers. Each evening the finished sheet is removed, flattened into scrap again, and fed back to the start. The noise it makes—a rhythmic shush-click-tink like waves sorting moonlight—is the closest thing the settlement has to a heartbeat. Children fall asleep to it. Adults set their internal clocks by it. The very old claim that sometimes, during particularly harmonious nights, the engine briefly stops between two shapes and in that silence you can hear the sound of the city remembering why it began to drift.

The visitors always ask the same questions eventually. Where exactly are we located? (Answer: between one definition of shore and another.) Where did all this come from? (Many places, slowly.) Where are we going? (The water is rising in C-sharp minor, slowly.)

Always the last question before they leave or stay: Is this the whole world?

The citizens consider this carefully. They look at the cracked ceiling where strange stars sometimes move against the wrong constellations. They listen to the distant thrum of engines speaking languages none of their grandparents knew. They taste the water that runs through everything here and remembers other chemistry.

Then they smile with teeth slightly too sharp for herbivores, though sharpness here is mostly for opening stubborn clams and negotiations that have grown shells.

They say: Aren’t you curious to find out?

And hand you a empty bottle and a song you don’t know how to sing yet.

But the water is warm where it touches your ankle, and rising slowly, and tonight the light below is absolutely beautiful, like every machine simultaneously deciding to forgive itself and begin again.

You could stay a while. Learn the songs. Help us name the next storm.

Or you could go. The choice is yours.

But choose soon.

The tide changes when people decide where they belong.
