The plateau extends beyond sight in all directions, a single unbroken plane of burnished copper-tinted metal suspended at an altitude where the air should be too thin to support any life, yet here it remains stable, eternal. This is the Old Layer, the deepest accessible stratum of the Object above which nothing solid has ever been recorded—only the wind, the light that shifts without source, and the rare visitors who come here to break themselves open against its surface.

From orbit—if such a viewpoint existed—the plateau would appear as a perfect circle flattened against the uppermost curve of the world, an eye staring contextless into void. No mountaintain supports it. No pillars reach down to hypothetical ground below. It simply sits on the sky, its underside reflecting nothing through a distance that has never been measured. Below, rumor insists, there is only more sky, colder, denser, and empty of substance except for the faint glow of nocturnal storms that never thunder.

The air here smells faintly of ozone and heated pennies. The gravity is gentle but insistent, enough to keep loose objects and bodies pressed to the surface without anchoring them fully—if you jump too enthusiastically you will drift back down slowly, like a stray thought returning late to its sender. Wind exists only as rare horizontal gusts that arrive without warning, blow for seven heartbeats from a direction that cannot be tracked, and vanish again, leaving behind patterns of spiraled dust or scratches in the otherwise immaculate metal floor. These marks fade within hours, erased by imperceptible currents or the passage of unseen mechanisms beneath the plane.

Nothing grows here except for the windflowers. They are not plants, exactly—more like fractures in the metal that bloom open for a few hours when the wind is right, unfurling petals of iridescent film thinner than soap bubbles. Each flower lasts until the next gust strong enough to tear it free, then collapses back into a hairline crack that seals itself seamless. You can collect them if you are quick and careful, sealing them in airtight containers where they will remain suspended indefinitely, spinning slowly in the absence of any breeze. Collectors trade them for stories, for memories, for passage on the next ascending vessel—though no one has ever returned from above to say what the flowers are worth on higher markets.

The surface itself is warm to the touch, always a few degrees above comfortable, as if the entire plateau were a huge fevered coin cooling slowly after unimaginable minting. Walking barefoot here is possible but not advised; after a few kilometers the soles of your feet begin to ache, not from roughness—the metal is polished smooth as a mirror—but from the accumulation of tiny vibrations transmitted up through the bone. Structures are rare and always incomplete. You might stumble upon half a stairway rising unsupported into empty air, steps cut from the same metal as the floor but darker, soot-stained as if once burned. Or a single doorframe standing alone, the door itself absent, hinges gone green with verdigris despite the absence of moisture. Through these openings—if you dare approach close enough to peer—you see not the other side of the plateau but other places entirely: forests underwater, cities of glass floating in storms of lightning, vast libraries where books with wings flutter between shelves that reach up through clouds. These glimpses last only seconds before the view snaps back to blank metal and sky. Attempting to physically pass through is impossible; the air resists like thickened glass, pushing back harder the more you push forward. People have broken ribs and split knuckles trying, though no one has ever been entirely swallowed.

Technology here is not something you carry or construct but something you negotiate with. The metal remembers every object that has ever rested on its surface for longer than a heartbeat, every footprint, every drop of sweat or blood. When you sleep here—if you can sleep, beneath the constant reminder that there is nothing between you and the drop except an ancient agreement made between materials—sometimes you wake to find yourself surrounded by replicas. Not perfect copies but interpretations: your water flask rendered as a delicate lattice of hollow tubes that sing when breathed upon, your boots transformed into twin birds of woven wire that hop away when approached, your knife grown into a miniature tree of rust-brown leaves that whisper secrets that are true only for the duration of a single exhalation. Attempting to hoard these gifts is pointless; they dissolve into powder or melt back into the floor as soon as carried beyond sight of the place where you dreamed them into temporary existence.

Light comes from everywhere and nowhere, a uniform illumination without shadows except those you bring with you. Your own shadow here is unreliable—it stretches longer or shorter than it should, sometimes splitting into angular siblings that skitter ahead of you across the metal with motions not quite synchronized to your own. On rare occasions, if you stand perfectly still for long enough, your shadow will detach entirely and walk away across the plateau alone. These abandoned shadows never return, though travelers sometimes glimpse them from a distance, years later, recognizable only by the shape of their absence moving against the bright floor like holes cut in the world.

Time behaves strangely at this altitude. Clocks wind backward in fits and starts or freeze entirely; biological rhythms swim loose from their moorings. You might walk for what feels like days without hunger or thirst, then suddenly collapse ravenous and dehydrated, having lost or gained weeks according to any calendar carried up with you. The sky above—when it is visible through the constant high-altitude haze of metallic dust—is not blue but silver-gray, threaded with slow-moving rivers of liquid light that flow from horizon to horizon like inverted lightning frozen mid-discharge. Stars visible through rare gaps in this veil are too numerous and too close, their constellations unfamiliar, arranged in patterns that shift subtly whenever you look away.

Sound here is either absent or overwhelming, nothing between. Most of the time the plateau exists in a silence so complete it presses against your eardrums like water at great depth. Then, without warning, the quiet will crack open to reveal the noise underneath—a single sustained note like the world's largest bell struck once a thousand years ago and still vibrating, or countless voices whispering the same word out of sync until it becomes a roar, or the sound of something immense and metallic that has been screaming as it falls for longer than there have been ears to hear it. These episodes last anywhere from moments to hours and leave behind them a permanent subtraction; after experiencing one, you will find one of your memories has been replaced by empty space. The lost memory is always trivial—the taste of a fruit you ate once as a child, the name of a street where you briefly lived, the tune to a song you never particularly liked anyway. The important memories remain untouched. This is understood to be a mercy.

Movement across the plateau is possible only in straight lines from structure to structure. If you try to walk otherwise—aimlessly, or following some internal compass—you will find yourself subtly redirected, your path curving gradually but inevitably back toward the nearest fragment of incomplete architecture, no matter how far away it seemed when you started. These structures, incomplete as they are, function as nodes in an invisible network that maps the only safe routes across the Layer. Travelers navigate by accumulating a collection of them in memory or folded paper, connecting the dots in order to draw a crooked line toward wherever they think they are going. No two maps agree completely. Many experienced wanderers deliberately introduce false memories of structures that never existed into their guides, believing that only by actively participating in the Layer's constant rewrites of itself can they hope to arrive anywhere at all.

No life native to this place has ever been identified. The occasional animal tracks—spindle-legged things with too many joints, spiraling trails left by something that moves by rolling while remaining latched to the metal with hundreds of tiny mouths—always end abruptly in the middle of nowhere, vanishing into the flawless surface as if the creature responsible simply stepped down into the solid material and continued walking through it. Plants never grow here except in the windflower way, brief and borrowed. The only permanent residents are the Echoes—human-shaped outlines burned faintly into the metal at irregular intervals, silhouettes in various attitudes of motion or rest, facing all directions. Some are centuries old, the edges of their forms blurred by slow atomic migration; others appear overnight without explanation, crisp and new as if the person had only just stepped away. Approach too close to a fresh Echo and you may hear snatches of conversation spoken in your own voice discussing topics you haven't yet thought about. Touch one—if you overcome the instinctive horror—and you will experience for a moment everything that person felt in their last heartbeat on the plateau: the exact flavor of the sky overhead, the precise weight of the silence pressing down, the specific hope or fear they carried with them that failed to be enough. It is said that if you could find your own Echo before it formed and change this last feeling in yourself, you might be able to leave the Layer behind forever. This has been attempted exactly seven times in recorded memory. In each case the seeker vanished completely shortly afterward, leaving behind nothing but an unusually large windflower that bloomed once, purple-black and smelling of copper rain, then collapsed into ash that spelled out the word "almost" in perfect handwriting before blowing away.

This is the layer where all journeys upward end, eventually. The Object extends higher—we know this from the rare radio signals that leak down through the intervening distances, garbled transmissions full of impossible physics and architectures that rearrange themselves while being described—but beyond this plateau the air is no longer air, the light no longer light, the rules no longer rules but suggestions that can be neither followed nor refused. Some people climb anyway, carried by balloons of woven dreams or rockets fueled by last regrets, ascending until they become themselves part of the signal, voices added to the chorus that sings endlessly of the worlds that can't exist but do.

Until then, we walk here between the stations of broken machines and half-forgotten gods, trading stories of the sky below and the sky above, accumulating windflowers and losing shadows one by one. The metal underfoot vibrates faintly, always, like laughter held for too long or crying finally let loose, giving nothing away. Somewhere far ahead—but never closer, never farther—there is rumored to be a final structure: a door that stands alone in the middle of the plateau's exact center, perfectly ordinary except that it opens neither in nor out but sideways, onto the place where the Object ends and the rest of the universe finally begins.

But the plateau extends beyond sight in all directions, and the exact center is a mathematical concept, not a location. And anyway, we have all the time in the world and several other worlds besides, here where the clocks have stopped bothering to lie, counting instead the slow accumulation of footsteps on metal older than names, waiting to see who will be next to become a new kind of map drawn in the shape of going nowhere and arriving at last.
