An ancient vertical city sprawls across the inner wall of an impact crater so vast its rim is lost to curvature, the whole structure tilted eighty-three degrees from what once might have been called horizontal. You stand on what locals still call a "street" — a striated iron ledge half a kilometer wide where magnetic tram rails remain bolted through layers of condensed meteorite scoria. Above you, not sky but more city: terraces, gantries, sealed pagodas of basalt and brass stacked upward through kilometers of darkness until they vanish into a phosphorescent haze lingering at the world’s lungsick horizon.

Gravity here pulls at a shallow angle, not down into the crater’s throat but along it, pressing everything gently sideways toward the endlessly receding rim. Buildings were once built upright against this diagonal tug — now they slump like candles frozen mid-drip, their upper stories slung from massive chains anchored back into the deeper basalt behind. Spires that started as verticals have become bridges; every window faces what used to be “down” into the crater’s absent center. Water gauges still mark apartment walls seven meters above floor level, memorializing a lake that boiled away before memory began.

The air is warm, dry, and perfectly still. It tastes faintly of copper and ozone even though no thunder has walked here for megayears. Suspended dust motes drift downward along toroidal pathways dictated by forgotten ventilation shafts, settling into undisturbed dunes against the leeward sides of abandoned cafés.

No trees, no mosses, no lichen — only mineral life. What grows here grows downward: long crystals of native nickel-iron extrude slowly from rusted gutter joints like icicles in reverse, their hexagonal facets glowing with Cherenkov blue where the planet’s depleted core still whispers through them. Leeward faces of older blocks are colonized by bushy accretions of whisker-tin that twitch faintly in the planet’s magnetic sleep-talk, rearranging themselves millimeter by millimeter over centuries to form ever more efficient lightning rods that will never again see a storm.

Fauna exists at thresholds you can only suspect: sudden cold spots on stairwells that move against the airflow; faint pitted trails across zinc rooftops as if something soft and heavy as regret had dragged itself along metal skin, leaving suction mark signatures that evaporate under inspection. Once every thousand hours the whole city hums together, a single infrasonic pulse rising from buried engines still trying to complete a rotation cycle started before continents were named. Sleepers in dream-lofts report hearing distant applause.

Light is scarce and hoarded. A few sodium lamps survive beneath leaded glass domes the size of cathedral bell jars, their dim orange circles painting islands of shadow inside vaster shadow. Elsewhere illumination is accidental: photon leaks from antique radium numerals on long-dead clocks; luminous paint on safety ladders that glowed green for centuries but has now decayed through jade into sullen rust; rare sheets of transparent aluminum in derelict greenhouse roofs that filter the invisible stars above into faint ellipses of ultraviolet ghostlight on floors below.

The city was once industrial — temples of cast iron and cast steel interconnected by pneumatic mail tubes thick enough to crawl through. Now the tubes carry only silence, but in some still-powered precincts you can find automatons lying on their sides with rubberized prayer wheels still turning in their chests, endlessly reciting maintenance sutras to machines that have already outlived their gods.

Everything measurable is slowly evening out. Gears stripped of teeth spin frictionless on bearings of solidified air. Compass needles have forgotten north and instead point with aching slowness toward local centers of mass regret. In the grand bourse at the city’s former apex, a bronze statue of a merchant holding a balance scale has become an actual scale: corrosion deposits have grown symmetrically on both pans until they balance within one atom’s weight of perfect equilibrium — a sculpture that records the planet’s final judgment upon the concept of profit.

No one remembers who built it, or why they chose to tilt the world before they left. The current inhabitants, few enough to occupy only a handful of clustered wards, speak a language composed entirely of past-tense verbs conjugated for regret. They move by climbing carefully along guidechains strung between handholds that were once street signs reading PROGRESS and UNITY and DO NOT ENTER AFTER CURFEW.

They say that if you descend toward the crater’s center — where the artificial horizon’s glow is brightest and the air grows first warm then hot then begins to singe your lungs with copper filings — you will eventually reach a place where all the city’s missing angles have gathered. There the buildings stand absolutely straight again, perpendicular to nothing, and the streets form perfect circles around a tower whose height equals exactly the depth of the sky it was built to reach. They say that at the foot of this tower lies a sealed door whose handle is shaped like a question mark, and that written above it in letters of dry light are the last words ever spoken aloud in this world: PULL TO EXIT.

But no one has come back to confirm this, and the inhabitants have grown old waiting for the universe to invent an answer to a question no one remembers asking. So instead they sit beside their drift-adorned windows, watching the eternal slow-motion avalanche of architecture sliding endlessly sideways under the patient weight of stars they can no longer see, and practice the art of breathing in a city where gravity is just another name for nostalgia.
