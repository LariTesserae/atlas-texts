At first the land appears volcanic—black basalt towers rising from a plain of red grit, their tops lost in heat shimmer—but look closer and nothing burns. The rock is cool, sweat-cold, even under a sky pale as bleached glass. The heat arrives in pulses, not from sun alone but from something deeper: every sixteen minutes fracture vents at the base of each tower hiss and fog the air with mineral steam. Condensation begins before the sound dies. Droplets bead on knife-edge ledges, migrate into cracks, and vanish between crystal seams. The cycle is older than the towers themselves.

No river carries the water away; no plant drinks it. Instead the towers are the plants, if plants can be columns of invaded stone. Inside each basalt shaft runs a hollow core lined with transparent quartz fibres—fossilized lightning paths left empty by millennia of slow dissolution. When the hidden aquifer below compresses, the fibres act as capillaries; when pressure releases, they sweat. The towers drink their own exhalations in a closed loop. At ground level this leaves only mist and maroon gravel dusted white with evaporites that never quite dissolve.

The plain between them is not empty. It tessellates: hexagonal plates of iron oxide crust, each a meter across, each slightly convex like the scales of a vast dead animal. Walk carefully—if one foot cracks the crust it sings, a single pure C-sharp that travels plate to plate in widening circles. Nothing attacks the walker, but everything listens. After a few chords the crust-fauna emerge: flat-bodied harvesters the color of dry blood, each no wider than a hand but joined in trains of hundreds by flexible mouth-tube chains. They suck up the salt film, synchronizing their pull with each resonance beat, then coil back into dormant rings around the fissures. Their feeding keeps the plates polished and the ground music alive.

A few towers are pregnant—bulged near the base like ripening galls. These are oaksap towers, infected centuries ago by spores blown on a wind whose source lies beyond the escarpment rim no one has reached alive. The infection hijacks the capillaries, forces them to secrete golden resin instead of water. Over decades the resin pools into amber lakes at the heart, thickening until pressure splits the rock with audible moans. When they rupture, fluid brick pours forth, cooling into hexagonal bars lighter than balsa but stronger than steel. The harvesters ignore this prize; it is for others.

Others arrive at dusk, though here dusk means only a bluing of the glare and a shift in the wind pattern. Two wide-winged machines glide down the density gradient between the cool stone and the hot air parcel above—gliders built of scrap aluminium stitched with scavenged tarp. On each underside a single human passenger hangs in a canvas sling, legs dangling bare. They slinghook onto the sweated flanges of a ripe tower and wait for the next hiss-cycle. Timing is everything: too soon, scalding steam; too late, the seam reseals. At the precise moment they hammer in a ceramic tap and fill hide bladders in seconds, then release the building pressure-valve inside the tower’s heart-cavity. The tower shrieks in two descending tones like an old steam horn mourning its own future death.

They call themselves the Cullet Guild. Three hundred souls living in a mobile lattice suspended between twelve tethered air-whales—great gas-bloated vertebrae of extinct megafauna whose buoyancy sacks still metabolize hydrogen from the iron-rich crust. The guild-city drifts at the mercy of thermal lifts and the faint magnetic pull of sleeping ore beds, always staying within sight of the Towers of Perpetual Thirst but never touching ground for longer than a resin-run. Their children learn fractions by listening to the cracked plate music and measuring the intervals of tower breaths.

They know nothing about who carved the first tap-holes, whose rusted sockets they still find fossilize-sealed behind fresh quartz growth, but they follow the old paths stamped in the crust like pilgrimage routes nobody remembers naming. At noon-when the heat swings harmonic the entire plain becomes a lens—air, stone, vapour, metal co-focus into moments when distant surfaces are visible as if etched on glass. Then, briefly, you can see the far escarpment: not rock but teeth, a kilometer-high zagged jawbone still chewing itself in slow geological anger, and beyond that something brighter than any noon shining up from the dark ground like a second sun that never sets.

Nobody has ever flown there and returned to say what feeds it.

They mark the calendar instead by catastala: twin ruptures when two ripe towers breach within the same hour. Last cycle it was shards and falling sky for days, splinters of golden resin raining fire because the wardens tapped both heartsides out of phase. The Plate Choir sang two conflicting melodies until half the harvesters starved, confused between rhythms. The guild lost fourteen people and an entire tranche ridge.

Today the air-traffic controller—an old woman suspended alone in the central sputtering stillness of a whale’s emptied skull-chamber—watches green glow thread curling on slate. Two towers show identical pressure curves rising toward sync, three days apart in space but converging like a tidal braid. She rings the bronze alarm whale-bone once, twice, holds the third stroke mute against her chest until the vibration stops her heart for four beats.

Below on the salt plain nothing moves yet except a lone apprentice running barefoot to catch a crate of fresh taps falling too fast from a misjudged drop line. He misses, loses skin from his palms when he slides on singing iron, but the blood beads bright gold like resin before it dries to black dust and is licked clean by the wind.

The towers breathe. The City of Chains prepares to drink or to die singing.
