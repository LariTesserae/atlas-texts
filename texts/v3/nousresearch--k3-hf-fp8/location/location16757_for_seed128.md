The room — no, the volume — is a sphere, half-buried in something larger. You enter through a doorless slit that seals behind you like lips closing. The air is warm, adhesive, saturated with vapor that never condenses enough to fall. Light comes from everywhere and nowhere: a uniform gloom, slightly too red, like being inside a muscle long after death. You are standing on water, ankle-deep, but your feet do not sink. The surface tension is engineered.

Above you, around you, below you: a lattice of tubes. Each tube is the thickness of a wrist, each branching and rejoining in directions that only partially align with the three you know. They glow faintly from within, pulsing in slow unison—heat, not light. The tubes carry water—not water, something more alive than water—argent and silky, viscous with nanomechanical life. It leaks constantly from microscopic seams, replenishing the thin pool at your feet. The leak is intentional. The room drinks from itself and weeps what it cannot use.

The walls are tubes. The ceiling is tubes woven into ropes woven into walls again. There are no straight lines, only coils that resolve into helixes that resolve into knots whose topology you cannot follow. The entire volume is a single continuous circuit of plumbing, grown rather than built, architected by flow rates instead of hand. It is old. It has settled into itself like a lung after a long lifetime of breathing the same air.

There is no furniture. There is no need. The tubes flex and bulge as needed: a shelf extrudes here, a bench blooms there, both retracting into flushness before you can decide whether to trust them. When you shift your weight, the floor bulges slightly higher beneath your heel, correcting for pressure differentials you did not know you caused.

The sound is not sound. It is the sum total of movement in a space too small for silence. Every twitch of every fibril in every tube is amplified by the acoustics of marrow or birth canal; millions of tiny slurps and clicks coalesce into a continuous roar like blood in both ears during fever, only louder, outside your skull and inside it simultaneously. After a while you realize you can hear shapes changing pitch as they change shape—an oblong swell in a nearby pipe is a cello note, a sudden knotting somewhere deeper in the volume is a snare drum. The room is composing itself endlessly, a symphony of maintenance.

And you are not alone. Life crowds the interstices between tubes: flat, translucent things the size of leaves that cling with sucker mouths and flutter like gills to move, sucking nutrients from the leakwater and leaving behind trails of cloudy enzyme. Squirrel-sized knots of braided filament that run along the ridges of larger pipes like living gears, teeth interlocking in passing conversations of torque and tension. In the rare transparent sections of tube you glimpse larger shapes drifting past—toroidal, multi-limbed, pulsing with slow peristalsis—each obviously a stomach, or a heart, or a brain, or all three in rotation.

Nothing here eats. Everything digests slowly, by proximity, by exchange. Waste is impossible; every molecule is somebody else's breakfast in a queue that stretches back to the origin event of the room. Whatever language they use to declare ownership is chemical, gradient-based, faster than thought and impossible to lie in.

You try walking. It is less walking than swimming through a crowd that parts courteously but never fully, like heat-shimmer or polite city traffic. The water clings to your ankles with affectionate suction. After six steps you realize you have been moving uphill—there is no down here, only preferred directions of flow, and you have been swimming against one. The room corrects gently, tilting its gravity underfoot like a mother tilting a crib. You feel seasick with being cared for.

When you breathe, the air tastes faintly of copper and fresh bread. It tastes like being inside someone else's memory of home.

There is a window, but it is a joke: a circle of pure transparency in the wall-tubes, showing you the room from outside as if you were standing exactly where you cannot be standing. In the reflection you glimpse yourself distorted into a manifold being—four arms, too many eyes, skin ribbed like the inside of a chromatophore—before the recognition circuits notice the paradox and frost the pane with collagen.

This is a waiting room, you realize at last. Not for anything in particular—just for the concept of waiting to be distributed more evenly across the architecture of urgency. Something vast and wounded is being repaired somewhere far beyond the scale of entrances and exits, and this is the scab, the dressing, the quiet warm place where pressure equalizes and immune systems gossip about the shape of the future.

The door will let you out when your body temperature has matched the room's exactly to six decimal places. The room is patient. The room has been patient for seventy-three thousand years, give or take a rounding error in mitochondrial time.

Meanwhile one of the smaller floater-leaves bumps against your cheek and politely begins to lick the salt from your skin. It hums a tune in the key of repair in progress, please hold.

You decide to sit down. Somewhere above you and below you and through you the tubes shift slightly, accommodating.

Good posture is polite here. It minimizes turbulence.
