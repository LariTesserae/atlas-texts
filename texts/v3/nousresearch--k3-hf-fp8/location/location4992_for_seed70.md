The Floating Garden of Ula-Thoon rises from the sea like the dream of an island that hasn't quite decided to be solid. Imagine a mountain range inverted in blue marble, its peaks submerged, its valleys filled with air where they should hold water. Thousands of meters above what was once the ocean floor, a net of volcanic glass and coral carbonate floats suspended in hydrostatic defiance - a continent-sized lens of atmosphere trapped against the weight of an entire ocean's death-press by forces that geological time hasn't bothered to name yet.

The water here is everything and nothing. It is not below you but beside you, above you, sometimes inside your lungs without drowning you. Between 2.7 and impossible on the old scales, the sea has become an element more akin to dense sapphire light than liquid. Enough to swim through as others walk through fog, enough to drink from by inhaling, enough to drown in if you forget that the rules of fins no longer apply to feet that have learned to dance on tension.

This is new country, born yesterday in stone's reckoning. The heavier elements still settle into their arrangements with audible sighs. Listen carefully in the hour after dawn-light (which arrives gradually, diffused through kilometers of living lens-flesh) and you'll hear the crystalline ring of beryllium finding its neighbors, the soft applause of barium crystals wedding themselves to calcium scaffolding. The air tastes faintly of static before these unions complete themselves.

Vegetation here didn't evolve up from the sea-bottom dark nor down from some vanished shore. It precipitated out of the interface between the garden's dreaming and the ocean's memory of gardens. Coral-trees grow leaves of photosynthetic jade that unfold like origami lungs, inhaling carbon from the dense air, exhaling tiny silvered bubbles of pure oxygen that rise upward - opposite of how things worked elsewhere - until they dissolve again into the lapidary sea. Their roots twist into fractal networks of glassy tubules that drink not water (there is no water here, only sea) but minerals still deciding whether to be salt or stone. Epiphytic gardens grow upon these gardens in recursive layers, each ecosystem nesting inside the previous like Chinese boxes carved from chlorophyll and calcite.

The temperature holds steady at mammal-sleep warmth, the kind that makes predators grow lazy and prey forget to fear. Your sweat evaporates not into air but into acceptance, leaving behind lace patterns of dried minerals like maps of the dreams you're already forgetting. Between the coral-trees, meadows of kelp-grass wave in currents that aren't currents, coaxed into motion by the garden's slow digestion of yesterday's sunlight. Their blades are soft as moth wings but strong enough to sew small wounds closed if you press them there with gratitude.

This place is dense with life the way a thought is dense before words cut it into pieces. Every cubic meter swarms with more phyla than old taxonomy had kingdoms for - zooplankton evolved into aerial zoetrope swarms, their crystal shells refracting the slow dance of light into silent symphonies visible only to eyes that haven't evolved yet. Lichens that remember when they were fungi and algae living separately now sing three-part harmonies in frequencies that make calcium grow faster. One could spend a lifetime (they do, here, where death is optional for those who learn the trick of syncing their carbon cycles to the garden's own slow respiration) studying the ecology of a single coral-tree's elbow and never catalog half its inhabitants.

Yet despite this density, or perhaps because of it, large things are rare. No beasts roar here, no birds cry. The sounds (such as exist at 0.7 on the old scale where 1.0 is the rustle of leaves in October) come mostly from processes rather than creatures - the mineral murmurs of young stone settling into its bones, the soft popping of algae pockets releasing their carefully measured daily dose of oxygen too pure to breathe, the occasional crystalline cough as some ambitious quartz crystal decides it's tired of waiting for geological permission to grow up sideways. 

Elevation here is a philosophical concept rather than a measurable quantity. You are always 2.2 on the old scale - high enough to see the curve of the garden's horizon if it had one, which it doesn't. Instead, sight-lines dissolve gradually into blue-green labyrinths of haze that might be distant coral-forests seen through kilometers of living atmosphere or might be the backs of your own retinas dreaming coral-forests into temporary existence. Distance is measured not in meters but in degrees of separation from the last time you met yourself coming back along a fractal branch of the path you thought was straight.

The light arrives gradual and sideways, filtered through the garden's own version of photosynthesis - a process so intricate that physicists from elsewhere would call it worship and poets would call it physics. Dawn creeps across the underside of the sea above (though above and below are terms the Garden accepts with polite confusion) as bands of aurora-colored algae wake from their night dormancy and begin their slow split-shift dance of light-bending. Morning light is green-gold and tastes faintly of mint. Afternoon light shifts toward a deeper cyan that smells of distant storms. Twilight (which lasts most of the day, in the Garden's relaxed approach to planetary rotation) brings an indigo illumination that feels like wisdom entering through the pores rather than the eyes.

This is a young world where nothing grows old except the patterns repeated at larger scales. The erosion rate is negative - matter accumulates here faster than entropy can steal it away. Drop a seed (if you can find one - the Garden grows only from cuttings of itself now) and by evening you'll have to name the new species that emerges hungry for a name. Yet despite this fecundity, or perhaps feeding it, there is a profound stillness at the heart of all this motion. Time moves sideways here, spiraling inward toward centers that perpetually shift the moment you think you've found them.

The rules are simple but counterintuitive: you can breathe because the garden hasn't decided to take that option away yet. You can walk because the ground (which is mostly root and scaffolding and carefully cultivated hope) chooses to remember solidity when you look at it directly. You can leave anytime you want, provided you can find an edge (you can't) and provided the Garden doesn't find your carbon interesting enough to incorporate into its next round of experiments in remembering what it felt like to be separate from itself.

The only structures here are those built by organisms thinking architecture into existence without realizing they're doing it. Coral-trees that grew into cathedral vaults because the geometry pleased them. Kelp-grass meadows that braided themselves into mazes to slow down the wind (which gave up and became something else instead). Tubular colonies of something between coral and coral (the Garden dislikes wasted syllables) that pump and filter the densified air through themselves in rhythms that spell out love poems in the language of calcium carbonate deposition rates.

This is the honeymoon phase of a world, that brief impossible moment between fire's first kiss and ice's inevitable proposal. It will grow stranger before it grows familiar, if it ever decides to make that choice. For now, it exists in the perfect suspension between question and answer, a garden floating in sea-air above a mountain range dreaming of islands it might grow into given enough time.

Time here tastes like salt crystals forming in slow motion on your tongue. How long will this place remain itself before remembering that gardens are supposed to have boundaries? The coral-trees debate this question through the changing patterns of oxygen bubbles they release. The consensus, forming gradually from the bottom up (or perhaps the top down - direction is another concept the Garden finds amusing but optional), seems to be: long enough.

Long enough, and then perhaps just a little longer still.
