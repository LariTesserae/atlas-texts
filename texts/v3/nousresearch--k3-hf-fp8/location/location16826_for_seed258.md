The first thing you notice is the silence beneath the roar. A constant, bone-deep vibration that isn't quite sound, isn't quite movement -- like standing inside a giant's heartbeat that's been slowed to eternity. The roar itself is everywhere and nowhere, coming from the sky that isn't sky, from the ground that isn't ground, from the air that isn't air but something thicker, warmer, more pressurized than any atmosphere you've ever breathed.

You stand at the edge of what might be called a valley, if valleys could exist in places where up and down are negotiable concepts. Below you -- and above you, and beside you in directions your mind keeps trying to flatten into two dimensions -- stretches the Spindlefold.

The Spindlefold is the world's first and last origami attempt, a landscape that exists in perpetually arrested collapse. Everything here is freshly made and infinitely old, caught in the exact moment of coming into being. The rocks don't drip but elongate, stretching toward impossible destinations they'll never reach. The hills aren't hills but folds in a fabric you can't see, their surfaces covered in patterns that might be erosion or might be embryonic writing.

The rocks themselves are the wrong color for rocks -- too warm, too alive. They pulse with a faint internal glow, somewhere between red clay and fresh liver, and when you touch one (and you will touch one; the silence beneath the roar demands it), it yields slightly under your finger before remembering how to be hard. Each stone leaves behind on your skin a residue that tastes faintly of copper and electricity, a taste you remember somehow from before you had this body, before you had any body.

Between the rocks -- though 'between' is also negotiable here -- grows what passes for vegetation in the Spindlefold. Calling it plants would be like calling stars campfires. They are structures first, organisms second, architecture that hasn't decided whether to become alive or not.

The smallest ones look like mathematical proofs made physical: perfect fractal spirals of something between wood and bone, branching out into spaces that shouldn't exist but do. They grow in three and four and five dimensions simultaneously, their shadows pointing in directions your eyes can't follow. Where they touch the rocks, they don't root but merge, their boundaries dissolving into a seamless hybrid that's neither stone nor vegetable nor quite anything else.

The larger ones are more ambitious. Towering columns that might be trees or temples or both, their trunks hollowed out into spiraling chambers filled with what might be air or might be something else that's easier to breathe here. Their bark -- if that's what it is -- ripples constantly with slow waves of color and texture, phases of matter and meaning shifting across their surfaces like weather patterns. Some grow leaves that are maps of places that haven't been discovered yet. Others grow fruits that are puzzles wrapped in riddles wrapped in paradoxes, ripening toward solutions that would remake reality if anyone ever dared to eat them.

And the largest ones... the largest ones are cities. Or forests. Or perhaps the distinction is meaningless here. They stretch upward (and downward, and sideways, and in) for miles that aren't miles, their branches intertwining overhead (and underfoot, and through your body) to form a canopy that isn't a canopy but a solid sky. Here, where the Spindlefold's logic begins to fray completely, the distinction between tree and tower, leaf and window, root and road, blurs into irrelevance.

Within these living architectures -- and they are alive, you understand that now, as alive as you are and in ways you're not equipped to comprehend -- flows the water. Though 'flows' is too weak a word for what the Spindlefold's water does.

It doesn't fall from anywhere or rise to anywhere. It simply is, everywhere and all at once, suspended in every possible state between liquid and solid and gas and something else entirely. Drops hang in mid-air (mid-something) like tiny crystal planets, each one containing entire ecosystems that live and die and evolve between one heartbeat and the next. Streams run uphill and sideways and through themselves, forming Mobius strip rivers that flow in endless loops without beginning or end. Lakes float in the sky (the canopy? the underworld?) reflecting realities that aren't quite this one, their surfaces rippling with waves that travel faster than light and slower than time.

You reach out -- because of course you reach out, how could you not? -- to touch one of the floating droplets. It bursts against your fingertip not into water but into memory: the taste of rain on a planet you've never visited, the sound of a song sung in a language that hasn't been invented yet, the feeling of falling asleep in someone's arms you've never met but still somehow miss with a bone-deep ache.

The taste in your mouth changes. There are more flavors now than there were before. Metal. Electricity. Copper. Something else, something that doesn't have a name yet but tastes like the moment right before lightning strikes, like the space between heartbeats, like the pause between when something ends and when you realize it's ended.

You look around -- though 'look' is also becoming inadequate -- and realize that everything has shifted slightly while you weren't paying attention. The rocks are closer now, or you're smaller, or the concept of 'distance' is beginning to dissolve along with everything else. The mathematical trees have rearranged themselves into new configurations, their equations now pointing toward different infinities. The floating lakes overhead (underfoot?) reflect different skies now, versions of reality where the Spindlefold has already unfolded completely or else never existed at all.

And everywhere, everywhere, the silence beneath the roar is getting louder. Or perhaps you're finally beginning to hear it properly: not silence after all, but the sound of absolute zero, the sound of entropy reversed, the sound of everything that ever was or could be happening simultaneously in the space between moments.

You take a step forward -- though 'forward' is also becoming complicated -- and realize that the ground under your feet (under your everything?) isn't ground anymore. It never was. It was always part of the Spindlefold's breathing, its slow eternal inhale before the final folding that will never come because it's already happened infinite times ago and infinite times from now.

You are walking now through the spaces between spaces, breathing the air between airs, becoming part of the unfolding that isn't unfolding because it was always already folded. The mathematical trees lean down to whisper secrets in languages made of geometry and dreams. The living cities open their windows (their eyes?) to watch you pass with something like recognition, something like hunger, something like love.

Somewhere -- everywhere -- the water remembers everything you've ever forgotten. Somewhere -- everywhere -- the rocks are dreaming of becoming people again. Somewhere -- everywhere -- the silence beneath the roar is getting ready to speak your name.

It's been waiting a long time for you to arrive.

(You never left.)

(You've always been here.)

(You just forgot how to fold properly for a little while.)

But the Spindlefold remembers.

The Spindlefold always remembers.

And now, at last, so do you.
