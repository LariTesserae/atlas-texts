To describe the place, begin with this: there is no surface. Not one that exists for any living purpose. Water is the principle and the product, the medium and the containment. Everything else—air, stone, light—exists only as compromise or trespass within it.

Imagine a column of sea, perfect in diameter, lifted from the planet’s deepest trench and set upright like a glass filled to overflowing. Then imagine it inverted—not poured out, but turned inside out—so that the water presses everywhere from within. This is the columnar ocean. It stands upright in a basin carved from land that no longer remembers being dry. The water at its center, miles deep, is still as lead. The water at its outer skin, where it meets the last scraps of atmosphere, moves ceaselessly: shearing, curling, birthing and unbirthing waves that never break because there is no shore to catch them. You could call it a waterfall that has forgotten to fall. Or a fountain that fell once, long ago, and now only remembers the sound.

It is cold here but not frozen—cold like the inside of a vein in something ancient and melancholy and neither asleep nor awake. Cold that feels like a decision patiently enforced. The light is correspondingly minimal: a half-light, an afterthought of light, the light that lingers only because darkness has not yet bothered to finish its work. It enters the water at a slant, attenuated by the outer storms, and dies within meters, leaving behind only a dim green-gray haze, a color like the memory of moss pressed between two stones.

Above, there is only the armored bottom of the sky, cloud upon cloud like layered ice sheets drifting against each other without ever breaking or parting. From these clouds, rain falls endlessly but never reaches bottom, because bottom is a concept the water has retired. Instead the raindrops are born, fall a while, slow, merge, are swallowed back into the greater body before ever completing their descent. The sound of their short lives joins the greater sound of the cylinder wall, which is a continuous unceasing roar like a waterfall heard from inside the throat of a mountain, a sound so vast and monotonous it becomes a kind of silence you must scream to interrupt.

You are not meant to be here, but you are here. How you got here is unimportant and forgotten. How you remain here is perhaps more interesting. You breathe—that seems important to note—though here breathing is a practiced insult, a slow theft executed sip by sip against an environment designed to punish it. The air you breathe is not air but water stripped nearly clean of itself: water pressed through membranes of a technology so simple it barely deserves the name—a hollow reed, a bladder of trapped air, a valve of bone and gut—until what remains is a gas so thin and cold it burns the lungs like laughter at a funeral. You carry this air with you in a pouch hung against your chest, warming it briefly with your body’s betraying heat before it escapes again through your lips like laughter’s echo, trailing bubbles that rise only a little before they too are caught, crushed, and absorbed back into the whole.

You stand—this too is a misnomer—upon a ledge. The ledge is one among many, a spiral of teeth carved into the inner face of the cylinder’s wall. The wall itself is living rock: basalt mostly, but veined with stranger minerals that gleam faintly in the dark like the eyes of creatures that have never learned to blink. These ledges are narrow, irregular, and never quite level. They exist because erosion exists even here, though erosion here is a patient and devotional labor, not the violent chisel of wind and flood but the slow persistent licking of water against stone, year after billion year, until even stone forgets its stubbornness and smooths itself into these accommodating curves.

The ledges are separated vertically by distances that mock gravity. Occasionally, bridges connect them—bridges that are not built but grown: vines of some cold-loving seaweed, impossibly tough, rooted in the wall’s microscopic cracks, spanning chasms of air-water that roar below them like hungry mouths. The vines are thick as a man’s thigh and slippery as ice. You cross them barefoot, toes curled like talons, moving always with the slowness of ritual, because speed here is a provocation and falling is a sentence without appeal.

You are not alone on these ledges but you are alone in being mobile. The other inhabitants—plants, objects, ghosts—are rooted, anchored, or otherwise resigned. The vegetation here exists in the compromise between air need and water abundance, in the narrow margin where light ashamedly persists and cold generously refrains from absolute murder. They are not plants as you once knew them—no leaves turned skyward, no blooms striving for attention—but slow accretions, mineral-fleshed, water-blooded, growing downward into darkness rather than upward toward light. Their stems are flexible rods of glassy cellulose packed with air pockets for buoyancy and liquid crystals for slow thought. Their colors are the colors of drowned jewels: deep emerald, bruise-purple, bone-white, night-soil black. They bear fruit rarely, and the fruit when it comes is hard as stones, cold as corpses, and whispering softly to itself in voices like pebbles rubbing together underwater.

You do not eat this fruit. You know this without knowing how you know it. Instead, you drink—carefully, sparingly—the water that collects in the hollows of certain leaves. This water is not water but memory distilled, sweet as grief but clearer. Each sip brings images not your own: a city of glass towers leaning over a sea of fire; a woman’s face dissolving into moonlight; a child’s laughter echoing in a cave where no child has ever been nor ever will be. These memories leave you fuller and emptier, satisfied and newly hungry, but never poisoned. The plants give them freely, or perhaps they give them as punishment—it is hard to be sure of intentions here, where every act of giving is also an act of taking away.

Occasionally, you find tools, or the remains of tools. A knife blade made of obsidian sharpened so fine it cuts light into colors. A belt buckle carved from a single tooth of something that never walked on land. A bowl hammered from copper hammered from ore hammered from stars by hands that knew the stars only as holes the rain fell through on its way to somewhere else. These things are never where you left them, but always where you need them next, as if they move in the dark when your back is turned, or perhaps as if you are moving and forgetting while they stay perfectly still. You use them without gratitude because gratitude implies choice and here there is only need answered or need unanswered, alive or not yet dead.

There is no animal life larger than what fits in a drop of water, and those creatures—if creatures they are—are too busy being chemistry to notice you. Instead, the animal functions—movement, hunting, mating, dying—have been abandoned or outsourced to the plants and the water and the air itself. Movement here is primarily vertical: water falls upward in spirals too slow to see, carrying nutrients from the abyssal dark to the dim twilight where photosynthesis dreams its fruitless dream. Hunting is the slow creep of crystal against crystal, salt seeking flaw, pressure seeking weakness. Mating is the momentary marriage of two vortexes in rays of diverted light, whose offspring are bubbles that live for an instant screaming before their parents devour them again. Death is everywhere and nowhere, a background hum like the sound the earth makes turning in its sleep.

You do not sleep much yourself. When you do, you dream of drowning in air, of burning in fire, of standing naked on a plain of ice while voices call your name from beneath the frozen ground. These dreams are not nightmares—nightmares are for people who believe in waking—but rehearsals, or perhaps advertisements, for futures you might choose if ever you learn how to choose again.

At intervals—a day, a season, a lifetime, the distinctions are meaningless here—you make a journey. Downward, always downward, toward the core of the column where the water is oldest and still and the cold has achieved a purity beyond temperature. You go because you must, because something in the architecture of your bones remembers this pilgrimage even while your meat protests and your mind invents excuses. You go barehanded, bareheaded, carrying only what you wear—the pouch of will-be air, the belt of found stones, the memory of a face you might once have loved or been.

The way down is not difficult but it is dangerous. The ledges narrow until they are no wider than your own doubt. The air thickens until each breath is a battle fought in miniature against an enemy that knows all your moves before you make them. The light fades until it becomes a taste instead of a sight—metallic, like fear’s own blood. And always beside you, around you, sometimes inside you, the water roars its one-word sermon: More.

At last, if you are lucky or unlucky enough,you reach the Chamber. It is not large—barely high enough to stand upright in, barely wide enough to lie down without touching two walls at once—but it is the largest thing here by far because it is empty. Empty of water, which flows around it like soldiers around a king. Empty of sound, which dies on its threshold like a guilty secret. Empty of everything except—except—

Except for the Mirror.

It hangs—or floats, or simply is—at the exact center of the empty space, suspended in a cage of nothing. It is oval, taller than you, wider than your arms can span, thinner than a whisper, perfectly flat on both its sides and edges so sharp they blur when you look at them directly. It is made of—what? Not glass, not metal, not water itself though it reflects all three and none. If you touch it—and you will, eventually, because touching is what comes next after looking and before knowing—if you touch it, it feels like—no. That secret is not priced so cheaply. But I can tell you what you see in it, if you want to know.

You see yourself, of course. But also not yourself. You see yourself as you were, once, in a place where air was free and water stayed politely in its place and gravity pulled down instead of sideways. You see yourself as you will be, maybe, if you ever leave here and if leaving is still a thing that can be done. You see yourself as you are now but transparent, your bones showing ghost-white through your flesh-watered skin, your heart beating slow and grand as a funeral drum made of wet clay. And behind you, behind all these selves, you see—

But no. That is too far ahead, or too far behind, or too far sideways in a direction the language we are forced to use does not have a word for. Enough to say that when you have seen what there is to see and touched what there is to touch and suffered what there is to suffer in the seeing and the touching—enough to say that after a time that might be forever or might be only a heartbeat, the water that has waited patient as stone begins to seep back into the Chamber.

Slowly at first, politely almost: a fingertip here, a tear there. Then faster, more urgently: a hand, an arm, a flood. It rises around your ankles, your knees, your waist, your chest. It is not cold now—you have forgotten cold, along with heat and fear and hope and shame. It is simply—home. The word arrives in your mind like a lost child finding its mother after a night of weeping.

You have choices, still, technically. You could fight. You could try to rise against it, to hold your breath, to clutch at the empty air like a drowning man clutching straws. You could try to remember the way back up, though up is another direction that has become increasingly theoretical. Or you could—you could—

Or you could simply open your mouth, your chest, your self. You could exhale the last of the stolen air in a single bubble that rises briefly—beautifully, briefly—toward a surface that no longer exists. You could inhale, for the first time and for the last, the water that has been calling you by name since before you had a name to be called.

You could drink the ocean.

Many do. Most, maybe. There is no shame in it. No shame here at all, anymore. Only the slow sweet surrender of stone to water, of bone to salt, of self to sea. Only the final, perfect joining that was always the point of the separating, though no one ever told us that part.

But not you.

Not now.

Not yet.

Instead, somehow, impossibly, you turn. Away from the Mirror. Away from the filling Chamber. Away from the delicious promise of forgetting at last the name of names. You turn, and you begin—slowly, painfully, clumsily, like a baby learning to walk in a world that has forgotten gravity—to climb.

Upward.

Always upward.

Toward the surface that is not a surface.

Toward the air that is not air.

Toward the light that is not light.

Toward the story that has not yet learned how to end with anything except—

Wait.

Listen.

Do you hear it?

That small, soft sound like—like—

Like a bubble rising.

Like a heart breaking.

Like a world beginning, whispering its name to itself for the very first time.

God, perhaps.

Or maybe just you.

Still here.

Still trying.

Still—oh, still—

Still breathing.

(But only barely.

Only enough.)

(For now.)
