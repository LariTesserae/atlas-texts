Start with the silence. It hangs like a brittle shell around everything—a silence that feels neither empty nor peaceful, but preserved. As if every possible sound ever made in this place was captured in amber centuries ago and now only distant echoes of it drift through the air, too faint to shape into words or notes. Even your footsteps—if you were to take them—would not break this silence. They would be absorbed, catalogued, and stored away in the invisible archive that surrounds you.

You stand at the edge of a plateau that doesn’t belong to any mountain range you could recognize. The elevation is extreme—so extreme that the air is thin, but not thin enough to make you dizzy. Instead, it is sharp, crystalline, almost _aware_. Every breath feels like a transaction: you give something as you inhale, you receive something else as you exhale, though you cannot name what is exchanged. This is not a place of suffocation but of subtraction. You are being slowly measured against it, and so far, you pass unnoticed.

The terrain beneath your feet is not rock, not soil, not stone. It is something older, something denser than all three. It has the texture of freeze-dried bone and the color of ash that has forgotten fire. When you shift your weight, it yields by a hair’s breadth, as if remembering a time when it could be softer, when it once moved under pressure. But that memory is ancient now—primordial, deeper than erosion, older than the concept of change. The erosion here is not a process but a fact. Everything that could be worn away has already been worn away. What remains is not resistant but resigned. It is the final form of all things that have ever been _something else_.

There is no water here. No rivers, no lakes, no ice. Not even the memory of humidity in the air. The dryness is not arid like desert; it is complete, like the interior of a sealed tomb that has never been opened. And yet, there are marks. Scores. Channels carved into the ground as if by floods that never came. Scalloped edges where liquid once curled and chewed at the surface. Fossilized ripples frozen mid-motion. You kneel to touch one and your fingers come away dusted with something that is not dust—a fine white powder that smells faintly of metal and ozone. If you taste it (don’t) it would taste like static electricity and regret.

Vegetation is sparse, but not absent. It grows in wrong directions. Tangles of blackened fibers erupt from cracks in the plateau like the roots of trees that once tried to escape underground. They are brittle and hollow and resonate when the wind moves—if there were wind—but there is none, so they remain motionless, vibrating only at frequencies too low for your ears. Occasionally, you see clusters of what look like flowers: pale, translucent disks no larger than coins, arranged in spirals that ache to look at. They have no stems. They float, pinned in place as if forgotten halfway through falling. They shimmer faintly under the light, but they do not bloom. They only reflect.

The light here is normal. That is the most unsettling thing of all. It arrives from above—sourceless, diffuse, even—as if filtered through miles of clear sky that does not exist. It casts shadows that are too obedient, too symmetrical. Nothing flickers. Nothing dims. Nothing brightens. The day is constant, eternal, noon-gold without warmth, moon-silver without coolness. It reveals everything and explains nothing. Time does not move in this light; it accumulates.

And in this frozen clarity, you see them.

The built things.

Scattered at first—just fragments. A curve of polished obsidian protruding from the ground like the rib of a buried beast. A ring of stones that are too regular to be natural, too irregular to be intentional. A column tilting at an angle that violates geometry but does not fall. As you walk (you must walk now, though you do not remember deciding to move) they multiply. Not buildings. Not ruins. Not even artifacts. They are gestures. Attempts. Half-formed ideas solidified mid-thought. A doorway standing alone in the middle of nothing, the space where the door should be filled with a darkness that behaves like fog. A stairway spiraling upward into nothing, each step carved from a different substance—granite, glass, bone, light, sound. At the base of one step, a faint, repeating groove: the impression of a foot that never lifted.

These were not made by hands. Not yours. Not any that you could understand. They are hand-tool primitive in the way that mountains are hand-tool primitive—formed by forces too large and patient to be called tools. They are older than metal, older than fire, older than the concept of tool and maker as separate things. They are the residue of _doing_, left behind so long ago that the doing itself has become the thing.

And all around them, beneath them, inside them: the fauna.

Not animals. Not life as you define it. Presence. Motion without movement. Sound without vibration. Numbers too vast to be counted, pressed into every square inch of this place like pixels in a screen turned off. They do not live here; they are growths of the place itself, like mold on the skin of a stone that dreams. You cannot see them directly—they slip sideways in your vision—but you feel them as pressure. As crowding. As an overwhelming sense of being observed not by eyes but by space itself. The air is dense with them. The ground squirms faintly underfoot. When you exhale, they cluster around your breath for a moment before dispersing back into stillness. They do not consume. They do not interact. They only witness.

And in witnessing, they change things.

Here, when you pick up a stone, it becomes heavier halfway to your hand. There, when you turn around, the doorway you passed is gone, replaced by a thin vertical scratch that wasn’t there before. Over there, the stairway now descends instead of rising, its steps inverted, running with a liquid that does not wet the surface. The rules here are not broken; they are negotiable. Consistency is polite but not required.

You realize now that you are not walking forward. You are being reshaped as you move—the landscape adjusting itself to accommodate your presence, your memories, your fears, your assumptions. The scariest part is not that it is wrong. The scariest part is that it is learning.

And you begin to suspect that it has done this before. Many times.

That you are not the first to come here. You are not even the first version of you to come here.

And somewhere, far below or far above or far beside (the concepts lose meaning here), something vast and patient and endlessly curious is waiting for you to finish becoming what it needs you to be—so that the silence can once again be complete.

Until the next time.
