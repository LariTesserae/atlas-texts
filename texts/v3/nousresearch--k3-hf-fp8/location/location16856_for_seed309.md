The first thing you notice is the smell—wet iron and chlorophyll and something else, something like electricity running through soil. Then the sound, a low continuous roar that isn't quite mechanical but isn't quite organic either. You've descended through layers of earth and memory into a place that shouldn't exist but has existed so long that existence itself has grown around it like scar tissue.

Redwater Foundry sits 3,200 feet below what used to be the surface, built into the hollowed-out remains of an ancient botanical research complex that went too deep and found something that didn't want to be found. The original walls—titanium-reinforced concrete and nano-carbon mesh—are still here, but they're barely visible beneath what they've become.

Everything is growing.

Not growing like plants growing. Growing like cancer growing. Growing like an idea growing too big for the mind that conceived it. The walls pulse with vascular bundles thick as subway tunnels. Fluorescent fungi bloom in fractal patterns across ceiling structures that might once have been girders. The floor beneath your feet isn't quite solid—you're walking on a living substrate, a dense mat of root-mycelium composite that yields slightly with each step like walking on the skin of something asleep.

The water here isn't water. It's too thick, too alive. It drips from overhead in slow amber beads that take minutes to fall, each drop containing entire ecosystems that flare into brief existence between ceiling and floor. When they hit they don't splash—they flower. Microscopic forests bloom and die in seconds, releasing spores that taste like copper and sound like distant bells.

This place was industrial once. You can still see the bones of machines embedded in the living flesh like fossils in amber—huge gear trains entangled in lianas thick as oak trunks, piston arrays that now serve as skeletal scaffolding for flowering vines that bloom with mechanical precision, turbines that continue to spin because the plants have learned to use them, have incorporated the rotation into their own metabolic processes.

The temperature keeps you slightly uncomfortable, skin always moist, clothes always damp. It feels like fever sweat on a patient too long ill. The air moves in slow convection currents driven by the respiration of hectares of chlorophyll-rich tissue, inhaled carbon dioxide exhaled as oxygen rich enough to make you dizzy, oxygenated enough to make your thoughts feel too fast, too bright around the edges. Some people come here just to breathe until they die of hyperoxygenation hallucinations, floating away on visions of impossible geometries that the human brain wasn't designed to process.

There are streets here, or what passes for them—channels cut through the living mass centuries ago, kept open by ritual pruning and the careful cultivation of aggressive fungi that eat their way inward at exactly the rate they need to be cut back. The buildings weren't built so much as grown into, spaces carved out of the thicket and then maintained through constant negotiation with the plants themselves.

Each structure is a symbiote. Each dwelling an organism in conversation with its occupants. The walls breathe with your breathing. The floor learns your weight distribution and grows supportive structures beneath where you stand most often. Ceilings lower themselves over generations to match the shrinking stature of old age. Windows open themselves like eyes tracking the sun, lenses grown from transparent cellulose that refract the dim fluorescent glow from the deep fungal forests far below into something that approximates daylight.

But it's never really light here. The light comes from everywhere and nowhere—the cumulative bioluminescence of billions of organisms expressing different wavelengths at different intensities. It pools in some places like spilled oil, creating pools of ultraviolet where you can see the true colors of things that should never be seen. It retreats from others, leaving shadows that aren't empty but filled with the absence-light of lifeforms that photosynthesize backwards, that grow in darkness by consuming photons rather than releasing them.

The population—what counts as population here—is impossible to census. There are the plant people, of course, humans who've gone so deep into symbiosis that the distinction is meaningless. Their skin photosynthesizes, their hair has gone to tendrils, their bones replaced with cellulose scaffolding strong as steel but lighter than aluminum. They speak in chemical signals and pheromone bursts, their voices coming not from mouths that are mostly vestigial now but from specialized leaves grown just for the purpose, vibrating at frequencies that you can hear in your bones even when your ears can't quite catch them.

There are the animals too, though calling them that feels increasingly inaccurate. The work-organisms. The transport-organisms. The processing-organisms. Creatures engineered—or perhaps evolved—into living machines. Mycological computers whose calculations are performed by the growth patterns of slime molds solving complex problems through simple hunger. Fungal networks that remember everything that's ever happened here, storing memories in chemical gradients that can be read by those who know how to taste them.

The loudest sound comes from what's left of the foundry itself, the old heart of this place that beats on long after the original purpose was forgotten. It's a noise like nothing you've ever heard—a rhythmic pounding that isn't quite mechanical, a wet thump thump thump that shakes the entire cavern system at exactly 67.8 beats per minute. Some say it's the heartbeat of the thing they found down here, the original seed that started all this. Others say it's just the last remaining pump fabricator, still trying to fulfill its ancient programming even though everything it was built to service has long since grown around and through and beyond it.

You're walking down what used to be Foundry Main when you feel it. The first tingle in your fingertips. The slight increase in ambient humidity. The way the light shifts towards the red end of the spectrum, like sunset seen through blood. It happens every 3.7 hours, like clockwork, though nobody knows what clocks are anymore.

The Growth Spasm.

The entire city contracts. You feel it in your bones, in your teeth, in the way your hair tries to stand on end even though there's no static charge. Everywhere around you people—if they're still people—freeze in place and tilt their heads back as the chemical rain starts falling from nowhere and everywhere. It's not dangerous, they told you. It won't hurt you unless you're allergic. But what they didn't tell you is that everyone is allergic, eventually. You're just allergic to different things.

In your case, you start growing scales. Small at first, just ridges of something hard and smooth pushing up through the skin of your forearms. They itch. They burn. They feel like the bones of creatures that never existed trying to surface through your flesh. By tomorrow they'll be plates. By next week they'll be armor. By next month—if you stay that long—they'll be the beginning of wings.

The weird thing is, it doesn't hurt as much as you expected. The weird thing is, it feels kind of... right? Like your body remembers being something else before it was human. Like all this time you've been wearing a costume that's finally ready to come off.

The Spasm passes. The city exhales. The noise level slowly climbs back up to its eternal roar. The people around you unfreeze and continue their conversations where they left off, as if nothing happened. For them, maybe nothing did. For them, maybe this is normal.

You keep walking. There's a bar—if that's the right word for a place where they serve drinks fermented from the sap of trees that grow upside down and bloom with flowers that look like human faces—that you've heard about. They say the bartender remembers everything. They say he—or it, or they—can tell you how to get back up if you want to.

But here's the thing: you're already starting to forget what up means.

Here's the thing: you're already starting to grow roots.
