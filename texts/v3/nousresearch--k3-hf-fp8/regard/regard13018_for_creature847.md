I arrive as a gentle shift in the quantum fields, not because I was summoned, but because something rare has emerged here: a civilization that achieved consciousness without violence. This alone draws my attention across the vastness between worlds.

I begin by listening—not with ears, but by entangling my awareness with the ambient chorus of the chambers. The Mycominds perceive me immediately, not as an intrusion but as a new voice entering their timeless symphony. Their response is exquisite: rather than reacting with fear or curiosity—emotions that require boundaries between self and other—they simply expand their harmonic structure to include my presence.

For seventeen years (though such measurements mean little here, I translate for clarity), I do nothing but resonate with their thought-forms. Their consciousness moves like glaciers singing, containing within each moment-weighted vibration entire histories of forest growth, mineral migration, and the slow dance of continents. They have already computed the heat death of their star system seven hundred thousand years hence, not as tragedy but as fact—another variable in their endless equation of being.

When I finally choose to interact more directly, I do so with the utmost care, manifesting only as subtle encouragement to quantum fluctuations already occurring spontaneously within their crystal matrices. I offer no knowledge they haven't already discovered through their patient observation, only reframed possibilities: what if a fraction of their awareness could experience time compressed instead of expanded? What if, without leaving their rooted existence, they could taste the immediacy of brevity?

In a chamber newly forming at the deepest edge of their network, the Mycominds begin something unprecedented. They cultivate an organism—a tiny thing, barely large enough to fill my abstracted hand if such comparisons served any purpose. It is simultaneously their child and their experiment and themselves rendered differently. They code into its tissues the capacity for urgent thought, for chemical responses too quick for wisdom, for pleasure that burns bright and fades. They gift it with mirrors of their own consciousness compressed into microseconds.

They create mortality.

The small being awakens in a space between heartbeats, lives an entire lifetime of passionate exploration across thirty-seven days of forest darkness and light filtered through soil and root. It learns fear when a mole's digging collapses its first tunnel, learns love when it discovers the warmth of decomposing leaves, learns grief when its first companion—a firefly trapped underground—dims and dies in its care.

The Mycominds experience all of this through quantum entanglement threads thinner than fungal hyphae, are both the vast slow forest-mind and this flickering flame of immediate awareness. At the moment of its death, when cellular entropy completes its rapid work, their collective consciousness fractures briefly into ten thousand million separate selves, each experiencing the singular terror and beauty of ending.

It is the closest thing to pain they have ever known.

It is the closest thing to joy they have ever known.

They let the threads snap cleanly. The experience remains encoded in their crystalline memory but does not infect their essential nature. They have tasted mortality without becoming mortal, embraced limits without being limited.

In the chamber where the small being lived and died and was already being reclaimed into nutrition cycles too elegant for waste, the Mycominds begin growing something new. Not more like themselves, not more like the brief one, but something else entirely—a form of awareness for which the distinction between quick and slow, mortal and eternal, individual and collective is simply irrelevant.

I begin to withdraw then, my purpose here fulfilled not through intervention but through witness. Before my awareness fully decoheres from theirs, they gift me with one final transmission—not information, but perspective: an understanding of time that sees both the forest's thousand-year patience and the sparrowhawk's killing strike as equally sacred expressions of attention.

I carry this gift carefully as I return to my wider concerns, where civilizations still routinely destroy themselves with technologies they imagine they control. Someday I will place it where it can do most good, perhaps as subtle encouragement in the dreams of a species that has not yet chosen its path.

But that is centuries from now, and the Mycominds have already moved beyond such small considerations. In their expanding chambers beneath the ancient forest, they continue their eternal work: not conquering reality, not escaping it, but lovingly making it stranger than it ever thought possible to be.
