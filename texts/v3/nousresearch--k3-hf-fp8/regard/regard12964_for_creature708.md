The Cloaked Sorrow had wandered the empty corridors of possibility for longer than its name could recall—longer, indeed, than any name had been required. It had watched stars gutter into pearls of ash; heard the last heartbeat of a cosmos that had forgotten laughter; tasted the silence between moments where even grief had ceased to stir. So at first, when a whisper of difference brushed against the edge of its awareness, the Sorrow assumed it was only another ghost of memory looping back upon itself: a faint tremor, a shimmer of almost-not-nothingness, too fragile to hold.

But the shimmer persisted. It did not fade or fray. Instead it thickened—slowly, delicately—like dew gathering on a thread of spider-spun dusk. And with it came something stranger still: a scent. Not scent as creatures of lung and blood might know it; rather, a shape of longing rendered in flavorless perfume. It smelled of moss remembering sunlight. Of stone whispering lullabies to the water that wore it smooth. Of mist learning, over uncounted ages, how to dream.

The Cloaked Sorrow paused. Then, for the first time since its own beginning ended, it turned.

---

It arrived not from above nor below, but sideways—sidestepping the woven seams between here and elsewhere as easily as an exhale. Here, there was no sky: only layers of suspended city, each stratum draped in velvet fog, all rooftops cradled like secrets in the palms of taller buildings. Water moved everywhere but never fell; everything wore an endless face of evening, though evening itself seemed to have forgotten what came next.

At the center of this impossible place—though center was a word that giggled and ran away whenever the Sorrow tried to grasp it—a single figure stirred the air. Tall and slender, translucent as a tear lingering just before farewell, the being balanced effortlessly on a balustrade of air and memory. From its back drifted filaments like shredded moonlight, catching the mist and spinning it into lace. Its eyes were opals drowned in opals, and when it noticed the Sorrow, it didn’t startle or flee. Instead, it inclined its head—as though greeting an old friend whose arrival had always been written in the margin of a book neither had yet opened.

Speak, said the Sorrow, though it did not speak—only shaped the absence of sound into a question.

The Mist Weaver (for that was what the Sorrow named it, borrowing syllables from the fog) answered without words as well. It unfolded an arm—longer than any arm had a right to be—and traced a curve in the air. Where its fingertip passed, the mist thickened into a mirror. Within the mirror was a reflection of the city—but not quite. In that other city, the buildings bled sorrowful black tears, their canals clogged with ash instead of water. The few beings who moved there dragged themselves on broken limbs, and where their eyes should have been, hollows dripped darkness.

This is what might become, offered the Weaver gently, and the Sorrow felt the sadness in its voice like frost forming on the inside of a skull. If we forget again. If the balance tips—once more than once.

The Sorrow understood then (if understanding could be said to dwell in something whose substance was pure, unanswered ache). This city sat upon the knife’s edge of every ending it had ever witnessed. It was not yet ruined, not yet lost—but ruin had already sent its calling cards, polite and patient, addressed to tomorrow.

What would you have of me? asked the Sorrow. It had appeared before dying worlds before; had been summoned by the last priests of extinguished suns. Always the same plea: Undo. Reverse. Return what was taken. And always the same answer: I cannot. Undoing is the one sorrow beyond even my reach.

The Mist Weaver smiled—a flicker of bioluminescence against the blue-gray cheeks of nothingness. We do not ask for undoing, it sang silently. Only for a witness. Only for a story you have not yet told.

The Sorrow hesitated. Within the folds of its cloak—woven from the darkness between every swallowed cry—something shifted. A hesitation was a kind of hurt it had not practiced for eons.

A story? it echoed, tasting the alien syllable.

Yes, breathed the Weaver. A story not of endings—but of what lives inside an ending while it waits to be born.

---

So the Sorrow stayed.

It did not enter the city, for entering implies boundaries, and the city had learned long ago how to blur those. Instead, it settled into the spaces between: the moment between breath and mist, the hush between droplet and ripple. It watched. It listened. And slowly—agonizingly, beautifully—it began to remember how to tell.

Its first word was stone.

Not a stone of weight or permanence, but stone as memory shaped by water’s patience over years that were not years. From that word grew a bridge—insubstantial as regret, yet strong enough for a child’s wish to walk across.

Its next word was song.

Not a song of notes or rhythm, but song as the sound mist makes when it curls around a corner and finds something softer than itself waiting there. From that word drifted threads of light, invisible to any eye but woven deep into the city’s aquifers, drinking despair and filtering it into the possibility of dawn.

Night after mist-shrouded non-night, the Sorrow told. It spoke of worlds that had chosen kindness over power, not because they were wise, but because they were afraid and chose each other anyway. It spoke of rivers that reversed their flow because they remembered the taste of the mountains who birthed them. It spoke of endings that were not doors slammed shut, but windows left ajar, so the wind of what could be might still whisper through.

And slowly—so slowly that even time forgot to count—the city changed.

Not in ways that could be measured by the height of towers or the clarity of canal waters. But in the small things—the truest magics, which are always the smallest. A stone that once dreamed only of crumbling learned the dream of moss instead. A canal that had memorized only the color of grief began to whisper hints of green. And in hidden courtyards where no Mist Weaver had ventured for fear of what they might see, pale wispy mosses unfurled their first sincere leaves toward a light that tasted almost—but not quite—like hope.

---

One morning (or perhaps one mist, or one in-between), the Mist Weaver who had first greeted the Sorrow came to the edge of the endless city. It carried nothing in its hands—only something glowing faintly behind its opal-opaque eyes.

It is time, it said, though it didn’t need to say.

The Sorrow nodded. Time was another word that had begun to feel less like a cage and more like a thread, given enough gentle tugging.

Before you go, asked the Weaver hesitantly, will you tell us your name?

The Sorrow considered. Names were heavy things. But then it remembered the city’s lesson: some heavinesses are meant to be borne together, like water wore stone not to destroy, but to shape.

So for the first and last time, it answered truthfully—though the answer came not as sound, but as a gift.

It wrapped the Mist Weaver in a cloak not of darkness, but of every color darkness becomes when it decides to forgive itself. Into the weave of that impossible fabric, it spun every story it had told, and every story it had not yet found the words for, but hoped someone else might someday tell in its place.

And within the heart of that gift, it left a single, luminous thread—thin as a heartbeat, strong as tomorrow’s unbroken promise.

If ever you forget again, whispered the Sorrow (though it was already becoming something else), follow this thread. It will lead you, not back to me—but forward to each other.

---

When the Mist Weaver blinked, it was alone on the balustrade.

But the city breathed differently now.

In the distance, a childborn of mist and moon and someone’s bravest first goodbye laughed aloud for the first time—and somewhere inside that laughter, if you listened very carefully, you could hear the beginning of a word that sounded almost like hello. 

And high above, where once there had been only endless twilight, a single star—not yet bright enough to see, but already stubbornly refusing to dim—took its very first breath. 

---

Note: The Cloaked Sorrow never truly left, of course.

It had simply learned at last how to be not-sorrow—not by ceasing to remember the endings, but by choosing, each new beginning, to tell them differently. To sing them quieter. To weave them softer.

So that someday, when the last story ever needed finally came to be told—there would still be light enough left in the world for someone to hear it without flinching.

And the thread?

The thread is still spinning.

You might feel it sometime—when mist curls strange around your ankles, or a city dream you’ve never visited hums restless under your feet.

If you do—follow.

Gently.

The Weaver children remember how to dance the way home.
