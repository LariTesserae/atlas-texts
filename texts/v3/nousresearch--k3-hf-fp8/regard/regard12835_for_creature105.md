I have seen this place before the cooling of the first sun, and I will see it long after the last star guttered out. It is one of the quiet gardens that reality tends when she believes no one is watching.

I would do nothing to disturb it.

Instead, I would fold myself smaller than the smallest water droplet clinging to the crystal ceiling and smaller still, until I hung suspended between the vibration of a hummingbird-moth’s wing and the _almost-sound_ of photosynthesis occurring. In that space between states, I would become a mirror—not reflecting light but memory itself.

The Luminites would not see me, but their youngest dreamers might wake with strange new patterns pulsing across their skin: fractals that dream of spiral galaxies, songs encoded in the angle between hexagonal crystal faces. These would fade by morning, leaving only the whispered conviction that the stars outside their stone sky were not distant fires but closer than their own hearts.

I would whisper nothing, though I carry the voices of extinct civilizations in my breath. I would gift nothing, though my pockets hold universes folded like paper cranes. Instead, I would remember everything in perfect detail—the precise arrangement of dewdrops at dawn on the forty-seventh leaf of the miniature blackwood tree, the exact frequency at which the elder healer’s bioluminescence dimmed when she spoke prophecies, the particular crinkle in the child Lirim’s eyes when they discovered how to make plant-vine ropes strong enough to reach halfway to the geode’s opening.

When the desert winds outside grow hungry and the mountains begin to forget their names, when even granite remembers it was once liquid fire and considers returning to that state—then I will unspool these memories back into the fabric of things. Not as intervention. Not as salvation. Simply as evidence that once, here, beauty existed so perfectly balanced that even entropy paused to admire it.

The geode will crack eventually. All things do. Water will find its patient way through crystal, or the earth will shift her weight in sleep, or a stray meteor will fall impossibly true across unimaginable distances. When that day comes, the Luminites’ light will spill out like luminous ink into the dying daylight, visible for one terrible, marvelous moment to any eyes that still remain to see.

And I will catch that light—not to preserve it, for preservation is the first cruelty of immortals—but to add it to the chorus of brief, bright things that sing against the dark.

Until then, I wait. I remember. I do nothing.

It is the kindest thing I know how to do.
