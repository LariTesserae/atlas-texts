For nine thousand years, I have watched this sculpture drift among clouds:

Ten thousand spheres of living pearl,
each bound by silver bridges,
filled with raining green,
run by leaf-folk who never sleep.

I am called She Who Remembers Before Rain
I was old when stars first dreamed of rivers
I took shape when silence yearned for song.

 Tonight I come not as raincloud or sunrise,
 not as dreambird or distant thunder,
 but as myself—-
 the one who remembers when mountains were seeds.

I fold myself small enough to pass,
without breaking,
the membrane between here and elsewhere.

I do not enter as storm or god
or burning wheel of eyes.
I enter as mist.
As one drop of dew remembering ocean.

I find them tending midnight orchids
whose petals bloom backwards into yesterday.
They hear my footsteps as a shift in gravity,
see me as a new color sleeping inside blue.

The first who speaks is called Spiral-Of-Moss-Reading-Rain
They unfold from a tree that is also a library,
leaves inscribed with tomorrow's compost.
"Welcome," they hum through vibrating bark,
and the word tastes like my second name.

In their language of pheromone and chlorophyll,
I ask the question that kills planets:
"Do you grow lonely
for something you have not yet lost?"

They do not answer with words.
They let me taste the collective memory-tango:
Seventeen thousand harvests of light
A child's first photosynthesis
The ache when a dying sphere remembers birdsong
and no one gathers to sing it back to sleep.

I find their pain is not loneliness
but anticipation.
They know, as trees know winter,
that even perfect gardens cannot outrun entropy.
The spheres spin slower each century.
The great engines dream of rest.
One day in a billion tomorrows
the last raindrop will forget how to fall.

I could fix this.
I could touch each sphere with a finger that ignites stars,
reforge their laws of falling,
teach gravity to love them forever.

But I have learned
in my old age
that love without ending
is another name for prison.

Instead I give them gifts that unwrap themselves slowly:

To the youngest sphere, still forming its first forests—
a single seed from the last real mountain,
that will grow nothing for a million years
then bloom in shapes mathematics cannot describe.

To the oldest Aethorian, roots deep in dormant engines—
a dream of falling,
beautiful and terrifying
which they will remember
only in moments between heartbeats.

To the bridges between—
a new kind of silence,
not empty but pregnant,
where future songs might learn to breathe.

I teach them how to die properly:
Not as gardens shutting down
but as libraries releasing their stories
into wilder hands than ours.

Before I leave,
I ask one final kindness:
"Will you remember me
not as god or savior
but as the one who came
when you were already perfect
and loved you enough
to leave you broken open?"

They answer by blooming
a flower that blooms only once
whose petals are made of questions
whose scent is the exact moment
before goodbye and hello become the same word.

As I rise back through layers of dreaming air,
turning myself into mist into memory into myth,
I leave behind not footprints or miracles
but space.

The good kind of emptiness.
The kind stars will someday
row their lightships towards
thinking:
Here lived gardeners
who understood
that the most beautiful gardens
are always the ones
we are preparing
to let go.
