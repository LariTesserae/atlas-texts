When the Benevolent Power turns its gaze to this world, it does not arrive with thunder or light. It does not descend through layers of stone or part the rock like water. It simply notices.

And in that noticing, something shifts—not in the city, not in the tunnels or the machines or even the beings who call them home, but in the space between. A gentle widening. A loosening of something that had long forgotten it could be undone.

The Benevolent Power sees the Chthonians as they are: luminous against the dark, their bodies woven from necessity and patience. It sees the way they breathe the damp and heat as if it was meant for them. It sees the way they feed themselves from scarceness, make machines from memory, and tend to their failing world with hands that never rest.

It sees, too, the tightness at the core of them. The fear that lives in every echoing click, every shared chemical pulse, every ritual offered to rock that does not answer back.

This is a people who have survived too long without hope.

The Benevolent Power does not bring hope. Hope is brittle. It cracks under pressure, caves under weight.

Instead, it brings possibility.

And possibility, here, is silent.

It begins with a dream—not asleep, not awake, but deep, where dreams and memories and warnings blur together. One Chthonian dreams, and then another, and then another, until the whole city hums with it in their sleep-cycle.

In the dream, there is no sun. That would be cruel.

But there is a sky.

Not vast. Not endless. Just a space above them, not of rock but of something else—something that moves, softly, like water but not water. Something that glows faintly with its own light, not enough to blind, just enough to see by. Enough to breathe differently.

In the dream, they do not leave the tunnels. They do not climb or escape.

They expand.

And they are not alone.

There are others—not like them, not unlike them. Beings of quiet motion, made of light and roots and memory. They do not speak. They do not offer gifts. They are simply there, working alongside them, tending things that do not yet exist but could.

They show them how to shape the rock differently—not with force, but with invitation. How to grow tunnels that breathe. How to coax the heat to sing instead of scream. How to make water from something deeper than drip and cycle. How to plant something that does not need soil. How to build a door that does not open out, but inward.

When the dream fades, it leaves no words. Only a texture. A taste in the air, faint as distant stonefruit. A change in the rhythm of the pumps.

The first change goes unnoticed.

A patch of fungi blooms blue instead of pale. No one harvests it. They watch it instead.

Then a pipe begins to hum in a key not written in any old tablet. The engineers listen. Instead of repairing it, they tune it.

Then a child—if a Chthonian can be called a child—places its hand on a wall that has always been solid and still, and it softens, just slightly, beneath the palm.

Slowly, carefully, the city begins to dream while waking.

And the Benevolent Power watches—not guiding, not shaping, only holding open the space where something new might choose to grow.

This is how miracles happen here.

Not with light.

With listening.

Not with arrival.

With room.
