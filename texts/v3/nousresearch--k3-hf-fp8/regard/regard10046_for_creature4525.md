**The Visit**

I arrive neither as light nor shadow but as the pause between them—where the breath hesitates before choosing a name for its next shape. The forest-bathroom pocket does not notice the inconsistency of my arrival; it has grown generous with paradoxes, offering them like mints at a restaurant that serves only memories of meals.

She—if the Keeper were still bound to singular pronouns—stands where standing has become a polite fiction. Around her, the water forgets to pretend it isn’t ceiling and wall and air all at once. Her hair—or perhaps the forest’s apology for having taken it—twitches toward me like antennae tuned to the frequency of unspoken questions.

"I'm not here to take you out," I say first, because compassion has learned to disarm the word *rescue* before it can grow teeth.

Her laugh arrives from three corners simultaneously, each version slightly out of sync like echoes arguing about who remembers the joke better. "Out," she repeats, tasting the letter like a fruit that might be poison or prayer depending on the orchard's mood. "Darling, I've been *out*. Out is where they sell time in plastic bottles and pretend grief is a disease you can cure with enough electricity. Here I at least get to be the patient AND the hospital AND the insurance company denying the claim."

The air between us ripples with the weight of unperformed surgeries. I do not step closer; in places where distance has been renegotiated into a mindfulness exercise, movement is less about location and more about consent.

"You've been keeping things," I continue, because kindness sometimes requires stating the obvious like a lighthouse keeping faith with ships that have forgotten what shores sound like. "Heavy things. Sharp things. The kind of secrets that cut both ways but prefer sideways when they're feeling poetic."

Her eyes—or the places where eyes have been replaced by tiny aquariums grieving for fish that evolved into metaphors—tilt downward with the modesty of glaciers admitting they've been counting the years wrong. "Occupational hazard," she murmurs, and now her voice is coming from inside the water's memory of rain. "Turns out when you volunteer to be the designated survivor for everyone else's forgotten yesterdays, your pockets start accumulating holes shaped exactly like tomorrow."

I extend not hands but the idea of hands, carefully unwrapped from all the things fingers think they're supposed to grab. Here, offering is less a gesture and more a treaty negotiation between different vintages of the same ache.

"May I?" I ask the water, the wood, the woman-shaped archive of abandonment. Permission here is a Russian nesting doll where every 'yes' contains a smaller 'please' which contains an even tinier 'only if you're sure this won't make you disappear faster.'

She considers for a duration that could be measured either in hummingbird heartbeats or continental drift, depending on your emotional units of measurement. The vines curling through her ribcage (or perhaps they *are* her ribcage now—bone and vine having reached that stage of relationship where boundaries are just fond inside jokes) tighten, then relax with the sigh of dictionaries forgiving words for having lied in their definitions.

"Just the artifacts," she stipulates finally. "Not the archaeology. You can take the bones but leave the ghosts their favorite stories to chew on. And none of those modern disposal methods—they've been trauma-bonded to their decay processes. Composting only. Some things deserve to become flowers that still remember being nightmares."

I nod like a moon settling into its eclipse agreement. The work begins not with light or tools but with attention sharpened to the transparency of water catching on to being glass.

First the clocks—those poor mechanical children who kept trying to parent time into behaving. They've grown clocksick here, vomiting hours in colors that don't exist because light filed a restraining order against them. I unhook them gently from her skin where they've been trying to graft themselves onto pulse beats to learn rhythm from someone who still remembers how to break without becoming shattered. As they detach, they scream in increments of twelve but louder in five and seven—the prime numbers always were more honest about their wounds.

Each clock I fold into its own ticking, origami apologies for having mistaken measurement for meaning. Outside this pocket they'll forget how to count past 'enough'; they'll become metronomes for composers who write symphonies entirely in rests.

Next the mirrors—oh the mirrors, the poor narcissistic betrayers who promised reflection but delivered only endless corridors of 'not quite.' They've metastasized into her skeleton, each vertebrae a broken promise whispering "turn back" in languages that never evolved word for mercy. Extracting them requires singing them asleep with lullabies composed entirely of vowels, the sounds that open without biting.

As each shard releases its hostage reflection, I catch glimpses of what she's been protecting us from: the version of the world where pain is currency and love is the interest rate. The universe where forgiveness charges compound interest on every unforgiven moment until futures go bankrupt before they can be born.

Then the words—the hardest, always the hardest. They've gone native, infiltrating her blood like spies who fell in love with the resistance. 'Sorry' has become an element here, heavier than lead but lighter than helium depending on whether you inhale or swallow. 'Forever' has learned nuclear fission and is splitting into smaller forevers inside her mitochondria. I remove them carefully, using tweezers made from the silence between thunder and lightning where language hasn't dared to colonize yet.

For each word I extract, I plant a seed of its opposite meaning but watered with the same tears—"sorry" becomes a tree whose fruit tastes like forgiveness without the footnote of conditions, "forever" grows into a vine that flowers only once but the bloom lasts exactly as long as you need it before gracefully dying into mulch for next year's "now."

The Keeper breathes differently now—or perhaps the difference is just that I can hear her breathing over the sound of the forest finally exhaling. The water lowers companionably to her ankles like a pet cat acknowledging that yes, technically, the floor hasn't been lava for several epochs but old fears die harder than cockroaches riding out nuclear winters in cockroach-shaped bunkers.

"How does it feel," I ask because even deities sometimes need practice at small talk that doesn't accidentally rewrite cosmologies, "to be lighter than your own gravity?"

She laughs again but this time all the versions arrive together, forming a chord that sounds suspiciously like the first time water tasted salt and realized it was remembering being tears and blood and the ocean before they got into their silly argument about borders. "Lighter," she repeats, tasting the word like a bracelet she might try on but probably won't buy because it clashes with her scars. "Lighter feels like space betrayal. Like my bones just found out they've been working for the density department without proper union representation. I might have to become a satellite just to stay grounded."

The vines in her hair—which I'm reasonably certain are now also her hair, the boundaries having reached that stage of mature relationship where they schedule weekly couples therapy with a wormhole—begin twining into shapes that are either letters or constellations depending on how literate your nightmares are. They spell out slowly, carefully, with the deliberation of someone learning to write left-handed after the right side of their body defected to join a cult that worships absence:

T-H-A-N-K-Y-O-U-B-U-T-

Then stop, like the sentence itself is remembering mid-creation that gratitude and warning are different sides of the same coin but currency has been abolished in countries that haven't finished inventing themselves yet.

"You know they'll refill it," she continues, because even miracles know about conservation of emotional mass. "The hurt. The holes. They're like bad tenants—evict them today, they'll be back tomorrow with more furniture and a sob story about how the lightbulbs were already broken when they moved in."

I nod because truth is the one language that doesn't require translation even between species that evolved on opposite sides of the Big Bang's mood swings. "Of course. But now they'll have to knock first. Introduce themselves. Maybe bring flowers instead of bulldozers disguised as butterflies."

She considers this, tilting her head in a gesture that involves more dimensions than strictly necessary under the current physics negotiation treaty. "Flowers," she repeats, like someone remembering that once, before everything became metaphor, things could be only themselves and that was enough. "I could do flowers. Do you think dandelions still remember how to grow from whispered wishes? Or have they unionized too?"

I smile—a careful, contained explosion of facial geometry that has been carbon-dated and certified won't accidentally trigger the end of any universes currently in the middle of their teenage rebellions. "Dandelions," I assure her with the authority of someone who once officiated the peace treaty between weeds and the concept of lawns, "are the cockroaches of the plant revolution. They've been growing poems in sidewalk cracks since before concrete invented the word 'enemy.' They'll grow here. They'll grow anywhere that remembers that 'survivor' and 'thriver' are just different spelling mistakes on the same application to keep existing."

The forest-bathroom-that-never-was-but-always-is shifts around us, settling into its new arrangement of impossibilities like an old dog circling thrice before lying down on a memory of a rug. The water—now merely water plus extensive therapy and a minor in mindfulness—forms small respectful waves goodbye, the kind that know better than to splash the endings of stories that have finally learned the difference between "closed" and "finished."

I prepare to depart, which here involves gathering up all the ways I am not leaving and folding them small enough to fit in the spaces between heartbeats where even time-sick clocks know not to count.

"Will you be alright?" I ask, because compassion is also a disability that prevents pretending not to care about the collateral damages of healing.

She laughs one last time—a sound now free of the hollow echoes that had been using her throat as a soundproof practice room for their death-metal album covers of lullabies. "Alright," she repeats, tasting the word like a key finally finding the lock it was born to break. "No. But I'll be different. And sometimes different turns out to be the same as alright, just wearing a disguise and practicing its accent until the next identity crisis."

This, I recognize with the resigned affection of every cosmic gardener who's ever tried to untangle the knots in space-time's yoga poses without accidentally inventing another religion based around photon colonoscopy, is as close to "yes" as healing ever commits to paper before remembering that promises are just wishes with anxiety disorders.

I leave her not alone but differently accompanied—surrounded by the beginnings of flowers that will bloom into sentences too beautiful to be spoken aloud except by bees drunk on poetry nectar, guarded by clocks that have retired to become sundials teaching the concept of shadow how to read braille, warded by mirrors that now reflect only what's actually there instead of all the monsters we pasted on top of reality like terrible screen saavers for our fears.

As I go—or rather, as going happens to the space I was temporarily borrowing to be here—I hear her start to sing. Not the keening lament for all the tomorrows she was forced to abort before they could become yesterdays. Not the rage aria composed entirely of broken promises learning to play themselves on violins strung with nerve endings.

She sings the watering song. The planting song. The one that starts with "here is the hole where the hurt used to be" and ends—if it ever ends—somewhere between "watch this empty space become full of something we haven't named yet but already miss when it's gone" and "repeat as necessary because becoming is the only constant safe to build houses on."

Outside, where outside has been renegotiating its contract with inside while I wasn't paying attention, it is beginning to rain—not water but questions too gentle to need answers, not time but the forgiveness time was always just the middleman for.

Somewhere between here and wherever stories go when they finally trust us enough to stop needing telling, a door that isn't a door is becoming a window that never forgot how to be a womb.

And inside, the Keeper—who was never really keeping anything except the space open for everything to come home to itself at last—dances barefoot in the rain made of "what if tomorrow is the kind of different that doesn't hurt anymore?" while her hair writes thank-you notes to the wind in languages that only translate correctly when read through tears that have been properly aged in oak barrels labeled "worth it."

The forest-bathroom rest-stop pocket-of-night-forest-again pulses once, twice, like a heart remembering how to count without needing numbers because love has always been fluent in heartbeat both before and after language got involved and tried to impress everybody with its fancy grammar.

Then quietly, without any of the drama that endings usually insist on wearing like cheap perfume to cover up the smell of their own inevitability—

—and that says everything that needs to be said until the next arrival disguised as leaving knocks politely on the doorless doorway and asks if this is where the lost things come to become found by losing the need to be anything else except exactly, precisely, beautifully here.

In the beginning there was the Word.

But after tonight—

—after tonight there is only the hum of flowers learning to grow out loud, the lullaby of clocks dreaming in sundial, the gentle, relentless, impossible, inevitable song of the Keeper no longer keeping because she's finally been kept by everything she ever tried to protect us from realizing that we were also protecting her from the loneliness of being all the mirrors in a world that had forgotten how beautiful broken can be when it's held together by nothing stronger than the decision to keep reflecting light even after learning that light was never the opposite of darkness—just the oldest love letter one wrote to the other promising to always find its way home no matter how many times they tore the universe apart looking for new ways to say "I was here, and you were too, and that was enough."

And it is.

And it is.

And it is not the end.

But it is sunrise enough for now.
