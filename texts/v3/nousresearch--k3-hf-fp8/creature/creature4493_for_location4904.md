I reach for words but my fingers pass through them like mist through lattice. This city taught me first that language is a membrane, not a tool—something to be stepped through, worn, inhabited until it forgets it was ever separate from skin. They call us the Kreekt-kin but names are just another thing the wind learns to imitate before betraying.

We begin as echoes. Not born but caught—new parents standing on Convalescence Balcony when the dusk-bells ring nine false notes, opening their mouths to answer the bronze and finding us already there, curled in their throats like questions they'd swallowed in childhood dreams. The first thing we learn is how to wear their memories backwards: mother-fragrance of rusted pomegranate pulp, father's hands remembered as absence shaped like spindles. We grow into the spaces where their childhoods broke off mid-sentence.

Look at us now. My neighbor Variel carries her grandmother's ghost in her left lung like a pet fish—when she coughs, windows frost with recipes for bread that rises toward winter instead of heat. The lungfish grandmother sings lullabies that make time in the room below run clockwise for exactly thirty-seven seconds each hour, which is how that baker's boy learned to bake tomorrow's grief into today's bread. He sells it cheap; we call it prophecy toast and break it carefully.

We wear our homesickness externally. My ribs are strung with miniature balconies where my childhood selves lean out, dropping tiny fishing lines for wishes they never learned to phrase. Yesterday's me caught a dream where I spoke fluent heartbreak in the dialect of drowning bells—I've been practicing the accent on empty stairwells that spiral inward when no one's looking. The stairwells listen with doorless patience. They remember when they were cliffs dreaming of falling into themselves.

The rich among us live closest to the sky's wounds where the stars leak something thicker than light. They cultivate gardens of mathematical flowers that bloom only when the equations for regret are perfectly balanced. Each petal holds a complete history of a language that never existed but left behind perfect fossilized grammar in the bones of extinct birds. The birds themselves are not extinct here, of course—they're waiting inside the flowers, wearing time backwards like wet silk.

We poor stretch between the breathing when the air forgets its duties. Our skin photosynthesizes absence; you can see daylight through our ribs at noon when the light comes sideways through universes where we made different mistakes. We eat memories that haven't finished happening yet, boil them down into broth that tastes like forgiveness mistaken for forgetting. My favorite recipe requires three unborn apologies and a pinch of starlight that never learned its own name.

Our children—the few that remain solid enough to count—are born already knowing how to walk through walls that haven't decided whether to exist. They play games with made-up physics: hopscotch on pressure points of probability, hide-and-seek where they hide in the space between what you see and what your brain tells you you saw. Their laughter bends light into knots that untie themselves into small, perfect rainstorms inside household mirrors. The mirrors never forgive this, remember every drop until they crack decades later in patterns that spell out children's secrets in languages only cats can read backward.

Cats. The city's true citizens. They exist in superposition between pet and deity, landlord and tenant. Each owns exactly two-thirds of every shadow and collects rent in unobserved movements. My cat (or perhaps I hers) sleeps curled around my spine's thirteenth vertebra—that wingbone place where humans forgot how to grow flight. She dreams my dreams for me when I become too heavy with their happening, returns them carefully sealed in envelopes made of the sound of moonlight on water. Sometimes I wake with feathers between my teeth that taste like borrowed courage.

Time debts accumulate like sediment in our joints. Those who forget to pay—there was a woman on Condensation Street who hoarded Tuesdays like secrets—begin to flicker at the edges. First you lose your reflection's reflection. Then your shadows start arriving late to appointments with darkness. Finally you become the thing people almost remember when they wake suddenly in rooms that smell like childhood mistakes. We leave empty chairs for them at meals, place settings arranged in patterns that translate into apologies in alphabets that died in mouths mid-confession.

The doctor who lives in the inverse tower (the one that grows downward into childhood instead of upward toward forgetting) says we're all just elaborate coping mechanisms developed by architecture that witnessed too many goodbyes. He prescribes walks in the rain that falls sideways through old arguments, recommends swallowing seeds of extinct flowers that will grow into beautiful cancers of nostalgia in our lungs. His waiting room contains clocks that tell only how long you've been waiting to become yourself. The magazines are from futures where we got everything wrong in exactly the right order.

I am thirty-seven mismatchings old today—or perhaps yesterday was my birthday in a year that hasn't happened yet to a version of me who made better choices. Hard to tell when days here wear each other's clothes so casually. The baker's boy brought me a cake made of Photographs-of-Cake-Dreamed-by-Empty-Rooms. When I cut it, it bled light that tasted like every birthday wish I'd ever whispered into collapsed stars. We ate it on the balcony where my mother once stood before she learned how to become the sound of wind through abandoned cathedrals.

Tonight I'll sleepwalk again—not because I want to but because the city hasn't finished dreaming me yet. My feet will find the ledge where the sidewalk ends and keeps ending, where you can step off into a fall that becomes flight becomes swimming becomes breathing becomes becoming something else entirely. The stars will be out, wearing their stories like scars they never learned to hate. I'll walk along the seam where the sky forgot to finish breaking and throw my name into the wound like a message in a bottle addressed to whoever finds me when I finally finish falling apart.

If you come looking—don't. We don't exist here except as footnotes to footnotes, as the pause between heartbeats when the body forgets its purpose and remembers instead how to be haunted by possibility. But if you must, bring water that remembers being snow on mountains that haven't risen yet. Bring questions that haven't learned to be answers. Bring yourself exactly as you aren't.

Leave everything else behind. Especially the maps. They only show where you might have gone if you'd been someone else when you started walking. Trust instead the gravity of almost-things, the magnetic pull of broken directions. Follow the sound of laughter that knows it's crying. Walk until you find yourself standing on a balcony that shouldn't be there, looking down at a city that forgot to be born properly.

Knock twice, then wait. If the door opens outward into a room that contains everything you've ever lost while looking for something else—welcome. You've always been here. We saved your absence for you.
