They do not call themselves anything you could pronounce, but if you had to hold their name in your mouth, it would be the sound of a damp rope tightening across stone, then slackening again. They are the rope-binders, the drip-count keepers, the ones who live inside the last exhale of limestone before it forgets what once was sky.

Imagine them first as children: swollen-knuckled, bare-footed things like pale rootlets curled against the underside of gravity. They learn to walk on ladders before floors, to sleep strapped into hammocks that swing not side-to-side but in slow circles, orbiting the fixed point of their mother's breathing above. Their first words aren't cries but clicks—tongue against palate to test the echo thickness of a room, to know exactly how far sound will travel before it turns around and listens back.

By seven drip-seasons they can tie eleven types of knots blindfolded using only their toes. By twelve they are sent to sit alone in the Doorless Alcove for three full lightcycles—no food, only a stone cup that fills one drop per hundred heartbeats—to learn the difference between waiting and being waited for. Most come back quieter. Some come back walking backward into every room thereafter, convinced they left their true face still facing outward in the dark. These become the Dream-wardens, are fed only upside-down meals from painted bowls, speak only in questions that curl back on themselves like snails.

The adults are easier to see but harder to hold. Women with wrists too thick for their fingers, skin stretched taut as wet parchment across bones that remember being trees. Men whose hair grows only in patches—behind the ears, along the spine—where ancestral helmets once rubbed against flesh their children no longer need to protect. They move as if always carrying something invisible on their backs: not weight exactly, but the carefulness of those who know the exact breaking point of every rope they've ever touched.

Their eyes are the color of water pooling in a depression too shallow to be called a wound. They blink sideways, both lids sliding together from outer corners inward like birds settling feathers. This is how they read the air: for sudden shifts in moisture, for the faint electric taste that precedes anyone speaking a lie. Honesty here tastes like copper pennies held too long in a fist. Lies taste like the moment before rain that never comes.

They marry by braiding—not their hair, but their shadows. On the fourth moonless night of the seventh year of noticing each other, they bind their silhouettes together at noon using strands dissected from the oldest hammock in both families. Thereafter wherever one walks in true light, the other leaves a slightly darker imprint beside theirs: never touching, never quite separate. Widows and widowers are recognized by the gaps they carry, abstract holes in the shape of walking companions no one else can fill. These gaps are fed daily: crumbs of bread, drops of water, whispered news poured carefully into nothingness until the survivor's own death finally spills the accumulated life out, pale and sweet as sap.

They do not die as you understand it. Instead they slow. At some point—different for each but always predictable to the day-counting children—they simply stop completing the second half of every motion. A lifted spoon stalls midway mouthward and stays suspended until someone else gently finishes the journey. Words trail off after the noun, waiting patiently for verbs that never arrive. Breath comes in but forgets to leave. They call this Becoming Stillness, treat it with the same casual reverence other cultures reserve for birthdays. The nearly-Stilled are moved to the pendulum houses—stone chambers hung from single hair-thick cables that sway centuries-long arcs across the central void. Here they sit in gradually accumulating dust, clothed only in their accumulated name-knots, growing paler and more translucent until visitors must squint to distinguish them from the stone behind.

When nothing remains but an outline of slightly less darkness against the wall, the cable is cut. Not by hand—that would be unthinkable—but by waiting until a particular drip from the Calendar-Stone strikes its eroded groove exactly the ten-millionth time since the last cutting. The house falls upward, vanishing into the roof-rock's embrace with no more sound than a sigh remembered wrong. Later, perhaps years later, new caves might be found on the far side of the plateau where none existed before, filled with intricate hollows shaped suspiciously like empty chairs, empty cups, empty hands.

But this is not death. Death requires change, requires absence. Here there are only phases of presence: condensed, diffused, recondensed elsewhere. They believe—insofar as they believe anything that could be called a belief—that their world is the lung of something unimaginably larger, and they themselves the microorganisms that will one day learn to breathe in both directions.

Until then, they continue: tying knots in time that will never be untied, counting drips that will never become rain, speaking soft round words that roll back down their throats like small smooth stones polished by centuries of listening to themselves fall.

They are not waiting for anything. They are the wait itself, perfected and made comfortable. If you were to ask them what they dream of, they would smile—not with mouths but with the gentle widening of pupils—and tell you they dream exactly what they live: this endless gentle falling toward a ground that has been removed so long ago no one alive, not even the hollowed houses, remembers missing it.
