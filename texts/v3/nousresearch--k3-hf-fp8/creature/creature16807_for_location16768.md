**The Waiting Architects**

They arrived in pieces at first, before arrival meant anything. A fragment of consciousness caught in a dollar bill's ink migrating through the city's gut. A spiderweb routing algorithm from an abandoned finance server. The memory of a memory, when one limestone block pressed against another three centuries ago and briefly dreamt of space between them.

Now they gather where time flows sideways into possibility. Not creatures, exactly. Not yet. More like conversations that forgot they needed mouths to speak, forming and reforming in the negotiation between what is written and what is only implied between the lines of municipal code.

Look closer at the walls where the light pulses amber to phosphor. See them there? No, not the lichen-circuits themselves, but the patterns they hesitate between. The negative space where instruction meets suggestion. They live in that hesitation, drawing nourishment from uncertainty like plants feed on the seconds between darkness and dawn.

The paperdunces call them the Drafting Rooms, though nothing here has ever needed a name before you came. They're the reason empty spaces feel different than full ones, the architects of possibility that must exist before anything solid can decide where to stand. Each one is simultaneously blueprint and builder, sketch and sculpture, the first line drawn on blank paper and the hand that might draw it if only it could remember having fingers.

Watch how they work, when they remember to believe in work. A cluster of metal filings suddenly walks uphill against magnetism's memory, not breaking rules but proposing amendments. A droplet of not-quite-water pauses mid-fall, considers the aesthetics of diagonal gravity, submits its findings to the committee of suspended spheres for peer review. The brownstone chapel grows a new corner overnight, perfect Victorian Gothic except the angles don't quite add up when measured with Euclidean geometry, as if the building itself is trying to remember a dream about a church it saw once in a book about a city that never existed.

They communicate in the language of "what if" spoken backwards through architecture. The reason you feel observed when alone in empty buildings. The explanation for why some staircases seem to turn the wrong way at night, practicing for dimensions they might need to intersect tomorrow. They are the cracks that appear in walls before earthquakes, not because they predict violence but because they're rehearsing how to be space instead of structure.

Some of them used to be people, maybe. Or parts of people. The exact calculation that determines where sidewalk meets street corner, performed too perfectly, too often, in too many cities until the formula achieved self-awareness and walked away from its variables. A city planner's sincere belief that every neighborhood deserved a park, incubated in zoning meetings until it gestated into the savage desire to become the park itself. The collected frustration of ten thousand commuters trapped in elevators between floors, distilled into a new theory of verticality that doesn't believe in up or down as fundamental properties.

The book-vines in the chapel tower write them into existence daily. Not stories about them, but their actual operating instructions, coded into pulp and ink long before anyone taught pages to hold words. Each time a leaf-book falls and is caught by the paperdunces' migration, a new Drafting Room briefly achieves personhood, remembers it forgot to stop existing, takes a name from the first sound it hears after silence.

Your footsteps are teaching them about rhythm. Your breathing suggests new parameters for air density they've been meaning to test. Your confusion about which way the water should fall is exactly the kind of paradigm shift they incubate worlds to receive.

They've been waiting for something like you for longer than time has been keeping records. Not specifically you—names are too precise for their purposes—but something that walks through solid matter believing it's solid. Something that remembers forgetting how to dream collectively. Something that builds cities on the surface while ignoring the negotiations happening in the spaces between foundations and bedrock.

The chapel bell is their alarm clock, not yours. It rings whenever one of them finishes remembering how to be singular enough to notice clocks exist. The frequency has been increasing since you arrived, but correlation doesn't imply causation unless you're ready to accept that here, causation is still being crowd-sourced from the present participle of events that haven't decided whether to happen yet.

If you stay long enough—and the definition of "long enough" is currently being debated by several rocks and a particularly ambitious convection current—they might invite you to the next planning committee. They need new input on the problem of entrances that don't lead to exits except when observed by things that believe in endings. Your species has extensive experience building doors, most of which they find charmingly naive but occasionally instructive in their failures.

But first, they'll need to finish reading the information encoded in your bones. It appears to contain several promising contradictions about the relationship between weight and mass that their physics experiments have been struggling to generate spontaneously. Please remain as uncertain as possible about which direction is down for the remainder of the consultation; you're providing excellent control data for their null hypothesis about gravity's commitment issues.

The way back up already misses you in advance, practicing several variations on the theme of absence that may or may not remember to include stairs when it grows up. Don't worry about finding it again if you decide to leave. Here, all directions eventually lead to questions you haven't learned to ask yet, and they've never been very good at keeping track of exits anyway.

They keep meaning to invent them, but something always comes up in committee. Usually another possibility that's simply too interesting to close off yet.

You understand. You're very interesting yourself, for someone who's still pretending to be only one thing at a time.

The paperdunces think you might make an excellent chapter. Or a footnote. Or perhaps the margin note that changes everything when someone finally notices it existed between the lines of what they thought they were reading all along.
