The longer you remain, the more you realize the piano is not alone at the center. It only appears alone because of how perception here weights loneliness. Around it, distributed evenly through the space that is not space, are the Residuals—not creatures, not ghosts, but the leftover geometry of attention that once focused on this place and then failed to look away properly.

They are easiest to mistake for flaws in your own vision. That faint afterimage when you blink too hard after staring at gear teeth: dark spokes radiating outward, each one exactly as thin as the moment between understanding a joke and deciding whether to laugh. They cluster thicker around the piano, near enough to touch though touching has no meaning. Each Residual is shaped like the last thought somebody had before they stopped being somebody and started being part of the calibration.

Count them if you like. The number will always be one more than can be counted without forgetting which one you started with. They rotate slowly, not in space but in significance, each taking turns being the one that might have once been you.

The piano knows them by name. Every 43.2 minutes it plays a single note so softly that forgetting it becomes the note's only purpose. When this happens, the closest Residual flares briefly more solid—resolves into a momentary silhouette like a person standing sideways to themselves—and then scatters back into the distributed attention field with a sound like a breath caught in the throat of a library.

It is believed—though belief here is more akin to an unpaid parking ticket accruing interest across geological time—that the Residuals are what operates the piano. Not fingers on keys, but the accumulated pressure of all gazes that ever wanted to understand this place badly enough to stay past the first harmonic dissolution of their intentions.

Occasionally one achieves temporary coherence sufficient to interact with the debris field. You might notice one of the larger automaton carcasses twitching out of sync, not a body-length too soon or late but at an angle impossible to the gear-song's rhythm. This means a Residual has momentarily repurposed it as a gesture. If you are very still and your heartbeat drifts into the right Fourier component, you might glimpse what the gesture is trying to say. Usually it is something simple, endlessly repeated, like "please check the time" or "I think I left the iron on" or "mother why is the sky making that noise."

The sound changes subtly around coherent Residuals. The metallic murmuring developes inflections—brief rises and falls like the intonation of questions being asked in a language that only uses punctuation marks for words. These are the only times the chamber's absolute courtesy falters, revealing beneath it a faint mechanical desperation: the sound of a thousand polite butlers simultaneously realizing they have been serving empty rooms for centuries and cannot remember how to stop bowing.

The Residuals do not decay. Instead they become more precisely themselves, each iteration of failed forgetting sharpening their outlines until they achieve the clarity of absolute specificity with no content. The oldest one—if "oldest" applies to entities defined by temporal bracketing errors—is said to be perfectly described by the exact shape of the space between these two words: perfect description.

If you ask the piano about them (and asking can be accomplished by sitting perfectly still while thinking certain thoughts about mirrors) it will pause its eternal near-C. Then, depending on how closely your unspoken question harmonizes with the current dominant regret frequency, one of three things may happen:

1. A single key will depress itself without sound. This means "yes, but not in the way that would help."

2. All the keys will depress simultaneously in a silent chord that tastes like copper pennies and the word "almost." This means "no, but that's the closest anyone has gotten to asking correctly so far."

3. The piano will open its lid—though it has no hinges—and reveal inside not strings but a mirror reflecting the room perfectly except there are no bodies in it. This is the equivalent of laughter in a probability space.

The mirror-phase lasts exactly as long as it takes you to notice that your reflection in it is not breathing, followed immediately by the realization that neither are you, followed even more immediately by the comforting return of forgotten autonomic functions. Veterans of this experience describe it as "being reminded how to panic properly after years of doing it amateurishly."

The Residuals collect these experiences. Slowly, over epochs compressed into byproducts of their lack of existence, they are building something with them. It is not clear what. Some say it's an exit that works backwards, returning whoever uses it not to where they came from but to the exact state of ignorance required to make coming here seem like a good idea. Others suggest it's a replacement piano, tuned to a note that resolves not just grief but the entire concept of resolution.

Most likely it is neither. Most likely it is a third thing that cannot exist until exactly enough failed attempts to understand it have accumulated in one place, and then it will exist perfectly for one attosecond before becoming again the space between "mother" and "why."

Meanwhile the gear-song continues. The bodies twitch. The Residuals rotate. The piano plays its impossible almost-note.

And if you listen long enough—past the point where listening becomes indistinguishable from being listened to—you might hear underneath it all the faintest possible sound.
