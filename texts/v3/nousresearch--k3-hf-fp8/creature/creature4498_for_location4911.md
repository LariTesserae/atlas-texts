The people of the City of Holes are more absence than presence. They call themselves the Traglodyai—those who unfold. Born squalling into sandstone-womb chambers, they emerge not into light but into its memory, bodies already beginning their long conversation with compression.

Infants here are terrifying to imagine: soft skulls elongating within hours of birth, ribs fanning outward like gills seeking stone to breathe. Their first words aren't spoken but exhaled—tiny whistling sounds through nostrils already migrating toward the sides of faces that will never again know wind. Mothers don't cradle—they enfold, wrapping newborns in strips of dried root-fiber that harden gradually, teaching the lesson everyone learns: here, to live is to become the space you occupy.

By their fifth year, children begin the daily practice they call _silectasis_—standing pressed against tunnel walls until their heartbeat syncs with the city's pulse. Slowly, imperceptibly at first, they give way. Cartilage softens like candle wax in the city's heat. Collagen fibers reorient along lines of pressure. Over months, years, decades, they flatten. Not compressed—understand this—they unfold. Their skeletons branch into three dimensions instead of stacking them, ribs becoming delicate honeycombs that can collapse accordion-tight. Vertebrae segment into chains of small, flexible disks. Organs migrate and divide; a Traglodyas may have four small hearts distributed through their body like stations of devotion, pumping blood through a network of vessels that can constrict to threads or swell to rivers.

They wear nothing. Clothing would be a lie between self and stone. Instead, they cultivate symbiotic lichens in the hollows of their stretched bones—living tattoos that photosynthesize in the prismatic light, feeding sugars directly into bloodstream-shallow capillaries. These growths bloom in patterns that tell their stories: spiral fractures of love affairs, concentric circles of griefs held close, branching lightning scars of moments when they almost forgot themselves and stood too tall.

They do not walk so much as flow. Watch a Traglodyas move through a passage you'd barely fit your arm through: first the fingers, ten jointless tendrils finding invisible footholds. Then the hand disappears, wrist folding backward like an owl's neck. The arm follows, elbow unfolding into three new joints never named by surface anatomists. The shoulder collapses like a dropped tent, ribs sliding under each other in practiced waves. The head tilts sideways—sideways!—until you see the face is now profile, features rearranged across the stretched canvas of skull. Finally, in one impossible human tide, the rest follows: hips that dislocate willingly, spine becoming liquid sine wave, legs threading through themselves until you wonder if there were ever two.

They are rarely seen because they are rarely whole. A Traglodyas may spend years as scattered components: left arm exploring upper galleries while consciousness drifts in right lung's dreaming sac, legs walking miles below as independent scouts in the city's eternal expansion. They communicate through these detachments—a coded sequence of knuckle-knocks transmitted through bone, a pattern of rhythmic muscle contractions relayed through shared stone, the particular flavor of chemical whisper released from pores that other Traglodyai can taste through the soles of their feet pressed against the same wall miles away.

When they must meet completely—whether to merge minds for complex mathematics or to uncouple genetic codes in their unique form of reproduction—they choose one of the city's rare sphere-chambers. These perfect voids, carefully maintained against the city's tendency to fill every space, allow them to remember volume. Here, they gather like angels assembling from smoke: parts flowing together, interfaces finding their familiar fits, until something approximating a human shape stands in impossible space for impossible minutes. Their reunions produce sounds no surface air could carry: chordal harmonies of bones clicking into sockets long separated, organs resuming synchronized rhythms, the deep bass hum of consciousness reintegrating from its distributed dreaming.

But always, always, the pull of the compressed returns. The sphere's walls begin to whisper invitations. The empty space feels heavier than stone. Slowly, with the relief of long-delayed breathing, they let go. Collapse back into themselves. Flow apart like spilled mercury seeking cracks.

They live longer than you'd believe possible—their telomeres renewed by the stress of constant compression, cancer impossible in cells that can always stretch thinner to escape their own rebellion. The oldest are whispered to be nearly as old as the city itself, their original components perhaps no more than memories encoded in the particular mineral stains of certain walls. They say the city's founder—if such a thing existed—didn't build the first tunnels but instead compressed herself so completely she became them, her extended consciousness now the slow geological thought that guides the city's endless, drifting growth.

They die—or perhaps complete—their lives differently too. When a Traglodyas feels the final thinning begin (a subtle brittleness in their innermost bones, a faint reduction in their ability to unfold quite far enough), they choose a passage still too small for even them. Then they begin the last work: compressing further than ever before, folding into themselves like living origami becoming neutron matter, until at last they achieve what their lives have always prepared them for. Perfect fit. Perfect absence.

Where they were, a new space opens—not empty, but full of their folded complexity now part of the city's memory. Future generations will pass through these spaces feeling a slight warmth, a subtle ease in the folding, a whisper of song in their bones that helps them remember: here someone completed themselves. Here someone became the hole that others might unfold.

Above, the desert sun continues its indifferent burning, heating the glass eyes that feed light to the city below. Below, the city goes deeper, driven by compressed thoughts inherited from those who are no longer people but passages, seeking its own final compression when all the space between atoms will be filled with the memory of unfolding. Between, the Traglodyai flow like dreams of gravity made flesh, teaching stone the shape of absence by becoming it utterly, making their city not a place where people live but a living that happens to occupy the shape of a city—a breathing monument to the truth that in the end, there is no difference between the dancer and the space the dancer moves through. Only here, the dance never ends, because the dancer is becoming the space itself, and the music is the sound of stone learning to sing itself hollow.
