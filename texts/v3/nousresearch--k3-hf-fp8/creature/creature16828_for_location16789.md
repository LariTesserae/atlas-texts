Whoever lives here must first learn to live within their own exhale.

The people of Nevvport-in-Cyan are called breath-dwellers, though they never named themselves this. It was visitors who noticed first—the way locals never quite finish speaking, letting sentences dissolve into the air like smoke rings they intend to inhale again later. Their true name for themselves is a sound made by slowing your heartbeat to sync with the storm’s rotation, which visiting lips cannot form.

They are born small, red, and furious, like all babies everywhere, but within seven days they lose this objection. The air is too patient for sustained outrage. Their cries thicken into coos, their fists unclench into questions. By the time they can walk, they have already begun to negotiate their dimensions with the space around them—adolescence here is less growth than a series of polite treaties between expanding bone and considerate architecture. The tallest adults stand 1.39 meters precisely, having learned that the doorway’s limit is not a constraint but a kindness.

Their voices are soft as moth wings mapping darkness. When they must speak loudly—during the Benthic Festival when tourists throng their compressed streets, or at funerals when messages must reach the storm’s deaf center—they do so by not raising their volume but by accumulating quiet. A gathering Nevvportian crowd doesn’t roar but whispers in perfect overlap until meaning accretes in your skull like pearl forming around irritation.

The first thing children learn is how to stop learning. Their schools are called forgetting-houses, staffed by elders who spend decades perfecting the art of saying “but have you considered not knowing that?” Facts are considered infectious here—useful in small doses but dangerous in concentration. What they cultivate instead is a sort of curated ignorance, a deep familiarity with the shape of questions whose answers stay politely inaccessible. The most respected citizens can discuss the same mystery for hours without ever suggesting they wish to solve it.

They have no word for loneliness, though they know the condition intimately. Instead they speak of *solitudinous density*—the rich paralysis of having too much self in too small a space. The city’s exact population is enforced not by law but by empathy: when a child is born somewhere, an elder elsewhere begins to feel time pressing against their skin like too many layers of clothing. Most depart voluntarily, walking into the storm with letters strapped to their chests like reverse astronauts launching themselves into the unread. The city mourns these departures not with grief but with rearrangement, shifting loved ones into the newly vacated spaces in their lives like letters in a living requiem.

Their eyes adapt to dimness until they can read emotions in the way shadows hesitate. Darkness is their element; they think in gradients of not-seeing. A well-timed blink is considered a profound conversational gambit.

Time lives differently in their bodies. The skin of a fifty-year-old Nevvportian looks like folded moonlight—smooth in places where years glided past, deeply creased only at the corners of eyes and mouths where decades of smiles or puzzlements accumulated like sediment. They don’t age consistently but in quantum jumps: a woman might wear the same face for twelve years, then wake one morning with a new line that contains her entire understanding of tides or regret. Birthdays are approached with trepidation, celebrated only if they fail to arrive.

They sleep in communal piles during daylight hours, like puppies conserving warmth that isn’t scarce but deserves respect anyway. Each family maintains a sleeping-room filled with cushions that remember the shapes of previous dreams. Alone at night, when their actual dreaming occurs, they sleepwalk in slow-motion parades through the empty streets, their bodies following dream-threads visible only to moss and memory. Morning finds them always safely home, though sometimes wearing someone else’s slippers and smelling faintly of conversations they didn’t have.

Their economy runs on attention traded in precise microdoses. A full minute’s uninterrupted gaze is considered dangerously intimate, worth either a family heirloom or a promise never to speak again. They pay for groceries with carefully metered curiosity—asking exactly the right number of questions about the grocer’s daughter’s new leaf-pattern, neither too few to seem disinterested nor too many to risk learning something private. The currency itself is alive: small silkworms that spin threads of differing colors depending on the quality of attention they’ve been fed. Wealth is measured not in material but in the richness of the air immediately surrounding a person—an aura of well-tended silences and thoughtfully unasked questions.

They court each other through absence. A love affair begins when one person starts taking slightly longer routes that pass nowhere near the beloved’s home, but carefully calculated to intersect with their shadow at 3:17 PM when the light angle is precisely right for creating a momentary penumbra embrace. Proposals are made by leaving increasingly complex negative spaces in places the other frequents—a library book with three pages removed whose absence forms a constellation significant to their inside jokes, a chair pulled not quite far enough from the table each morning so that its emptiness begins to curve the air like gravity. Marriages are celebrated with a party to which nobody comes, each guest sending instead a perfectly detailed description of the exquisite time they are not having elsewhere.

They grow no food, only cultivate situations where eating might happen if all parties agree to be surprised by it. Gardens are planted with memory-seeds that sprout into conversations about hypothetical vegetables. Hunger is considered vulgar, so they practice anticipatory satiety—feasting on the memory of yesterday’s tomorrow’s meal until actual nourishment proves unnecessary. The one exception is the annual Feast of Accidental Harvest, when the city’s digestion towers produce some unexpected byproduct (last year: blue cheese that remembered being limestone) and everyone eats gathered in the street, feeding each other small bites while carefully not discussing flavors.

Their religion has no gods but one saint: the first person who successfully explained a joke without ruining it. They worship through the sacred art of the unpunchlined story: narratives that circle plausibility in tightening spirals until listeners find themselves believing six impossible things about breakfast without realizing they’ve been converted. Their holy day is whenever the storm at the center coughs particularly softly—detectable only by the precise tremble it sends through every wineglass in the city at exactly the same moment, causing an involuntary toast to whatever wasn’t said.

They die twice. The first death is ordinary: hearts slowing to match the city’s basal murmur, breaths spacing themselves out to become indistinguishable from air currents. The second death comes later, when the last person who remembers remembering them also forgets. Between these deaths, they become the city’s unofficial librarians of marginalia—ghosts who haven’t noticed their own passing, responsible for remembering the spaces between important events, the pauses that gave sentences their meaning, the exact color of nothing happening at 2:35 PM on a Tuesday. They live in the corners of photographs, the dust motes caught in beams of light that no longer enter rooms, the slight hesitation before answering questions that nobody asks anymore.
