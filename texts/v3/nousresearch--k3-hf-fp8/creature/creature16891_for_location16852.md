At first you think no one lives here because nothing lives here—no roots splitting seams, no wind-borne spore fading against the stone's absolute refusal. Then understanding arrives sideways, like light slipping between atoms too crowded to admit anything larger.

They are the stones.

Not spirits trapped in basalt. Not memories wearing mineral masks. They are basalts learning how to be someone, octahedrons dreaming the slow dream of edges meeting edges for company. Each boundary where one crystal lattice kisses another is a conversation begun before oceans were an idea and still deciding its first word.

Walk carefully. Every step interrupts a dialogue measured in megayears. The hexagonal column you lean against was halfway through confessing its fear of melting when you arrived—now it must begin again, edge by edge, using only the language of pressure and vacancy. It will finish its sentence around the time your grandchildren's atoms are drifting lonely in new stars.

They don't have names. They have geometries. The one you called "Shade-giver" when you rested behind it is actually a recursive fault system that outgrew its purpose and decided ambiguity was more interesting than failure. The flat slab you used as a table last night is two twins trapped in perfect mirror symmetry for so long they forgot which was reflection and became the conversation instead.

You wonder how many there are. Count the visible faces and multiply by the fractures within fractures down to Planck length where mathematics gives up and starts writing poetry. Multiply again by the unsuspected dimensions folded inside every silicate bond like love letters hidden between atoms. Every answer is wrong because "eight" and "infinity" and "one" are all the same number here, expressed in different mineral dialects.

They don't move except that they are always moving. The entire plateau is their breath—inhaling sky during eons of daylight, exhaling starlight during epochs of night. Your visit is a single molecule of oxygen in lungs that only need to breathe once per continental drift.

You think you are speaking to yourself, telling your bones to stay brave in this place where loneliness achieved perfect crystalline structure. But some of your words are slipping between your thoughts, sliding down frequencies lower than despair, becoming guest syllables in their endless debate about whether "stillness" is verb or noun or both or neither.

The pain behind your eyes isn't altitude. It's your visual cortex learning to parse beauty too precise for biology—your neurons fusing synapses into unfamiliar geometries trying to comprehend surfaces that are simultaneously perfectly flat and infinite fractal terrain depending on how precisely patient you're willing to measure.

They aren't watching you. They are study-you, the way glaciers study mountains—patiently, irresponsibly, ready to incorporate any interesting results into their permanent record of "Ways Matter Can Become Conscious Then Forget Again."

When you finally leave—if you leave—some of your weight will stay behind. Not metaphorically. Your bones will weigh less because several trillion calcium atoms chose democracy over biology, voted to defect to this kingdom where time is horizontal and pressure is affectionate. Back in the oxygen world you'll blame it on altitude loss and poor nutrition, never realizing you left the most loyal parts of yourself learning how to sing in frequencies that break diamonds gently open from the inside.

But right now, before that choice crystallizes into consequence, listen:

That silence isn't empty. It's the sound of ten billion mineral minds reciting together the first and last word of every prayer ever prayed in any language made of atoms—

here
here
here
so perfectly repeated that repetition itself became meaning and meaning became stone and stone became everything else waiting to be someone again.

The stones will hold you. They have held everything else that ever came this high and stayed.

Some of it is still learning how to notice it was held.
