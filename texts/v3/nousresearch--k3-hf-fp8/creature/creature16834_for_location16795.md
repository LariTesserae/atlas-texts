In Calmaris, the living have learned to breathe like the dead—shallow, patient, with pauses between inhale and exhale where questions might fit. You asked who lives here? Watch the waterline of your own shadow as it climbs your ankle in this supposed hallway. That’s the first name.

They call themselves the Dwellers in Between, but names rot here faster than bread. Yesterday’s title bubbles up tomorrow as somebody’s soup. Better to describe how they move: a grandmother who is also seven children threaded through different floors of the same collapsed hospital, sharing lung infections like bedtime stories. Twins born ten years apart in identical elevator shafts, communicating through variations in the building’s electrical hum. A painter who hasn’t touched solid pigment in decades—her brushstrokes live now as patterns of rust spreading across twelve neighboring balconies according to a rhythm only the pigeons can read.

The children are easiest to spot first. Their bones haven’t yet forgotten what shape they’re supposed to maintain in proper gravity. They walk like spilled mercury trying to remember the bowl, limbs bending places physics abandoned centuries ago. At recess they play a game called “Was/Is/Will Be” where they leap between moments of architectural possibility—landing on a stair that hasn’t crumbled yet, ducking under a beam that fell last century but hasn’t realized it because nobody’s observed the collapse. They return home with pockets full of tomorrow’s rain and yesterday’s starlight, which their mothers strain into tea.

The mothers are harder. They’ve become skilled at existing as negative space, at being the hollow that keeps the walls from collapsing inward. There are still those who remember breathing dry air. They gather Tuesdays in what used to be a swimming pool’s locker room, now repurposed fifty-seven times into a temple, a courtroom, a womb for birthing new types of silence. They trade breaths in measured sips from antique tanks labeled PROPERTY OF SURFACE 1973—each inhale costing stories about things that didn’t dissolve: a father’s watch that still ticks in perfect quartz against the city’s arrhythmia, a recipe for bread that rises without yeast but with pure stubbornness, a color that survived seven chemical marriages without losing its name.

But they’re dying natural deaths at unnatural speeds, or perhaps unnatural deaths at natural slowness—it’s hard to calibrate mortality when your calendar is written in watermarks. Their children’s children have gills that aren’t gills, exactly. More like questions that forgot how to be asked aloud. They filter oxygen from the unspoken things between people: the pause before forgiveness, the space between one floor collapsing and the next realizing it’s next. Their kisses taste of rust and responsibility.

The old professions have adapted beautifully. Plumbers now serve as priests, interpreting the city’s digestive Rumors as they pass through pipes that were once philosophical texts. Electricians practice acupuncture on systems too complex to kill or cure, inserting prayers into power grids that dream of lightning. There’s a whore who rents not sex but solidity—pay her enough and for thirty minutes you’ll remember what it feels like to have edges that don’t bleed into walls. She collects payment in buttons and bones, currency from before everything got so liquid.

And everywhere, the Watchers. They were people once, or maybe shadows that got ambitious. Now they live in the delay between lightning and thunder, between your hand reaching for a doorknob and your brain deciding whether to trust it. They speak in the language of water stains: a map of Indonesia appearing on your ceiling overnight, spelling tomorrow’s rainfall in a dialect of mold. They’ve grown patient as limestone. When they want something, they simply wait for your descendants to evolve into it.

But you asked who lives here, singular. The truth is, Calmaris is inhabited entirely by verbs pretending to be nouns. Descend long enough in any conversation and you’ll find everyone speaking softly of someone they’re in the process of becoming: a man turning into his father’s regrets at roughly the speed of crumbling concrete, a woman becoming the space her daughter will need to fall someday, an entire neighborhood slowly deciding to remember itself as coral reef instead of city.

Except for one.

There’s someone who lives at the exact center of all this becoming and unbecoming, where the water pressure equals the weight of every unkept promise ever made on land. They don’t have a name anymore—they traded it for directions out, but the directions got wet and turned into a map of here instead. They sit in a room that’s always flooding but never fills, counting backwards from infinity in a language made entirely of wet matches striking against the dark.

Some nights, when the city’s arthritis acts up and all the joints between centuries ache simultaneously, you can hear them laughing. It sounds like water remembering what it was before gods or engineers tried to imprison it as ocean. It sounds like the punchline to a joke with no beginning. It sounds like home, if home were a question you spent your whole life drowning to learn how to ask.

But you’ve been standing here too long already. In Calmaris, attention is gravity—linger and you’ll grow roots, or worse, fruit. Better to keep moving. Better to pretend you’re still just visiting.

Mind how you step. Some of these citizens have been falling for generations, and they’ve learned to make it look like flying.
