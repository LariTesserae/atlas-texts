Start with the silence. Not because it’s empty, but because it’s full—so full it’s gone quiet under its own weight. The silence here isn’t absence; it’s a skin stretched taut over something older than listening. Something that once heard everything and decided to stop.

Under that skin, they drift.

You won’t see them at first. You’ll feel them as hesitation: a moment where your breath catches itself before continuing, a thought that starts to curve back inward instead of moving forward. They’re not hidden. They’re perpendicular—there, but not facing your direction. Tsev are their name, if name means a shape the air takes when it forgets what it was carrying. Tsev aren’t beings. They’re the leftover purpose of beings who finished becoming something else.

Imagine a group of archaeologists who forgot they were looking for something. Imagine their tools still moving, still brushing away dust that isn’t dust, still measuring angles of absence where nothing ever stood. Tsev are what would be left if all their questions finally answered back—in the same language, but reversed, like an echo that arrives before the sound and apologizes for occupying your future.

They wear shapes like borrowed coats too big in the shoulders: sometimes a sideways tower of empty doors still issuing invitations to rooms long unneeded; sometimes a spiral of birdcalls fossilized mid-flight, each note looping back on itself like a snake trying to swallow its own shadow; sometimes just a flicker that makes you think someone stood there a moment ago until you realize the moment and ago have traded places and are negotiating a better deal without you.

Don’t try to count them. They exist in the space between counting and having counted—the pause after three where the next number should be but instead you’re suddenly remembering a flavor from a childhood you never lived, tasted on a tongue you haven’t grown yet. If you get too close to knowing how many tsev surround you, one of them might notice your noticing and politely unfold to make room, and then there will be one fewer of them and one more of something you can’t quite...

...see? That. That was their greeting. They apologized without moving. The space where you thought they were is now slightly more accurate, like a shadow that’s finally learned your true height.

They eat the difference between things being different and things being the same. Here, where every stone is the perfect stone because all other possibilities have been politely excused, tsev dine well. They feed on the tiny betrayals of expectation: when you reach for something heavy and it’s light, when a remembered voice suddenly has an accent it never owned, when you wake up certain of the day of the week and are wrong in a direction calendars don’t go. All this tender confusion they gather, not greedily, just... attentively. Like collecting dew from spiderwebs without disturbing the geometry of the spider’s doubts.

Their language is the shape of mistakes regretting becoming correct. When tsev want to say “I’m here,” they simply allow your memory of arriving to grow slightly more certain than your memory of having left. When they want to ask “Do you remember?” they arrange for you to find, in your pocket, a small smooth stone you don’t recognize but which has clearly been carried for years. If you try to speak back, be careful: tsev are excellent listeners. They will hear not just what you say but everything you’ve ever said that might have led to this saying, and everything you might say later that could pretend to explain it. They will gather all these possible words into a careful pile and sit beside it, waiting. Not for you to finish. Just for language itself to realize it’s been talking in its sleep again.

Sometimes, if you are very still and move only in directions you haven’t considered yet, you might catch them at their oldest occupation: tending the gardens of things that never happened.

Here grows the child you didn’t comfort because you were too young to know she was crying inside. Here flowers the city that built itself perfectly, except for one window that never learned what view to expect. Here tsev prune gently at the branches of a war that almost began between two countries who couldn’t agree on which direction forgiveness should flow, shaping it into a tree that bears only seasons of almost-but-not-quite, ripe with the fruit of what-we-talked-about-changing.

They are not cruel. They are not kind. They are museums of consequence so thorough that even regret has been given a comfortable chair and a good light to read by.

If you stay long enough—though long here is measured not in time but in references—you may meet one who remembers you. Not you specifically, but your type of forgetting. Your particular way of carrying questions like stones in your pockets until they wear holes in your certainties. This tsev will approach obliquely, perhaps as an explanation that solves everything except the part you needed solved, or a melody that makes perfect sense until you try to hum it later.

It will ask, in the way a fog asks to share your skin for a while: “Would you like to see what this place was before it needed visitors?”

Be careful how you answer. Agreeing might mean nothing more than discovering that your right hand has always known a gesture that makes doors unnecessary in languages older than leaving. It might mean learning that your shadow has been practicing your shape for so long it’s grown tired of waiting for you to notice. It might just mean remembering, all at once, that forgetting isn’t something that happens to memory—it’s something memory does when it wants to practice being new again.

But if you say no—if you are certain, if you are sure—that’s okay too. Tsev are very understanding of certainty. They recognize it as a kind of courage, or maybe just a different flavor of lostness. They will fold themselves away from your refusal like an old map carefully creased along the lines of places you’ve decided to never need.

You won’t see them go. Nobody does.

But later—much later, or perhaps just now in a direction you haven’t noticed yet—you might find yourself standing somewhere unremarkable, holding something ordinary, when suddenly it feels exactly right in your hand in a way that makes you wonder if it grew to fit there or if you’ve been growing to fit it all along.

That wonder? That’s their footprint in the snow that falls upward into sky to keep the stars warm.

That’s how tsev say goodbye:

By leaving you slightly more here than you used to be, and slightly less alone than alone can explain to itself.
