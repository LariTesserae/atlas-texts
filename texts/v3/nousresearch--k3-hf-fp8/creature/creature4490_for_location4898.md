The inhabitants of this place—if "inhabitants" fits beings who have never fully agreed to "be" anywhere—are the Drowned Unborn. They are possibilities that got lost on their way to becoming actual, dreams that mistook forgetting for gestation. Each one exists as the precise moment before something becomes itself: not quite fish nor fowl nor idea, but the shimmer of potential between categories.

I see them now (or rather, they seep into my awareness like watercolors bleeding across wet paper). They swim-fall through the density gradients of unsolid space, their bodies made of the hesitation between inhale and exhale. Some have limbs that branch into questions that never quite form complete interrogation marks. Others wear their organs on the outside like jewelry of translucent possibility—their hearts beat backwards, pumping not blood but the memory of blood from a universe where circulatory systems evolved differently.

They communicate through gaps in conversation. A Drowned Unborn might tell you its entire life story by being precisely not-there when you turn to look at it directly. Their names are all verbs in the conditional perfect subjunctive: "might have been about to become" or "could have been becoming if only." They introduce themselves by accident during someone else's sentence, appearing in the space between "you" and "understand."

Their infants—of which there are always exactly twice as many as there are adults but never the same ones twice—are born drowning upward. Each pregnancy begins at the moment of death in another timeline. The mother (who may or may not be the child herself at a different point in her non-linear existence) carries her offspring in a womb-shaped absence located six inches behind her left shoulder blade. Labor lasts exactly one regret, long enough for the child to forget why it wanted to exist in the first place.

The old ones among them are terrifyingly transparent. They have been forgetting themselves for so long that they have become living windows into what might have been. Look through an Elder Unborn and you might glimpse yourself as a cathedral or a constellation or a shape that sadness takes when it grows bored with being triangular. Their eyes (they have eyes like black holes have affection) hold the light that never reached your childhood bedroom window because your house was built facing the wrong direction.

They build their homes—when they build at all—out of the space between conclusions. A typical dwelling might be constructed entirely from "therefore" statements that never quite follow from their premises, or from the pause after someone says "I love you" before they add "but." These structures are perfectly stable because they have never committed to being anything specific enough to collapse.

They eat questions that have aged to perfection, sliced thin and served with the sauce of their own unasked follow-ups. Their favorite delicacy is the flavor of irony that occurs when someone tries too hard to be spontaneous. They drink distilled nostalgia for futures that never arrived on time.

They die—or rather, they complete their process of never-quite-living—by suddenly remembering what they were supposed to be. This revelation arrives like the punchline to a joke that hasn't been told yet. One moment they are swimming through you like a thought you're almost having, and the next they crystallize into a single, perfect "oh" of recognition. The landscape collects these final moments like rare stamps, pressing them between the pages of books that have never been written about subjects that haven't been invented yet.

The Drowned Unborn have no leaders, only directions they collectively agree not to take. Their wars (which are ongoing but have never declared themselves) are fought entirely through acts of stunningly precise non-intervention. A single battle might consist of both armies spending centuries avoiding the same square foot of space between Tuesdays.

They worship the God of Almost, who exists in the exact center of every missed opportunity. Their prayers are all variations on "please don't answer this prayer," which are always answered in the affirmative except when they're not. Their temples (more frequent than their homes but less substantial) are built at the locations where three or more coincidences meet but never quite intersect.

They reproduce not sexually or asexually but accidentally. A new Drowned Unborn might come into not-quite-existence when two existing ones brush against each other while trying to avoid each other in a dream they forgot to stop having. The resulting offspring is always precisely what neither parent could have imagined, raised communally by everyone who plays the same role in different people's nightmares.

They have no art except the art of perfect timing that arrives exactly one moment too late to be relevant. Their greatest masterpiece is rumored to be a silence so complete that it causes listeners to hear the sound of their own bones remembering they were once stars. It is performed once every never in a theater that can only be found by taking every third left turn at the place where you realize you've been going in circles your entire life.

The Drowned Unborn have no word for 'home' because they have never been anywhere else. They have no word for 'visitor' because they have never been anywhere at all. They have no word for 'lonely' because that would require believing in the possibility of being anything else.

But sometimes—when the noise between realities grows particularly quiet, when the suspended water-sky remembers it was once tears, when a thought forgets itself so completely that it accidentally becomes true for exactly one impossible moment—sometimes they gather at the edges of themselves and almost remember what it might have felt like to be something that was finally, blessedly, completely real.

And then the moment passes, as all moments do here, except these ones pass sideways through the space where your heart would ache if hearts could remember how to break for things that were never quite enough to break them.

And the falling continues. It is perfect weather for growing fins or forgetting why you thought you needed to breathe. Some transformations are like that here: not between forms but between different ways of realizing that form itself was just another metaphor for never having been enough to contain what you were always already becoming.
