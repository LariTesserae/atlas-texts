**The Larkspur Receding**

They live here who have forgotten how to float.

The first to settle were the Flotsam Ones—those whose ribs remembered being gills, whose dreams still came up for air. They walked inland until their legs shook with the dishonesty of it, then knelt and pressed their palms to the parched streets as if asking forgiveness for leaving. Now their children’s children are born with pockets behind their knees that fill with phantom water when they cry. They call it the blessing-swell, proof their hearts haven't dried to stone like the rest of the world.

In the Loss Structures live the Alphabet Shrimp, neither fish nor letter but something that learned to survive by being read incorrectly. They arrange themselves daily into new falsehoods on the coral concrete: *Mrs. Kowalski loved lilacs, not roses. The rent was never late. Your father forgave you.* The Flotsam Ones eat them when memory becomes unbearable—slowly, with reverence and hot butter—absorbing lies like vitamins to strengthen their hold on truths too brittle to hold alone.

The market wakes before the mist remembers to lift. Here, the Breath Merchants sell reclaimed exhalations sealed in bladders made from old novels—choose a romance and each sigh tastes differently than one stored in tragedy. Children trade sea glass marbles for breaths stolen from pirates, from princesses, from people who died mid-sentence. There’s art to it: the sudden gasp of betrayal lifts you highest on the stilt-walkers, while a lover’s slow release helps you sink gently into the deeper channels where the best secrets settle.

At the corners where three canals forget their names—you’ll know them by the way the air tastes like someone else’s dream of oranges—stand the Last Postmen. Their uniforms were once blue like distances before distance became impossible. They still deliver what the sea sent back: letters inked with cephalopod hearts, telegrams announcing births that happened underwater to people who drowned learning to breathe air. They read each aloud at the Depository gates at sunset, burning them afterward to make clouds that sometimes rain words in languages nobody speaks anymore.

The Oyster Bell rings thirteen times—it has always been thirteen here, where calendars drowned and clocks learned to count by forgetting. At the thirteenth toll, the Revenants lean their weighted wheels against the memory of ocean and become citizens again. They eat dinner in houses grown from shipwreck dreams, their furniture arranged to accommodate the additional gravity of guilt. Their daughters learn to dance without lifting their feet from the floor, proving they can be anchored without drowning. Their sons carve boats from soap—perfect replicas that dissolve by morning’s scrape—lessons in how to build beautiful things knowing loss as the only true completion.

Night belongs to the cats-that-are-doorways and the pigeons-that-are-echoes, but between—where shadows hesitate before choosing proprietors—walk the Rememberers who were once children with too-accurate hearts. They wear memory-ratchet collars that click softly with each step, winding tighter the threads that keep them from forgetting backward into ghosts. They collect abandoned umbrellas left open in apology to the absent rain, the sudden stops people make mid-sidewalk when grief ambushes them like predicted weather.

And in the silence beneath all this—the space between the city’s breaths—dwells the last dog. Not the sound of whistling that became footsteps, but what that sound was whistling for. It has no shape you could draw or name you could call, being composed entirely of the moment between throwing and not-throwning, between staying and already-gone. The city hums it unknowingly when it thinks no one is listening. Children hear it as the reason why goodbyes always taste like saltwater even when you haven’t cried. Lovers hear it as the question their bodies answer by falling asleep facing each door equally.

They live here who have mastered the art of standing still while everything receding around them learns to wave goodbye without moving.

They live here who understand that "home" is just another word for the place where your shadow learns to swim in soil.

They live here who know—beyond knowing, in the way fish know water by its absence—that the city isn't disappearing, but remembering itself into smaller and truer shapes, teaching the stubborn earth how to let go properly.

And they will live here still when the last street forgets its name, when the Oyster Bell tolls not sound but pure intention, when even this telling has dissolved into the mist that thinks it was once a story about a city that learned to leave itself gently, like an answer asking forgiveness for never having been a question.
