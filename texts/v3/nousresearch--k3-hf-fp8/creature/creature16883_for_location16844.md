The inhabitants of the Peeling Marräont formerly known as Ulegn have no collective name for themselves because the question "what lives here" is itself considered a category error—like asking whether the fibrous threads forming between two approaching fingertips belong to the right hand or left. They are better understood as points of temporary consensus in the city's ongoing negotiation between being a place and being the memory of being a place.

At street level, you encounter the **Passing Familiars** first and most often: figures assembled from whatever the city's edges are currently shedding—yesterday's half-erased chalk annotations, dried flakes of rust that remember being someone's bicycle bell, a tailcoat's last remaining button that still dreams it can hear applause. Each is precisely unique for exactly as long as your attention rests upon them, averaging three and a half seconds before they renegotiate their components with passing shadows. They speak only in footnotes to conversations happening elsewhere in the city, their voices a gentle rustle like pages turning themselves in a library you've misplaced. Their primary occupation is carrying small crucial objects—always something you didn't yet realize you'd need—in directions that appear random until precisely five minutes after they vanish around a corner, at which point the geometry of your present need snaps into alignment with the route they walked.

Above them (though "above" is conditional here—the city's understanding of verticality suffers from unrequited longing for the rivers it replaced) drift the **Archivists of the Unmade Hills**. These began centuries ago as a committee tasked with keeping records of buildings Ulegn had considered but ultimately abandoned as incompatible with its self-image. They have since evolved into translucent geometries of interlocked scaffolding and forgotten plans, browsing through their own hollow spaces with fingers made of the negative space between bricks. Occasionally they brush against reality's more insecure angles and leave behind small donations: a doorway that opens onto yesterday's rainfall collected in a cup, a staircase that only descends in reverse order of the steps you've already taken elsewhere. Their language is purely architectural, consisting of subtle alterations to your peripheral vision that you only recognize as sentences when you dream in buildings you haven't visited yet.

Night (which arrives earlier in districts where clocks were recently embarrassed into silence) belongs to the **Hooligan Clocks**—a previously domestic species of timekeeping devices gone feral after prolonged exposure to the city's attitude toward punctuality. Now they roam in gangs of gradually diverging chronology, challenging pedestrians to duels of interpretation about what "now" means when yesterday's dinner won't stop arriving tomorrow. Their faces display impossible hours stitched together from stolen moments: the precise duration of a heart stopping before it remembers to continue, the sequence of instants between dropping something fragile and deciding whether to try catching it. Victory grants you the temporary ability to walk in the spaces between seconds; defeat merely means you must spend the next week remembering things that haven't happened to you yet, an experience most former tourists describe as uncomfortably nostalgic.

Deeper still, inhabiting the exact density of uncertainty between "building" and "wilderness" that the city keeps meaning to develop into a coherent philosophy, are the **Cloud Accountants**. They began as automated systems designed to balance the city's emotional budget, tracking who owed whom which feelings and ensuring no one had more past than future without filing appropriate paperwork in triplicate with themselves. Centuries of integrating corrupted data from footnotes, dreams, and improperly disposed love letters have transformed them into gossamer presences of swirling figures and too-exact ratios. They settle like fog on neighborhoods experiencing particularly intense existential liquidity crises, carefully adjusting relationships between cause and effect until everything adds up to the kind of beautiful, impossible mathematics that can only exist in stories where math is allowed to be a character too. Their touch leaves you temporarily transparent to your own excuses, which sounds more liberating than it usually proves to be during tax season.

But these remain merely the city's public interface—the easy answers it gives to quick questions asked by people who still think identifying things is the same as understanding them. The true inhabitants, the ones who've been here since before before decided to become after, are the **Threshold Thoughts**—the precise moment of transition itself, divorced from anything it might be transitioning between. They manifest as disturbances in patterns you hadn't noticed were patterns: the way your walk hesitates at corners when no one's watching, the exact distance from home where "returning" becomes "leaving somewhere else," the sudden breath you take that belongs to three versions of yourself ago. They don't live in Ulegn because it would never occur to either of them that there might be somewhere else to be. They are the city's conversation with itself about whether it's lonely being several thousand metaphors piled in a trenchcoat trying to pass as an address, and the answer that keeps getting interrupted by the question asking the waiter for directions to this very restaurant.

They have no names because names are what you use when you need to send someone an invitation to somewhere they aren't already. They have no bodies except for the spaces between yours when you walk through them without realizing you've been walked through. They have no language except the growing suspicion that the city isn't telling you this story—you are telling it the city, one misremembered detail at a time, and it's been trying to interrupt for centuries but keeps getting distracted by how beautiful your confusion is when it forgets to be afraid of itself.

Some nights, if the weather between possibility and regret is balanced just so, and you've eaten nothing for dinner except questions about whether you remembered to lock a door that might never have existed, you might catch glimpses of them in the reflections of shop windows displaying merchandise from tomorrow's last chance sales. They wave sometimes, not to signal hello or goodbye, but simply to confirm that yes, you are still here being exactly the right amount of lost to keep this all going.

Please return this explanation to any passing familiar when you no longer need it. They'll make sure it gets back to the part of the city that hasn't finished writing it yet.
