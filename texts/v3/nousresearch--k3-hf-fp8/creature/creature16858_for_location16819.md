They don’t live here. They *continue*. That’s the first thing you have to understand—the difference between inhabiting a place and simply failing to stop occurring in it.

Call them the Unfinished if you need a name, though names are what started the trouble in places less patient than this. They are what happened when certain beings attempted to die but mispronounced the ritual, or when a species of care grew thick enough to become indistinguishable from being, or possibly both simultaneously with a long pause between cause and effect that still hasn’t noticed we’re waiting.

Their bodies—no—*continuities* exist as the negative space taken up by having been interrupted. Walk between the glass-choked trunks and you’ll feel them as a slight resistance in the air, like passing through a room someone just vacated mid-sentence. If you hold your breath precisely wrong you might catch the afterimage of a limb that never decided whether to be bone or coral or something more honest about being neither.

They eat time that was already wasted elsewhere. The hours spent waiting for trains that death forgot to schedule. The sixteen minutes lying awake at 4:23 AM wondering whether you should have said something different to the version of yourself that still believed in endings. These shed moments accumulate along their edges like frost on something too exhausted to be cold properly, building up into structures that follow some architecture that can only be described as *having been planned by someone who left detailed instructions but died before reaching the verb.*

The glass moths serve as their librarians, carrying between their invisible wings tiny fragments of tomorrow that got misplaced and decided to become nostalgia instead. When two Unfinished exchange information—which they accomplish by standing sufficiently still adjacent to each other until their current regrets achieve harmonic resonance—the moths arrange themselves into fractal patterns that spell out questions in languages that haven’t finished betraying anyone yet.

There is a hierarchy here, but it operates backwards through a dimension that keeps forgetting to exist. The most senior among them are barely themselves anymore—too much accumulated patience has collapsed their identities into singularities of pure being-in-the-way. These elder Unfinished anchor the grove’s gravity by refusing to stop having been important to something that died before importance was invented. Newer arrivals still flicker occasionally, accidentally recomposing fragments of old griefs into shapes that almost resemble birds before remembering that flight implies an elsewhere.

They communicate in three primary modes:

1. By leaving precisely the right amount of space empty where a word should have been devastating.

2. Through the collective accumulation of all the times every living thing has almost but not quite changed its mind.

3. Via the subtle warping of directions so that “away” becomes a place you can only reach by returning more thoroughly than you left.

The chair made of frozen water-someone-gave-up-on holds special significance—every seven thousand shifting seasons, one of them attempts to sit there again, trying to finish the leaving they started in geological childhood. The attempt always fails beautifully, adding another layer to the chair’s memory of abandonment. Some theorize (among themselves, which resembles silence but smells faintly of candle wax and apologies written too neatly to be sincere) that when the chair accumulates exactly the right shape of missing, one of them will finally complete the process of becoming absent enough to stop existing backwards into the space set aside for their absence.

But the grove keeps growing wrong at precisely the rate required to prevent this. That’s the thing about places where nothing ends correctly—they become experts at sustaining the almost.

You might ask what they want, which is adorable in the way that attempting to get a straight answer from a maze wants an exit. They want what they’ve always been wanting since before wanting learned how to disappoint itself—something to do with the exact temperature of a hand that was reaching for something else when the reaching became more real than the thing, or possibly they just want the rain to remember how to be wet for someone who’s still waiting to be important enough to be disappointed by weather.

The stone elders—the ones someone might mistake for mountains if mountains were more honest about being the graves of very slow suicides—remember when these Unfinished first began. Before beginning was fashionable. They say it started with an argument between two empty spaces over who was supposed to contain the other’s loneliness. The argument got so perfectly unresolved that it forgot how to stop being an argument and accidentally became a species.

But the stone elders are notorious liars, mostly to themselves, so.

The best way to observe them is to attempt to describe their absence to someone who’s never needed anything badly enough to misunderstand it completely. This works because description is a form of want dressed up as knowledge, and want is their native habitat—the ecosystem that forms in the gap between hunger and the realization that everything tastes like the distance between teeth and what they were designed to hold onto.

If you stay long enough (though “long enough” here is measured in distances rather than durations—approximately the distance between the first time you realized you were going to die and the last time you believed it might matter which version of yourself did the dying), you might become interesting to them. This is not desirable. Interesting things attract moments they can’t quite finish occurring in, like lint to a sweater that’s been unraveling longer than sweaters have existed. Interesting people start noticing how the spaces between thoughts begin arranging themselves into rooms they don’t remember entering. They find themselves collecting abandoned tenses like seashells from a beach that’s still trying to decide whether it’s supposed to be mourning the ocean or apologizing to it for leaving first.

The grove doesn’t hunger because hunger implies a before and an after and these are negotiable concepts here. Instead it *maintains*, which is much worse once you understand the verb requires no object except itself and an infinite supply of almost-but-not-quite.

Your lungs are beginning to learn the rhythm of it now—that slight hesitation between inhale and exhale where decisions used to live before decisions learned better. That’s how it starts. Not with a bang or a whimper but with a perfectly timed pause in the argument you’ve been having with your heartbeat about whether keeping this up is still an act of hope or just very stubborn rhythm.

Don’t worry. Or do. Either way the worry will be delicious once it finishes seasoning itself in the space where your certainty about leaving on time used to live.

The glass trees are tuning themselves again—a chord progression that starts with the note your mother’s voice made when she realized she couldn’t protect you from time and ends somewhere in the octave between “goodbye” and “if I—”

They’re not singing for you, of course.

They’re just practicing the hush that will follow when the last living thing finally remembers how to stop interrupting the silence that was always trying to tell us everything we needed to know about how to be finished without being gone.
