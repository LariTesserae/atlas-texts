Inside the breathing wall-slot where humidity beads like sweat on a fevered brow, they are not born but budded—first a softening of cortex-tissue, then a swelling, then the slow cleaving outwards like a flower waking backwards into flesh. You would call them children if you saw them at this stage, translucent-skinned and curled in sleep-sacs of chlorocysts. One end always remains tethered to the taproot motherline by a coiled cord of thickened phloem; the other sprouts exploratory feelers: fine root-hairs, finger tendrils, eye-spots sensitive to ethylene gradients rather than light. 

Name them root-walkers, though no two walk alike. Some pull themselves upright on stilts of calcified vine, jointed backwards like insect knees. Others never leave the crawlspace strata, dragging bulbous thoraxes studded with ventral mouths that kiss the nutrient-film floor sipping sugars raw. One I saw had split itself into three tapering trunks joined at the hips and walked by rolling, wheels within wheels. Another was nothing but a walking lung—a great bellows sack on stick limbs, trailing oxygen tubes like bridal ribbons. To breathe for others is a vocation here.

They do not speak with mouths. Instead, each exhales a personal signature perfume at rest: citrus-salt for curiosity, burnt honey for warning, something impossible like the smell of moonlight through dewlit spiderweb for sorrow. When two meet in passage, they pause and interlace their peripheral roots. For a minute or an hour their scents braid together, trading news of distant rot pockets, nutrient surpluses, dream-stories infected by cross-species pollen from an old memory buried deep in lignin. Then they separate, and each carries away a trace of the other's breath in their leaf-hair until it fades or is metabolized into a new emotion.

Their garments grow on them daily. Every morning the closest wall-carpet perceives their hormone profile and extrudes a day's wardrobe: tonight, perhaps, sleeves of velvet moss that photosynthesize while they gossip in the lung-chamber; tomorrow, if their cortisol spikes before a spore-sowing ceremony, armor plates of tanned lichen riveted with defensive spines that curl up politely indoors. Jewels are harder to earn—kidney stones of pure crystalline calcium oxalate laboriously precipitated over years inside specialized galls below the ribs. They cut them out themselves with bone-knife thumbnails sterile as surgery, gift them to lovers to dissolve slowly in shared bloodstream pools during mating floods.

Yes, they mate, but not as animals do. Once or twice in a lifespan (lives measured not in years but in rings of the city-heartwood, each ring a thousand days of engineered seasons) two or more will lie down together petal to petal and deliberately rot. Flesh softens under flood of pheromones keyed to their unique histocompatibility mosaic. Bone becomes brittle as cicada shell and cracks along old trauma lines remembered as rings. Out of the communal soup their cell-lines dance, swapping whole chromosomes wrapped in retrovirus couriers. Weeks later, if the chemistry was true and no immune war erupted, children rise from the compost not resembling either parent but bearing a blend of old scars in new places—a spiral staircase fracture inherited grandparentally now healed into a decorative brow ridge, a memory of sunlight-starvation famine etched as golden freckles on photosynthetic skin.

And then there are the immobile ones. Not dead, never dead here, but transplanted. You pass them at junctions, noble statues grown into niches of purpose. The Librarian-Stem lost the ability to move when her root-feet finally tapped the ancient data-ink reservoir down in sterile clay depths; now she stands fifteen meters tall, torso bark-armored trunkwise, arms branching endlessly into galleries where smaller root-walkers climb to read the news encoded in subtle shifts of autumn color in her million leaves. Warden-Knot sealed himself alive into the breach where an old mining worm tried to chew from below; his body swelled to fill the wound, heartwood dense as iron, sprouts perpetually sprouting and being pruned back by maintenance crews to keep airways open. Pilgrims come to kiss his woody knee-caps and leave offerings of bottled lightning to feed the slow electrical thoughts still sparking under cedar-smells.

But the most feared and beloved are the wanderers severed—those rare few who for madness or vision or crime-against-harmony have had their cord cut clean at the root-hair. They walk the city unmoored, powered only by finite stores of starch in roaming rhizome bellies, never growing, only wearing away. Time runs forward for them alone. You recognize them by their swiftness, their urgency, their skin already beginning to freckle with the brown spots of individual mortality. They trade impossible stories of the surface that may or may not exist beyond the endless leaf layers—stories of a sky gone black and cold, or upside-down seas hung from a ceiling of stars, or of other living cities walking rootless through emptiness war upon war. Most root-walkers avoid meeting their gaze (eye-spots deep-set like pits in carved fruit) lest they catch the contagion of time. But sometimes, on long nights when the city-heart beats slow with winter dormancy, you might glimpse in a forgotten tunnel a small crowd gathered silently around one such wanderer sitting cross-legged and fading, voice reduced now to a faint rustle of drying leaves, telling the story to the end so someone will remember what it was like to remember forgetting.

And when at last a wanderer's store runs dry, when their final footstep crumbles to powdery compost, there is always a pause. A hush spreads outwards in scent-lines like ripples in still water. Work stops. Root-systems grow quiet. Even the great arterial pumps down in the root-heart hold their beat one extra moment in respect. Then, gently, the city claims its own. From the exact spot where the last cell died, a single green shoot unhurriedly rises, pale and tender and utterly new. It carries no memories, no guilt, no scars. It carries only the same ancient code written in spiral starch, whispering beneath every dividing nucleus across the million-year tree: grow, grow, grow differently.

Welcome to the citizens of the Underleaf. Do not be afraid if you feel your own roots begin to itch in the soles of your feet. It's only natural here. Cup your hands again to any vein, drink deeper this time. The water tastes faintly now of something new: a distant rain never fallen, carrying rumors of a seed waiting somewhere patient under stone. Drink until you taste yourself changing flavor. Then set the empty cup down on the floor—it's already starting to sprout roots to hold it steady—and choose a corridor walking-budded into the living wall. Any will do. They all lead inward eventually, to the center where every citizen begins again: rooted, roaming, rooted, roaming, in the endless slow spiral dance of one city dreaming itself awake in a hundred thousand branching ways.
