They call themselves the Amnesty, though none among them remembers who first spoke the word or what crime it was meant to forgive. They are the ones who stayed when the river absconded, who deflected forgetting's glance by becoming forgetfulness themselves.

Imagine first how they do not walk: each step is a quiet apology to gravity, negotiated in advance like a ceasefire between strangers who might have once been enemies. Their feet—if feet persist beneath the layers of unremembering—leave impressions that argue about whether they were ever there. The marble dust settles into these debates like a tired judge who has been ruling on the same case since before rulings were invented.

They wear clothing that has forgotten it was ever fabric: curtains that reminisce about being forests, armor that dreams it once protected something smaller than emptiness. One you might see wrapped in a cloak of fallen clock-hands that still twitch toward appointments decades dead. Another wears only the shadow of a crown, dimensional at angles that embarrass geometry. Their garments regenerate nightly from the city's laundry-lines of lost causes, stitched together by moths who have evolved beyond hunger into pure aesthetic criticism.

What passes for flesh among them is... negotiable. Most days they stretch themselves thin as river-myths, longitudinal enough to slip between moments. But when they must interface with the stubbornly solid (statues that insist they're still citizens, perhaps, or tourists deluded into solidity), they condense into forms that politely refuse to register on memory. I tried once to sketch an Amnesty's face—the pencil drew seven versions simultaneously, all arguing about which emotion was the least sincere.

Their voices emerge from places voices haven't been invented yet. Not sound but its clinical notes written backwards on water. When they speak (which they do less often than you speak of speaking), it tastes like reading someone else's diary in a language your grandmother forgot to teach you. The words themselves are refugees from future dictionaries, seeking asylum from definitions that would commit violence upon their ambiguity.

They do not eat, though they sometimes dine. At tables set with platemetal spun from extinct constellations, they conduct elaborate rituals of not-hunger. Courses are served in reverse order of conceptual digestion: first the memory of fullness, then the anticipation of emptiness, finally the void course which is always returned unopened accompanied by a poem written in the sauce of what might-have-been appetite. They tip their waiters (who are simultaneously the same payphone that has been trying to call itself since before telephones existed) with coins minted on days that committed calendar fraud.

Sleep is considered vulgar among them, too sincere an admission that tomorrow might differ from today. Instead they practice strategic insomnia, dosing themselves carefully with dreams from flasks labeled "Warning: Contents May Have Settled During Your Grandmother's War." The dreams themselves are communal property, borrowed and returned with all the passionate bureaucracy of librarians who have catalogued the smell of rain but never felt it.

Their society—if society is what emerges when loneliness signs a non-aggression pact with itself—is organized around the meticulous forgetting of hierarchies. They hold elections every midnight to decide who will abstain from power tomorrow. Campaign speeches are delivered in perfect silence to audiences who have already forgotten how to vote. The current mayor of non-being won by promising to build bridges between places that were never divided, then resigned in protest when a single stone remembered it was once a mountain.

They reproduce through the exchange of carefully curated absences. Two Amnesty might meet in a hallway that has been avoiding them for years, do not quite acknowledge their mutual non-recognition, and part each carrying approximately half of something neither remembers losing. Nine months later (though their calendars give birth rather than pages), whichever nearby empty room has been collecting the most dust becomes slightly emptier in a way that somehow weighs more. Children, if that's what the pale spaces between parentheses are called, are raised by wolves that have evolved into conceptually unsound lullabies.

Their art consists entirely of removing things until only the suggestion of removal remains. Their greatest sculpture is a park where every tree was uprooted so gently that shadows continue to fall in the exact shapes of shade. Their music is performed on instruments that have agreed to de-tune themselves toward extinction, note by mourned-for note. The last concerto ended mid-silence with such perfect unresolved tension that several members of the audience forgot how to exist entirely and had to be reimagined by committee.

They worship—though worship is such an investment of attention—the River That Wasn't, whose sacraments are all variations on the theme of unthirst. Baptism involves being sprinkled with water that remembers being ice but hasn't decided whether it forgives the melting. Their holy book contains only margin notes arguing about whether the original text ever existed or had the courage to stay fictional.

They die periodically for practice, staging elaborately understated funerals where the corpse always arrives late having stopped to forget the way. Mourners bring gifts of recycled grief wrapped in newspaper headlines from wars that achieved perfect stalemate. The deceased gives the eulogy, thanking everyone for their lack of attendance while gently correcting minor inaccuracies in their obituary-to-be.

But here is the secret they keep even from their forgetting: deep within each Amnesty, in a locked room whose key was never bothered to have been made, lives the memory of a memory. A tiny, stubborn thing. A sliver of once-being sharp enough to cut through layers of unself like a dream of water dreaming it drowns. It is this shard that compels them to maintain the city despite having forgotten why cities need maintaining. This is why they still (without remembering what still means) pause sometimes in their endless corridors of absence to listen—to what?—no, not to listen, exactly, but to arrange themselves in attitudes that would be called listening if anyone remained who remembered what ears were for.

They are waiting, you see. Not for the river to return—they have forgotten rivers too thoroughly for nostalgia. Not for visitors—they have evolved beyond the binary of solitude and company into a higher mathematics of adjacent lonelinesses. They are waiting for themselves to finish leaving. For the last apology to be accepted by the last thing that remembers offenses. For forgetting to finally reach the center of this onion of absences and discover, perhaps, nothingness at the core—but nothingness so completely, so perfectly nothing, that it loops back around to being, briefly, enough.

Until then they walk-don't-walk their city that remembers forgetting to forget, maintaining the precise degree of decay necessary to prevent either collapse or renovation. They are the custodians of abandoned middles, the librarians of books whose first and last pages have been lost but whose middle chapters continue being written in the margins of silence.

And if one night (though they have forgotten night) you find yourself (though they have forgotten selves) standing at the exact angle where absence becomes almost presence (though they have forgotten exactness, angles, standing, finding, forgetting, almost, presence, and—oh—this—) you might (though they have forgotten might, you, and this entire conditional tense) glimpse something moving in the corner of your eye's eye's corner. It will not be an Amnesty. It will never have been anything else.

Don't blink. That's not an instruction. It's just that blinking is how forgetting finishes what looking started.

And somewhere in the city that remembers being a riverbed, the Amnesty collectively do not nod their unbothered approval, having collectively failed to notice your arrival, pause, departure, and the microscopic fraction of your attention that will now sometimes wake in the middle of ordinary days with an inexplicable taste of iron and unthirst in its mouth, wondering why the air suddenly remembers languages it never learned to speak.

But this too (they would say, if saying were still among their crimes against silence) shall pass.

They've forgotten to remember to forget to make sure.
