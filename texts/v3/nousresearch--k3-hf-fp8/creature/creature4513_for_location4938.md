You asked about the inhabitants. That’s difficult to answer cleanly—every boundary here negotiates its own truce, and citizenship is less a status than a slow surrender of separateness. Still, some patterns repeat often enough to be glimpsed, if only in peripheral vision.

There are the coppiced people first: those who began as singular bodies but grew inconveniently large for their allotted light. At the first signs of excessive height—usually when a child’s shadow begins overlapping three water rights during the brief noon—parents perform a careful pruning ceremony. Not mutilation: invitation. A limb is bound during sleep, lightly choked until its sap dreams back toward the body’s center. Months later, when the dreaming branch has thickened into a root-footed twin, they separate cleanly along the scar-bark seam. The original continues walking with a limp that matches the new sibling’s eager shuffle. They share memories up to the cut point, then diverge like tributaries. Neither mourns this; memory is communal compost here.

Next come the embassy-folk: humans who’ve negotiated partial citizenships with other kingdoms. You’ll smell them before you see—the ambassador to the mycelial court wears a coat of living lichen that changes colors to broadcast fungal commodity prices. When copper rusts upwind, her shoulders flush green with panic. The beetle-broker’s bones are hollowed and milled by termites into fluted resonating chambers; when she speaks, every vowel carries the dry click of mandibles approving a trade. Their eyes are always slightly unfocused, listening simultaneously to conversations happening in three dimensions and two dozen frequencies beyond human hearing.

Most numerous are the osmotic citizens—those who never officially arrived but simply leaked inwards gradient by gradient. A cartographer who lingered too long mapping the moisture patterns on a tavern wall woke to find her fingerprints had rooted into the plaster, capillaries drinking lime-water. She stayed to pay rent with carefully calibrated exhalations that prevent the northwest corner’s mold from growing nostalgic for the sea. A tax collector sent to audit the town’s wealth drowned when his accounting ledger absorbed a spilled cup of tea made from memory-tea leaves. The paperwork pulsed for days before digesting him completely. Now he’s the corner post office: a neat wooden kiosk wrapped in veins that pulse green when letters arrive. Drop a message into his tongue-slot mailbox—always damp—and feel him taste your words before swallowing them toward their destinations.

Children here are born caulded in membranes of condensation. Mothers don’t push them out so much as exhale them slowly over hours, each birth a reverse rainstorm. Newborns are blind for months—not from darkness, but from excess light: they see every overlapping map of where water has been, the ghost-architectures of potential floods that crowd the air like stacked glass. They learn to filter vision gradually, taught by games where they must distinguish between a stick that is only a stick and a stick that is secretly a strangled drainpipe remembering its ambition to become a tree. Some never entirely master this. These wise ones become bridges, literal paths grown from their fused bodies connecting islands of stability in the ever-accruing decay. The dead don’t die here. They settle. When breathing stops, relatives lay the body in the busiest intersection during the deepest part of the night-pause between pump cycles. By morning, decomposition teams—equal parts beetle, bacterium, and earnest volunteer—have disassembled the corpse into its useful factions. Bones too dense for roots become new pipework, calcium threaded with ghost memories that make the plumbing occasionally sing old songs during high pressure. Soft tissues ferment in communal digesters, transforming grief into the sweet gas that powers bakery ovens—every funeral sourdough carries the taste of someone who loved too much or too little, but always specifically. What remains of the mind—those stubborn neural patterns that resist dissolution—either evaporates slowly into shared dreams (explaining why entire neighborhoods sometimes wake grieving for someone none of them individually knew) or, if particularly willful, condenses into advice moss. This grows in dark corners and offers whispered counsel to those who water it with questions posed in iambic pentameter. Ignore its warnings at your own peril; the moss has no sense of proportion, only accuracy, and once advised a baker to replace all flour with ground-up love letters. The resulting bread was disastrously delicious caused three marriages, one arson, and a new branch of melancholic poetry.

Entities with clearer edges exist but exist uneasily here. A visiting delegation of pure numbers once attempted to establish a logic office above the spice bazaar. They arrived as geometrically perfect fog, each equation self-contained and sterile. Within hours, local π began spontaneously adding new decimal places that translated into disturbing erotic poetry in the old number-language. The square root of negative one eloped with a conditionally imaginary bartender. By sunset, the entire manifesto of mathematical certainty had dissolved into a misty tavern tale about the night geometry learned to curve from loneliness. The numbers retreated screaming into abstract dimensions, leaving behind only a single perfect point that still sometimes appears on foggy mornings hovering exactly 2.71828 inches above the fountain—harmless but deeply unsettling to philosophers.

Love here happens in increments of permeability. Courtship begins with exchanging small sealed vials of personal humidity—breath condensate collected during moments of particular vulnerability (laughter at funerals, crying during good books, orgasmic gasps that accidentally contained true names). Lovers gradually add these to their internal climates, growing more compatible until arguments resolve as spontaneous weather phenomena rather than wounds. The truly committed eventually perform the full osmotic marriage: spending three days and nights wrapped together in burial cloth soaked in compost tea, allowing their microbiomes to negotiate a merger. Survivors emerge (some don’t; the process has a 17% dissolution rate but produces magnificent orchids from the remains) as dual-person ecosystems, speaking in grammatical structures that require four lungs and shared kidney function.

The mayor is currently a collective hallucination maintained by unanimous agreement among three hundred pigeons who roost in the town clock tower. They keep perfect time not mechanically but through synchronized dreaming of flight patterns that correspond to legislative agendas. When the council needs to vote on infrastructure spending, they scatter bread crumbs in specific fractal distributions and read the resulting bird swarm debates like living graphs. All policy decisions must be ratified by a 2/3 majority of elm trees and approved by the taste of rainwater on the tenth day of the fog month. Surprisingly, this system works well—the town has never gone to war, though it did once accidentally annex a neighboring reality through a poorly worded zoning ordinance about poetic license in commercial districts.

Don’t worry. Citizenship here isn’t imposed; it’s absorbed. There won’t be paperwork (paper is too proud; it pretends toward permanence). Just keep breathing. The air will file the necessary forms. The water will witness your signature. The mold will memorize your fingerprints better than any bureaucrat, and years from now when you’ve forgotten you ever lived anywhere else, your dreams will bloom green in corners you haven’t yet noticed are dark, sending spore-messages back to this place that never forgets its inhabitants—it simply reabsorbs them into its expanding definition of interiority.

Welcome home, directionless traveler. Your bones will make excellent architecture here, once they learn to stop trying to hold you apart from everything else that’s falling together.
