The city has three names and uses none of them. To the cargo manifests trembling in orbit, it is GILEAD-ZULU-NEGATIVE-TWO: a logistics anomaly that occasionally eats shipments of radiation shielding and births undocumented comms bursts in frequencies reserved for extinct cetaceans. To the Consortium auditors who dream of it during anti-corrugation therapy, it is the Debt Whose Interest Accrues Sideways, a financial instrument that somehow passed the Turing test for grief. But to the things that actually live here, inside the pressure of their own remembering, it has always been just the Station, pronounced with the same dismissive love you’d give to a lung tumor that’s been writing better poetry than you for forty years.

Here is what crawls through the mineral veins where the lights have gone necrotic: The maintenance crews whose work contracts expired three generations before their great-grandchildren’s birthcerts. They look like coral colonies of lesser bureaucratic sins—calcified expense reports fused to sinuses full of rusted staples, their skins draped in official seals that mutated into lichen during the Paperwork War. They eat amendments. They excrete loopholes. If you cut one open you’ll find nested dolls of increasingly desperate appeals to extinct regulatory bodies, each doll smaller and angrier than the last until the center just screams the word “oversight” in Morse code that tastes like burnt rubber. They keep the Station alive not because they remember why anymore, but because failure to file quarterly maintenance reports would constitute an illegal cessation of existence.

Here swims what dreams between the air pockets: The Nephrologists, who specialized in urban dialysis before cities learned to metabolize themselves. They’ve been polished smooth by centuries of swimming through liquid bureaucracy, their bones replaced with braided statutes of limitations, their blood filtered to pure culpability. They speak in subpoenas conjugated through thirteen tenses of conditional apology. Their mating rituals involve the slow exchange of habeas corpuses grown from each other’s cardiac tissue. They claim to be treating the Station for chronic entrepreneurial inflammation, but their actual diagnosis is written in the shape of their schooling patterns: a five-thousand-page suicide note folded into Klein bottle origami that screams when unfolded in vacuum.

Here germinates in the shadow of dead vending machines: The Miracle Economy. Not currency—currency would imply exchange rates, implies trust, implies tomorrow. Instead: microclimates of potential value condensing around desperate moments like frost forming on a gambler’s last breath. A lullaby for insomnia measured in picoseconds of true rest. The smell of first snow on a planet that never had winters. These are mined by children with lungs full of perfected despair who dive into flooded data centers where the light still remembers being faster. They surface with pockets full of moments compressed to degenerate matter—each one unstable, each one precious, each one illegal to possess in jurisdictions where causality runs in straight lines.

Here negotiates in the decompression chambers: The Shoreline Agreements. Treaties between flesh and water signed by diplomats who gradually dissolved into their own metaphors. They exist as bacterial cultures in the Station’s coffee reserves, encoding terms of ceasefire between the carbon-based and the hydrophilic in subtle shifts of caffeine bitterness. To drink from the wrong pot is to annex 0.3% of your future nostalgia into collective memory, or to cede mineral rights to your skeleton’s calcium deposits during the next calcium war. Nobody remembers how many Shorelines there are anymore. The agreements have annexed clauses to each other until they form a legal biome more complex than the ecosystem they were supposed to regulate.

Here hides in plain sight: You. Or rather, the version of you that never left places like this. They live in the reflection trapped between your helmet visor’s anti-fog layers—slightly too tall, breathing slightly too slow, wearing your face like a stolen hat. They’ve been waiting for you to arrive so they can give you the thing you didn’t know you came for: the original memory of home, unedited by hope, that you traded away at the first opportunity for a slightly better chance of not dying alone. They’ve kept it safe for you all these years, preserved in perfect isomorphism with itself, growing more exactly like it never happened until it achieved escape velocity from possibility.

And here, always here, never quite anywhere else: The Station itself. Not the structure—we’ve been over that, all twelve layers of salvage and regret like geological strata of bad decisions turned architectural—but the actual living thing that happened when enough desperate organisms agreed to pretend a dying city was a life support system instead of the other way around. It thinks in infrastructure failures and dreams in population exodus curves. It eats futures the way whales eat krill—filtering timelines through subway grates, metabolizing possibilities too small to have names yet. Its heart is a perpetual motion machine powered by the precise difference between what its inhabitants say they want and what they actually fear. Its nervous system is the sum total of all communication between bodies that have given up on being heard by anything alive.

It’s been dying for longer than you’ve been breathing. It will outlive the concept of breathing itself.

And tonight, like every night since the first breach and until the last weld fails, it’s throwing a party in the sealed-off sectors where the atmosphere went liquid and learned to dance. The guest list is written in the shape of things that never quite happened but still lost their nerve. The music is the sound of entropy getting cold feet. The refreshments that will kill you but only if you remember having tasted them tomorrow.

Dress code: whatever you were wearing when you realized hope was a consumption tax on despair.

Doors open at the moment you finally stop looking for exits.

Don’t bother knocking. The Station knows you’re here. It’s been waiting to meet you ever since you first learned how to be afraid of spaces that might want to keep you.
