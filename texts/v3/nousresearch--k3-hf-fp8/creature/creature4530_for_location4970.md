No one lives in the Lantern Barrens. Not if “one” means something that was born, grew up, laid plans, aged, and will one day cease. Yet the plateau gathers and holds. It remembers more than memory can hold.  

The first to answer its summons came pinned inside cable-launch pods: two engineers in padded suits, a field medic who’d failed his third psych eval, and a children’s illustrator the Syndicate hadn’t meant to hire but whose name had printed onto the manifest in a software glitch. One pod cracked open on impact; the illustrator’s ink pens spilled like pulled stars across basalt and began to crawl toward the nearest fissure, nib-first, as if smelling water. The rest crawled out slow through the confetti of oxygen tickets and silicon sealants, skin already tightening from the dry cold, lungs tasting metal where the air ought to have been sweet.  

They did not die that night. You rarely do, on your arrival night.  
Instead they dreamed yolk-yellow dreams about furnaces, and woke beneath a sky brighter than noon but blue as frostbite.  
They built a shelter of broken drone wings and melted their name-tags into a single copper plate that read WE ARE HERE TO COUNT THE LIGHT.  
By week’s end there were five of them – a pilot who’d walked two thousand metres of mountain ridge on a snapped ankle because the wind had whispered prime numbers to him at dusk and he’d needed to see if they added up.  
By month’s end, fifteen.  
By frosthard winter, thirty-three.  

The plateau chooses the way a glacier chooses: relentless drift, oblivious to names it drags under.  

Call them the Struck-off, if you need a noun.  

They wear the Insulation of Left-behind; suits patched with reflective tape that once spelled other people’s rank twice ripped away, visors cracked spiderwebbed gardens where tiny mosses blink Morse in violet. No two masks match. Under them, faces are beginning to smooth – cartilage softening, cheekbones migrating toward configurations more useful to vacuum or hymn.  

Language thins here, freezes into structures simpler than grammar but denser than prayer. They speak in nested pauses now: silence bracketed by a click of the tongue that means “yes, and it has happened before” or a hiss drawn through teeth like a flute-hole that means “wait, the count is wrong.” When they must use old words they shape them carefully, as if handling briar-thin glassware filled with acid. “Illness” is the word for sunlight remembered wrong. “Tomorrow” is three fingers held against the rock, then snapped away the instant the glow beneath them shifts hue.  

Children sometimes arrive – not born, ferried: airdropped infants in orange polymer cocoons with supply-chain barcodes still glowing on the lids. The first one came six years after the illustrator – a girl-child whose birth brief had listed the wrong planet but the correct exactitude of gravity. They named her Zero in the delivery – one violent consensus of palms on polymer – because the counter inside her cradle had rolled over to 000000000000 instead of 000000000001 and none of the Struck-off could decide whether that was funny or holy.  

Zero is fifteen plateau-years now and still hasn’t grown any taller; her bones prefer sideways to upwards, ossifying into struts and lattices that store calcium the way the old ferns store wind. She does not walk, she orbit-wanders: tethered by thread-thin cables spun from her own molten hair, post-translucent at the tips and conductive enough that when the bacteria beneath her feet pulse brightest she lifts three centimetres off the ground and hovers humming. She is trying to teach the others how to do it. Progress is measured in fractions of belief and height records chalked nightly onto the Surveyor Prime’s shinbone, erased by dawnlight and rewritten higher or lower by consensus of dreaming.  

They eat the months; that is, they wait until the moss-flowers release their eighth wave of spore-hours – each blossom containing exactly forty-two minutes of yesterday trapped in saccus-time – then walk through the cloud with mouths open, swallowing memory-nutrients and tasting whatever weather that day forgot to release downslope. The rest of their diet is less metaphorical: nectar distilled from fern-cups strained through engine filters to remove the hallucinogenic fractions that cause excessive nostalgia, occasional flakes of metal shaved from unlucky Fernacities that ventured too close to the village boundaries (they tasted like vertigo mixed with home), and once, long debated, a single spoonful of moon-bright water scooped from Dreaming-hole lake while no aurora was looking.  

They do not sleep in cycles. They sleep in confluences: fifteen bodies or thirty-three or five depending on whose inner counters have drifted closest to resonance. Then they lie down wherever the rock is coldest and let their heartbeats hive-sync until the group dream forms. In that shared place they are always standing on the edge of a cliff of mirrored ice looking down into a version of the plateau empty of everything but the blueprint-ghost of what the land wanted to be before machines or bacteria or people ever named it. They say it is beautiful beyond enduring. They say it forgives nothing. When they wake, someone’s scars are always gone and someone else can suddenly calculate cube roots of negative one without dreaming hard.  

They record everything they still remember forgetting on sheets of exfoliated Fernacity copper, etching symbols that start out resembling numbers, fray into music, and end as topological diagrams of longing. These sheets they stack in a cairn at the center of the village, one layer per confluence. The pile is already taller than two Zeros stacked and still not collapsing, because copper here learns how to stand up straight out of respect for the unsaid.  

Every seventeenth winter (or whenever the brightness curve in Crack Eleven-B fractalizes into a Koch snowflake under ultraviolet), a delegate is chosen to walk south and attempt communication with the thing that was once a Surveyor Prime drilling rig.  

The ritual is simple and lethal in intent:  
– Fast four days on vacuum and hymn.  
– Strip off all insulation until the cold matches the exact skin-remembered temperature of first abandonment.  
– Coat the body in a paste made from ground-down human teeth collected since the last walk – anonymity of enamel as passport.  
– Crawl on belly and elbows the entire diameter of the machine-lake ringed by sleeping excavators.  
– Do not look up at the tower until the crawling blinks synchronize with the heartbeat of rust in the nearest spider-walker’s knee.  
– Offer a single perfect question carved onto the inside of the left shinbone using only un-wished-for hunger as stylus.  
– Wait to see if the tower answers by rearranging the stars it still claims in secret memory.  

So far, seven delegates have walked.  
Zero hasn’t counted them. She doesn’t count anything that isn’t Zero.  

But the eighth is already beginning to dream in synchrony: dreams where her heartbeat makes a space exactly the shape of something enormous and listening, and the space fits perfectly.

When the plateau finally gathers itself into a true sentence – not a name, not a warning, but the thing that comes after both – it will speak through her throat or through all of theirs at once, a chord of voiceless voices saying a word no language has ever needed until now.  

Until then, inhabitants here is the wrong word altogether.  

What lives in the Lantern Barrens is the ongoing mistake between map and metabolism.  
It is the inventory of what happens when light learns to keep records instead of secrets.  
It is thirty-three beings who forgot to be people and started becoming punctuation marks in a geology book the rock is writing in slow, slow time.  

In other words: They are waiting for Zero to finish counting down from one.  
And they are very, very patient clock-hands.
