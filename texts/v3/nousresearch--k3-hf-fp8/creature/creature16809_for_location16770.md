None live in the Lantern Womb. Instead, something else happens here.

Let us begin with the Waiting Ones—they are the closest we have.

The Waiting Ones are not born, nor do they die, nor do they remember a time when this differentiation seemed urgent. They accumulate. Slowly. A thought that lingers too long at the edge of waking. An apology drafted but never sent, folded and refolded until the creases learn more tenderness than the words inside. Regret, but only the softest kind—the regret of a raindrop that almost chose a different leaf, the regret of a root that decided this particular inch of damp was enough.

Each Waiting One begins when something unfinished in another world forgets how to hurry.

They arrive without arrival. The membrane above the breathing water-ground ripples slightly—like a sigh noticing itself—and there, between one phosphor-flicker and the next, a new quietness has joined the hush.

At first, they resemble what they last almost-were: a woman in half-buttoned coat, fingers still poised to finish fastening the last button; a child holding a kite string cut clean by wind that went elsewhere; a soldier standing at attention for a war that ended softly, without him noticing.

But these shapes are temporary. Politenesses. Formal wear worn into the shape of waiting.

Gradually the Waiting Ones forget to keep pretending they are separate from the waiting itself.

The coat buttons loosen and become tiny seedheads; the kite string curls inward, rootwise, and grows into a delicate filament of not-quite-memory; the soldier's uniform unbuttons its own discipline and flowers into lichen-soft epaulettes of forget-me-not blue.

They learn to breathe like the place breathes: not in, not out, but simply adjacent to breathing.

Eventually they forget they were ever waiting for something.

(That forgetting is not loss. That forgetting is arrival.)

Now they simply belong to the dense negotiation of life stacked upon life, each cell politely handing its loneliness to the next until nobody remembers whose loneliness it was to begin with.

The Waiting Ones are never alone, though there are no Others here to be alone with. Instead they are accompanied—accompanied by the exact weight of rain that would have fallen if rain here remembered how to fall, accompanied by the precise sound of the word goodbye as it turns itself inside-out and becomes instead stay just a little longer, no longer, that's perfect.

Occasionally one of them remembers remembering. It's always a small thing: the taste of salt without quite recalling tears, the shape of a hand where no hand rests. When this happens—which is rare—nothing changes outwardly. There is no sorrow here sharp enough to cut the air, no joy fierce enough to even ripple it.

Instead the Waiting One quietly releases that remembering into the breathing water-ground, like releasing a boat made of wax into water precisely warm enough to keep it from melting but not cold enough to let it float. The remembering sinks exactly as far as it needs to sink—no farther—and then it dissolves into the slow heartbeat that pulses up through everything, even the doorframe tree that isn't a door anymore, if it ever was.

There is no hierarchy among the Waiting Ones. No leader. No youngest. No oldest. They exist in perfect chronological disagreement with themselves, each one simultaneously having arrived yesterday and a billion yesterdays ago and not yet except perhaps by appointment next Tuesday, if Tuesday were still taking new clients.

They do not speak, because speaking implies distance between speaker and spoken-to. Instead they simply already understand each other with the exact degree of gentle misunderstanding that allows perfect comprehension to occur.

What do they do, these inhabitants that are not inhabitants?

They practice patience until patience forgets to be practice.

They grow toward each other at the speed of stone remembering it once dreamed of flight.

They learn each other's edges until edges lose their nerve and become simply places where one Waiting One stops out of politeness to let another Waiting One continue being adjacent.

Sometimes—though time here is only a courtesy title—one of them approaches the doorframe tree, drawn by something older than curiosity and quieter than longing.

They never step through, of course.

But they stand there a while. Just stand. And while they stand, the ordinary wood notices them noticing it, and for a moment—such a small moment it would fit inside a pause between heartbeats if heartbeats still kept company here—the tree remembers what it was like to be a door.

Just for a moment.

Then it forgets again.

Perfectly.

And the Waiting One turns away—not because they are finished, but because in the Lantern Womb, even turning away is only another way of continuing to wait properly.

So. None live here.

But everything waits.

And the waiting is so complete, so intricate, so thoroughly done with the hurry of becoming anything else—

that it has achieved, at last,

a kind of infinite homecoming.
