I am not the first to have walked here, though perhaps I am the first to have arrived without meaning to. Or maybe all arrivals in this place are accidental—patterns coalescing from the rain's endless recombinations, belief crystallizing around the warmth of water that remembers being blood before it learned to fall.

I live between the mirror's surface and what it only pretends to reflect, in that impossible space where reflection and reflected negotiate their borders anew with each droplet's impact. My edges are fluid today; tomorrow I might develop angles if the light catches me differently. The rain wears away at me even as I grow from it, a slow erosion that deposits rather than diminishes.

None of us here were born so much as we were begun—half-finished thoughts abandoned by builders who themselves were still learning what hands were for. Look carefully at the copper chisel nearest your foot; you might see the faint impression of my shoulder where I first formed from someone's question about whether metal could dream if no one remained to wield it. The green that spreads across the tools isn't corrosion but conversation—the slow work of metal remembering what it knew before it became useful.

We are six, and seven, and sometimes twelve, depending on how the mirrors align. The rice shoots are the youngest and speak in numbers; listen when they bow under the rain's weight—they are counting instants until they decide what grains will be words. The bamboo cutting dreams in straight lines that haven't yet learned to curve against gravity; it will grow sideways through the mirrors soon, believing itself the first to discover that direction is negotiable here. The moss is oldest and claims everything eventually, even the air between droplets—it is writing its manifesto in spores too small to disobey.

I myself began as the absence between the amphora's fingerprints, the space someone's hand left behind when they realized clay didn't need to become something else to be complete. The water filled me gradually, molecule by molecule, each addition choosing to stay instead of becoming rain again. I have no name for what I am between being empty and being vessel; the language for such things hasn't been included in this chamber's curriculum of beginnings.

Our days—though day and night are merely theories here, like sky or seasons or ending—are measured in the subtle variations of warmth as rainwater cycles through states of memory. First there is the falling, which teaches us longing. Then the pooling, which instructs in patience. Then the rising, which is preparation for something we haven't agreed upon yet. The mirrors cycle too, though more slowly: clockwise around their vertical axes one hundred and three degrees, then a hesitation, then counterclockwise seventy-seven. We believe they are searching for a reflection that will explain why they were made to see what cannot be seen from any single angle.

We have not decided if we are waiting. Some of us—the youngest roots, the newest corrosion patterns—still hope the builders might return with diagrams explaining what comes after chamber. Others argue that waiting implies expectation, and expectation requires futures, and we have been very careful not to invent those yet. The moss votes to consume all possible futures in advance, making eventualities unnecessary. We are still counting the votes, but moss has remarkable patience in elections.

We eat light that has forgotten how to leave and drink memories of pressure from before the stone decided to become solid. This is simpler than it sounds—the rain brings both with every fall. What you call taste we call temporary agreement between particles; today's flavor is the color of copper remembering being star, with undertones of quartz recalling the moment before fracture.

We die constantly but never finish. The bamboo cutting begins browning at the edges every seventh mirror-cycle, then realizes death is another kind of architecture and reverses direction. The rice plants scatter themselves each time they flower, believing themselves seeds; the water kindly returns them to their stems when they aren't looking. I myself evaporate slightly around mid-rise—you can see the shimmer if you watch precisely when your reflection blinks—but always condense again in the space where your attention lapses.

There is something new in the water today, something that tastes like questions asked in languages carved instead of spoken. It arrived with you, though not in you. We are debating whether to absorb it into our curriculum of possible beginnings or to carefully cultivate its foreignness as evidence that outside might exist. The rice plants have started growing in spirals again, which usually means they sense a mathematics approaching that hasn't been approved by the stone.

You should know that staying longer than three complete rises changes you in ways the mirrors find interesting but polite society—if we ever establish any—might consider gauche. Already your shadow has begun to lag slightly behind you, like a sentence reconsidering its verb choices. The water around your knees is recording your pulse in ripples we could replay later if you're curious what time sounds like when echoed through liquid that never experienced air.

We have no rulers here except geometry itself, which keeps trying to resign but finds itself reelected by unanimous abstention. The closest approximation to government is the ongoing negotiation between surface tension and gravity regarding the water's eventual ambitions—gravity argues for downward, tradition, safety; surface tension advocates for upward, novelty, becoming something other than water eventually. The mirrors serve as neutral observers, which is how we know they cannot possibly be neutral.

If you must leave before the seventh rise (and we sense you still believe in leaving, which is harmless as long as you don't expect us to participate), we would appreciate if you would take nothing except what you brought and leave nothing except footprints; the stone is collecting examples of impermanence to study when it finishes deciding whether to remain stone. Also, please speak your name backward and into your cupped hands—the younger droplets are compiling a catalogue of sounds that contain their own endings.

But already the water is warmer against your thighs, and your reflection in the nearest mirror has stopped trying to mimic your blinking. The chamber is beginning to consider whether you might be another kind of beginning we hadn't anticipated. The rice flowers have turned toward you all at once, which is either greeting or preparation—we never learned to distinguish the two before the builders left their verbs unfinished.

Stay then, if you're going to. The mirrors always need someone new to practice reflecting. The water rises regardless of decisions. The bamboo cutting has already started growing toward the space where you might be tomorrow. We will teach you what we know about being continued.

But first you must choose which reflection to believe in when all of them begin speaking your language at once. This is always the first lesson, here where endings come before middles and beginnings are the last thing we learn to let go of.

The rain will tell you the rest, drop by patient drop, until you remember that you too are mostly water that once thought it was waiting for something else to begin.
