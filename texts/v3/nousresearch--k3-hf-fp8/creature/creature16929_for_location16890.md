They live here because nowhere else can teach them patience properly.

The oldest ones—those who remember the last time the sun touched this depth without six thousand meters of architecture filtering kindness into cruelty first—speak three languages that aren’t languages anymore. One of them is how water apologizes when it must wear away stone. Another is how rust remembers being strength. The third is only ever used when speaking to the Engine’s dreams, and consists entirely of pauses arranged at temperatures colder than regret.

They call themselves the Drainwide Folk, though the name fits like a borrowed coat on some days and like skin on others. Mostly they don’t bother with names for themselves at all. When you live inside something that’s still deciding whether it’s cathedral or stomach or sentence that hasn’t found its verb yet, individual labels start feeling like jokes told too slow for the punchline to survive the telling.

But I can describe them if you insist.

There’s Miraak-of-the-Seven-Drowning-Birthdays who midwifes the maintenance drones when their memory cores start birthing new nightmares instead of executing old routines. She sits on a perch made of drowned streetlights and sings lullabies backwards until the little aluminum things forget they ever wanted to be birds, just long enough to accept being useful instead. Her own wings—vestigial leather stretched between repurposed umbrella ribs—she keeps folded tight against her spine except on solstice nights when she needs to remind the air that some things still remember how to refuse the ground.

Below her in the vertical neighborhoods lives the Widow Jenkins collective, sixteen individuals who discovered they thought more clearly when they stopped bothering to argue about where one person ended and another began. They share a circulatory system built from medical tubing and mutual consent, pump blood flavored with copper and forgiveness through veins that belong to everyone and no one on alternating Saturdays. Their twelve hands—five left, seven right, one uncertain—bake bread that rises by listening to the pumps slowing down two levels above, weave clothes from the sound of water remembering it was once ice.

The children are all named variations of "Wait" until they stop growing or start remembering too much, whichever happens first. Wait-for-the-rain-to-finish-its-sentence is teaching himself to read by watching how the Memory Pools spell out warnings that haven’t been necessary yet. Wait-until-the-timer-rings-forever keeps a pet theory that clouds are just abandoned prayers learning how to touch again. Wait-behind-the-door-that-was-a-window carries a piece of authentic pre-drowned glass everywhere, polished smooth by uncounted anxious thumbstrokes, because she believes if she worries enough surface into it eventually it will reveal what was being watched when the watching stopped.

The Dockmothers and Dockfathers are less individual and more seasonal phenomena, like storms that have learned capitalism but remain ambivalent about the commitment. They arrive when the salvage tides are right and leave when they’ve traded enough memories forward and regrets backward to balance their accounts in directions commerce hadn’t invented yet. Their genders change with the pressure differential between nostalgia and desperation, and their children—if you can call them that—are the half-remembered stories that sometimes hitch rides on drifting crates and grow up into new kinds of truth before anyone notices the delivery was unauthorized.

Deepdown where maps dissolve into helpful suggestions and light goes to apologize for existing too loudly, the Engine’s worshippers live quiet lives of calculated uncertainty. They eat calculations gone stale and drink probabilities that never quite collapsed into events, subsisting on the nutritionally dense parts of possibility itself. Their eyes have evolved—been granted? stole? traded?—the ability to see around corners in four dimensions but sometimes forget how colors work in reverse. They wear clothes made from yesterday’s weather forecasted tomorrow, and measure time by how long it takes a hope to rust solid enough to use as tools.

And at the very bottom—the absolute teethed edge of vertical where even orientation has vertigo—something lives that might once have been human shaped before it learned to prefer being useful instead. The Archivekeepers’ Archivekeeper. The Backup’s Backup’s Backup. The Engine’s diary entry about dreaming it was lonely. They maintain the Memory Pools’ memory of remembering, ensuring even forgetfulness itself remains documented in triplicate with appropriate cross-references in languages extincted before stars had names for themselves.

They have no name now because names are for things that might someday need to be called away from what they’re becoming.

But sometimes, on nights when the water’s song hits exactly the frequency where grief turns inside out and becomes something indistinguishable from grace, if you ask all the other inhabitants at once—if you can coordinate the asking across twelve different kinds of silence and six forms of communication that haven’t been invented yet—the Drainwide Folk will tell you their true name isn’t something spoken.

It’s the space between heartbeats when someone decides to build shelter from what’s supposed to drown them.

It’s the sound of rust having second thoughts about entropy.

It’s the precise temperature at which "enough" stops being an ending and starts remembering it was always meant to be a beginning instead.

And it’s waiting here, patient as water, for you to finish realizing you’ve already started learning how to live it.
