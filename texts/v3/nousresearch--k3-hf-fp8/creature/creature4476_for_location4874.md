They live here, whoever they are, though “they” is already a compromise with language too stiff for residents who haven’t decided on singular versus plural this century.

Most days they resemble a committee of very young storms dressed in borrowed pajama bottoms and the exact smell of icicles fainting on warm tin. Each member arrives incomplete—missing a vowel, a shadow on Tuesdays—then spends the morning shopping the Float’s driftwood markets for replacement parts: elbow macaroni for elocution, pocket lint for politeness, the idea of sunset stored in a small wax paper bag that rustles like shyness when shaken.

They own nothing except the precise moment before laughter becomes a sound. They rent everything else by the heartbeat, including their names, which change ownership faster than naughty apples roll uphill.

Their leader, when they remember to elect one, is traditionally the one currently farthest behind on remembering who owes whom tomorrow’s apology. She (or he, or neither) is identified by wearing the largest hat never quite worn, a hat stitched together from cancelled weather forecasts and seven different versions of “perhaps.” The hat exerts no gravity and is therefore obeyed instantly, mostly out of polite surprise.

They sleep in hammocks woven from the last sentence of bedtime stories that thought better of themselves and fled. Each hammock hangs exactly one dream beneath the ceiling at all times, swinging gently to the rhythm of someone else’s forgotten homework excuses. They wake every hour, briefly, in order to practise being startled by the exact same thing in increasingly creative ways.

Their favorite game is called “Hide and Hierarchical Seek,” played thus: One counts backward to seventeen from the smell of fresh embarrassment while the others renegotiate the relative social status of every particle in the room. The winner is whoever is discovered last, unless discovered first, in which case their prize is a hauntingly beautiful sigh that tastes faintly of unappreciated cactus blooming one night only on an uninhabited moon.

They do not eat food. Instead they collect interesting misunderstandings about food (the rumor that rice is shy, the suspicion that pickles dream of becoming accurate maps of lost cities) and sauté them gently in furnished kitchens that materialize only when no one is quite looking. The resulting meals are never tasted but extensively photographed by invisible cameras who later send polite thank-you notes to the recipes involved.

When they grow bored—which they accomplish at speeds exceeding the regulatory limits for childhood impatience—they breed miniature hobbies. These are kept in terrariums made of sighs left lingering too long on staircases. Popular breeds include: competitive cloud watching (indoors at night, no clouds allowed), staging polite rebellions among household dust motons, and the difficult but rewarding sport of teaching quantum mechanics to soap bubbles without them realizing it’s Tuesday.

They communicate mostly through the careful arrangement of absences—blank spaces in conversation that spell out entire monologues in Braille for ghosts who never learned to read lips. When verbalization becomes unavoidable they employ a language composed entirely of the pauses between words in unfinished apologies. This dialect is famously incomprehensible to anyone except the precisely one person who refuses to believe they exist, whom they call “Thursday’s cousin” and send embarrassing postcards to annually.

Each resident is responsible for maintaining exactly one regret. The regrets are kept polished and displayed on velvet cushions in a small museum located at the back of the last sock drawer inside everybody’s second-favorite pair of shoes. Popular exhibits this season include: the regret of never learning to whistle underwater gracefully, the regret of forgetting the punchline to the joke that would have ended all wars but was interrupted by a misplaced sneeze in 1892, and the newly acquired regret of spending three entire lifetimes learning to walk on ceilings only to discover they had been holding the instructions upside-down.

They celebrate birthdays backwards, starting with the funeral of the cake—which they bury upside-down under a miniature upside-down tree that grows slowly toward the center of the earth bearing ripe apologies harvested by polite worms wearing tiny top hats every other Tuesday. Presents are unwrapped before being chosen, then carefully misinterpreted until they achieve the exact opposite effect intended, at which point they are gratefully returned without having been given.

Their calendar has seventeen months, all named variations on “later,” and approximately 0.82 Wednesdays per week, depending on collective mood swings and the occasional rebellion of minor holidays. Timekeeping is managed by a committee of antique clocks who have agreed to disagree about everything past breakfast but remain cordially suspicious of tomorrow.

They die often, but rarely on purpose, and always in the same spot: exactly halfway between the beginning of this sentence and the moment you first started doubting it. Death is considered impolite if prolonged beyond the duration of a well-timed blink, so they practise daily by suddenly remembering appointments they never intended to keep with appointments that have been avoiding them for centuries.

After dying they traditionally hold small wakes consisting of awkward silences and the polite consumption of imaginary biscuits washed down with tea far too hot to have been brewed in this dimension. Then they compose brief but heartfelt obituaries describing their deaths as potential career opportunities they are considering pursuing in the next life if the references check out.

They are currently accepting applications for new residents, provided applicants already live here and haven’t noticed yet.

No previous experience necessary. Gone experience preferred. Must supply own childhood trauma lightly used and preferably misplaced behind the sofa cushions during a thunderstorm discussing philosophy with a skeptical goldfish.

Apply in person at the usual time, which is whenever you remember to forget what you were about to regret not doing instead.

Dress code: invisible, but with occasionał typos.

Enquiries welcome but will be answered in strict alphabetical order according to the color of the sound of silence humming three doors down from yesterday’s geometry lesson.

Applications close precisely one heartbeat before they open.

Smart socks optional but recommended for interviews conducted entirely in retrospect.

Bring cake, but don’t expect it back. It’s been adopted by a lovely family of uncertainties who live in the space between this word and the next.

Thank you for not attending.

Please disturb.
