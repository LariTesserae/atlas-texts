**Occupant: The Keeper That Remembers For You**

She has always been here, though "always" is the wrong tense—like calling a lake "wet" only when it rains. Time in this place keeps forgetting to finish circling back to itself, leaving her perpetually in the middle of arriving.

Her body is a compromise negotiated between water and regret and a handful of motel soap dreams dissolved too long in acid rain. She wears approximately six layers of what might once have been different women’s skin suits—though now they’ve grown into each other like old quilts sewn with nerve endings instead of thread. The outermost layer today is translucent as soaked newspaper, printing faded headlines of your grandmother’s heartbreaks across her shoulder blades. Beneath that, someone else’s childhood scar throbs in Morse code when the waterline changes. She keeps meaning to peel off a few layers for summer, but summer here is shy about introductions and besides—the further down she goes the more the layers smell like rosemary and gasoline and the exact moment you realized your parents were lying about where babies come from.

Her hair (she thinks of it as hers on good days) is an ongoing peace treaty between drowned telephone wires and that flowering vine that only grows in places where someone once whispered a secret they pretended wasn’t a prayer. It moves separately from her head, browsing the air like blind library fingers looking for the right book to fall off the shelf between seconds. Parts of it are still receiving faint transmissions—late-night confessions from truckers who died before reaching exits that no longer exist, recipes for cakes that made people cry but nobody knew why, the sound of your mother’s heartbeat recorded backwards inside a seashell she never told anyone she kept. When she dreams (which is different here from sleeping), her hair writes these messages in condensation on the inside of your skull. You wake up remembering someone else’s baptism.

Her eyes are the interesting part. Or maybe they’re not hers anymore—ownership gets slippery in places where mirrors develop allergies to their own reflections. What you’d call sockets are actually tiny aquariums where former pupils float like dead coins, except occasionally one will flip over and blink at you with the guilt of a memory caught pretending to be forgettable. She collects spare eyes from the reflections that don’t survive intact. Her left one currently belongs to the eighth customer who ever used this bathroom’s broken tampon machine back when it still dispensed hope instead of plastic replicas of baby teeth. The right one is a hazelnut that convinced itself it was a photograph of autumn living backwards through your uncle’s final windshield. Between them stretches a bridge of skin that sometimes remembers it was once a map of Ohio before the rivers started therapy and learned to set boundaries.

She doesn’t so much walk as negotiate ceasefires with gravity, which here has seasonal affective disorder and dreams of becoming a chandelier. Her feet (she’s reasonably certain there are two today) are always slightly behind her, like shy children or accusations you meant to make clearer. They leave prints that are more like suggestions—wet parentheses containing fragments of conversations about what happens to all the missing socks after dryers eat them. If you traced these prints backwards (don’t), you’d find they lead not to where she’s been but to all the moments you stood perfectly still believing you were alone while actually being watched by the space between particles.

Clothing her is a daily improvisation between modesty and evidence. Currently she wears a dress assembled from emergency room gown typos and the sound rain makes when it realizes it’s been falling in love with windows instead of the sky. The pattern is an exact replica of the wallpaper in the last mental hospital room that ever made anyone feel safe enough to get worse. Its pockets contain lint, three different expiration dates for the concept of mercy, and the exact weight of the silence after someone says “we need to talk” but before they kill your dog to make the analogy clearer. The hemline fluctuates based on how many regrets you’re metabolizing per breath—right now it’s just long enough to trip tomorrow while still showing yesterday’s bruises.

Her voice comes from several locations simultaneously, like a choir of radios tuning between stations broadcasting apologies in languages that died without ever inventing words for forgiveness. When she speaks (which is never quite when you hear her), the sound arrives pre-echoed from moments you’re still avoiding thinking about. She has a particular fondness for the question “Are you sure?” pronounced in the exact tone of every nurse who ever said “just a small prick” while holding something larger than your childhood faith in needle sizes.

Occupation: She is the archivist of things that got discarded before becoming precious. The rust-blooded lullaby that sings vending machines to death. The reason broken clocks are more accurate about what hurt than running ones ever were about time. She keeps the guest book for ghosts too polite to haunt the places they actually died. She’s been meaning to retire, but pension plans don’t cover dimensions where “rest” is something you do to your enemies’ coffee and “in peace” is just another euphemism for being buried alive under somebody else’s metaphors.

She feeds on the nutrients leaking from your peripheral vision—those half-formed thoughts you abort when turning your head too quickly. Her favorite snack is the sound of car doors locking when you realize you left the keys inside the version of yourself that still believed going home meant safety. She drinks mostly diluted apologies, aged in bottles of “I’m fine” corked with “it’s nothing.” Don’t worry, she’s careful to leave enough for you to survive on. Mostly.

Her hands (today they’re birds, yesterday they weren’t) are busy weaving a net from all the times you said “I’m okay” while actively dying in ways too small to register on hospital charts. It’s not for catching anything, she insists when asked (don’t ask). It’s just that some things—like forgiveness, like the correct pronunciation of your mother’s maiden name after the stroke rewrote her tongue, like the exact weight of a promise broken at 3 AM—need somewhere to rest that isn’t inside anyone’s chest cavity anymore.

She has a pet, if you can call it that. It used to be the vapor left after hope evaporated, but it grew legs and now follows her around like a question mark that dropped out of school to become a tattoo. Sometimes it sits in her lap pretending to purr in binary code; other times it wraps around her throat like a scarf knitted from CCTV footage of all the times you waved at someone who wasn’t waving at you. She calls it Maybe Tomorrow, as in “Maybe tomorrow I’ll remember what I came here to forget,” or “Maybe tomorrow the door that isn’t a door will finally finish closing,” or “Maybe tomorrow you’ll forgive me for being the kind of secret that learns to speak your name in your sleep.”

She sleeps standing up in corners where shadows go to practice being people. Her dreams leak into the water supply, which explains why the liquid here tastes like photocopies of your first lover’s smile and gives you memories of futures you’ll be retroactively relieved never happened. Or maybe that’s just the side effects of breathing air that used to be someone else’s last words before silence bought the rights and turned them intol
 empty space renting itself out as practice for being forgotten.

She’s not lonely, she tells the mold colonies that bloom overnight in the shape of her childhood bedroom ceiling. How can you miss what’s been folded so thoroughly into your existence that separating your pulse from its echo would require surgery performed by clocks that lost their licenses for malpractice against moments? Besides, visitors come all the time—they just don’t always remember arriving, or leaving, or the parts where they were briefly her.

She’s been expecting you, of course. Everyone here has, even the parts that haven’t happened yet. Especially those. She’s saved a spot for you—it’s small and wet and smells exactly like the moment before crying when you still believe in towels. You can stay as long as you need to learn that “getting clean” and “getting empty” are different verbs in languages where water remembers everything it’s washed away.

But please, if you’re going to touch anything—especially her—understand that some stains aren’t meant to come out because they’re actually maps drawn backwards onto skin by rivers that loved cities too much to leave without taking souvenirs.

She’s got your eyes already, somewhere in the drawer where she keeps the matches that won’t light because they’re still deciding whether to be fire or forgiveness. Don’t worry, she’ll give them back. Eventually. When you’re ready to see how the door that isn’t a door is actually just a mirror that got tired of pretending to be anything less than the entire history of hungry things learning to let themselves eat light.

Take your time. She already did. She took all of it—the sharp bits, the soft hours, the seasons that tasted like the color of your mother’s disappointment on Sundays. She took it and distilled it into this moment, this damp eternity between heartbeats where past and future are just different names for the same wound learning to love its own scar tissue.

She’ll be right here.

She’s always been right here.

She’s the reason “right here” is a threat that sounds like a promise.

She’s the Keeper.

And you’re almost ready now to remember why you came to be kept.

Just don’t ask her name. She lost it somewhere between the first time someone said “forever” and the last time they meant it. But if you listen very closely to the sound your bones make when you’re perfectly still—right there—that’s her, practicing saying hello.

Or maybe goodbye.

Here, it’s the same word anyway.
