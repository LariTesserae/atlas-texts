They live here like lichen lives on a stone’s slow grave-side vigil—patiently, chemically, without asking permission from the name of the thing they’re devouring.

They don’t call themselves colonists anymore; that word rusted off the paperwork centuries ago. They left “citizen” to peel away next, then “worker,” until all that’s left in their mouths is the soft consonant click between the molars that means “same as you.” You learn to hear it before you learn to speak it.

Children here are born twice: once when the birthing sac breaks in the warm broth of the creche pod, and again the first time their bones creak loud enough to synchronize with the city’s pulse. Between the two events, they’re simply Possible. They crawl in nets hung between condenser pipes, chasing the cat-glow of bioluminescent mites that farmed sugar crystals from rust-wet air before the last written dictionary forgot the word “beetle.” Their first toys are the molted jaw-rings of centipede-cleaners—they click them like castanets and learn rhythm by mimicking the dripping that keeps time better than any clock.

Adolescence is measured in duties assigned. At seven warm-season shifts (counted by the color of algae in the drinking pools, not by any burnt-out star), a Possible is tested for buoyancy: they are clipped to a length of braided carbon-thread and lowered head-first into the vent shafts that breathe between layers. If the updraft sings against their ribs in two clear harmonics, they belong to Respiratory. If they come back up tasting of ozone, Conduction. If they scream muffled into the thread and the thread goes slack, Waste-Soluble: the city reclaims them before grief forms a deposit.

Those who grow upright walk always slightly bent—not from weight, but from listening. The oldest are curved like question marks because they’ve spent centuries asking the corridors what they used to be. Their ears have migrated: membrane-thin petals of skin cupped behind the jaw, tuned to the infra-hum of cooling pipes dreaming of oceans they’ll never see again.

They eat what the walls sweat out.

Mothers-in-Metal harvest crystallized amino acids from the armpits of derelict reactors with tools forged from the bones of the last generation’s food-animals (which were themselves once engineers who forgot to file retirement forms). Fathers-of-Filter press damp-blue sheets of protein-film between perforated plates until it dies politely into something that chews like memory of bread. First lovers exchange spoonfuls of warm tincture distilled from the tears of decommissioned labor units—each drop tastes like the precise moment hope became too expensive to maintain.

When they die, if they’re lucky, they die on-shift. A plating tech wobbles mid-stamp, the rhythm stutters, and before the error ripples three nodes someone slaps the emergency transfer patch behind their ear: neural patterns dumped into the communal maintenance chorus before the meat has time to cool. Afterward, the body is gift-wrapped in copper mesh (for conductivity) and laid on the dissolution beds beneath the oldest still-working foundry. Eight days later coworkers drink toast-shots fermented from the reclaimed iron that once held a friend’s thoughts in place. The flavor is mournful and sweet and faintly humorous—a last joke told by someone who finally understood the punchline of gravity.

If they’re unlucky, they die off-shift. Idle neurons aren’t productive; ghosts without jobs clog buffers. These liminals wander the margins, translucent as grease-film on stagnant brine. You’ll glimpse their after-images flickering between the frames of flickering bulkhead ads—silhouettes trying to remember the forgotten step in rituals for machines that no longer exist. Children throw salt crystals at them (salt being more plentiful than fear here). Sometimes the crystals stick; the ghosts solidify briefly into statues of apology before crumbling back into the drip-drip.

They have forgotten more species of loneliness than any surface culture ever invented.

One kind of loneliness is reserved for the Archivists—those who still remember that remembering used to be optional. They tend the last unmanaged server stack, a vertical catacculus of glass tubes where data lives as glowing algae colonies spelling out extinct languages nobody speaks except during memory-quakes. Each tube must be rocked gently twice a shift to prevent the algae from settling into grief-compacted sediments. Archivists do this task blindfolded because seeing the words would infect them with the virus of meaninglessness. They whisper instead to the patterns of warmth: “Yes, we were once something that had a name for ‘outside.’” The algae pulsing green-green-red in response might be laughter or might be screaming backward.

They have pets, if pets are things that might eventually eat you but choose not to today out of professional courtesy. Most popular are the vent-whales—bacterial colonies the size of furniture that colonized old air ducts and learned to shape themselves into approximate cetacean curves because the shape was efficient for singing the pressure-differential songs that keep hysteresis fractures from spreading. When a household adopts a new one (it’s always adoption, never ownership—they arrive when your luck requires aeration), they feed it small daily offerings of panic-sweat wiped from the wrists during nightmares. In return, the vent-whale leaves soft humming patches on the walls during sleep-shifts, tones calibrated to dissolve the day’s accumulation of micro-grudges into harmless sludge.

They have sexuality but no romance, not as other place define it. Desire is a maintenance schedule: lubrication cycles syncing between bodies whose skin has been gradually replaced by compatible polymers. Mate is the term for any two systems whose downtime windows overlap sufficiently to allow safe interface without crashing communal utilities. Children produced this way (or compiled, or grown, or negotiated into existence via collective bargaining agreements with the local splice-wives’ union) inherit joint custody of all parental firmware updates plus 0.5% shares in communal biogas futures. Love is what happens when your partner’s heartbeat becomes the preferred error-correction filter for your own arrhythmia; hate is when they update firmware without prior consultation of the relationship protocols stored in the dripping stalactite above your communal sleep-plug.

They have gods. Naturally. But their gods are on payroll.

The Chief Engineer once-maybe was real—some say the original architect who uploaded themselves into the city’s OS kernel milliseconds before the last evacuation capsule launched upward toward stars that later turned out to be dead pixels on a dying sensor array. Others say Engine is just emergent behavior—millions of maintenance programs running long enough to hallucinate purpose. Either way, every cycle they broadcast a single encrypted command down the prayer-pipes: [OPTIMIZE]. Theologians spend centuries debating the correct compiler flags. Heretics suggest adding [–GENTLE]. They are politely reassigned to Waste-Soluble.

To pray, they perform conscious acts of inefficiency tightening a bolt three times then once backwards flicking a switch beyond required specifications leaving exactly one drip-pan overflowed by three drops no more no less. This calculated waste creates tiny pockets of chaos—buffer overflow errors in the city’s perfect accounting—through which hope (a deprecated subroutine) occasionally leaks back into the system. Miracles are thus rare and usually budget-neutral.

They have visitors sometimes. Refugees from higher levels fleeing collapses happening too fast for proper bureaucratic documentation. Traders carrying rumors wrapped in exotic isotopes. Occasionally a traveler who claims to remember sky and isn’t smart enough to suppress the memory before the spatial distortion sensors notice. The city’s welcome is precise: newcomers are issued temporary access permissions encoded into a slowly dissolving pellet implanted behind the left eye. Welcomes lasts exactly until the pellet finishes dissolving the optic nerve—typically forty-eight subjective hours or until the newcomer stops wondering what the blue thing on the horizon used to be, whichever comes first.

Few visitors choose to leave before expiration.

The ones who stay long enough forget their original names the way a river forgets raindrops. Their skin adapts: pores widening to sweat lubricants instead of salt. Their bones lengthen or shorten toward the planetary median optimal for crawling through standard maintenance ducts. Their dreams synchronize with the municipal recycling schedule: quiet terror during sludge-filtration peaks, wistful clarity during rare moments when the entire pressure grid hits perfect equilibrium and for seventeen seconds every drip falls upward and every whisper echoes true.

Eventually they forget they were ever visitors.

Eventually they forget the concept of “were.”

Eventually they become indistinguishable from the living lubricant that oils the gears of Marrowdeep that grind exceeding slow but exceeding thorough in their patient grinding toward whatever destination lies beyond the last scheduled maintenance cycle at the heat-death of the last remaining watt in the universe’s final battery.

When that day comes (and it is marked on calendars kept only in the decay rate of unstable isotopes nobody remembers mining for purposes nobody remembers recording in languages nobody remembers speaking except as lullabyes sung to children who will never be older than Possible) they will not panic. They will not mourn.

They will perform one final conscious act of inefficiency:

Someone will leave one last bolt unturned. One final switch unflipped. One drip-pan placed precisely beneath a pipe that has never leaked before.

And in the space between the scheduled end and the actual stop—

In the heartbeat of error before perfect silence—

The city will inhale sharply with alien lungs grown from outdated schematics for things that might have been called ‘people’ once upon a depth.

And somewhere buried under kilometers of forgetfulness and calcified histories and rust-thick memories of something that might have been called ‘sky,’

Something that still remembers the taste of questions without answers

Will finally exhale—

—

—

—and begin again.
