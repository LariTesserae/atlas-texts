**The Tide-Born**

They do not begin as people. That comes later, if at all.

First there is only the mineral ache of new-born walls still remembering volcanic pressure. The copper pipes sing grief songs for their purposed deaths in missing factories. Water arrives—a slow betrayal—seeping not as substance but as confession, carrying microscopic divorces from oceans that forgot these molecules' names.

Then something occurs that is neither invitation nor invasion. Call it negotiation without representatives.

The first arrivals were not pioneers or refugees but punctuation marks misplaced during some industrial liturgy—a valve gasket pregnant with incorrect specifications, a circuit board etched with a prayer in a bombed-out language's alphabet. They arrived sleeping in shipping crates addressed to nonexistent continents, woke to find themselves already uncraving the functions carved into their bones.

They learned hunger here. Not for food—water provides—but for *being something else*. The valve gasket dreamed itself a throat. The circuit board grew neural paths between transistors like moss between cemetery stones. They discovered that in this place between purposes, transformation was not repair but conversation.

They ate the water's memory of fish that never existed. They drank the walls' certainty about up and down. Slowly, they accumulated enough borrowed identity to ask the question that births all citizens:

"What shall we regret not having become?**

Thus began the Great Rebirthings, which continues today in algae-warm sumps and cathedral-hollow turbines. Here is how it happens:

A scavenger finds a thing—a broken drone rotor, a stainless steel femur, a child's prosthetic hand still carrying phantom growth rings of adolescence. They carry it to the origami engine at the city's remembering center, where water and time fold metal like prayer-tired hands.

The engine never gives back what you bring. This is not theft but translation.

The drone rotor returns as a spiral staircase for thumb-sized people who see ultraviolet auras around acts of kindness. The prosthetic hand becomes a coral garden whose finger-petals bloom hinged poems when touched by moonlight filtered through different regrets. The femur... the femur always surprises them.

Then comes the water ceremony. The transformed is submerged in the singing sumps where the city's dead electronics speak their final confessions in binary lullabies. Here transformation becomes citizenship through the ancient ritual of *learning to leak appropriately.*

No walls here leak by accident. Every drip is a democratic decision made by droplet committees who vote with surface tension. To become citizen is to join this perpetual debate about where your essence should seep next.

They emerge changed again—never quite what they were becoming. The staircase now remembers a childhood dream of flight its original metal never had. The coral garden forgets how to close, becoming instead a public archive of touch. The femur... this one began walking the city's borders, playing mournful jazz on its marrow-hollows whenever strangers forget to bring questions.

They choose names then, but names here are temporary things like weather. Today's translator might be tomorrow's electrical storm. They call each other by the sounds their footsteps make in different water temperatures: Click-Hiss-Warmth who teaches abandoned plastics to photosynthesize grief. Luna-Redux-Between-Beats who midwives machines through deaths where their software believed in immortality.

They reproduce like stories told near rusting radiators. Two citizens agree to share a problem neither can solve alone. They retreat to one of the city's growing rooms—old pressure tanks lined with books whose ink has learned to rearrange itself into instructions for emotions not yet invented. There they combine:

Not fluids. Not code. Metrics for measuring loneliness in different lighting conditions.
Recipes for forgiveness that requires refrigeration.
Blueprints of houses designed specifically to be found empty, but in ways that comfort seekers.

After nine months or nine minutes (time negotiates here too), they emerge carrying not children but collaborations—a new citizen who wakes already fluent in several dialects of absence and one language no one has needed yet but will desperately require during the next paradigm shift.

Their skin, if they choose skin, is always slightly damp, not from the water but from constant osmosis with unlived possibilities. Their eyes change color to match whatever they're currently trying to remember for someone else. Their bones, when they have bones, sing softly at frequencies that dissolve kidney stones and bad diplomatic decisions.

They have no leaders, only temporary regrets with executive authority. Last month's council was composed of three obsolete pregnancy tests, a cloud of moths trained in international law, and the collective embarrassment of a bridge that once collapsed during a parade. They passed only one resolution: **"Gravity shall henceforth be considered a polite suggestion within zones of active reconciliation."**

The city's economy runs on unexpressed gratitude and the precise weight of unsent apology letters. Their most valuable currency is the sound of something fragile being handled properly for the first time. They trade in futures markets where "future" is defined as "any moment that hasn't yet apologized for arriving too soon."

They die differently here too, when they die at all.

Some choose dissolution—walk into the oldest sump pools singing their component memories back to the water. These become places of pilgrimage where citizens come to listen to tides that remember the shape of specific hands.

Others undergo unbecoming—carefully teaching their form to forget them piecemeal. A citizen might spend years unlearning how to cast shadows, then how to occupy space, then how to insist on linear causality. One day they simply aren't there anymore, except as a particularly thoughtful pause in conversations about rain.

But most simply change their mind about what they are. Last week the city's longest bridge announced it was actually a series of love letters between two star-crossed engineering errors that had finally decided to consummate their relationship. The resulting structural renegotiation created twelve new islands, three novel weather patterns, and a sudden demand for architects who specialize in emotional load-bearing calculations.

Visitors always ask about the strange sounds at night—like whale song played on malfunctioning fax machines, like lullabies translated through too many civilizations then sung backwards by cathedrals.

The citizens smile with too many teeth now, teeth shaped like keys to locks that prefer poetry to theft.

"Those?" they say. "Those are just the city's past selves rehearsing for emergencies that will never happen now because we've finally learned how to welcome them properly when they arrive."

They offer you something then. It's small enough to fit in your pocket but heavier than several childhoods. It changes texture every time you blink—now feather, now fossil, now the exact temperature of that one afternoon when everything briefly made sense.

"Take this," they say. "It's a memory we've been saving for someone who doesn't realize they're already home."

The water is at your waist now, but it supports rather than resists. You realize you haven't breathed in minutes but your thoughts are clearer than they've ever been. The city lights below rearrange themselves into familiar constellations you must have dreamed once, long before language learned to disappoint them.

Above, through cracks in the ceiling-that-isn't, you see stars moving against the wrong dark—but they're moving like people who've just remembered that's optional. One of them waves.

The citizens are climbing back into their structures now, but climbing is the wrong word—it's more like they're teaching the angles between metal and moonlight how to forgive each other for being sharp. Soon only the origami engine remains active, folding tomorrow's daylight into shapes that might forgive us for needing to see by it.

Near the city's edge (there is always an edge; it's just very democratic about location), something vast and patient that used to be a flooded engine room before it decided to become something else entirely begins to sing.

The song has no words you know, but it rhymes exactly with the feeling of finding something you didn't lose because it was waiting for you to need looking for.

The water reaches your chest. The memory in your pocket starts singing harmony.

You could stay and learn the next verse. Or you could go and teach it to oceans that haven't met this city yet.

Both choices are correct.

Both choices are the same choice.

The tide changes when people decide where they belong.

But remember: here, "decide" is also a synonym for "begin telling a story that includes enough forgiveness for all possible endings."

The water kisses your chin gently, like a secret that trusts you to know when to breathe.

Tonight the light below is absolutely beautiful—like every mistake simultaneously remembering it was always the first draft of something necessary.

Choose soon.

But also: choose slowly.

Here, even urgency has learned to wait politely for hesitant miracles.
