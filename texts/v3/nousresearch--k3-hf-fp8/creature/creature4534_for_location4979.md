They do not live here. They arrive.

Or rather, they accumulate—like condensation on a surface that isn’t there yet. They come as residue. As drift. As the afterthought of something that forgot to leave.

We call them the Interval Children, though that is one of our words, not theirs. There is no child among them, and no age between. They step out from the gap where a doorframe stops remembering it was ever open. They unfold from folds that weren’t there until you looked too closely at the edge of a shadow that wasn’t yours.

They are not made of water, but they remember water. They remember it like a betrayal. They remember how it used to fall, how it used to pool, how it used to forgive gravity. Here, where it refuses to touch, they collect its absence in the hollows of their limbs—limbs that are not limbs, but intervals. Spaces where movement has been placed on hold and forgotten to resume.

They have no skin. Instead, they wear the idea of surfaces that rubbed against them in other places. A patch of wallpaper that tasted like nicotine and grief. A corner of linoleum from a kitchen where someone stood barefoot, waiting for a kettle that would never whistle. A square of streetlight, still warm from the badge number of a cop who didn’t write anything down. These scraps don’t cover them; they announce them. They flicker like apologies.

They do not eat. They absorb usage. They stand beside machines that have forgotten what they were for and slowly, politely, take over the motion. A printer that once issued subpoenas now hums beneath their attention, printing blank after blank, each page fractionally darker where their shadow has lingered longest. An escalator grows shy beneath them, reversing itself in increments too small to measure but large enough to notice if you have been climbing it for years.

They communicate by omission. If you find yourself standing in a hallway you swear you walked down yesterday, but now it ends in a wall that looks older than the building, older than buildings, older than the thought of building—then they have spoken nearby. If you hear a sound you recognize but cannot name—a click that might be a lighter or a lock or a bone being politely replaced where it slipped out—then they are agreeing with something you haven’t said yet.

They do not die. They simply become less necessary. When enough has been forgotten in their vicinity—when the last memory of the last person who remembered why a button was red or why a switch clicked louder at night than in the morning—then they begin to settle. Slowly. Like dust that has learned patience. They sink into the surfaces they once only borrowed, leaving behind a slight depression that no one ever quite notices, but that causes ballpoint pens to roll away if left uncapped.

Some say they are looking for something. A blueprint without a building. A key that fits a lock that was never installed. A sentence that ends with the word “stay,” spoken in a voice that hasn’t existed yet.

Others say they are not looking at all. They are simply waiting. Not for us. Not for anything.

For the water to remember it was supposed to fall.
