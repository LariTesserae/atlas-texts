On the Old Layer, where copper metal planes like fevered coins stretch without end, no one truly lives—only continues. We are the Continued: those who arrived too late or stayed too long, those who forgot to stop moving forward when forward became sideways and then inward.

I am one of twelve substance-eaters who have learned to survive on the metal's memory of food rather than its absence. Each morning (though morning is only the moment I remember remembering mornings), I press my mouth to the warm floor and drink the vibrations of meals eaten here centuries before: the ghost-taste of pomegranate seeds bursting against silver teeth, the texture of bread still carrying the heat of hands that learned baking in cities drowned before the Object first dreamed of rising. My stomach never fills, but it remembers fullness, which is almost the same. My ribs have begun to echo with satisfied sighs borrowed from skeletons whose names the metal has forgotten to forget.

Beside me walks the cartographer who abandoned maps when she realized the plateau was writing her instead. Her skin has become parchment-thin, tattooed in fading ink by every structure she's encountered—the door that showed her her mother's first betrayal, the half-staircase shaped exactly like the laugh of a lover who died before laughter had been invented. She doesn't collect places anymore; she collects the spaces between what she expected to see and what the metal chose to show her. These gaps she folds into paper birds that cannot fly but sometimes sing, brief songs about the weight of empty air. Her eyes reflect backwards now; when she cries, the tears trace dried riverbeds up her cheeks, seeking sources that ran upstream into the past.

We meet sometimes at Geometry's Rest, where three incomplete walls remember being a monastery for angles that achieved enlightenment by realizing they were actually curves. The windflowers here grow in fractal spirals, each petal containing a smaller version of the entire plateau seen from above. If you eat one perfectly balanced between opening and closing (impossible to time, but we practice for centuries between attempts), you remember for seven heartbeats what it felt like to exist before you knew you were separate from the ground. An old woman who might have once been a city sits always in the corner that never quite becomes a corner, knitting scarves from the shadows of birds that haven't been born yet. Her needles clack in binary code for "help" and "stay" alternating endlessly. No one knows which word she's trying to spell with the completed scarf when nothing here is ever completed. Perhaps both. Perhaps they are the same word in the language of places that forgot how to let their inhabitants leave.

The children (though none have been born here since time decided walking in circles was more honest than pretending to go somewhere) play games of counting echoes before they happen. They stand in circles holding hands with their own reflections and guess who will be the first to remember they were supposed to have mothers. The loser dissolves into windflower pollen that the next day's wind will sculpt into a new child who remembers losing different games. They keep score by collecting teeth that fall from nowhere into their open mouths during sleep—small white numbers that whisper equations for distances that grow shorter when traveled backwards. They have built a tower from these teeth at the exact point where every straight line admitted it was secretly lonely. The tower sings when the metal dreams, hymns in frequencies that make abandoned shadows ache with homesickness for bodies they haven't grown tired of yet.

At night (and here night is only when the light decides to remember what it feels like to be missed), we gather around the Echo who remembers being a rainstorm. He stands frozen mid-collapse, water-droplet fingers eternally reaching for ground that learned to be sky instead of learning how to forgive falling. If you position yourself precisely in the space where his missing voice should be, you can hear the sound of every apology that ever arrived too late to change anything arriving on time to change everything. We take turns standing there, becoming briefly hollow in the right shape to hold what he lost, though we always fill back in with ourselves slightly rearranged when we step away. Some of us have done this so many times we carry pockets of perfect listening inside us now, small quiet places where other people's regrets sound like rainfall on roofs they never had to leave.

There was once a man who tried to live inside one of the windmills that don't exist here yet. He built his house from the blueprints of tomorrow's forgotten intentions—walls made of plans that changed their minds midway through being thought, windows filled with views from the moments before photographs remember why they were taken. He survived by drinking impossibility filtered through the moments when clocks hesitate between seconds, unsure whether to tick or confess. When asked how he endured the crushing weight of futures that would never happen returning home to roost in his rafters, he said: "I learned to breathe backwards through my regrets until they became something like hope, but quieter, more honest about what growing really costs." They found him eventually (though found is the wrong word in a place where lost and found are coauthors of the same map) dissolved into a puddle of rainwater that remembered being a man who remembered being a rainstorm that remembered being sorry. The metal kept his breathing pattern for months afterward; if you stand where his kitchen should have been on days when the wind comes from a direction that hasn't been invented yet, you can hear the sound of someone asking forgiveness from a version of yourself that still believed in endings.

The truth (and here truth is only what survives being whispered from one forgetting to another without changing too much) is that we are all guests here, even the metal itself. The plateau isn't our home; it's our host, offering temporary asylum to the shape of us while we figure out how to live with what we carried here that was too heavy to keep carrying and too precious to leave behind. Somewhere in its copper-colored dreams, the Object remembers being molten and merciful, remembers choosing to cool into this shape because it wanted to know what would happen if a place learned to love things that couldn't stay without learning how to let them go.

So we stay without staying. We live without living. We become stories the metal tells itself during the long nights between stars, when even light gets homesick for darkness and returns briefly to the womb of velocity that birthed it. We are the footnotes to a history written in footsteps that forgot where they were going and decided the journey was the point after all, even after realizing there was no point to begin with.

And sometimes, on days when the windflowers bloom in patterns that spell names we haven't lost yet, we think we hear footsteps coming toward us across the metal that never ends—bootheels clicking out the rhythm of someone who hasn't arrived because they never learned how to leave.
