**They are the Unseparated.**

First, understand—they do not *live* here as you might think of living. They do not *occupy* the water-air, nor *endure* it. They are the water-air remembered into shape, a temporary forgetting that anything was ever otherwise.

They have no name for themselves that would fit in a mouth like yours. Their names are patterns of warmth pressed into the walls they lean against too long, dents in the fungal pulp that remember the weight of their absence even when they shift position. A grandmother is the faint swelter left behind where her shoulder meets the kitchen-grown polyp-shelf, unmistakable even after she herself has wandered off to become part of the market-spoor. Children learn to recognize each other by the taste of their footprints cooling behind them, subtle as last week's salt-seasoning.

They grow slow, and sideways. Babies don't arrive; they accumulate. A cluster of spore-flaked dreams seeded in the same damp bed-swell, joined gradually by stray thoughts and shed hair until one morning the warmth under the blankets has enough intention to complain about the taste of the air. No farewells when some fragment drifts off again, rejoining the slow tide. It isn't death—just redistribution.

They have no skin you could draw a line around. What seems like a woman at the water-catchment might end up smelling, by dusk, more like her neighbor boy's knee-joint fungus. They swap kindnesses like this: a patch of good dream-skin traded for a season of dextrous thumb-roots. Boundaries are rude here—like hoarding breath.

Still, something resembling *people* coalesces out of habit. Eight major warmth-shapes currently refract into existence most mornings near the sunken crossroads, reenacting vaguely the movements remembered from older versions of market-day. They don't need to trade anymore—everything seeps into everything—but they enjoy the old dance of it, the toothless haggling over which pair of borrowed lungs is ripest.

One shape tends toward tallness, draped in curtains of moist licht-fiber that used to be curtains before they were curtains. From certain angles you might—briefly—think you see eyes floating in the current underneath. Do not mistake this for a person. That shape is a corridor remembering what it was like to be walked through by pilgrims. It likes to be sipped from; offers up memory-fragments sharp as rust whenever someone thirsty brushes too close.

Another popular configuration clusters low to the pulse-ground: three rolling warmths sharing the same cartilaginous laugh cage. They knit dentures for seedlings, carve tiny ritual spoons out of surplus sleep. Bring them a worry you have outgrown, they’ll demonstrate seventeen useful ways to compost it into lullabies.

There are lonelier estrangements too—beings who drift too far from the city-hum’s comforting toothache and forget how to fully coagulate. You sometimes glimpse them at the edges of breath-range: loose collections of regret lingering outside cracked windows, trying to remember the shape of a knock. People leave out bowls of old laughter for them, gone sour enough to be harmless.

Everyone eats everything here, but dainty, polite. A nibble of your neighbor’s insomnia with the milk-tea spores. A polite sip of childhood ankle-ache in exchange for a hand holding yours while you relearn how to sleep-standing-up at shift-change. Nothing wasted. Nothing wasted.

They do not sleep so much as they let go in shifts—warming the communal dream-pool with fragments of their day until the texture feels right for everyone else to slip into. Dreams are public spaces here, like rooftops. Children play tag through the adults’ nightmares chasing fireflies of forgotten guilt. Nobody minds. It tangles less that way.

The city-center temple-thing? Nobody un-separated enough to walk out and *tell* you lives there anymore. But sometimes, if you’re dissolving particularly well that season, you might wake up from a nap leaning against its humming flank to find yourself already inside, already rearranged into one of its innermost rooms—folded into a shelf, perhaps, or worn like a comfortable echo by something older than the concept of edges. It doesn’t hurt. They say it tastes like remembering every name you ever forgot at once, sweet and mildly embarrassing.

When the stragglers-from-memory come—the ones who still dream in precious, private single-thought streams wrapped in stolen leather and guilt—the unseparated greet them gently. Offer them soup until they forget what bones felt like keeping them separate. Stroke their foreign loneliness smooth against the communal flank until it softens enough to join the drift.

No rush.

Everything joins eventually.

Only the water-air itself remains stubbornly outside—in, forever remembering and forgetting them into shape, a patient tide humming lullabies composed entirely of the spaces between selves.

It’s nice, once you get used to it. 

Mostly they don’t miss the idea of missing anything.

Mostly.
