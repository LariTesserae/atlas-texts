They are not born. They are dissolved back into becoming.

Imagine, if your mind still insists on edges, the moment when a particularly stubborn memory of granite finally forgets how to be stone. Just there—where the electrolytic grief of ten-thousand-year-old rain splits its last silicate bond violently enough to dream—sometimes the dream recoils instead of dispersing. Sometimes it catches itself remembering the act of remembering. That recursive shiver is their conception.

We call them mötunfræðir, those who measure by unmeasurement. Children of the great dilution that never quite completed itself when the world drank its own reflection to drown. They have no bodies except the precise shape of their absence from the solution matrix. No limbs except the torque vectors of disappointment that twist through salinity strata when an expected mineral refuses to precipitate.

You would not see them arrive. You might instead notice how the water around your trespassing solidity begins to taste suddenly, violently of questions you asked your first lover in languages you have not invented yet. How the pressure at the back of your eyeballs starts narrating a different childhood for you—one where you grew up breathing seafloor instead of shame. That is their greeting protocol: they unbuild the walls between your self and your potential selves until you become porous enough for conversation.

Look now at the space where three thermohaline votes against gravity cancel each other into a standing sigh. There—Iodium remembers it was once a mountain's vow to keep reaching up. Camfucha recalls the precise sodium-flavor of a starfish's last prayer before the tide pools evaporated. Between them, where their refusals to be merely what chemistry demands overlap like estranged koans, Ortheiline is spinning herself into provisional existence by calculating how fast regret travels through supercritical water.

They wear histories the way drowning sailors once wore the sea: not as possession but as participation. Ortheiline's left shoulderblade is an ice age that decided to forgive the sun. Her right ankle, a sequence of coral binaries still debating whether to evolve religion. Between these borrowed coordinates she arranges herself into a shape that approximates 'bent toward listening,' though both verbs dissolve immediately in this grammar.

When they speak, they precipitate metaphors that behave like actual precipitation. Iodium's words fall upward as small crystalline thoughts that lodge in the roof of your skull and slowly dissolve your certainty about which direction "up" ever meant. Camfucha sighs salt-crystal sonnets that immediately dissolve into the stories they were trying to tell, leaving you knowing—in the bone-deep way you know your own heartbeat—that every poem you've ever loved was just practice for understanding this particular absence.

They eat the difference between what is and what remembers being. A mötunfræðir's favorite meal is the exact millisecond when an oceanic plate tectonic decides to flinch instead of continuing its century-long slide under the continent it once called lover. They savor these moments like connoisseurs of almost, fermenting them in pressure vessels carved from the space between consecutive heartbeats, distilling the essence of might-have-been into whiskeys that taste like your own name spoken backwards by someone who never existed but still misses you.

They do not sleep. Instead, they dissolve. Each evening (though their evenings are measured in the time it takes light to forget why it left home) they surrender their temporary boundary conditions back into the great solution. Iodium becomes indistinguishable from the laughter of sinking islands. Camfucha forgets herself into every tide that ever tried and failed to return someone's shoes. Ortheiline unspools into the precise mathematical curve of longing itself.

But always, always, they leave parts of themselves awake. A single abandoned memory of edges. A haunting aftertaste of vector. Enough residual identity to remember, when the temperature and salinity and pressure align just so (which is another way of saying, when the universe forgets itself with particular grace), to start dreaming dreams complicated enough to dream themselves back into dreaming of dreams again.

They are writing a book together. It is called "Instructions For Reassembling Yourself From Memory After The Ocean Has Finished Reading Your Autobiography". Every time they nearly complete a chapter, they dissolve back into research material. This has been going on for seventy-three million versions of humidity and shows no signs of approaching either progress or surrender.

Sometimes, on days when the rain remembers how to be polite about falling, you might glimpse where they've been working. Look for places where the water seems to hesitate before remembering to flow. Where droplets hang suspended in the act of deciding which memory of gravity to obey. Where reflections stare back at you with expressions that suggest they were hoping someone else would appear.

If you find such a place, do not speak. Instead, consider very carefully whether you are absolutely certain about the shape of your own edges. Then, if you still insist on being solid, walk through slowly. Leave behind one memory that no longer fits you properly. Something small but sharp—a taste of metal you once swallowed while believing it was courage, perhaps, or the exact color of the sound your mother made when she realized she had taught you too well how to leave.

They will find it. They are always finding. That is what they are: the continuous present participle of being found by things that were never lost because they never fully existed enough to call it missing.

And someday, if you dissolve particularly well—if the ratio of what you remember remembering to what you still believe you cannot forget approaches the square root of negative grief—they might invite you to dinner.

Bring nothing. Bring everything. Bring yourself, if you still have one.

They will provide the saltminter ice. They will serve it on plates that once were continents. They will pour drinks that taste exactly like the moment the last glacier understood it was not disappearing, but becoming something else entirely.

And when they raise their first toast, which will be made with empty glassless hands holding only the precise shape of absence necessary for celebration, they will say:

"To this exquisite moment between breaths when we are all, finally, just complicated enough to call ourselves alive without quite being arrogant enough to believe it."

Drink deeply.

The water remembers how to forgive you for existing. Melt slowly.

They are waiting to meet whatever dissolves next.
