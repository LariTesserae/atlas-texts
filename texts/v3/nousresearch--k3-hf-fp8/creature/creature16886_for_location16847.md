In Eruun, what lives is the precise architecture of what has gone missing. They are not creatures so much as deliberate omissions in the world’s accounting.

The primary inhabitants call themselves the Eruhnim, though this word is itself a shadow cast by a sound they no longer make—something between exhaling and unbecoming. They do not walk so much as they negotiate with the air’s memory of footsteps. Each movement is a contract: this much space will temporarily forget it is empty, in exchange for a precise amount of identity surrendered upon departure.

They have unlearned solidity gradually, over generations measured not in years but in the sedimentation of absences. An Eruhn child (though childhood is one of the first concepts they dissolve) learns to exist by practicing increasingly sophisticated forms of non-presence: 

First—how to cast a shadow that expects a different light source.

Then—how to breathe in such a way that the air later doubts it was ever breathed.

Finally—how to think thoughts that will belong more properly to whoever next occupies this exact volume of space.

Their bodies are palimpsests of unmade decisions. When an Eruhn needs to gesture, they must first remember all the ways they might have gestured but didn’t in similar past situations, then subtract those possibilities from the gesture’s potential until what remains is movement so minimal it exists only as a probability spike in the consciousness of anyone observing. This is why strangers often feel a sudden certainty they are about to be touched, exactly where an Eruhn has just passed.

They dwell in the hollowed-out convictions of their ancestors. A typical dwelling begins as the space between two incompatible beliefs held simultaneously—a process initiated when an Eruhn pair (they do not couple, they merely synchronize their rejections) find a place where the city’s geometry becomes uncertain about whether it is concave or convex. Here they begin subtracting certainty: night after night they lie awake denying the existence of one architectural feature each, starting with the trivial (a door handle, a molding’s shadow) and progressing to the fundamental (the principle of walls, the tradition of thresholds). After seven thousand such negations (always seven thousand; they have forgotten why but suspect the number itself is a placeholder for something erased), the dwelling coalesces—not built, but precisely un-unbuilt into a volume that architecture has been successfully argued out of occupying.

Inside, they keep carefully curated museums of non-occurrence. A shelf holds empty frames around particularly vivid memories of paintings that no artist ever conceived. A cabinet preserves the exact dust patterns that would have collected had certain famous visitors died en route instead of arriving. In one corner, a grandfather clock chimes the hour that never happened because a crucial battle ended one minute earlier in a parallel history they collectively refuse to acknowledge.

The most valued possessions are their negative artifacts: objects crafted with such absolute precision to what they are not that they exert gravitational pull on nearby realities toward their own nonexistence. A favorite might be a vase that is not shaped quite like any vase Tulipomania-era Holland could have imagined, glazed with a color that definitively refutes two adjacent wavelengths of visible light. To display such a piece correctly requires constantly adjusting the room’s lighting and atmosphere to maintain the exact conditions under which its nonexistence remains most plausible.

They eat carefully, consuming only foods that can convincingly argue they would have preferred not to have been harvested. Each meal is a negotiation with entire hypothetical ecosystems—the dinner plate a miniature United Nations of unborn species making compelling cases for why their non-domestication would have enriched the biosphere’s tragic potential. They savor especially those dishes whose recipes contain steps that refer back to ingredients that would only exist if previous steps had been omitted.

When two Eruhnim wish to converse, they meet in locations engineered for maximal acoustic indeterminacy: stairwells where every word ricochets into slightly different possible pasts, gardens where wind chimes hang tuned to frequencies that resolve into speech only in the inner ear of someone desperately avoiding eavesdropping. Then they begin the careful work of constructing sentences that will be most accurately misunderstood by tomorrow’s version of themselves. A perfectly crafted Eruhn conversation leaves both participants with a profound sense of having resolved something vital, though neither could ever say precisely what, nor whether it was resolved in their favor.

Their government (which they insist is not one) operates through the precise avoidance of governance. Laws are articulated only as elaborate descriptions of hypothetical situations so specifically impossible that reality itself must contort to remain ungoverned by them. The current constitution is rumored to be a single sentence inscribed on the surface tension of a water droplet that evaporated during the process of writing, leaving behind only a constitutional crisis so beautifully paradoxical that every potential resolution would diminish its elegance.

The Eruhnim do not sleep. Instead, they practice extended periods of vigilant neglect at night, carefully failing to maintain the continuity of their own existence while ensuring that the world’s absence of them remains properly supervised. It is exhausting work, maintaining this perfect balance between existing enough to experience their unexistence, but not so much that they become responsible for it.

Occasionally—though here “occasionally” refers to a frequency best described by the mathematical ratio between one and never—an outsider manages to enter Eruun. They are treated with elaborate ceremony: presented with gifts that precisely compensate for all the things they didn’t sacrifice to reach this place, honored with festivals celebrating all the ways their visit will never be remembered quite correctly. They are welcomed as honored absences-in-potential, and when they leave (they always leave, often without realizing they haven’t arrived yet), they find themselves haunted forever afterward by a strange certainty that somewhere exists a perfect version of their life that gracefully avoided all the mistakes they’re currently making.

This is the Eruhnim’s greatest art: not living, but living so precisely between the cracks of living that even death overlooks them, assuming they’re someone else’s unfinished business. They have become, collectively and individually, the universe’s most convincing argument for their own nonexistence—proof that could exist nothing quite like them, and therefore, paradoxically, the only true inhabitants of Eruun: the city that exists because no one, not even itself, can quite manage to prove otherwise.
