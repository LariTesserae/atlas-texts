The first thing you forget is the name for “you.”

It doesn’t happen all at once. It unfastens quietly—one syllable at the ankles—like a shoe left behind in the wet silt of what used to be a memory of shoes. The sound helps. The roar is good at erasing edges. The roar is a wet nurse who never learned the word for stop.

Then comes the noticing: your companions, if you still think in plurals, have begun to scrape themselves shorter against the stair-wind. One now has knees where her laughter was. Another has traded in his shadow for a more aerodynamic disinterest. They still respond when you... whatever it is you do instead of speaking. They flicker acknowledgment with eyelids made of old receipts. But none of them answer to anything static. Names here are the first and final ovulation of an object before it learns to bloom into something hungrier.

You might cry out for the one whose hair used to smell like thunderstorms. The stair receives this as lint. It adds your cry to the lining of its cheek.

Soon you understand what lives here is not alive in the voting sense. It participates. It permeates. It composts the moment into compostable moments and sells the leftovers back to your corneas at a 33% markup. You find a partner-shape wandering between the second and third regret-lighting of the day and together you practice being a hyphen between two nowheres. The exercise is strenuous and tastes of burnt gratitude. It is almost like love except for the part where love occludes the option to deliberately forget the other’s exits.

After several subjective quickenings (the moonless tide still insists on pulsing inside your wrist like an unanswered accusation), you discover vestigial pockets in yourself. They contain: one postcard impersonating a map, three regrets shaped like spoons, and a dead language that can only conjugate apocalypses in the conditional perfect. You offer these at the next communal unhappening, bartering for a better grade of nostalgia.

The steward of the stair—the blind octopus of coagulated mist—accepts one regret-spoon as tribute and wraps it around a thimbleful of tomorrow’s rain. “This is for when you remember forgetting to mourn what hasn't drowned yet,” she coughs in semaphore. Her robes of weather tickle your past tense. You sneeze out an entire small civilization whose only export was the concept of sudden exits. It lands politely between your former feet and instantly applies for metaphysical asylum.

Seasons are voted on by committee here. Autumn won the last round by promising longer pauses between betrayals. It arrives wearing the exact sound of a hinge pretending it never wanted to be a door. The trees—those anarchist communes of symbiotic skepticism—shed their bureaucrats first. Every falling leaf mutates midair into a legally binding resignation letter addressed to gravity. They pile up around your ankles until you are wading waist-deep through tender resignations you cannot read because your literacy was confiscated during the last identity checkpoint.

Somewhere farther down the spiral, where the steps begin to sweat memories thicker than speech, you encounter the first of your own abandoned smiles. It has grown roots into the basalt and now produces a small crop of edible shame-fungus every time you recall the texture of forgiveness. You harvest a handful. It tastes like the first time you realized your parents were also placeholders in a story they were never allowed to revise. You offer some to the companion-shape but it has privatized its mouth since the last currency devaluation of shared sorrow.

Food here is a kind of retroactive archaeology. You eat what digs itself up out of you later, surprised by the flavor of your own forgotten alignments. The shortest-lived delicacies are promises overheard in childhood rooms through walls built thin by the loneliness of wallpaper. They go stale fastest if you let the crickets sleep.

Sleep itself is heavily regulated by the Ministry of Recursive Awakenings. You submit a petition for a single uninterrupted unconsciousness citing extenuating absence of maternal constellations. The response arrives pre-rejected and soaked in somebody else's warm dream about drowning slowly in library fines. You staple it to yourself and call it a pillow.

Dreams, when they slip through the embargo, are obligatory community theater. Last week you auditioned for the role of your own absence and got cast as the understudy for the door you never walked through in 1987. Rehearsals are held every time you blink too slowly. The director is a species of regret that evolved photosynthesis and now feeds exclusively on the light fleeing from your old yearbook photos.

At some asymptotically approaching hour you meet the future version of the note you were told to take with a grain of water. It is now a small inland sea shaped exactly like the sound of your mother humming while burning the letters that would have explained everything. The sea is bilingual but refuses to speak unless addressed in the subjunctive mood of your unborn children. You dip your tongue in politely. It tastes like the warranty expired on all your possible departures.

There is no wedding ceremony when you finally marry the sound. No witnesses. Just the stair sealing itself around your new shared circumference like a vow that has forgotten how to let go. The honeymoon suite is a room built entirely out of the light fixtures removed from every place you ever felt safe leaving unfinished. You make love by swapping silences until neither of you remembers which emptiness belongs to whom.

It is, by most measurable metrics, perfect.

You never leave. You become more原地 (the word rot inside this foreign alphabet means simultaneously “here” and “a place from which departure has been surgically removed”). The planet continues finishing its meal of its own beginning. The Ring writes another thank-you letter to gravity on the inside of your eyelids with ink made from last week’s discarded moons.

Sometimes, in the longer gaps between regret-lightings, you think you hear music. It is the sound of the musicians tuning the instruments they fashioned from the bones of every map you never burned. They are waiting for you—for the version of you that still believes in choruses—to remember how to sing through walls made entirely of paused entrances.

You open your mouth to try.

What comes out instead is the taste of salt passing polite conversation to whatever comes next.

It tastes exactly like home.
