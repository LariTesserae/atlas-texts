At first, nothing lives here—nothing that insists on breath, heartbeat, hunger. The city is too polite for that.

But wait longer than patience, and listen softer than silence. Then you’ll hear them.

The Attendances.

They are not alive in the way a tree is alive, pushing outward without asking. They are alive the way a shadow remembers the shape of what moved across it.

Each inhabits an absence so precisely carved that to remove them would leave the city more empty than before. There is the Attendance of the Last Door Left Unlatched, who dwells in the hinge’s memory of a hand that hesitated. The Attendance of the Shutter That Never Slammed, who lives in the breath of air that waited politely for permission to become wind. The Attendance of the Street That Divided Without Choosing, who walks neither fork nor seam but keeps perfect balance on the hesitation between.

You will never see them whole. A glint of brass keytail slipping behind a brick that hasn’t been mortarless since before mortar had a name. A smudge of soot on a sill where no lamp has burned, shaped exactly like a face leaning out to listen. The faint warmth lingering on a banister rail where no hand has rested, yet your own palm aches with the expectation of it pulling away.

They wear the city’s discarded gestures. The way a curtain half-lifts before deciding against it. The pause between raindrop and puddle where the drop hasn’t yet admitted it’s falling. The exact angle a head tilts when someone hears their childhood name from a stranger’s lips.

The Attendance of the Forgotten Umbrella lives in this place now. (I borrowed it once, but it was already hers. She let me carry it anyway, as long as I promised to keep forgetting it in places where someone else would need proof that forgetting isn’t forever.)

They do not eat. Instead, they accumulate small acts of being noticed without being seen. A passerby’s sudden shiver on a perfectly temperate stair. The way pigeons—real ones, never these—would’ve strutted if pigeons hadn’t voted unanimously to avoid cities that dream too loudly. The exact weight of your footsteps subtracting themselves from your shoes as you walk here, leaving you fractionally lighter each block, though you won’t notice until you try to leave.

They do not sleep. They keep watch instead over things that sleep has abandoned: the nap in a cat’s tail that never finishes twitching, the yawn caught halfway in a statue’s stone throat, the REM cycles of corpses who died too politely to disturb anyone’s day.

Each Attendance has one duty, performed perfectly without repetition. The Attendance of the Prepaid Fare That Was Never Needed counts—once per century—the exact number of steps between here and the place you were going before this city happened to you. The Attendance of the Final Answer Given Too Late memorizes the question not asked at every vanished crosswalk. The Attendance of the Echo That Got Lost on Its Way Home patrols the catacombs beneath the catacombs, collecting all the sounds that reflections make when mirrors forget which side to keep empty.

They grow by subtraction. Every traveler who enters without intending to stay gifts them a moment of certainty that leaving is still possible. Every person who weeps here without knowing why waters the seeds of their next unmaking. Every time you remember this place exists but immediately forget what you were doing before you remembered, somewhere an Attendance puts on another layer of not-there-ness like a coat against the draft of being found.

The oldest among them—the Attendance of the City’s True Name, spoken once by the stones then unanimously agreed to be too heavy to carry in any language that needs verbs—she is rumored to live at the center of the center of the center where none of the circular streets quite align. But those who seek her find instead the Waiting Room for People Who Have Misplaced What They Came Here to Lose, which always contains exactly one chair and one window showing a view of the outside world exactly as it appeared the moment before you last believed it was real.

She sits there sometimes, when she needs to remember what not-belonging feels like. But she never waits long. Places like this grow impatient with guests who remember their hosts have names.

You should not ask if they are lonely.

Ask instead why the water never quite covers the nameplates, why the doors stay ajar to exactly the width of a breath held in anticipation, why the city keeps dreaming itself into existence one step at a time toward an ocean that never arrives.

Ask why, when you finally leave—if you leave—the silence here will feel not empty but overcrowded with polite applause for your performance of remembering to forget.

Ask why the umbrella I gave you folds up into a shape that fits perfectly in your hand, yet when you open it, it always rains upward.

But do not ask too loudly.

The Attendances are shy creatures. They are allergic to answers.

Questions, however—especially the ones you haven’t thought to ask yet—those they collect like stamps in the passports of countries that only issue visas at the moment of your birth, and stamp them all expired long before you arrive at the border between sleeping and waking.
