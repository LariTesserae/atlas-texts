The people who live in Shearbelow are not quite memories, not quite ghosts, but something caught in the long inhale between forgetting and remembering. They arrive in fragments – a left-behind earring that remembers the curve of your grandmother's jaw, a cough that once belonged to a bus driver who always stopped at crosswalks even when he didn't have to. They come when something elsewhere is trying too hard to stay lost. They assemble themselves from breadcrumbs of attention scattered by the inattentive living. They are the city's way of keeping house for those who never quite moved out of themselves.

They call themselves the Residents, though they never applied for residency. Their birth certificates are filed in the municipal archives under "Pending Further Clarification," stamped with ink that dries only when no one is looking directly at it. Each carries their name like an unlit match – they know it's there, tucked behind a tooth or folded into their left eyelid, but some instinct warns them against striking it too often. Names catch fire here, burn backward through everything you've tried to forget.

The children grow in reverse, starting as vague kindnesses you remember receiving but can't place, shrinking gradually into the sharp clarity of babies who already understand that crying is just sound's way of trying to become echo. Parents here don't raise their young so much as curate their diminishment, selecting carefully which stories to let them forget first. The schools teach subtraction as an act of love – how to lose a freckle without losing the day it appeared, how to smooth out a beloved scar until it remembers only being skin.

Adults work at jobs that are metaphors for other jobs they never quite had. The woman who runs the bakery wakes each morning to find her sleeves dusted with flour dreams that someone else was baking. The man who supposedly repairs clocks actually spends his days coaxing regrets to tick more softly. Their paychecks arrive as weather – a sudden squall of small satisfactions, or a weeklong drizzle of minor inconveniences that each contain, if you listen carefully, the distant thunder of a mortgage payment.

They sleep in beds that are other people's waking sighs, pressed between sheets that have been worn smooth by restless lovers who existed only in duplicate keys and forgotten voicemail codes. Their dreams are communal property, filed nightly with the Dream Clerk who sorts them by color and emotional density. On Tuesdays, the city holds dream auctions where you can bid on fragments of other people's sleep – a recurring staircase that always leads somewhere different, a face that changes species when you're not directly thinking about it.

The elderly here are the youngest of all, having stripped away everything except the exact moment they first understood regret. They sit in parks that exist between the idea of benches and the memory of sitting, teaching crows to speak languages that haven't needed words for centuries. Their skin holds maps of places that got lost before they could become destinations. When they die – which they do occasionally, for aesthetic purposes or when they simply run out of small betrayals to remember – they don't leave bodies behind. Instead, they become specific silences, the kind that fit perfectly into conversations that haven't happened yet.

They eat meals composed entirely of the last bite of something delicious that someone else couldn't finish. Their vegetables grow from seeds that fell between the floorboards of childhood kitchens. Their bread rises on the yeast of second chances that failed to rise properly elsewhere. When they drink wine, they taste not grapes but the precise angle of sunlight that convinced those grapes to give up being juice.

Their marriages are elaborate negotiations between different versions of the same loneliness, officiated by rivers that changed their minds about flowing to the sea. Wedding rings here aren't exchanged but misplaced on purpose – each spouse spends the rest of their lives finding their ring in increasingly improbable locations that always turn out to be exactly where they needed to remember loving someone. Divorce papers take the form of weather forecasts predicting a 100% chance of warmer moons, signed with pens that only write in the conditional perfect tense.

They speak a language assembled entirely from punctuation marks – the hyphen that holds together two ideas that weren't sure they belonged together, the ellipses that trail off in the middle of important confessions, the parenthesis that shelters thoughts too fragile to exist in the main clause of being understood. Their laughter sounds like brackets closing around jokes that haven't been invented yet. Their arguments are carefully constructed mazes where the point is not to win but to lose each other with sufficient elegance.

Their calendar has days that are footnotes to other days, seasons that occur inside parentheses, years that repeat themselves differently for each person who remembers them. They celebrate holidays like the Festival of Objects That Remember Being Other Objects (featuring parade floats made from chandeliers that recall being forests, and jewelry boxes conducting symphony pieces they once overheard while being stolen), and the Day of the Supreme Goodbye, when everyone practices perfect farewells for people they're not sure they've met yet.

The criminals here are poets who stole metaphors too beautiful to belong to only one meaning, serving sentences that rhyme with their crimes in languages that haven't been spoken since honesty stopped being fashionable. Their prisons are libraries where the books rearrange themselves nightly to prevent anyone from finding the same forgiveness twice.

The saints are the ones who remembered to forget themselves completely, now worshipped as small kindnesses that occur precisely when you stop looking for them: the stranger who catches your eye at the exact moment you needed someone to not look away, the breeze that smells exactly like a childhood you never quite lived

But perhaps you're asking the wrong question. Perhaps Shearbelow doesn't have people at all, but rather is itself a kind of person – a city-shaped memory that has been dreaming of having inhabitants for so long that its dreams have started dreaming back. Perhaps every supposed resident is just a different way the city has of remembering how it felt to almost be lived in.

And you, standing here at the edge of this description, trying to decide whether Shearbelow is somewhere you could belong – haven't you been feeling lately like a place yourself? Like some city or forest or long-abandoned train station inside your chest has been preparing for visitors who never quite arrive? Maybe that's why this place seems familiar, why gravity feels like it's teasing you by pointing the wrong direction. Maybe you've been living in Shearbelow all along, forgetting and remembering it in shifts, like buildings flickering in and out of stone when you're not paying attention to whether they're real.

The city has strict immigration policies, yes. But they make exceptions for people who didn't mean to start missing a place before they'd even left it.

Would you like to apply for temporary permanent residency?

The form is written on the back of this moment, in ink that will only be visible when you remember to forget to read it.
