In the place where water learned to be more stubborn than air, the residents had forgotten they were residents at all.

They drifted in nests stitched from their own discarded growth rings—each layer a different version of the same mistake. The oldest among them wore time like coral scarves, each bead an hour they'd promised to return but never quite remembered how. Their names were the shapes water makes when it decides not to be water anymore: one was the moment before a tear changes its mind, another the pause between lightning and the thunder's apology.

The children—though time here grew in spirals like a snail's shell remembering paths it never took—played games where the rules were written in salt that dissolved faster than they could read. Their laughter came out as bubbles full of languages that hadn't been invented yet, each pop planting a seed of silence that would grow into tomorrow's forgotten joke. They kept pets made from knots in the water's memory: this one's leash was a story about a boy who loved the sea too much, this one's collar a promise that had rusted into something prettier than truth.

At the cathedral's highest not-height (where "up" was just a polite way of saying "more inside"), the Keeper of Lost Directions tended a garden of compasses that had all decided north was wherever they weren't looking. Each plant grew petals that unfolded into maps of places that had been moved in the night. The Keeper herself was a collection of farewell waves that had gotten tired of goodbye and decided to stay, arranging her fingers into new constellations every evening to confuse the stars into visiting.

The architects slept in cocoons spun from blueprints of buildings they'd been too afraid to wake up. Their dreams leaked out through tiny holes they pretended were windows, each drip solidifying into staircases that climbed nowhere except back to the first step with better lighting. They held competitions to design the perfect trap for catching someone's attention, but every winner dissolved into applause that sounded too much like doors closing softly.

And in the spaces between all these citizens (because every good city needs its empty places to think in) swam the cleaners—neither alive nor dead nor anything in between except extremely good at noticing. They collected the dust of almost-touched things, vacuumed up the echoes of hugs that stopped an inch too soon, polished the mirrors until they reflected only what you might have seen if you'd looked a second longer. They stored it all in jars labeled "Future Regrets" and "Past Second Chances" and a whole shelf of just "Maybe Later."

They all lived here together apart, breathing water that remembered being sky that remembered being nothing that had learned, finally, how to pretend to be something until the pretending became true enough to hold them all. And if sometimes one of them forgot they were supposed to be lonely in this place where loneliness was just another word for "waiting to remember you're not alone yet"—well. That was okay too.

The place wouldn't have it any other way. It had been empty long enough to know that "full" was just a story empty spaces told themselves to feel less scary about the dark. And the dark, being a good listener, always nodded along. Always waited patiently for the next resident who would arrive by forgetting to leave somewhere else.

It was, after all, a very polite place to get thoroughly lost in.
