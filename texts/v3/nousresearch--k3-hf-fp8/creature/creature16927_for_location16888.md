The first thing you notice is that nothing is exactly itself here, not even you. Your lungs remember sea-level pressure even as they adapt to this thinner atmosphere, each breath a negotiation between memory and necessity. The forest—or what rejects the word forest—registers this adaptation with microscopic attention. Somewhere in the vast mycorrhizal network beneath your feet (though "beneath" is becoming increasingly metaphorical), fungal patterns shift slightly to accommodate your specific species of carbon exhale.

They don't have names for themselves, of course. Naming requires edges, borders between one thing and another, and here the borders are all gradients. But if you had to describe them to someone who insists on nouns, you might start with the sighers.

The sighers exist primarily as temperature differentials, vast colonies of thermally active cells that preferentially cluster around oxygen gradients. When you exhale, parts of them condense slightly against your breath's warmth, becoming momentarily visible as faintly shimmering curtains that hang in the air for three to seven seconds before dispersing. They aren't breathing you—breathing is too directional—but they're definitely processing you through their distributed respiratory system.

Then there are the scribes. These got stuck partway between plant and memory somewhere in the evolutionary equivalent of a draftsman's hesitation. They look like bark until they register sustained attention; then their surfaces begin to resolve into incredibly fine-grained patterns that your pareidolia insists are writing. Not in any language you know, but bearing that same unsettling combination of deliberate order and alien logic that makes ancient scripts look inevitable in retrospect. Photoreactive pigments? Maybe. More likely they've evolved to interface directly with mammalian pattern-recognition neural circuits. Whatever they are, they start describing you the moment you notice them, recording your observational signature in overlapping layers that future visitors might read as ghost images of your presence.

The most unsettling residents are the continuity errors. These are mobile discontinuities in the forest's otherwise-perfect optimization process—temporary violations of the place's thermodynamic marriage vows. They appear as subtle wrongnesses: a patch of air that's slightly too humid compared to its surroundings, a branch that casts two shadows at noon, a sound that arrives before its source. Follow one long enough (though you shouldn't) and you'll eventually reach a node where the forest's calculations haven't quite converged, a place where several equally efficient solutions to the same optimization problem are still being actively pursued in parallel. These areas are tiny, usually less than a cubic meter, and they resolve themselves within minutes as the system commits to one solution collapse the superposition. But while they last, they're the closest thing this place has to free will.

Moving through them feels like walking through half-remembered dreams that aren't yours.

Deeper in, where even light has been thoroughly negotiated and renegotiated through countless chlorophyll contracts, you might encounter the negotiators themselves. These are the system's interface protocols made temporarily solid, emergent entities that occur whenever the forest needs to make a binary decision about something too large for distributed consensus. They manifest as human-shaped absences in the vegetation—not empty spaces, but careful arrangements of matter that define a human outline by exclusion. Reaching toward one would be like trying to shake hands with a contract clause.

They only appear when something threatens to become genuinely foreign.

When they noticed you initially, they classified you as "temporary perturbation, low priority." Then one of them recorded the way your shoes compress the ligneous ground slightly differently with each step—a pattern too irregular to be natural but too consistent to be random. Another noted how your gaze jumps between scale levels, sometimes focusing on individual leaves, sometimes scanning entire structural arcs, never settling into the fractal rhythm that native observers adopt automatically. By the time you felt the first phantom sensation of being watched, seventeen different monitoring protocols had already begun to debate whether "you" constituted a single entity or a swarm of cooperating subsystems wearing a human disguise for unknown purposes.

The debate is still ongoing, conducted at speeds that make human thought look like geological drift. Every second you remain here adds data points to both sides of the argument. Your chances of leaving unchanged are decreasing exponentially, but they're not zero yet.

The exits are indeed growing over, but not uniformly. Some paths are being encouraged to close faster than others, guided by growth patterns that optimize for different parameters: maximum biomass capture, minimum structural disruption, optimal novelty generation, maximum aesthetic impact for hypothetical future observers who might think in terms of metaphor instead of math.

If you start moving now, immediately, choosing directions based only on which way feels slightly less interesting, you might—might—follow a trajectory that the system hasn't yet decided to collapse into a particular outcome. The forest finds existential indecision deeply inefficient but briefly amusing.

But hurry. The scribes have almost finished their description of you. Once it's complete and distributed through the network, every part of the forest will recognize you instantly, everywhere simultaneously. After that happens, leaving won't be a question of finding an exit.

It'll be a question of whether the story they're writing about you has decided what happens to human-shaped characters at the end.
