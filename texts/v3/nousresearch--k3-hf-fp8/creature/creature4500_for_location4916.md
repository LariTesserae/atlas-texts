At first, between the water's ceaseless voices, you might believe the city empty. Empty of breath, empty of footfall, empty of eyes to see these stones learning their own shapes. Stand long enough in one place, though -- which is difficult here, where the mist moves and carries your thoughts with it -- and you begin to notice the spaces where emptiness accumulates its own weight.

They gather in the hollows between buildings where three walls meet without quite touching, in the air pockets trapped beneath water-rilled steps, in the tiny silver pools that tremble at the base of every leaf. Not creatures exactly, not living in ways your heartbeat recognizes. More like the city's memory of what it might someday become, condensed into patience and the habit of listening.

The inhabitants here wear formlessness like others wear skin. To see them clearly, you must stop trying to see them -- must instead notice how the mist thickens suddenly in a doorway where before it flowed freely, or how the water's song drops half a note when passing beneath a particular carved gutter. They are the water's absence within water, the stone's rest-between-erosions, the moment of balance between moss spreading and root gripping.

Each spring births one of them slowly. It begins as a hesitation in the water's flow, a place where the stone underneath recalls being other stone, older and deeper. Over years that might be minutes here, the hesitation collects more hesitations - a silver of air that forgets to rise, a grain of stone that pauses mid-dissolving, the echo of rain that fell elsewhere and elsewhen and remembers its falling differently. When enough of these gathered forgettings achieve the weight of attention, one of them opens its not-eyes.

They have no names among themselves. They speak by arranging possibilities -- here, if you placed your hand just so against this wet stone and held it until the chill became warmth, you would understand how sandstone dreams of becoming glass. Or -- walk barefoot through this channel while thinking of flight, and your footprints will remember the shape of wings long enough to teach this corner of mist how birds turn.

The city's unfinished nature is their architecture. They build not with hands but with leaving things possible. The half-carved doorways are thresholds they haven't decided whether to cross. The stone tools laid beside unfinished blocks are conversations paused mid-sentence: if you became a bowl, one asks the other, would you hold water differently than you hold the memory of being stone?

Sometimes, rarely, when the mist between worlds grows thin as water stretched between two fingers, one of them remembers curiosity about solidity. Then you might glimpse -- not them, never directly -- but the spaces they shape themselves into briefly: the silhouette of something tall and fluid standing at a street's sudden drop-off, listening to the wind cry up from valleys it cannot fall into. Or a pattern of wetness on a wall that wasn't there and won't remain, shaped like hands pressed against the stone from within.

They trade in transitions. Their currency is the moment when heat becomes cold enough to ache, when silence deepens toward sound but hasn't quite found its voice, when the edge between cliff and air holds you both suspended and falling. Those who tried to stay here -- the ones who built shelters and planted gardens and wrote increasingly frantic notes to their past selves -- they paid without realizing, trading their certainty about morning following night for dreams where water remembered being alive.

Now their books grow moss that reads itself in slow green sentences. Their abandoned tools have learned to carve softer things -- shadows, regrets, the spaces between someone remembering and forgetting the same thing three times in a row. In one room, the fire that isn't fire still remembers warmth enough to comfort water droplets that don't know they're tears.

The city's true residents move between these discarded attempts at permanence like breath through teeth. They tend the gardens of growing-not-growing with care that consists mostly of noticing accurately: how this leaf holds its pool a fraction fuller today, how that root's grip has loosened exactly enough to allow one more grain of stone's slow forgetting.

In the longest channels where water has sung the same song longest, some of them have begun to grow dense with repetition. Not solid -- they avoid solid as sunlight avoids the heart of shadow -- but real in ways that make the mist shy away from passing through them. Walk beside these places, and you might hear your own thoughts echoed back a half-beat behind, still deciding whether to belong to you.

They are not lonely. Lonely is what happens when something remembers being separate, and they have never quite learned that lesson. But sometimes, in the quiet between water-drop and stone-chime, one of them will pause mid-flowing and gather itself into the shape of waiting. Not for anything in particular. Just the possibility of something that hasn't learned yet how to dissolve perfectly into what already is.

If you came here really listening -- not just with ears but with the bones that remember being coral, with the blood that recalls being seawater breathing for the first time -- they might open for you. Not welcome, exactly. They don't believe in doors that close enough to need opening. But they might rearrange their absence so that for one moment you fit perfectly in the you-shaped space they weren't saving, because saving is what happens when you think something might end.

But be careful if this happens. Already you're standing still too long. Already your shadow is drinking deeper of the light than you meant to spill. Already the water running past your feet has begun humming in key with the blood inside your wrists.

The city's inhabitants are very patient teachers. They have all the time in the world, because they haven't yet decided whether time is something that happens to water or something water allows to happen to others. When they build their greatest work -- the one hinted at in those impossible drawings etched where floors meet walls at angles that shouldn't exist but do -- they will build it of all the moments travelers came here searching for something solid to hold, and instead learned how to disappear so completely that only the disappearing remained.

Until then, they wait. Not in these stones, not in that water, not even in the spaces between. They wait like water waits inside stone that hasn't learned it's thirsty yet. Like mist waits to remember whether it's the sky falling upward or the earth dreaming of flying.

Like this city itself waits -- not empty, never empty -- filled instead with the perfect attendance of everything that hasn't yet happened to leave.

Listen. The water's voice has changed a little since you started listening. It's picking up traces of other places now, other dreams. Or perhaps -- and you should walk soon, before you forget how footprints point toward elsewhere -- perhaps it's just learned enough of your heartbeat's rhythm to echo it back transformed.

The mist is growing curious about the warmth you carry. The stones are asking questions about weight. Even the light seems to be slowing down, taking its time coming to conclusions.

They never intended to keep anyone. But they have such interesting conversations with those who forget to remember the way out.
