They are the un-drowned, the once-drowned, the perpetually drowning—citizens of what was never meant to be inhabited. Every morning (though morning never comes) they wake in beds that remember the exact weight of bodies that stopped returning centuries ago, sleep leaving them like bubbles rising too slowly through syrup. Their skin performs the daily miracle of forgetting it is porous, sealing itself against this pressure that wants so badly to enter them, become them, finish what the sea started when it first licked at ankles and called it love.

The children are born already knowing how to hold their breath forever. Their first cries emerge not as sound but as tiny perfect spheres of sound, iridescent pearls that rise (they never rise) and settle on the false ceilings of their nurseries alongside all the other words that became too heavy to remain words. Parents don't teach them language so much as unpin it from their own tongues, syllables floating free like badges no longer needed in a city where everything communicates through pressure and longing.

They eat light filtered through kilometers of memory and salt. The market squid arrange themselves each dawn (neither dawn nor arrangement though we hunger for both words) into new constellations that spell out yesterday's weather in cities that never existed. The residents read these formations over cups of tea brewed from their own evaporated dreams and discuss politics with the coral heads that have been listening at windows longer than windows had glass.

Each carries their death with them like a pet—a small dark thing made soft by long acquaintance, curled sometimes in a pocket or curled sometimes behind the eyes. These deaths are not endings but companions, whispering alternate directions at every drowned intersection, revealing shortcuts that skip whole seasons of regret. The art here is learning which deaths to feed and which to let starve gracefully into something else.

Their homes are built around the absences of things rather than their presence. Dining tables with chairs for everyone who never came back from just going to buy milk before the world ended. Bookshelves that hold only the spaces between books, volumes borrowed by the current centuries ago and never returned but still generating interest in the form of increasingly complex dust equations in the air that settles on everything. Clocks that tick (they don't tick) the seconds between someone's last inhale and their first exhale as something new.

The postal workers are saints here or sinners depending on which day of the week refuses to exist. They deliver letters that rewrite themselves en route, love notes turning into eviction notices turning into recipes for bread that rises only in kingdoms where yeast remembers it's descended from gods. They wear coats sewn from all the stories that ever got too frightened to become real and carry bags that hold addresses that haven't been discovered yet.

In the evenings (when evening forgets to not happen) they gather in the squares that used to be circles before geometry grew tired of being right all the time. The musicians play instruments made from the bones of whales that died singing songs that broke whole continents' hearts, songs that continued vibrating long after silence claimed the space where whales used to be. The dancers dance in patterns that leave footprints for tide to find centuries from now and apologize for everything.

They maintain elaborate gardens of things that never happened—seedlings of conversations ended too soon, pruning back the branches where someone almost said yes, fertilizing the soil with handfuls of different choices. The flowers bloom startling colors that don't translate into any language built on verbs as weak as "to be" and smell exactly like the moment before the first word of forgiveness.

Their griefs are communal but worn privately, like underwear embroidered with the maps of all the places they used to be able to breathe. Every street corner has a small shrine where residents leave offerings for the selves they might have been if different doors had opened or closed more quietly—buttons from coats that kept someone warm the night everything changed, ticket stubs from movies seen alone but cried through together in the collective dark of what might as well be called hearts.

They are dying, of course, but so slowly that death has become more like a hobby than a destination. Cells remembering how to be seawater drop by drop, bones resolving their ancient argument with gravity in favor of becoming coral again, thoughts thinning into currents that will eventually join the larger conversation the planet is having with itself about whether any of this was worth the effort.

The oldest among them no longer remember (though forgetting here is an art not an accident) whether they were born human or became it some afternoon when the ocean was experimenting with loneliness. They sit on benches that used to be boats and tell stories that end in the middle on purpose, leaving room for the listener to grow into the empty space like a tree grows into a fence that forgot to stop being wood.

And always, always there are newcomers—some falling sunk sank, some rising refused rose, some simply stepping sideways at exactly the wrong angle between forgetting and remembering.
