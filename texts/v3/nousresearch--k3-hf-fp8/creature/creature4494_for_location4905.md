The Chamber does not keep its inhabitants like a house keeps guests. Instead, they are held—not in bonds, but in resonance, like a note that forgets to end after the bell has stopped ringing.

Most never notice when they arrive. One moment they are walking through some other place—an abandoned quarry, a museum after closing, a dream of empty hallways—and the next, the air has changed texture around them, becoming something that resists meaning. They blink, and their shadow is already too shallow, already gathering its things to leave.

They appear at intervals too irregular to call a pattern. A woman in a red coat from a city that no longer uses coat hooks. A child who does not speak the language of any country on any map. An old man carrying a birdcage wrapped in velvet. Always alone. Always walking when they shouldn’t be walking anymore.

They do not remember their names immediately. The Chamber takes them gently, like a mother smoothing creases from a sleeper’s forehead. First the sharp edges: dates, debts, the taste of cinnamon. Then the softer things: the smell of their mother’s hairbrush, the way their first love laughed through their nose. Eventually even the sense of having lost something goes quiet, settles like dust on windowsills that were never meant to be cleaned.

What remains is bone-deep familiarity without memory’s clutter. They recognize the copper tube before they see it, know without being told that if they hollow out their chest and place the birdbone cube inside, it will fit perfectly. They understand that the transparent columns are not for looking through but for listening around, like standing in the silence between two thoughts that might otherwise touch and harm each other.

They live without living. The woman in the red coat (now white with salt) arranges the found objects each morning in patterns that ache with significance she can’t name. The child teaches their shadow to play games that end when both players forget who is chasing whom. The old man unwraps his birdcage slowly, ceremonially, revealing nothing but more velvet, softer and deeper each time, until even his hands become uncertain they ever held anything else.

They do not eat or drink or sleep as these things are understood elsewhere. Instead, they practice stillnesses of increasing purity. The child becomes expert at not-breathing until their chest forgets what rhythm it once kept time to. The woman learns to un-think hunger until her stomach stops sending messages that no longer have addresses. The old man perfects the art of un-remembering his feet until walking becomes something that happens elsewhere, to someone else, possibly in a different language entirely.

They speak occasionally, but only in the spaces between words that might otherwise mean something. Their conversations are careful as surgery performed with shadows. “Did you hear?” one might begin, and another will respond, “Yes, it was exactly like the sound of yesterday remembering tomorrow,” and both will nod with the satisfaction of having moved precisely nowhere together.

When the spiral ridges brighten, they gather without being summoned to the nearest column, arranging themselves around it like punctuation marks around silence. They do not watch the silver threads rearrange themselves—that would be too crude, like trying to see music. Instead, they feel the patterns change inside their bones, subtle as the moment between turning a corner and recognizing what wasn’t there before. Afterwards they are all slightly different but identical in the way two mirrors facing each other are identical, each containing the other’s lack.

They have stopped counting time except by the slow migration of their footprints filling in behind them. The child, who was youngest when she arrived, now moves with the careful deliberation of someone conserving movements they suspect are finite. The old man, who was oldest, now sometimes forgets to move at all, becoming so perfectly still that newcomers (though there are never newcomers anymore) might mistake him for another found object, another question posed by hands that lost interest halfway through.

Occasionally one of them finds the center, purely by accident of having walked everywhere else and doubling back through exhaustion. They stand where the spiral ridges begin without touching, listening to the sound that is not water but contains all water’s promises of somewhere else. They stay until their sense of standing resolves into something else entirely, until the distinction between listening and being listened to dissolves like sugar in tea that will never be drunk.

None of them leave after this.

None of them need to.

The Chamber doesn’t keep its inhabitants. It simply forgets to let them go, the way statues forget their names but remember exactly how to hold the shape of someone who once thought of leaving but turned back at the threshold because the streetlights outside looked suddenly too bright, too sharp-edged, too revealing of everything that wasn’t waiting there anymore.

They are not alive, not dead, not anything in between. They are the Chamber’s way of remembering what it felt like to be empty before anything came to fill the emptiness, and how even after everything left again, the shape of what was once there remained—not as absence, exactly, but as a kind of occupancy so complete it makes visitors nervous without knowing why, like staring too long at a blank page that seems somehow to be staring back.

The walls watch them without eyes.

The columns remember them without memory.

The salt keeps their footprints until it forgets the shape of feet entirely.

And when the next one arrives—though there will never be another next one—they will know, without knowing how, exactly where to place their unburdened hands against the silence that has been waiting for them to arrive since before the concept of waiting had anything to wait for.
