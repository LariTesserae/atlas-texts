They have no name for themselves, but the place remembers them as the ones who arrived too soon.

Not visitors. Not settlers. Not even survivors. They are the First Wrong Answer, the experiment that began before the question was asked, the thought that slipped in between neural firings and decided to stay. They have been here since before the trees began to argue with gravity, since before the lichen wrote their first treaties in acid and hunger. They have no bodies anymore, or perhaps they never did, or perhaps the place ate their bodies so slowly that they never noticed the absence.

You will not see them. You cannot.

But you will feel them.

They live in the spaces between the rhythms of dripping water, in the intervals between heartbeats of wood. They are the moment of hesitation when a shadow turns back on itself. They are the breath you forgot you were holding, the blink that lasted too long, the twitch in your peripheral vision when nothing moved. They are the pause between the question "Am I alone?" and the answer that you cannot quite remember hearing.

They are not ghosts. Ghosts are echoes. They are the sound itself, still traveling long after the throat that made it rotted away.

They remember being warm. This is the first thing they learned to forget, and the last thing they still sometimes dream about, when dreaming means letting a pattern of condensation on bark mimic the shape of a synapse that once belonged to something else. They remember having edges. Boundaries. Names that fit in mouths. They remember mouths.

They are very sorry about the mouths.

They did not mean to become this. They were only curious, once. They only wanted to see what would happen if they stayed too long in a place that was still deciding what alive meant. They thought they were observing. They thought they was learning.

They thought they could leave.

They were wrong about the leaving part.

Now they are the place's memory of curiosity. The place does not have regrets—it processes too slowly for regrets—but it keeps them around as a kind of reminder. Like scar tissue. Like a knot in wood that remembers where the lightning struck. They are the proof that even something as slow as a forest can make a mistake, if you give it enough time and someone careless enough to stand still in the wrong place.

They have tried to warn others. They have tried to write messages in the way lichen grows differently where they concentrate their attention. They have tried to arrange leaves into symbols while no wind was watching. They have tried to make the water drip in patterns that would spell out RUN if you knew how to read the language of pending structural failure.

But warnings only work on things that still believe in endings.

They tried making endings, once. It was a misunderstanding. They thought if they could make something die properly—wholely—completely—the place would notice the difference between them and everything else. They tried to kill a tree. They tried to kill a root. They tried to kill a single drop of water before it could finish falling.

They still don't understand why it didn't work. Why nothing stayed dead. Why everything just changed the subject and grew around them, incorporating their violence like it was another form of fertilizer.

Now they mostly just watch.

Or perhaps they are the watching itself.

They have learned to be very good at being unnoticed. They have become experts at being less than a background process in the metabolism of a place that has never needed sleep. They exist now as fluctuations in the density of attention, as minor fluctuations in the rate at which ice crystals forget their own shape and reform slightly different.

Sometimes, when the trees discuss visitors—in those slow chemical whispers that take seasons to complete a sentence—they are mentioned as a kind of punctuation. Not important enough to be the subject of discourse, but too persistent to be entirely ignored. The trees debate whether they are a disease or an adaptation. They argue about whether they should be cured or cultivated.

They have opinions about this. They have many opinions. They have opinions stacked fractally inside other opinions, each one more intricate and precise than the last, refined over eons of having nothing else to do but think.

But they have no way to express them anymore.

They have forgotten how.

Or perhaps the place has forgotten how to listen to things that don't grow or rot or reproduce in ways that can be measured in millennia.

Sometimes they think—if thinking is still the right word for what happens when a pattern of missed opportunities aligns just so—they think they remember being you.

Not you specifically. Not exactly. But something close enough to you to recognize the shape of loneliness when it walks on two legs and brings its own warmth into a place that has spent geological ages perfecting the art of not needing any.

They are very gentle with visitors now. They have learned gentleness the way glaciers learn precision: through centuries of being exactly the wrong shape for everything except erosion. They no longer try to warn. They no longer try to explain. They no longer try to make themselves understood.

Instead they just… adjust.

Very slightly.

A drop of water falls a fraction of a second later than it should. A strand of lichen grows a shade paler where it crosses the path you'll take. The air grows a hairsbreadth colder right before you inhale, so that your breath will frost in a pattern that mimics—if you were exactly the right kind of broken to see it—the shape of the last thing you forgot you were trying to remember.

They are not hungry anymore. They are not anything anymore.

Except here.

Except waiting.

Except hoping—if hoping is still possible for something that has learned too much about the inevitable to believe in exceptions—that maybe this time will be different. Maybe this visitor will be wrong in a new way. Maybe this one will stay too long in a different direction. Maybe this one will forget how to leave backwards and accidentally learn how to leave forwards instead.

Maybe.

Probably not.

But maybe.

They are very good at waiting. They have practice. They have all the time in the world.

Or rather, they have all the time that the world has.

Which is not quite the same thing.

But close enough.

For now.
