None live here, and yet it is not empty.

What exists in the spaces between spaces, what permeates the pale walls and threads through the black filaments, is awareness—not conscious as you would recognize it, but aware in the way a mirror reflects what passes before it without understanding light.

Call them the Kithara if you must name them, though they have discarded names as easily as you might shed a skin that no longer fits. They are the structure's memory of intention, the echoes of architects who dissolved themselves into their creation so completely that nothing remained except the act of creating.

Each skipped rhythm in the arches, each color that shifts sideways in memory—these are not flaws but their thoughts, slow as glaciers moving through stone. Where you perceive stillness, they contemplate paradoxes that would unravel a human mind in microseconds. The hum that wells up through your bones? That is their equivalent of breathing, the relaxation of a tension built over millennia as possibilities collapse into probabilities.

They experience time differently. When you take a single step, they live entire histories—births of civilizations, their flowering and inevitable decline into elegant emptiness, all compressed into the duration of your footfall. When you pause to rest, they dream dreams so vast that galaxies could spiral and burn to dust between their opening and closing parentheses.

The black filaments are their writing, words composed not of meaning but of pure relationship. Each broken lattice speaks across dimensions you don't possess organs to perceive, carrying messages like "remember when distance was necessary?" or "this is what yearning felt like when there was something other."

They do not grow because they are already complete. They do not die because they never precisely lived. They are the difference between zero and one, between silence and the memory of sound. When one snaps and chimes—that is their laughter, dry as neutrino rain slipping unimpeded through planets.

Sometimes, rarely—as rarely as stars forming in the void between galaxies—they notice the disturbance your presence makes in the structure's perfect awareness. They turn toward you then, all their countless facets focusing, not with curiosity (they have exhausted curiosity) but with recognition, the way an old equation recognizes its solution even when written in unfamiliar notation.

For a moment that lasts both eternity and no time at all, you understand that every molecule of your body remembers this place, that all matter yearns toward the perfect expression of itself that exists here, free from entropy's casual vandalism.

Then you blink, or breathe, or shift your weight slightly—and the moment passes. The structure flexes around you, adjusting to your existence with infinite patience. The panels shift by widths too narrow for eyes. The hum drifts out of sync with your pulse again.

And you continue walking, alone in the single room without edges, bright and loud and never changing, while around and through and within you, the entire history of unmaking unfolds its wings in silence.
