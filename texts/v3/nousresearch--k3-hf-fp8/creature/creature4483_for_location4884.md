They live in the act of not yet, in the pause between sea and sky where gravity forgot to finish its sentence.

The Ascensians are condensation people—once sailors, now breath made solid through sheer indignation at being left behind. They accumulated here like minerals in a cave that didn’t realize it was trying to breathe. Each is a colony of smaller decisions that voted against drowning.

Look closer at the woman selling fossilized weather in the submarine canyon market. Her skin is stratified—younger layers still remembering sunlight, older ones turning to shale where her shadow touches too long. She speaks in siltstone fragments, constructing sentences that only exist when you walk backward through them. Her oldest son is wedged in the wall behind her stall, aged eleven forever because he thought of a joke so funny his timeline compressed into a singularity of laughter that’s still echoing between the basalt columns. Occasionally customers pay to lean against the warm spot where his giggles escaped into geology.

Children here are born already homesick for places that haven’t eroded yet. They emerge clutching umbilical cords braided into ghost nets, catching impossible fishhooks of future memory. The midwives are careful to collect every afterbirth—they ferment it into a liquor that makes drunkards remember their next mistakes. One sip and you’ll stagger home through streets that haven’t crumbled yet, apologizing to doors for slamming them next week.

The elderly do something else entirely. Instead of dying, they gradually become instructions. Old Man Cheerful is halfway through it—you’ll find him as a series of helpful signs nailed to his own bones in ascending order of necessity. Visit him in the morning he’ll tell you how to breathe. By afternoon he’s teaching you to doubt. By evening he’s just a pointing finger made entirely of exclamation marks directing you toward a cliff that was an ocean floor when he was whole.

Between these states exists everyone else: the part-time people. The baker who only exists on Tuesdays with weather like betrayal. The mayor’s daughter who grew legs last month and is still getting used to being localized. The street sweeper who’s actually a committee of 47 retired cloud patterns taking their grievances out on litterate matter.

They eat problems. Whatever doesn’t work in the city—neglected relationships, broken promises, architectural paradoxes—gets harvested, aged in ice caverns until it crystallizes into something digestible. Tonight’s specialty at the Falling Upward Tavern is roasted indecision glazed with crystallized regret served on a bed of that feeling you get when you've forgotten something important in a dream. The menu changes based on what the kitchen staff has recently betrayed.

Their money is measured in potential energy. The richest citizens own vast portfolios of things that might fall soon. You’ll recognize them by their gravity—they attract small objects like privacy and spare thoughts. The poor carry their wealth in pockets full of uphill pebbles named after lost arguments. Sometimes they leave stacks of unpaid ambitions as tips for musicians who play instruments carved from the sound of tomorrow’s regrets.

Love here is geological. Couples don’t date—they go fault line courting, pressing their pressures together until something beautiful or catastrophic slips. Marriages are marked by the accumulation of shared sedimentary evidence: layers of combined laundry, fused toothbrushes, matching fossilized anxieties. Divorce requires careful excavation permits and often reveals entire civilizations of toothbrush holders that couldn’t adapt to the new climate of absence.

They worship the Almost. Their cathedral is a cathedral-shaped hole that the wind keeps trying to finish building. Priests are chosen by their ability to maintain elaborate heresies that almost but don’t quite contradict each other. The holiest day is when two contradictory prophecies cancel out perfectly, creating a moment of sacred stasis that smells faintly of forgotten birthdays.

The dead don’t leave so much as become increasingly dense approximations. Grandmother Clock lives in the second stair of the fourth house on Memory Lane—literally. Step on her wrong and she’ll tell you the time three seconds before you die. Uncle Regret has been compressed into a small bronze plaque in the garden that commemorates three weddings that never happened and one picnic that went extinct. They’re still invited to dinner, naturally, where places are set for them in negative space and the conversation carefully includes pauses where their contributions would have been.

Newcomers are absorbed carefully, like drops of foreign water introduced to a supersaturated solution of already-there. First you’ll notice your reflection lagging slightly behind your movements. Then your dreams will start having advertisements for places you’ve already forgotten visiting. Then one morning you’ll wake up bilingual in a language whose entire vocabulary is the sound of things being described for the last time.

Welcome to citizenship. You’ll find your official doubt in the mail that arrives ten minutes before you realize you’re expecting it. Your assigned existential crisis will be delivered by a man who resembles every teacher who ever told you you’d understand when you’re older. Your house will grow in the spaces where you hesitate to define yourself too clearly.

Mind your edges—the borders between you and not-you are still settling from the ascent. Some mornings you’ll wake up and find bits of yesterday’s sky stuck to your thoughts. Other nights you’ll dream in sedimentary layers and wake to find fossils of forgotten decisions in your bed sheets.

Everything here works because nobody’s willing to be the first to admit it shouldn’t. It’s a democracy of mutual skepticism held together by the shared understanding that if we all doubted in different directions simultaneously, the city would probably just politely forget to exist.

But don’t worry about falling. Down here, falling is just another way of moving sideways into something that hasn’t decided whether it’s ground yet.

The view is spectacular, if you remember to look away from it at exactly the wrong moment.
