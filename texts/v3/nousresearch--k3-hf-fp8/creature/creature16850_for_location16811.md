They are called the Imminent, and no one knows when they began, because the moment of beginning is always deferred here. Perhaps yesterday. Perhaps tomorrow morning when the eastward cloud strikes a fresh note in the ringing stone and births a child who remembers too much of the cliff's dreams.

They do not walk, exactly—they negotiate. Every step is a treaty with gravity, signed in the tense of "yet to be agreed." Their bones are hollow reeds filled with tomorrow's air, their muscles braided currents of intention only slightly ahead of what their bodies have achieved. Watch closely: you'll see their feet sometimes touch ground two inches below where ground currently exists, resting on stone still being quarried in the future's patience.

The Imminent are born when an idea about a person becomes too specific to remain abstract. A carver dreams too precisely of her apprentice's eventual hands—those fingers that will never quite follow instructions but will discover the grain's secret humor—and wakes to find those hands already shaping cloudbread in her kitchen, attached to a youth who remembers being born last spring when the moon struck the cliff at a particularly suggestive angle. The youth will grow younger until their features blur back into possibility, or older until they calcify into useful stone. They parent their own grandparents across these loops; inheritance flows both uphill and down, like the time-rich water here.

Their language is carved in the negative space between words. To say "I love you" properly, two Imminent must sit facing opposite hours and chip away at a single granite breath until only the shape of what wasn't said remains—a hollow so precise that wind through it produces the exact conversation they feared having. These conversation-sculptures litter the city like unfinished thoughts, each doorway framed by one, each bed carved beneath another. Lovers sleep inside their misunderstandings here, and are gently polished by years of never-quite-resolving wind.

They eat three meals daily: what memory will taste like when it returns, what regret digests into in good company, and plain stone soup flavored with herbs that have not yet evolved. Their teeth are slightly ahead of their mouths—whiter than possible, already preparing for foods that will require different kinds of puncture. When they drink, you can see the water's future shapes inside their transparent throats: tomorrow's tears, next century's rain, the precise tear-drop that will one day wear this very cup smooth. They are always slightly thirsty for what they are becoming.

Their clothes exist in drafts. A typical sleeve might be silk here, wind there, unfinished argument around the wrist. Each citizen maintains a personal tailor who is actually a committee of cloud formations—they drape themselves in mist while sleeping, and whatever patterns their dreams settle into become tomorrow's garments. The most fashionable wear clouds that remember being oceans, heavy with unspilled storms. The poor wear single white hopes, carefully nothings that barely cover their shame but promise excellent embroidery later.

Work is their religion and their romance. Each Imminent is responsible for carving the space where their next self will stand. The finest artisans can create such eloquent hollows that when they step away, the absence continues their gestures for hours—a perfect performance of hands that have moved on to better potential. The greatest sculptor of the last century (by which they mean the century beginning next Thursday) spent her life carving the exact hole where her heart would want to rest when it finally gave up climbing. When she finished—the hollow was said to sing mathematics in the key of almost—she stepped into it and vanished backward through her own future. On the anniversary of her completion (which hasn't occurred yet but is already a national holiday), they leave empty chisels at the site and listen for them to begin working in reverse.

They do not sleep, but they practice for it intensely. Each night, entire districts lie down in carefully calculated positions, arranging their limbs to form sentences in the language of stone dreams. If carved correctly, their collective insomnia becomes tomorrow's geology—a vein of particularly thoughtful marble, or a seam of quartz that remembers the taste of moonlight in seven thousand years when the first archaeologists arrive to misinterpret everything.

Their government is a board of directors who haven't been elected yet and probably won't accept the positions. Laws are passed in the conditional perfect subjunctive—they describe precisely how things would have been handled optimally in situations that could have happened differently. The most serious crime is premature certainty, punishable by enforced completion. Solitary confinement means being locked in a room where everything is already finished, including your sentence.

Children here age sideways. A newborn might speak in elegant regrets while their body remains soft as possibility, while their grandparents play tag in the apprentice canyon, getting younger and faster until they blur into the city's next crop of beginners. Education consists of unlearning whatever yesterday's tomorrow taught too firmly. The university's motto, carved eternally unfinished above its largest absence, reads: "We promise to leave you precisely as ignorant as genius requires."

They die into beginnings. When an Imminent senses their ideas about themselves becoming too heavy to carry up tomorrow's cliff, they announce a "final project"—a carving so ambitious it can only be completed by their absence. The sculptor who took thirty years (next week) to carve the wind's autobiography finished it by walking into her last unfinished sentence and becoming the space where winds pause to remember themselves. The composer whose final symphony consisted entirely of rests between notes worked backward through the city's gutters until his heartbeat became the tempo all future rainfall follows.

They have no word for goodbye, only "until the next beginning of now." They greet each other by carefully not finishing sentences, trusting that the interruption will age into proper meaning eventually. Their most tender gesture is to lean close enough for their shadows to overlap slightly, neither more nor less than tomorrow's intimacy requires.

Watch now as the evening carvers begin their shift. See how they don't touch the stone—how they work instead at convincing the moonlight that it wants to fall slightly differently, revealing forms that were always there but needed permission to be seen. This is their greatest art: not making, but allowing. Not building, but remembering what the future has already forgiven them for.

Tomorrow morning (if mornings come here), when you wake to find your hotel room has moved three degrees left into a better view of yesterday's ocean, and your mirror shows you wearing the face you might grow into if you stay—do not be alarmed. This is simply the Imminent being polite. They are carving the space where your next decision will want to stand.

They haven't decided yet if you'll stay long enough to become part of the city. But they are already being very careful not to finish making up their minds about you.
