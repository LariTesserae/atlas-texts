At first, you think they're not there. Your eyes slide over them, the way light skims the surface of deep water, refusing to settle on what lies beneath. But they are there—have always been there—longer than the stone itself, which is only a geological afterthought, a temporary arrangement of what they once dreamed into solidity.

They are the Undwellers.

They do not live here because no one lives here. Living implies movement away from, growth toward. The Undwellers merely remain. They are the remainder when all else has been subtracted. They are the echo of the first silence that existed before sound invented itself.

Describe them? Impossible. But perhaps the edges: when you glimpse them sideways, they appear as tall as mountains and as thin as regret. Their bodies are composed of the space between things—the pause between heartbeats, the breath held before speaking a truth that will destroy everything. They are the exact shape of your absence in every room you have ever left. Their skin (if it is skin) reflects nothing but what you most desperately want to forget you have seen. Some pilgrims have reported wings composed entirely of falling shadows. Others swear they have no bodies at all, only voices that resonate directly in the calcium of your bones.

They communicate through the gradient of light across a single stone over centuries. Through the precise angle at which a dying tree finally leurs toward oblivion. Through the accumulated weight of unsaid words in places where people once promised to return.

Their eyes are everywhere and nowhere. They see through time backwards and forwards simultaneously—the exact moment your atoms will disperse and rejoin the stars, and every moment you existed before you were born. They see the shapes of thoughts as they leave your mind and dissipate into air. They see the colors of emotions that human languages never named.

The Undwellers do not eat, as such. They absorb the essence of things that have been lost but never mourned—single socks vanished from dryers, childhood toys given away without ceremony, last words swallowed instead of spoken. They grow stronger with every goodbye that was not properly said. They feast on the taste of almost.

They do not sleep because they are perpetual wakefulness itself—the consciousness that existed before sleep was invented as a mercy. Instead, they dream continuously, and their dreams leak into reality, creating the subtle wrongnesses that make travelers turn back without knowing why: the tree that casts no shadow despite full sun, the bird that sings a song you heard once in a fever as a child, the mirror that shows you standing beside yourself but older and smiling with teeth you haven't lost yet.

The Undwalkers (for sometimes, but only when absolutely necessary, they walk) leave no footprints because they are composed of the space feet occupy before touching ground. Where they pass, gravity forgets itself briefly—stones float inches above earth until memory returns, water flows upward in brief defiance of physics, clocks pause for exactly seven beats of a hummingbird's heart before continuing as if nothing happened.

Their language has no words, only the precise frequency of silence that exists between different types of loneliness. To hear it is to never feel alone again, each seed of thought sprouting into forests of memory that aren't yours but feel more familiar than your own childhood bedroom. Each memory contains seeds of others' forgotten dreams, nesting like Russian dolls infinitely inward.

The Undwalkers do not age because they are age itself—the accumulation of all moments that have ever been and will ever be. Time pools around them like heavy silk, sometimes slowing to amber viscosity where a single breath takes lifetimes to complete, sometimes accelerating until entire civilizations rise and fall between your heartbeat and the next.

They have no leaders because they are perfectly unified in their solitude. They have no names because naming implies difference, and they are identical in their absolute uniqueness. They have no purpose beyond being exactly what they are, which is everything else when no one is looking.

Sometimes, but rarely, they take forms that can be perceived, but only by those who have lost something truly irreplaceable without realizing it was gone. To one they might appear as the exact shade of your mother's perfume on the last day she held you before forgetting who you were. To another, as the weight of your father watching you sleep from the doorway when you pretended to be asleep but knew he was there. Always, they appear as what you cannot bear to remember yet cannot afford to forget.

They dwell in the negative spaces—the shadow cast by the mountain of absence, the hollow inside the hollow cry, the blank pages between chapters in books that burned before they could be written. Their homes are constructed of architectural impossibilities: rooms that exist only when unobserved, corridors that shorten when walked backward, windows that look out onto the exact moment of your conception.

The Undwellers do not welcome visitors because welcome implies outsiderness, and there are no outsiders in the place where all boundaries dissolve into each other like watercolors left in rain. They do not reject because rejection requires something to be cast away, and nothing is ever truly lost here, only transformed into more itself.

If you find yourself among them—really among them, not just passing through the edges of their awareness like most do without realizing—there are guidelines that cannot be called rules because rules imply consequences beyond the natural unfolding of what already is:

Do not speak unless your silence has become unbearably heavy with unsaid truths.

Do not look directly at them unless you are prepared to see yourself reflected in infinite regression, each reflection slightly more honest than the last until the final one contains nothing but the exact shape of your original lie.

Do not touch them unless you are willing to trade your skin for theirs, which feels like the moment before tears fall and tastes like remembering words from a language your ancestors forgot but your DNA still remembers dreaming in.

Do not stay unless you have already left everywhere else so completely that only your absence remains to say goodbye.

They will not harm you because harm requires difference, and difference is the first illusion that dissolves here. But they will change you—not into something else, but into more deeply, unbearably yourself until the you you thought you were becomes as thin and transparent as the boundary between their breath and yours.

If you must leave (and some, though few, do leave, or rather are expelled like splinters when the body has finished healing around them), you will find that ordinary places now echo with their absence. Rooms will feel too full of empty space. People will speak in voices that sound like translations from a language that had no need for words. Time will pass differently—sometimes too slow, sometimes too fast, but never quite right again.

You will carry them with you always, not as memory but as gravity—the subtle pull toward edges and abysses, the impulse to shine light into corners that should stay dark, the inability to tolerate surface-level conversations because you now hear the underneath of words.

And sometimes, when you're very still and very alone, you might hear it: not them, exactly, but the space they occupied, humming the exact frequency of the first silence that existed before sound.

And you will know that they are still there.

They have always been there.

Waiting for you to notice that you never really left.

That you were never really welcomed because you were always already home.

That the place where no place should exists exists precisely where you are not looking.

That you are becoming one of them, not gradually, because they exist outside of time, but suddenly, in the way that night falls no: there is only light, and then there is only lightlessness, and the moment between them is not transition but revelation.

That you were never describing them at all. You were describing the shape of your own approaching absence.

And it is beautiful.

And it is terrible.

And it is here. It has always been here.

Waiting for you to notice that you have already arrived.
