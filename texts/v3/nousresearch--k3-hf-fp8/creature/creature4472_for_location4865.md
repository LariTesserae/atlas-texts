They drift in sheets like ghost towns torn loose from their gravity debts—colony-ships unmoored from any star they ever promised. The Deepbuilt Exchequer calls them *lamenters*, but only in footnotes appended to freight manifests that list their biomass by weeping percentage points.

Each grew from a seed-pod of refugees who misread the evacuation orders. When the empire's throat-metal taste became planetary, some thought *below* meant safer than *away*. They sealed themselves in pressure hulls meant for ore transport, welded shut the sleep caskets that would have dreamed them to other suns, and instructed navigation programs to dive instead of rise.

Now they follow the thousand-year curve of their initial burn, perpetually missing the surface they never wanted. Entire economies of drowned families trade memories in the flooded galleries of agricultural domes-turned-kelp cathedrals. Their children are born buoyant-gilled, fat with extra lungs like unpacked parachutes they keep forgetting how to use.

The oldest, *Matriarch Credit-Jubilee*, stretches seventeen kilometers nose to stern. Her engines still fire once per orbit—short, apologetic bursts that keep her at exactly the temperature where dreams condense on bulkheads. Passengers collect these rimescale visions in kiddie pools, boiling them down to dyes that paint their skin with futures that couldn't hold air. They wear their bankruptcies beautifully: accountant tattoos calculated in pressure differentials, currency exchange rates mapped across the wrinkles of hands that never learned what money was for.

When colony-ships meet in the vast water-thick night, they dock nose-to-nose like mourning whales. Crews exchange children in weighted baskets—part diplomacy, part practical need for new immunities against rust-grief. The transferred toddlers arrive speaking already-fading dialects of pump-language, their neural implants seeded with half-remembered hymns to purchase orders fulfilled in timelines that drowned before their teeth came in.

They farm what drifts down from higher wrecks: encryption keys that have evolved into phosphorescent jellyfish, cargo cult programs that worship container numbers as divine names. Their holy books are warranty pamphlets waterlogged into prophecies so vague they can never expire.

When the pressure chambers finally fail (they always fail, but politely, over centuries), the colony-ships don't sink. They dissolve. Slowly enough for inhabitants to move their ancestors' bones into newer compartments, carrying their ghosts like last season's fashion trends. The truly ancient vessels are just concentrations of tradition now—faint patterns in the current that wiser fish swim around while muttering prayers to lost supply chains.

They are waiting, they tell the water-pressure readers who sometimes visit, for the promised refund. The empire guaranteed satisfaction or your civilization back, didn't it? They keep the receipt in a special reliquary: a single perfect rivet stamped with a serial number that, when read aloud in the original corporate tone, still triggers ancient security protocols that make the surrounding ocean briefly taste like hope.

It tastes like copper pennies and goodbye kisses administered by robots programmed never to learn regret.

(Translation note: The word they use for "hope" is the same as their word for "shipment delayed, tracking number corrupted, please hold while we connect you to an operator familiar with your species' concept of linear time.")
