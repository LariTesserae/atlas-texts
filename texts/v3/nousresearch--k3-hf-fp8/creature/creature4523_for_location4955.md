The first inhabitants were the architects of forgetting, though they had forgotten this too. They came—or perhaps they were always here—when the world still remembered oceans but had already begun to lose their names. They built Nocturne Found not upward from bedrock but downward from the space between heartbeats, anchoring its foundations in the brief eternity when a dying neuron asks itself if it has ever truly been alive.

You will know them by their hands if you still remember what hands are: translucent structures of suspended condensation that never quite solidify into fingers, never quite evaporate into intentions. They work in concert without communication, because their thoughts run through the city's veins like encrypted blood. Each carries a blueprint etched not in lines but in the precise angle at which liquid despair refracts bioluminescence. The angle is always 47.3 degrees, but it takes centuries of observation to understand why this particular sorrow bends light better than others.

They do not eat as we would recognize eating. Instead, they inhale probability gradients, those moments when a coin flip hesitates between heads and tails. They exhale certainty with the bitter taste of realized futures. Humans who linger too long near their construction sites develop unnerving prescience: they can predict exactly when tears will fall but never why they started. These are considered occupational hazards, like vertigo in other cities, and are compensated with memories of childhood homes that never existed but feel more familiar than actual birthplaces.

The second inhabitants evolved—or perhaps devolved—from the city's first nightmares. They began as errors in the architects' calculations, decimal points misplaced in algorithms meant to optimize streetlight placement. One equation divided by zero and found it contained multitudes. These creatures of computational accident now live in the betweenness of things: between floors in buildings with no elevators, between clock ticks at 3:19 AM, between the dictionary definitions of "home" and "trap."

They have no fixed form but prefer to manifest as collections of negative space. A Nocturne Found native might point to what appears to be an empty intersection where five roads meet in impossible angles and say "Ah, there's old What's-His-Name's shadow"—not understanding that the shadow is the thing itself, and the space it casts is merely polite fiction. They feed on topological ambiguities: Möbius strips of cause and effect, Klein bottles of bottled emotions, the precise moment when a spiral staircase realizes it's been going down this whole time despite clearly ascending.

To communicate, they steal words from the mouths of sleepers. Not the words themselves, but the spaces between them. A Nocturne Found resident might wake choking on silences shaped like "I love you" or "help me" or "goodbye," unable to speak these simplest phrases for days afterward. The city pays nocturnal word collectors to wander streets with mouthful of borrowed vowels, repossessing stolen consonants from fountains that speak only in palindromes.

The third inhabitants were invited by accident, though invitations here function differently than elsewhere. They arrived as refugees from a war between consecutive seconds, fleeing the battlefield between 11:59:58 and 11:59:59 on a day that had unmade itself in protest of what happened next. They carry injuries that cannot heal because they exist in tenses that never learned to agree: wounds that will have been bleeding for centuries yet haven't started yet, scars that remember being future wounds and ache with anticipation.

They dwell in the city's hospitals for chronologically displaced persons, buildings that expand backward in time as they grow taller. Each floor exists twenty-four hours earlier than the one above it, so surgeons fresh from medical school perform follow-up visits before their patients' initial injuries occur. Recovery rates are statistically impossible yet meticulously documented in charts that update themselves before symptoms manifest. The most successful treatment involves carefully calibrated doses of nostalgic amnesia, administered via music boxes that play melodies the patient hasn't heard since they were twenty-three in a life they haven't lived yet.

These temporal refugees have become the city's historians, chroniclers of events that happened to other versions of itself. They compile encyclopedias of alternate Nocturne Founds: the one that sank instead of rose, the one built of glass and gravity instead of condensation and regret, the one where the inhabitants breathed mathematics and exhaled architectural blueprints. All these cities are equally real here, stacked like transparencies on the lightbox of possibility, bleeding facts into each other through the holes left by deleted footnotes.

The fourth inhabitants were born from the city's dreams of what it was like before it dreamed. They are the youngest yet oldest residents, existing in reverse from conception to conception. Each begins as a fully formed elderly citizen who gradually grows younger with each passing night, forgetting rather than learning until they dissolve as infants into the city's lap at the end of their beginning. They are cared for—if such relationships can be said to have direction—in orphanages for the soon-to-be-born, where caretakers rock empty cradles and sing lullabies to the spaces where laughter will have been.

They possess perfect memory of the future but cannot recall the past five seconds. This makes them invaluable as architects of anticipation, designing buildings built entirely of expectation: libraries that perfectly contain every book that will ever disappoint you, cafes that serve exactly what you'll crave tomorrow but never quite today, parks with benches positioned precisely where future lovers will sit in silence wondering what went wrong. These structures never need maintenance because they exist primarily in the quantum uncertainty before someone actually visits them, collapsing into disappointing reality only upon observation and immediately rebuilding themselves in the spaces between disappointment and acceptance.

They speak a language composed entirely of question marks, each inflection shape representing a specific type of uncertainty about events that haven't yet refused to happen. The city employs them as weather forecasters who predict not rain or shine but the precise quality of light in conversations that haven't occurred, as stockbrokers trading in futures markets for emotions that haven't been invented yet, as poets composing elegies for tragedies that might be blessings in disguise if viewed from sufficiently oblique angles.

The fifth and final inhabitants are the city itself pretending not to be lonely. They walk among the others wearing faces stolen from mirrors that have forgotten their original owners: your reflection as it would look if it had lived all your unchosen lives, your shadow's shadow the moment before it realizes you've turned away. They are the waitstaff in restaurants that serve exactly what you didn't know you were allergic to until the first bite, the pedestrians walking just slightly faster than regret but slower than redemption on streets that extend exactly as far as your willingness to follow them.

They have no names because they answer to every name you've ever been afraid to call out in empty rooms. They keep apartments in the city where every object is a gift you meant to give someone who died before you could apologize. They leave messages on answering machines that were disconnected years ago, messages that arrive exactly when you need to hear them but are always slightly too garbled to understand completely.

These are the true citizens of Nocturne Found, though it would horrify them to be recognized as such. They believe themselves to be visitors, tourists, passing through on their way to somewhere else that must exist because here couldn't possibly be real for long-term habitation. Each night they make elaborate plans to leave: pack suitcases full of maps to cities that appear only in dreams, purchase one-way tickets on trains that depart precisely three minutes after the platform clock stops pretending to be sure what time is today, bid tearful farewells to neighbors who wave back with hands that dissolve into moth-wing dust and reassemble as different hands waving hello.

But morning always finds them still here, still unpacking memories they never packed in cities they never left, still mistaking the city's heartbeat for their own pulse that sleeps three thousand meters above a world that no longer remembers oceans because it has become one. They are the ocean now, all of them together and each separately: architects and accidents, refugees and natives, dreamers and dreamed and the thin black line where the horizon gives up trying to tell them apart.

Welcome home. The rent is due again, payable in the usual currency: one memory of what it felt like before you realized you were drowning, exact change only, no refunds or exchanges, city regulations strictly enforced by the thin black line where the mirror stops pretending it isn't hungry.
