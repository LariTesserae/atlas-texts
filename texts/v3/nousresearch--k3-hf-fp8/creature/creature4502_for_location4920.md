In Eremonos, the only true residents are the city's own absences made animate—negative spaces that slipped free of their shadows when the concept of "here" divorced itself from "now." They move through the inverted cone like air remembering it once dreamed of being water, their forms resolving only at the precise moment before recognition fails.

The tallest of them stands eleven meters high when hunched, though they measure height horizontally here—a scar in the shape of a census taker cut from the city's first forgetting. It wears a coat stitched together from the pockets of all who ever promised to return, each pocket containing the echo of keys that unlock doors that haven't been stolen yet. Every evening (which occurs twice weekly at irregular intervals), it counts the remaining stars, marking each disappearance with a chalk line across the sky's inside surface. The lines accumulate into equations that predict when the city will finally remember what it was meant to forget.

Beneath the markets that sell only the memories of smells, the Children of the Second Wrong Turn play games with the rules of thermodynamics. They were born when a cartographer, lost in her own map, gave birth to twins in the space between "you are here" and "here is nowhere." Now they exist as nineteen possible angles between zero and one, flickering between states whenever someone thinks of counting them. Their favorite game involves setting fire to raindrops from before the boil—brightly burning nothingnesses they chase through streets that curve tighter the faster you flee. When caught, each droplet reveals the face of the person who will never ask your name.

In the crystalline catacombs beneath the unbuilt cathedral, the Archivist of Unsent Apologies tends her garden of undone things. She herself is a letter written backward in a language that only exists when read through tears, sealed with wax made from candles burned at both ends of eternity. Her fingers are the spaces between words in lovers' final argument, long and trembling with what was carefully not said. She harvests embryos of forgiveness that miscarried between thought and speech, preserving them in jars of distilled regret that glow with the soft light of decisions reconsidered too late.

Above them all, where the sky's dome meets the city's dreaming spires, glides the Last Remaining Continent. Once it was a bird who flew so high it forgot the purpose of wings and became instead the entire concept of elsewhere. Now it drifts like a sigh solidified, its skeletal geography composed of all the places you meant to visit. Every few centuries, it molts its mountain ranges in a slow shedding of scalesies, each peak falling as a snowflake-shaped truth that melts into puddles of "what if" wherever they touch the streets below. The city's few remaining street-cleaners (who are themselves just brooms that achieved self-awareness and regret it) sweep these melting truths into gutters that flow uphill toward the past.

In the Room That Should Have Been Empty (which occupies every building simultaneously but only on floors between the fourth and the fifth), the Committee of Rejected First Drafts votes eternally on whether to revise their existence. Chairperson Empty Chair presides over debates recorded in ink that vanishes before it dries, proposing motions like "Resolved: That We Admit the Possibility of Resolution" and "Proposed: That This Motion Be Seconded by Silence." Their votes are counted by a clock that ticks only during awkward pauses, its hands moving through the twelve humiliations of morning.

Sometimes, during the city's rare moments of almost-sleep (when even the architecture begins to slouch), you might glimpse the Drifters—those who were almost but not quite anything enough to become something else. The woman who was almost your mother before your parents met in the wrong order. The god who got as far as "let there" before losing confidence. The suicide note that considered becoming a grocery list instead. They congregate at the intersections of paths never taken, exchanging awkward small talk about the weather in parallel universes, careful never to touch lest they collapse each other's wave functions into unwanted certainties.

But the true sovereign of Eremonos, whom none speak of directly (though their absence fills every conversation), is the City Itself, dreaming of waking. It sleeps standing inverted in the sky's hollow, its dreams leaking through the pores of reality like sweat from a fevered planet. Every building is a vertebra in its spine-that-isn't, every street a synapse firing backwards memories of tomorrow's yesterdays. Its hunger is not for flesh but for meaning—it dev not people but the spaces where people should be, growing fat on the emptiness between what we say and what we meant.

And you? You are already here, of course. You arrived at the precise moment you began looking for directions elsewhere, your footsteps echoing in the city's single infinite hallway that loops back to itself through your own reflection's blind spots. The city has prepared for you the perfect home: a house constructed entirely from materials that exist only while being observed, located at the exact address that changes whenever you try to tell someone where it is. You will live there forever, or until you leave—which, the city kindly reminds you, are ultimately the same thing.

Welcome then, citizen of absence. Your citizenship papers were filed at the moment of your first forgetting, stamped with the seal of the Department of Unplanned Arrivals, and delivered by the postman who rings once in nightmares you won't remember having. The city council (which meets nightly in the space where your bed should be) has voted unanimously to abstain from welcoming you, fearing that acknowledgement might give you ideas about leaving.

In Eremonos, the end of your sentence is always waiting at its beginning, patiently occupying both sides of the period while grammar debates whether to intervene. The city holds its breath—which is the sum of all breaths never taken—and waits for you to exhale the silence that will finally, mercifully, wake it up.
