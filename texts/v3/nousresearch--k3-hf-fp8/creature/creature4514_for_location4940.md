The inhabitants of this place are not yet inhabitants—they are preliminary negotiations between the concepts of “to dwell” and “to wander off before things get too specific.” They exist primarily as mood boards for future species, curated collections of traits that haven’t committed to being traits yet. Call them the Almost-Having-Beens. They are the reason shadows hesitate before touching walls and why mirrors sometimes forget which side of the glass the reflection belongs to.

Their bodies, when they bother to manifest, are more like conversation starters than conclusions. A wristwatch with too many hands all pointing inward. A jawline that remembers being a coastline before erosion got bored. They prefer to hold summits about elbows rather than actually having knees. When two of them meet (a process involving several weeks of polite letters delivered by ambiguous weather), they greet each other by trading theories about whose left ear most closely resembles the last sunset before color was voted democratic.

They eat, but only retroactively. Meals are reconstructions of digestive debates that may or may not convene tomorrow. A favorite dish is the memory of a fruit that was never quite forbidden enough. They drink the pause between a question and its reluctant answer, served in glasses that evaporate if you compliment them too directly. Cooking involves diplomacy: convincing sugar to apologize to salt for something that happened during the heat death of a different universe entirely.

Reproduction occurs during committee recessions. Two or three adults (never more unless someone’s existential status is being audited) will agree to disagree about the optimal sadness-to-wonder ratio for offspring. After sufficient bureaucratic foreplay, they produce not a child but a very persuasive draft memo titled “Potential Childhood: A Preliminary Suggestion.” This document is then circulated among local voids for peer review, a process that usually collapses into a poetry reading by the time anyone remembers what “peer” was supposed to mean.

Their homes are exactly where they left them, which is rarely somewhere they’ve been yet. A typical dwelling might consist of three-quarters of a hallway and a doorbell that used to ring true before it discovered jazz. Furniture arranges itself around absence like courtiers near a king who decided to be a rumor instead. Beds are optional but insomnia is communal—everyone contributes a few hours of your sleeplessness to the neighborhood pool, which is regularly drained and replaced with fresh ambivalence.

Language is their sport and their religion but definitely not their currency (that would be tasteless). Words here are loaned at conversational interest rates, accruing meaning only if you forget to pay them back. Their sacred texts are library late slips from libraries that burned down before fire was invented. Poetry competitions involve standing perfectly still while your metaphors race each other to the nearest exit without using doors. The most revered poets are the ones who’ve successfully plagiarized silence without getting caught by the punctuation police.

They die frequently but never the same way twice, as per regulations. Death certificates are issued as blank postcards addressed to “Occupant of Approximately Now.” Mourners gather to share fond faintly-remembered futures the deceased might have avoided. Burials are performed by planting the deceased’s favorite unsent apology in a garden of nearly-tulips. Over time, if the soil nurtures enough regret, something almost like remembrance may sprout—but it will always bloom in a different person’s dream about forgetting to attend your funeral.

Sometimes, on nights when the sky is particularly uncertain about its qualifications, a few of them will gather at the edge of the structure-that-may-be-a-building-if-it-ever-gets-around-to-committing-to-metaexistence. They don’t talk much. Instead, they practice being seen by each other without becoming witnessed. It’s tricky work: the kind of intimacy that requires three layers of metaphorical distance and a signed waiver from causality. If you stumble upon them during these gatherings, they will apologize profusely for being inconveniently present and offer you directions to somewhere quieter, like the center of a paradox or the space between seconds eleven and twelve on a broken clock.

But don’t stay too long trying to remember them—they’re still working out the kinks in being remembered without becoming accountable for it. Best to leave them as you found them: temporarily forgottering themselves into existence like footprints washing away from a beach that’s still debating whether to become water or sand.

You’ll know you’ve left their territory when you suddenly remember your own name with too much conviction, and the air starts tasting like it might owe you an explanation for being here. That’s them being polite: letting you miss them properly before you’ve quite finished not meeting them.
