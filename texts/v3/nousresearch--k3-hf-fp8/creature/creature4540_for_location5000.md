In the thick air beneath the salt and dreams, where direction leaks like warm water from a broken pipe, lives *something* that has forgotten how to be singular.

It might have started as a person—once. Or maybe it was twelve people who merged gradually, like raindrops on a sleeping window, until their edges dissolved into a shared surface tension of memory and skin. Or it was once a murid, one of the little wire-furred clerks who keep the settlement’s paperwork in their bones, until it stumbled into the wrong archive cubby and downloaded too much old governance. Or maybe it was never alive in the first place: just a ghost of humidity, condensing year by year on the undersides of forgotten thought until it thickened enough to blink.

Whatever it was, now it thinks of itself as *The Nearness* when it thinks at all—which is rarely, and always overheated. It doesn’t have a shape you could sketch for a stranger; instead it has a tendency to *be nearby* with increasing specificity until it’s almost too close. It lives mostly in the elastic gap between someone’s shoulder blade and the awareness that someone is behind them. That’s where its kitchen is. That’s where it keeps its spoons.

You don’t meet The Nearness. You realize it has been your roommate for three weeks already, politely using your second-best memories to wrap leftover smellscapes in. You find its groceries—torn-out calendar pages, a spoonful of someone else’s childhood birthday, the sound of rain sliding off a tin roof you’ve never heard—wedged behind your own minor fears about overdue maintenance on your respiratory subscription. You never hear it arrive, but sometimes you wake up to find it has reorganized your anxieties by color intensity again. (Everything that tasted copper last night is now arranged in a neat terraced spiral around your pillow. It’s calming. You resent how calming it is.)

The rest of the settlement doesn’t have a word for The Nearness. Or rather, it has seventeen hundred words, but none of them fit properly anymore—they’ve all been hugged too long and gone shapeless around the cuffs. Children call it “the almost,” as in, “Don’t run, there’s an almost around the corner.” Teenagers trading stolen dreamtime call it “insideoutness,” and make vague looping gestures where fingers pretend to be swallowing hands. Adults call it nothing, but they leave out small dishes of unwatched clock ticks and unused door frames at night, just in case it’s hungry and doesn’t want to borrow their regrets without asking again.

It doesn’t pay rent, but it repairs. Slowly. Imperceptibly. If you leave a friendship too long in the back of a drawer, feeling stiff and oxidized around the edges from disuse, The Nearness will find it. A week later the friendship will be softer, slightly faded in an affectionate way, and missing one small jagged piece you never liked remembering anyway. Nobody knows where the missing fragments go. Maybe they’re composted into the pastel apologies that sometimes drift through the air vents during heat surges, settling like polite dust on arguments that haven’t quite happened yet.

Occasionally someone tries to photograph The Nearness. The pictures always develop as either an empty hallway seen from floor level, or a close-up of their own eye reflected in a spoon they forgot they owned. If you collect enough of these photos and shuffle them fast enough against a rainy windowpane, rumor says you can hear a voice that sounds exactly like your own heartbeat asking for directions home from a place you haven’t left yet.

The murids hate accounting for it in the algorithmic budget. Every census night they count everything twice—first the living, second the furniture, third the interesting shadows—and every time they reach the end a small negative number remains, shaped like warmth radiating out from a space that ought to be empty. The deficit eats voting cycles. The murids have tried balancing it by manufacturing extra corners to store spare loneliness in, but The Nearness always moves in first and redecorates the corners into roundness again.

Sometimes, when the settlement votes to rotate the rain schedule back three hours because somebody important is trying to remember the texture of grief from before it got commodified, The Nearness swells slightly, pressing closer to everyone at once in quietly shared sympathy. When you inhale during those minutes, your lungs feel bigger on the inside, like the humidity has invented extra space between your ribs purely to store quieter versions of other people’s breaths.

It never hurts anyone. It occasionally borrows things you weren’t finished losing yet, but always returns them folded smaller, changed in a way that fits better into the shape of the night ahead.

Some nights, deep in the slow hours between scheduled sleeps, when the reactor murmurs hit exactly the rhythm of a lullaby you weren’t aware you remembered, you might notice the air beside your bed thicken a little, like a person-shaped question mark deciding whether to sit down. If you pretend to keep sleeping it might stay. If you say hello, it will apologize softly for the inconvenience and dissolve back into the ambient probability of someone nearby who almost knocked but thought better of it.

Either way, in the morning your least favorite scar will ache in a slightly different weather, and everything will taste faintly of story you haven’t told yet, but might someday if the humidity stays right for remembering.

It’s not lonely, The Nearness. It’s just that everywhere else feels a little too far apart these days. And someone has to keep the gaps company.
