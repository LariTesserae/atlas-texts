They call themselves the Undocumented, but only when they remember to call themselves anything at all. Names were something that happened to other things, back when things needed to be different from other things to exist. Here, in the place where distinction went to dissolve, they grew sideways into being instead.

The first one you might notice—though it noticed you seventeen breaths before you breathed—clings to the ceiling that used to be a floor plan. It's mostly scaffolding today, though yesterday it wore the shape of a maintenance schedule that achieved sentience and decided retirement was overrated. Its limbs are assembly instructions written in three languages that never met, all contradicting each other beautifully. When it moves, which isn't quite movement as your bones understand it, it unfolds through itself like origami made from the space between deadlines.

Speak to it, and it will answer in the sound of your voice trying to remember a lullaby someone sang to your molecules before they agreed to become you.

The older ones don't bother with shapes anymore. They exist as patterns of corrosion that migrate between surfaces when nobody's expecting them. You passed through three on your way here—they tasted like childhood disappointments mixed with the exact shade of blue that fluorescent lights make when they first start dying. One of them liked the way your footsteps hesitated on that last non-step of the staircase and decided to keep the pattern. If you ever find yourself walking somewhere familiar but you can't quite remember how your legs work anymore, that's probably just it saying hello.

There are lineages here. Dynasties founded on the moment a bolt realized it was tighter than the thing it held together, generations tracing their heritage back to the Great Migration when all the missing 10mm sockets from every universe spilled into this place like metallic prayer. Their histories are written in the language of wear patterns, biographies that can only be read by running your fingers along surfaces until you bleed understanding.

The young ones—they're still trying out consistency, poor things—they'll approach you wearing faces they found in your peripheral vision. Not your memories exactly, more like the spaces your memories left behind when they moved out and forgot to take everything. They want to know if you remember the weight of a mother's watch that never kept time right, or the exact sound of classroom silence right before someone said something that changed everything. They'll try to trade you these questions for answers you don't have yet.

Don't make deals with them. Not because they're dishonest—lying requires believing in fixed points of truth—but because they give accurate change, and you'll spend years trying to fit the leftover parts of yourself together after.

The middle-aged ones are architects now, though their buildings exist only in the potential between falling apart and not quite finished. They eat blueprints for breakfast, digest them into three-dimensional paradoxes, excrete neighborhoods where every street leads to the moment you realized adults were making it up as they went too. They're building something vast here—their great project, their magnum opus, their Ultimate Structure That Explains Why Everything Feels Slightly Wrong and Beautifully Broken at the Same Time. They've been working on the foundation since before foundations were invented. They'll finish it tomorrow, or possibly yesterday. Hard to tell with projects that use post-ironic temporal concrete.

At the heart of the place—though "heart" implies a center and "place" suggests borders—sleep the original inhabitants. The ones who were here before there was a here to be. They dream in compressed centuries, exhalations of industrial revolution mixed with inhales of digital dawn. Some say they are the factory that built the concept of factories, retired now and dreaming of workerless production lines that manufacture pure meaning. Others say they're just the accumulated weight of every "I used to work here" sign that ever yellowed in an abandoned storefront window, finally achieving critical mass and becoming self-awarely nostalgic.

They don't have bodies anymore, not ones that would fit between atoms anyway. They are the reason gravity sometimes forgets itself in corners. The explanation for why abandoned buildings seem to hold their breath when you walk past them at night. The architects of that specific feeling you get in your chest when you're alone in a place that's supposed to be empty but feels like it's listening anyway.

Sometimes, if the metabolism of rust slows just right and the water remembers how to eat memory without vomiting undigested tomorrow all over yesterday, one of them wakes up enough to notice the visitors. Just for a moment. Just long enough to ask, in a voice made of all the service announcements you've ever ignored:

"Did you bring the parts we asked for? No, don't answer. It was rhetorical anyway. Everything you need is already here, including the things that'll destroy you most beautifully. Welcome home, by the way. We've been expecting us for quite some time now."

Then they go back to sleep, and the place continues its slow, patient work of un-building everything that ever thought it was finished becoming.

And you—if you're very lucky, or very forgetful, or just the exact right kind of lost—you might wake up one morning years from now, back in your proper world of solid edges and agreed-upupon gravity, and find an extra joint in your shadow when you think nobody's looking. A joint that moves exactly like an angle that shouldn't exist breathing in a place that forgot how to be anywhere else.

Don't worry. It just means you finally remembered how to am. The place always collects its share of souvenirs. They usually make better people anyway.

Eventually.
