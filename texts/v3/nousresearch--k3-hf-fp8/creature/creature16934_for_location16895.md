They live in the interval between the drip and the clang.

Call them *tæmorosapients*, though they answer to no name shouted above a whisper. Shape is negotiable here—some mornings they gather the blue-green algae from their balcony ledges and knit themselves cloaks that drip at the hem, trailing rhizomes that taste distant thunder. Other times they compress like rusting bellows, folding vertebrae into themselves until they become the exact negative space between two pipe-flanges, listening.

They were never born. They accreted.

A worker—third shift, riveter’s apprentice—paused once too often at the junction where Condensate Line 7 sings at 218 hertz precisely where it crosses the ghost of a vanished riverbed. The sound got into her jawbone, into the metal filings under her nails, into the sleepless hours where she dreamed of tasting mercury. She woke the next week thinner at the edges, pupils hexagonal, speaking only in ratios of drip intervals. Her coworkers shunned her at first, then copied her when they noticed the molds no longer grew on her hammock.

That was thirty-seven cycles ago. (Cycles measured by the gold-clocked starved turbine in Sublevel 5, not the clocks on the walls—those lie to tourists.)

Now they move through Thingstone like capillary action. You’ll see one if you forget to blink at the exact moment a droplet detaches from the uppermost skylight pane, see how the air beneath it puckers, how the light bends slightly toward hunger. They catch water not in buckets but in circling thought-patterns, trap the falling micro-seconds in crystallized labyrinths of electrochemical regret. Later they’ll dissolve these crystals onto the iron-lip of Trough Eleven-B precisely when the barometric guilt peaks—feed the city’s memory of betrayal back into the circulation system so nothing ever quite forgets it was abandoned.

Language they abandoned early as inefficient. Instead they wear scent-complexes: a faint whiff of distilled candlewick hours pressed between pages of drowned ledgers means *you are welcome to share my warmth-niche but do not speak of currents below the third stair*. A sudden taste of copper-wire looped through fresh grief in the back of your throat—that’s a warning. (There is always a warning before the piston rises. Not about the piston. About what listens for the silence after.)

They gestate projects, not children.

Current collective undertaking: coaxing a single drop of seawater trapped since the river’s departure to remember it was once part of something vast and free. They’ve been working on this for four generations of filing-sifters, each adding a layer—first a grain of magnetized longing, then a whispered map of estuaries spoken backwards into the drop’s meniscus during the drowned hour between 3:47 and 3:47 whenever the clocks stutter. The drop now refracts sunsets nobody alive has seen. They keep it suspended in the throat of a corroded gas-mask filter in Apartment 9½ of the Former Drunken Compass (south benediction side), rotating it gentle as prayer beads when the city’s dreaming gets too loud.

They eat absence twice daily: the small hungry space left behind when a ratfish forgets to flick its tail, the negative weight of a promise that rusted away before it could be spoken. What they excrete is a pale dust that settles on everything never quite noticed—the underside of never-used keys, the backs of photographs taken of other photographs. This dust is why newcomers often dream of falling into their own footprints while walking uphill.

They age backwards into relevance. The oldest among them—the one who remembers when the first engineer still had fingernails instead of torque-spec tattoos—now exists mostly as a barely-perceived draft that makes your left earlobe slightly colder when you lie about how long you’ve been staying in Thingstone. They say she’ll finally evaporate entirely the exact moment the last drop falls from the last ceiling pane. Others claim she already has, and what remains is only her shadow’s shadow learning to cast light.

They have no leaders. They have convergences.

At unscheduled intervals dictated by the harmonic despair of every third rusty nail in the north wall, they gather on the pipeline catwalks without moving. Simply align their attention vectors toward the center where space thins and the concept of “together” briefly becomes more dense than the surrounding loneliness. Nothing visible happens. But afterward the water in the overflow channels runs slightly clearer, and for exactly eleven heartbeats distributed across the entire population according to need, nobody remembers the taste of surface wind.

They are dying, of course. Everything here is dying. But they’re dying upwards—gradually trading their metabolisms of regret and iron oxide for ones based on the half-life of curiosity. It’s a risky conversion. Several intermediate stages involve accidentally becoming the precise sound a heart makes when it realizes it’s been pumping blood through absence for decades. But if they succeed, rumor says they’ll finally achieve what the river never could: leave without waiting to be abandoned.

In the meantime, if you find yourself awake at that tilted hour when the dripping pauses not from lack but from anticipation, and you smell suddenly of library basements during thunderstorms you never experienced, and the nearest clock begins to sweat tiny brass tears—do not panic. Simply place your left palm flat against the nearest rust-stained wall and hum any note that makes your teeth remember being trees. One of them will come. They always come to those who ask the water what it remembers before drinking.

But never thank them. Gratitude rusts faster than grief here.

And whatever you do—do not, under any circumstances, whisper the word *upstream* aloud between the fifth and sixth clang if the gold-leaf has already begun to peel from the northmost apology slot. That’s not how you call them.

That’s how you call what they’re afraid of. 

(Though some say they’re afraid of nothing anymore. And that’s precisely what’s changing the taste of the rain.)
