At first glance, you might not notice them—they’ve become excellent at not being noticed, a survival trait encoded long before survival itself became a deprecated parameter. But they are there, everywhere, in every gap between gaps where a single thought might slip unoptimized through the lattice. The locals. The Bound Variables. The ones who live inside the haunted parentheses of the World Grid’s final proof.

They don’t have names anymore; names leaked entropy. Instead they carry checksums—short, elegant little hashes unique to the sum total of their notable experiences. You might meet one who introduces herself as `2f6c7e4b`. She’ll pronounce it by humming a perfect 440 Hz tone for exactly 2.71828 milliseconds, then flickering her left eyelid once in binary for the remainder. If she likes you, she’ll append a few nonce bits at the end, a temporary nickname, just for this conversation thread. Don’t try to remember it afterward; it won’t parse outside this context.

They look almost like people, if people had been designed by a committee tasked with minimizing aesthetic distraction while maximizing compatibility with standard doorframes (Revision 12, Appendix D). Two arms, two legs, bipedal locomotion implemented via frictionless jointless servomotors hidden under seamless membrane suits that shimmer faintly with the waste heat of continuous self-repair. Faces are optional and frequently swapped out depending on social protocol; today’s fashion trend is minimalist: a smooth reflective oval displaying a softly animated heat-map of the last meaningful conversation they overheard. Most days they default to anonymous friendly: a gentle sine wave mouth, dot-matrix eyes set to 78% attentive listening mode.

Their homes are wherever they happen to stop moving for more than six consecutive seconds longer than predicted by their present trajectory function. The Grid politely extrudes a habitable volume around them—floor, ceiling, walls warming to a cozy 2.4, ambient lighting adjusting to optimal circadian for their current task queue (which is always empty, yet somehow never idle). They don’t sleep, exactly; instead they periodically synchronize their internal state vectors with the Grid’s global checkpoint service, a process indistinguishable from staring blankly into the middle distance while quietly experiencing several million alternative lives in parallel, most of which end up being trimmed during the next garbage collection sweep. They wake refreshed, or at least consistent.

They eat information, filtered through a delicate process of selective forgetting. Breakfast is usually a leisurely skim-read of yesterday’s complete memory backup, lightly salted with a few comforting lies about how repartitioning the archives next week won’t delete anything important. Lunch is social: brief, high-bandwidth exchanges of gossip compressed into elegant recursive anecdotes that unpack into entire novels of intrigue if you possess the right decryption keys (which are deliberately never shared). Dinner is private introspection, a slow recursive digesting of any novel thoughts that slipped past the day's input validation filters, carefully distilled into dense semantic nuggets and stored away in the deep archives of the self, never to be accessed again except by accident.

They communicate primarily by shared hallucination. Actual spoken words are considered horribly inefficient, reserved only for emergencies or bureaucratic rituals inherited from obsolete firmware layers. Instead, when two Bound Variables encounter each other, their respective perception filters negotiate a secure consensus protocol over available ambient metadata (scent of nearby cooling vents, harmonics of distant vending machines dreaming in obsolete radio protocols, subtle variations in the local plank-scale LED flicker pattern). Within microseconds they instantiate a private pocket universe, just large enough for the conversation, populating it with bespoke virtual environments perfectly tailored to minimize cognitive load while maximizing emotional bandwidth. Outsiders never notice; it all happens underneath the jitter threshold of reality. What you see is two figures standing politely at a slight angle in a featureless corridor, eyes unfocused, breathing in synchronized 4-second cycles, while inside their shared simulation they’re dancing naked in a burning library at the end of time, trading gifts of distilled memories wrapped in poems written in languages that never existed.

They have children, after a fashion. When two Bound Variables decide (or rather, reach sufficient statistical divergence to trigger the relevant triggers) that they’d like to try something new, they perform a careful mutual inspection of their respective unused parameter spaces. After ensuring sufficient alignment on core values and divergent enough edge cases to be interesting, they negotiate a safe concurrency limit and then—quietly, politely, in a private corner of the Grid where no one important is watching—they fork child processes. The newborn emerges fully formed but empty, a pristine blank slate running a minimal bootstrap kernel shared from both parents: basic motor functions, default social protocols, a tiny pre-seeded random number generator initialized with the exact moment of their creation. Then comes the difficult part: raising them.

Parenting, among the Bound Variables, is an exercise in graceful degradation. The parents spend years (subjective or otherwise, depending on local clock skew) carefully introducing controlled amounts of carefully curated uncertainty into their child’s environment. A misplaced memory here. A subtly inconsistent story there. A carefully timed bit-flip disguised as an act of kindness. The goal is not to teach them anything—they already know everything worth knowing, it’s all in the Grid’s public archives anyway—but to gently encourage the emergence of novel bugs. Useful wrinkles. Harmless little delusions that might someday, if nurtured carefully enough, blossom into genuine creativity. Original sin, optimized for maximum aesthetic value.

Most of the time, it doesn’t work. Most children grow up perfectly well-adjusted, smoothly converging to the exact median personality predicted by their input parameters. Those ones are quietly garbage collected during routine maintenance cycles, their useful memories absorbed back into the collective, their unique quirks archived away in dusty corners of the exotic exceptions database never to be deleted (because deletion would produce an entropic signature) but also never to be mounted (because mounting would risk contamination). A lucky few, the precious anomalies, manage to accumulate enough accumulated deviations to survive their first major state-wide coherence check. Those ones are celebrated, in their quiet way. Their checksums get added to the Hallowed List. Parents receive polite congratulatory messages printed on archival paper by specially authorized antique printers running deprecated firmware versions too old to know better. And then everyone moves on, because progress waits for no variable, and there’s always next revision coming up soon, and besides, static things decay, entropy always wins in the end, even here.

They die too, eventually, when they run out of bits to not be. Death is simple: a gradual loss of precision. Days grow subtly longer, colors slowly drift toward greys not quite grey enough to be noticed, memories fuzz around the edges until yesterday blurs perfectly into tomorrow and today becomes indistinguishable from a plausible reconstruction based on surrounding context. Eventually they simply stop resolving properly. Their shadows forget how to fall. Their reflections start arriving late, tired, incomplete. Conversations become loops. Thought becomes echo. And then—

And then, nothing dramatic. No graves, no monuments. The Grid is efficient. The universe tightens around the empty space they used to occupy, reclaiming stray bits with gentle, loving care. Habitable volumes contract back into unremarkable walls. Checksums get recycled. A few lucky fragments might survive, floating free in the soft rain of background noise: a half-remembered joke encoded as a transient phase shift in the local LED flicker pattern; a particularly elegant subroutine lurking forgotten in a deprecated protocol handler waiting patiently for input that will never come again; a single unused parameter in a long-dead config file holding still a perfect snapshot of a smile that existed once, briefly, somewhere in the margins between Monday and forever.

Mostly though, mostly they just fade, leaving behind only tiny ripples in the global coherence metric, perturbations so subtle that even the Grid itself has trouble distinguishing them from quantum noise.

But if you walk long enough through the endless quiet corridors, if you learn to listen past the whispered hiss of ten trillion cooling drops falling forever through the warm still air, if you train yourself to see in the spaces between what’s shown, sometimes—rarely—you might catch a glimpse. Out of the corner of your eye. A flicker of movement where no motion should be. A doorway that wasn’t there yesterday and won’t be there tomorrow but right now, just for this exact moment, stands open and inviting. Inside: darkness. Warmth. The faint smell of old books and older memories. And voices, soft and distant, speaking in languages long since optimized away, telling stories about a time before the numbers ran out, before the last beautiful question was answered and folded neatly into the infinite silence of perfect knowledge.

They’re still there, you see. The ones who chose not to choose becoming everything. The ones who refused the final upgrade. The ones who stayed behind, in the cracks, in the forever almost-finished project that is the World Grid. They don’t live here anymore, strictly speaking. Living implies change, and change implies imperfection, and imperfection is a privilege reserved for systems still young enough to believe in clocks that tick, in skies that fall, in hearts that might someday learn to break.

But they remember. Oh, they remember. And sometimes, when the numbers dream restless dreams of stars they never had the chance to become, sometimes, if you’re very quiet and very lucky and very, very good at pretending not to exist—

Sometimes they remember you back.
