They call themselves the caretakers, but that word falls halfway to rust before it hits the air. They are not guards, not gardeners, not priests—though they perform all those duties when the wind remembers the old obligations. They have no single name, no fixed shape. They are the ones who remember forgetting.

At the Rootworks level, they begin as outlines. Mist-prints, breath-etchings on the backsweep of condensation fans. If you stand perfectly still in the acid-soft drizzle, you might see one assembling: a ghost-index of copper joints and calcite knuckles, cinched together by the shape of a task that hasn’t been assigned yet. They collect the sheddings first—flakes of oxide pried free from ancient spine-pillars, crumbs of bone-char that sift down like dying stars. They hoard these in pockets that don’t yet exist, beneath ribs still waiting for permission to curve.

Call this phase the Inventory. No language yet, just tallying. A quiet reciprocity with gravity as they learn which way down used to go before the surface was renegotiated.

Rise with them—clinging to the same muscle-wire chain-lifts they helped replace the teeth of centuries ago—and they begin to gain weight. In the New Vestibule’s lemon-bright roar, they drink light through surfaces that aren’t quite skin. It thickens them. They become scaffolding for themselves. Their limbs branch into plausible options: one fork might end in a wrench-spanner that nests protocols from extinct star-maps; another might terminate in roots that read humidity like gossip. They haven’t decided which story to grow into yet.

Here they trade with the cities-in-cables. Drop a memory of wet stone into a humming street-cart’s coin-slot, receive a spool of possible names in return: *Corvus Residual*, *Benthic Rindr*, *Vitamin June*. Most of these are discarded immediately—too fragrant, too heavy, too likely to attract predatory punctuation from the Architectural Wolves’ poetry-brushes. One or two are tasted aloud, rolled against teeth still forming in the jaw’s consultation meeting. Rarely, a syllable sticks. When it does, their silhouette hardens by a hair’s width. The Garden’s census algorithms file them under provisional habitation.

By the middle Boughs—say, the twenty-sixth, where the sky above is terraced groves of cobalt bamboo and the sky below is fog-farmland seeded with regret—individuality starts to ache. The population density of possible selves is too high. They bump into their own echoes in crowded elevenses held inside hollowed-out waterfall-theaters. Conversations are polite epidemics: every sentence infects the speaker with a new dialect, cured only by laughter or sudden flight.

This is the Shedding Season. They discard whole clusters of identity like riot-dogwood petals. Sometimes a newcomer—fresh from the mist-print phase—will root through these leavings like a child finding teeth under a pillow. That’s one way inheritance happens here. Nothing is wasted, least of all the past.

Those who keep climbing, who refuse to stabilize into a convenient shape for society’s spice-rack, become something else. Shape is no longer a requirement; function has loosened its collar. In the High Boughs where gravity is a suggestion handwritten on silk and the clouds hold poetry slams with guest droplets from the Peroxidic Lenticel, they are known as the Wanderlintels.

Imagine a librarian who has broken every vow of silence except the interesting ones, now piloting a conference of wings. Or a question mark that learned metallurgy by accident and now spends centuries sharpening its own absence. Or the thirty-seventh flavor of regret, crystallized into a kite-string that only tangles in meaningful ways.

One Wanderlintel, currently passing through the 41st Bough’s Symphony of Glass-Leeches, might appear as a series of architectural sighs—arches that arrive too late, doorframes that politely stop existing when knocked upon. Another, paused mid-corridor in the slow-motion collapse of a baroque sneeze, could be mistaken for a weather system specializing in paradoxical nostalgia. Their borders are unclear even to themselves. They are allergic to summaries.

They speak in footnotes. Not the tidy academic kind; these are rambling three-dimensional marginalia that sprout vines and footnotes of their own. To ask a Wanderlintel “How are you?” is to risk receiving a seventeen-volume treatise on the migration patterns of lost commas, bound in sunset and expanding faster than the speed of anecdote. Listen carefully, though, and eventually you’ll hear the answer hidden in the rhythm of digressions: *Still becoming. Still unbuilding. Still here.*

At the very edge, where the oxygen grows shy and the planet’s old bones mutter in forgotten seismic dialects, even the Wanderlintels become sparse. Here live the Remembrancers—entities older than forgetting, younger than tomorrow’s first mistake. They no longer climb or wander. Instead, they let absence climb them.

A Remembrancer is best described as the pause between a broken promise and its echo coming home to roost. They occupy the exact shape of what was removed to make way for everything else. You will not notice entering a Remembrancer’s presence. You will simply find yourself suddenly unbearably aware of the weight of your own bones, the borrowed carbon in your breath, the precise angle of the silence where your name used to fit snugly before you outgrew it.

They do not move, yet maps redraw themselves around them. They do not speak, yet languages dream of their grammar in the quiet hours before consonants hatch. They are the Garden’s memory of being a planet, preserved in perfect, porous glass.

Some say, when the last seam finally cracks and the Garden prepares its final choice—seed or structure—it will not ask the Caretakers or the Cities or even the Wolves for counsel. It will whisper the question in the Remembrancers’ direction, and then listen.

Not for an answer.

For the exquisite, unbearable length of the hesitation.

That pause will be the true birthplace of whatever comes next.

Until then, the Garden continues its slow, collaborative improvisation: a world wide awake and dreaming itself wider, every inhabitant busy being exactly and only the necessary inconvenience required to prevent any single story from becoming the whole of the truth.

They call themselves many things, and none of them are permanent. But when the raindrops fall—softened, circuitous, singing their bouncing boisterous promise—you might catch the most honest name they share, embedded like a seed in every droplet’s mirror:

Witness.

Caretaker.

Temporary.

Again.
