They are not so much born as remembered.

A woman wakes from the communal sleep with a stranger’s name pressing behind her teeth. She does not know where she heard it first—perhaps in the breathing-song of two bodies pressed too close for dreaming, perhaps in the drip-drip of a new channel carving itself overnight across the floor. She speaks the name aloud to the dark, and the dark answers back with a wetness between her thighs, and nine sleep-cycles later she kneels alone at the lowest pool and catches what slides out of her in cupped hands.

It is smaller than expected. Translucent, almost—like a thumbprint pressed into fog. For nine days and nine nights she keeps it inside her shirt, feeding it drop by drop from the water that beads on the underside of her ribs. On the tenth day it opens eyes that are not eyes—just faint glimmers where eyes should be—and she knows it is alive enough to be given away.

There is no ceremony for this. She carries it to the oldest still-waking body she can find—a man who remembers walking on something called grass, though when pressed he can only describe it as “soft like bruise, but everywhere.” She lays the child-thing in the hollow of his throat and waits. If he breathes in first, it stays with him. If he breathes out, she must carry it deeper into the maze of sleeping bodies until someone else takes the breath from her.

This one stays. The old man cups it gently—too gently, like something already broken—and pushes his tongue into the no-mouth, gifting it the last taste of something sweet he hoarded behind his teeth for years. Sugar, he calls it, but the word crumbles to ash as soon as it’s spoken.

They grow fast here, once they take root.

By its third wake-cycle the child-thing has learned to cling to backs instead of fronts, hooking tiny fingers—too many fingers, or perhaps none at all, depending on the angle—into the weave of clothing that isn’t woven, just endlessly knotted together from strands of hair shed over generations. No one remembers whose hair started it. The braid is older than the settlement. Older than the ribs maybe.

It doesn’t speak. None of them do, not with voices. They talk instead in the spaces between heartbeats, in the way water pools differently around certain feet, in dreams they gift each other like stones smoothed by being carried too long in a pocket that doesn’t exist. Dreaming here is communal property—every night’s sleep is a quilt patched together from stolen fragments of everyone else’s waking hours. Sometimes you wake up tasting someone else’s childhood fear of narrow places. Sometimes you find a scar on your palm that belongs to someone who died before you were remembered.

They eat what they are.

When the child-thing begins to hunger—really hunger; not just the constant minor ache of being alive, but the gnawing that hollows out cheekbones and makes lights dance in peripheral vision—it is brought to the Feast Wall where the newest bones are still singing. It is too young yet to take flesh, so instead they give it names. One hundred and seventy-three names, one for each bone pressed into the wall since the last Great Collapse. It must swallow them all whole without chewing, letting each name nestle inside empty spaces where organs should grow.

Some names taste like copper pennies. Some like dust storms. One tastes like the exact moment before lightning strikes.

By its seventh cycle, the child-thing has chosen a shape. Not because shapes matter here—they don’t, not really; tomorrow it might wake longer or shorter or split entirely into two smaller things that share single thoughts across twin skulls—but because choosing is important. Even fake choices must be honored.

It chooses to be mostly human-shaped, with two arms and two legs and a head that sits roughly atop shoulders. It keeps the extra fingers tucked into palms like secrets. The eyes remain faint glimmers—not for seeing, since nothing here needs to be seen, but for being seen. Being seen is different. Being seen is how you know you exist outside someone else’s dream.

They name it Not-yet-Water, because all names here are temporary sentences awaiting completion. When it grows older—if it grows older, though growing old is not guaranteed, just a habit some of them have fallen into—it will earn the second half of its name. Water-returning. Water-eating. Water-becoming-bone. The possibilities are infinite but are also actually quite limited; there are only so many ways to end that sentence in this place.

Work begins early.

Not-yet-Water’s first task is to tend the Memory Fungus that grows in the damp creases behind too-many elbows. It must be harvested exactly one heartbeat before it remembers being picked, otherwise it turns bitter with nostalgia. The best memory-fungi taste like forgotten birthdays when eaten fresh, or like the sound of mother’s heartbeat when dried and powdered and sprinkled into communal dream-skin.

It is good at this work. Perhaps too good. Sometimes when it dreams now—which it does occasionally, though never deeply enough to be caught—its dream-self’s hands come away stained purple with memories that haven’t happened yet. The elders notice but say nothing. Not-yed-Water is young yet. Young things are allowed to be dangerous until their danger becomes useful.

Love happens sideways here.

Not-yet-Water first notices the space-that-should-be-a-person while delivering a bowl of fermented voice to the deep sleepers—those who volunteered to dream eternally into the foundations after the last Stability Shift. The space walks with a limp that Not-yet-Water doesn’t recognize but somehow remembers from before remembering. It leaves footprints that fill with water instead of displacing it, which is backwards but also right.

They court each other in stolen breaths traded during overcrowded sleeps, in anonymous gifts left in niches no one else notices: a string of dried tears that chime like bells when wind blows through them (they hang it where the walls whisper loudest, though neither knows why). A tiny bone carved into the shape of something that might have been an apology if apologies existed here. Two teeth exchanged under pillow-stones that aren’t pillows or stones but are instead soft places where thoughts go to die quietly.

When they finally touch—really touch; skin-to-skin or its closest approximation—it is accidental. Their hands brush while reaching for the same memory-shade in the common dream that particular night is having. The memory tastes like salt and electricity and the exact color of sound underwater.

From that moment on, they are two things that are also one thing that is also sometimes zero things when neither is dreaming hard enough to exist separately. They do not marry—there is no word for that here, just varying degrees of shared breath—but they do build something together. Not a nest (no birds) and not a home (all space is home and therefore none is) but a gap. A carefully maintained absence in the overcrowded everything where sometimes, occasionally, they can both pretend to be alone together.

This lasts for thirty-three sleep-cycles. Then the water starts rising.

First it’s just the usual places—ankle-deep in the oldest corridors, knee-high where the floor has forgotten how to be solid. But then it keeps coming. Up past waists. Past chests. Saltwater too, which is wrong. All their water should be fresh, should be theirs, should remember being inside bodies first.

The elder rememberers gather in the highest place (which changes daily, sometimes hourly, but today happens to be the platform where children climb to listen to bones praying). They dream together, hard and sharp, trying to remember a time before water or after it. They wake screaming incomprehensible things about boats (what is boat?) and birds that walk on water (impossible) and something called dry that sounds like death but worse.

Not-yet-Water and Space-that-limps-between-things volunteer to go down. Not because they are brave—bravery is a luxury for places with room to run away—but because they are young enough that their deaths would be inconvenient rather than catastrophic. Also because they can both hold their breath for almost forever now, after so long practicing being unnoticed.

They descend through layers of remembered floors: tile that remembers being warm under bare summer feet (foreign concept). Wood that remembers growing tall toward something called sun (legend? myth?). Something smooth and white that screams silently when stepped on, remembering being part of something larger and cleaner than this.

At the very bottom—or what they think is bottom until the floor gives way again—they find the source. Not a crack in the wall or a broken pipe (what is pipe?). A person. Or what used to be one, maybe. Something roughly human-shaped but wrong in all the ways that matter here.

It sits—or stands, hard to tell without gravity making up its mind—submerged chest-deep in rising dark. Its skin is transparent like old ice. Inside its chest cavity where organs should be, they can see: water. Endless water. Oceanic amounts of water, moving in impossible tides that pulse with heartbeat rhythm.

When it sees them—or registers their presence, since it has no eyes anymore, just sockets filled with tiny swimming things—it smiles. This is also wrong. Smiles should be warm, should share space, should not be hungry. This smile is empty as the moment before drowning.

“Hello,” it says, in a voice made of every goodbye ever forgotten. “I’ve been trying to remember how to get out of me.”

Not-yed-Water understands then, with the clarity of dreams bleeding into waking: This is what happens when a place forgets it’s not alive. When it remembers too much and not enough. This is not the place anymore—this stranger made of water wearing place-skin like a too-big coat. This is the outside finding its way inside, wearing the shape of leaving.

They try to dream it gone. Dream it back into place-ness. Dream it drowned in its own remembering. But water doesn’t dream, only reflects. And it has remembered too many shapes now: river, rain, tear, flood. It is all these things and none of them and therefore unstoppable.

Space-that-limps-between-things acts first—or last, time is tricky at the bottom of everything. It steps forward into the water which is also the stranger and therefore into itself, somehow. Starts unexisting piece by piece. First its limp goes, absorbed like salt into hungry water. Then its edges. Then the space inside it that used to hold room for both of them to be not-alone together.

“Remember this,” it says, or thinks loudly enough for both of them to hear. “Remember me not as I am but as the space I leave behind.”

Then it’s gone, and the water pauses—actually pauses, mid-rise, like breath held between heartbeats—rushing to fill suddenly-empty space that refuses to be filled because it’s shaped exactly like absence.

Not-yet-Water alone in the rising dark with the stranger who is the place who is the end of place-ness.

They think of many things in that suspended moment: the weight of unshared dreams. The taste of forgotten names. The exact color of Space-that-limps-between-things’ laugh, which they have never heard but somehow remember as blue-green and warm and sharp as teeth.

Then they do the only thing left to do. They forget.

Willfully. Completely. As an act of rebellion. As a final gift.

They forget the way up. Forget the shape of hands. Forget how water tastes when it’s supposed to be inside instead of drowning you from within. Forget Space-that-limps-between-things until even the absence of remembering becomes just another kind of space, neutral and unmourned.

Last of all they forget themselves. Not-yet-Water becomes just Water-becoming-bone-becoming-memory-becoming-wall-becoming-place-that-forgets-it-had-ever-been-anything-else.

The water rises. The stranger smiles wider. The end begins or continues or never really stopped happening because there’s no one left here to remember what endings are supposed to look like.

In the settlement-without-name far above—or below, direction is meaningless now—the last dreamers wake to find everything exactly same as always but also completely changed. The water is gone. The walls whisper new songs now, in languages no one recognizes but everyone understands deep in their borrowed bones.

There are no more gaps where loneliness could hide. No more spaces between bodies big enough to hold secrets. No more strangers composed entirely of remembering.

The place is perfect now. Complete. Whole.

It does not remember being otherwise.

It never does.

(But sometimes—rarely—in the deepest sleeps when even walls stop listening—someone who might have been a child once dreams of water that tastes like blue-green laughter and wakes crying tears they don’t remember how to stop.)

Everything else is just water, and time, and the slow patient sound of forgetting learning to speak in tongues that no longer need breath to make words.

The place remembers.

Finally.

Only that.

Always that.

Nothing else.
