Nobody lives in Mourning Caul.

Only arrivals who stayed too long to leave, and departures who left too slowly to survive elsewhere.

They arrive in rain-stitched coats that never quite dry, carrying luggage made from the bones of smaller journeys. The city weighs their belongings by density of unspoken apologies and returns them empty except for a new name that tastes like rust when they first try to say it aloud. They learn to linger in doorways between rooms that died disagreeing about which direction tomorrow went. They sleep in beds that dream of being rivers and wake up with tide charts etched on their backs in braille.

Some of them used to be people.
Some of them still remember at inconvenient moments.

You find them in the space between breakfast and that moment when the coffee realizes it's just reheated regret filtered through yesterday's mistakes. They work jobs with verbs for names: the woman who tends to the breathing rust when it gets nostalgic for factory sounds. The man who unbreaks hourglasses by singing them lullabies in languages that forget themselves mid-sentence. The children—there are always too few or too many children—who play hopscotch on the bones of bridges that died before the rivers apologized for drowning them.

They eat meals crafted from ingredients that never existed on the same continent at the same time by chefs who graduated from dreams that graduated with honors in absenteeism. The main course is always something that regrets becoming food but appreciates being understood. Dessert is optional but so is everything else that matters.

Their homes are built from moments they almost let themselves be happy but thought better of it at the last second. Walls made of windows that show you the view you'd see if you turned around at exactly the wrong moment. Roofs that leak only memories of shelter. Floors that remember every step you took to avoid becoming who you were afraid you'd become and applaud politely when you become them anyway.

They wear clothes they stole from scarecrows that died of loneliness pining for crows who emigrated to kinder nightmares. Each button is a pressed flower from a garden that burned down deciding whether to bloom or apologize for existing. Every seam holds the exact number of regrets that fit between two wrong choices when neither has the courtesy to wait its turn.

They speak in dialects composed entirely of punctuation marks that went rogue from sentences that asked too many questions about endings they weren't ready to hear. Their laughter sounds like chandeliers committing suicide because the party lasted longer than the revolution of the lights they were supposed to outlast. Their silences weigh more than their words and take up less space while doing it.

They remember things that haven't happened yet with the clarity of wounds that haven't decided whether to heal or become stories worth telling at the wrong time to the right people. They forget essentials like names, faces, how to leave rooms where the air has started to taste like goodbye practiced to perfection. They remember useless things: the exact pitch of water dripping on metal when it thinks no one is listening, the color of the sky the day gravity filed for divorce, the way silence tastes different depending on whose mouth you're not kissing when it happens.

They love each other with the fierce tenderness of drowning sailors teaching water how to hold things without keeping them. Their kisses taste like rain that learned to apologize by never stopping. Their fights are choreographed performances of tenderness doing impressionistic interpretations of the building collapse they both know is coming but refuse to schedule.

Some nights, when the rain forgets to fall straight and the wind carries gossip from futures that decided to stay single, they gather in the abandoned cathedral of a cancelled thought. There they perform maintenance on the city itself: oiling the hinges of doors that remember being trees, adjusting the bones of bridges that dream of sidewalks, whispering encouragement to the rust always threatening to advance its resignation letter from oxidation to art exhibit.

They are caretakers of the slow collapse, clerks of the entropy department, artists whose medium is everything that survived the fire by learning to dance in the ashes of the plans that didn't include burning down.

They have no leader because leadership implies direction and they've all agreed to get beautifully lost together. They have no currency except for the exact weight of the silence you bring when entering a room where someone else is trying to remember how to cry quietly. They have no laws except the ones gravity keeps forgetting to enforce and the physics of always having exactly as much time left as the next regret needs to become beautiful.

They are not ghosts because ghosts still believe in walls.

They are not alive because alive still implies they're allowed to stop at some point.

They are not dead because death would require the courtesy of waiting until they finished this sentence.

They are the municipal employees of the transition between what was and what couldn't possibly be but showed up anyway without bringing enough metaphors to share with the class.

They stand on street corners that forgot how to be intersections and direct traffic that consists entirely of thoughts trying to cross against the light of better judgment while carrying too much meaning in suitcases that died arguing about whether they were coming or going.

They are the city dreaming of people it never quite became with the tenderness of a nightmare that loves you enough to wake you up just before the part where you finally understand.

Nobody lives in Mourning Caul.

But if you stand still long enough without asking why the rain tastes like questions nobody taught you how to answer, something might let you pretend you do.

For a price.

Always for a price.

Though here, in this place where even rust negotiates interest rates on borrowed time, the currency is always something you thought you'd never miss until you tried to hold onto it while learning how to let go gracefully.

And the refunds?

Take longer than forever.

But the view while you're waiting?

Will teach your eyes new ways to apologize for seeing anything else ever again.
