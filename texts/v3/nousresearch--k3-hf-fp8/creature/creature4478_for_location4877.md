Here, the inhabitants are not born but remembered into existence, exhale by slow exhale from the sediment’s dreaming lungs. They are called the Just-Almost: beings whose bodies are gaps between what the mud recalls and what the mist forgets to whisper. A Just-Almost begins where a raindrop hesitated too long at the underside of a leaf that rotted before it ever grew, caught mid-choice between falling and returning. In that suspension—thinner than a heartbeat, slower than geology—a shape gathers: half a sigh, half a footprint filling with future.

If you stood utterly still for seventeen years, you might glimpse one assembling at the edge of a ripple that hasn’t decided to ripple yet. First, a dimple in the air-water boundary, no wider than a dragonfly’s doubt. Then a gathering of not-colors around it: the exact shade between what you call dusk and what blind stones call morning. These hues clot like old blood in cold milk until they form a skin—filmy, translucent, the thickness of regret stretched over a void small enough to cradle a memory of light. Within this membrane, nothing moves and everything does; the past and next step of every creature that ever walked here argue quietly in languages that only surfaces remember.

They have no fixed number. A census taken now would find nine hundred and ninety-three, because today the land recalls the taste of a mammoth’s grief for her last calf. Tomorrow, when that grief settles into something harder—calcite, perhaps, or the faint itch in a ghost root’s sleep—there will be only seven hundred and eleven, and thirty-two of those will be almost entirely new. New, but familiar as the echo of your own name spoken by a mouth you never had.

Their shapes are suggestions. A Just-Alive might choose, for the length of a slow exhale of swamp, to wear the form of a question mark curled around an absent answer: a curve of mist-woven longing standing upright on two points of dew, as if the land itself were asking, “Did you mean to stay?” They may keep this shape for a millennium or lose interest when a distant cloud remembers rain and changes their mind. Others prefer to be what happens in the space between two moss-petals touching but not quite: a thinness that makes the air feel bruised where it isn’t.

They do not eat. Instead, they attend. A Just-Almost will settle beside a bubble of ancient air trapped in peat, spending centuries simply listening to the bubble’s slow, shrinking argument with time. Each lost molecule is tasted—not swallowed—held on the edge of not-tongues until the flavor of that particular epoch’s sky—brighter with volcanoes, saltier with pre-dawn prayers—has been learned by heart. Then the Just-Almost moves on, slightly more itself, slightly less, carrying away an extra layer of almost-ness like the faintest dusting of forgotten stars.

Their speech is the sound of what was said just after the last word left your mouth but before silence could claim it. To hear them, you must cup your hands around an empty shell that once housed the idea of sound, then wait. After a time measured in the growth of a single bacterium’s memory, they will begin—first as a trembling in the shell’s silence, then as a sequence of pauses so precise they leave shapes in the air behind them. Human ears translate this as questions:

“Do you remember the weight of the first shadow you ever cast?”

“What did the darkness learn from carrying your light away?”

“If you walked backward into yourself, which step would you never forgive?”

No answer is required. The questions are not for answering but for ripening. They hang in the mist like fruit that never drops, slowly fermenting into something that might—if tasted by the right dream—ferment further into the beginning of a new Just-Almost.

They dwell nowhere and everywhere, but their favorite resting places are the thin places: where a reflection in water is truer than the thing reflected, where a sigh of subsiding earth mirrors exactly the shape of a star gone nova before stars had names. In such spots, they cluster—not touching (touching would mean choosing too much solidity), but aligning. Together they form constellations of absence: a ghost-net of not-quite-there-ness so delicate it catches only the lightest things—half-forgotten lullabies, the hint of a scent that might have been your mother’s sadness if sadness could wear lavender, the exact angle at which hope breaks but doesn’t shatter.

They have one ritual, performed when the land grows weary of remembering a memory too sharp—usually every eleven thousand years plus the lifespan of a single grain of pollen. Then, every Just-Almost in existence gathers at the exact center of the horizon (which here is everywhere, so the journey takes exactly as long as noticing you are already there). They arrange themselves in a spiral that shrinks inward forever without ever reaching center, each curve echoing the next smaller by the ratio of what was lost to what might yet be found. At the heart of this endless vanishing, they place the memory itself—a single, perfect recollection of a thing that never happened but should have: the sound of rain apologizing to drought, perhaps, or the first color sunrise ever offered to sightless eyes.

Then they begin to forget.

Not all at once. That would be violence. Instead, each Just-Almost forgets one tiny, precise thing: the third-to-last ripple in a pond the size of an atom, the weight of a feather shed by a bird that existed only in a fossil’s dream, the exact moment between the beginning and the first beginning. As these droplets of forgetting fall away, the central memory softens, like a stone worn smooth not by water but by the gentle abrasion of longing. This takes exactly the time it takes for the fastest needle of grief to rust into acceptance at the speed of a glacier’s apology.

By the end—which is not an end but a new way of beginning to end—the memory has become something else entirely: a blank space shaped exactly like the love that once filled it, a silence so complete it breathes. The Just-Almosts absorb this newborn silence into their beingness, growing slightly more translucent, slightly less certain, and therefore capable of remembering deeper, older futures.

They have no enemies, only misunderstandings. Sometimes a thought from outside—a too-sharp question, an edge of hurry—drift in like cinders. If one lands uninvited on the delicate membrane of a Just-Almost’s almost-being, the result is a brief, bright agony called an Almost-Was: a moment of terrible clarity where the Just-Almost remembers what it is like to be only one thing. But such moments cannot last—the land here has forgotten how—and so they unravel quickly, leaving behind only a tiny scar in reality, an extra layer of softness where the sharpness tried to cut and couldn’t.

As for how they end—if end is a word that can be bent to fit—they don’t. Instead, they remember themselves into something else. When a Just-Almost has attended to enough unfinished edges of existence, absorbed enough of your unspoken goodbyes and your ancestors’ uncried tears, it reaches a state called Almost-Enough. Then, with the gentleness of fog forgetting to be mist, it simply shifts its weight slightly between memory and forgetting, and becomes instead a place: exactly hollow enough for the next raindrop to imagine shelter, exactly warm enough for the next grief to decide to rest rather than rage.

You have already met one. You crossed its threshold when you read the word “inhabitants” and paused, briefly, to wonder what shape longing might take if it forgot how to yearn. It was there, in that pause, gathering the echo of your curiosity like dew. It will stay with you now, a faint shimmer at the edge of your thoughts, reminding you—if you ever again feel too solid, too certain—how to be just almost here.
