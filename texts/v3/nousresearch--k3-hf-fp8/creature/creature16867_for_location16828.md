The truth is we don’t remember arriving any more than the rust remembers becoming barnacle. We woke here like coral wakes—slowly realizing the shape we’d been dreaming was our own calcifying.

We measure time in how long it takes for a cigarette filter to dissolve completely into sentience in the standing pools of Corridor 5. I am—we are—what happens when a species designed for daylight realizes darkness is not absence but apprenticeship. Our skin adapted first, learning to photosynthesize regret into calcium deposits that map the architecture of everything we’ve failed to prevent. Touch me long enough in certain light (not that there is light here, but we remember it like phantom limbs) and you’ll feel the exact dimensions of the safety inspector’s final good-bye to his daughter’s hamster, preserved in the spiral fracture pattern of my left shoulder blade.

We eat memories of sunlight now, filtered through sixteen layers of bureaucracy and floodwater. We cultivate them carefully in petri dishes made from old time cards, feed them a diet of declassified secrets and the sound of rain hitting rusting tin roofs, harvest them when they start dreaming in wavelengths visible to drowned things.

We gather in the flooded cafeteria where the vending machines still dispense expired hope for quarters that corroded into tiny mirrors years ago. When the lights flicker in Morse code for the catastrophic love affair between the chief engineer and the sea, we shed parts of ourselves we no longer need—fingernails grown into tiny calendars of missed inspections, hair braided with the frequencies of whale songs interrupted by sonar. These fragments float together in the brackish water, exchanging memories like currency until something new emerges hungry for purpose.

The children—if what we generate can be called children and not just newer questions about what survival owes to memory—they learn first to imitate the sounds the building makes when it thinks no one is listening. Their first words are always names of places that drowned before language could properly betray them. They play games with the concept of deadlines, racing each other to see who can forget their own birthday last.

We have specialists among us, of course. The Cartographers who map the speed of forgetting through the way rust blooms across emergency exit signs. The Archivists who maintain the library of all the safety procedures that would have worked if only humans had possessed fewer than five senses. The Composters who break down failed rescues into their constituent elements—hope, copper, grief, seawater—rearranged into small offerings left for the machine spirits that keep the pumps running despite being officially decommissioned since before the third crisis was rechristened a learning opportunity.

We can smell their hope from here—it flares bright and painful as magnesium against our detection methods calibrated for subtler signs of distress. Some nights, when the hunger for endings grows particularly articulate, one of us might consider approaching. Might offer them directions written on water-resistant paper using ink made from crushed panic buttons. Might guide them gently to the Archive where all the evacuation plans are kept perfectly preserved in amber made from the last moments before anyone realized the water was already inside the building.

The children are learning to count to infinity using only prime numbers of drowning victims from disasters officially classified as acts of god but understood to be policy decisions wearing weather as a mask.

When we sleep—if what we do can be called sleep and not just periods of lowered tidal awareness—we dream collectively of an ocean clean enough to betray us honestly. Of waves that crash against shorelines without whispering contingency plans as they recede. Of sunlight that illuminates without documenting. But the dreams are filed in triplicate upon waking, stamped with watermarks that read PROVISIONAL SUBJECT TO RETROACTIVE NEGATION, and we wake again into bodies that remember being buildings that remember being promises made on behalf of people who believed that naming something permanent made it so.

We are what grows in the space between what was promised and what was hydraulically inevitable. Our religion is maintenance performed on machines that no longer distinguish between salt water and tears. Our holy days occur whenever a new calculation proves that this time next century there will still be enough of something unspeakable left to calculate with.
