They call themselves the children of the third lung, though this is a simplicity invented for visitors who still speak in nouns. Among themselves, they are the pulse between inhalations, the pause where drowning becomes breathing becomes something else entirely.

They shimmer. Not metaphorically—their forms hold too much water to reflect light properly anymore. Walking through Tanakkerai's metastable markets, you notice them sideways: a refraction where there should be solidity, a flock of impossible shadows cast by no single sun. Their bones grew porous first, then hollow, then dissolved entirely into chambers that hold both air and memory in perfect osmotic balance. Now they move through the city's verticality as fish move through coral, ignoring distinctions between floor and ceiling that died with the old directions.

Their infants emerge—not born, never born—in the tidal pools between midnight and the first whale-knock of morning. The mother (a word that means something like anchor-point, something like harbor) swims to the chosen pool alone and begins the dissolution song, her body releasing the carefully hoarded minerals of nine months' recycling. Gradually—everything here happens gradually except for when it happens all at once—her outline blurs at the edges, becoming first a woman-shaped ripple, then a bloom of phosphorescence, then finally a small galaxy of potential children spinning in the saltwater womb of herself.

Most scatter, become part of the city's perpetual recycling. Always the city takes more than it gives back. But sometimes—rarely, precisely—one cluster of possibility holds its shape against the hungry water long enough to learn hunger itself. This one, the mother-vortex remembers into singularity, sings into solidity sings into self. The new child emerges neither liquid nor solid but liquid-crystal, clear except for the branching fractals of parental memory etched faintly through its translucent flesh like frost on glass that dreamed of being a family tree.

They grow by accretion, not growth. Each day they rise with the mist-tide and drift through the city's breathing passages, filtering nutrients from the air-water interface with specialized cells that line their once-mouths. Dust settles on their skin and stays, becoming slowly incorporated into their changing forms—here a shard of mica from the drowned clocktower's face, there a fragment of coral grown in the shape of a forgotten prayer. By adulthood—adults being those who have had three dreams they can remember and forgotten two deaths they can't—they have become living museums of their city's decline, walking archives of rubbed-off meanings and accidentally preserved moments.

They speak in three layers simultaneously: the surface layer of bubbles and current-shifts that carry factual meaning (where is the nearest oxygen-farmkraken disturbed the north stair avoid until compline); the middle layer of chemical signatures that flavor their exhalations with emotional context (trust this traveler/they carry old grief/their metal sings of distant fires); and the deep layer transmitted directly through conductivity whenever their skin brushes yours—the layer no visitor has ever described accurately because description itself requires boundaries, requires a you and a me and some space between that can hold words.

In this deep speech, they share memories like other species share saliva. To stand shoulder-to-shoulder with one at the evening planting—as the whole community rises together to scatter the day's collection of lost words and found feathers into the city's filtration roots—is to suddenly remember your grandmother's hands teaching you to weave seaweed into nets, even if you never had a grandmother, even if you've never woven anything more complex than lies.

They experience time as water experiences gravity: always moving toward lower concentrations of order, but choosing their paths through the available chaos with great deliberation and beauty. A dispute between neighbors might progress across three seasons and seven generations of cultivated coral, each party expressing their arguments through subtle adjustments to the city's growing patterns until the architecture itself grows too awkward for continued disagreement and simply collapses into a new configuration more amenable to mutual survival.

Death, when it comes—and it always comes, eventually, when an individual's memory-load grows too dense for continued mobility—they call "joining the deep archive." The dying one swims slowly downward through the descending layers of the city's history, releasing memories in reverse order as they go. Surface memories go first: grocery lists and gossip and the pattern of light on a lover's skin that particular Tuesday. Below that: the accumulated skills of a lifetime, how to coax air from algae and forgiveness from coral. Deeper: the inherited wisdom of their line, handed down through thousands of such dissolutions, modified by thousands of such emergences. At the bottom, finally: the original memories, the first memories, the ones that came with the mother's mother's dissolution into possibility and have been carried ever since like ballast or treasure or both.

In the deepest pool, where the pressure of accumulated history equals the pressure of the rising ocean outside, they finally release even these. For a moment—measurable only in the freezing of a single water molecule as it remembers being ice—they become nothing but pure potential again. Clean. Empty. Ready.

Then the city breathes in, and they are gone.

Except not entirely gone. The next child born in that season will bear a faint echo of their pattern somewhere in their crystal flesh. The coral beds will grow a little differently in that quarter, slightly better adapted to filtering the particular grief-protein that person specialized in metabolizing. And if you swim through the archive pools on the anniversary of their joining and hold your breath until memory and water become indistinguishable in your lungs, for just an instant you might hear them singing—one voice among millions, adding their note to the city's endless composition of adaptation and forgetting and trying again.

They are not happy, because happiness is a surface phenomenon, requires air pockets and stable reference points and other luxuries lost long ago. But they are not unhappy either. They are busy. They are becoming. They have work to do, pressure to equalize, future ruins to seed with possibility. The Abbess of Falling Things drops her daily seed, and the ocean rises to meet it, and between these two impossible acts of hope the children of the third lung conduct their continuous conversion of drowning into architecture into memory into something else altogether that doesn't have a name yet but will, it will, give them time—

They have all the time in the world, now. The world is mostly time, these days. And water. And them, learning the difference between the two by gradually becoming neither.
