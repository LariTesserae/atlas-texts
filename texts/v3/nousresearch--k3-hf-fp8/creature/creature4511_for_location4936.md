**The Choirtown Raftcradle**

Inhabitancy here is less occupation than negotiation—a slow recitation between breath and echo where to exist is to audition perpetually for the privilege of being heard. The living are numbered carefully: 539 adults who have learned to walk with their weight slightly to the left to compensate for the town's eternal three-degree lean; 22 elders who've thinned to semi-audibility, their voices preserved in the fogging edges of windows where widow's frost blooms nightly; 113 children counted in fractions of future sound-effects—a girl measured as three-fifths laughter waiting to peak, a boy listed as one-quarter whistle not yet learned, their growth monitored by the Tapestry Guild embroiderers who unravel and reweave childhood reports monthly to match census projections.

Between these footnoted citizens dwell the interstitials—the reasons the census keeps rounding down. The woman who became a comma crab infestation after editing her sister's obituary too ruthlessly, her thoughts now emerging sideways through other people's pauses. The shoemaker who fed his voice to fuguecats piece by piece until customers must imagine their fittings, leaving footprints of potential shoes that fade before payment. The entire third generation of the Bellweather family who voted themselves into becoming a leaky section of plumbing during the Great Silence Tax of '83; now they express opinions through evening pressure drops and the seasonal flavor of drinking water (this month: regrets over summer romances with a hint of pipe dreams).

Occupying the gaps between gaps, the Choir proper—those who completed the vow of Audibility and dissolved into the town's mnemonic architecture. They live in the precise shape of the silence after questions unanswered too long, manifesting as choirs of ill-fitting boots down empty corridors, as congregations of coughs during speeches that forget their purpose, as the collective memory of every goodbye rehearsed but never spoken that gathers at dusk in the Singing Pit to practice becoming hymns. New members are recruited from the spaces between sentences spoken in attics, from the breath held between "I" and "love" in letters left unmailed, from the pause that follows "it was nothing" when both participants know it was everything.

Even death negotiates residency here. The deceased are not buried but edited into collective memory—a carefully orchestrated fade achieved by gradually decreasing variants of their stories in daily conversation, their possessions loaned out until all associations dilute. Except for the Unremembered: those who die mid-sneeze during the Soundful Number reconciliation, whose final sounds spike the census meters and temporarily elevate them to legend status. They become permanent echoes trapped in specific locations—the reason one must always knock twice before entering the Confluence Library's west wing (lest you interrupt the Librarian Who Never Finished Clearing His Throat), why children avoid skipping the seventh step on Staircase Thursday (out of respect for Anna Three-Quarters-Tickles who fell upstairs laughing in perpetuity), why no one completes the punchline of the frog joke in the Slow Moon Tavern (because Mrs. Gibbons's son who would have invented the perfect ending drowned before his punchline could crest adulthood).

And underneath, below the Rootlaw's severed stone—that last forbidden contact where the mountain's amputated heart hangs like a promise unspoken—something else occupies the silence beneath silence. The town's dreams, perhaps. Or the world's second ideas, the ones discarded before invention. Or possibly the original inhabitants, the pre-Choir who first hollowed the mountain, now grown patient as continents, waiting for the conversation above to finally pause long enough to remember: that every place lifted in song eventually yearns for the ground it left behind. But don't speak of them. The lawyers like their comas where they can see them, and some negotiations are best left unfinished.

For now, space itself waits on the housing list, renting rooms by the metaphor while applying for permanent residency in the pauses between your breaths. The audition continues every time you inhale without meaning to keep this place real. Try not to sneeze. There are children counting on your silence to grow into complete thoughts someday.
