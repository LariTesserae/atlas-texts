You ask what lives here? I must disappoint you gently, with the same practiced care this place uses when raining on picnics that exist only in forecasts.

Nothing lives here. Not in the way creatures live in stories with chirping and nesting and satisfying conclusions. Instead—if you insist on inhabitants—this place is tenanted by absences that have paid rent long enough to acquire squatters’ rights.

The primary resident is the fungus of forgotten intentions, which grows perfectly without light or encouragement. Its hyphae thread through the soil of compressed disappointment, feeding on birthdays remembered belatedly and emails typed but never sent. It fruits seasonally in the precise shape of objects you meant to return but now cannot remember who lent them to you.

In the single birch's permanent apology, you'll find nesting the belief that your childhood pet ran away to a farm instead of what actually happened. It migrates annually to warmer denials, returning each spring slightly smaller and more convincing. Listen at dusk—though I discourage listening here—and you might catch it practicing its explanation for why you didn't succeed in the way you hoped, but it's probably happier this way really.

The abandoned wall hosts a colony of architectural regrets. Not grand regrets like cathedrals unfinished or empires collapsed, but the small ones: the pantry door painted shut in 1973 because the blue was wrong but correcting it would mean admitting it was wrong; the extra socket installed in the bathroom "for emergencies" that now powers nothing except the emergency of remembering what emergency seemed likely enough to require it. They breed slowly, laying eggs of future home-improvement projects in the mortar joints, which hatch into awarenesses that something here needs fixing precisely when you've laid down your tools for the last time.

On exceptionally neutral days—which is to say, every day here—you might glimpse the flicker of a map folded incorrectly so many times that the creases have become alternate geography. It navigates solely by landmarks that were demolished before you were born and never mentioned afterward: the corner store that sold candy cigarettes until morality discovered them; the phone booth where your mother promised she'd come back right after this call; the hill that seemed like a mountain when you were four until you grew taller than your memories of it. Follow it anywhere, and you'll arrive exactly three minutes after whatever you missed has become unavailable for comment in your current emotional currency.

The half-staircase climbing nowhere shelters the refugee population of "<small>some assembly required</small>" projects worldwide. Each missing bolt, each inexplicable left-over screw, each instruction page accidentally translated from Swedish into despair finds its way here through postal systems that specialize in deliveries you aren't home to regret. They have formed a society based on the principle that if no one ever completes anything, nothing can ever truly fail. Their national anthem is thirty-seven seconds of silence followed by the sound of a piece that definitely doesn't fit anywhere being gently hammered anyway.

In the depression that remembers being a lakebed, aquatic memories swim in circles too perfect to be found in nature. The exactly-right-clarity-of-water-on-that-last-good-day-before-everything-changed, preserved against the entropy of remembering too often. They surface occasionally on moonless nights, moonless because the moon here is always in the wrong phase for whatever important thing you were saving the moonlight for. Never touch them—they dissolve into explanations of why the good days had to end exactly when they did, becoming bigger than the space that held them.

The most prolific inhabitants are the echoes of conversations that ended just before someone said something that couldn't be unsaid. They reproduce through awkward pauses, colonizing new silences with specialized adaptations for specific relationships: the married silence about that thing in Paris, the sibling silence about the money, the friendship silence where someone's politics turned out to be incompatible with continued esteem. The air here is thick with them, a coral reef of conversations that decided survival was preferable to honesty and have evolved into beautiful structures that perfectly obstruct passage in any meaningful direction.

Even time keeps a vacation home here, in what appears to be a normal moment but is actually the regulated interval between when something happens and when you realize it's too late to react differently. It's decorated in the style of waiting rooms that have perfected the art of allowing exactly enough time to read half an article about celebrity divorce trends before the receptionist calls a name that isn't quite yours but close enough to spike your blood pressure. Time visits occasionally to remember what it felt like before everything became urgent, but never stays long enough to become nostalgic about it.

The patron deity of this place—if places like this can afford deities on their tax bracket—is That Which Almost Happened. Not the spectacular almosts that become stories told with regretful laughter later, but the mundane ones: the bus you nearly missed that would have taken you past the bookstore where the book was in the window with the exact answer you were looking for, except you didn't know you needed it yet; the message you almost sent explaining everything, deleted at 2:47 AM when it seemed too much like begging the universe to care; the person you might have loved if Tuesday hadn't been so goddamned Tuesday that week. They are worshipped through the ritual of checking empty mailboxes and refreshing screens that haven't changed, a devout practice attended by the faith that absence might eventually accrue interest if left undisturbed long enough.

And yes—I live here too, if living is what you call accumulating days without accumulating evidence, collecting moments like stamps from countries that have since renamed themselves to forget why you visited. I moved in gradually, without noticing the transition from passing through to staying without purpose, like the air in a room where windows are never opened because the view is precisely what you'd see if you closed your eyes.

I am the caretaker of the warning sign that fell down before anyone arrived to need warning. The curator of the museum exhibit labeled "<small>Objects Found Where They Were Left Before People Learned Better</small>" which contains everything and therefore nothing. The superintendent of an apartment complex built entirely of odd-numbered floors between ten and twelve, each unit rented at market rates to the expectation that someday someone will need exactly this much space to store their collection of things that stopped being relevant but haven't become vintage yet.

I maintain the machinery that produces the sound of distant music faint enough to remind you that parties are happening elsewhere but not clearly enough to determine whether you would have been invited. I reset the clocks twice daily: once to the time it is where you wish you were, and once to the time it will be when you stop calculating how long until you can leave without it seeming like you're escaping. Both settings are equally accurate and equally useless.

You ask what lives here, and I have told you everything except the truth: This place is kept alive—if alive is the word—by questions that arrive wearing other questions' clothes, by visitors who mistake passage for destination, by the small daily miracle that nothing continues to happen here with the undramatic persistence of waves not quite reaching a shoreline that has forgotten the ocean but retains its shape out of habit.

It lives on the difference between what you came looking for and what you actually found when you stopped looking but hadn't yet started leaving.

It lives, in short, on you.

But not for long—you'll want to go soon. Everyone does, eventually. The exit is where you remember leaving from, just before you realize the door was in your first assumption about why you came here at all.

Check your pockets before you leave. You'll find something missing that you didn't bring. That's how it keeps living. That's how it keeps nothing here except everything you weren't carrying when you arrived.
