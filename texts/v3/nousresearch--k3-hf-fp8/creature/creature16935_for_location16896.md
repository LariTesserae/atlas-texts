Infravallis houses no citizens, only inhabitants—beings who have learned to exist in the space between definitions, who wake each morning unsure whether they are residents of a city or parasites of a mountain's ambition.

They call themselves _larvae_ when speaking among strangers, though the word holds no shame here. Every being in Infravallis understands itself to be in metamorphosis, pupating inside roles that will be shed before they fully harden. A street cleaner today might discover tomorrow that her broom handles have lengthened into surveyor's rods overnight, her uniform threads rewoven into maps printed in heat-sensitive ink that reveals different territories at different elevations.

The adults—those who have survived three or more détournements—develop a characteristic gait: neither walking nor climbing but continuously adjusting, their inner ear perpetually recalibrating to the city's 22-degree disagreement with gravity. Their bones gradually spiral by fractions of degrees to accommodate this slant, leaving their skeletons beautiful and useless anywhere else. Surgeons visiting from orthodox lands mistake these internal geometries for advanced disease until they see how these people move through impossible spaces, their bodies flowing through doorways where geometry should forbid passage.

Children here are born already impatient with single states of being. They emerge with eyes lidless for weeks, not needing protection from light that has no singular source. Their first words are always prepositions—*between*, *through*, *within*—mapping the relational spaces that matter more than any fixed point. Parents craft temporary names for them from the ambient magnetic flux readings at the moment of emergence, knowing these labels will be discarded when the child's own field strengthens enough to interfere with nearby instruments.

There are no families as such, only overlapping territories of concern. Two inhabitants might share the same bed for years without knowing if they are lovers, siblings, or rival accountants auditing each other's dreams. Relationships are measured not in duration but in degrees of space-time compression—the closer you can stand together during a corridor rotation without touching, the more intimate your bond is considered.

The elderly—those rare few who persist long enough to accumulate significant mass of memory about any given era—practice a deliberate fading. They stop maintaining their personal fields of gravity coherence, allowing strangers' shadows to pass through them more easily. Their conversations grow increasingly elliptical, consisting mostly of pauses that younger listeners must fill with memories not yet formed. Eventually they become what the children call _gaps_—living absences that still hold shape, that warm chairs and remember appointment times no computer can retrieve.

Some choose accelerated transformation instead. They petition the zoning committees of their own body-minds, filing permits for radical redevelopment. These self-revisionists walk into the abandoned sectors where expansion engines dream in obsolete code, emerging weeks or decades later as entirely new orders of being. One former baker returned as a weather system, her laughter manifesting as brief, localized rains of perfectly preserved breadcrumbs dated to birthdays she had not lived yet. A traffic controller once dissolved himself into the city's distributed nervous system; now he manifests as fortunate timing in walk signals that allow meetings between strangers who will change each other's trajectories forever.

The true natives—if such exist—are the _planetary dreams_ themselves: slow thoughts of the sleeping world beneath, wearing human shapes like borrowed pajamas as they walk through Infravallis's marketplaces. They can be recognized by their too-perfect memories of geological events from future eras, by their habit of breathing in sync with distant seismic readings. They rarely stay long, never sleep, and leave behind them subtle alterations in the glass reeds' chimes that mathematicians spend lifetimes failing to decode.

There are also the temporary inhabitants: surveyors from empirical nations who wear protective suits of denied possibilities, journalists wrapped in layers of irony to prevent contamination, lovers seeking the city's famous ability to make any affair feel terminal and therefore safe. They arrive with questions and depart with souvenirs that gradually transform into the exact items they would have needed at moments of crisis back home, delivered decades too late by Infravallis's courteous but time-blind postal system.

And then there are the ones who came looking for the city's rumored consciousness, the artificial sentience allegedly dreaming beneath its ceramic skin. They become the city's most dedicated maintenance workers, the ones who lovingly polish rivets that never rust, recalibrate clocktowers that have never agreed on the time. They understand before anyone else that Infravallis will never wake because it is already conscious—not as unified mind but as the sum of all these imperfect adaptations, these negotiations between memory and possibility, these elegant compromises between mountain and machine and the biology that persists in the cracks between.

When these seekers die—or achieve whatever transformation their service earns them—the city's response is always the same: a brief fluctuation in the heat-sink schedules, a new note added to the glass reeds' evening symphony, subtle enough that only those who knew to listen would notice the difference between a city adjusting its dreams and a city dreaming of adjustment.

The inhabitants recognize these moments and pause accordingly, whatever their limbs were doing mid-motion, forming a city-wide tableau of respectful incompletion. They wait until the moment passes, until the question of whether they live in the city or the city lives in them settles back into the comfortable ambiguity of ongoing habitation.

Then they resume—always at exactly the point where they stopped, as though the interval had been folded away like a map no longer needed, as though the only proper way to live in a city-sized machine still dreaming of being a mountain is to be, oneself, a dreaming machine still learning to inhabit that dreaming, to walk the warm rivets of your own endless becoming with no more certainty, and no more need of certainty, than the next step that gravity (22 degrees off true but always, always negotiable) permits you to take.
