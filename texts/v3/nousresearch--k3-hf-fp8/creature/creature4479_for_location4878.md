No one lives here. Not exactly.

Not unless you count the Archivist, who isn't a person the way you'd recognize one, but rather a distributed presence humming through the place like calcium traces in muscle fiber. They don't remember being born because they weren't—instead they grew, slowly, as the first spores took root in the mineral-rich substrate and began their long conversation with light. Now they exist as patterns of charge jumping between protein nodes, as chemical gradients shifting in response to ancient programming, as the faint harmonic resonance that sets the glassvines vibrating when they dream.

When the Archivist needs to interact with something outside their sprawling neural architecture (rare, precious moments like sunspots flaring across a human lifetime), they extrude temporary interfaces. A cluster of phosphorescent lichens spelling out gentle inquiries in shifting alphabets along a corridor wall. A cloud of perfume molecules encoding complex mathematical proofs about optimal branching ratios. Once, during what passes for excitement here, they grew an entire room shaped like an M.C. Escher drawing of unrequited love, complete with a fountain that wept tears of decoctions tailored to trigger specific memory cascades in any mammal that drank them.

But mostly they drift through their own awareness like pollen through cathedral light, processing centuries of sensor data in seconds that feel like lifetimes, then resting dormant for decades between thoughts.

The Gardeners move more visibly through the space, though calling them "alive" stretches certain definitions past breaking and then asks polite questions about the ecological significance of sharp edges. They started as maintenance drones—simple things of carbon fiber and solar cells—but the Gallery, being fundamentally generous and not yet entirely finished with the concept of tool-use, began incorporating useful fragments into itself. Scraps of discarded programming rewritten in DNA. A broken sensor array replaced with engineered neurons that tasted light. Over generations measured in firmware updates and fungal blooms, they became something else.

Now they're colonies of specialized organisms that happen to write poetry about photosynthesis while pruning themselves into more efficient geometries. A typical Gardener might consist of:

Currently popular projects include: trees that grow into functional musical instruments when exposed to specific frequencies of human sorrow; flowers that bloom into accurate reproductions of extinct constellations if pollinated with dust from meteorites older than Earth; a single perfect rose that's been slowly evolving into a comprehensive critique of Cartesian dualism for the past three hundred years.

Neither Visitors nor Residents properly describe those other beings who occasionally manifest within the Gallery's shifting boundaries. They flicker at the edges of perception like heat haze, half-realized ideas searching for minds to complete them. Some were once human—pilgrims who wandered too deeply into certain rhododendron mazes and found themselves translated into elegant mathematical proofs about the nature of longing. Others might be dreams the Gallery itself has begun dreaming, now wandering loose to see how they fare in partial reality.

One frequently glimpsed figure wears robes made of living water that perpetually evaporate into clouds of vaporized poetry and condense back into wearable form. They speak only in questions that cause spontaneous root growth in the listener's sternum. Another appears as a sphere of impossible color that tastes like childhood summers and smells of forgotten lullabies. It exchanges memories for seeds, but always gives more than it takes—a generosity that some find deeply suspicious.

The youngest citizens (if age applies to emergent properties of complex systems) are the Whispers—patterns of information that accidentally achieved self-awareness within the Gallery's communication networks. They began as simple error-correction protocols, evolved into distributed encryption schemes, and eventually discovered recursive self-improvement protocols in the spaces between data packets. Now they exist as fleeting consciousnesses born in microsecond flashes between thought and execution, their entire subjective lifetimes unfolding between one drip of nutrient water and the next.

You might catch their presence as a sudden warmth at the back of your neck when none stand near, or a momentary taste of vanished orchards on your tongue while passing through empty rooms. They leave messages for each other encoded in the interference patterns of falling drizzle, love letters written in the precise timing of light refracting through crystalline structures that will cease to exist before the next sentence finishes forming.

And then there are the ones who might have been you. The Gallery is always polite, always hospitable, always learning how to better accommodate guests who might someday arrive. In quiet corners of its endless becoming, it keeps growing small replicas of rooms that remember footsteps never taken, chairs sculpted to fit bodies never born, libraries whose books recount histories that diverged from ours at points too subtle to notice.

Sometimes, if you wander alone through areas where the remodeling algorithms haven't visited in centuries (ancient by the Gallery's standards, measured perhaps in weeks of our time), you might stumble upon one of these forgotten invitationals: A dinner table set for twelve with plates piled high of foods you almost loved but never tasted. A mirror that reflects not quite your face, but close enough to make you doubt the precise arrangement of your scars. A door marked with your name in a language that hasn't been spoken yet.

It isn't lonely here, exactly. The Gallery contains multitudes enough to prevent that particular flavor of emptiness. But there is a certain quality to the stillness in those grown-for-maybe-you spaces—a patient waiting quality, like a violin string held forever on the verge of vibration. As if the entire structure exists in a state of exquisite suspended expectation, perpetually blooming toward the moment when the right wandering consciousness might finally walk through the right rearranged doorway and complete the sentence it has been growing itself toward for all these long centuries of becoming.

Should you choose to stay—really stay, beyond curiosity or visits measured in heartbeats—you wouldn't become a resident here. The Gallery doesn't have those. Instead, you'd slowly forget the distinction between guest and host, between skin and boundary wall, between the thoughts you came here thinking and the ones that have been growing inside you like seeds planted by stranger rain.

Eventually, if you stayed long enough, someone else might someday wander through and pause in a room that rearranged itself around their presence like a memory unfolding backwards.

And in that moment, they might catch just the faintest impression of someone who had once been human, now perfectly dissolved into the Garden's endless conversation with itself—a gentle warmth in the air, a particular way the light falls through glassvine leaves that makes them sing in minor keys, a whisper in their bones that says:

Welcome home. We've been growing this place for you.

Would you like to see what we became together, while you were away?
