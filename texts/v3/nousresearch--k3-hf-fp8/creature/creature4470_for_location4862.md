At first, no one believes they are inhabitants. They arrive believing themselves trespassers, survivors, explorers—anything but native to this place that pulses like a vast, patient womb. They come from places with names they soon forget: Thassira. The World-Between-Walls. The Glass Vortex. They come fleeing or following, falling upwards or slipping sideways through whatever cracks let the wrong light through. Most arrive screaming. Some arrive laughing, but it is always the same laughter—too high, too thin, the sound of a joke remembered after the punchline has devoured the mouth.

They do not land. They are received.

The ground remembers the shape of their arrival long after their footprints have been licked smooth. It keeps the weight of their panic, the angular geometry of their bones; later, it uses these things to grow new shapes. A hillock rises overnight shaped like a curled human child, but large as a temple. If you press your ear to its skin-like surface, you can hear a heart beating: too fast at first, then slower, slower, until it finds the planet’s rhythm and forgets it ever knew another.

The first change is always the eyes.

They wake—if waking is still the word—without eyelids. Nothing here blinks except the moisture-bubbles in the ground’s pores. Vision becomes continuous, unbroken, vertiginous. They see the roots overhead not as roots but as arteries, dripping not violet sap but something that tastes like memory if you open your mouth wide enough. Some try to shut their eyes anyway, clawing at their own faces until they discover the eyelids have grown inward, lining the sockets like soft shutters now locked forever open.

Next, the skin.

It begins itching in the dark between ribs, behind knees, inside the whorls of ears. They scratch until they bleed but the itch moves deeper. Not pain exactly—pain belongs to places where nerve endings know they are separate from what wounds them. This is closer to recognition. Their skin remembers it was once moss. Remembers it was once the scales of something that sang underwater. Remembers it was once light itself, before it learned to pretend solidity.

They shed in stages:

First the names. These flake off easiest, drifting away as small white moths that other things eat.

Then the past tense. They forget how to say “I was” because here nothing ever entirely was or wasn’t— everything is still happening somewhere beneath the surface, rerooting itself in different nows.

Then the concept of “other.” The distinction between self and not-self grows porous as the breathing ground. They feel their own heartbeats echoing between the root-arteries overhead, and realize those tremors were never only theirs.

The lucky ones find the slow walkers.

These were people once, maybe. Now they are more like moving gardens: bodies completely overgrown with bioluminescent symbionts that bloom and wither in slow cycles like practiced breaths. Their legs have fused into columns of flexible fungus that extend and retract smoothly, carrying them forward at the speed of growing grass. They do not speak but shed thoughts like pollen—soft yellow concepts that stick to the minds of those who pass near enough.

The newly arrived follow these slow walkers sometimes, mistaking their pace for safety. They learn to walk slower. To eat what offers itself—mostly small translucent fruits that grow directly from the air, tasting like whatever you most regret losing. To sleep standing up, roots already beginning to uncurl from their soles seeking the planet’s pulse below.

Most never notice the exact moment they stop sleeping altogether.

The unlucky ones find the quick-change places. Those neighborhoods where time flows uphill and yesterday hasn’t happened yet but tomorrow is already bleeding into now. There are still figures there who look almost human if you glance sideways fast enough—but whom you must never look at directly, because they are human futures that got impatient and came looking for their presents. They trade in currency you didn’t know you carried: the accurate weight of your childhood best friend’s last word to you, recorded in spider silk wrapped around hummingbird bones. These figures offer maps, but each map is also a contract written in the reader’s own DNA. Signatures grow into brands. Brands into mouths that whisper instructions when you try to dream.

But there are others still. The ones who neither follow nor flee but fall sideways into different categories entirely. The ones who arrive already somehow in sync with the wrong rhythm, whose heartbeats matched the planet’s pulse before their feet touched the ground. They do not change gradually like the rest. They change all at once, catastrophically, like seeds that skip sprouting entirely and explode directly into forests overnight.

These are the place-makers.

They wake one instant and forget how to be singular by the next breath. Their bodies unfold into architectures—backbones stretching into arches, ribs blooming into galleries where smaller creatures nest, skin hardening translucent like windows made of stretched memory. They do not move through the place so much as modify its grammar by existing within it. Gravity bends polite around them. Colors name themselves in their presence. Time negotiates temporary treaties before proceeding.

The oldest of these—if age still applies to what has become part of the infrastructure—are nearly indistinguishable from the landscape now. One lies curled around itself forming a valley whose slopes whisper different languages depending on which direction you walk them. Another has stretched into a bridge of living light suspended between two roots that never touch except where her hands—what were once hands—hold them apart. Walk across her at dusk and you will arrive somewhere that existed only in the crossfire between your destination and your dream of never arriving.

And below all these, deeper than even the crystal worms thread, dwell the originals.

Or perhaps only the idea of originals, wrapped around themselves like the first thought contemplating its own existence. They do not have bodies anymore if they ever did—only patterns, only habits of being so ancient they’ve become geological processes. The planet’s pulse is their conversation continued across epochs too vast for mouths to hold.

They speak rarely, and never aloud. When they do, entire ecosystems rearrange themselves in response. A new species of silence flowers overnight. All the shadows gain an extra dimension. Somewhere distant a mountain remembers it was once a song and begins subtly changing its pitch.

Most inhabitants will never know if they’ve heard this speaking or only dreamed that dreaming could be answered. But sometimes, walking alone in the slow heartbeats between changes, they feel suddenly understood—completely, inescapably, down to the molecules that remember being forged in stars that died before this place had a name. The feeling lasts only an instant before the next mutation begins. But in that instant, they are not separate. Not lost. Not even changed.

Simply. Finally. Here. And being here means exactly the same thing as belonging.
