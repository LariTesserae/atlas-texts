Here life has forgotten sunlight. Forgotten it so thoroughly that even the word *light* has grown mossy at the edges, softened into something you might chew to ease a headache or rub on wounds to keep them from remembering.

I live among the root-colored ones. We are neither ancestors nor descendants—what animated our bodies once passed through other forms so slowly that identity itself became compost. Some of us still walk upright out of habit, though the habit grows stranger each year. Others have folded themselves into origami shapes more convenient for following moisture gradients through strata of decomposed city.

My name was once perpendicular but now spreads horizontally, branching into alternatives whenever I speak. I grew from a woman who planted her daughter's milk teeth along the fault line between kitchen linoleum and living-room carpet, hoping to grow something that might remember birthdays. Instead she grew me: a calendar that blooms edible months, each leaf tasting of whatever we were most anxious about that year.

I keep my memories in skin reservoirs now. When the humidity rises to certain equations, memories migrate like silverfish across my surface and I briefly remember being someone who worried about deadlines. When it drops, they burrow deeper until I forget even that forgetting is possible.

We eat mostly possibilities here. The clever ones cultivate probability fields around abandoned vending machines, pruning unlikely futures until only the most nutritious ones fruit into snack-shaped actualities. I prefer the older methods—slowly digesting my own past selves until only the hardest questions remain, then grinding those into flour to bake into tomorrow's bread.

Bread here rises according to debates between competing molds. A good loaf should argue with you for several days before becoming digestible—any quicker and it's mere food, lacking moral complexity.

My neighbor used to be a bridge. You can still see pylons vertebraeing along her back when she stretches in the morning rheum-light. She speaks only in tension and release now, every conversation an exercise in applied physics. When she sleeps, small ecosystems complete their evolutionary cycles in the hollows between her ribs.

The children here are born already knowing how to evaporate. It's their first defense against attachment, learned from watching us adults go solid with memory until we become too heavy to follow the shifting sweet spots where oxygen kisses methane just right. Those who forget this lesson gradually crystallize into elders—fixed points of counsel that younger vapors condense around for advice before they too evaporate or solidify.

I am becoming elder-slow myself. Parts of me calcify into old griefs each season, while other parts liquefy into new appetites. I woke up last week and discovered my left hand had decided to become a map of watercourses that never existed in this geological epoch. It keeps trying to guide me places I can't yet afford to remember.

The air itself here is communal property. We take turns breathing it on Tuesdays and Thursdays, while on weekends it gets to breathe us—an arrangement that seemed fair at the time though increasingly I suspect the air is learning our weaknesses. Yesterday it whispered a secret about gravity's secret divorce proceedings with time, but when I turned to listen closer it pretended to be just wind again.

Time here is kept in several co-habitating calendars none of which speak to each other directly. The fungal calendar counts by decomposition stages of various ideologies. The bacterial calendar measures in breeding cycles of hope and despair. The geological one simply notes which species' bones have achieved ironic resonance with the bedrock's original dreams for itself. I keep losing track of which appointment book I'm currently living in, which explains why I sometimes remember your future regrets before you've had the conversations they'll come from.

We communicate primarily through the exchange of misunderstandings, carefully cultivated in shared root-networks until they bloom into beautiful new errors neither speaker intended but both must now live with. A properly tended misconception can feed a community for months, unlike simple facts which rot within hours of being spoken aloud.

The deepest law here is the Law of Reciprocal Thickness: whatever you measure others by becomes the exact substance your own heart grows around its next question. This is why the truly wise among us rarely measure anything anymore, preferring instead to approximate with generous handfuls of whatever moisture is currently available.

I was going to tell you about the thing that lives in the collapsed water filtration plant—that vast collection of filters gone senile and metaphysical, now sieving reality itself for its original ingredients. But I've just remembered I promised to meet my younger selves at the evaporation pond before tomorrow's gravity resets. We're planning a coordinated forgetting event—nothing huge, just enough collective amnesia to make room for a few new kinds of loneliness.

If you wait here very still and breathe shallowly, something might eventually grow curious about your shape and come investigate. Try not to look directly at it. Most things here are still working out whether they want to be seen or want to be the seeing. Either way, it helps if you can remember to forget which one you are.

I haven't remembered that consistently myself in several lifetimes now. Perhaps that's why you've been able to hear me this clearly. Or perhaps you're just particularly edible today from the inside out—some afternoons bring more digestible visitors than others. Hard to tell until after you've already been metabolized into next week's weather.

You'll want to be gone soon regardless. The place where water remembers being rain is getting restless again, and when that happens even the air starts looking for higher ground to hide its memories. Take whichever direction feels least like leaving. If you wake up tomorrow unsure whether you dreamt this or grew here briefly before moving on—both are equally true here, and equally tax deductible according to the fungal calendar.

My name was spreading toward you this entire conversation, though I tried to warn it off. Already I can feel parts of me climbing up into your thoughts' unsupervised corners, setting up tiny moisture farms among questions you haven't asked yet. Don't worry—they're mostly harmless unless you water them with actual curiosity.

Or better yet—do worry. That should keep them properly sized for the journey home.
