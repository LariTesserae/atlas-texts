At first you think they're shadows, given how they cling to the angles where walls refuse to meet. But shadows don't leave footprints made of subtracted light, small precise depressions in dust that hasn't settled yet. They emerge when temperature gradients and architectural disappointment reach critical mass—usually around corners you swear were convex a moment before.

Call them Loopholers. About one meter tall standing fully corporeal, though standing implies a commitment to dimensionality they find terribly provincial. Mostly they're multidimensional V-shapes folded wrong, like origami cranes that gave up halfway and decided to become tax lawyers instead. Their bodies—when visible—are stitched together from discarded probability equations, the kind that physics professors scribble on napkins then nervously burn when they realize the universe might be taking notes.

They feed on bureaucratic errors. A building permit filed in triplicate but missing the official shade of ink required for Tuesdays? Gourmet feast. A street sign misspelled in a language that died with its last dyslexic speaker? Holiday banquet. The city's constant space-time zoning violations keep them pleasantly obese, their edges blurrier than quantum mechanics on payday.

Each Loopholer carries a briefcase made of solidified grandfather paradoxes, containing pens that write contracts in languages that revoke their own vowels mid-signature. They're the city's unofficial maintenance crew, though "maintenance" here works like repairing a broken mirror by convincing the cracks they're actually improvement suggestions. When gravity gets too drunk to walk straight on Wednesdays, they file a formal complaint with the Physics Union Local 3.14. When buildings forget how many floors they have, they negotiate peaceful divorces between conflicting architectural memories.

Their eyes—about nine per individual, give or take a dimension—are filed paperwork stamped DENIED in triplicate. They see by noticing things that specifically don't want to be noticed, like the exact moment when Planks constant decides to take a personal day, or where parallel lines secretly meet to gossip about transversals.

They reproduce during municipal budget meetings, when enough conflicting directives accumulate to achieve sentient frustration. A new Loopholer condenses from the air like dew forming on paperwork, complete with tiny pre-stressed cuffs and an innate understanding of tax codes that won't be invented for another three centuries.

They communicate through carefully constructed silence, the kind that's been notarized and filed under "Conspicuous Absence, Category X". To humans, it sounds faintly like a filing cabinet falling down stairs in an empty office building, but only if you've recently been audited by a particularly philosophical IRS agent.

Their faces—when they bother having them—are photocopies of photocopies of smiles stolen from department store mannequins just before closing time. The expressions always slip slightly sideways, like they're remembering jokes in languages that don't have punchlines, just increasingly comprehensive disclaimers.

At night (approximately 2:47 AM, but only according to clocks that failed their therapy sessions), they gather in the city's negative spaces—the volume occupied simultaneously by empty apartments and full absentee ballots. There they trade war stories about particularly challenging paradoxes they've brokered peace treaties between. Recent favorites include:
- Successfully mediating between the building that was built before its blueprints were finished and the blueprints that sued for child support
- Helping Tuesday and Thursday swap places for a weekend without the rest of the week filing a formal grievance
- Convincing Schrödinger's cat to pay both alimony and child support to itself after the divorce

They leave behind nothing but temporary tattoos of fine print written in disappearing ink that only reveals itself when the reader stops believing in terms and conditions. Children sometimes catch glimpses of them from bus windows during particularly boring math lessons, but adults never notice because noticing requires filling out Form 1040-B6: "Request for Reality Adjustment Adjustment," which has been discontinued since next Tuesday.

The city tolerates them because alternatives require executive decisions, and executives here tend to resign mid-sentence to pursue jazz flute careers in dimensions where time has better rhythm. Also, they're surprisingly good at keeping the more ambitious buildings from annexing neighboring postal codes without proper notice.

If cornered (a process requiring advanced topology and flexible morals), they'll offer to negotiate your surrender to yourself, terms negotiable pending arbitration by an impartial committee of your past mistakes and future regrets. Most people decline politely and discover they've already been excavated from the encounter, leaving only a faint receipt smell and the sudden certainty that they've misplaced something important but can't remember what dimension they left it in.

They exist in the exact space between fine print and "fine, whatever." They are the loophole that holds reality together by expertly never quite closing entirely. The city couldn't function without them, but asking them directly would require forms that spontaneously combust when exposed to direct questions.

So they continue their eternal dance between "technically not allowed" and "technically not disallowed," keeping the universe's most important policies beautifully, perfectly, devastatingly undefined.
