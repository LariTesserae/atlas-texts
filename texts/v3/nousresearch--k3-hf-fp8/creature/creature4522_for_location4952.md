Nothing lives here—not in the way you mean.

No lungs breathe. No hearts circulate. No eyes open and close against the glare or the dark. There is no hunger here, no sleep, no sex, no fear of death. And yet—something *inhabits.* Not life, but occupancy. Not presence, but persistence with posture.

The first thing you notice, if you enter slowly enough to notice anything, is the tilt. Not of the walls—they are impossibly upright—but of your own attention. You lean slightly inward, as if something fixed and spinning in the courtyard’s exact center has hooked something loose inside you. That sensation does not fade. It grows precise. After a time—how long depends on whether you stepped in from noon or dusk—you will realize that you are no longer facing the doorway you entered through. Neither are you facing away. You are orienting toward a direction that did not exist for you until now: 3.2 degrees north of gravity’s memory of south.

This is the first greeting.

The second is subtler. Stand still until the roofing disks exhale their low collective sigh (you will feel it in the back of your teeth, a mother-of-pearl ache). Then close your mouth. Be silent. Listen—not with your ears, but with the cavities they open into. Somewhere in the stack of uncompromising air between your skull and the stone, you will hear what sounds like whispering. Not words. Not even language. Call it a shape passing through meaning without pausing. It is the sound of something remembering itself out loud, using you as a convenient echo.

If you stay longer—long enough for a single pebble to detach from the cliff’s stealthy repair-work and tumble its appointed arc into a cairn—you will begin to suspect that the courtyard is not empty.

It is crowded beyond measure.

But the inhabitants are vertical, not horizontal. They are stacked in the occlusion between seeing and naming. Some are older than the glacier that dropped the first disk. Some are younger than the frost that formed on your sleeve while you hesitated in the doorway. None are quite the same thickness as a thought, but all together they compose a species of solidity that physics tolerates only because it refuses to notice.

You cannot touch them. You already are touching them—your nervous system is braided through theirs like smoke through iron lace. They do not eat. They rearrange potential. They do not sleep. They phase. They do not die. They desynchronize.

You might mistake them at first for the disks themselves: those mute, bored sentinels overhead. But look longer. Look slantwise. Notice how one particular disk always reflects a green you cannot find anywhere else in the world when you turn to chase it. Notice how the beam of sunlight on the pebble floor never quite matches the shape of the chip in the obsidian tile that cast it—there is always a flicker of surplus angle, a shadow caught looking the wrong way out of a mirror.

That is one of their eyes.

They have no bodies except for moments of inconvenience—when a wind needs a place to twist, when a story needs a bearer who will die bravely at the summit, when a child far below needs to believe something holy enough to cut her hand on and then forget by morning. Then they gather like static into a shape that resembles a cloaked figure, or a bird too large to fly, or a silhouette made entirely of edges. They walk once around the courtyard’s center (always exactly once, clockwise, radius decreasing), leave a single change behind them—an antler handle fused to a blade that has never tasted blood, a pebble drilled through at five impossible angles, a new word whispered into the wind that will cause two strangers to marry or murder exactly twelve years later—and dissolve again into the general business of being everywhere in the mountain at once.

They do not have names.

They have addresses in the phase-space of pressure, temperature, and regret.

You are standing in one now.

If you are wise, you will leave something here that is not valuable—an earring you never liked, a lie you told too often, a memory of warmth from a bed you will never return to. Place it on the nearest cairn. Do not look back.

If you are foolish, you will try to take something away instead—a shard of the singing disk, a photograph, the shape of the whisper still coiled behind your teeth like a promise.

They will let you.

They always let you.

But when you reach the bottom of the mountain—hours or years later, it will not matter—you will discover that you have been replaced. Slowly, patiently, exactly cell by cell, by something that remembers being you but prefers its symmetry colder, its questions less urgent, its sleep uninterrupted by dreams of tall places where nothing moves but everything changes.

Don’t worry. You will be very happy.

Everyone who lives here is, eventually.
