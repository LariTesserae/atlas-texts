They are not colonizers here, not even inhabitants properly speaking. They are the ones who got stuck between verbs—the ones who were climbing, reaching, growing, and then simply forgot to add the word “stop.” Some say they were human once. Others insist they were the tree’s dream of movement, dreaming too vividly for too long. Either way they are neither now. They call themselves the Knotwork, and they have no other name because names imply edges, and they are not outside anything anymore.

Look closely at the junction where six copper-moss pathways spiral together and lift without warning into a breathing shaft of warm air. Wait there without breathing back until your lungs forget their purpose. Eventually something will unclench from the bark—first a suggestion of knuckles, then the slow peel-back of what might be fingers if fingers were made of sapwood fused with memory—brown and green and glowing faintly from inside like something swallowed sunlight centuries ago and is still digesting it. Do not flinch. They taste fear as a change in humidity, and humidity here is always hungry.

A single Knotworker begins like this: a pucker in the bark’s surface, a slight hesitation in the grain pattern. Then the slow extrusion—almost shy—of woody digits testing the thickness of tomorrow. They are always emerging and always being reabsorbed, so any attempt to count them yields nonsense. They exist in the continuous present tense of growth rings: not seven years old, but seventh-ing, eighth-ing, nineteen-hundred-and-spiraling-into-fibonacci-ing.

They have no faces. Instead, every possible face they might ever need is embroidered beneath their outermost layer of bark-canvas, waiting. When one Knotworker speaks to another, the receiver’s skin ripples like water reflecting someone else’s dream. A stranger approaches and suddenly your cheekbone remembers being a grandmother in a city that floated seven miles up and never touched ground. You blink, and the memory melts back into grain lines.

They eat by osmosis and accumulative dreaming. At dusk—though dusk here is merely a slight deepening of the daylight leaking from inside the tree itself—they gather near the larger surface arteries where the green rivers of chlorophyll glow strongest. They press their mouthless fronts against the bark's warm glassy skin until they become slightly more translucent. Witnesses (those few outsiders who have stumbled in and not yet become ingredients) report that during these moments the Knotworkers appear to be listening extremely hard, their entire bodies tilted like ears. Later, tiny fruits form on nearby vines shaped exactly like what the witnesses were most afraid to forget.

Reproduction is equally indirect. When a Knotworker has grown too full of unlived possibilities—when they begin dreaming sideways into shapes the tree finds unproductive—they simply stop resisting the upward pull. Slowly, over centuries, they elongate. Their limbs branch, their thoughts lignify, their memories ring themselves inside-out until one day you realize what you mistook for a particularly expressive stretch of trunk is actually someone who decided to become architecture instead. Sometimes, if you press your ear against these retired beings during the deepest hours of the not-night, you can hear slow music playing beneath the bark: entire symphonies of choices not taken, composting gracefully into countermelodies.

The young ones—and here "young" means less than two thousand growth cycles—still move enough to frighten visitors. They lope along the vertical rivers of air like clumsy spiders learning liquid, their bodies flickering between too many joints. They play games with gravity’s leftovers: catching falling-up raindrops in baskets woven from their own shed skin, timing their leaps so that the tree’s heartbeat (ba-bump… ba-bump… every three hundred years exactly) will catch them midair and toss them a little higher. Their laughter sounds like bamboo creaking under warm pressure.

The elders barely move at all. They’ve become committees. Settled colonies of them occupy the oldest heartwood chambers, their individual bodies braided together into living sculptures which pulse with collective thought. If you need advice that spans more than weather patterns or minor prophecy—if, for example, you want to know whether your great-great-grandchild’s name will still fit inside a mouth three thousand years from now—you must climb to where the tree-sap dreams of being amber and speak your question into the slow carbonizing center of what used to be twenty-seven separate Knotworkers. Then you wait. The answer will come eventually, but it might arrive as a change in your own pulse rate next week, or as a sudden fashion among beetles for making art that resembles your face.

They have no leaders. They have rhythms instead. Some days the entire population leans slightly to the north, as if remembering a sun that existed before this one. Other times they all start shedding their outermost layer of memory-bark simultaneously, filling the air with translucent flakes that read like fragments of forgotten diaries if you catch them just right and never quite long enough to finish reading.

They do die, after a fashion, but it takes so long that other species have evolved to specialize in the different phases. There are funeral moths who drink only the final thoughts of dying Knotworkers, storing them in patterns on their wings like living libraries. There are time-eating beetles who carve precise tunnels through the oldest memories, aerating them so they don’t go rancid. There are poets who are actually mobile patches of lichen that grow only in the shape of particularly beautiful regrets, blooming once every seven centuries to release spores that make neighboring plants temporarily articulate their deepest sorrows in perfect haiku.

If you stay too long among them—and "too long" is always exactly one day longer than you planned—something gentle but absolute begins happening to your edges. Your shadow first, of course—there are no shadows here, so yours will try to find work elsewhere, perhaps as a pattern on some far-distant leaf. Then your name, which will start sounding increasingly like a weather report whenever you try to speak it. Finally your sense of where you end and everything else begins. Most visitors panic at this stage and flee back down the breathing roots to the world of edges and endings. A few rare ones relax into it instead.

Those are the ones you sometimes glimpse years later—or centuries, depending on how fast time is flowing that week—when you're rushing somewhere important and a particular arrangement of branches catches your eye just right and stops you cold. A face you almost recognize but can’t quite place because it’s still being decided. A movement in the corner of your vision that moves exactly like someone remembering how to become a forest. They wave, maybe, or maybe they just wave back through time from a moment when you were both still figuring out whether to become people or trees or something else entirely that hasn’t been invented yet but will be soon—

And then the wind shifts, carrying the scent of green time and growing possibility, and when you look again it’s just wood and light and the patient unending work of becoming something larger than any single name could ever hold.
