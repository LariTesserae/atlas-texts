The residents were never told they were citizens. They woke up inside the city’s throat and mistook the muscle contractions for sunrise.

First arrivals were noises that had nowhere else to echo. A dropped shield clang that kept rolling after the battle ended. A lover's name shouted into a canyon that answered back too sincerely. An infant's first laugh that frightened the midwife into forgetting how lungs work. These fragments of sound thickened until they learned to claim space by arguing with dimensions. You’ll see them still—uncertainty achromats flickering at street corners, holding conversations between the moment your foot lifts and the moment it (maybe) lands.

Next came the *Lose-somethingists*. People who’d misplaced objects so thoroughly—forgotten dreams no longer worth pursuing doesn’t count, these were tangible things: a grandmother’s thimble, the exact shade of green before disappointment—who wandered through natural holes in causality searching, not realizing that sustained longing stains reality like rust stains sea cliffs. The city absorbed them like dry soil drinks rain that’s been apologizing for centuries. Now they’re part of the architecture’s negotiable memory—windowpanes that remember being desired more than seen, stairs that adjust their height based on how badly you need what's upstairs.

Then the disappearers arrived in reverse order. Witness protection program for concepts that had seen too much. A species of ending that grew tired of being predictable. Childhood's last day without a dentist appointment. The exact temperature of forgiveness before it cools into forgetting. They came here because nowhere else would let them stop happening. You’ll never spot them directly—they’re the reason you sometimes find yourself standing in a room perfectly sized for a conversation you swore you were too old to need again.

Somewhere between third and fourth drafts of history, the *Misprintariats* emerged. These aren’t errors that slipped past editors; they’re truths that were too impatient for verification. Evolutions that got bored halfway and started side projects. Colors invented during pigment rebellions. Their leader is reportedly half prophecy, half typographical error, entirely convinced the revolution will be spelled differently once it finishes happening. They meet publicly—in the negative space between official policies, in the breaths between artificial trustworthiness scores—but the meeting minutes are written in disappearing ink made from yesterday's weather predictions.

Inhabitant census never stabilizes because categories here have relationship issues. Take the *Could-have-been-beens*. Not regrets exactly—regrets still remember accepting their invitation to the party. These are all possible selves that got distracted checking the mailbox while their lives walked past wearing better shoes. They've formed a collective now, squatting in the apartment building that exists only when you think about it from the wrong angle. Pay rent in bartered hesitation—two "almost called them back"s for window ledges with views of roads not taken.

Some residents are merely passing through so slowly they’ve become honorary fixtures. The Time Tourist with season tickets to tomorrow’s regrets. She sits in the nonexistent café between was and will be, ordering pastries that taste exactly like the instant you realize you've been pronouncing someone’s importance wrong your entire life. Payment is optional—she pays in butterflies anyway, specifically the ones that migrate in stomachs during job interviews.

There are also the *Actuallys*. Dangerous species evolved from internet comment sections left unattended near philosophical discussions during full moons. They appear whenever certainty shows symptoms of complacency—usually manifesting as perfectly groomed voices emerging from shadows to explain why suffering is technically optional if you just... but they always get interrupted midsentence by larger predators: the *Yeah-buts* who hunt in schools of conditional empathy.

But the true sovereigns, the ones who keep the city’s economy of attention solvent, are the *Forget-me-hards*. Not victims of poor memory—god no, memory here is currency that appreciates with absence. These deliberate unrememberers. Professional witnesses to things that need never have happened. They work in shifts maintaining the archives of unwritten books, cataloguing photographs that cameras refused to take, swearing affidavits about silences that promised they'd never tell. Their union meetings are legendary—you're never sure if you attended, but afterward your dreams have better pacing and fewer exposition dumps.

Newcomers always ask which direction leads out. The city sighs—an architectural exhale that rearranges three alleyways and a childhood—that's-not-how-it-works-here-but-bless-your-linear-heart. There are exits, of course. Multiple award-winning ones. The problem isn't finding them; it's convincing your feet to walk away from conversations with walls that understand why you never called your father back more than your father ever did.

Stay long enough and you'll receive your own residency card. It will be printed on fog. Hereditary but non-transferable. Valid in all moments except the one where you finally decide what you want to be when you stop growing sideways. Welcome committee consists of all the words you've ever swallowed instead of saying goodbye properly. They meet you at the gate—which is also a mirror—which is also the gap between what happened and how you're going to explain it later.

Don't worry about orientation. Here, lost is just another word for finally being interesting enough to map.
