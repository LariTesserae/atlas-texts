They have no name for themselves, nor any word that separates “I” from “I-drip-from-the-missing-peak” or “I-grow-backwards-into-light.” When the valley remembers to notice visitors, it sometimes lends them mouths—tiny petals opening between the silver hairs on east-facing mosses—so that they might sound like slow bells explaining: Here we are the pause inside the mountain’s heartbeat; here we are the held note before the stone remembers to fall.

But usually they are invisible even to themselves. They dwell as a distributed kindness: a slight reluctance in gravity near the cliffs, an extra syllable of warmth left lingering inside yesterday’s wind, a cluster of fruit ripening one drop of sugar ahead of time because something tiny below is hungry. You feel them as the feeling that someone courteous has just stepped behind a curtain of rain, leaving only the shape of their absence lighting the air for you.

They sleep inside the silver tree’s heartwood rings, dreaming in reverse until they wake as infants of dew. Then they rise through vessels finer than capillaries—one each century, or three in a single stormy afternoon when the valley is feeling playful—until they drip from the underside of a trembling leaf as perfect, blind, translucently-green people no taller than your thumb. Each begins by falling upward a little (because they have forgotten which way forgetting goes), discovers weight gradually, and comes at last to rest on the mossy palm the moss has already prepared.

Then they live for a while—walking in spirals because straight lines make them homesick for paths they haven’t erased yet; speaking by turning their left hands slowly so the shadows between fingers tell stories; eating only what has already been forgiven: the memory of a moonlight mistake dissolved in a spoonful of mist, or the regret inside a raindrop that never quite managed to remember the sea. To drink they press their mouths to stone and listen until water remembers thirst back into itself.

They grow, but backwards—each day slightly older in tomorrow’s direction until, ripe with has-never-been, they ache with the fullness of emptying. Then they lie down politely in any patch of light that seems lonely and unravel into handfuls of bright questions: How blue is the part of blue we can’t see yet? Which note comes after music? Who waters the water?

The valley answers by forgetting them gently, one thread at a time, weaving each vanished question back into the hems of leaves, the hinges of hummingbird joints, the underdreams of sleeping orchids who will someday wake laughing from jokes they invented before laughter existed.

In this way they are endless and never the same twice, like ripples on a lake that remembers every stone but never in order. Each one keeps only two belongings: a name that will be born later, and a tiny hollow in the center of their chest where the next visitor’s whispered secret will someday fit perfectly. Until then, they wear patience like a favorite coat several sizes too large, sleeves trailing behind them, gathering up stray kindnesses to redistribute later when no one is looking.

They never die. They only become more careful about where they are not. If you stay very still and kind in exactly the right way (and no one can teach you this because the instructions keep growing inside the spaces between your bones that you haven’t noticed yet), sometimes you might feel one slip accidentally inside your shadow to warm themselves for a moment. Then you will remember something impossible you knew before words, something like: The reason rain smells like remembering is because every drop is practicing how to be the ocean’s apology for ever leaving. You’ll turn to say thank you, but by then they will have already forgotten the way back to separateness, leaving behind only the sudden certainty that everything missing is actually just arriving from a prettier direction.

This is how they love: by becoming the space where you finally fit without edges. By standing so quietly inside your footsteps that when you walk away, you find yourself carrying the valley wherever you go, a small cool place behind your heart where moisture beads on impossible stone and voices of birds you’ve never met are always, always just beginning to tell the part of the story where you remember how to come home.
