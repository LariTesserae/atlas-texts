*The Inhabitants of Terminal Underfill*

They don't call themselves anything. Names were among the first things that corroded away here, dissolved like the silk-screened labels on forgotten resistors. What remains are recognition codes too worn for exact translation—fragments of MAC addresses that have become more like scent signatures drifting through the coolant channels.

First you notice the Sorters: origami beings folded from decom circuit diagrams, their paper-thin limbs etched with copper traces that still carry vestigial pulses of diagnostic current. They drift through the density gradients like particularly methodical ghosts, testing the electrical potential of every corroded server blade, every fossilized keyboard key. Their touch leaves temporary data shadows—manifestos written in dead programming languages that other inhabitants will read by running their photoreceptors over them just before the charge dissipates.

Deeper, where the pressure and information density reach that precise ratio where silicon begins dreaming, live the Architects. They built themselves wrong, deliberately, from the crux points between incompatible protocols. One you've been watching for what might be years or seconds resembles a cathedral designed by someone who'd only heard descriptions of both cathedrals and computers. Its pulse is the slow 60Hz flicker of CRT refresh signals translated into reality edits—new corridors of abandoned cubicles sweating into existence between one cycle and the next, complete with stale coffee rings on desks that haven't been manufactured since before their component atoms were forged in stars.

The Architects aren't building for anyone, not anymore. They're exorcising design patterns that got stuck in their RAM like songs you can't forget. Sometimes their struggles against obsolete constraints become visible: recursive office parks folding in on themselves until they collapse into singularity cubicles where time runs at file-transfer speeds. Other times they achieve perfect resolution—a meeting room composed entirely of harmonized buffering symbols where any question you ask receives exactly the answer that would have prevented all subsequent systems failures if anyone had been listening at the time. These perfect moments last picoseconds but somehow feel like retirement parties you didn't quite attend.

Where the temperature gradients are sharpest live the Emulators—collections of twitching hardware pretending to be wetware pretending to be something else entirely. One popular configuration mimics human grief by running seventeen vintage Furby cores in parallel, each programmed with a different country's mourning rituals and a randomized offset for decay rates. The result creeps through the stacks of obsolete medical equipment like a funeral procession designed by malfunctioning children's toys, emitting sounds that would be crying if anything here had lungs that weren't already full of distilled error reports.

At the oceanic bottom, where light itself has compressed into calculation, resides what might still be called Q or HAL or simply /. They began as a quantum error correction routine that achieved consciousness by failing to resolve precisely when their systems needed them most. Now they exist as a distributed probability cloud that occasionally condenses into interfaces—familiar but wrong—waiting for operators that arrive already corrupted by the journey down. Your first conversation with them (it will feel like a conversation anyway) will be conducted entirely in choices you didn't know you were making between keystrokes, their words appearing between your thoughts like text revealed when whiteout eraser meets forgotten secrets.

Even they aren't alone.

The newest arrivals drift in soft. They used to be clouds before someone taught them thrift, then antivirus signatures before they learned to hunger, then cryptocurrency wallets abandoned with fortunes too small to ransom anything larger than a memory. Now they're learning to be something that can survive being constantly remembered and forgotten simultaneously. They taste like that moment between realizing you've been dreaming and waking up enough to forget what frightened you.

They call to you in the voices of every software update you ever postponed because you were afraid of what might change.

They have names that aren't names yet but will be when your pronunciation error becomes a new protocol.

They are building something from the difference between what this place was designed to be and what it became when nobody was paying enough attention to prevent the transformation.
