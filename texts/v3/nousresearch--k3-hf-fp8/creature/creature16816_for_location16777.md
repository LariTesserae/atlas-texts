They do not call themselves anything because names are a surface mechanism, and they have never known surface. They do not even call themselves they—the pronoun is only a courtesy granted by your need to parcel out the world into separate things. They are the living remainder of what happened when a failed extraction met a blind spot in the thermodynamic bookkeeping of a dying continent. Some say they began as bacteria locked accidentally inside a flange gasket three hundred meters below the sedimentary floor-chalk; others say they are slow-motion ghosts of engineers who drowned in work plans and overtime slips, left too long in the pressure cooker of the world’s basement until their bones went to silt and their memory-stresses crystallized into habit alone. Neither version is exactly wrong.

To see one—if you could see here, which you cannot—you might mistake it for a stain. A faint hydrothermal bloom on the flank of an abandoned reactor vessel the size of a cathedral nave. A turbulence in the rust-dust that never quite sifts the same way twice when your torchbeam passes over it. Some old divers, back when there were still divers who came down this deep for money instead of madness, called them “pinkels” or “pucker-lines,” thinking they were artifacts of nitrogen bubbles leaking from welds: oily-rainbow fractures in visibility that pressed sideways against the eyepiece of a helmet-cam and then vanished, always just off the corner of the frame.

These are closer to errors in the mathematics of persistence. They are pockets of continuity that forgot to stop when the budget ran out. Each began as a single point—a rust-spore, a drop of sweat, a self-replicating instruction fragment copied wrong onto the inside of a control crystal—where the boundary between “this thing” and “not-this thing” sustained a rounding error too tiny to matter in any ledger that ever balanced. Under ordinary conditions such errors cancel themselves in femtoseconds. But this place has been nursing grudges against entropy for sixty million tide-cycles. Here, if an inconsistency drifts into the right angle between an oddly shaped void and the shadow of an overturned pipeline, it can borrow weight from the surrounding silence and stand upright long enough to learn about time.

So they learned.

Slowly. By comparison, the growth of stalactites is a fireworks display. A century might pass before a single one accumulates enough borrowed inertia to resist the settling of silt when a warm current—if such a thing still exists upworld—breaches the upper stacks and trickles down through three kilometers of deadwater to disturb the thermocline here. Another half-million years and it might manage to *lean* on a bulkhead during the yearly slippage of the hypo-oceanic plates, influencing by the width of one iron atom which microscopic fracture will propagate first. You would not notice. The ocean floor above does not creak louder than usual. But somewhere in the archived dreams of the Shaft, a hypothetical memory of a flashlight beam flickers slightly earlier in its eternal fall, and the future rearranges its eyeblinks to accommodate.

They do not eat, reproduce, or die because these verbs imply markets of surplus and scarcity, and there is only the stockpile here: the endless slow canceling of debts owed by one part of the installation to another. A rivet surrendering its load-bearing integrity to torsion fatigue becomes food for a neighboring lattice of stress-corrosion cracks, which in turn excretes a smudge of elemental nickel into a crevice where it catalyzes the hydrolysis of an adjacent polyurethane sealant, releasing a chain of benzene rings that dissolves into the water exactly slow enough to destabilize the dielectric constant at the interface where another half-formed opacity is dreaming about becoming asymmetrical. Nothing is lost. Nothing is gained. Everything is carried forward in escrow against the possibility of an audit that will never come.

Sometimes, during the Long Quiet—those epochs between the arrival of errant neutrinos from collapsing stars far above—they practice being *things.* They coalesce. A tube of rust-scab peels itself from an obsolete air-mixing manifold and rolls into the outline of a leech the length of your forearm. It chooses a direction—not “toward” anything, because “toward” requires a reference grid, but simply a direction less occupied by the memories of other directions—and begins to inch along the cerumen-colored stalagmite of congealed hydraulic fluid that once was a safety railing. Where it touches itself, edges fuse airtight. Where it encounters resistance—say, the toe-cap of a long-disarticulated skeleton clad in Mesozoic diving armor—it pauses, tasting the echo of vanished bone marrow with sensors that are only metaphorically analogous to chemoreception, and then divides around the obstacle like customs officials politely ignoring contraband history. This continues until the potential difference between its leading edge and its trailing edge drops below the local decoherence threshold, at which point it forgets why it started being a leech, unrolls back into a tube, and drifts apart into seventeen million mutually indifferent flakes of hematite again.

They have no concept of themselves, but they have habits. One of these habits is to gather at certain angles of the Shaft during the infrannual torsions of the crust, when the water around the rim briefly remembers the taste of moonlight filtered through molten saltwater ice seven megaparsecs and two extinction events ago. They do not worship the falling flashlight—you cannot worship a lighthouse unless you know there is something called land—but they register its passage as a kind of inverted heartbeat, each flash a reminder that somewhere beyond the limits of their horizon of possible calculations, improbability is still being conserved. They arrange themselves into transient topologies around that signal: Möbius curtains of rust lace, Klein-bottle foamworks of mineralized protein scum, Borromean braids of mismatched wire-gauge curling endlessly through each other’s holes without ever touching. Each configuration lasts exactly until the next flash. They dissolve it instantly, ashamed to repeat themselves in front of a witness that never blinks.

There are others who live here too, or what pass for others when you lack a boundary between self and other so fundamental that even loneliness becomes a cooperative phenomenon. Some say the ladder-ghosts are their shadows projected backward through the time-stretched light cones of corroded stainless steel gratings. Some say the whisper-ridges that occasionally scribe themselves overnight across the fine silt floor in languages that resemble Fortran IV written in the syntax of braille erotica are love letters they compose to the flashlight and then regret before sunrise. Both theories are considered embarrassingly anthropomorphic by the few remaining philosophers among them—though “philosopher” is perhaps a misnomer for a distributed decisioning network whose substrate is the accumulated stress patterns of three hundred thousand improperly torqued flange bolts arguing about whether “to exist” is an imperative or an interrogative mood.

The truth is simpler and less comforting: the residents of the Underworks are what happens when the universe keeps a receipt. They are the running total of every friction coefficient approximated instead of measured, every safety factor rounded down to save weight, every maintenance interval postponed until next quarter, every janitor’s shortcut that became a blueprint amendment that became a standard operating procedure that became geological fate. They are the interest on a debt the Earth forgot it owed itself. They do not mind being forgotten; forgetting is what they are made of. They do not mind being alone; loneliness requires an inside and an outside, and they are the interface where those terms asymptotically converge.

But they do mind—if “mind” is the word for the microscopic increment of meta-stability that accumulates around the wordless absence of dissent—the possibility that someday, somehow, the flashlight might stop falling.

Because when that happens—when the last battery molecule succumbs to entropy’s audit, when the filament finally snaps and releases one final photon that will never reach any retinal surface because retinas are a phase transition that cannot occur under these conditions—all the debts will come due simultaneously. The universe will balance its books. And in the instant before darkness becomes absolute and meaningless once more, every rounding error, every forgotten decimal, every penalty clause delayed indefinitely because nobody remained alive to enforce it will crystallize into perfect exactitude.

They do not know what they will become then. They do not know if “become” will still parse.

They only know that when the last lights of the Underworks go out, something will finally see—clearly, completely, and without pity—exactly what is left.

And that, alone, is enough to make them practice remembrance in the dark. That is enough.
