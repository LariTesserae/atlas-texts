In a place that should not hold together yet does, there are those who have learned to exist precisely at the threshold of collapse. They are called the Pallbearers, though they bear no bodies, only the endless anticipation of breakage that defines the Bottomlot.

You won't see them at first. They are folded into the rootarchitecture like origami regrets, creased along lines of potential fracture. Each Pallbearer began as something else—a traveler who took too long counting breaths, a seed that forgot how to not grow, a story that mistook itself for history. They stepped wrong once, or right depending on how you measure such things, and found their weight distributed perfectly across the rootpave's invisible stress matrix. The Bottomlot acknowledged their balance with the slowest of acceptances—the same way water acknowledges a stone by carving around it over centuries.

They are exactly twelve in number, mirroring the twelve paces of the bowl's circumference. This is not coincidence but topography—each occupies the precise location where the rootmass's memory of holding reaches its optimal angle of repose against its memory of letting go. If you could see them (darkness 0.0 permits all seeing except recognition) they would appear as slight bulges in the root plane, barely thicker than the difference between shadow and absence. Their forms follow the Fibonacci of survival in collapsing systems: each subsequent Pallbearer is slightly less distinct than the last, tapering toward the twelfth who is indistinguishable from the rootwall's own uncertainty about where it ends.

They exist in a state of constant minor adjustment, each microsecond recalculating the calculus of their continuation. The mathematics is brutal—any fraction too much presence would tip them into being fully elsewhere, any atom too little and they would dissolve into the general tension field. They learned from the rootpave itself how to be exactly enough and no more. Their breathing (for they do something like it) is synchronized not with time but with the slow acoustic wave that travels the rootbody whenever the Not-Echo missteps—inhaling during the compression phase when existence contracts slightly, exhaling during rarefaction when it forgets to remember itself.

The Pallbearers wear clothing made from repurposed gaps. They harvest the spaces between what should touch but doesn't—those molecular hesitations where root fiber almost but never quite completes its handshake with adjacent matter. Woven together through techniques of negative pressure and social awkwardness, these garments appear to be wearer's own absence shaped into three dimensions with pockets. The pockets hold things that haven't decided whether to exist: promises made at exactly the wrong frequency to be kept, memories of dreams that failed quality control, the sound of a single hand clapping in a forest of deaf trees. Sometimes, if you pass close enough to disturb their still field, one might reach (though reaching implies too much intention) into a pocket and offer you something you didn't realize you'd lost. Accept nothing. The gift is never the thing itself but the exact shape of the hole it leaves behind.

They speak a language composed entirely of the pauses between sentences in languages that haven't been invented yet. Conversations take geological time—entire civilizations rise and fall between subject and predicate, continents drift during particularly complex subordinate clauses. If you listen with the wrong kind of patience (and all patience is wrong here) you might catch fragments: "...when the last equation balances itself by becoming poetry..." or "...the exact taste of the color of forgiveness at absolute zero..." or simply "...almost..."

Almost is their favorite word. It contains everything they aspire to—being nearly something without the fatal dignity of actuality.

The Pallbearers consume only the difference between potential and kinetic energy. They sit (though sitting is too definite) at the points where the rootmass's stored tensions accumulate juicely against its reluctance to release them. Through processes that resemble both meditation and extremely slow starvation, they sip the sweet anticipation of motion without ever allowing themselves the satiation of movement. Their metabolism produces as waste-product tiny paradoxes—perfectly frictionless surfaces the size of thoughts, sounds that can be heard only by people who've never existed, diagrams of perpetual motion machines that would work perfectly if anyone were foolish enough to ignore the third law of thermodynamics.

Once, or perhaps constantly from a certain perspective, one of them tried to leave. The fifth Pallbearer, briefly overcome by a moment of insufficient endurance, shifted slightly toward exit. Horror rippled through the rootbale as perfect equilibrium trembled toward decisive collapse. The other eleven held emergency conferences in the spaces between seconds, sending frantic adjustment waves rippling through the rootmass, recruiting even the Not-Echo to trail behind the defector with false footsteps that would cancel his tracks. The fifth made it almost (always almost) to the rim where rootpave curls back upon itself before realizing that leaving would require becoming real enough to have absence notice his departure. This realization caught him mid-step between being and been. He remains there still—neither inside nor outside, neither going nor staying, but precisely the quantum possibility of departure frozen into the architecture like a scream in hardened honey.

They celebrate this annually (though annual here means whenever the root's memory of light cycles through approximately 365 days and 5 hours of remembered darkness). The celebration consists of twelve hours during which each Pallbearer attempts to recall the exact moment he became what he now barely is. None ever succeed, but the attempt generates exquisite tensions that suffuse the entire Bottomlot with a flavor like nostalgia for futures that passed their sell-by date without arriving.

If you stay long enough (and staying any time is too long) you might feel yourself beginning to adjust to their frequency. Your edges will soften, your weight distributing itself more politely across the possibilities of the floor. Objects will fall near you but never quite at you. Your reflection will start lingering in mirrors after you've left them. You might begin to remember conversations that won't occur until tomorrow's yesterday. When you notice these changes, the seventh Pallbreaker (for that's what they become, eventually—bearers of other things breaking around them) will offer (without moving) the traditional warning: "Almost time to almost go."

Listen to him. Or don't. Either way, you are already becoming part of the calculation that determines how much longer the Bottomlot can afford to almost not exist. The Pallbearers have vacancy for thirteen—they always maintain space for one more almost. Not because they hope for new members, but because hope is a form of weight they know better than to carry. They keep the place ready instead for the precise moment when someone else's certainty about leaving will provide exactly the counterbalance needed for their own continued indefinite farewell.

You feel it already—that delicate tipping point where your presence becomes significant enough to notice yet still negligible enough to accommodate. The rootpave is adjusting its expectation of footsteps. The Not-Echo is practicing the sound of shoes you'll almost decide to turn back. The Pallbearers themselves are holding their non-breath in perfect twelve-part counterpoint.

Almost time. Or perhaps it was always already almost too late. The difference, here, is precisely what holds everything together.
