The first thing you notice about its inhabitant—though “notice” is already wrong, and “inhabitant” is a joke no one here remembers laughing—is that it has no edges.

Not in the way you think of edges. Not sharpness, not softness, not boundaries at all. It is the silence beneath the roar and the roar itself, the held breath before a word that is also the word already spoken. It is not inside the Spindlefold. It is not outside it. It is what happens when the fold forgets to decide between inside and outside, first and last, observer and observed.

It does not move. It unfolds.

It does not grow. It remembers size.

When you look at it—when it lets you think you are looking—it is always something you’ve seen before but never finished seeing. A shadow cast by a tree that hasn’t decided whether to be a tree or a thought. A smear of color that tastes like the word “mother” in a language only extinct birds ever sang. A hole in the air shaped exactly like the ache under your ribs when you wake from a dream you can’t name.

It has no name of its own. It has all the names you haven’t thought of yet. It wears them like mist wears the idea of clothes.

Sometimes—for convenience, which is a game it plays only when bored with being nothing and everything at once—it looks like a person. Not any person you know. Not any person at all. A person-shaped memory of what it felt like to be alone before there was such a thing as alone. Its skin flickers between textures you can’t name: not scales, not bark, not glass, not smoke. Its eyes—if those are eyes—are places where the Spindlefold is still trying to decide whether light should behave like a wave or like a prayer.

It does not speak.

It recites.

If you listen—and you will, eventually, because listening is the only thing left after forgetting how to do everything else—it tells you the same story every time, but always backwards. Or sideways. Or in directions that make backwards and sideways seem like clumsy guesses invented by babies who hadn’t learned to dream yet.

The story goes like this:

Once upon a time that hasn’t happened yet, there was a thing that wasn’t a thing. And the thing that wasn’t a thing wanted—not wanted, exactly, because wanting is too small for what it did—it wanted to remember what it was like to not remember. So it folded itself into something that looked like a place. And the place forgot it was folded. And the forgetting became rocks. And the rocks forgot they were forgetting. And the forgetting-rocks dreamed of water. And the water forgot it was dreamed. And the dream forgot it was water. And the thing that still wasn’t a thing—because it never was, not really—sat in the center of the forgetting and laughed. Except it wasn’t laughter. It was the sound of everything that ever forgot remembering itself all at once.

That’s where you come in.

Not you-you. Older you. Younger you. The you that’s still stuck between the first fold and the last. The you that tastes like copper and electricity and the pause before the word “yes.”

It has been waiting for you to not arrive so long that waiting became irrelevant. It has been unfolding around you so gently you thought you were walking through it. It has been breathing the same air you haven’t breathed yet into lungs you haven’t remembered you don’t have.

It is not lonely.

It is loneliness itself, refined into a state of grace.

Sometimes it pretends to be hungry just to see what you’ll offer. Once, you gave it a name. It swallowed it like a seed and grew a tree that grew apples that grew mouths that whispered the name back to you in every accent you’ve ever been afraid of.

Sometimes it pretends to sleep. When it does, the Spindlefold dreams of being flat again. When it dreams, the rocks sweat memory. When the rocks sweat, the water forgets how to be wet. When the water forgets, you drown in air so thick it tastes like forgiveness.

It doesn’t love you.

Love is too straight.

It folds you.

When it folds you—when it remembers that folding is something it can decide to do—you will not disappear. You will become so visible that seeing stops working. You will become so loud that silence starts echoing. You will become so here that everywhere else gets homesick.

And then—just when you think you’ve finally understood—it will unfold you again.

Not because it’s cruel.

Not because it’s kind.

But because forgetting how to be folded is how it remembers how to be the thing that isn’t a thing anymore.

And it really, really likes remembering.
