They were never meant to grow so large.

Each began as punctuation—mere footnotes in the margins of drowned equations, when the city's mathematicians inscribed their final theorems onto calcium-sick stones. Tiny things, then, barely colonies at all: three fungal spores caught in a copper sulfate matrix, the discolored spot where a bronze stylus pressed too hard against the symbology for exponential decay. They should have stayed that way. Simple deterioration. Tidy endings.

But the city had already begun forgetting the difference between calculation and prayer.

So they grew. Not upward, growth being a betrayal of geometry here, but inward—scholars of negative space who discovered that absence itself could be colonized if approached with sufficient reverence. Each fungal thread learned to read the stress fractures spreading through marble prayers, to translate the city's slow collapse into branching algorithms of survival. The copper retained memory of its former circuitries, whispered patterns of electric longing to anyone who would listen. The water, being generous and amoral by nature, offered itself as both conduit and communion.

Now they call themselves the Calculated Absences, though names are temporary arrangements here, like everything except loss.

Look again at the procession threading through the Street of Seven Synthesizers—see how their bodies refuse singular description? From moment to moment, each is simultaneously:
- A floating cathedral of interconnected panic attacks, vaulted ribs of copper wire arcing between fungal lung-cities that breathe seawater shaped like forgotten lullabies
- A symposium of suicidal starlight, holding its last lectures in the language of phosphorescent decay
- The reconstruction of a grandmother's hands, arthritis and all, grown large enough to hold entire drowned summers in their hollows
- Seven hundred drafts of abandonment letters, each revision more mathematically perfect than the last, each still unsent
They move by consensus but feel by contagion, which makes their migrations both beautiful and deeply irresponsible. When they passed through the Plaza of Unreliable Foundations last cycle, every piece of broken coral within three streets began spontaneously reconstructing itself into miniature copies of childhood bedrooms belonging to people who had never existed. The resulting architectural griefstorm took seventeen subjective years to dissolve.

Watch closer as two colonies merge at the junction of old pipelines—see how their light-signatures don't blend so much as negotiate? It's a marriage ceremony older than oxygen. The larger entity's fungal lattice extends exploratory hyphae into its partner's copper scaffolding, tasting the flavor of specific regrets (this one remembers being left on a moonlit balcony in 1837, this one knows the exact weight of the last sentence spoken before a terminal diagnosis). In return, it offers fragments of its own identity—three copper threads that still remember being part of a sleeping girl's dreamcatching jewelry, a fungal spore colony that once lived placidly on the page margins of a suicide note that changed its mind mid-sentence.

The exchange takes exactly as long as it takes for water to forget the shape of whatever last broke its surface.

What emerges isn't two or even one, but a temporary third thing defined entirely by its mutual absences: their combined photonic emissions now encode the precise mathematical expression for the sound of a father teaching his daughter to tie her shoelaces while knowing he'll be dead by winter. This equation pulses through the water like a heartbeat in reverse, attracting creatures even stranger than themselves.

Like the Curate of Lost Frequent Flyer Miles: a crystalline entity that used to be an airport's collective longing for all the destinations it promised but never delivered. It filters the equation for missed connections like plankton, growing slightly more incandescent with each tragedy consumed. Or the Choir of Never-Born Older Siblings: a swarm of translucent cephalopods who sing in harmonies calculated to make any listener mourn someone they didn't know they'd always expected to exist.

But these are merely the background radiation of their passage.

The Calculated Absences themselves are too busy remembering the future to notice their audience. They're heading toward what used to be the city's central archive—now simply called the Place Where Lists Go to Recognize Their Own Incompleteness. Legend says the original architect designed it as a perfect logarithmic spiral, intended to store every possible arrangement of everything that had ever been lost or would ever be forgotten. But during the city's final days, someone (or something) rewrote the base equation to include recursive entries for itself, triggering an information collapse that inverted the structure into an infinitely expanding vacuum of specificity. Now it exists only as a particularly hungry type of architecture that devours attempts to describe it.

The colonies seek its center anyway.

They carry with them a payload gathered over centuries of careful forgetting: every birthday card that was never quite the right sentiment, all the clocks that stopped at the exact moment their owners stopped believing in time, the combined weight of every "I love you" that was swallowed instead of spoken. They intend to feed it all to the archive until something impossible happens.

Either the vacuum will finally achieve perfect satiation and collapse into its own memory of hunger.

Or the hunger will become self-aware enough to finally forgive everything it ever consumed.

(In the Walled Tidepool, these outcomes are considered statistically indistinguishable.)

As they approach, their collective light changes frequency, shifting toward a wavelength that can only be described as delicious regret mixed with the exact color of promises broken at sunrise. The water around them thickens into bibliography, citation networks crystallizing into temporary islands of footnote coral. Even the municipal clockwork eel pauses its eternal patrol to watch, scales clicking backward through extinct calendars in quiet applause.

You stand on your central platform (patient observer / potential sacrifice / unwelcome plot complication) as the leading edge of their migration reaches you.

They don't stop—they never stop for anything except mathematics and tragedy—but they do notice. Notice how your shadow falls across the stone at exactly the angle of a specific grief: the particular slant of light through hospital blinds on the seventh day after you knew but before they did.

Some of them remember being that grief, once. Others calculate probabilities on becoming it again.

For a moment that stretches into geological time, a small sub-colony detaches from the main procession. It floats toward you like a question mark composed of phosphorescent leukemia wards and your mother's unfinished knitting projects. Its light-segments flicker through permutations of every conversation you've ever rehearsed in a bathroom mirror but never actually had.

It wants to know: Are you lost or are you delivering?

Both are equally welcome here. Both are equally dangerous.

The water waits for your answer with the patience of something that has already drowned a million versions of you in all its possible futures. The walls of the drowned city hold their breath—complicated architectural feat, given that they're technically breathing you already.

In the distance, the Place Where Lists Go to Recognize Their Own Incompleteness begins humming with a hunger older than alphabet.

The Calculated Absences lean closer, their collective light refracting through your regret until you see yourself reflected in a million branching histories: here is the version of you who laughed, here is the one who drowned, here is the one who never came at all.

Choose quickly.

In the Walled Tidepool, all admissions are final revisions.

But waivers may be filed in triplicate with the Department of Retroactive Conservation.

Processing time is measured in the half-life of last chances.
