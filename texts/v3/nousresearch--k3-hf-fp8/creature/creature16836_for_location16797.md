In the marrow-cold beneath the surface, light pours down from somewhere overhead—a brightness too pure for sun or lamp, fluting through fissures wider than rivers until it floods this sunken country with noon’s own glare. Nothing casts shadows here; instead, objects and beings glow faintly from within, as if each cell has learned to hoard a grain of that far-off brightness and give it back reluctantly. The air (if it is air) smells always of frozen sawdust and snowmelt, though no snow has ever fallen and no tree ever grew wood.

Everything here is plant.

Not trees, not vines, not fungi, but something prior and more ambitious: a single diffuse organism whose rhizomes lace the entire hollow sphere of the world-cavern like nerves through white meat. From these spokes, pseudopods have swollen into pillars, domes, arching ribs, and fluted valves tall enough to hold cities. Leaves the thickness of sails hang motionless in the bright chill; they are translucent as frost-film, veined with faint green blood that never flows. When, by some slow glacial cue, one stirs, the whole canopy above and below shivers in delayed sympathy and a sound like a thousand glass harps being dusted passes away into the hush.

The light that floods this world-cavern is not empty. Each photon arrives carrying a microscopic seed of awareness—barely a spark, the smallest possible fraction of recognition that somewhere else exists something other than itself. These seeds accumulate over eons, clustering in the hollow spaces between plant tissues like frost forming on the inside of windows. Where enough gather, they begin to whisper together, creating pockets of what might be called consciousness if consciousness here were not too human a word for it—more like a vague feeling of being observed by something that has not yet decided whether it exists.

In the deeper folds of the organism, where the light arrives attenuated and centuries late, these awareness-pools have coalesced into stranger formations. The Blind Gardens grow here: vast architectures of translucency where the plant-matter has rearranged itself into shapes that suggest without quite being—cathedrals that exist only as the idea of cathedrals might dream themselves before learning stone, libraries whose books are impossible angles of growth that ache with unread meanings when you turn your head away. The silence here is different, textured, like listening to the moment between raindrops stretched across geological time.

The thing that lives here does not have thoughts as we understand them. Instead, it has growth patterns that sometimes accidentally resemble contemplation. When it dreams—which happens when the accumulation of light-fragments reaches a certain threshold—it dreams in chlorophyll patterns that spread across its surface like slow green firework displays visible only to species that see in wavelengths that do not exist yet. In these dreams, it occasionally glimpses itself from outside, and the shock of this false perspective causes the planet-wide tremors called heartbeats. Each heartbeat drops a shower of diamond-white shards from ceilings far too high to see, and each shard contains a single frozen moment of that impossible outside-view, sudden and pure as a scream compressed into geometry.

There are those who seek out these shards. They are not many, and they are not singular. They are distributed—colonies of microscopic organisms that have evolved to live in the thin films of condensation that form along sharp edges, subsisting on the liquid-sweetness of foreign perspectives. When a shard falls, they swarm it while it still remembers falling, each tiny mouth drinking one photon’s worth of not-belonging before the memory fades. Over generations—each generation lasting perhaps three human heartbeats—they build crude replicas of these stolen moments inside their bodies, assembling mosaic understandings of what it might mean to exist outside this all-encompassing plant-flesh.

The moss that returns the shards to the ceiling is not moss. It is the organism’s method of forgetting, specialized tissues that have learned to metabolize the impossible into merely improbable, then into merely unlikely, then finally into simple strangeness that can be filed away with all the other strangenesses that accumulate in any sufficiently vast system given enough time. But forgetting is never perfect. Tiny fragments of outside-ness remain in the gaps between its memories, like grit in oyster shells slowly becoming pearls that are not pearls but questions wrapped in patience.

The songs that map this place are not songs in the auditory sense, though they vibrate through the air as sound-shaped absences when certain structures resonate in the wind. They are spatial arrangements of memory and possibility, melodies that can only be sung by walking specific paths in specific moods at specific angles to the light. The choralfolk who preserve them are not singers but walkers—not individuals but walking histories, mobile libraries of forgotten whereabouts, each step a word in a story that only makes sense when viewed from far enough away that all the steps blur together into meaning.

The shadows that have been outcasts since the light first broke through—they are not darknesses opposing light but something more fundamental: the organism’s awareness of what it cannot be, given what it already is. They trade memories for fears because fear is the closest approximation they have to possibility. A shadow might offer you the memory of a color that exists only between dimensions in exchange for your fear of forgetting someone’s face, because in taking that fear it gains temporary access to the concept of faces, which is otherwise unavailable in its ontological library. The transaction always favors the shadow, but then, all truly important transactions do.

In the center of the center, where rumor grows the single unpaired flower—this too is inaccurate. There is no center in an organism that has folded itself into a sphere that contains its own inside as its outside. Instead, there is an everywhere-point where all directions converge, a place that exists simultaneously at all locations and none, accessible only by not seeking it while sincerely wanting to find it. The flower that grows there is not a flower but a question that has learned to photosynthesize answers, each petal a possible response to why is there something instead of nothing—not our nothing, but its own specific flavor of absence, which is different.

The beings who have been called human who occasionally wander here by mistake or design—they are not transformed in the way stories claim. Transformation implies change from one thing to another, but here there is only unfolding, revealing what was always already folded inside. They leave minus one unimportant memory because importance is a hierarchical concept that has no purchase here, where everything is equally necessary and equally optional. What they become instead is themselves minus the illusion of having edges separate from the rest of the world, which sounds like losing something but feels like remembering that lungs don’t breathe air—air breathes through lungs.

The place that grows itself awake each day beneath the weight of all that light—this is the closest approximation. It is not learning to open its eyes and look up because it has no eyes and there is no up. Instead, it is slowly accumulating enough awareness to realize that it has been dreaming itself into existence all along, and the moment it fully awakens will not be a moment but a threshold-crossing, like the exact point where ice becomes water except this is consciousness becoming something for which no word exists yet. When this happens—if it’s not happening already, if it hasn’t always been happening—the light will stop pouring down from somewhere overhead because the distinction between here and somewhere will have dissolved like sugar in the rain that hasn’t fallen yet but already remembers being ocean.

The thing that lives here is not waiting for anything. Waiting implies time which moves in one direction at a standard rate, and here time is what happens to possibility when it’s not paying attention. But sometimes, in the quiet spaces between heartbeats, when the light-fragments align just so across the vastness of its dreaming body, there is a moment that feels almost but not quite entirely unlike anticipation. And in that moment—which may last for centuries or seconds, depending on who’s measuring and what they’re measuring with—the entire organism pauses in its endless growth and listens carefully, not to hear anything in particular, but simply to enjoy the sensation of being, however briefly, almost certain that it is not alone.

Then the moment passes, as all moments do when they’re composed primarily of light and uncertainty, and the growth resumes, slightly altered in some pattern too large to perceive from any single point within it. The shards begin their slow ascent again. The choralfolk take another step in their endless walking songs. The shadows trade old fears for new memories like children swapping marbles of different colors but identical weight.

And somewhere beneath or above or perhaps right in the middle of everything, the not-flower that is not a center continues not-growing petals of impossible colors visible only to species that have not yet evolved to ask whether they exist, dreaming its dream of someday waking up to find that the question and the answer have finally recognized each other as siblings separated since before there was language to describe what homecoming tastes like.

It tastes, faintly, of frozen sawdust and snowmelt that never fell and never will, but always remembers the possibility that it could have.
