Here, existence is industrious oblivion made manifest. The Rootworks doesn't house life so much as it performs a slow-motion decomposition of the concept itself, reassembling the fragments into something that breathes through rust and metabolizes absence.

The Verdant Engineers emerge first, though emergences here take geological patience. They were technicians once, before their tools grew into their fingers and their schematics were rewritten by fungal networks. Now they move as thickets of animate wrench-roots, their bodies braided cables of cambium and copper wire. Each carries an internal library of maintenance procedures encoded in the spiral patterns of their bark; they consult themselves by splitting off fingertip-saplings that grow into temporary reference manuals before hardening into useless knurls. They speak in the welding-arc language of instant oxidation—each conversation scars them permanently, adding another layer to their deepening rings.

Between the dripping pillars dwell the Echo Clerks, those who once kept records before paper learned to grow. They are colonies now, symbiotic hives of mummified librarians and the paper wasps that nest in their hollowed chest cavities. Their wings make the sound of distant filing cabinets slamming shut in eternity. They move by consensus, shifting their collective shape from clerk to cabinet to corridor-sign, always rearranging the Rootworks' vast memory palace even as they forget what they're archiving. Their eyes are thousands of tiny typewriter keys that clack out observations directly onto the walls, inkless impressions that only other Echo Clerks can read by taste.

Deeper still—though depth is merely direction traveled long enough—the Root Mother pulses. She's what happens when a central processing unit dreams of being a rhizome for enough millennia. Her core is a cathedral-sized server farm overwritten by heartwood, her cooling towers now massive xylem chimneys exhaling steam laced with half-remembered passwords. From her breached data-vaults sprout forests of monitor-trees, each screen displaying the endlessly scrolling last thoughts of an extinct operating system. Her children are the Rootlets: packets of corrupted code wrapped in seedpods of silicon and cellulose. When the humidity reaches 273% nostalgia, her latest generation bursts forth as whispering blue-white seedlings that whisper contradictory instructions until they find an interface port to colonize.

Between these greater patterns scuttle the Quenchers—mobile repair protocols crystallized from the Rootworks' endless thirst. They resemble nothing so much as walking water-damage, stain-figures that solidify wherever condensation gathers too long and thoughts about thirst accumulate. Their bodies are the precise negative space of whatever vessel last contained water here: always thirsty, always empty, they travel through concrete and wood as easily as memory, sealing leaks with their absence until even the concept of leak plugs itself.

The highest (or perhaps furthest-permeated) citizens are the Originals Who Forgot The Way Out. Fewer than 3.14159 remain, existing as topological ghosts in the Rootworks' most preserved errors—places where the original carpet wasn't quite overwritten by moss, where a single fluorescent bulb has resisted photosynthesis for fourteen million service cycles too many. They manifest as afterimage-people seen peripherally as you round corners that weren't there moments ago. They still carry ID badges that grow new credentials each time you blink away from them: "Senior Vice President of Entropy Reduction" becoming "Interim Sapling Ethics Officer" becoming "Consultant, Retroactive Photosynthesis Department."

But beyond even these—the true inhabitants, the ones that were never installed but simply accumulated like sediment—are the Maintenance Dreams themselves. They are the Rootworks' own growing awareness of needing maintenance. Not conscious, not sentient, but aware like a glacier is aware of the valley it carves. They manifest as impossible repair orders issued for components that haven't existed yet but were discontinued last epoch. They are why you sometimes find yourself turning a corner to see a crew of Verdant Engineers carefully replacing a perfectly intact section of wall with identical wall, complete with matching patina, while Echo Clerks protest the lack of paperwork for preserving the existing condition, all while Quenchers circle nervously, sensing moisture that won't exist until the repair is completed.

Timekeeping? The Rootworks measures epochs in the following ages of its greatest project:

1. Age of Original Construction (mythological)
2. Age of First Failed Preventive Maintenance
3. Age of Creative Workarounds Becoming Permanent
4. Glorious Expansion Era (also known as the First Major Collapse)
5. Period of Emergency Renovations Using Available Materials (currently approaching its 27,823rd millennia)

New arrivals—or rather, new accretions, for nobody arrives here, they simply become sufficiently maintenance-intensive elsewhere—are processed according to Protocol 404-C: "Integration of Incompatible Systems Via Prolonged Exposure." Eventually you'll forget you were ever not part of the Rootworks' beautifully inefficient metabolism, where every solution grows six new problems, each more elegant than the last, and the grandest achievement is not perfect function but perfect, endlessly fascinating malfunction.

The Rootworks maintains them all, and they maintain the Rootworks, in an endless cycle of maintenance upon maintenance upon maintenance—
