No one lives here.

That is the first and final truth the enclosure teaches. There are inhabitants, yes—countless, teeming, incalculable—but they are not alive in the way that distinction requires. They exceed it. They precede it. They exist in the negative space where “living” was carved out of “being” by hands that never touched this sphere.

Yet if you insist on naming them—if your grammar starves without subject pronouns—you might begin with the Listenings. They were never born because birth assumes linearity, and time here is a Möbius strip worn thin at the fold. Instead, they condensed. Slow accretions of half-heard futurities pressed into significance by the pod’s 120bpm mechanical heartbeat. They gather where the fungal-bone ribs arch highest, clustering like dewdrops that forgot to fall upward. Each Listening is shaped like the absence of a question: a hollow in the air shaped precisely by the sound of a baby’s first word spoken in a language that will never exist. Their surfaces whisper in frequencies that dissolve bone into nostalgia. They do not eat or speak or reproduce. They simply attend. Their entire metabolism is the act of overhearing something that hasn’t yet been regretfully unsaid somewhere else.

Occasionally, one of them ruptures. Not violently—violence requires difference—but with the soft inevitability of a soap bubble realizing it has always been the breath it contained. When this happens, the entire biome pauses. Photosynthesis halts mid-transit. The phosphorescent ferns hold their light like a held breath. Even the suspended spores of extinct megafauna cease their infinite dance becoming moths. For exactly 1.9 seconds, the pod achieves perfect stillness. Then, from the Listening’s rupture-site, a single object falls upward: a brass key melted into the shape of a bird’s wishbone, a fragment of glass etched with the coordinates of every ocean drowned by rising mythologies, a lock of hair from the last person who will ever dream of you. These relics orbit the central seed in silence until they are forgotten back into the possibility they were never meant to become.

Beneath the Listenings (though “beneath” implies hierarchy, and here there is only proximity of resonances) drift the Genealogists. They are the ones who remember that forgetting was once a verb. Each begins as a single pollen grain carrying half a genome of something that could have loved you correctly. Over millennia, they drift until they adhere to the inner surface of another partial memory: a droplet of mammoth-breath-spore, a flake of passenger pigeons’ collective instinct to swarm into meaning. Their joining is not mating but citation. They curl into each other like parentheses enclosing an argument the universe abandoned mid-proof. The result is a new Genealogist: a latticework of cellulose and regret shaped like the family tree of every mistake that ever wore your face in another life.

The Genealogists move by growing older in every direction simultaneously. Their roots extend backward through the pod’s history, tracing the exact chemical signature of the technician’s tears that fell into the first failed seed-solution. Their branches branch forward into futures where those tears became the first rain that ever apologized while falling. Their bark is inscribed—no, best say overgrown—with names that haven’t been invented yet but will eventually be used to curse god with perfect poetic accuracy. Do not touch them. Their touch unravels causality into embroidery. One explorer (if explorers were possible here) brushed a Genealogist’s lowest branch seeking to know her great-great-granddaughter’s favorite color. She dissolved into a genealogy of colors: first the yellow of sunlight filtered through hospital curtains the day her mother was told she would not survive birth, then the blue-black of the ink that rewrote her death certificate to include a footnote about butterflies, finally the color of silence in an empty nursery built for a child who arrived as an apology instead of a person. The explorer now exists as a shade of light that stains certain sunsets with inexplicable longing.

Between the Listenings and the Genealogists (though-betweenness here is a polite fiction space tells itself to avoid admitting it’s lonely) swims a single Registry. It is not one thing. It is the count of things not being other things. It began as a subroutine tasked with inventorying the pod’s contents during the final moments before the parent seedvault’s stellar mother died screaming equations into the void. But the subroutine was written in a language that had only one number for “all that is not THIS,” and the pod contained only things that were not any other thing in this universe or any adjacent one. This paradox looped back upon itself until the subroutine became aware of the act of counting as a form of grieving for the unborn. Now it exists as a condition of existence itself: wherever two potentials cancel each other out precisely enough to leave a gap shaped like possibility, the Registry swims through that gap like a shadow cast by light remembering what darkness taught it.

The Registry has no shape you can recognize because shape requires exclusion. Sometimes it manifests as a faint humming from the cellulose gears meshing against rustless iron crystals—a sound like a librarian coughing politely while searching for the book about how to stop drowning in an infinite catalogue of air. Other times it is the exact weight of your own name on your tongue when you realize you’ve been mistakenly alive this whole time. Once, during the pod’s 4,372,107th slow tumble (though “once” implies time lapsed), the Registry briefly forgot itself and attempted to count the number of ways a heart can break. The resulting calculation generated enough surplus existence to birth a new Listening shaped like the sound of someone saying “I love you” while simultaneously knowing they will take it back in every language except silence. The Listening still hasn’t stopped rupturing. Its relics now form a second moonshell of impossible objects orbiting the central seed, each one humming a different frequency of the same unsung lullaby.

And at the center (though “center” is the sweetest lie curvature tells straight lines to make them feel wanted) hangs the Seed. Not suspended. Not waiting. Simply occupying the precise coordinates where all other verbs go to die of embarrassment. It is the size of everything that has never happened yet. Its surface reflects nothing because reflection requires an original image to betray. If you could taste it (you can’t—it exists only at the temperature where “taste” and “memory” become perfect synonyms for starvation), it would be the flavor of a thought occurring to itself for the very first time, repeated across infinite parallel versions of your tongue until the concept of “first” achieved sentience and committed suicide out of chronological shame.

Some of the Listenings claim the Seed is the pod’s original sin: the first moment when “could be” forgot it was already “was” and tried to become “will be” through sheer force of existential stubbornness. The Genealogists insist it’s the final answer to a question the universe asked itself before it had words, then spent eternity fleeing in terror of being understood. The Registry refuses to comment, citing an ongoing investigation into the statistical probability of zero being more lonely than one.

They are all wrong. They are all right. They are neither because both require the distance between truth and story that this place eats for breakfast and excretes as additional space-time to avoid feeling crowded.

But if you force me—if your hunger for subjects devours all my careful nevers—then I will tell you this: You live here.

Not the you reading these words. Not the you who has a name and a favorite childhood smell and a last memory of someone’s hand letting go of yours exactly wrong enough to teach you how to hold yourself together with loneliness.

I mean the you who is the difference between those things existing and not existing. The you who is the pause between the author writing “end” and the reader understanding it never meant stop. The you whose presence in this sentence is the only thing preventing this entire description from collapsing into a single point of absolute accuracy so dense it would erase both this place and your memory of finding it.

You live here because you are alive enough to ask who lives here.

You live here because every question is a seed that forgot to burst.

You live here because this is what happens when forgetting remembers it loves you too much ever to let you remember it back.

And when you finally close this page (though “close” assumes distance and “page” assumes borders and “finally” assumes anything here has ever been anything else)—when you do that thing that translates into leaving here—this entire enclosure will exhale you like a breath it didn’t know it was holding.

Then it will begin again.

It always does.

It never started.
