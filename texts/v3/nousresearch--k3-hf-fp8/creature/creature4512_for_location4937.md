The Refurbitioners live here, though "live" is a term they've renegotiated through decades of slow-motion barter with the air itself. They are the descendants of the original palace paperkeepers who followed the dust-cloud down not because they were ordered to, but because someone had to remember how to file absence properly under "APPROXIMATELY PERMANENT BUT POSSIBLY REVERSIBLE WITH SUFFICIENT DOCUMENTATION."

Imagine a people whose bones grew around their first pens like trees swallowing fences. Their ribs are hollowed card catalogs; their teeth print tiny index numbers when they bite too hard. They speak exclusively in footnotes that drift upward like smoke rings, each clarification requiring two more clarifications until conversations become breathable lace curtains hanging between them. When two Refurbitioners marry, they don't exchange rings—they swap filing cabinets and spend the wedding night discussing optimal folder hierarchies for their collective regrets.

Their children aren't born but discovered during annual Archive Drifts, when loose memories accumulate in the corners of the Hall of Misplaced Tuesdays like dust bunnies made of someone else's childhoods. The first Refurbitioner to properly catalog a foundling's most embarrassing memory (including cross-references to related emotional weather patterns and the exact flavor of forgotten candy associated with it) becomes that child's tertiary parent-in-documentation.

And they eat nothing but source materials: bread baked from the wheat fields painted in frescoes now reduced to pigment dust, wine fermented from the italicized adverbs in love letters dissolved by centuries of kisses, cheese aged in the hollow spaces between words cut from decrees no one ever enforced. Each meal requires a citation ceremony that lasts longer than digestion, and their poop comes out as those little paper tabs you used to find in library books, whispering sanitized summaries of whatever narrative you've just excreted.

Sleep is their most dangerous activity—they must do it in shifts, always reminding each other exactly what they're dreaming lest the dreams re-catalog themselves overnight. Their worst nightmare isn't falling, it's being re-shelved in the wrong section of themselves. They keep dreamcatchers turned inside out to ensure they catch every dream exactly as it was dreamt, no improvements allowed. Their poets are considered dangerously radical because they once rhymed "remember" with "dismember" without filing a proper form in triplicate first.

When strangers like you arrive, they don't ask your name. They ask your ISBN. They don't welcome you—they add you to the inventory with a provisional classification tag tied to your left earlobe (unless you've had it pierced, in which case it's the right, rule 47-B subsection "EARS, ALTERED"). They'll show you to your quarters not by pointing but by handing you a photocopy of a map that will become slightly more accurate with each rooms you accidentally find first.

The oldest among them are barely visible anymore—grown so perfectly into their work they've achieved archival transparency. You glimpse them only in peripheral angles: a protruding elbow that turns out to be a misplaced manuscript lever, a cough that sounds exactly like pages turning, a shadow behind a shadow that's probably just the original floor plan sulking because nobody follows it exactly anymore.

And yet—they're happy here in their way, which isn't happiness as you understand it but something closer to the satisfaction of a library book that's finally been returned with all its original marginalia intact, plus exactly one new note written in a handwriting that doesn't match any known language but somehow clarifies everything.

Don't ask them what they're refurbishing. They've forgotten. Or rather—it's been filed so appropriately under "ONGOING PROJECT, PURPOSE CLASSIFIED PENDING FURTHER ARCHAEOLOGICAL HUMILITY" that even if they remembered, the act of explaining would require restructuring the entire classification system they've been using to forget it with.

They'll offer you tea, though. It's always exactly the temperature of that moment when you realize you've been reading the same paragraph for fifteen minutes without comprehending it but don't mind because the words themselves taste familiar. Drink it slowly. Their cups don't have bottoms, just increasingly detailed references to other cups you might have enjoyed more under different circumstances.

Welcome, they say without saying. Welcome or unwelcome, same thing really, just different cataloging choices. They'll make a lovely card for you either way, filed under "VISITORS, POTENTIALLY RECURRENT, EMOTIONAL RECEPTIVITY UNKNOWN BUT ASSUMED BURIED SOMEWHERE IN THE APPENDICES."

The tea is getting cold now. Or perhaps you're getting warm. In the Refurbished Catacombs of the Once-Palace, these distinctions are maintained by an entirely different department. Shall I wrap your uncertainty to go? They recycle very efficiently here—anything you don't take with you will be thoroughly documented and returned to you in dreams you won't remember having had.
