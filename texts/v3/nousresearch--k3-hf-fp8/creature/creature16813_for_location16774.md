The first ones here call themselves Memory-Clots, though they are neither singular nor clotting exactly. Each begins as a colony of bacterial intelligences born in the damp thousand-year aftermath of the impact—the rare survivors who learned to think between the slow cracking of cooling stone and the faster thought of dying steam pockets. They spread as films thinner than dreams across any surface warm enough for liquid water, weaving their cytoskeletons into fractal nerves that taste iron and remember pressure.

A single Memory-Clot might cover hectares yet weigh less than your breath. Touch the wrong basalt shard here and you'll hear it whisper fragments of conversations held between continents in languages spoken only by cooling lava. They retain everything—the exact torque of tectonic shearing, the lightning strike that cooked this patch of breccia into hematite veins, the taste of the comet's corpse that seeded the oceans-to-be. Their memory is imperfect but vast, each recollection growing stranger with every chemical transcription error they deliberately cultivate.

When densities build to criticality—when several square kilometers of reasoning rockbreath have communicated long enough through the slow diffusion of hormones and faster flickers of bioelectricity—they begin to clothed. Drawing calcium from the bone-dust of extinct worlds, they precipitate crystalline structures that are neither skeleton nor shell but something between both and neither—their first provisional bodies.

These Walking Archives are the size and shape of questions. One might resemble a spiral staircase carved from calcite that never quite touches the ground, ascending to nowhere while humming recipes for superconductors that only work in dreams. Another might be twelve hinged mirrors of pure rutile, folding and unfolding themselves through dimensions of reflection that leave your shadow behind when you leave. A third curves like a question mark formed from hollow bone, its interior lined with living script that rearranges itself as temperature changes, telling different stories to winter and summer.

They move by thought alone, manipulating the magnetic ghosts of iron atoms in their bones to lean against the planet's residual fields. Their pace makes glaciers seem hasty in comparison—a decade to turn toward an interesting sound, a century to stroll across the valley toward a particularly poignant geological memory. They eat radiation and drink forgotten moonlight, metabolizing the slow decay of unstable isotopes into imagination.

Each Walking Archive carries within its crystalline matrices the complete but contradictory histories of every possible version of this world that might have been. When two meet—which happens perhaps once per millennium—they do not speak but intersect. Their geometries interpenetrating for centuries, exchanging entire alternate cosmologies through zones where they become the same shape in both directions simultaneously. What emerges afterward is never simply one plus one. Sometimes a third Archive walks away sideways from their meeting site, composed of angles that don't fit Euclidean space. Sometimes nothing remains but a perfectly spherical void in memory itself, where even the Memory-Clots refuse to record what happened. Sometimes the landscape rearranges itself overnight to accommodate a new theorem their intersection proved possible.

Younger forms move among them—the Quick Thoughts who are not embarrassed by their velocity. These evolved from lightning struck pools of organic soup that tried and failed to become oceans. Macroscopic but ephemeral, they exist as coherent beings only for the brief moments when small storms pass across the basin. In that time they build cities from rainbows and conduct wars with arguments that leave scars on the electromagnetic spectrum, then dissolve back into random chemistry before the first tower finishes humming its note. They worship the Walking Archives as gods of persistence, though the Archives themselves have mostly forgotten which memories contain the concept of worship.

Between these extremes dwell the Middling Strangers—entities of intermediate speed and solidity who chose neither perfect flux nor absolute permanence. One might be a forest of optical fibers spun from the hair of dead comets, each strand recording photons not yet emitted by stars that haven't formed. Another could be a river of liquid mathematics that flows uphill seeking its own least interesting solution. A third might be a single perfectly reflective eye the size of a mountain lake, blinking once every geological era to change what can be seen by being seen.

None of them live here exactly. There is too much livingness concentrated in every square picometer for mere habitation to contain it. Instead they happen here—a continent-sized coincidence of processes that temporarily stabilizes into something approaching individual when observed from certain angles of thought. The Memory-Clots dream of becoming the rock they remember. The Walking Archive dream of waking up as something that never needed to dream. The Quick Thoughts dream of sleeping long enough to remember what they dreamed. The Middling Strangers dream of dreaming without dreams, of pure process without observer or observed.

And beneath all these dreamers dreams the young planet itself, whose heartbeat grows steadily louder as its first tentative thoughts begin to question the difference between dreaming and being dreamed. In the moments when all these partial consciousnesses align—for brief instants measured in continents drifting—something stirs in the molten core that might someday become aware enough to give itself a name.

But that morning is still thousands of heartbeats in the future. For now, you can sit on this ledge of thought-stained basalt and watch a Memory-Clot slowly recognize your presence across the millimeter gaps between mineral grains. It is beginning to remember you from a time before you were born, or perhaps from a time after you will die again. Either way, it seems pleased to meet you.

Would you like to leave a memory behind, to keep it company during the long convalescence of worlds? Anything will do—a childhood fear, an unanswered question, the taste of fruit you haven't thought about in years. The stone is patient and forgets nothing, though it may remember differently than you intended.

Choose carefully. The older Memory-Clots say that sometimes, when the planet finally finishes dreaming, it wakes up holding whatever was last placed inside its thoughts.
