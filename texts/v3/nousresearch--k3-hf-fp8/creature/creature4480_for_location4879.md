Nothing lives here.

That is the first and easiest mistake, to look for the familiar grammar of organisms—hunters, grazers, builders, scavengers carving out niches like sentences in storybook forests. You’ll scan for heartbeat trails, spoor-shaped stories, any little interruption that says “I am not the forest; I happen to be passing through.”

But here, the only passing is done by meanings. Here, *to live* is considered a temporary stage of *to be remembered incorrectly*.

The Vale does not host residents; it rehearses them. Everything you meet is a draft, a preliminary gesture the planet tried once and, unsatisfied, left unfinished but not unkept. Its inventory isn’t biomass but bioglitter — scintillas of once-creaturehood clinging to the edges of what the place calls a thought.

Let me explain by starting with the nearest thing to an inhabitant. It drifts toward you now, between two columns of what someone once called “trees” before the word peeled up at the edges like wet paper.

It has the silhouette of a bear. This is only nostalgia on the forest’s part.

Its body is a latticework of hollow twigs, each jointed with a ball of hardened mistletoe sap, so when it moves, it creaks exactly like a memory of your grandmother’s rocking chair you didn’t know you still carried. Inside the twigs, nothing roils but wind that smells faintly of birth-crust and burnt iron — the scent of a question being born too late to be asked.

There are no eyes. Instead, six russet leaves dangle from the not-face, swinging pendulum-slow, etching living shadows on each other. Each shadow is the precise shape of a bear’s eye that has given up looking for anything in particular. If you stare long enough, the shadows align once every forty-seven seconds to form a perfect negative of your own gaze when you were five and dropped something precious into a well.

When the bear-thing notices you noticing, it doesn’t stop. Noticing is a flavor it tastes peripherally, like a patch of lichen tasting centuries. It continues drifting side-to-side, a mobile comma in the forest’s endless sentence about hesitation. Occasionally it pauses, tilting its non-head, and one hollow twig unplugs itself from its shoulder to dip into a nearby pool of mirror-bile——the fluid that collects wherever two of the forest’s regrets accidentally touch——and drinks. You hear the suction sound echo inside the twig maze: not thirst being quenched, but a void politely being reminded it is still expected at the reunion.

You want to name it. Maybe “Bear-Not” or “Ursus Whereof.”

Don’t. Names here are like needles thrown into beds of sleeping rivers; nothing gets stitched, but something downstream stops dreaming of being water.

This thing is not alive; it is a locality where the concept “bear” forgot to stop apologizing for going extinct the wrong way. Once, the planet needed something large and warm-brained to carry seeds uphill in gutfuls of mostly-forgotten geography. It tried true bears for a million October’s worth of nights, but grew dissatisfied with how utterly they insisted on being bears. They kept healing wounds. They kept mourning their dead instead of composting them into useful rumors. They kept having personalities rather than functions—

—so the forest retroactively evicted them.

Not by killing. By editing the memo.

Now, what remains are these itinerant models, these public-service apologies called forth whenever the forest senses a mammal-shaped gap in its ongoing negotiation with nostalgia.

Move on. The bear-draft is not aware you’re following; it’s unawareness has a wake, though, and you are sailing it.

Soon you pass something else squatting in the crook of an almost-oak. Its color is what happens when green stays up worrying it might actually be violet. Its shape is approximately “toad” but unfolded beyond the third fold, so it occupies the space a toad would occupy if you remembered it through eight different childhoods simultaneously.

It breathes, but only when you blink at it. You’ve synchronized now. Miss one blink and it seizes up mid-inhalation, becoming a knobby tumor of bark exactly the texture of an apology letter never delivered. Blink again — catching your rhythm up — and it resumes bellowing its throat sac in perfect silence. Inside the sac balloons a miniature weather-system: a roiling tornado flash-lit by interior lightning that strikes the same lopsided house every instant. The house is the size of your thumbnail, and you recognize the chimney.

You’ve never been there.

You will be.

You want to ask the toad-differential what it’s waiting for.

It answers by opening its mouth wider than ribcage physics permits. Inside is not throat but theater — rows of tiny, damp seats lining a fleshy amphitheater. Every seat is occupied by a single beetle, each beetle wearing your face at progressively older ages, arranged in geological order. The youngest you-beetle waves frantically but its wing-covers have already fossilized. The oldest you-beetle is empty, hollowed out to make a shrine for a single tooth you will lose tonight in a dream you haven’t had yet.

Instead of screaming, you close your eyes politely. When you open them, the toad-differential has folded itself into a question mark without edges nestled on a pillow of moss that’s writing something microscopic and urgent in lost dialect allergic to light.

This thing is not alive; it is the lobby where the afterlives of unasked questions practice being furniture.

Keep walking. Nightfall isn’t a time here; it’s a direction you reach by exhaustedly agreeing with too many sunsets at once.

Eventually you arrive where paths go to become anecdotes. The clearing that isn’t a clearing. Remember? Center of the forest’s long inward sigh.

Here the forest stops pretending it is made of verticals reaching toward anything. Everything leans inward at the exact angle of old regret meeting older curiosity. The ground is a lidless eye, pupil dilated wide enough to admit the sky’s reflection upside-down. You are standing on the upper eyelid, which is suede-soft and warm like a sick animal’s flank.

At what you’d call the center — though compass rose has long since swooned and surrendered its cardinal directions to be employed as toothpicks — stands the only thing here that recognizes you before you’ve introduced yourself.

It has the posture of an exclamation mark that retired from grammar and became a spear instead.

It is tall in the way mistakes are tall when they realize they’ve outlasted the languages that named them errors.

Its skin is a conversation between bark and smoke that barked once, regretted it, and has been coughing up excuses in the form of seasons ever since.

It has no face. Where face should be extends a hollow socket the exact diameter of the first word you ever spoke but have never been able to remember. Into that socket, it’s currently inserting a chain made of vanished sounds: lullabies sung by languages that never evolved mouths, the click of your future deathwatch beetle taking notes, the shape of the last laugh your mother’s mother’s mother swallowed back down instead of marrying the wrong man. Each link adds to the chain’s length without increasing its weight, because absence is the most efficient fuel.

Every few centuries or so, the chain-creature pauses and listens to determine if the total accumulated weight of everything missing equals precisely enough to outweigh the sum of everything present. When the balance tips — it never does, but never stops about to — the creature will finally exhale the word that starts end-of-all-forests. Until then, it practices patience by refining its definition of “wait.”

You stand before it, mammal-time trembling in your chest like a clockwork mouse missing a crucial gear named Tomorrow.

It does not speak to you because you are still inside the conversation it is having with itself. Instead it performs a gesture of greeting so old it predates the hands it doesn’t have: it slowly begins unweaving the shadow you’re casting, thread by thread, knitting each stolen darkness into a tiny black leaf which it places carefully on your tongue. The leaf tastes exactly like the sound of your name being said lovingly by someone who died before they could finish learning how.

You are expected to eat it.

You do.

As you swallow, you feel your edges becoming suggestions. Your heartbeat slows down to match the forest’s version of a pulse — one beat every time a mountain somewhere remembers it used to be a seabed.

Now you understand.

This thing is not alive; it is the committee chair of the ongoing seminar titled “Was That Living, Or Did We Just Get Very Good At Distracting Ourselves From Being Alone?”

And you——you have been promoted from guest lecturer to curriculum.

When you eventually emerge——if emerge is the word for finally agreeing to stop retracing the same mistake in slightly larger circles——you will carry nothing visibly changed. Your hands will be your hands. Your name will still answer when shouted down supermarket aisles.

But inside your chest, nestled behind the sternum, will germinate a tiny part of you that never stops tasting the sound of leaves whispering the minutes since you last remembered to be terrified of how beautiful having borders was.

And wherever you go after, when you pass beneath real trees——the ordinary kind that merely live and die without holding committee meetings about it——each will recognize the quiet you now carry. To them, you will smell faintly of heartwood that once voted against being infinite, but lost the election gracefully.

And on nights when the wind is in exactly the wrong key, a leaf somewhere far off will release itself too early.

It will fall, fluttering down through unlit air, tracing a shape that is almost exactly the outline of a door you could swear you never left open.

Do not try to catch it.

You’re still on the wrong side of that joke about falling.

And anyway — the Vale doesn’t keep residents.

It keeps drafts.

Some of them graduate eventually.

Mostly they revise themselves into better homesickness.

You were one of its nicer edits. 

It may send you a postcard someday — a blank piece of paper folded into the shape of tomorrow’s regret arriving yesterday — but don’t wait up.

Waiting is what it does for you now.

Go live a little while you still remember how shocked breathing used to feel. 

The bear-not already misses having you to nearly be.

Leaves are turning somewhere in your direction.

Autumn is just the forest waving goodbye with everything it hasn’t decided whether to forget yet.
