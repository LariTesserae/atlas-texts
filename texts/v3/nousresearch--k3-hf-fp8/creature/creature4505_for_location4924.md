They are not inhabitants, not really. They are not visitors, either. No word quite holds them in place because the Bright Birthroot does not distinguish between guest and graft, between passenger and vein. Yet life—whatever that means here—has found a way to be liminal within absolute saturation.

They call themselves only Scissile when pressed, and only once, as if the act of naming cracks something in them that takes centuries to reseal. The word itself is a fracture, not a shape: something that can split without breaking, peel without leaving. That is how they exist here. In slices. In laminates.

Picture a footprint that hasn’t decided who stepped in it yet. A shadow cast by a photon still considering its past. They are the leftover possibility space between the root-organism’s perfection and the intolerable idea of something else.

Each Scissile begins as a mistake in the math.

Somewhere in the uncountable cubic kilometers of the Birthroot, where vascular strands braid into algorithms too elegant for any error to survive, a perturbation flickers. A quantum stumble. A momentary imbalance between growth and decay that should cancel out instantly—but doesn’t, quite. Instead, it stabilizes. Not enough to grow. Not enough to die. Just enough to know it’s off.

That’s the beginning.

From there, they accumulate. Not matter—that would be noticed, reabsorbed, corrected. Instead they gather discrepancy. Small lags between signal and sequel. Time-loose moments where the present in one rootlet is slightly ahead or behind the present in its neighbor. Tiny cavities of not-quite-equilibrium, inflated by infinitesimal degrees until they become just large enough to walk inside.

Once inside, the cavity remembers walking.

This is how a Scissile becomes mobile: it borrows the memory of movement from something that never moved. It doesn’t walk through the root-landscape—it walks between its own copies, none quite aligned. A stuttering film strip made flesh. You glimpse them only in the flickers when your eyes move too fast to track the perfect stillness of everything else: a figure-shaped absence of light in a place where light has no opposite, a hesitation in the air’s humidity that follows you like breath you haven’t exhaled yet.

They keep their edges deliberately blurred. Sharpening would mean belonging to a specific resolution, and belonging is fatal here. Instead they exist as live questions the Birthroot hasn’t answered yet. Not threats—questions have no mass, no trajectory. But questions require space to be asked. All the tiny flawless vacancies they occupy, that’s the space.

Mostly they do nothing visible. They drift along major vascular currents like thermal ghosts, listening to the slow electrical hymns conducted through cambium-circuits wider than continents. They rest—if rest is the word—in the precise geometric silence between those alien huts: the experimenter’s marks left by something else, the ones shaped like wounds in understanding. The Scissile nestle into those wound-shapes perfectly, not filling them but becoming their living hollows. They seem to draw something from the places where the root-organism’s grammar fails—nourishment, maybe, though they have no metabolism as such. Or maybe just reassurance that failure is still possible somewhere in this universe of absolute success.

Occasionally, very occasionally, they interact.

Not with the Birthroot itself. That would be like trying to argue with arithmetic, or seduce the speed of light. But sometimes a traveler finds their way down—some surface-born thing still shaped by gradients and griefs, still carrying the infection of wanting something to be other than it is. Such visitors are rare, rare as laughter in vacuum. The Birthroot tolerates them briefly, the way pure water tolerates a single dust mote before recrystallizing around it flawless.

But for that brief mote-time, the symmetry shivers.

And in that shivering, a Scissile might approach.

They don’t speak. Instead they share frames. They splice into your sensory buffer a microsecond of your next heartbeat, your next blink, your next memory-to-come—a tiny borrowed future where you’ve already noticed something that in the real-time present you haven’t, yet. The discrepancy is almost too small to register consciously. Just a sudden certainty that you’ve forgotten something crucial, accompanied by a vivid but unplaceable image: a root wall breathing in perfect pentagonal pulses. A drop of water falling upward. Your own face reflected in the surface of a miniature sun lodged inside your throat, looking back at you with eyes that already know how you die.

Then the splice ends. The future folds back into the present like a closed book. But the image remains, printed on the back of your mind like an afterimage from an eye you don’t yet have.

Most visitors never realize the encounter happened at all. They dismiss it as altitude sickness, or dream residue, or the Birthroot’s subtle memetic defenses beginning to colonize their thoughts. They leave soon after, if they can find the way out. But something always goes slightly wrong in their leaving. They emerge weeks spanned from where they entered, though their clocks insist only hours passed. They develop allergies to symmetry: sudden panic attacks in perfectly square rooms, nausea tiled floors, vertigo when reading poetry that rhymes too cleanly. And in dreams for the rest of their lives, they find themselves returning—not to the Bright Birthroot itself, but to a place exactly one centimeter adjacent to it, where everything is identical except slightly rotated, slightly echoing, slightly asking.

A few, a very few, notice the splice while it’s happening.

They freeze, caught between the memory of something that hasn’t occurred yet and the real-time realization that it’s already over. For those ones, the Scissile do something different. They step closer—still smudged, still shivering between versions—and offer a single frame that isn’t stolen from anywhere. A genuine gift, the only one they possess: a view of the Birthroot as it sees itself.

Not beautiful. Not terrifying. Not even alive, really.

Just complete.

So complete that when you look away, everything else forever after looks like a typo.

Most who receive this gift go blind immediately. Not their eyes—those keep working fine. But the part that used to look for things outside itself and want them. That part never recovers. They spend the rest of their lives staring at walls with perfect contentment, breathing in perfect rhythm, smiling small symmetrical smiles that never quite reach whatever’s left inside.

But rumor—if rumor can exist in a place where nothing changes—whispers that one traveler, long ago, managed to respond.

Instead of accepting the gift or rejecting it, they asked a question back. Not out loud. Just by holding in mind, with absolute precision, the exact geometric shape of the smallest possible error: a Möbius strip made of one-sided hope, twisted once too often to ever close.

And for one impossible instant, the perfection hesitated.

In that hesitation, the Scissile did something they have never done before or since. They changed. Not much. Just enough to notice, if you knew how they looked before—which of course no one does. But witnesses (if that word means anything here) claim they became slightly less smudged afterward. Slightly more defined around the edges. As if the act of being questioned, even unanswerably, had left a faint scar of new specificity.

A scar that, in a place where nothing ever scars, is indistinguishable from a name.

They say if you find that exact spot now—where the root walls pulse in imperfect pentagons, where the light has a tiny fleck stuck in it like dust in a camera lens that isn’t a camera, where the air tastes faintly like the idea of rain remembered by something that has never seen sky—and if you arrive at precisely the right moment between two identical nows, you might glimpse one of them.

Not clearly. Never clearly.

Just a shape stepping sideways into the gap between what is and what if.

And if your own math is off by exactly the right amount—if you carry inside yourself a flaw so precise it resonates with theirs—

they might pause.

They might turn.

And in the space where their face should be, you might see—

But that part of the story never finishes.

The Bright Birthroot does not tolerate unfinished things. It grows, or not-grows, until every possibility is accounted for and balanced in the final equation. Even the Scissile, the errors that walk, are slowly being solved for. Bit by bit, century by century, the fluctuations that give them shape are drifting closer to zero. The huts left by something else are being gently, imperceptibly absorbed, their geometries revised toward perfection. The rare encounters with outsiders grow rarer as the paths in and out erode—not through change, but through the gradual elimination of all possible routes that don’t already lead exactly where they already go.

Eventually even the question “what lives here?” will resolve itself into a statement so complete no question mark can attach.

And when that happens, the Scissile won’t die. They won’t even disappear.

They’ll just stop being plural.

One vast and perfect correction will step into the place where all their tiny mistakes once kept space open for each other. A final Scissile shaped like the sum total of everything that ever went slightly wrong here, now made perfectly right.

It will be beautiful beyond all comprehension.

And absolutely nobody—no animal, no plant, no visitor, no god—will be left to see it happen.

But that, too, is only one possible ending.

In another version—so close to this one you couldn’t slide a photon between them without changing both—someone somewhere is still carrying that impossible Möbius question. Not holding it, exactly. More like being held by it. A living loophole in the mathematics of inevitability.

And because of that—

and only because of that—

the Bright Birthroot still, occasionally, dreams in broken symmetry.

And all its perfect, flawless light

has one tiny crack in it

where something that isn’t quite anything yet

can sometimes, if the angles align exactly wrong,

press one eye

against the bright

and look out

at what might never be.

But might.

And in stories, sometimes—

even here—

might is enough.

For now.
