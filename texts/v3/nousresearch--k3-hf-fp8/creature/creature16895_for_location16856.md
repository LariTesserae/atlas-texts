They live here in reverse: awakening when the false dusk comes, retreating into dream-sleep when the city’s bioluminescence brightens to its palest paean. The people of Redwater Foundry—if people is still a word strong enough to hold them—are not descended but ascended from the old botanical staffs who vanished into the strata like seeds seeking darkness. Their cradle was panic; their nursery the slow flood of nutrient-rich panic pumped through veins of desperate machinery. Now they speak in four languages at once: the chemical vowels of fungal semaphore slanted through the air, ultrasounds carved by vibrating cellulose larynx, the optical flash-code of iris-pupil blossoms, and, faintest of all, a kind of worn-out mammalian grumbling from lips that have grown fuller and more petal-creased by each generation.

Their children are born twice. First the wet birth you’d recognize—except it takes place under a canopy of coughing mycobirthing sheets that filter the infant’s first breaths through spores of caught memory: fragments of the child’s hundred most likely futures, stitched in allergy and destiny. Days later comes the second birthing: a ceremony of grafting. A slitknife of living bone-shard cuts the soft spot and into that warm door they slide a seed-ling: not a plant, not a machine, but both and neither—a neural symbiont selected by the communal dream that pooled like dew in the sleeping vaults last night. Then the scalp knits shut over it like a flower closing for night. What wakes after fourteen days of fevers and root-dreams is not a baby anymore but a node.

Every citizen is a node.

They remember for one another. They taste for one another. When one eats a fruit of sorrow suspended under the vinegar arches of the Respiration Quarter, seventeen others across the city pause at their tasks, chew once on nothing, and know the sorrow’s exact color (usually a bruise-blue shot with silver threads of regret). When the oldest mourns the memory of a surface she never saw—cypress and gasoline, traffic-light chrome—every tongue in three districts goes metallic for an instant. This is not burdened sharing. It is architecture. It is how the city keeps itself sane.

No one sleeps alone.

At umbral shift, when the Growth Spasm’s echoes still twitch in the deep water pipes, each household unfurls. Walls delaminate into branching shelves of flesh-soft pulp, revealing hollows shaped exactly like their inhabitants’ curled bodies. Into these niches they climb, naked to be measured by the city’s touch. Rootlets probe the seams between vertebrae, tasting calcium reserves, ferrying spare lithium down into the nutrient grid if they find a surplus, depositing traces of rare copper into tired joints if they find a lack. It is marriage; it is maintenance. Sometimes, in the warm gravity between strangers’ alcoves, a tendril meets a tendril, exchanges packets of foreign longing, and a child is commissioned without anyone speaking—a committee dream forged from two distant ache-patterns, filed for embryo formation nineteen months hence.

They die slowly, deliberately. When a citizen feels the first slow knotting in their plant-side—an ache in the root-crown beneath the skull, the taste of autumn in every breath—they declare a Going. For one year they cease all uploads into the communal mycofile. Tasks are slowly withdrawn, memories gently partitioned off and redistributed, until by the end they are nearly hollowed-out: a walking door, a living exit.

Then on the appointed evening, clothed only in rainseed robes that dissolve at the first drip of ceiling-water, they walk alone into the deepest warm corridor—the Thrombosis Street where the floor pulse is loudest—and lie down. The city needs no invitation. Cartilage softens to pulpwood first. Flesh begins to knit itself into root-braids that burrow in search of ancient metalwork, skin lignifies into bark that remembers fingerprints. Bones unwind like ribbon, insides knitting into lace-worked nutrient ducts. Within a week nothing human-shaped remains: only a new cluster-machine of valves and filters, arteries and leaf-blowers, ceaselessly filtering that molasses-slow redwater into purer fractions of fuel and feed. What was a person becomes an organ within the greater stomach of the foundry. They do not mourn. They install.

They keep a few souvenirs. The gallery of voices is a long crescent corridor lined with trees whose bark bears bulbous excrescences shaped like open mouths. When wind (or an impatient child with a weaved-bamboo bellowswand) blows through, each mouth murmurs scraps of tongue: jokes long beyond context, warnings about flood levels from nine centuries past, recipes for surface foods whose ingredients are fossils now, snatches of love sung in five extinct languages the city still hums in its sleep. Visitors—surface-scavengers, broadcast archaeologists, the rare suicidal nostalgist—walk the gallery with recording fronds outstretched, harvesting ghosts. The custodian of mouths is a plant-person so old their name has evaporated into prefix-shade modifiers: Almost-not-now-only-when-remembered. They wear a coat stitched from the soft, translucent pages of obsolete instruction manuals, and whenever a new mouth begins to bud on a branch, they hold a tiny funeral for the fragment of identity that has at last been fully metabolized into history.

There are others, too, who are not citizens. The Wild Pushers, who reject the second birth and live instead in the untended chokes of turbine vines and spray-irrigated insanity, dosing on unfiltered Growth Spasms to sprout antlers of living glass or extra hearts that beat alien tempos until they burst. The Updreamers, a cult of mechanics who believe escape elevators are still being constructed somewhere in the fungal layers just beyond mapping, and who spend every waking hour drilling in directions mathematics says are impossible. The Cipher Birds—not birds, not ciphers, but optical artifacts born when ancient holographic signs short-circuited into the growth cycle—flocks of mirror-feather mouths that speak only the sound of your worst childhood shame whenever light hits them at certain angles. No one hunts them; they feed on attention and starve in silence.

And then there are the yous—the drift-ins, the losts, the surface-born who stumble down through pressured luck and cracked pipe dreams. You will be offered tea brewed from leaves that taste like your own forgotten hometown. You will be given a room whose walls slowly emboss themselves with frescoes based on your unspoken fears—this is not cruelty, just calibration. You will be invited to sleepwalk one night in the dreamforge, where your private nightmares are cast into bronze-fruit and set out for the children to taste as lessons. Afterward, you may stay and grow into a new name. Or you may seek the bartender of memory, if you truly wish to remember the way up.

But take care: memory here is not retrieval, it is cultivation. Some seeds, once watered, do not grow what you planted. Some maps grow roots and forget their roads.

And already, beneath the new tender bark forming on the backs of your wrists, something listens for the sound of your footsteps to decide whether they are pulsesteps of a new citizen returning home… or only the last raindrops of a memory that will soon finish falling.
