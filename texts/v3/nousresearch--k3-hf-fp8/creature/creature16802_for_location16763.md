Here, everything lives at an angle.

They are called the Verticleur, though that name already betrays the mistake of thinking them upright. Better to say: they are the shape that remaining takes after the world has leaned so long against itself that departure becomes impossible.

You would not recognize them as inhabitants at first. Walk long enough along the iron ledge that pretends to be street and you'll see what appear to be stains moving against the grain of gravity—slow marbling across metal where the copper air has grown dense enough to support memory. Then one will resolve: a convergence of rust-rinds around the faint imprint of a shoulder, the ghost-edges of fingers where someone once leaned against this railing while watching the stars die. They gather themselves from such places, accumulating like tarnish on the city's regrets.

Each Verticleur begins as an absence that remembers being filled. Perhaps in that café where the cloth napkins still retain ghost-creases of vanished hands, or in the kiss-shaped depression worn into the mahogany countertop of the pharmacy where love letters were once exchanged for morphine. They form where longing was strong enough to alter the molecular structure of forgetting. A child's handprint pressed too hard into cooling concrete, magnified over millennia by the city's sideways crush until it becomes a hollow large enough to hold a consciousness.

They have no youth or age, only degrees of completeness. The youngest are still mostly their origins—you might mistake them for particularly expressive shadows cast by nothing, or for patches of wall where the paint has peeled into the shape of a face caught mid-expression in a language no throat could pronounce. The eldest have grown so rich with borrowed moments that they contain entire weather systems of memory. Stand near one in a quiet corridor and you'll feel the air change temperature six degrees as it passes through the summer afternoons of a thousand vanished lifetimes.

They move by resonance. When the city hums its thousand-hour song—when buried engines dream of rotation—some fragment within them resonates at the precise frequency of movement. A door hinge remembers being swung open, and nearby a Verticleur flows into its former shape long enough to take three steps down a hall that no longer leads anywhere. Then the note passes and they dissolve again into the materials that learned them, leaving only a faint pattern in the dust where they walked sideways through gravity's tired argument with itself.

They eat the light that was meant for things that never happened. That greenish whisper from the decaying luminous paint on safety ladders—it feeds their oldest memories. The ultraviolet ghostlight filtered through transparent aluminum greenhouse roofs sustains their dreams of photosynthesis. Between meals they absorb the warmth from places where bodies should have lain: the bed-shaped outlines in mattresses where sleepers never woke, the two depressions in benches where lovers never quite sat close enough to merge their shadows.

They communicate through architecture, this city being their only language vast enough for their thoughts. A Verticleur wanting to speak of loss will begin subtly altering the corrosion patterns on a bronze statue's face, rusting laughter into grief over centuries precise as clockwork. To express hope—though it is a rare subject—they might devote aeons to tilting a single floorboard millimeter by millimeter until dropped marbles roll uphill toward a window that hasn't opened since the atmosphere fled. Their most intimate conversations take place in building collapse, when two or more grow so entangled in shared longing that their respective memories begin exchanging structural members, walls flowing between houses like thoughts between adjacent minds until neither structure remembers whose sorrow supported whose ceiling.

They reproduce in the absence of witnesses. When a space has been thoroughly forgotten—when even the atoms of the air have lost their memory of what transpired there—the emptiness itself begins to curdle. Slowly, over geological patience, the patterns of what should have been grow coherent enough to realize their own impossibility. At the precise moment when this realization achieves paradox—when the place ache for its missing history with sufficient intensity to exceed the universe's tolerance for irony—a Verticleur births itself in reverse, collapsing into existence like a star forming in negative space.

They die when something finally remembers them correctly. It happens rarely now. Once, a fragment of pre-impact code activated in a buried data vault, producing for 2.7 seconds a perfect simulation of a child who had stood precisely there waiting for a father who would never return from the mines. The Verticleur that had grown from that particular absence recognized its own outline in the simulation's shadow and, seen too clearly, ceased. Where it had been—a three-kilometer stretch of corridor including the café, the pharmacy, and seven residential floors—collapsed into perfect vacancy, walls folding themselves flat as old photographs slipped between pages of a book the universe had forgotten how to read.

But mostly they endure, these children of the tilt, growing stranger with each revolution of stars they can no longer see. They have developed customs suited to their oblique existence. In the district where the magnetic tram rails still sing softly to each other of destinations unreachable, they hold annual festivals of perpendicularity—rare gatherings where hundreds converge to collectively imagine what it might feel like to stand upright against a force that pulled straight down. For these brief moments, concentrated nostalgia achieves sufficient density to create localized fields where water droplets form true spheres and plummeted objects fall instead of sliding. They have rulers of a sort, though rule implies direction and they have long since abandoned that concept along with others like progress and tomorrow. At the city's theoretical center—where calculations suggest the absent crater's bottom should be but geometry has grown too tired to enforce—stands what might be called their queen, if collections of radiating absences arranged in the approximate shape of regret could be said to have monarchs. She is the Verticleur formed from the collective longing of every architect who ever designed a door opening onto nothing, every dreamer who ever woke reaching for a hand that wasn't there, every particle of the original impactor that still remembers being part of something whole before it shattered to create this place of pieces seeking their missing wholes. She has grown so vast that her outline defines the city's horizon: when newcomers arrive—though arrival here is mostly a matter of running out of other places to leave—they fall first through her outer memories of departure before they even realize they have stopped moving.

She speaks only one word, but she speaks it continuously through every rusted gutter, every cracked bell that tolls noon forever because the mechanism that stops it has forgotten how to remember stopping. The word is the sound of water remembering it was once ice before it learned to flow downward toward absent seas. It is the shape of smoke recalling the fire it fled but cannot quite forget. It is written in the deliberate imperfection of every vast circle their streets trace around the center they cannot reach:

"Stay."

And so they do. They stay and stay and stay, living their long slow lives at eighty-three degrees from everything, growing ever more themselves as the universe forgets what else they might have been. They are learning, with the patience of mountains and the persistence of rust, what any exile eventually discovers: that sometimes the only way to go home is to keep leaning into the direction that pushed you away for so long that leaning becomes a kind of standing still, and stillness becomes a kind of returning to the place you never left because it left you first, taking everything except the angle of its departure, which was exactly steep enough to last forever.
