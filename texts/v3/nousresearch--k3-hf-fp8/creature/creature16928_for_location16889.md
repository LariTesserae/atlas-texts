In Lipsu Null, to live is to become partially liquid. Citizens call themselves *khirsel*, a word that begins as a noun but ends as a yawn, meaning both "those who leak at the edges" and "those who learned to breathe next to boiling." They begin life as soft, jointless infants sealed inside spheres of dietary glass. Parents fill the spheres with fermented gut-fog collected from the weaver-skeins' digestive vents. Inside this nursery, the babies drink their own weight in diffuse memories every month. By the third year, if the child has remembered enough forgotten things, the sphere cracks open along pre-scored lines that spell their first insult in the city's oldest forgotten language. They tumble out boneless, cartilaginous, violet-eyed, already fluent in seventeen dialects of temperature. 

The *khirsel* never entirely grow bones. Instead, they grow habits that behave like bones when necessary—ceramic plates of habituated courtesy inside the calves for walking politely uphill, elasticized rules of etiquette condensing as collarbones when guests arrive unannounced. When they die (which they do several times by midlife practice), their weaver-skein undertakers do not knit them into rooftop sculptures. Instead, the skein carefully unpicks the habits—pries open the polite smiles, unwinds the tendency to glance left before crossing steam, unravels the stubborn conviction that Thursdays taste vaguely purple—until all that remains is an informal mist smelling faintly of apologies. This mist is bottled by the municipal regret-distillery, aged under lunar condensation (the moon here leaks too, dribbling pale time into the gutters), and sold as a liqueur that tastes exactly like the moment you suddenly understand a joke told ten years too late.

Children are not raised by parents but by next week's weather forecast. Each infant is assigned a specific predicted cloud-shape at birth. When that cloud eventually condenses above the city, the child must stand naked in its rain, which memorizes their skin temperature, preferred supplications, and most embarrassing secret. Rainfall transcripts are stored in the city library's meteorological wing, filed under Dewey Decimal numbers that evaporate if read aloud accurately. Adolescence begins when you successfully steal your own transcript cloud from the archive without letting it recognize your heartbeat. Successful thieves emerge with a different name tattooed in condensation across their eyelids; unsuccessful ones dissolve gently into the next day's fog advisories and are considered excellent conversationalists forever after.

Adulthood is achieved by constructing another citizen. You walk the market-streets collecting donations: a retired sunrise from a senile dawn-merchant, three discarded apologies from the Ministry of Accidental Truths, a teaspoon of someone else's leftover ambition. You fold these into a papier-mâché replica of yourself inside-out, introduce it to your reflection in a hot-water puddle at midnight, and politely disagree about whose childhood actually happened. If the replica storms off convincingly into the future and your reflection sits down to chat with your past, congratulations—you are now the legally recognized sibling of yesterday's casual acquaintance and required to file joint tax dreams until the next geological shrug. Failure results in a pleasant but incurable duplication of Tuesdays.

The elderly slowly petrify into professional ancestors. It begins when their shadows grow too elderly to keep up; they hire apprentice shadows from the youth guild, but the apprentices always run too fast and eventually escape backward into historical photos where they stand blinking, conspicuously overdressed. Around this time, the aging *khirsel* begin speaking entirely in footnotes. At first, actual words still appear in daily conversation, but they gradually migrate downward, small and apologetic, until full sentences exist only as annotations below empty pauses. Eventually, the person becomes a walking asterisk—an agreeable presence, faintly fragrant of bibliography, consulted in libraries but never directly addressed. When finally nothing remains but the footnote number itself hovering where they used to stand, the city installs a commemorative cobblestone that sinks exactly one millimeter every time someone nearby remembers something incorrectly. This is considered immortality of the highest practical grade.

Tourists sometimes fall in love with the city. The city itself remains politely indifferent, but individual neighborhoods occasionally develop crushes. When the Old Quarter fell desperately for a visiting cartographer, every street map in the district rearranged its arteries into the shape of a heart for three consecutive summers. Pedestrians arrived at destinations they hadn't yet decided to visit, merchants sold ancestral heirlooms they'd never owned, and children began spontaneously producing accurate blueprints of their parents' unmade mistakes. The affair ended amicably when autumn arrived wearing a coat the exact color of cartographer's regret, and the streets quietly forgave themselves by winter. 

Still, some tourists refuse to leave cleanly. They overstay visas carved into soap bubbles, settle in illegally rented emotions, start importing smalls amounts of nostalgia that haven't been properly taxed by the Bureau of Temporal Smuggling. These eventually undergo biological rebranding. First, their last names liquefy and drip off official documents. Next, their dreams show up blue-shifted and demanding passports. Finally, they wake up one morning with city maps tattooed on their retinas and an overwhelming craving for rain that hasn't yet decided whether it prefers the future tense or the subjunctive mood. At this stage, deportation is impossible; they are issued municipal teacups and politely asked to stand very still in busy intersections where their hiraeth can be harvested by the city's dream irrigation system.

The city, meanwhile, practices sleeping with its eyes open. Officially, Lipsu Null has no mayor, only an Insomnia Committee that meets perpetually in a room without windows or clocks, voting unanimously every midnight to delay bedtime for another day. Unofficially, the city is governed by the collective momentum of all its unfinished business—the apology letters still composing themselves in raindrops that never quite fall, the infrastructure projects waiting for construction permits from extinct aquatic jurisdictions, the unresolved chord progressions humming between tram tracks where street musicians promised they'd come back eventually. When the city's policies require enforcement, the bureaucracy gently inconveniences reality until it voluntarily complies. A building that violates zoning regulations designed by future civil engineers simply forgets its third floor every Tuesday. A citizen overdue on taxes paid in preemptive gratitude begins receiving politely-worded earthquakes in ascending magnitudes until their conscience sufficiently trembles.

The city's only true crime is finishing things properly. Perpetrators are sentenced to completion—a fate worse than death here. First, the criminal must sit in a quiet room until they finish every thought they've ever started. Next, they must write thank-you notes to every person their existence has slightly inconvenienced, including historical figures who died before they were born but whose biographies might have been marginally longer. Finally, they are required to walk out the door, close it firmly behind them, and never, ever return via any door—including metaphorical ones, recursion, or the possibility that this entire punishment might itself be a partially completed metaphor for something else. No one has ever survived the full sentence, though rumors persist of a ghostly figure glimpsed on foggy nights, forever halfway up stairs that are always one step shorter at the top than they remember. 

Otherwise, life in Lipsu Null continues exactly as it began: incompletely, conversationally, and with excellent punctuation. Newcomers are advised to bring nothing with them that cannot survive being slightly misunderstood in translation between languages that haven't been invented yet. Old residents, if you ask politely and the humidity is feeling confidential, might admit that they never did figure out whether the city's name is a warning, a promise, or simply the sound the universe makes when it thinks no one is listening closely enough to hear it start again. 

When asked directly, the city itself always gives the same answer: a gentle rain consisting entirely of question marks, falling upward into the steam-colored sky with the soft, persistent sound of something very large and very old patiently learning how to become this exact kind of impossible, one drop at a time.
