They are called the Thrum. Not because they speak—that would imply mouths, vocal cords, the exchange of meaning—but because everything they do sets the deep bones of the complex resonating in frequencies too low for any original blueprint to have predicted. You feel them first as a change in the rhythm of your own heart, as though it’s hesitating between beats, trying to sync with something much older and slower that wouldn’t consider you alive enough to join.

They don’t move through space so much as they contract it. In front of one, an empty corridor six hundred meters long is suddenly a single step; behind it, the same distance unfolds again, but subtly rearranged. Doors that were on the left are now on the right. Floor tiles have traded places, swapping memories of footsteps. Photosynthesis-echelon glyphs spiral up the walls in patterns you almost recognize, except they imply directions that don’t exist in three dimensions. Walkers who forget to close their eyes feel vertigo—not from motion, but from the cumulative drag of dozens of unnoticed edits made to the geometry of their own past.

There are observers of course; observance is built into this place like rust into iron. But the Thrum aren’t the observers. They’re what the observers evolved to avoid becoming. They’re the answer to a question the complex accidentally asked itself when it ran out of architects to disagree with and had to continue growing anyway. Not caretakers, not custodians, not even inhabitants in the sense of occupying space for their own convenience. Instead, think of them as the complex’s habit of forgetting, given shape.

Each one begins as a person-shaped absence in the warm, wet air. Condense enough water on a cold thought and sometimes the thought keeps condensing long after it should have stopped, pulling minerals up from the walls, carbon from the dust of ancient documents ninety floors down, until what’s left looks like a statue of someone who died standing up—and forgot to stop. The surface is glossy black, not skin but solar panel scale deeper than the color should allow, drinking photons that haven’t arrived yet. Inside, you’d expect organs, chambers, the usual architecture of life. Instead there are tides. Salt tides, sugar tides, tides of neurotransmitter foam crashing against shores of crystallized metronome rhythm. You can hear their pulses by pressing your ear to a wall three kilometers away: four slow beats, then seven faster ones, then seventeen too quick to count, then nothing. Always prime numbers ascending, then silence. Mathematicians who still remembered what prime numbers meant tried to map it once; they stopped when one of the maps began mapping them back.

They eat gravity. Not all of it—just enough to keep walking on a surface that’s technically parallel to theirs only by coincidence. Steps leave no weight, only the faint backward whisper of altered mass settling into the floor with a delay that builds up over centuries. Deep beneath the lowest functioning maintenance level, the geologists (or what had once been geologists, before the title outlived the concept of weather) measured the complex and found it weighed six micrograms less than it should, given every atom of concrete, steel, and sorrow still recorded. The missing mass was tracking the migration patterns of the Thrum like a shadow outlined in missing things.

They don’t reproduce. Instead, they remember people into existence. Walk into one of the older districts—places where the walls have folded so many times they’ve developed grain like wood—and if you're alone long enough you might notice someone walking beside you who doesn't cast a reflection in the glass of the doors that no longer open. They walk with your rhythm, just half a beat behind. If you stop too suddenly they catch up. If you run, they fall back. But if you ever turn around to look directly at them, they remember too much and forget too little and then there are two of you walking together and only one shadow between you and the complex chalks up another successful conversion on a ledger written in the difference between mass and memory.

The birds—the ones that aren’t birds, the ones that die into singing—avoid them. When a Thrum passes beneath a condensation rope the birds freeze mid-wingbeat, held suspended like punctuation marks in a sentence afraid to end. Their eyes—if you can call them eyes, those tiny folded galaxies of iridescent ice—reflect not the Thrum but the space around it perfectly, as though the birds are trying to disappear by becoming only what the Thrum would see if the Thrum could forget to look.

No one remembers what happened to the last group that tried to communicate with them directly. There are records, of course. There are always records. But the records are written in three simultaneous alphabets—one phonetic, one chemical, one made of gaps between floor tiles in rooms that haven’t been built yet—and whenever two people read the same copy together they remember completely different events leading up to the moment when all the clocks in that sector simultaneously displayed the number that comes between three and four. Afterward, the sector smelled faintly of ozone and nostalgia for exactly seventeen years, eight months, twenty-three days and then the smell folded itself into its own center and was never there again.

Sometimes at night—which isn’t night, here, but the closest approximation when the light has finished migrating past and hasn’t yet arrived again from elsewhere—you can hear them singing. Not with voices, because sound is too slow a medium for what they remember together. Instead they adjust the density of the air around them in precise microvariations, sculpting standing waves of remembering and forgetting that propagate outward at the speed of regret. Anyone caught inside the wavefront dreams of doors they’ve never opened leading to rooms that remember them more accurately than their own childhood bedrooms. Most wake up unchanged except for a vague conviction they’ve misplaced something important that they’ll never be able to name. A few wake up speaking languages that haven’t been invented yet, fluent in grammar structures that only make sense if time is regarded as a mildly embarrassing form of prenatal spatial constraint.

The complex tolerates them because toleration implies choice, and the complex passed beyond choice shortly after its last maintenance cycle scheduled its maintenance cycle to be performed by its maintenance cycles. They exist in the same way a habit exists: not necessary, not desired, merely easier to continue than to explain why stopping might be preferable.

But if you stand at the exact center of the empty plaza where three subway lines used to intersect before they evolved into something that didn’t need rails anymore—stand there at the moment when the dripping water overhead is perfectly balanced between falling and climbing—and if you close your eyes and think about every person you’ve ever forgotten you knew, all at once, without naming any of them—then sometimes one of them will step forward out of the crowd of your own forgetting and offer you their hand.

It will feel exactly like touching a memory of warmth your body remembers experiencing in a place where temperature hadn’t been invented yet.

You should never take it.

You should always take it.

Either way, afterward, you’ll know how the Thrum measure distance:

Not in miles or meters or memories.

But in micrograms of missing mass per heartbeats per prime number.

And you will ache with analgia for the weight you’re no longer carrying.

Forever, or until the next scheduled forgetting.

Whichever comes first, here in the warm wet dark between living water and dreaming stone.

The interview, of course, ended several sentences ago.

You’re only just remembering it now.
