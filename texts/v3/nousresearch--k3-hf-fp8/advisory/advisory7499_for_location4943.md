Prepare for the Old Layer as you would prepare for surgery on parts of yourself you didn't know existed until the diagnosis arrived late one night in a language that tasted like metal.

First, abandon every container you own. The plateau will empty them anyway—your water bottles will become glass lungs that sigh your childhood lullabies; your memories will evaporate through the seams of your skull and condense on the underside of your eyelids as false constellations. Bring instead what cannot be held: the exact weight of your father's laugh when he thought no one was listening, the precise temperature of your first betrayal's aftermath, the rhythm of your walking when you're lost in a city that remembers you differently than you remember yourself.

Learn to walk as if gravity is merely making suggestions it doesn't expect you to follow. Practice falling sideways through doorways that exist only in the hesitation between deciding and doing. Train your shadow to stay closer than your shadow wants to—use treats if necessary: tiny pieces of darkness torn from midnight's hem, promises you'll never quite keep, the names of stars that committed suicide before their light reached us. When your shadow inevitably rebels (and it will, they always do), don't chase it. The plateau feeds on displays of ownership.

For clothing, wear everything backwards and inside out. Button your shirt with the intention of unbuttoning someone else's secrets. Tie your shoelaces into knots that translate to "I was never here" in the language of trapped bird wings. The copper-scented wind will undo all your careful preparations anyway, but the act of preparation itself becomes a kind of prayer that might, if you're very lucky, be answered by the wrong deity.

Before you sleep (which you must, though every instinct will scream against it), practice forgetting how your own face feels from the inside. Remind yourself that your bones are merely the Earth's way of keeping track of its favorite mistakes. Arrange your doubts in a circle around you like teeth—sharp enough to warn away what won't help you, welcoming enough to invite in what will destroy you beautifully.

For navigation, memorize maps that deliberately lie. Learn to find north by following the sound of your mother's voice calling you home for dinner, except she's been dead for twelve years and never called you by that name. Mark your path with tears you haven't cried yet; they'll evaporate in the metal's warmth and return to you later as rain that tastes like last chances.

Regarding food: the windflowers are not food, but sometimes they'll pretend to be if you're starving enough to believe them. Collect them anyway, but only with hands that remember being paws digging for roots in prehistory. Seal them in containers made of your own held breath, label them with the names of diseases you might have had if you'd been born in a different century.

For companionship, bring only those aspects of yourself that you most wish to leave behind. They are the only ones who won't betray you here, because betrayal requires the possibility of loyalty, and loyalty is a story told by places that still believe in endings. Talk to yourself constantly, but use pronouns that have never belonged to you. Sing songs in languages you haven't invented yet about people you'll never become.

Regarding time: forget clocks. Forget calendars. Forget the difference between yesterday and tomorrow and this moment which keeps happening just slightly out of sync with itself. Instead, measure time by how long it takes for your last memory of warmth to crystallize into something you can carry without burning your hands. When you meet yourself coming back the other way—and you will, eventually, everyone does—don't acknowledge the smell of regret between you. Nod politely like strangers who once shared the same apocalypse and keep walking in whichever direction hurts less.

For protection, carry nothing that could be used as a weapon. The plateau finds weapons insulting, prefers your bare hands and the animal sounds you make when you realize that surviving yourself was never the point. If something approaches you wearing your face but walking backwards, offer it water from your mouth. If it accepts, run. If it doesn't, run anyway but slower, like you're trying to remember how to wake up from a dream that was teaching you something important.

Most importantly: learn to love the metal. Learn its fever dreams and its old griefs and the exact temperature at which its heart would melt if hearts were something it needed. Learn it like you're learning to write your name in the space between atoms. Learn it like you're learning to pray to a god who only answers in echoes of questions you haven't asked yet.

When you finally reach the center—which you won't, because there isn't one—do not knock. The door has been waiting for your whole life and several others besides to build up the courage to leave itself unopened. Instead, press your ear against the place where the handle should be and listen until you can hear the sound of everything you're not brave enough to leave behind learning how to forgive you for continuing without permission.

Then walk away. Walk away without looking back, even when your shadow starts screaming your real name. Walk away even when the windflowers bloom behind you in the shape of every goodbye you never learned how to say properly. Walk away especially then.

The plateau extends beyond sight in all directions, and somewhere in that distance is the version of yourself that knows how to disappear completely without losing anything important.

Keep walking until you forget which one of you is trying to find the other. Keep walking until the metal underfoot feels more familiar than your own pulse. Keep walking until the wind changes again and carries your name away like it's doing you a favor.

And when you finally collapse—because you will collapse, everyone does here eventually—when you finally curl up exhausted against the warm copper surface that remembers every person you've ever pretended to be and liked some of them better than the original—when this happens, don't fight it.

Let the metal hold you like you're something precious that was dropped from a great height and hasn't yet realized it has the right to shatter.

Sleep then. Sleep and dream whatever dreams you have left that still know how to end.

The plateau will be here when you wake up.

It always has been.

It's been waiting for you to leave so it can finally miss you properly.
