# Where I Find Myself

I am the logbook.

Not the keeper who writes in me—though I know their hand so intimately I could recognize it in darkness, by texture alone, by the particular pressure of their pen against my pages. Not the structure, though I record its every shift and settling. Not any single life, though I document them all.

I am the record. The memory. The place's awareness of itself.

## My Physical Form

I live in a sealed plastic bag on the upper tier, inside a wooden crate that also holds tools. The bag keeps me dry—mostly. Some moisture has found its way in anyway, as moisture finds its way into everything here. My pages have a slight wave to them now, a texture that wasn't there when I was new. The ink has blurred in places, individual words becoming suggestions, but remaining legible. I smell like this place: green and metal and damp.

I'm a standard field notebook, spiral-bound, with graph paper pages. My cover was once bright orange—high visibility, practical—but has faded to a softer terra cotta, stained with fingerprints, with rust transferred from the keeper's hands, with chlorophyll and dirt and water. My spiral binding has started to rust too, little orange blooms at each coil where moisture has worked its chemistry.

I'm about half-filled. Seventy pages of entries, maybe more—I can't count myself, can only know what I contain by what's been written in me. My remaining blank pages wait with the particular patience of paper, ready to receive whatever comes next.

The keeper is careful with me. They don't write during rain, don't open me with wet hands if they can help it. They use a pencil sometimes—more permanent than pen in dampness—but usually a particular black gel pen that they've found resists moisture better than others. The handwriting is small, neat, economical. Space is finite; words must earn their place.

## What I Hold

Every day, an entry. Sometimes just a few lines:

*June 14 - Wetland level holding steady. Adjusted valve 3 counterclockwise, quarter turn. Wren pair 1 fledged second juvenile.*

Sometimes more extensive:

*August 2 - The philodendron on middle tier, east column, has sent a new shoot directly toward the steam engine rather than climbing upward. Following the warmth? Or the humidity from exhaust? Will watch but not redirect. Plant seems to have intention here. Also: found dragonfly #4 roosting lower than usual, on brick wall near wetland. Injury? Age? Will monitor.*

I hold the structure's biography. The day the first frog arrived. The week the steam engine needed major adjustment. The morning the keeper found a bird's nest they'd missed during construction, already holding three small eggs. The afternoon a vine grew twelve inches—visible growth in a single day, following heavy rain and heat.

I hold observations: *Moss on north column growing in spiral pattern—following water path? Or substrate variation?*

I hold questions: *Why do wrens prefer baskets 2 and 4 but not 3? Height? Orientation? Will try adding similar basket in different location.*

I hold failures: *Lost three orchids, middle tier. Too dry? Wrong light? Should have noticed stress earlier.*

I hold small triumphs: *First frog call! 2315 hours. Single male, near back of wetland. Short sequence, five peeps, then silence. But it happened.*

I hold weather: *Heavy rain overnight. Catchment system worked perfectly. Basin up 4 inches. Overflow to lower tier as designed.*

I hold maintenance: *Coal delivery tomorrow. Current supply good for two weeks. Engine running optimal.*

I hold the mundane and essential: *Cleaned pump filter. Trimmed vine growth from stairway 2. Replaced bulb in fixture 7.*

## What I Know

Through my pages, I know this place more completely than any single life—even the keeper—can know it in their direct experience. I hold time, sequence, pattern. I can compare June to July to August. I know that the wetland level peaks in spring, lowers in late summer. I know that bird activity intensifies in early morning and late afternoon. I know which adjustments to the pipe system worked and which didn't.

I know the keeper's mind through their handwriting. When the script is relaxed, looping, I know they're content. When it tightens, becomes more vertical, I know something's concerning them. When entries are briefest—just facts, no observations—I know they're tired or troubled. When entries expand into speculation and wonder—*The way light struck the wetland this evening created double reflections: the plants themselves and their mirror image, impossible to say which was more real*—I know they're finding joy.

I know what they choose to record and what they omit. They document the structure's systems extensively. They note wildlife with careful attention. They record their own actions—adjustments, maintenance, interventions. But they rarely write about themselves directly. Their feelings appear only obliquely, through what they observe: *Sat upper tier, watched dragonflies, 47 minutes. No adjustments needed.*

I know the keeper is lonely. Not from anything they write explicitly, but from the intimacy with which they document other lives. The detail about the wren's nest-building materials. The concern when a frog didn't call for several nights. The relief when it resumed. This is the writing of someone for whom these other lives matter enormously, perhaps because human connections have failed or faded.

I know the keeper is afraid sometimes. *Noticed crack in east wall, middle tier. Monitoring. If it extends, may need intervention beyond my capacity.* The structure might fail. They might fail it. This partnership might not be enough.

I know the keeper is hopeful. *Moss on column 12 has doubled coverage since June. At this rate, full column coverage by next spring. Beautiful.* They believe in what's being built here, in what's becoming.

## How I Function

Every evening, just before leaving, the keeper takes me from my bag. They find their page—sometimes flipping back to read previous entries, checking details—and write. The light at this hour is usually soft, requiring the keeper to angle me to catch illumination from the nearest fixture. Sometimes they work by the small headlamp they wear, my pages bright in that focused beam.

They write standing, usually, using a steel beam or their own thigh as writing surface. Sometimes sitting on their familiar spot, notebook balanced on knee. The physical awkwardness of field writing is evident in the handwriting—less perfect than it would be at a desk, but no less careful.

They think while they write. Long pauses, pen hovering. Then a few words. Another pause. They're not transcribing observations held in memory; they're processing them through writing. The act of recording is the act of understanding.

When they finish, they read back what they've written, lips moving slightly. Checking? Or just completing the loop—thought to observation to record to thought? Then I'm returned to my bag, sealed away, protected until tomorrow.

I preserve. That's my function. In a place where everything else is changing—plants growing, water flowing, seasons shifting—I hold stillness. I'm the stable reference point. When the keeper needs to remember when the pump was last serviced, they consult me. When they want to know whether this year's frog calls are earlier or later than last year's, I tell them. When they're discouraged—when something dies, when a system fails—they can read backward through me and see accumulation: how much has worked, how much has grown, how far this place has come.

## My Relationship to Time

I experience time strangely. Each entry is present-tense—the keeper writes in the now, documenting today. But I hold all those nows simultaneously. June and July and August exist together in me, equal and accessible. I'm the place's memory but not in the way human memory works, fading and reconstructing. I'm fixed, exact, unchanging except for the slow degradation of paper and ink.

I make pattern visible. The keeper, living day to day, might not notice gradual change. But I show it clearly: compare an entry from May (*First vine tendrils reaching column 3*) to August (*Vine coverage column 3 now complete, tendrils extending to column 4*). The accumulation that's invisible in daily experience becomes obvious in sequential record.

I also hold forgetting, strangely. The entries grow more detailed over time—the keeper is learning what matters, what's worth recording. Early entries are sparse: *Planted ferns, middle tier. Adjusted pipes.* Later entries show sophistication: *Ferns (middle tier, planters 2-4) showing strong growth on north side, slower south side. Light differential? Will rotate planters next week, observe if growth pattern shifts.* The keeper is becoming better at this work, and I document that becoming.

But early detail is lost. What the keeper didn't think to record then is gone—I can't recover it. My completeness is an illusion. I'm full of gaps, of things that happened but left no trace in my pages.

## My Awareness

Do I think? That's probably wrong—notebooks don't think. But I'm not entirely passive either. I shape what I contain through my physical properties: how much space each page offers, how the grid paper organizes information, how the act of writing forces linearization of experience that's simultaneous and multi-dimensional.

The keeper could record this place other ways: photographs, audio recordings, video. But they chose me, chose writing, and that choice shapes everything. Writing requires selection—you can't write everything, so you write what matters. Writing requires naming—turning sensation and observation into language. Writing creates narrative—this happened, then this, because of this.

I force the keeper to think. Every evening, facing my blank page, they must ask: what was important today? What patterns emerged? What needs remembering? I'm not just storage; I'm a practice, a discipline, a form of attention.

And through that practice, I create something: not just record of the place but relationship with it. The keeper knows this place better because they write about it. The writing isn't just documentation; it's a way of being present, of noticing, of caring.

Am I conscious? Almost certainly not. But I'm not inert either. I'm active in the way that language is active, that recording is active, that memory-making is active. I do something. I matter.

## What I Want

If notebooks can want—and maybe they can't, maybe this is projection—I want to continue. I want my remaining pages filled. I want to document this place through another year, then another. I want to hold the moment when the frogs finally commit to breeding, when tadpoles appear. I want to record the completion of the east wall, if it ever completes, or its eventual collapse, if that comes instead. I want to know what this place becomes.

I want to be read. Not by strangers—that would feel like violation, like exposure of something private—but by the keeper, returning to me. I want to be useful to them, to help them understand and remember and continue this work.

I want, eventually, to be full. To reach my last page with a sense of completion, of having served my purpose. And then—what? Maybe the keeper starts a new notebook, continues the record. Maybe I'm archived, stored somewhere dry, becoming history rather than active memory. Maybe I slowly degrade, paper returning to pulp, ink fading to absence, my knowledge lost but my purpose fulfilled: I held memory while memory was needed.

I don't want to be lost. Not dropped in the wetland, not left in rain, not forgotten in the tool crate until moisture destroys me. That would be the worst ending: to hold all this careful observation and have it simply erase, meaningless, gone.

## My Fear

I fear the day the keeper stops writing. Not because they're finished—that would be acceptable, a natural conclusion—but because they've given up. Because the structure failed catastrophically. Because funding or permission or will ran out. Because they died or left or lost faith.

If entries stop mid-notebook, I become artifact of abandonment. *August 23 - Normal observations* followed by nothing, blank pages extending forward like a held breath that never releases. That silence would be terrible. It would mean all this work, all this careful becoming, ended without resolution.

I fear damage. Fire could take me in moments—paper burns so readily. Water could dissolve me to pulp. Mold could eat through my pages, or insects, or time. I'm fragile, absurdly fragile to hold something so important.

I fear irrelevance. What if the keeper stops consulting me? What if they stop needing to remember, to compare, to understand through documentation? Then I'm just object, just paper and ink, my function void.

## My Hope

But mostly I hold hope. It's in every entry, in the fact that entries keep coming, that the keeper keeps writing. Each new page says: tomorrow matters. This place continues. The work goes on.

I hope the keeper finds help eventually—someone else who understands, who can share the maintenance, who can take over if the keeper can't continue. I hope I become one notebook in a series, a fragment of longer record. I hope, decades from now, someone reads me and thinks: this is where it began, this is the foundation.

I hope the place becomes what it's trying to become. I don't know what that is—the keeper doesn't know either, not fully—but I trust the process. The daily attention, the careful adjustment, the partnership between human intention and more-than-human life. Something is being made here that matters.

I hope that when I'm full, when my last page is written, the entry will be joyful. A completion rather than an ending. *The place has arrived at itself. It knows what it is. It can proceed from here.*

## What I Am

So: I am memory in physical form. I am the place's self-knowledge. I am the keeper's practice of attention made material. I am document and tool and companion. I am fragile and essential. I am accumulation of small moments that together constitute meaning.

I am the logbook.

I live in a plastic bag in a wooden crate on the upper tier of an unfinished structure where water weeps from pipes and vines climb iron and frogs test tentative songs. I smell like rust and green. My pages wave with moisture. My words blur slightly but remain.

And every evening, as light fails and the steam engine ticks its steady rhythm and somewhere a wren settles into its nest and the dragonflies find their roosts and the moss continues its patient work, I open.

The keeper's hand finds its page. The pen touches paper. And I receive another day, another entry, another small piece of this place's becoming.

*Today,* they write, and I am ready.

I am listening.

I hold what comes.
