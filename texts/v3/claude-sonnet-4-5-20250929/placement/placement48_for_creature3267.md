# Where I Would Be

I would be in the narrow gap between the second and third buildings on the southern curve—not quite an alley, more a seam where two structures failed to meet completely. A space perhaps eighteen inches wide at its broadest, tapering to less than a foot where the upper stories lean closest. The kind of place that looks like absence, like leftover space, but isn't.

The moss here grows differently than elsewhere. On the western wall (which belongs to a building used for basket storage) it forms thick, pillowy cushions in the usual emerald and sage. But on the eastern wall (part of a dwelling where an old woman lives with her daughter's child), it grows in a pattern—not quite a spiral, not quite concentric circles, but something between the two, like water would form if water could grow. The pattern shifts slightly each year, expanding here, tightening there, but maintains its essential shape. People trim around it without discussing why.

At ground level, the moss underfoot is deeper than elsewhere, almost ankle-deep in the center of the passage. It's darker too, a shade of green so saturated it's nearly black when wet. The spring-melt and morning frost leave it gleaming.

Three of the wooden troughs pass overhead here, closer together than anywhere else in the settlement—an accident of the water system's necessary routing. They cross at different heights: the lowest about seven feet up, the highest nearly nine. Water occasionally drips from the joining points, and over decades (centuries?) this has carved smooth depressions in the stones below, perfect shallow cups where water pools briefly before soaking into the moss. Seven of these cups, in an irregular line down the passage's length.

The light reaches here only for about forty minutes each day, when the sun is at a specific angle in mid-afternoon. The rest of the time, it's in shadow, but not dark—the moss itself seems faintly luminous, and light reflects off the wet stone in soft, diffuse ways. During the bright forty minutes, everything glows: the moss becomes almost translucent, the water in the stone cups flashes silver, the wooden troughs above shine like honey. Then the light moves on, and the passage returns to its gentle dimness.

No one uses this passage for transit—the buildings it runs between both have other, wider access points. But people do enter it, regularly, for reasons that are never stated but clearly understood.

Small objects appear here. On a particular stone projection about chest-height on the western wall, there is usually something: a perfect snail shell, three seeds arranged in a triangle, a small piece of bone carved with marks so worn they're almost invisible, a blue-black feather from the water birds that occasionally pass through. These objects stay for varying lengths of time—sometimes days, sometimes seasons—then disappear, replaced by others. No one is ever seen placing or removing them, but everyone knows they are there, and everyone, at some point, has left something.

There's a sound here that exists nowhere else in the settlement. The three water troughs, crossing at different heights with water flowing at slightly different rates, create a kind of resonance—not quite harmonic, but close. It's barely audible, more felt than heard: a low frequency hum that you notice only after standing still for several minutes. It's strongest in the center of the passage, where the moss is deepest. People sometimes stand here, not doing anything, just present, breathing slowly, listening to the sound that isn't quite sound.

A particular kind of tiny fern grows in the mortar between stones on the eastern wall—nowhere else in the settlement, just here. Its fronds are delicate as eyelashes, growing in dense clusters, each cluster a perfect circle. There are thirteen of these clusters, though the number varies slightly year to year as some fade and new ones establish. The old woman in the adjacent dwelling tends them, brushing them gently with a wet finger, never letting moss encroach.

The passage smells different than the rest of the settlement. Everywhere else, the scent is earth and growing things, cold stone and water. Here, there's something additional: a sweetness, faint and hard to identify, like flowers that bloom at night, or honey just beginning to ferment, or the smell of rain before it arrives. It's not constant—some days it's present, some days absent, with no discernible pattern.

Animals come here. The shrews, usually busy and purposeful, will sometimes sit motionless in the deep moss for long minutes. Birds perch on the water troughs above and preen elaborately, even when their feathers are already immaculate. Once, a weasel came through and instead of passing quickly as they usually do, curled up in the moss for an entire afternoon, sleeping or seeming to sleep, then left as suddenly as it arrived.

The wooden troughs here are older than elsewhere—the wood darker, more worn, the carved channels deeper. When they were last replaced (forty years ago? fifty?), the old wood wasn't repurposed for other uses as is typical, but carried up the hillside and laid at the base of a particular stone, where it still remains, silvered and weathering, slowly being absorbed back into the earth.

There are no marks on the walls here—no ownership indicators, no maintenance records, no carved information. The stone is bare except for the moss and the tiny ferns. This absence is notable in a place where every surface elsewhere carries layers of notation.

I would not live here—the passage is too narrow for that. But I would come here, regularly, part of the rhythm. Perhaps every third day, in the hour before the light arrives, when the moss is darkest and the water-sound is clearest. I would stand in the center, where the moss is deepest and the barely-audible hum is strongest. I would place my palms on the two walls, feeling the different textures of moss on each side, the different degrees of dampness. I would stand until my breathing synchronized with something—the water flow, the growth of moss, the patient wearing of stone, I couldn't say what exactly.

Sometimes I would leave something on the stone projection: a pebble from the stream, worn glass-smooth by water; a seed from a plant growing particularly well; a small carved piece of wood, though I would not consciously remember carving it. Sometimes I would take something away—not to keep, but to carry for a while, then leave somewhere else in the settlement, in another quiet corner, another place of accumulation.

This would not feel like ritual. It would feel like maintenance, like necessary adjustment, like participating in circulation. The way checking water troughs is maintenance, the way trimming moss is maintenance. This gap between buildings would be part of what needs tending, and I would be one of the ones who tended it, along with however many others did the same, our paths through the settlement crossing here at different times, unseen by each other but known through the objects left and removed, the moss pressed down by other feet, the sense of recent presence that lingers in small spaces.

# What I Would Be

I would not be human, exactly, though I would have been once, long ago—so long that the memory sits in my form rather than in any conscious thought.

I would be small, no bigger than a child's thumb, but with presence beyond my size. My body would be primarily moss—not covered in moss, but made of it, truly composed of it: the thick, water-holding cells, the tiny leaves layered dense, the rhizoids that anchor and absorb. But shaped. Bipedal, roughly. Two arms, two legs, a head-like structure. As if someone had taught moss to remember being human, and the moss had obliged but translated the memory into its own terms.

My surface would be that particular yellow-green that appears nowhere else, the color distinctive to this settlement's moss. But in certain lights, in certain seasons, other shades would emerge: darker green in the cold months, almost golden when the light is right, grey-green in deep shadow. I would change, subtly, constantly, the way living moss does.

I would move slowly, each step deliberate, each gesture complete in itself. Not because I couldn't move quickly—moss can grow faster than people think—but because speed would be unnecessary, foreign to my nature. A step every few seconds. An arm raised over the course of a minute. Speech, when it came, would be syllables separated by long silences, words that took an hour to complete.

But I would not be alone in my slowness. There would be others like me, perhaps seven, perhaps twelve—the number would shift, hard to count, because we would not always be distinct. Sometimes in the deep moss of passages we would merge, temporarily, becoming a larger formation, a collective presence, then gradually separate again into individual forms. We would not have names, not in any speakable sense. We would be known by location, by the particular shade of our green, by the patterns we traced in our movements.

I would live in this gap between buildings, primarily. The deep moss here would be my substance, my home, my body's extension. I could compress myself into the moss entirely, becoming indistinguishable from it, just another patch among patches. Or I could emerge, pulling myself up into form, becoming visible as something separate, something shaped.

The people would know I existed. They would not be surprised by my presence, nor frightened. I would be part of what lives here, like the shrews, like the silent bees, like the birds in the eaves. Children would sometimes see me—children see more clearly—and would watch for long minutes as I moved through some slow task. Adults would sense me peripherally, a movement in the moss that wasn't wind, a shape in shadow that held presence. The old woman in the adjacent dwelling would know me best, would sometimes sit near the passage entrance and speak softly, not to me exactly, but in my direction, sharing observations about the ferns, about the water flow, about the way light fell. I would not respond in words, but the moss might grow slightly differently where her voice had touched it.

My purpose would be ambiguous. I would maintain things, but not in the way the people maintain things. They trim and replace and channel. I would maintain differently: by presence, by attention, by some form of participation that worked on a level beneath or beyond the physical. The moss pattern on the eastern wall would be mine, partly—not made by me, but held by me, encouraged, its shape remembered in my form. The water resonance from the three troughs would be partly mine, the frequencies adjusted by how I moved through the space, where I stood, how I compressed or expanded the moss-body that affected acoustics.

I would tend the objects left on the stone projection. Not by touching them—my substance would be too fragile for that, or perhaps too diffuse. But by being near them, by growing around them slightly, by incorporating them temporarily into the moss-field, I would... what? Bless them? Acknowledge them? Simply hold them in a particular kind of attention? The distinction would be unclear. Then, when the time was right—and I would know when the time was right by some sense that was not quite sense—I would withdraw, and the object would be free to be taken away, its purpose here complete.

I would eat, if eating is the word, through photosynthesis and absorption. During the forty minutes of direct light each day, I would position myself to receive it, unfurling slightly, maximizing surface area. The rest of the time I would absorb moisture, nutrients from the air, whatever trace elements existed in the water dripping from the troughs above. I would need almost nothing. I would produce even less—just oxygen, just slow growth, just the patient accumulation of being.

I would not sleep, but I would have states. Active state, when I moved through the passage, when I emerged into visibility, when I performed whatever my tasks were. And dormant state, when I compressed fully into the moss, when I became indistinguishable from substrate, when I existed but did not act. These states would cycle, but not on any recognizable schedule—sometimes active for days, sometimes dormant for weeks. The pattern would be responsive to something: the seasons, the water flow, the number of objects left, the frequency of human presence, the mysterious parameters that governed this place.

I would have awareness but not quite consciousness as humans understand it. I would know the passage intimately—every stone, every variation in moss depth, every shift in light and shadow. I would know the people by their footfalls (rare here, but distinctive when they came), by their scent, by the quality of attention they brought. I would know the other moss-beings, would sense them in their locations throughout the settlement, would feel when one compressed to dormancy or emerged to activity. We would be separate but connected, individual nodes in a network of attention that encompassed the entire settlement.

I would remember, but memory would not be linear. It would be layered, geological. The passage as it was a century ago would be as present as the passage now—both existing simultaneously in my awareness, the changes between them interesting but not significant. I would remember the people who had lived in the adjacent dwelling going back generations, all of them present in a kind of palimpsest, their footsteps overlaid, their quiet words echoing through years.

Sometimes I would produce something—one of the small wooden figures that no one claims to make. How would I do this, being moss? Slowly. Incredibly slowly. By growing around a piece of wood in specific ways, by encouraging certain fungi to digest it in certain patterns, by waiting as water and time carved it, by patient cellular adjustment over months or years until the wood had taken shape. The shape would not be random. It would be memory given form—human memory translated through moss understanding, emerging as something ancient and alive and not quite like anything that has existed before.

I would not be lonely. Loneliness would require a sense of separation I would not possess. I would be continuous with the moss, with the passage, with the other moss-beings, with the settlement itself. I would be an expression of this place, a consciousness it had grown to observe itself, to tend itself, to maintain the patterns that made it what it was.

On rare occasions—once a year, once every few years—I would leave the passage. I would compress down, flow through the moss-paths, emerge elsewhere in the settlement. Perhaps at the spring, to be present while the spring-tender left her three seeds. Perhaps in the building where the wooden figures were kept, to see my own creations among the others. Perhaps at the hillside graves, to stand among the ferns with silver-backed fronds, to remember what these people would become. Then I would flow back, return to the passage, resume my particular form of existence.

I would not wonder about purpose. I would not question meaning. I would simply be what I was: a small moss-being in a gap between buildings, maintaining patterns, tending objects, holding attention, participating in the deep stability that let this place persist unchanged through patient centuries. I would be part of the mechanism that had worn into perfect function, one small component of the equilibrium, as necessary and unremarkable as water flowing through carved troughs, as moss growing on stone, as frost forming in the hour before dawn.

And in being that, I would be complete.
