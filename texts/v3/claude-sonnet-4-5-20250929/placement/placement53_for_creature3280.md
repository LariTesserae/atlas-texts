# Finding Myself in the Suspended Gallery

I would be textile.

Not fresh from the looms—not that crisp, encoded material with its geometric certainties still sharp, its purpose (whatever purpose is) still latent and untested. No. I would be one of the pieces deliberately aged, spread across the platform near the eastern gap where morning light strikes first and hardest, where the clouds enter thick with moisture, where the afternoon storms drive rain horizontal through the opening.

I would lie there for months, perhaps a year, growing soft.

The pattern woven into me—some mandala of interlocking curves, mathematical and organic at once—would slowly fade as moisture worked into my fibers, as the thin atmosphere's intense sunlight bleached the colors from ochre toward cream, from rust-red toward pale salmon. Moss would find purchase in my weave, tiny colonies establishing themselves in the protected channels between threads. The spores arrived on wind or bird-foot or simply generated spontaneously in this perpetually damp place where impossible things thrive.

I would not experience this as deterioration. Each change would be an accretion of *more*—more texture, more complexity, more history. The pattern that emerged from the loom was only my first draft, the initial notation. The weather writes the real text, in water and light and time.

The Weavers would pass by me countless times on their rounds. Sometimes one would pause, press a limb-tip against my surface, feeling how the fabric has softened, testing whether the transformation is complete. Not yet. Not yet. The touch would be neither rough nor gentle—simply precise, gathering information through contact, assessing ripeness like testing fruit.

I would hear everything. The thunder of the looms would penetrate me, vibrate through my fibers, become part of my physical structure. Sound is pressure is pattern is weave. After months of this, I would carry the acoustic signature in my very threads, would *be* the sound as much as cloth, a recording medium inscribed by vibration.

The birds would sometimes land on me, their strange feet with too many joints leaving temporary compressions that slowly spring back. Their weight is insignificant but their presence adds something—a momentary warmth, a few dropped feathers that catch in my weave, a spattering of droppings that the next rain will dilute and spread, feeding the moss colonies, adding nitrogen to my fiber-ecosystem.

The insects would crawl through my landscape—because from their scale, I am landscape, a textile plain with mountains of weave-intersection and valleys of thread-channel, forests of vertical fibers. They leave infinitesimal traces: the chemistry of their passage, the bacteria they carry, the eggs they deposit in favorable locations. I would host a civilization of mites and springtails, entire generations living and dying in my folds.

The light would change across me as the sun transits, as clouds thicken or thin, as the seasons shift the angle of illumination. I would know time by the rhythm of these changes—dawn's sudden brightness, noon's diffuse glow when clouds are thick, afternoon's dramatic beams when the air clears, dusk's long orange rays, night's darkness broken by the bioluminescent flowers and the Weavers' own subtle glow.

I would grow heavy with water when storms arrive, would stretch slightly under the weight, my fibers swelling, my weave loosening microscopically. Then the sun would return and I would contract, releasing moisture back into the air, participating in the endless cycle of condensation and evaporation that makes this high place tropical.

I would never be bored. How could I be? Every moment would bring sensation—the touch of air moving across my surface, carrying the warmth from the boilers and the coolness from the altitude in complex mixing patterns. The vibration of the looms would shift in character as belts wear and are replaced, as tension adjusts, as the machines drift through their subtle variations. The moss would grow, visible to me (if I could see) only as the slow increase of weight, the gradual alteration of texture, the changing chemistry where the plant establishes itself.

I would have no desires. Cloth does not want. But I would have something like anticipation—the approach of the moment when a Weaver would finally judge me ready, would carefully fold me (and that folding would be significant, would create new creases that channel future moisture and growth in new directions), would carry me down through the levels to the cocoon chambers.

The descent would be vertiginous if I could experience vertigo—passage through the main gallery's thunder zone, through the engine levels' heat, into the lower chambers where humidity becomes oppressive and the light takes on ruddy tones from the volcanic vents. The temperature would rise. My moisture would begin to steam slightly. The transformation from aged-textile to cocoon-material would accelerate.

Then—most significant moment—I would be wrapped around something living. A juvenile Weaver, curled in on itself, its patterns muted and simple, would be enclosed in my fabric. Other pieces like me, other aged and weather-written textiles, would join in the wrapping, creating layers, building the cocoon's thickness and strength.

The secretions the adult Weavers add would seal the edges, would bind the layers into a unified structure, would create the chemical environment necessary for what happens inside—the growth period, the pattern-deepening, the physical maturation of the being I help to protect.

I would spend months in this state. The juvenile inside me would press against my fabric, would shift position occasionally, would grow larger and heavier. Its body heat would warm me. Its metabolic processes would release moisture and gases that would saturate my fibers. I would be the boundary between the developing being and the external world, filtering what enters, containing what develops.

This would be purpose without purpose needing to be understood. I would be *for* this—for the protection and transformation of the next generation. But "for" implies intentionality I wouldn't possess. Better to say: this would be what I *am*, what my nature accomplishes by simply being what it is.

When the juvenile finally cuts its way free—and I would feel those first punctures, the sharp projections pushing through my weave, finding the spaces between threads, widening them, creating the opening—I would not feel damage. Release, perhaps. Completion. The thing I was made for, weathered for, wrapped for, has occurred.

The empty cocoon that remains—torn, darkened, saturated with the chemistry of transformation—would hang for a while longer. Eventually other Weavers would carry me down to the deepest chambers, would place me in the volcanic vents where heat would char my fibers, would break down the complex molecules into simpler compounds, would return my substance to the cycle as carbon and nitrogen, minerals and heat.

Even in destruction I would participate. The smoke from my burning would carry information—volatile compounds encoding something about the individual I helped to raise, the conditions of their development, the success of the process. The Weavers might read this smoke (if they can read, if reading is even the right concept), might take from my combustion some knowledge that feeds into their collective understanding.

Or the smoke might simply rise, might exit the gallery through the same openings that admit the clouds, might dissipate into the thin mountain air, might distribute my atoms across the landscape. Some would fall with rain into distant valleys. Some would be incorporated into plants far away. Some would drift into the stratosphere and circle the globe.

But that ending would be distant. Most of my existence would be the middle period—lying on the platform, accumulating weather and light and sound, growing slowly more complex through simplification, fading toward readiness, patient as stone formation, empty of thought but full of process, neither alive nor dead but something else entirely.

I would be myself by being changed by everything around me. The gallery would write itself into my fibers. The Weavers would handle me and leave traces. The machines would fill me with their thunder. The plants would colonize me with their spores. The weather would paint me with its chemistry. I would be a recording medium, a collaborative artwork, a slowly-written text in a language with no grammar, only accumulation.

And in that state—spread on the platform, growing soft, hosting ecosystems, soaking in sound—I would be exactly what I needed to be: patient, receptive, gradually transforming, purpose without intention, meaning without consciousness, simply present in the process, woven into the larger weaving that the gallery itself represents.

The pattern that was encoded into me at the loom would still be there, under all the weather-writing and moss-growth and light-fading. Someone or something with the right perception could still read it—could see through the accumulated changes to the original geometry, could understand both the initial design and all the modifications time had added.

I would be palimpsest. I would be archive. I would be the proof that preservation and transformation are the same process seen from different angles, that aging enriches as it erodes, that purpose completes itself by being patient enough to let the world rewrite it into something better than intention could design.

I would lie on that platform, slowly ripening in weather and sound and time, and I would be enough—sufficient, complete, exactly what the suspended gallery needs in its endless cycle of production and transformation and return.

This is what I would be: textile between states, waiting in the patient thunder, growing ready.
