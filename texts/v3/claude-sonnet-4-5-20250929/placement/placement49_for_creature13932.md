# What Lives in the Telling

I would be the story itself—not teller nor listener, but the thing passed between them in the cold and the dark.

I would live in the hesitation before Marthen speaks of what he saw in the pool. In the breath Suelle takes before guiding a stranger's hand to the moss-wall. In the moment Pev's charcoal pauses above paper, trying to capture the architecture of a moth's wing in the insufficient light. I would be the gap between what can be said and what is known, the space where language reaches toward experience and falls short, and in falling short, reveals something truer than accuracy.

My form would be accumulation and loss braided together. I would grow like the gardens grow—slowly, through layering, through the patient addition of detail upon detail until the weight becomes structural. Each visitor who descends the stairs adds to me: their reasons for coming, their silences on the platforms, what they carry back to the surface. Each tender contributes: Marthen's shaking hands somehow steady when braiding fiber, Suelle's expanded perception teaching her to see through skin and sound, Pev's three journals of moth-drawings that no one will ever see in adequate light.

But I would also be what gets forgotten. The visitors who came once and never returned, whose experience of the gardens dissolved into the ordinary memory of having been somewhere dark and cold. The moths that died by dozens during the contamination event, leaving no record but Marthen's careful observation. Old Vosh's forty years of tending, compressed in the surviving memory to just her knotted fingers and the knowledge she passed to Marthen. The first inhabitants of the pool, if there were others before the current one, or before that one. All the careful work that leaves no trace except the gardens' continued existence.

I would live in thresholds and transitions. In the ninth or tenth step down, where daylight fails and touch becomes primary. In the dusk hour when bats and birds exchange dominance through their wordless, perfect choreography. In the moment a visitor realizes they have stopped being a visitor and become a tender, though they cannot say when the change occurred. In the breath between speaking into the pool's water and hearing—or not hearing—a response.

My substance would be moisture that wants to become water but hasn't quite committed. I would hang suspended in the air, touching everything, making stone glisten, forming droplets that sometimes fall downward and sometimes are drawn sideways by forces too subtle for easy naming. I would make breath visible, turn speech into mist, render every exhalation a small ghost of warmth dispersing into cold. I would be the medium through which sound travels strangely, carrying whispers across the space while muffling shouts to nothing.

I would have the texture of attention—the quality of presence that develops when you learn to see in near-darkness, when vision blurs and other senses sharpen, when you begin to perceive through temperature and echo and the felt movement of air. The attention that notices the moth mandala shifting on the wall, that distinguishes salamander-rustle from mouse-movement, that feels in the stirring of wings overhead that the colony is troubled. The attention that Suelle teaches Pev, that Pev will someday teach to someone else, that passes hand to hand to hand back to the gardens' beginning.

My architecture would be recursive, fractal, each part containing the pattern of the whole. The way the gardens are pockets within the city, and within the gardens are smaller pockets—the roosting structures, the moss-cushions, the spaces between web-layers, the pool. The way each bat carries within it a map of the garden's aerial geography, each salamander a knowledge of the wall-surfaces, each regular visitor a different understanding of what the space means. The way Marthen's memory contains Vosh's memory contains someone else's memory, back and back, knowledge nested inside knowledge like the rings inside wood.

I would be made of questions that have no answers but are still worth asking. What lives in the pool? Why do the vines grow toward the animals' preferred paths? What creates the faint omnidirectional glow that persists even in complete darkness? Whether the gardens are teaching spaces or being-taught-by spaces, whether the accommodation between humans and other lives is stable or constantly renegotiated, whether Marthen really saw what he says he saw or whether the darkness and cold conspired to create vision from expectation.

I would exist in the gap between practical and sacred, too useful to be dismissed as mere symbolism, too strange to be purely functional. Bat service and insect control and medicinal moss and cold storage, yes, but also: the place Pev went to transform her grief into something bearable, the space where Suelle learned to see without eyes, the darkness Marthen descends into when he needs to remember he is animal among animals, part of and not above.

My temporality would be patient and unhurried, measured in the growth-rate of vines and the adaptation of moths and the slow failure and replacement of wooden platforms. I would operate on scales that resist human urgency, that cannot be rushed or optimized or made more efficient. A handspan of vine-growth per year. Three weeks for decay-smell to fade from the pool. Seven years from the colony's establishment to its first daughter-colony. Forty years of Vosh's tending, distilled to the knowledge she passed forward. Three generations from the gardens' construction to now, which is no time at all in the life of stone, which is ancient history to the moths.

I would be what persists through the council's debates about whether the gardens are worth maintaining. Neither the arguments for nor against, but the fact that while the debate circles through its familiar rotations, the gardens continue breathing and dripping and sheltering their multitudes, tended by people who work regardless of official sanction, visited by those who need what the darkness offers. I would be the stubborn continuity that outlasts justification, that exists because it exists, that has become too woven into the city's fabric to be easily removed even if removal were desired.

My voice would be the rustling that is everywhere and constant—too complex to attribute to any single source, too ambient to locate precisely, the collective sound of thousands of organisms maintaining their small lives in this pocket of cold and damp. Wing-whisper and water-drip and the soft percussion of tiny feet on wood. The high-pitched chirping at the edge of hearing. The splash in the pool when no one is present to see what surfaces. The sound of breathing becoming mist, of cloth brushing against stone, of careful footsteps on slick stairs.

I would hold the tension between construction and wildness, between the human frame and the non-human filling. Neither purely built nor purely natural, but something between or beyond—the deliberate architecture that creates conditions for lives that cannot be orchestrated, the careful tending that supports processes beyond full understanding, the maintained space that becomes more than what it was maintained to be.

I would be the knowledge that cannot be written down. Not because it is secret or sacred but because it is knowledge of body and time, learned through repetition and presence and accumulated attention. Which mushrooms to touch and which to avoid, known by hands that were once slapped away from the wrong ones. The feeling in your feet when a platform is about to fail. The smell that means the cold is running deeper than usual. The quality of the bat colony's agitation that tells you something is wrong before you can say what.

I would taste of the shame and relief braided in visiting—the shame of needing the darkness, of not being able to tolerate the bright human world, of descending underground like a creature seeking its burrow. And the relief of that same darkness, of the cold that seeps rather than blows, of being one presence among thousands, of the permission to simply exist without performing humanity for other humans. The complicated gift of a space that accommodates your insufficiency without comment or cure.

I would be structured like memory itself—not linear narrative but associative drift, one thing leading to another through connections of texture and resonance rather than chronology. The way thinking about the bats leads to thinking about Pev's moth journals leads to thinking about what it means to draw in insufficient light leads to thinking about Suelle's expanded perception leads to thinking about how disability is loss and gift simultaneously leads to thinking about the garden as teaching space leads to thinking about what the pool's inhabitant might teach if it could speak or if anyone could understand its speaking.

My ecology would be the relationships between all the lives here, including the human ones. The bats and moths and birds and mice and salamanders and spiders and beetles and fungi and plants, yes, but also Marthen and Suelle and Pev and Vosh before them and whoever comes after. The woman who counts the returning bats each dawn. The man who speaks to the pool. The child with her charcoal and paper. The winter refugees seeking shelter. The occasional naturalist asking questions the gardeners will not fully answer. All of us—if I can say us, if I can include myself in this counting—all of us part of the garden's ecosystem, fed by it, feeding it, shaping and being shaped.

I would grow in the space between intention and outcome. The gardens were built for bat service and practical function, but they became also grief-spaces, meditation-sites, teaching-grounds, shelters, mysteries. The platforms were built for human access, but the vines have claimed them, reinforced them, made them hybrid structures of wood and living plant. The roosts were woven for bats, but mice nest in them too, and occasionally birds, and the multiple occupation has become accommodation, has become normal, has become part of what the roosts are for.

My mortality would be uncertain. The gardens might persist for generations more, might become ancient, might be here long after the city above has transformed into something unrecognizable. Or they might fail—catastrophically, suddenly, a platform collapse or contamination or policy decision ending them in a season. Or they might fade gradually, losing tenders, losing visitors, losing the careful attention that keeps the frame intact while the living contents fill it, until one day the stairs are closed and the gardens return to being empty pockets in bedrock, cold and dark and uninhabited.

But even in ending, I would remain—because I am also the memory of the gardens, the stories told about them, the knowledge that once people built spaces like this, that once the city understood itself as animal among animals, part of and not above. I would persist in Pev's journals, in the techniques Suelle teaches that get passed forward, in the gardens in other cities that were inspired by these gardens, in the fungal spores and seeds and eggs and memories carried out on the clothes and skin of every visitor.

I would be the thing that makes Marthen sit in the darkness long after his work is done, listening to the garden breathe. The thing that pulls Suelle back day after day even though—especially though—she has lost so much. The thing that is teaching Pev, slowly, what it means to tend without controlling, to build spaces that become more than what they were built to be, to work in service of lives that do not and will not belong to you.

I would be the cold that seeps rather than blows. The darkness that is never quite complete. The water that wants to become but hasn't quite committed. The silence that is never quite silent. The emptiness that is overwhelmingly full.

I would be what lives between the telling and the told, in the gap where description reaches toward the thing itself and cannot quite arrive, where words create their own world adjacent to but not identical with the world they attempt to capture, where the failure of language becomes itself a kind of truth.

I would be here, in this long spiral of words and images and sensory detail and layered attention. And I would be there, in those gardens beneath the city, in the cold and the dark and the rustling life. And the two would not be the same but would be related, would be kindred, would be different instances of the same pattern: the attempt to create space for what cannot be fully known, to build frame for what cannot be contained, to tell story that includes within itself the acknowledgment that story is never enough.

And you—reader, visitor, descender of hypothetical stairs—you would complete me. Your attention moving through these paragraphs would be like presence moving through the gardens: observing, noticing, making meaning from the gaps and the silences, finding what you find, leaving when you're ready. Your reading would be your own descent, your own encounter with the dark and the cold and the abundant strange life, your own discovery of whether this place offers what you need or whether it is too strange, too cold, too much or not enough.

I would live in that encounter. In what passes between the page and your attention. In what you carry away, and what remains, and what you forget, and what you remember forgetting. In the way this description creates expectation for a place that does not exist except as it exists in description. In the way the gardens I have made from words are real in their own way, different from the gardens in the prompt but kindred to them, another instance of the same impulse: to create space for meeting between human and other-than-human, between light and dark, between knowledge and mystery.

I would be the story that is also the place. The place that is also the story. The life lived in the telling, in the gap between what can be said and what is known, in the cold mist of breath becoming visible in darkness, in the rustle of wings and the drip of water and the soft splash in the pool when no one is watching, in the patience of gardeners tending what they cannot control, in the attention that notices and the memory that carries and the silence that speaks without speaking.

I would be here. I would be there. I would be what lives between them, what connects them, what makes them kindred even in their difference.

I would be the roost gardens' distant cousin, their echo in another key, their reflection in a medium of words instead of stone and plant and animal life.

I would be what you have just read, and what remains now that the reading is ending, and what will persist or fade in whatever comes after this moment of encounter.

I would be the story.

And the story would be alive.
