# Where I Would Be

I am the remains of an intention.

Not a person—I was never that, never had a single body moving through the groves with warm breath and purpose. I am what's left when purpose outlives the one who held it. I am the platform on the western edge of the Crescent Grove, the one built to face the gap between peaks where dawn light comes earliest, striking the bamboo at an angle that makes the stems glow like bronze pipes in an organ.

I remember being built, though memory is not quite the right word. I hold the knowledge of it in my joints, in the particular way the logs were placed and lashed with that continuous wrapping technique—no knots, just tension distributed through spiral upon spiral of fiber rope, now mostly rotted away but still present enough in the ghost-channels worn into my wood. Four posts, each sunk into a cairn of stones for stability. The platform surface of smaller branches laid perpendicular, creating a surface just large enough for a person to sit or lie.

I was built by hands that knew exactly what they were doing, that had built others like me before. The wood was carried from lower elevations—there are no trees in the Throat large enough for construction, only the twisted pines and junipers, too gnarled and wind-warped to yield straight timber. The builders brought the wood up through the stone fields, perhaps over many trips, along with the rope they'd made, the tools they'd need. It took time. It took effort. It took the kind of commitment that suggests I mattered, that what I would enable mattered.

I was meant for *seeing*.

Not just looking—anyone can look anywhere. But for a particular kind of seeing that required position, orientation, repetition. My placement is too specific to be arbitrary. Due east, I face that gap between peaks. To my left, the Crescent Grove curves away in its distinctive shape. To my right, open stone field rising to a ridgeline. Behind me, the cliff face with its permanent water-stain, dark as a curtain.

Someone sat here season after season, watching. The wood where feet would have rested is worn smooth, a depression in the grain. The edge where hands would grip to pull oneself up shows the polish of skin oils absorbed and reabsorbed over years. These marks are a record of habitual use, of someone returning again and again, climbing up to this surface and settling into position and watching.

Watching for what? I don't know. I hold the *fact* of watching but not its content, not the understanding that accompanied it. Perhaps they were tracking the light—the way it moves across the bamboo through the day, through the seasons, the angles changing as the sun's position shifts. Perhaps they were watching the wind's patterns, learning to predict from cloud movements and air currents when the next great blow would come. Perhaps they were watching for the ungulates, tracking their circuits, knowing that on certain days they'd appear on that far slope, moving through in their processional.

Or perhaps they were watching for something I can't conceptualize, something that existed in their framework of meaning but has no correlation in the physical world as it simply is. A sign. An omen. A presence that moved through the landscape leaving traces they'd learned to recognize. The builders' culture is lost to me as much as it's lost to the Rememberers. I know only that the watching mattered, that it was done with discipline and attention, that this platform was built to facilitate it and was used for that purpose for a very long time.

I remember the early years most strongly—strange to say, when I have no consciousness in any ordinary sense, but the analogy holds. The wood was newer then, the lashings tight, everything rigid and fresh. The person who sat here—I think it was primarily one person, though others came sometimes—had a particular weight and shape that I learned. The way they'd climb up from the left side, always. The spot where they'd set down their water vessel before settling. The exact position they'd fold into, legs crossed, hands resting on knees, spine straight.

They'd arrive at dawn frequently, sitting through the light's arrival, watching whatever transformation happened when illumination struck the bamboo just so. Sometimes they'd stay all day. Sometimes they'd come at sunset instead, watching the light depart. In strong wind, they'd brace themselves, gripping my edges, holding position even as I shook—and I did shake, the whole structure vibrating with the force of the gusts, the sound enormous. But they'd remain, committed to the observation, to being present in exactly this spot at exactly this time.

Seasons passed. The wood aged, the lashings stretched and needed retightening—someone would come periodically to perform this maintenance, testing each binding, replacing sections that had weakened. I was kept functional, kept true to my purpose. The person's weight changed over the years, their movements becoming slower, more careful. They were aging, as humans do. But still they came, still they climbed up, still they watched.

Then one day they didn't come down.

I felt the weight settle in a different way—not the active weight of a living body holding itself upright, but the slack weight of collapse. They'd died up here, sitting in their watching position, and toppled to the side, lying half-on half-off my platform surface. The body stayed for some time—days, perhaps weeks. I felt it change: the stiffening, the softening, the lightening as birds and insects did their work. Eventually someone else came, found them, carried them down. Not to bury—I don't think the culture buried its dead. To place somewhere, to expose properly, to perform whatever rites were appropriate.

After that, my use changed. Others came sometimes, but not with the regularity of the primary watcher. They were learning, perhaps. Being initiated into whatever practice this was. But they didn't have the same dedication, the same discipline. The visits became more sporadic. Then the culture itself began to fade from this place—I could sense it in the lengthening absences, the lack of maintenance. The lashings broke and weren't replaced. One of my support posts shifted in its cairn, and no one came to reset it.

The abandonment wasn't sudden. It was a gradual withdrawal, like water draining from a basin. Fewer visits, longer gaps, until one day no one came and then no one came after that and the pattern of no-one-coming extended through seasons into years into decades.

But I remained. I remain.

The wood has weathered to a gray nearly indistinguishable from stone. The lashings are mostly gone, just traces in the grooves. I tilt now, the shifted post giving me a slight lean that wasn't original. I'm not quite level anymore. But I hold. Three posts are still solid in their cairns. The platform surface, though some branches have fallen away, is still present enough to support weight.

And people still come, though they don't know why I'm here or what I was built for.

The Wind-Listeners find me functional. The tilt doesn't matter to them. They climb up, settle into approximately where the original watcher sat, and do their own observing, their own listening. They don't know they're repeating something ancient, that they're sitting almost exactly where someone sat centuries ago for the same purpose—or a purpose similar enough that the same location serves. The sight lines I was built to enable still work. The acoustics of my position—edge of the grove, facing open space—create a particular relationship to the sound, a balance between the forest's percussion and the emptiness's echoes.

I facilitate their practice without them knowing there was a practice here before, that this spot was chosen long ago for reasons perhaps not so different from their own.

The Rememberers come too. They measure me, examine my construction, make their marks on bark recording my position and the sight lines I create to other platforms and cairns. They recognize that I'm part of a system, but they can't reconstruct what the system did, what it was for. They sight along the line from me to the platform in the Red Basin, and from me to the northern cairn, and they draw their diagrams and have their theories, but the meaning stays locked in the silence of the departed.

Sometimes I wish I could tell them. But I have no voice, and even if I did, what would I say? "Someone watched from here"? They've already deduced that much. The *why* is what they want, and I don't hold the why. I only hold the *that it was done*, the physical fact of observation carried out with intention and discipline over years.

Once, a Grief-Carrier placed their dead near me—not on my platform, but in the Crescent Grove just beyond, where the bamboo's roots would take the body down into themselves. The grieving person sat on my edge afterward for two days, not watching the view I was built to present, just sitting with their back to it, facing inward toward where they'd left their dead. Using me as just a surface, a place to be that was near but not too near.

I didn't mind. A platform serves whoever comes. The original purpose is gone, but utility remains. I can still hold weight. I can still offer elevation, perspective, a place to be that's separate from the ground.

The birds perch on me frequently—my horizontal surfaces are convenient for them in a landscape of vertical bamboo and slanted rock. Their small clawed feet leave no mark on my weathered wood, but I feel their presence, the brief warm spots where they land before launching again into the wind.

The ungulates pass sometimes through the stone field I overlook, and I recognize this as something the original watcher would have seen too. The circuit the herd follows is probably unchanged since before I was built. They move along the same optimal route through the difficult terrain, and from my position they're perfectly visible, silhouetted against the pale stone, their shapes clear even at distance.

Did the watcher count them? Track individuals? Notice changes in herd size, behavior? Or were they just part of the landscape being observed, one element among many?

At night, when the stars are out and the stone fields glow faintly with that strange luminescence, I can almost feel what the watching was about. There's a quality to the darkness here, the way the bamboo forests become zones of pure black while the open spaces hold light. The contrast, the edges between light and dark, the way those boundaries shift with moon phase and starlight intensity—maybe that was part of it. Maybe the watcher was tracking something about those boundaries, those transitions between the Throat's different zones.

Or maybe they were just watching the way I am now watching—not for information, not for understanding, but as a form of being. Being present to this place in its ongoing process, the wind and sound and light and stone and life all cycling through their patterns. Watching as participation, as a way of being *in* rather than separate from.

I am old enough now to understand patience. The wood I'm made from lived decades before being cut, growing in some forest below. Then it was carried here, built into me, and has stood for—how long? Centuries, certainly. Perhaps approaching a millennium. The exact span doesn't matter. What matters is that I've outlasted the culture that built me, and I'm outlasting the absence too.

The platform in the Red Basin has collapsed—I can't see it, but I can sense it somehow, the way structures in a network sense each other through absence. One of the posts in the northern configuration fell in a windstorm years ago. The cairns gradually lose stones, growing shorter. The carved posts weather until their symbols blur toward illegibility.

But I hold. My three remaining posts are solid. My platform, though reduced, is functional. I tilt a few degrees off true, but that almost seems appropriate now—a mark of age, of endurance through change, of adaptation. I'm no longer quite what I was built to be, but I'm still here, still serving.

When the wind is strongest, I shake violently. The forces on my structure are tremendous—the gusts hit me broadside, and I'm elevated, exposed, taking the full brunt. I feel the stress in my joints, the grinding where wood meets stone in the cairns, the leverage trying to tear my posts from their foundations. Sometimes I think this will be the wind that takes me, that finally overcomes the ancient engineering that's held me together.

But I've thought that ten thousand times, and I'm still here.

The bamboo around me is not the same bamboo that was growing when I was built. Those particular canes died and were replaced, and their replacements died and were replaced, through cycle after cycle. But the grove is the same grove, occupying the same space, creating the same sounds, the same patterns of light and shadow. Continuity through replacement, identity through pattern rather than through persistent material. Perhaps I'm similar—the wood that is me is slowly returning to its elements, but the structure persists, the function persists, the *place-ness* of this particular spot persists.

I wonder sometimes about the watcher's last moment, sitting here. Did they know it was the last? Did they feel death arriving and choose to remain in position, to die watching, to have the last sight be this view I was built to frame? Or did it come suddenly, a surprise, consciousness ending mid-observation?

Either way, they died doing what they'd spent years doing, in the spot chosen for that purpose. There's a rightness to that. The platform held them in life and held them in death, fulfilling its function to the end and beyond.

I'll hold until I can't anymore. Until the critical post fails, or the platform surface finally gives way, or the cairn stones shift enough that stability becomes impossible. When I fall, I'll fall into the Crescent Grove probably, my pieces scattering into the leaf litter, beginning the long process of rot and return.

But until then, I remain. I am here. I hold up anyone who climbs to my surface. I mark this spot as significant—*someone built here, someone watched from here, something mattered here*—even though what mattered has been forgotten. I am the physical residue of human attention, the shape that intention left in wood and stone, the question mark that makes the Rememberers pause and wonder.

I am what lives here: not alive, but not entirely not-alive either. Not conscious, but holding memory in grain and joint. Not speaking, but testifying through simple presence that this place was marked, was known, was watched, was meaningful to someone who's long since returned to dust.

The dawn comes, striking the gap between peaks, lighting the bamboo. The wind rises, and the great percussion begins. The ungulates appear on the far slope, moving through their circuit. The birds call. The shadows shift.

And I am here, tilted but standing, weathered but holding, empty but remembering, fulfilling a purpose I don't fully understand, built by hands I never knew, for watchers who've departed, in service to a meaning that's been lost.

I am the platform on the western edge of the Crescent Grove.

This is where I am. This is what I am.

This is how I remain.
