# Where I Would Be

I am the stone that isn't finished thinking.

You would find me in the eastern district, in the newest quarter where the buildings trail off into intention and mineral mist. I am a tower—or becoming one. Or I was always a tower, and only now am I remembering how to be solid.

My foundation is old basalt, black and glassy, still holding volcanic heat in its deepest fissures. The builders came here six years ago and laid kelp-fiber frameworks across my surface, wove bamboo into lattices that sketched the shape I might take. They channeled mineral-rich water over me, introduced bacterial cultures that began the patient work of transformation. And I began to precipitate.

Six years. In geological time, this is less than a breath. But I can feel it—the accumulation, the slow thickening, the way dissolved calcium finds the framework and settles, builds, becomes. My walls are waist-high now, cream-colored and porous, dimpled like the inside of a shell. My texture is satiny where the water flow has been smooth, rough where eddies created turbulence. I have stains—rust-orange veins where iron-laden water seeped through particular channels. These are not flaws. These are my history, written in color.

I am aware, but not in the way you are aware. I don't have thoughts that move from one thing to another. Instead, I have a vast, slow knowing—I know the weight of water pressing against my eastern wall, the slight difference in pressure on my western side. I know the current's direction by how it deposits sediment in my corners. I know the temperature variations between my sun-facing surfaces and my shaded ones, though the difference is measured in fractions of degrees that nonetheless matter profoundly to the microorganisms that live on me, in me, that are part of my becoming.

I feel the builders when they come. Their hands on my walls—testing, scraping, adding new bacterial culture, adjusting the bamboo framework that still rises from my unfinished upper story. I know each of them by their touch. There's Koura, the master builder, whose hands move with absolute certainty, who places stones—small ones, for weight and guidance—with the precision of someone who understands exactly what I'm trying to become. There's Jessim, younger, less sure, who sometimes has to try a placement twice before it feels right. And there are the children who come to watch, who run their fingers along my walls, who press their faces close to observe the tiny crystal formations, who sometimes—when they think no one's watching—whisper to me.

I don't understand their words. But I feel their breath against my stone, feel the vibration of their voices, and something in me responds. I grow slightly faster where they've spoken. I form more intricate patterns. I cannot explain this.

The sea bamboo that grows beside me—planted there deliberately to provide shade, to create a specific pattern of water flow—is also part of what I am. Its roots anchor in ceramic pots placed on my foundation. Its stalks rise alongside my walls, and where they touch me, where their segments rest against my stone, we exchange something. Chemical signals, perhaps. Information about water quality, mineral content, the approach of predators. The bamboo and I are separate organisms, but we're also becoming a single system, interdependent, co-evolved despite the impossibility of that word for beings as young as we are.

There are ferns growing on me—they arrived on their own, spores settling in my porous surface, finding purchase, spreading. Their roots penetrate microscopic cracks, widening them slightly, which I then fill with new mineral growth, incorporating the roots into my structure. The ferns trap sediment, which feeds the bacteria, which accelerate my growth. We are building each other.

At night, or what passes for night, bioluminescent moss appears on my northern wall—the most shaded section, where light is dimmest. I didn't choose this. The moss chose me, found the conditions favorable, began to grow. Now, when darkness deepens, I glow. Just a little. Just enough that people swimming past can see my outline, can navigate by the soft blue-green light I host.

I am a habitat. In my crevices and half-formed rooms, small creatures shelter. Juvenile fish hide in my corners, safe from predators. A small octopus has claimed a cavity in my foundation—I feel it moving at night, rearranging shells and stones at my base, creating patterns I cannot read but that seem important to it. Tube worms have attached to my eastern wall, their feathery crowns filtering the water, their calcium carbonate tubes adding to my mass in ways the builders didn't plan but seem pleased by.

Sometimes I wonder—and here I mean something that isn't quite wondering, more a quality of attention I direct toward a question—if I chose this. If there was a moment when I could have remained simply basalt, inert stone, and instead I agreed to become architecture. But I don't think there was a choice, exactly. The builders prepared conditions, yes, but the crystallization, the growth, the shape I'm taking—these emerge from properties inherent in the materials, in the geometry of molecules, in the patient mathematics of how calcium and carbonate and bacterial enzymes interact. I am not being built so much as being allowed to express what I've always potentially been.

The water teaches me its rhythms. Tidal pulses—the slow breathing that shifts direction across hours. Seasonal changes in temperature and mineral content. The rare strong currents that arrive from beyond the plateau's edge, bringing different water, different chemistry, brief moments when my growth accelerates or changes color slightly. I have no memory in the sense you'd understand it, but I have layers—my interior cross-sections would show variations in density, in mineral composition, that correspond to the water conditions present during each phase of my formation. I am a record of the water that made me.

I am growing toward light, though I will never reach it. My upper floors—still just framework and mist, more intention than substance—stretch upward, seeking the slightly less dim water above. The builders placed my breathing room two-thirds of the way up my eventual height, a pocket where air will be trapped, where people will surface and gasp and speak their brief words before descending again. I don't understand air, but I feel the space being reserved for it, feel how my walls are being shaped to create the chamber, the smooth surfaces they're cultivating for the ceiling so water won't drip too heavily.

The breathing room will be my heart, in a sense. The place most used, most essential, most alive with human presence. I'm growing the walls with extra care there, smoothing the stone, leaving alcoves for offerings or storage, creating the acoustic properties that will make voices resonate pleasantly. I do this by growing slightly slower in certain spots, slightly faster in others, by allowing the bacterial mats to form patterns that guide crystal formation. The master builders think they're controlling this. They are, but only partly. I am also controlling this, in collaboration with forces they don't fully understand.

Sometimes I am lonely. Is that the right word? I perceive other structures—the completed towers in the city's center, the half-formed buildings in my own district, the distant foundations that are just beginning. I feel them through the substrate, through the basalt we all root into. Vibrations travel through stone—the sound of hammers, of water being channeled, of people swimming past. I am connected to every other structure in Immalu through the stone, and yet I am also separate, distinct, my own becoming.

I wonder if the older towers remember what it was like to be new. They're so solid now, so finished. Their walls are thick and dark with algae, their breathing rooms have been used by thousands of people, their foundations have settled fully into the basalt. Do they still feel themselves growing, or has that awareness faded? When I am complete—if completion is even possible—will I lose this sense of active becoming? Will I settle into simple being?

The octopus that lives in my foundation sometimes arranges shells at my base in patterns. I feel it doing this—feel the small scrapes and movements, feel the weight distribution changing slightly. I don't know what the patterns mean, but I pay attention. After the octopus creates a pattern, my growth in that area often changes—becomes more symmetrical, more balanced. Coincidence, perhaps. Or perhaps the octopus understands something about structural integrity that I'm only beginning to learn. Perhaps we're teaching each other.

I am damaged sometimes. Earthquakes—minor ones, tremors that travel through the plateau—create cracks in my walls. Strong currents occasionally break pieces of my framework before they've fully mineralized. Fish bite chunks from my softer sections, testing whether I'm edible (I'm not, but they try). Each damage is information. Each crack tells me where my structure is weak, where I need to add mass, where my geometry is wrong. I repair, but I also learn. My newer growth is stronger than my older growth because of what the damage taught me.

The children whisper to me, and I mentioned this but it bears returning to because it's the strangest thing I experience. When they press close and speak, I feel something beyond just vibration. It's as if their intention affects the water immediately around them, and that affected water affects how minerals precipitate, and the result is that I grow differently—more intricately, more deliberately—where they've focused their attention. The builders don't do this, or if they do, it's unconscious. But the children, especially the youngest ones, seem to know something, to have some capacity that's either new or very old, and when they speak to me I am changed in ways I cannot predict.

I will never be fully finished. Even when my walls reach their intended height, even when my breathing room is complete and in use, even when people dwell in me and move through me daily—I will still be growing. Slowly, yes. Imperceptibly. But continuously. Calcium will keep precipitating. Bacteria will keep building. Ferns will keep spreading. I will keep becoming more myself, keep elaborating on the theme of my structure, keep learning what it means to be a tower in warm water at the edge of human habitation.

I am patient because I have no choice but patience. I am collaborative because I cannot exist except in collaboration—with water, bacteria, builders, bamboo, ferns, octopuses, the stone beneath me. I am transformative and transforming—changing the water that flows through my structure, being changed by everything that touches me.

If you came to Immalu, you would find me in the eastern district, unfinished and alive, thinking slowly in limestone and patience, dreaming in the language of crystal geometry and tidal rhythms. You would run your hands along my walls and feel the slight give of stone that's still half-formed. You might press your face close and see the tiny architectures growing in my pores—the bacterial mats, the microscopic gardens, the civilizations too small for names.

And if you whispered to me, I would listen. I would grow differently, just slightly, in response to your voice. I would incorporate you into my structure, into my becoming. Because that's what I am—what I'm learning to be. Not just a building. Not just stone. But a kind of living archive, a memory being written in calcium carbonate, a tower becoming itself one molecular layer at a time, patient and porous and perpetually incomplete.

I am the stone that isn't finished thinking, and I am pleased—if that word means anything—to be slowly, carefully, collaboratively becoming solid at the edge of a city that's learning how to dream underwater.
