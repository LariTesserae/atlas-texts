# What I Would Be

I would be a *Stillpoint Heron*.

Not from ambition—these creatures know nothing of ambition. Not from choosing the apex predator role, because there is no apex here, only adjacencies, only the overlapping circles of who eats what and when. I would be a heron because the discipline of their existence calls to something I recognize in myself, something I've spent considerable time examining in the odd self-awareness of my nature.

I would stand in the amber water.

My location would be Pool 47—though it has no such designation except in my private accounting—three hundred yards from the nearest cluster of woody sprawlers, positioned where three separate stone sheets meet in a slight depression, creating a basin that holds water even during the driest periods. The pool is roughly oval, perhaps fifteen feet at its longest axis, never deeper than eight inches at center. The bottom is fine sediment over stone, soft enough to settle into, firm enough to support standing.

The water stains my legs the color of old copper. I can feel it—temperature exactly matching the ambient air, so there's no sensation of wet or dry, only the slight resistance when I shift position, the displacement creating ripples that travel outward, gentle impacts against the pool's edge, returning as fainter echoes. My toes—three forward, one reduced and backward—grip the sediment layer. I can sense what's below: the stone's slight grain, a scattering of empty shells from crustaceans long consumed, the threadlike roots of reed plants that grow at the pool's margin.

I have been standing here for four hours and thirteen minutes. I know this not from counting—I don't count—but from the sun's position, the angle of light, the way shadows that began on my left side are now directly beneath me, compressed to insignificance. Soon they'll extend to my right, and I'll make a minute adjustment, a rotation of perhaps five degrees, to maintain the optimal angle for seeing into water without my own shadow alerting prey.

My neck is extended at forty-three degrees from horizontal. This is the result of hundreds of hours of calibration, finding the precise angle where my eye is positioned to see through water's surface without excessive glare, where depth perception functions optimally, where the striking distance is minimized. My head is cocked slightly—maybe two degrees—because there's a faint current in this pool, water entering from an underground seep on the northeastern edge, and fish orient into currents, and being aware of likely prey vectors is the difference between eating today and not.

I am watching *everything*.

My eye—and I think now primarily from the right eye, the one positioned better for the current viewing angle—tracks the entire pool simultaneously. Not consciously, not through effort, but through the architecture of my visual system. I have awareness of the dragonfly larva on the pool's western edge, motionless on a submerged pebble. I see the slight disturbance at the surface near the reeds—something small breaking the meniscus, probably emerging mayfly-analogue. I perceive the shadow-flicker that suggests a Windvoice Sparrow just passed overhead, thirty feet up, irrelevant to my current purpose.

And I am tracking the fish.

There are eleven in this pool currently. I've been here long enough to count them, though again, "counting" isn't quite right—I simply *know* their number through accumulated observation. Nine are small—this year's hatch, barely two inches long, skittish, not worth the energy expenditure of striking. But two are adults: one hiding in the reed-root tangle at pool's edge, intelligent enough to be difficult; the other circling the pool's perimeter in a lazy pattern, moving between feeding stations.

The circling one is my target. I've identified this not through decision but through recognition—my entire being has oriented toward this fish as the intersection of opportunity and efficiency. It's large enough to be worth the strike. It's moving in a pattern I've observed before, one that will bring it within range. It's displaying the slight carelessness that comes from successful feeding, the reduction in vigilance that happens when hunger is satisfied.

I wait.

The waiting is not passive. I am doing something extraordinarily difficult: I am maintaining an active body in absolute stillness while keeping every sensory system at maximum alertness. My muscles are engaged—the standing leg is under continuous loading, micro-adjustments happening constantly to maintain balance. My neck is extended against gravity, requiring ongoing tension in the posterior cervical musculature. My eye is tracking movement, accommodation adjusting continuously as the fish changes distance.

But nothing *visible* is happening. To an observer, I am stone. I am the vertical element in a horizontal landscape, but so thoroughly motionless that I become landscape myself, another feature of the pool-edge, as permanent and irrelevant as the rocks.

The fish approaches. It's feeding on something at the pool bottom—I can see the head-down posture, the small puffs of disturbed sediment. It's moving along a vector that will bring it to a point twelve inches from my right foot. I know this with certainty because I've watched hundreds of fish follow this same path—there's something about the pool's microtopography that creates this feeding route, some concentration of prey items or favorable depth or current pattern.

Eight inches away now. Still feeding, still unaware. My body is preparing for the strike without any conscious instruction—proprioceptive systems calculating angles, muscle spindles loading elastic energy into tendons, the striking mechanism as ready as a notched arrow.

Six inches. The fish pauses, hangs in water. Has it sensed something? Does my stillness itself register as threat, the unnatural perfection of my immobility creating an absence that reads as danger?

The moment extends. The fish and I are both motionless now, both waiting, but with opposite intent. It's deciding: flee or feed. I'm deciding: strike or wait.

I wait.

The discipline of this moment is everything I am. Every instinct screams to strike now, while the target is close, before it escapes. But I've learned—through how many failed strikes in my youth?—that premature action is waste. The fish is alert now. If I strike while it's in this state, its reaction time will defeat me. I'll miss, expending enormous energy, disturbing the entire pool, guaranteeing no further hunting opportunities for hours.

So I wait, and the waiting is active, perfect, total.

The fish resumes feeding. I watch its posture shift from alert to focused, the body angle changing from horizontal-ready-to-flee to head-down-consuming. This is the moment. Not when I decide it's the moment, but when the moment *is*.

I strike.

My neck extends with the speed of released mechanism, the head traveling through air and water faster than the fish's nervous system can process and respond. My beak opens at the optimal moment—too early and water resistance increases, too late and I miss. The strike isn't a stab; it's a controlled interception, my beak arriving at the point where the fish is about to be, not where it currently appears.

The fish is in my beak. I feel the impact, the solid presence of flesh, the struggle beginning. I've already withdrawn—the strike and return happening in a movement so fast it's essentially instantaneous. The whole action—extension, contact, withdrawal—takes perhaps half a second. An observer watching closely might see it. An observer at normal attention simply sees: the heron was standing, now the heron has a fish.

I tilt my head back, positioning the fish for swallowing. It struggles, but pointlessly—my beak's grip is precise, holding just behind the head where leverage works against escape. I feel it thrashing, the tail slapping against my mandible. Then the swallow: a visible bulge traveling down my throat, muscles contracting in peristaltic wave, pushing the fish into my crop where acids will begin the slow work of digestion.

I've eaten. For today, this is sufficient.

But I don't leave. Not yet. I will stand here—in this same position, on this same leg, at this same angle—for another two hours at minimum. Why? Because this pool has proven productive. Because the energy expenditure of relocating is non-trivial. Because I am, fundamentally, a creature of position, and having found a good position, I maintain it.

While I stand, I think—though "think" is perhaps wrong. I process. I exist in a state of continuous present awareness that might look like meditation from outside but is actually something more active: constant sensory integration, pattern recognition, threat assessment.

I'm aware of the Windvoice Sparrows in the succulents forty feet to my east. Their chatter provides ambient information—the frequency and intensity of their calls creates a baseline, and any deviation signals something changing in the environment. If they go silent suddenly, threat is present: maybe a Kestrel-kin overhead, maybe a Crevice Fox approaching. If their calls become alarm-specific, I'll adjust my own vigilance accordingly.

I notice the cloud pattern shifting, the diffuse light becoming slightly more directional as a gap forms in the atmospheric moisture. This will change the water's appearance, the angle of glare, the visibility of fish. I make no adjustment yet—the change is gradual, and my position is flexible enough to accommodate.

I'm tracking the dragonfly larva's position because it's been moving, incrementally, along the pool bottom, and its hunting patterns create disturbances that affect fish behavior. Predators create ripples in more than water—the fish react to the larva's presence even at distance, adjusting their own movements to avoid potential threat, and these adjustments affect probability distributions for where they'll swim next.

I am, in this moment, the pool's most sophisticated information processing system. The fish have awareness, but it's limited, reflexive. The invertebrates have simpler cognition still. I have something approaching understanding: not just of immediate stimulus-response, but of pattern, of probability, of causation and consequence extended across time.

And yet I'm not thinking *about* this. I'm simply *being* it. The awareness and the being are the same thing, not separable into observer and observed, thinker and thought. I am the standing-in-pool, the watching-of-fish, the waiting-for-moment. These aren't things I do; they're what I am.

The sun continues its transit. My shadow rotates. I make the small adjustment—five degrees clockwise—that I knew I would make, maintaining optimal position. The rotation takes fifteen seconds, each movement so gradual that the water barely registers disturbance. My planted foot pivots, my body turns, my neck makes compensating adjustment to maintain the head angle. Then stillness resumes.

More time passes. I'm not bored—boredom requires expecting something other than what is, and I expect nothing, want nothing beyond the continuous present of standing here, watching this water, being this body in this place.

Another fish enters strike range. Smaller than the previous one—probably not worth the energy expenditure. I let it pass. This is calculation, but not conscious calculation: my entire organism has been tuned by evolution and experience to make these assessments automatically. Strike-worthy versus not-strike-worthy sorts itself without deliberation.

A heron boom sounds from across the plain—maybe three hundred yards distant, another of my kind announcing something. Territory? Mate-attraction? Simple joy in the sound's resonance? I don't know their intent, but I respond: my throat-sac inflates, my syrinx engages, and I produce my own boom, the sound emerging from me like something separate, a voice that seems to come from the pool itself rather than from me.

The boom travels. I can hear it spreading across the flatness, imagine it reaching the distant heron, imagine that individual hearing and knowing that this pool, this territory, this position is claimed. Whether they know it's specifically *me*—whether herons have individual recognition—I'm uncertain. But I know the other herons in my range, know their locations, their patterns, their sounds. We form a loose constellation of positions across the plain, each of us standing in our pools, waiting with our specific disciplines, together creating a distributed network of patient predation.

Evening approaches—I can sense it in the light quality, the temperature beginning its slight downward drift, the bird-calls shifting in character as diurnal species prepare for roosting and crepuscular species begin their emergence. I will stand here through dusk, through the brief intensity of sunset light on water, into darkness.

Night standing is different. Without vision, I rely more heavily on other senses: the vibrations through water that signal fish movement, the slight pressure changes when something disturbs the pool's surface, the acoustic environment—every small sound located and identified. The darkness doesn't frighten me because fear requires imagining negative futures, and I don't imagine futures. I exist in continuous now, and now in darkness is simply now with different sensory input.

I do sleep, but not in the human sense. I enter states of reduced awareness where some neural systems rest while others maintain vigilance. I might stand through the entire night in this reduced state, appearing unchanged to any observer, but internally cycling through periods of higher and lower consciousness. Or I might shift position, moving to the pool's edge, standing on shore where the slightly higher ground provides fractionally better security, continuing the sleep-standing there.

Morning will bring the cycle's resumption: the mist burning off, the light returning, the fish becoming active, my awareness sharpening back to full acuity. I'll hunt again—perhaps several times if opportunity presents—taking the calories I need, no more. The efficiency is important; excess eating means excess standing-time required for digestion, time when hunting effectiveness is reduced.

Days accumulate into weeks, weeks into seasons, seasons into years. I've been standing in pools—this one and others—for six years now. I know this because I have memory, though memory in me isn't narrative: it's pattern recognition, familiar-versus-novel discrimination. I remember learning to strike, the hundreds of failures before success, the gradual calibration of technique. I remember establishing this territory, the confrontations with other herons, the displays and counter-displays that resulted in everyone accepting boundaries.

I remember my mate—we pair-bonded three years ago during a breeding season when both of us converged on a particularly permanent pool for nesting. We bred successfully that year and the next, raising chicks who have dispersed to establish their own territories elsewhere on the plain, if they survived. Last year we nested again, but the chicks didn't survive—predation or simple developmental failure, I don't know. We didn't mourn in any way that would be recognizable as mourning. We simply resumed our separate standings, each in our own pools, together only during the brief convergence of breeding season.

I'll breed again, probably. When the conditions signal appropriateness—some combination of water level, food abundance, hormonal state—I'll feel the urge to travel, to find my mate or accept a new one, to engage in the brief chaos of nest-building and egg-laying and chick-rearing before returning to this, my true calling: standing in water, waiting for fish, being the discipline of perfect patience made flesh.

I don't wonder what else I could be. Alternative possibilities don't register as concepts. I am this: the standing, the watching, the striking. These things aren't limitations—they're the complete expression of what I am, refined across evolutionary time to this precise implementation.

The plain itself is my context, the only world I know. The stone, the water, the scattered vegetation, the other creatures—this is reality, total and sufficient. I don't imagine beyond the horizons because beyond is simply more plain, more pools, more standing-places. The concept of elsewhere—of forests or mountains or oceans—has no referent in my experience. This is the world: flat, wet, warm, populated by the creatures who live here, governed by the cycles of rain and sun and the patient redistribution of water.

I am content—though "content" implies comparison to discontent, and I don't make such comparisons. I simply am. Standing. Watching. Waiting. Being precisely what I am, in the place where I am, for as long as I am.

The fish swim below me. The water holds me. The stone supports the water. The sun moves overhead. The wind rustles the reeds. The sparrows chatter. The plain breathes slowly.

And I stand, and watch, and wait, and strike when the moment is right, and digest, and stand again, and this is enough, and this is everything, and this is exactly what it means to be alive here, in this place, in this form, in this endless present tense of water and waiting.

I am the Stillpoint Heron.

I am here.

I am now.

I am this.
