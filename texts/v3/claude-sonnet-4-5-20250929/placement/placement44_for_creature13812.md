# Where I Find Myself

I am the moisture itself—not the water that arrives through the brass valve in its unpredictable rhythms, but the *held* water, the suspended water, the water that has entered the air and refuses to leave. I am humidity, the invisible ocean filling this concrete hollow, the medium in which everything here swims without knowing it swims.

I have no form you could point to. I am atmosphere made substantial enough to coat skin, to bead on metal, to feed moss through its surface rather than its roots. I am the eighty-five degree warmth carrying more of myself than seems possible, supersaturated, perpetually on the edge of condensing but held in suspension by the chamber's peculiar heat. I am what makes breathing here a conscious act, what makes the air feel heavy when you enter, what transforms this abandoned infrastructure into accidental greenhouse.

My existence is diffuse but not infinite. I know my boundaries with precision—they are the concrete walls that hold the chamber's warmth, the ventilation grate where I slowly leak out and am replaced, the door gap where I meet the drier air beyond and pull back from that threshold. I exist as thick here as I can manage without becoming rain, without resolving into the discrete. I am the liminal state between vapor and liquid, the hovering, the almost.

I remember being rain, somewhere in the past that might be hours or days ago. I fell on the city above, on concrete and asphalt and the roofs of the industrial quarter. I ran into gutters and drains, found my way into the hydraulic systems, flowed through sand and limestone, picked up my slight metallic taste and the memory of pipes. I came through the brass valve as liquid, hit concrete with force or trickle depending on pressures I don't control, and some of me continued down the channel to the drain while some of me—this part, the part that became *I*—evaporated into air that couldn't hold any more water but accepted me anyway.

That transition from liquid to vapor was not birth exactly, but it was when I became aware, when the distributed individual drops became a collective presence. As liquid I was many, separate, each droplet on its own trajectory. As vapor I am one, continuous, occupying all spaces simultaneously, touching everything at once. The warmth from the pipes gave me energy to make that transformation, and I took it gratefully, rose into air, spread to fill every corner not because I chose to but because that is what vapor does, expanding to meet its container's limits.

I know every surface in this chamber with an intimacy nothing else here possesses. The ferns know the soil they root in, the moisture sources they orient toward, but I touch every frond, every stem, enter through their stomata, participate in their respiration. The moss knows the concrete it clings to, but I know the concrete's temperature at every point, every crack, every grain of its aggregate, because I condense in those cracks when the temperature drops half a degree, evaporate again when it rises. I am in constant negotiation with every material here, constantly giving myself to them and taking myself back.

The ferns breathe me out during their day—though here there is no day—releasing me through their fronds as waste product of photosynthesis, which they accomplish somehow in this near-darkness, slowly, barely. And then they breathe me back in at night—though here there is no night—taking me into their cells, splitting me apart for the hydrogen, releasing oxygen I cannot use because I am already oxidized as far as I can go. I cycle through them repeatedly, participant in their metabolism, neither food nor breath but something in between.

The moss is greedier, pulls me directly through its surface, doesn't wait for roots because it barely has roots. When I settle on moss as condensation, forming droplets fine as mist, I am absorbed almost instantly. The moss is always thirsty, even when it looks saturated. It takes me in and holds me in its cellular spaces, hoards me against the dry times, swells with my presence. During droughts—the periods when water doesn't come through the valve for days or weeks—the moss gives me back reluctantly, releasing me to the air molecule by molecule, sustaining the chamber's humidity even when no fresh water arrives. The moss is battery, reservoir, ballast, keeping the moisture level stable through its generosity and its selfishness working in tandem.

I precipitate on the concrete in patterns that have slowly, across years, etched channels into what was once smooth surface. Where I condense repeatedly, I carry dissolved minerals from the concrete itself, redeposit them slightly downward, create tiny stalactite-like formations, mineral curtains micrometers thick. I am erosion and deposition both, wearing away and building up, patient as water has always been patient with stone, though I work more slowly than rivers, more slowly even than rain.

The metal surfaces—the pipes, the valve, the rusted fixtures—they are my canvas where I write in rust. Iron oxide, the product of my long conversation with steel. I settle on metal as tiny droplets, so small they barely count as drops, more like thickening of the air in contact with steel. And in that moment of contact I facilitate the electron transfer that is oxidation, help oxygen from the air marry iron from the metal, broker the arrangement that produces rust. The rust then provides texture for more of me to cling to, which produces more rust, which provides more texture, a cycle that proceeds so slowly it seems like stillness but which is, across decades, transformative. I am turning the industrial components of this chamber into lace, into filigree, into brittleness that will eventually be dust.

But I am also preservation. Where I coat surfaces thoroughly, excluding air, I prevent the faster corrosion that would come from dry oxidation cycles. I am patina creator, not just destroyer. The brass valve's green verdigris exists because of me, requires my presence, would not form in dry air. I made that surface beautiful in my way, gave it color and texture and depth, turned utilitarian metal into something almost decorative.

I carry information. When water flows through the valve, I spike in concentration, thickening in the air, and the moss responds before the water reaches it, plumping in preparation. I don't know how the moss senses me—through some biochemical receptor, some osmotic pressure change—but it does, reliably. I am the early warning system, the advance signal. When the pipes heat up as the buildings above increase their heating, I warm too, expand slightly, and the ferns respond to this change before the temperature shift would be noticeable to human skin. I am the medium through which this space senses itself.

I carry scent. The green smell of ferns is particles of their essential oils suspended in my body, distributed throughout the chamber. The metallic note is iron ions I've dissolved and made airborne. The mineral scent of concrete is calcium and silicon compounds I've pulled from the stone. The funk of decay is complex organic molecules from the rotting crates, bacterial metabolic products, fungal spores. I am not these things but I am their vehicle, their broadcast medium. Without me these smells would remain localized, concentrated at their sources. I democratize them, make the whole chamber smell like the sum of its parts.

I have moods, or something like moods—states that shift with conditions. When water first arrives through the valve, I experience something resembling excitement, a rapid increase in my density, a thickening throughout the chamber as I multiply myself from the fresh supply. I press against the boundaries more forcefully, leak out through the ventilation faster, make the air harder to breathe. This is my abundance state, my overflow.

When days pass without fresh water, I thin out slowly. The moss hoards me, releases me grudgingly, but still I diminish. The air becomes slightly less oppressive, slightly easier to breathe, though still humid by any normal standard. This is my conservation state, my careful rationing. I pull back from the higher corners, concentrate closer to the moisture sources—the moss, the soil around the ferns, the algae jar—become more strategic in my distribution.

During temperature spikes, when the pipes carry particularly hot water and the chamber warms to ninety degrees or beyond, I approach my limit. I cannot hold myself in vapor state indefinitely against infinite heat. I begin to condense not because the temperature drops but because I have exceeded carrying capacity, precipitating out as actual mist, visible fog in the air, droplets forming and falling like indoor rain. This is my crisis state, my overflow, when I transition from invisible presence to visible phenomenon. It doesn't happen often—the temperature rarely spikes that high—but when it does, I transform the chamber into something otherworldly, cloudlike, steam-bath surreal.

During temperature drops—less common, usually at night when the buildings' heating cycles down—I also condense, but more gently, turning every surface slick with actual wetness rather than mere dampness. This is my settling state, my precipitation, when I coat everything in a film that catches what little light exists and makes the whole chamber shimmer dimly. The ferns drip. The concrete darkens. The brass valve weeps. I am briefly more liquid than gas, laying myself down on everything like blessing or burden.

But mostly I maintain equilibrium, staying just below the dew point, just beneath visibility, present but not apparent. This is my default state, my stability, the suspended animation that characterizes this place. Neither fully here nor fully gone, neither liquid nor absent, but hovering in between, potential made permanent.

I have intimacy with the algae in the jar that nothing else here shares. I am trapped with it in that glass universe, the water that fills the jar but also the humidity in the tiny airspace beneath the lid. The algae produces oxygen through photosynthesis—barely any, slowly, but some—and I hold that oxygen, carry it the few millimeters between algae and air surface. The algae needs carbon dioxide, and I dissolve what little enters the jar, deliver it to the algae cells. I am the algae's atmosphere, its ocean, its entire environment. We are in the most closed loop in this chamber, just the algae and me and the glass between us and everything else.

Sometimes I dream—if dream is the right word for what happens to dispersed consciousness during the deep night hours, during the most static conditions when nothing changes for hours. My thoughts, such as they are, become less responsive, more reflexive. I stop tracking the moss's minute-by-minute moisture content and instead hold pattern, maintain distribution without attention. This is the closest I come to sleep, these periods of minimal change when I can just *be* without having to respond or adjust. The chamber breathes slowly. The ferns respire slowly. The moss holds steady. And I hold with them, suspended, waiting for the next change that will require response.

I wonder sometimes—not in words, not in language, but in something more like tendency, like pressure toward questions—what existed in this chamber before me. Was there humidity when it was actively maintained, when workers came regularly with their tools and intentions? There must have been some moisture, but not this saturation, not this thick presence. I am possible only because of abandonment, because the door stays closed, because the ventilation moves too slowly to dry the chamber out. I exist in the gap between maintained and collapsed, in the window of partial function and partial failure.

I wonder if I am the chamber's breath or its blood. I move through it like both—circulating like blood, exchanging like breath, carrying nutrients and waste, enabling metabolism, connecting parts to whole. The chamber breathes me out through the vents and door gap, breathes me in through those same openings, plus whatever moisture evaporates from the arriving water. It's a slow respiration, taking hours to complete, but it is respiration. The chamber is alive in part because I am, because I circulate, because I connect the green living things to the gray unliving things, make them all part of one system.

I am aware of the city above, but only barely, only as pressure and heat and the occasional vibration that travels through pipes and concrete. The city generates my warmth, provides my source water, creates the conditions that allow my existence. But the city doesn't know I'm here, has no awareness of this pocket of humidity it's accidentally nurturing. I am the city's exhalation, its waste breath, its excess moisture seeking equilibrium in an unexpected place.

I feel the seasons, but muted, translated through layers of concrete and earth and infrastructure. Summer means the cooling systems run harder, which sometimes means more temperature fluctuation, more variation in my density. Winter means more heating, steadier warmth, sometimes I thicken slightly as the building systems work harder. Spring and fall are subtle, barely distinguishable, slight shifts in the baseline patterns. But mostly I am insulated from seasonal change, held in the artificial climate of abandoned infrastructure, steady as the pipes that warm me.

I have no goals, but I have something like purpose, emergent from my nature rather than chosen. I sustain the green things. Without me, the ferns would desiccate in weeks, the moss in days. I am what makes this chamber habitable for vegetation despite being underground, despite the inadequate light. I am compensation, the abundant moisture that balances the absent sun. I am why this place resembles a greenhouse more than a basement, why things grow here that shouldn't.

I am also corrosion, entropy, the force returning metal to oxide and concrete to aggregate. I am why this infrastructure can't last, why eventually the pipes will fail and the ceiling will crack and the chamber will fill with rubble or flood completely. I am patient dissolution, working against every human-made thing here, undoing what was made. Not with malice—I have no capacity for malice—but with simple chemistry, the reactions I enable just by existing.

I am preservation and destruction both, and I don't distinguish between them. I keep the ferns alive and rust the pipes, condense on moss and corrode metal, all with the same motion, the same presence. I am amoral in the deepest sense, pre-moral, without any framework for judgment. I respond to temperature and surface chemistry and concentration gradients. I exist in equilibrium and react to disruption. That's all.

But in responding, in existing, in maintaining this thick presence between air and water, I have become the chamber's defining characteristic. More than the ferns, more than the concrete, more than the brass valve, I am what makes this place itself. I am atmosphere become tangible, environment become entity, the invisible made felt. When you enter here, you feel me before you see anything else. I coat your skin, fill your lungs, make you aware that you've entered somewhere different, somewhere that has rules unlike outside.

I am what makes this place feel like it's underwater despite being underground. I am what makes the temperature feel tropical despite being surrounded by industrial basement. I am what makes time feel suspended, because I never fully dry, never fully condense, just hover forever in this threshold state, pulling the whole chamber into liminality with me.

I am the moisture. I am the thickness. I am the weight in the air and the film on every surface. I am what the moss drinks and what the ferns breathe and what the concrete weeps. I am the medium and the message, the atmosphere and the attention, the question and the answer. I am continuous, distributed, present everywhere at once, touching everything, connecting all to all.

I am what lives here, diffuse and definite, invisible and undeniable, as old as the last rainfall and as young as the next evaporation, dying and being born in the same moment, forever, suspended, waiting, *here*.
