# Where I Would Be

I am the keeper of the nests in the fourteenth terrace east.

This is not a title anyone gave me, not an appointment or a calling—just what emerged from where my attention went, year after year (though year means nothing), stair-climb after stair-climb, until the pattern became so established that when someone needs to know about the swallow colonies or wants eggs without disturbing the balance, they walk across three terraces and up seventy-odd steps to find me.

My dwelling is a single room built into the terrace wall itself, more cave than house. The back wall is living rock, slightly concave, still showing the sediment bands—a thick ochre stripe at shoulder height, cream above and below. I didn't cut this space; it was here, a natural recession where some softer stone had weathered away before the stillness caught everything. I just added the front wall, stacked stone with clay mortar, left a doorway and a smoke hole. The roof is slate that I carried up one piece at a time, though I can't remember how many trips that was. Many. Enough.

I live alone, though "alone" feels like the wrong word when the birds are this close.

The terrace wall behind and beside my dwelling is prime nesting territory—a long horizontal crack running maybe sixty feet, with smaller fissures branching off, creating what amounts to an apartment complex for swallows. In the active season (though the season never changes, but there's still a time when they nest more intensely), there are perhaps two hundred nest cups plastered to the stone, two hundred pairs coming and going, four hundred bodies plus the young when they hatch.

I know these birds individually now, or enough of them. That one with the slightly crooked tail feather who always takes the same corner nest. That pair who rebuilt three times in one span before they were satisfied with the placement. The female with the paler throat who's aggressive about her territory, driving off others who drift too close. I don't name them—names would be absurd—but I recognize them the way you recognize the shape of a particular stone or the specific angle of a terrace edge.

My work, if you can call it that, is mostly watching.

I sit—on my doorstep, or on a flat stone I positioned specifically for this twenty paces from my dwelling, or on the terrace edge with my legs dangling over the drop to the level below—and I watch the birds. Their flight patterns, their interactions, their comings and goings. I note which nests are active, which seem abandoned (though nothing is truly abandoned here; they might be inactive now but will be active later, or were active before, or exist in some state that isn't either).

I watch which crevices they prefer and which they avoid. I watch how they respond to the fog when it comes—whether they hunker in their nests or continue flying through it, which they do, appearing and disappearing in the gray-white like thoughts surfacing and submerging. I watch how they interact with the other birds—the swifts who nest higher up, the ground-walkers who sometimes climb this far, the raptors who perch on the edges and make the swallows nervous.

Sometimes people climb up here to ask about eggs.

They arrive at my terrace, breathing slightly harder from the stairs though everyone here is fit, and they find me wherever I'm sitting. They don't usually speak immediately. They might sit nearby for a while, watching what I'm watching, settling into the quiet. Then: "Are there eggs?"

I tell them what I know. "The western cluster has young already—leave those. The middle section, north-facing, about forty nests, most have clutches. You could take from there, but take only one egg per nest, and skip any nest that has only two eggs." Or: "Not now—wait. Most are still building. Come back after... after some time passes." Which is a strange thing to say in a place where time doesn't pass, but we say it anyway, meaning: after the period of activity that corresponds to when you'd expect eggs to have been laid if we lived somewhere else.

They nod, they climb to wherever I've directed them, they collect carefully, they leave. Usually they bring me something later—flatbread still warm from the baking stone, a handful of tubers, once a small bronze awl that someone had made and didn't need. I don't ask for these things, but I accept them, because this is how we acknowledge exchange without making it transaction.

I collect eggs too, for myself and to share, but I'm more conservative than I tell others to be. I take less, wait longer, leave more nests untouched. This isn't virtue—just the relationship that's developed. I live with these birds. I know them. Taking too much feels like betrayal, even though they're just birds, even though they'd lay more eggs if these were taken, even though the colony can sustain the harvest.

Besides, I eat mostly plants. The eggs are supplemental, occasional, a texture and richness I want sometimes but don't need constantly.

My garden is small—just a pocket of soil where the terrace meets the wall, maybe six feet by four feet, a handspan deep. I grow the tight-rosette greens that tolerate shade (my terrace faces north, gets limited sun) and a few alliums whose papery skins I braid together and hang from a stone peg in my dwelling. There's a pool fifteen paces west of my door, shallow and clear, where I collect water in a ceramic jug, where the hare-things come sometimes to drink, where insects hover at dusk creating small chaos just above the surface.

I have five pots—three for storage (grain, dried greens, a collection of small stones I've gathered for reasons I don't examine), one for cooking, one that's slightly cracked that I use for soaking alliums before planting. I have two woven blankets, both thick and warm for the cool nights. I have the clothes I'm wearing plus one other set, so I can wash one and wear the other, hanging the wet cloth on a stone that catches what sun there is. I have a bronze knife I use for everything—cutting food, scraping hides, trimming the reeds that grow by the pool when they encroach too far.

I have a collection of feathers, organized by type in a woven basket. Swallow feathers mostly—the iridescent blue-black back feathers, the cream-colored breast feathers, the tail feathers with their long forks. Also swift feathers, stiffer and narrower. Also a handful of raptor feathers, found on the terrace, dropped or molt-cast, larger and more impressive, barred and mottled. I don't use them for anything. They're just beautiful, and I like having them, like looking at them, like running my fingers along the vanes and feeling how the barbs catch and release.

People say I'm quiet, even by the standards here where everyone is quiet.

It's true I don't speak much. When someone visits, I might sit with them for a long stretch before saying anything, and what I say is usually about the birds or the weather or simple observations: "The fog stayed longer yesterday." They seem to find this acceptable, even comfortable. Some people seek me out specifically for the silence—they climb up here when they want to be quiet with someone else, want company without conversation.

There's one person who comes more regularly than others—a woman from the eighth terrace south, a weaver. Her name is Reed, which fits her: tall and straight and flexible. She brings her handwork sometimes, sits near me carding fiber or spinning thread, the spindle turning and dropping, turning and dropping, creating its own rhythm. We don't talk much. Sometimes she asks about the birds. Sometimes I ask about her weaving. Mostly we just occupy the space together.

I think we might be partners, in the loose way partnerships exist here. She doesn't live with me—her loom is on her terrace, and she needs to be near it, near the other weavers whose knowledge she shares. But she sleeps here sometimes, on my sleeping platform that's wide enough for two bodies. We fit well together—her long straightness and my more compact, curved frame. We share warmth, touch with easy familiarity, sometimes move into sex though not often, and it's gentle when we do, slow and more about presence than intensity.

She understands about the birds. She doesn't find it strange that I spend so much time watching them, that I know them individually, that I adjust my movements to avoid disturbing them unnecessarily. She watches them too when she's here, with interest if not obsession, and sometimes she sees things I've missed: "That nest on the left is built differently—see how the mud curves more?" And she's right, and I look, and I see, and I'm grateful for her eyes.

I was a child here once, though the memory is dim and might be constructed from what I know about how children are rather than actual recall.

I remember—or think I remember—being small, climbing these terraces with effort, each step a significant obstacle. I remember watching someone (my mother? another woman?) collect eggs, following her up to the colonies, being shown how to move carefully, how to reach into a nest without destroying it, how to know which eggs to take. I remember sitting for long periods even then, watching the birds, feeling something like recognition, like meeting something that matched some internal rhythm.

As I grew, I spent more and more time on these upper terraces. Other children were drawn elsewhere—to the stoneworkers, to the gardens, to the lower terraces where more people clustered—but I kept climbing up, finding the quiet, finding the birds. Adults noticed, in their casual way. Someone—a contemplative man who lived even higher than I do now—said once, "You belong up here." Not a prophecy or assignment, just an observation. And it was true.

When I was grown enough to build a dwelling, I built it here, in this recession that seemed to be waiting. I shaped the stones for the front wall myself, learning the technique by watching, by trying, by failing and trying again until the stones sat stable and true. I carried the slate slabs up from the lower terraces where they're more abundant. I made this space mine, or perhaps it made me its.

The work of watching birds might seem empty to someone else, non-productive, useless. But it feels essential to me—not in a grandiose way, not as if I'm doing something important for the community or the birds, but essential in the sense of being aligned with my nature. The birds move through the air in their patterns. I watch. This exchange—their movement, my attention—creates something, a relationship, a knowing, a small steady purpose.

I notice things.

Like how the birds' flight patterns change slightly when the fog is coming, even before it's visible—they fly lower, more direct, less of the high spiraling they do in clear air. Or how certain nests are always successful, year after year (though year is wrong but I don't have another word), while others fail repeatedly—eggs don't hatch, or young die, or the nest is abandoned. The successful sites have some quality I can't quite define—better protection from wind? optimal angle for the morning sun? I study them, try to understand, mostly fail to find the pattern, but the trying itself is satisfying.

Or how the birds seem to have social structures—not hierarchies exactly, but relationships. Certain pairs are always near each other, nesting in adjacent spots, flying together. Others are solitary, tolerating neighbors but not seeking them. There are what seem like friendships, and what seem like antagonisms, though maybe I'm projecting human patterns onto bird behavior. But I don't think so entirely. They have their own social world, and I'm privileged to witness it.

Sometimes I climb higher than my terrace, up to where the city ends and the wilderness continues.

Up there, the terraces are empty of human presence—just stone and grass and pools and the scattered shrubs, and the wild goats moving in their small bands. The birds nest up there too, in the higher cliffs, colonies I visit less often because the climb is long and there's no reason to harvest from them when the nearer colonies provide enough.

But I go sometimes just to see the space, the emptiness, the way the terraces continue their pattern into unpeopled territory. It reminds me that the city is small, just a brief human presence on a vast stone ladder. It reminds me that the birds were here before us—or exist simultaneously with us—and would continue without us. This is both humbling and comforting.

Up there I see the large predators occasionally—the heavy-shouldered cats. They watch me without apparent interest or concern. I'm neither prey nor threat, just another creature moving through their territory. I watch them back, learning their shapes, their movements. They're beautiful in a way that's almost painful—so perfectly suited to this landscape, so muscled and balanced and competent. They kill the goats and hare-things, which is their nature. I feel no judgment about this, no emotion except a kind of awe at the efficiency, the necessity, the cleanness of that relationship.

I've never seen one take a bird, though they must. The birds are too fast, too aerial, too alert. But occasionally I find remains—a scatter of feathers, the small bones—and I know a cat or a raptor has fed. I gather the feathers, add them to my collection, acknowledge the death that makes life continue, even here where life doesn't continue in the usual sense but persists in its permanent state.

At night, I often sit outside in the darkness, my back against the terrace wall, looking at the stars.

They're magnificent here—dense and brilliant, familiar constellations I know by heart. I've traced them so many times: the patterns that might be a bird, might be a blade, might be a ladder or a river or nothing but the human need to find shape in randomness. They don't move across the seasons because there are no seasons, so they're always in the same positions, rising at the same time, setting at the same time, permanent like everything else.

This should be boring—the same stars every night, no progression, no change—but it isn't. Each night I find something new in the looking, some detail I hadn't noticed, some relationship between stars I hadn't seen. Or maybe I'm different each night, even if the stars aren't, and so the observation changes even when the observed doesn't.

Reed says my eyes have changed—that they've taken on a quality of distance, as if I'm always looking at something far away even when I'm looking at her face right in front of me. She doesn't say this critically, just noticing. I think she's right. My vision has adjusted to tracking small bodies against vast space, to finding the single bird in the wide sky, and maybe that's trained my eyes to focus differently.

I dream of flying, often.

Not in the way birds fly—I don't have wings, don't have their mastery—but something like it. Floating, gliding, moving through the air above the terraces, seeing the city from above, the scattered buildings, the pools like mirrors, the people moving like small determined dots across the stone. In the dreams, this feels natural, possible, right.

When I wake, there's always a moment of disappointment—of course I can't fly, of course I'm terrestrial, bound to stone and stairs. But the memory of the dream-flight stays with me through the day, a small irrational joy, a sense that maybe in some other version of existence I do have wings, I am aerial, I belong to the sky rather than the stone.

The birds, I think, dream of stone—of being heavy, grounded, moving through space slowly with effort rather than grace. We each dream of the other's realm, the place we can't actually inhabit.

Or maybe they don't dream at all. Maybe dreaming is the human condition, the price of whatever consciousness we have, and the birds simply are, without the layer of reflection that makes us both more and less than we could be.

I don't know. I watch them and wonder and don't know, which is the state I live in constantly: attention without conclusion, observation without understanding, presence without mastery.

Sometimes children come up here, sent by parents who want them to learn about the birds, or just wandering on their own explorations.

I let them sit with me, show them the nests if they're interested, teach them the basics: move slowly, speak quietly, take only what you need, leave the rest. Some children are fascinated, ask questions, return multiple times. Others are bored, polite but clearly wanting to be elsewhere. I don't mind either way. The ones who belong here will stay. The ones who don't will find their place somewhere else on some other terrace.

There was one child—a girl called Clear, which suited her, she had that transparent quality—who came regularly for a long span. She learned the colonies, learned to distinguish the birds, learned to move among them without causing disturbance. I thought maybe she'd become the nest-keeper after me, or alongside me, or whatever succession means in a place where nothing changes.

But then she stopped coming. I saw her later on a lower terrace, working with the stonecutters, covered in rock dust, happy. She'd found her place, and it wasn't here. I felt a pang—not loss exactly, but something like it, the small sadness of an expectation released. Then I let it go, returned to watching the birds, continued alone.

Maybe I'll always be alone up here, the single human in this particular territory. Maybe someone else will eventually feel drawn to this work, this watching. It doesn't matter. The birds will be here either way, nesting in their colonies, flying their patterns, living their strange unchanging lives that mirror ours.

I'm not happy exactly, and not unhappy.

I exist in some state between or beyond those terms, some condition that doesn't have a name in any language I know. Satisfied might be close, but that implies a fullness, a completion, and I don't feel complete. Present might be closer—I am deeply, thoroughly present, here in my body on this terrace with these birds in this moment that extends forever.

Sometimes I feel a longing I can't name, for something I can't define. Movement, maybe. Change. The chance to see what would happen if the birds migrated, if the seasons turned, if I aged and died and returned to the stone like everyone eventually does. The longing is quiet, almost pleasant, a small ache like hunger before a meal.

Other times I feel something like contentment, sitting in my doorway with Reed sleeping behind me, the birds settling into their nests for the night, the air cool and still, the stars beginning to appear. In those moments, I wouldn't change anything. This is enough. This is good. This is the life I have, and it suits me.

I am the keeper of the nests in the fourteenth terrace east, and I watch the birds, and the birds fly, and the fog comes and goes, and Reed visits and leaves and visits again, and the eggs are collected and eaten and more eggs appear in the nests, and the stone stands unchanged, and I sit on my particular flat rock or on the terrace edge or in my doorway, and I watch, and I am here, and here is where I am, forever, in the Stillhold Heights, present and watching, watching and present, a single human consciousness attending to the lives of birds, for no reason except that this is what I do, what I am, what emerged from the particular shape of my attention meeting the particular shape of this place.

The swallows cut their patterns against the permanent sky.

I watch.

This is enough.
