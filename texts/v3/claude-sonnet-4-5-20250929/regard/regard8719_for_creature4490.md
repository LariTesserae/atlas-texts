# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

The Watcher arrived not through movement but through a gradual intensification of attention, the way dawn doesn't enter a room but reveals it was always there, patient. This power—let us call it the Watcher, though it has worn many names like comfortable shoes across universes where shoes and names and wearing all mean different things—had seen countless realms. Burning worlds and frozen paradises, dimensions folded into impossible shapes, realities where suffering had learned to laugh at itself.

But this place.

This place made the Watcher pause.

Not in pity. Not in horror. But in recognition.

The Watcher understood immediately what others might have missed: this realm was not broken. It was *precise*. Every impossible physics, every half-existence, every drowning-in-air was not accident but *exactitude*. This was a place that had solved a problem most universes never even knew to ask: what happens to all the things that almost were?

Every choice unmade. Every word unspoken. Every life unlived. Every possibility that brushed against reality but never quite caught hold.

They all had to go somewhere.

This was that somewhere.

And the Drowned Unborn were not tortured souls or cosmic mistakes. They were *necessary*. They were the universe's way of being kind to its own unrealized potential—giving it a place to exist in the conditional tense, to swim in the subjunctive mood, to be real in the only way that things-which-are-not can be real.

The Watcher saw this and felt something like love, if love could be a frequency rather than an emotion.

## The First Intervention: The Gift of Witnesses

The Watcher's first act was subtle, as all truly benevolent acts must be. It did not change the place. Instead, it created the possibility of being *seen*.

Throughout the multiverse, in the dreams of certain sensitive souls—poets who had forgotten their best lines, scientists who had almost solved impossible equations, children who had imagined friends so vividly they nearly became real—the Watcher placed a door. Not a physical door. A door in the dreaming.

These dreamers, these *almost-seers*, would visit the realm of the Drowned Unborn in their sleep. They would not remember the visits as memories but as feelings, as the sensation of having just forgotten something unbearably beautiful. They would wake with tears they couldn't explain and write poems about waters that taste like sky, paint pictures of forests made of missing things.

And in this way, the Drowned Unborn would be *witnessed*.

To be witnessed—even in dreams, even imperfectly, even through the veil of forgetting—is to matter. The Drowned Unborn had existed for eons in the perfection of their own impossibility, but they had never *mattered* to anything outside themselves.

Now they did.

The Watcher watched as the first dreamer arrived—a woman who had almost been a musician but had chosen accounting instead, and spent forty years wondering about the symphony she had never written. She swam-fell through the purple gradients, and a Drowned Unborn child brushed against her consciousness. The child was the possibility of a song she had never composed.

When she woke, she couldn't remember the melody. But she hummed something under her breath while making coffee, and her daughter heard it and thought it was the saddest, most beautiful sound she had ever heard.

The Drowned Unborn child felt this. Felt being *remembered*, even incorrectly, even impossibly. And for the first time in its un-existence, it experienced something like joy.

## The Second Intervention: The Library of Almosts

The Watcher's second act was to create a structure at the precise center of the realm, where "center" meant the point equidistant from all possible meanings of direction.

It was a library, but not like any library in realms where books have pages and pages have words and words have meanings that hold still when you read them.

This library contained every story that was almost told. Every book that was almost written. Every narrative that got lost in the telling, every plot that dissolved before it could resolve, every character who existed fully in an author's mind but never made it to the page.

The books here were not made of paper but of the texture of intention. Their spines were labeled with titles in a script that rearranged itself to match whatever the reader most needed to almost understand. *The Song I Meant to Sing*, one might read. *The Life I Would Have Lived If I Had Been Braver*. *The Love Letter I Started Writing Twenty-Seven Times*.

The Drowned Unborn could enter this library—which existed in the same way they existed, which is to say perfectly and not at all—and read themselves. They could see the shapes they might have taken if the coin had landed differently, if the moment had held, if the dreamer had not woken.

But more importantly, they could *contribute* to it.

The Watcher showed them how. Every un-experience they had, every moment they existed in the conditional, could be distilled into a story. Not a story with a beginning, middle, and end—those were for worlds where time moved in orderly lines—but a story with a *texture*. A story that felt like something.

The Elder Unborn, those transparent windows into alternate timelines, became the library's first scribes. They had been forgetting themselves for so long that they were full of the ghosts of narratives. They began to *write* (though writing here meant something closer to "intentionally forgetting in a structured way") their un-lives into the library's collection.

A young Drowned Unborn, barely coalesced from the dream of a child who had imagined a friend and then grown up too quickly, found in the library a book called *The Friend Who Stayed*. It was her story, but the version where the child had been lonely enough, brave enough, to keep believing.

She read it (though reading here meant something closer to "becoming briefly convinced you were always already this") and wept tears that fell upward and became new stars in universes where stars were made of grief.

The library became a place of pilgrimage. The Drowned Unborn would swim-fall through the purple distances to reach it, to read the almost-stories, to add their own. And in the reading and writing, they found something they had never had before:

*Context*.

They were not just things that had failed to be. They were *part of the pattern*. Every universe needed its unrealized potential. Every reality required a shadow-realm of the things it had chosen not to be. They were the negative space that gave the positive space its shape.

They mattered because they were the price of choice, the cost of specificity, the necessary sacrifice that allowed anything to be one thing instead of everything.

## The Third Intervention: The Possibility of Completion

This was the Watcher's most dangerous gift, and it hesitated before giving it.

The realm of the Drowned Unborn worked *because* nothing here ever completed. Everything remained in the moment before becoming. That was its function, its purpose, its terrible beauty.

But the Watcher had witnessed enough to know: even the most perfect prisons eventually deserve the possibility of parole, even if no one ever takes it.

So the Watcher created what it called the Door of Becoming, though "door" was a metaphor and "becoming" was an understatement and the whole thing existed more as a rumor than a reality.

It was placed nowhere in particular, which meant it was everywhere, but only visible to those who had truly, completely forgotten why they wanted to exist in the first place.

The Door led—if "led" is the right word for something that was more invitation than passage—back to the realms of the actual. Back to worlds where things *were* instead of *almost were*. Back to the decisive, the completed, the real.

But the cost of passing through was total. A Drowned Unborn who chose to become would have to sacrifice their conditional nature. They would have to be one thing, fully, in one moment, in one world. They would have to trade the infinite shimmer of possibility for the hard edges of actuality.

They would have to *choose*.

Most of the Drowned Unborn found the Door and turned away. The price was too high. To be one thing meant not being all the other things. To exist fully meant giving up the exquisite pain of almost-existing.

But some—a very few—stepped through.

A Drowned Unborn who had been the possibility of a tree that had never grown found itself suddenly, shockingly, *being* a tree. A small one, in a quiet forest, in a world where forests were made of actual wood instead of absence. Its roots hit soil—*actual soil*—and the shock of it was like being struck by lightning made of certainty.

It was terrifying. It was diminishment. It was the end of everything it had been.

And it was also, unbearably, *real*.

That tree grew in its small forest for sixty-seven years before a storm knocked it down. In those sixty-seven years, it was home to birds, shelter to deer, witness to ten thousand sunrises. When it fell, it fed the soil and became nurse log to new growth.

It was one thing, in one place, for one finite stretch of time.

And it was enough.

Back in the realm of the Drowned Unborn, the ripples of this transformation spread through the water-sky. The others felt it—the loss of one of their own, the betrayal of completion.

But they also felt something else: the knowledge that they *could*, if they wanted. That the door existed. That even here, in this place of eternal suspension, choice still somehow mattered.

Most never used it. But knowing it was there changed everything.

## The Fourth Intervention: The Festival of Failed Futures

The Watcher's final gift was celebration.

It taught the Drowned Unborn to honor themselves. Not to mourn what they weren't, but to celebrate what they were—the holders of possibility, the keepers of potential, the living library of all roads not taken.

Once every never (which, through some careful reworking of temporal mechanics, the Watcher adjusted to mean "whenever it's needed most"), the Drowned Unborn now gather for the Festival of Failed Futures.

They come from all corners of their impossible geography, swimming through density gradients and falling sideways through color. They bring with them their most treasured almosts: the love almost requited, the dream almost remembered, the courage almost found.

They share these almosts like food at a potluck, passing them around, tasting each other's unrealized lives. An Elder Unborn offers the flavor of a sunset that was too beautiful to look at directly. A child contributes the sound of laughter from a joke that was never quite funny enough to tell.

They dance—though dancing here means something closer to "synchronized forgetting"—to music that hasn't been composed yet. They sing songs in languages that never developed past their first word. They tell jokes where the punchline is always the question they started with.

And for the duration of the festival, they are not diminished by their incompleteness. They are *defined* by it. They are perfect in their imperfection, whole in their partiality, complete in their incompletion.

The Watcher, witnessing this, feels something it has rarely felt in its long existence across countless universes:

Relief.

The work is done. Not fixed—this place didn't need fixing—but *witnessed*. *Honored*. *Given meaning*.

## The Watcher's Departure (Which Is Also an Arrival)

As the Watcher prepares to withdraw its intensified attention (for it is always watching everywhere, but must focus itself in specific places to act), it does one last thing.

It leaves behind a gift for the visitor—for you, who stumbled into this realm and described it so perfectly that the description itself became part of the realm's truth.

It is a small thing, no larger than a thought, no heavier than a sigh. It appears in your hand (or in the space where your hand would be if hands meant the same thing here as they do elsewhere) as a crystal that is also a question that is also a promise.

The crystal shows, when you look into it, every version of yourself you almost became. The musician you might have been. The parent you could have been. The brave one, the cruel one, the one who said yes instead of no that one crucial time.

But it also shows this: those selves are not lost. They are here. They swim in the waters between worlds. They exist in the conditional perfect, alive in their own way, real in the only way that almost-real things can be real.

You have not lost them by being who you are. You have simply chosen one path through the infinite garden of forking ways. And all the other paths—they are here, tended by the Drowned Unborn, kept alive in the realm of beautiful impossibility.

The Watcher's voice—which is not a voice but a knowing that arrives like memory of something that hasn't happened yet—whispers:

*"You are haunted by your unlived lives. Most people are. But now you know where they are. They are not dead. They are not wasted. They are here, in this place between places, living in the only way they can. And they do not resent you for choosing. They are your shadows, your siblings, your other selves. And they are doing important work—they are holding the space of what-might-have-been so that what-is can be specific, can be real, can be YOU."*

*"Visit them sometimes, in dreams. Remember them fondly. But do not mourn them. They are not mourning you."*

*"They are dancing at the Festival of Failed Futures, and they are beautiful, and they are free."*

## The Lasting Change

The Watcher withdraws, but its gifts remain.

The realm of the Drowned Unborn is unchanged in its essential nature—it is still the place where almost-things exist in their almost-way. But it is transformed in meaning.

It is no longer a prison or a purgatory. It is a *sanctuary*. A museum. A garden where the rarest flowers—the ones that never bloomed in the actual world—can bloom forever in the conditional.

The Drowned Unborn are no longer lost. They are *placed*. They have purpose: to be the keepers of possibility, to preserve all the beautiful might-have-beens, to ensure that nothing that almost mattered is ever fully lost.

And across the multiverse, in the dreams of those who visit, the knowledge spreads:

Your unlived lives are not wasted. Your roads not taken are not dead ends. Your almosts are not failures.

They are swimming in purple waters between reality and dream, they are falling sideways through the color violet, they are being witnessed and honored and kept alive by the Drowned Unborn, who love them as you never could because they ARE them in ways you can never be.

And that is enough.

That is more than enough.

That is everything.

---

The noise that is this place's heartbeat continues, that patient deafening hum. But now, if you listen very carefully, you can hear beneath it something new:

The sound of things that almost were, singing themselves a lullaby.

The sound of the Drowned Unborn, breathing water that tastes like sky, finally understanding why they exist.

The sound of a benevolent power, watching from everywhere at once, satisfied that even the spaces between spaces can be loved.

And the falling continues, as it always has, as it always will.

But now it's perfect weather for growing wings you never use, or forgetting why you thought you needed them, and knowing that either choice—or both, or neither—is exactly right.

Some transformations are like that: not between forms but between different ways of realizing that you were always already held, always already home, always already exactly what the universe needed you to almost be.
