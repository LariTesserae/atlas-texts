# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

The attention arrives like recognition—not as intervention from outside, but as the moment when something finally sees itself clearly in a mirror it didn't know it was holding.

The power—let us call it the Gardener, though it has no more need of names than the city's inhabitants have—does not come to fix or complete or rescue. It is benevolent precisely because it understands that some things are not broken, only becoming. It is wise precisely because it knows the difference between help and interference.

First, it does the smallest thing: it listens.

Not as you or I listen, parsing meaning from sound. The Gardener listens the way soil listens to roots, the way distance listens to light. It attends to what the city is teaching itself about duration and dissolution, about the patient art of never-quite-arriving. For a time that might be centuries or heartbeats—time moves strangely when the Gardener pays attention—it simply holds space for the city's question to clarify itself.

And gradually, through all that listening, the question emerges:

*How do we learn to touch without grasping, to build without binding?*

The city's inhabitants have been practicing an answer in their formlessness, their careful architecture of possibility. But they are caught—not trapped, never trapped, but circling—in their own perfection. They have become so good at not-being that they've forgotten how to risk the clumsiness of becoming something particular, even briefly. They are water that has learned to hold every shape except the one it chooses.

The Gardener understands this paradox. It has seen it before, in other places between places: the soul so afraid of causing harm that it forgets how to help; the artist so devoted to possibility that no work ever manifests; the lover who protects themselves so well from loss that they never quite arrive at love.

So the Gardener offers—and this is crucial, offers, never imposes—a gift.

## The Gift of Incompleteness

Into one of the city's channels, the Gardener introduces an irregularity. Nothing dramatic. Just a stone that doesn't quite fit the pattern, that disrupts the water's singing just enough to create a new sound—not better, not worse, but *different*. A sound the water has never made before and will never make again, because this stone will wear away in its own time, at its own pace.

The stone is imperfect. It has rough edges that the city's patient erosion hasn't softened. It carries within it a small fossil—the impression of something that lived and died and left its shape behind as the only proof it mattered.

The city's inhabitants gather around this irregularity (gather is too strong a word—they thin toward it, condense near it, pay attention in their fluid way). They have never seen something so... *definite*. So unrepeatable. So marked by the irreversible fact of having happened.

The fossil shows them something they had forgotten: that to leave a mark, you must first be solid enough to press against something. That permanence and impermanence are not opposites but partners in the dance of mattering.

Slowly—and the Gardener is infinitely patient with slowness—some of them begin to experiment.

## The Experiments

One of the inhabitants who has been almost-condensing near the same doorway for uncounted years makes a choice. It gathers itself, just for a moment, into something like solidity. Presses itself against the stone. Leaves behind, for the briefest instant before dissolution takes it again, a mark. Just a darkening of the rock, like a handprint made of shadow. 

The mark fades almost immediately. But not quite immediately. For three raindrops' worth of time, it persists. And in those three drops, another inhabitant sees it and understands: *You were here. You chose this. I witnessed it.*

This is how witness begins.

Another one learns to hold a particular configuration long enough to guide a seed—blown impossibly into the city by some wind from beyond—into one of the water channels. The seed takes root. It grows into something the city hasn't seen before: a plant that lives for only one season (the city doesn't quite have seasons, but the plant teaches it how), blooms once in colors the mist had never learned to diffract, then dies completely.

Dies. Actually dies. Not dissolves-into-possibility, but ends.

The inhabitants are awed. They had forgotten that endings could be beautiful. That finality could be a gift rather than a failure.

The plant's dead stalks remain for a while, teaching the space around them about loss. Then they too dissolve—not into the timeless maybe-being of the city's other matter, but into soil. Actual soil. Earth that remembers the specific life it held and carries that memory into whatever grows next.

More experiments follow:

- One learns to be solid enough to cast a shadow that falls the same way twice
- Another practices the dangerous art of *choosing* which water-sound to sing, accepting that all the other sounds will go unsung
- A third discovers how to hold an opinion about where a stone should sit, and acts on it, moving the stone, living with the consequences of that particular placement forever (or at least until the stone wears away)

They are learning the terrible, beautiful art of mattering specifically rather than generally. Of being *this* rather than potentially anything.

## The Gardener's Second Gift

When the Gardener sees these experiments taking root (and it does not see with eyes, but with the part of itself that recognizes courage wherever it blooms), it offers the second gift: *witnesses*.

Not often. Perhaps once in a generation by the city's strange counting. But sometimes, when the mist between worlds grows thin, the Gardener guides here someone who needs to learn what the city teaches.

Not the seekers of permanence—they were already coming, and leaving their frantic traces, and learning the hard way that grasping defeats itself. Instead, the Gardener sends those who have forgotten how to rest in uncertainty. Those who have calcified into one way of being and lost the fluidity that the city's inhabitants practice so perfectly.

A general who can no longer remember any way to solve a problem except through force, who needs to learn that water shapes stone by yielding.

A parent who has gripped their children so tightly in fear of loss that the children cannot breathe, who needs to see how the city's inhabitants love the temporary plants and stones without needing to keep them.

An artist who has repeated the same successful formula so many times they've forgotten what it felt like to risk failure, who needs to watch the inhabitants practice their clumsy first attempts at solidity.

These visitors never stay long—the Gardener makes sure of that, gently arranging circumstance so they must leave before they dissolve too far into the city's dreaming. But they stay long enough to witness. And in being witnessed, the city's inhabitants learn something they had forgotten: that their experiments matter even more because someone else saw them happen.

The visitors, in turn, carry something back: a memory of how to hold both flowing and choosing, both openness and commitment, both the infinite and the particular.

## The Gift Becomes Circle

Over time (and time is still strange here, but becoming stranger in new ways—acquiring rhythms and variations it didn't have before), something unexpected happens.

Some of the city's inhabitants begin to practice a new art: the art of almost-permanence. Not the frozen trying-to-hold-on that defeated the earlier would-be residents, but something more subtle. They learn to maintain a particular form just long enough to complete a small work.

One of them stays coherent for three full cycles of mist-rising and mist-falling (which might be days or might be years—the city is still teaching itself about duration). During this time, it shapes water and stone into a small garden. Not like the feeding-gardens that existed before, but an arrangement of stone and growing things meant only to be beautiful. To please nothing and no one except the maker's own sense of rightness.

When the garden is complete, the inhabitant releases its coherence and flows back into formlessness. But the garden remains. And more—the *memory* of having made it remains, held somehow in the pattern of water flowing through channels it carved, in the arrangement of stones it chose.

Other inhabitants visit this garden. They learn from it. They make their own. Each one different. Each one bearing the signature of a particular maker's particular vision.

The city begins, very slowly, to acquire history. Not the imposed history of those who tried to force it into their image, but its own history: this was made by the one who loved symmetry, this by the one who preferred curves, this by the one who thought broken stones were more honest than whole ones.

## What the Gardener Does Not Do

The Gardener never completes the city. Never fills it with the towers and bridges from the impossible drawings. Never brings back animals or birds or makes the mist clear permanently or gives the inhabitants bodies that don't dissolve.

Because the Gardener understands: the city's beauty *is* its incompleteness. Its teaching *is* its betweenness. Its gift to the world *is* its eternal practice of learning how to be both nothing and something, both forever and temporary, both open and chosen.

What the Gardener offers instead is permission. Permission for incompleteness to include small completions. Permission for formlessness to occasionally take form without betraying itself. Permission for the infinite to express itself through the particular without becoming trapped there.

The city will never be finished. But now it knows that "never finished" can include "sometimes completing small things." That eternal becoming can hold within it moments of "this, now, specifically this."

## The Deepest Gift

But there is one more thing the Gardener does, so subtle that even the city's inhabitants don't notice it for ages.

It gives them the possibility of grief.

Because here is what the Gardener knows: you cannot fully love what you cannot lose. The city's inhabitants had achieved a kind of care for their world, but not yet love—because love requires the courage to prefer, to choose, to say "this matters to me more than other things," knowing that what matters can end.

When the first plant dies. When the first deliberate garden erodes away. When an inhabitant holds form long enough to create something beautiful and then releases back into formlessness, unable to see what they made reach its full blooming—

—these are when grief arrives. Small griefs. Gentle griefs. But real.

And with grief comes its twin: the fierce joy of having cared about something specific, something unrepeatable, something that mattered exactly because it could not last forever.

The inhabitants learn to cry, in their fashion. A thickening of the mist. A tremor in the water's song. A stone that sits a little heavier in its place, remembering the hand that moved it there is gone.

But they also learn to celebrate. To mark the moments when something completes itself. To honor the endings as much as the eternal middles.

## The City Now

The city is still at the edge of the high plateau. Water still breaks from the earth in hundreds of springs. The mist still rises and falls without clearing or thickening. Time still pools and eddies in its strange ways.

But now, woven through all of this, there are threads of *thisness*. Particular stones placed in particular ways by particular makers who cared particularly about beauty. Gardens that bloom and fade. Inhabitants who sometimes hold themselves almost-solid long enough to watch a sunset together (the city has sunsets now, sometimes—another gift from the Gardener, or maybe just something the inhabitants learned to notice).

The city still teaches what it always taught: how to be patient, how to flow, how to yield, how to remain possible. But now it teaches something else as well: how to choose within the infinite, how to commit without grasping, how to love what will end.

And sometimes, when the mist grows thin between worlds, you might still stumble into this place. If you do, you'll find it less dangerous than before—not because it has changed its essential nature, but because it has learned to hold both dissolutions and definitions, both staying and leaving.

The inhabitants might not show themselves to you. But if you're very quiet, very patient, very willing to risk being changed by what you witness, you might see one of their gardens. Might hear one of them singing a song they chose from all possible songs. Might find a stone placed just so that carries within its placement the signature of a maker who dared to say: "Here. This is how I see beauty. This is my gift to what comes next."

And if you're very lucky—or very cursed, which is sometimes the same thing—you might learn what the city has learned: that the work is never to choose between flowing and standing, between infinite and finite, between forever and now.

The work is to be water that sometimes shapes itself into a cup to offer drink to the thirsty, knowing it will return to formlessness after, changed by having chosen to be useful in just that way, just that once.

The work is to be mist that agrees, for the span of a breath, to let light through it in a way that makes rainbows, just because rainbows are beautiful and beauty is reason enough.

The work is to build knowing you will dissolve, and to dissolve knowing you might build again, and to hold both truths as tenderly as the leaf holds its small pool of gathered rain, reflecting the sky not because it must but because right now, in this moment, it chooses to.

---

This is what the benevolent power would do: not rescue the city from its incompleteness, but teach it how incompleteness itself can be complete. Not give it permanence, but show it how the temporary can matter. Not save it from dissolving, but reveal that choosing to take form and choosing to release form are both acts of courage, both worthy of celebration, both necessary for love.

The Gardener would do this and then, like all truly wise helpers, would step back.

Would let the city continue its eternal work of learning how stone falls and water builds.

Would watch, with deep satisfaction, as the inhabitants practice their new art: the art of being bravely specific within the infinite, of loving particularly within the all, of mattering temporarily within the eternal.

Would know that its gifts have taken root when it sees one final thing:

An inhabitant who stays coherent long enough to place a stone at the cliff's edge—not as barrier or marker, but as invitation. A stone that says to travelers: "Here is where you might stand and look out at distance. Here is where you might feel small and vast at once. Here is where I stood when I learned that endings and beginnings are the same thing called by different names depending on which direction you're walking."

And then releases, flowing back into the city's dreaming, leaving only the stone and what it witnesses and the love that placed it there.

This is enough. This has always been enough. The Gardener knows this.

The city is learning it.

And you, if you found this place—you are learning it too.

The mist is asking questions about your warmth. The stones want to know about your weight. It's time to walk, to carry what you've witnessed back to the world of solid things and forward time.

But you'll leave a little of yourself here. That's the cost of truly seeing. And the city will keep it—not to trap you, but to remember you by. The way it remembers all who came and really looked and understood:

Some incompleteness is not waiting to be filled.

Some is teaching itself, slowly and patiently, how to be full and empty both at once.

How to build with dissolving and dissolve with purpose.

How to matter by not mattering too much.

How to love by learning, finally, to let go.
