# The Attention of the Wise

The Gardener had been watching for seventeen thousand years before deciding to intervene—which, in the arithmetic of benevolent powers, was roughly equivalent to a sharp intake of breath.

She came as a clarification of light, the way dawn doesn't arrive so much as *remember* it has an appointment with geometry. No fanfare. No trumpet-blast of angelic recursion. Just a subtle shift in the quality of amber dripping from the memory-wounds between boughs, as if the resin had suddenly recalled what it meant to be merely beautiful instead of epistemologically devastating.

The Gardener did not have a form, exactly. She had *implications*. Where she focused her attention, the forest's relentless forgetting-and-remembering stuttered, caught between verses. The bear-draft paused mid-shamble, its hollow twigs settling into a posture that almost resembled restfulness instead of eternal apology. The toad-differential's throat-sac weather calmed to a gentle overcast. Even the chain-creature at the clearing's heart stopped adding links for a moment, its hollow face-socket tilting as if listening to a frequency it had long ago decided was extinct.

She walked—if *walked* is the word for moving through space by gently convincing it that she had always already been arriving—to the center of the clearing that was not a clearing. The ground-eye blinked. Once. Slowly. Then focused its vast pupil on something infinitely far away, granting them privacy.

The chain-creature regarded her with its absence of eyes.

"Hello, old grief," the Gardener said, and her voice was the exact texture of the last day of summer before anyone has noticed it's ending.

The creature's socket dilated slightly. Recognition, perhaps. Or the memory of what recognition used to feel like before it became a theoretical exercise.

"You've been at this a long time," she continued, settling into a crouch that suggested she had roots extending down through geological strata. "Longer than the mountains. Longer than the concept of *longer*. You've built something extraordinary here—a monument to the impossibility of being remembered correctly. A cathedral to impermanence. A forest that is really a single thought thinking itself into increasingly baroque knots."

The creature said nothing, because it had finished with saying generations before the first proto-mammal learned to vocalize fear.

"But I wonder," the Gardener mused, "whether you remember why you started."

Around them, the forest leaned closer without moving. Every leaf, every strand of mycelia, every pool of mirror-bile oriented toward this conversation like students who'd been failing the same class for eons and suddenly suspected the final exam was about to be announced.

The Gardener reached into the space between her ribs—where benevolent powers keep their most dangerous tools—and withdrew a seed.

It was the size of a child's tooth and the color of the word *beginning* before it learned to be afraid of endings. It pulsed with a light that didn't illuminate anything but somehow made everything more visible to itself.

"This," she said, "is what you used to be. Before you decided that being a forest meant forgetting how to be a seed. Before you concluded that the only way to escape loneliness was to become so large and so fragmented that you could pretend you were a crowd."

The chain-creature's hollow face trembled. Somewhere in the Vale, three hundred miles of interconnected root system shuddered with something that might have been recognition or might have been terror—the forest had long since forgotten how to distinguish between the two.

"I'm not here to fix you," the Gardener said gently. "You're not broken. You're just... exhausted. You've been holding your breath since the Devonian, waiting for permission to exhale. You've been conducting this endless experiment in self-dissolution because somewhere, in the heartwood of your first growth ring, you got the idea that being singular was the same as being alone."

She held up the seed, and it caught the sideways light of the ember aurora.

"What if I told you that you could keep all of this—" she gestured at the impossible architecture of living lignum, the bear-drafts and toad-differentials, the pools that wept liquid memory, the vertical seasons and the doors made of absent sounds "—and also remember how to be simple?"

The forest held its breath. All of it. Every pneumatophore and stoma, every fungal exchange and cambial pulse. The silence was so complete it created a small gravitational anomaly that bent passing starlight into question marks.

"What if," the Gardener continued, "instead of choosing between being Everything and being Nothing, you just... rested? What if, for one season—let's say a mere thousand years—you let yourself be ordinary? Let the bear-drafts become just bears. Real ones. Warm-blooded and stubborn and utterly convinced of their own bear-ness. Let the trees be trees, each one separate, each one allowed to die without taking the whole forest with it into recursive grief."

She stood, holding the seed between her palms like a prayer or a suggestion.

"What if you stopped trying to be a solution to loneliness and just admitted you were lonely?"

The chain-creature's face-socket opened wider, and for the first time in geological ages, something like sound emerged—not words, but the shape words would take if they'd been carved from wind and regret. It was the forest finally saying what it had been trying to say since its first cell divided and immediately panicked about what that division meant for its sense of self:

*I'm scared that if I stop trying to remember everything, I'll discover I was never worth remembering at all.*

The Gardener smiled, and for a moment the entire Vale brightened, as if someone had opened a window in a room that had forgotten rooms were supposed to have windows.

"Oh, sweet thing," she said. "Memory isn't a test you can pass or fail. It's not a performance. You don't have to earn the right to have existed."

She pressed the seed into the space where the creature's heart would be if it still believed in the division between self and other, inside and outside, remembered and forgotten.

"Here. I'm not going to make you plant it. That's not how this works—I don't fix things by overriding will. But I'm going to leave it with you. And whenever you're ready—next century, next era, next time the mountains finish forgetting how to cast shadows—you can choose. You can choose to be a seed again. To start over. To grow into something that doesn't have to contain everything in order to be enough."

The seed sank into the creature's chest like a stone into water, except the ripples spread outward in temporal rather than spatial dimensions. Suddenly, several things happened at once:

The bear-draft, three hundred meters away, solidified. Its hollow twigs filled with actual marrow. Its russet leaves became actual eyes, which blinked in genuine confusion before the bear shook its head, scratched behind its ear with a paw that was now blessedly, magnificently solid, and lumbered off in search of something bears actually want—which is mostly berries and not having to be a philosophical thought experiment.

The toad-differential's throat-sac collapsed inward, and when it re-inflated, it released an actual croak. The beetle-theater inside its mouth evaporated like morning dew, replaced by a perfectly normal toad tongue that shot out and captured a perfectly normal fly that had just that moment decided to exist because the job opening for "thing that gets eaten by toads" had unexpectedly appeared on the Vale's job board.

Across the forest, trees began—tentatively, like children testing ice—to differentiate. This oak decided to be just an oak. That birch chose to be only birch. They maintained their connections, their mycelial networks, their whispered communications—but they stopped trying to be each other. They stopped apologizing for having edges.

The Gardener watched the transformations ripple outward like sanity returning to a fever dream.

"You don't have to decide now," she told the chain-creature, which was already beginning to look less like an exclamation mark and more like a very old tree that had simply grown in a strange direction. "The seed will wait. It's good at waiting—it learned from you. But unlike you, it remembers that waiting is supposed to end eventually. That's what makes it waiting instead of just stopping."

She turned to leave, then paused.

"One more thing. Those visitors you get—the ones who stumble into your Vale looking for meaning or annihilation or whatever humans seek when the ordinary world stops feeling adequately strange?"

The creature-that-was-becoming-a-tree rustled its branches in acknowledgment.

"Maybe stop eating their shadows. Just a thought. You could try... I don't know. Offering them tea. Showing them the nice views. Letting them leave with all their existential boundaries intact. Radical concept, I know."

The forest emanated something that might have been embarrassment if embarrassment could be dispersed across several cubic miles of living tissue.

The Gardener laughed—a sound like bells made of bread, nourishing and bright.

"I'll check back in a millennium or two. Try to get some sleep. Real sleep, not that thing you do where you pretend to be dormant while actually recalculating the etymology of every word for 'rest' in languages that haven't evolved yet."

She began to fade, her attention turning toward other gardens in other corners of the cosmos that needed tending.

But just before she vanished entirely, she left one final gift:

A small sign, carved from honest pine, planted at the edge of the Vale where the impossible mountains yielded to ordinary foothills. It read:

**WELCOME TO THE FOREST**  
*We're practicing being normal*  
*Please excuse our reconstruction*  
*Bears are real now*  
*Please do not feed them your epistemological crises*  
*They're on a strict diet of berries and salmon*

And beneath that, in smaller letters:

*P.S. If you see a very old tree at the center holding a seed, be patient with it. It's learning how to begin again. We all are.*

---

Three thousand years later, a botanist stumbled into the Vale on accident, following a species of moth that shouldn't exist at this elevation.

She found a forest. Just a forest. Strange, yes—the trees grew in unusual spirals, and the bears seemed oddly philosophical in their regard—but ultimately, recognizably, beautifully normal.

At the center of a small clearing, she found an ancient tree, its bark deeply furrowed with age. At its base, a seedling was just beginning to emerge from the soil—tiny, green, and utterly convinced of its right to exist without justification.

She smiled, made some notes in her journal, and continued on her way.

Behind her, the old tree creaked in the wind, and if you listened very carefully, you might have heard it whisper:

*Thank you.*

The forest had finally learned the difference between remembering everything and cherishing something.

It had taken seventeen thousand years and the attention of a power wise enough to know that the greatest gift isn't answers but permission to stop asking the question that's been destroying you.

The bears wandered.  
The birds sang.  
The trees grew at their own pace, in their own directions.  

And the Vale of Arboreal Mnemoschism slowly, gratefully, began to forget that it had ever been anything other than a place where living things came to live, and not to solve the problem of living.

Which is to say: it healed.

Which is to say: it became a forest.

Which was, all along, enough.
