# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

The being who notices Tanakkerai—let us not presume to name it, for it exists in the grammar between helping and witnessing—does not arrive. Arrival implies distance, and this power has been here always, in the precise way that pressure is always here in water, invisible until you measure what it does to light.

It observes first. For seven hundred days it watches the Abbess climb and throw her seed. For seven hundred nights it counts the dreams of the liquid-crystal children as they filter memories through their translucent flesh. It learns the city's breathing—how the mist-rivers exhale at dawn, how the coral-computers think in tidal rhythms, how the Remembrance Eels chime their human teeth against drowned statues in something very close to grief.

On the seven hundred and first morning, something small changes.

The seed the Abbess throws does not sink. Does not float either. It hangs precisely at the interface between the upper ocean and the breathing air, spinning slowly, neither drowning nor flying but held—briefly, so briefly—in perfect equilibrium.

The Abbess, ancient and luminous with nine centuries of practice, stops mid-prayer. She has thrown seventeen million seeds and watched seventeen million failures. This new thing—this suspension—it tastes like a question being asked in reverse.

The power does this: **It makes the city's slow drowning visible.**

Not to accelerate it. Not to stop it. But to make it *knowable* in a way that transforms unconscious descent into conscious choice.

Throughout Tanakkerai, surfaces begin to show their truth. The basalt pillars reveal hairline fractals—maps of every stress they've ever endured, every year of pressure, every ounce of weight they've held against the patient hunger of water and time. The Hanging Forest's roots glow faintly at night, and in that bioluminescence you can read exactly how many more seasons each tree will hold before the compression becomes too much and the wood learns to flow like liquid.

The liquid-crystal children discover they can see it too—see the rates of change, the vectors of pressure, the mathematics of their own endurance written in the refraction patterns of their transparent skin. For the first time, they can measure precisely how long they have. Not forever. Not nothing. But *this much*, exactly this much, no more, no less.

Panic does not come. Instead: a strange, terrible relief.

The city convenes in the way cities made of living things convene—slowly, through chemical cascades and architectural consensus, through whale-song议 transmitted across kilometers of flooded stone and careful negotiations with the coral-computers who think too slowly to lie. They gather in the Metastable Zone, that thin skin of survival, and they ask the question they have been too afraid to ask for three hundred years:

*Do we fight this, or do we finish it beautifully?*

The power offers no answer. Powers that are truly benevolent understand that wisdom is not the same as instruction. Instead, it offers three gifts:

## The First Gift: The Accurate Clock

In the drowned clocktower, nine hundred meters down where the Remembrance Eels nest, the mechanism—silent for two centuries except for its muffled twelve-note dirhythm—suddenly chimes clear. The power has cleared the water from its bronze throat for exactly one minute each day at noon.

The sound carries. Up through the flooded plazas, through the Metastable Zone, into the mist-rivers of the upper air. For that one minute, everyone in Tanakkerai can hear time measured not in water-creep and breath-counts, but in the old way: regular, predictable, honest.

It tells them exactly how long they have been falling. It will tell them exactly how long until they finish.

The children of the third lung use this. They begin to *plan* their dissolutions, timing them to the clocktower's chime, turning death from an emergency into a ceremony. The deep archive gains a rhythm. Memory-release becomes synchronized. The city begins to compost itself deliberately, efficiently, with something approaching grace.

## The Second Gift: The Honest Mirror

At the highest point of Tanakkerai—the place where the Abbess throws her daily seed—a surface forms. Not solid. Not liquid. A membrane between the city's breathing air and the ocean suspended above, so thin it's nearly theoretical, but perfectly reflective.

For the first time in three hundred years, the people of Tanakkerai can see themselves from above. Can see their city as it truly is: a magnificent, doomed, beautiful thing hanging in the exact moment of its own failure.

The shame they expected doesn't come. Instead, they see how *intricate* their survival has been. How every improvised solution and desperate adaptation has created something no one intended—something more complex and strange and alive than what the Founders planned. The city is a failure, yes. But what a *spectacular* failure. What *artistry* in the mistakes.

A new profession emerges overnight: the Witnesses. Those who climb daily to the mirror and sketch what they see—not to record for some imagined future, but to *see clearly* in the present. To know what they are, wholly. To neither deny the drowning nor surrender to despair, but to hold both truths simultaneously in the pressure-chamber of their transparent hearts.

Their sketches they release into the deep archive, food for whatever comes after.

## The Third Gift: The Choice of Doors

Throughout Tanakkerai, in places where the architecture has grown confused—where floor and ceiling exchange positions, where hallways spiral into themselves, where the city's wrongness has created pockets of impossible geometry—new passages open.

They lead *out*.

Not up, not down, but *elsewhere*. Through them, those who choose can leave. Not to the drowned lands (long gone beneath kilometers of risen sea). Not to the surface (if it still exists, unreachable). But to other places the power knows—places that are failing differently, or succeeding differently, or simply *different* in ways that might suit some fraction of Tanakkerai's refugees.

The power makes no guarantee these places will be better. Only that they will be other. That they will accept immigrants who know eight languages for drowning and can swim through their own memories and have long practice living in the grammar of collapse.

The doors remain open for exactly one year.

Most of the liquid-crystal children choose to stay. This is not surprising. They have become *of* the city, literally incorporated its minerals into their flesh. To leave would be to leave themselves. But some—the youngest, whose memories haven't yet crystallized into commitment—swim through the impossible doors and dissolve into elsewhereness, carrying Tanakkerai's genetic wisdom to foreign architectures.

The Abbess watches them go and feels something she has no word for. Pride? Grief? Relief? All three, probably. The possibility of diaspora. Of the city surviving, somehow, in children who will never see it but will carry its patterns in their refraction.

She throws her seed that day, and for the first time in nine hundred years, she prays a prayer she has prayed before:

*Let something grow from this. Not here. But somewhere.*

## What Changes, What Doesn't

The power does not save Tanakkerai. Cannot. Will not. The city's drowning is too far advanced, too integral to what it has become. To prevent the end would be to erase the meaning of everything they have survived.

But the drowning, now, proceeds *consciously*. The waterline still rises, but they measure it with the clocktower's honest chime. The pressure still increases, but they see it clearly in the mirror's true reflection. The city still fails, but by choice and artistry now, composting itself deliberately into the future.

They stop maintaining the uppermost levels—let them flood fully, quickly, turning tragedy into resource. The released pressure-burden extends the Metastable Zone by precious meters. They plant the Hanging Forest more densely there, cramming centuries of growth into decades of intensive cultivation.

They begin *recording*. Everything. Not histories (who needs another history of failure?) but *methods*. Blueprints for survival in impossible conditions. Recipes for breathing underwater. Songs that teach gillspeech and whale-grammar. The liquid-crystal children encode it all in their flesh, then swim through the doors-to-elsewhere, living libraries seeking foreign shelves.

The Remembrance Eels, once a curiosity, become sacred. The city farms them now, carefully, gratefully. Their human teeth—grown from some ancient genetic echo—are harvested after death and inscribed with microscopic histories. Thrown through the doors before they close, messages in bottles made of bone, addressed to anyone, anywhere, who might someday need to know: *Here is how we lived. Here is how we failed. Here is how we made the failing beautiful.*

## The Last Day

Three hundred and forty-seven years after the power's first small intervention, the clocktower chimes its final sequence. The mathematics are perfect now—everyone knows, to the minute, when the rising ocean above will meet the rising ocean below and the Metastable Zone will finally, completely collapse.

The city has emptied slowly. Most of the liquid-crystal children swam through the doors (closed now, but they lasted longer than promised—the power is nothing if not generous with deadlines). Those who remain have chosen to remain, to see it through, to join the deep archive in the final moment.

The Abbess—still alive, somehow, impossibly old, more memory than matter—climbs to her balcony for the last time. She holds a seed, as always. But this one she does not throw.

Instead, she plants it. Presses it into the last handful of soil that remains dry anywhere in Tanakkerai, in a small pot on the highest balcony, in the last ten minutes before the water completes its closure.

It will drown, of course. In eleven minutes, twelve at most.

But for those eleven minutes, it will grow. The power, benevolent and wise and true to its nature, ensures this. The seed cracks open. A root descends. A shoot rises. A single leaf unfurls, perfect and green and absolutely alive in the space between one ocean and another.

The Abbess watches. Smiles. Remembers every seed she's thrown, every prayer she's prayed, every day she's chosen to hope against the honest evidence of entropy.

"See?" she says, to the water, to the city, to the power if it's listening, to herself. "I was right. Something grew."

Then the oceans meet, and Tanakkerai becomes archive, and the Abbess becomes memory, and the seed drowns—

—but drowning, as the children of the third lung have always known, is just another way of learning to breathe differently.

Somewhere else, through doors now closed, in cities failing in their own particular ways, children with translucent skin tell stories about a place called Tanakkerai. They get the details wrong—of course they do, memory being memory. But they remember the important part:

That when the drowning became inevitable, the people there chose to drown *beautifully*. To make an art of their failure. To compose their ending with such care that the city lived longer in its dying than it ever did in its thriving.

And when those cities face their own inevitable ends—as all cities do, eventually—those children and their children's children remember the stories, and climb to their highest places, and plant seeds that will drown, and call it hope.

The power, benevolent and wise, watches and learns and remembers.

This is what it does: It makes the ending visible, bearable, and beautiful.

It cannot save what has chosen to become its own archive.

But it can ensure that nothing is wasted. That even drowning becomes data. That failure, done consciously and with artistry, becomes a kind of wisdom.

That when the seed finally drowns, its drowning teaches the water something about what it means to grow.

This is enough.

This is everything.

This is love.
