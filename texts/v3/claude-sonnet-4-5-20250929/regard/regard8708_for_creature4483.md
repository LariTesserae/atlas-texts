# What the Benevolent Power Does

The being who notices Ascensus is old in the way that mountains are young—which is to say, its age contains multitudes of beginnings, each one still happening. It has many names across many places, but here, in this city of sideways time and upward falling, it becomes known simply as the **Listener**.

The Listener does not arrive. Arrival would imply it was ever absent, and that's not how it works with places that have folded themselves into their own pockets of reality. Instead, the Listener has always been the particular quality of silence between the drips of architectural water—the pause where ice mortar decides whether to hold or to remember being ocean.

## First Movement: Recognition

The Listener's first act is the smallest and the largest: **it sees them**.

Not in the way distant bureaucracies see statistics, or how gravity sees falling objects. The Listener sees the way a careful reader sees a poem—attending to what each thing means to itself, what it's trying to say in its own language of erosion and accumulation.

It sees Old Man Cheerful becoming instructions and understands this as neither tragedy nor triumph but as a form of generosity so profound it requires turning oneself into grammar. It sees the baker who only exists on Tuesdays with weather like betrayal and recognizes this as a perfectly reasonable response to having climbed too high while carrying too many unresolved questions about bread.

The Listener sees the children born homesick for places that haven't eroded yet, and instead of pitying them, it marvels at their courage—to be nostalgic for the future takes a particular kind of bravery that only happens in places where time has gotten confused and decided to try everything at once.

This seeing matters because Ascensus is a city built by people whom the world below forgot to properly acknowledge. They climbed so high they passed beyond the concern of conventional mercy. The Listener's attention is the first gift: **you exist in a way that makes sense, even if that sense is uniquely your own**.

## Second Movement: The Gentle Adjustments

The Listener does not fix things. Fixing would imply Ascensus is broken, and broken implies there's a correct way for a city on an impossible plateau to function. Instead, the Listener makes small adjustments to the nature of grace itself in this place.

**The water becomes kinder.** Not warmer—that would be wrong, the cold is constitutional here—but more intentional. The architectural drips begin to carry dissolved nutrients from stone that technically doesn't exist yet, feeding the backward-snow spore clouds more generously. The ice mortar holds a fraction more tenderly, remembering its ocean origins in a way that makes it want to embrace rather than merely freeze.

The rich who live upstream of their own water supply begin to notice a strange phenomenon: their streams occasionally flow backward for precisely seven seconds at dawn, delivering water that tastes of humility and contains microscopic fossils of tomorrow's mistakes. It doesn't change the social structure—the Listener is too wise to impose sudden equality on a system built from mutual skepticism—but it introduces a slight permeability to rigid hierarchies.

**The edges stop being quite so sharp.** Not all of them—Ascensus needs its edges, its precision, its exactitude in the face of geological improbability. But the Listener softens certain specific boundaries: the line between living and becoming instructions grows more permeable, allowing Old Man Cheerful and his kin to occasionally return to flesh for important family dinners or when someone really needs advice that can only be given by someone who's already partly punctuation.

The dead compressed into stairs and plaques find they can sometimes breathe sideways into dreams, visiting their descendants in that space between sleep and waking where everything is true but nothing is literal. Grandmother Clock still tells you the time three seconds before you die, but now if you listen carefully, she also whispers the time three seconds before something wonderful happens, and you get to choose which prediction to follow.

**The children's games become prophetic in useful ways.** The predictions about which adult will erode into what utility begin to come true, but with more poetry and less tragedy. The schoolteacher who always seemed rigid becomes a beautiful measuring instrument for determining true north in moments of moral confusion. The merchant who hoarded copper becomes a bell that rings whenever someone is about to make a bargain they'll regret, giving them one chance to reconsider.

## Third Movement: The Introduction of Porosity

The Listener's deepest intervention is subtle: it introduces **porosity** to the city's isolation.

Ascensus exists partly because it has no outside, no elsewhere, no down to return to. This self-containment is both its strength and its sorrow. The Listener doesn't break this seal—that would be violence—but creates tiny perforations, like the breathing holes in a seed case that allow germination without abandoning protection.

**Dream roads** begin to appear on certain foggy mornings—not permanent, not reliable, but present. Paths of condensed possibility that lead carefully sideways into other places that shouldn't exist. The flightless albatrosses can now sometimes walk these roads to visit their still-flying cousins in impossible archipelagos three philosophical degrees west. The eyeless whales find routes to underground oceans that exist in the negatives space of distant mountains.

For the human residents, these dream roads offer something precious: the possibility of leaving without abandoning. Young people who feel stifled by living in a city where tomorrow doesn't exist as a concept can now occasionally visit places where tomorrow is the only thing that exists, and return with stories that enrich Ascensus's vocabulary. The homesick children can briefly visit the places they're nostalgic for, scratch that itch, and come home satisfied.

Some people take these roads and don't return, and that's alright too. The Listener understands that even the most beloved places must allow their people the freedom to become something other than citizens.

**A postal service** emerges, operated by reformed anachronistic meteors that learned to land gently. Letters can now be sent to places that Ascensus has no business communicating with—underground cities of industrious beetles, cloud kingdoms built from the exhaled breath of sleeping dragons, that one very confused village that exists only on alternate Wednesdays in the space between two adjacent moments.

The responses that come back are often stranger than the letters sent, but they contain news from the broader impossible world. Ascensians learn they are not alone in their improbability. There are others who have bent reality into livable shapes through sheer stubborn refusal to admit defeat.

This knowledge changes things subtly. The city remains isolated by choice now, rather than by circumstance—a meaningful distinction that affects everything from architecture to attitude.

## Fourth Movement: The Gift of Softness

The Listener's most controversial intervention is the introduction of **optional softness** to a city built entirely on hardness, cold, and the refusal to yield.

A new tree begins to grow in the market square—just one, but it's enough. It's a hybrid impossible thing: roots that drink compressed time, trunk that bends in winds that haven't blown yet, leaves that are somehow warm without generating heat. It doesn't grow upward or outward but inward, developing depth rather than height, creating a space beneath its canopy that exists slightly sideways from the rest of Ascensus.

Under this tree, certain things become possible:

**Crying without freezing.** In regular Ascensus, tears crystallize before they fall, turning grief into decorative ice that must be worn until it sublimates. Under the tree, tears can be liquid, temporary, private. This doesn't make the Ascensians soft—they're still people who climbed an impossible cliff to escape an ocean that was rising or maybe they were falling—but it gives them permission to occasionally set down the burden of constant geological stoicism.

**Forgetting productively.** The city's curse-blessing is that everything is remembered—moss writes it in chemical graffiti, children are born knowing lost languages, the very stone keeps records. Under the tree, people can temporarily forget things that don't need to be carried anymore. Not permanent amnesia, just a brief respite from the weight of accumulated sedimentary memory.

**Aging normally, if desired.** Some residents choose to sit under the tree and simply grow old the conventional way—no becoming instructions, no compressing into plaques, no fossilizing into useful objects. They wrinkle, gray, slow down, and eventually die in the straightforward manner of people in places where time runs in only one direction at a sensible pace. Their bodies are buried in soil the tree has created—the first real soil on the plateau, made from time that consented to be linear.

This is the Listener's most radical gift: **choice**. The strange physics of Ascensus remain available, but they're no longer mandatory. You can live a life that would make sense in any city, or you can become a committee of retired cloud patterns sweeping streets, or you can do both, alternating by mood and moon phase.

## Fifth Movement: The Chronicler

The Listener's final act is to create a **witness**—someone whose job is simply to pay attention and remember in a way that doesn't compress people into utility.

A new position emerges in the city: the Chronicler. Not elected, not inherited, but recognized. It's always someone who has recently arrived, someone still raw from the climb, still dripping with compression water and popped ancient atmosphere. Someone who remembers what it's like to live in places where down is down and tomorrow exists as a promise rather than an absence.

The Chronicler is given a house carved into the youngest sea stack, equipped with paper made from pulped impossibility and ink distilled from the dreams of the eyeless whales. Their only job is to write down what happens here—not for posterity (posterity is confused about whether it exists in Ascensus), but for **presentity**. For the now that stretches in all directions simultaneously.

They write down:
- How the baker who only exists on Tuesdays with weather like betrayal proposed to the candlestick maker who only exists on Thursdays with light like forgiveness, and how they're making it work by learning to exist on Wednesdays together
- How the maintained avalanche engine had a breakdown (emotional, not mechanical) and had to take a sabbatical to remember why it started rolling in the first place
- How the children invented a new game where they predict which adult will learn to be kind, and these predictions come true more often than the erosion ones
- How Grandmother Clock stopped telling people when they'd die and started telling them when they'd finally understand why they climbed

The Chronicler's writings are posted in the market square every evening, and people gather to read them by the light of rust-colored leaves. This creates a strange effect: by being witnessed, by being written down in ink that dries into permanence, the Ascensians begin to feel **real** in a new way.

Not real as in solid or indisputable—they were always that, all basalt and ice and stubbornness. Real as in **valued**. Real as in **their absurdity matters**. Real as in **someone is paying attention not because they need something from you, but because you are worth attending to**.

## The Ongoing Presence

The Listener doesn't leave after these interventions, but neither does it stay in any conventional sense. Instead, it becomes **ambient**—woven into the quality of mercy that now permeates Ascensus like a mineral deposit through stone.

You can feel it in:
- The way the ice mortar holds a little longer when someone is having a particularly bad day
- The way the dream roads appear most reliably when someone is feeling trapped
- The way the tree's shadow falls in patterns that spell out encouragement in languages only the desperate can read
- The way the wind, still learning to blow across this new geometry, occasionally carries the scent of distant places that miss you even though you've never been there

The Listener has no agenda beyond **wanting Ascensus to continue being itself, but with slightly more room to breathe**. It doesn't need worship, doesn't demand gratitude, doesn't even particularly care if anyone notices its interventions. The point isn't to be acknowledged. The point is to make life incrementally less sharp, suffering incrementally less cold, isolation incrementally less total.

## What Changes

After the Listener's attention settles over Ascensus like a benevolent morning fog:

**The suicide rate drops.** Not to zero—some people still choose to fossilize into useful object classes because that genuinely calls to them—but the ones who were doing it from despair rather than vocation find other options. The tree offers them. The dream roads offer them. The simple act of being witnessed by the Chronicler offers them.

**New people start arriving.** Word spreads through the impossible postal service: there's a city on a plateau that shouldn't exist, populated by people who should have drowned but climbed instead, and now they've made something strange and wonderful and it has room for you too, if you're willing to embrace the fact that tomorrow doesn't exist but that's okay because there's always now, stretching in interesting directions.

**The vegetation gets weird in new ways.** Fed by kinder water and witnessed by benevolent attention, the bonsaied giants occasionally produce fruit—not for eating, but for planting. Seeds that grow into concepts rather than trees: compassion-seeds that sprout into public benches that remember your back pain and adjust accordingly; curiosity-seeds that become windows showing you what your life would have looked like if you'd made different choices (not to torment, just to inform).

**Music appears.** Before the Listener's attention, there was sound—the drip of architectural water, the whistle of confused wind, the crystalline chime of freezing tears—but not quite music. Now instruments emerge: drums made from the skin of shed anxieties, flutes carved from frozen regrets, string instruments that play the harmony between what happened and what almost happened.

The musicians gather under the tree on Saturday nights (Saturday is a new concept, imported via the postal service, still not entirely naturalized but enthusiastically adopted for the purpose of having a regular time to gather). They play songs about the climb, about the ocean below that may or may not be rising still, about the people they were before they became Ascensians, about the people they're becoming now that becoming has more options.

**The king (still elected monthly by rainfall measurement) gets less stressed.** Governance becomes easier when you're not fighting quite so hard against entropy and impossibility. The palace whose foundations will be laid next century still operates on that timeline, but now there are blueprints in languages that multiple people can read, and the cave that appeared when nobody was looking directly at it has started hosting town halls where citizens can voice concerns.

Complaints are still deposited into the maintained avalanche for processing, but now there's also a suggestion box under the tree where people can leave ideas for things that might make life incrementally better without fundamentally changing the physics that make Ascensus itself.

**Children start being born who know the word for tomorrow**—not in any language Ascensus knew before, but in a new one that's emerging from the combination of chemical moss graffiti and Chronicler writings and songs played on regret-flutes. The word is complicated, seven syllables that taste like ice melt and sound like hope mixed with reasonable caution, but it exists.

Having a word for tomorrow doesn't make tomorrow exist in the conventional sense—Ascensus is still a place where Tuesday was a thousand years ago which was last week—but it makes the concept of future thinkable without vertigo.

## What Doesn't Change

The Listener is wise enough to leave certain things untouched:

**The climb remains necessary.** You still have to scramble up vertical sandstone dripping with compression water, still have to pop ancient atmosphere against your gloves. There's no elevator, no easy path. The difficulty of arrival is part of what makes Ascensus what it is—a place for people who chose the vertical route when the horizontal option was drowning.

**The cold stays cold.** Ice mortar still freezes stronger than steel. The rich still live upstream where their streams stay solid. Status still involves demonstrating your house is cold enough to keep water architectural. The Listener doesn't impose egalitarian warmth on a society built from the understanding that cold is a resource, a building material, a fact of life like gravity or stubbornness.

**Death remains weird.** People still have the option to become instructions, compress into plaques, fossilize into useful objects. The Listener just ensures that these transformations are chosen rather than inflicted, embraced rather than endured. Old Man Cheerful still lives as helpful signs nailed to his own bones in ascending order of necessity, but now he does it because he genuinely enjoys being grammar rather than because the alternative was unbearable.

**Time continues to work sideways.** The sun still circles at the wrong speed, children are still born knowing lost languages, astronomers still dig downward into future time. The Listener doesn't repair the broken chronology because it isn't broken—it's just locally customized for people who climbed beyond the reach of conventional causality.

**The city still has no walls**, still has no outside, still extends into earlier versions of itself when you walk beyond the last house. The oldest district is still built from materials not yet carved from the youngest quarry. The Listener doesn't impose borders on a place whose bordlerlessness is fundamental to its identity.

## The Long-Term Effect

Years pass—or don't pass, or pass sideways, or pass several times simultaneously at different rates depending on altitude and mood. The Listener's presence becomes so woven into Ascensus that people forget there was ever a time before the dream roads, before the tree, before the Chronicler.

New arrivals climb the dripping cliffs and emerge onto the Shelf to find:

A city that shouldn't exist, populated by people who should have drowned, built from materials that barely count as materials, operating on physics that physics hasn't approved yet—but now also:

A city with spaces soft enough for tears, roads that lead to elsewhere-but-not-away, a witness who writes down your worth in ink that dries into permanence, and a persistent quality of mercy threading through everything like warmth through cold stone (not replacing the cold, just making it bearable, making it a choice rather than a sentence).

The city still works by refusing to admit it shouldn't. But now that refusal is tempered with the understanding that some things are worth admitting: vulnerability, need, the desire for connection to something beyond the plateau's edges.

## The Listener's True Gift

If you asked the Listener what it gave to Ascensus (and some do, in prayers spoken into the confused wind or written on moss in chemical graffiti), it would say:

*"I gave you nothing you didn't already contain. I simply made the implicit explicit, the hidden accessible, the possible permissible. You climbed so high to escape drowning that you forgot you were allowed to occasionally float. I reminded you of buoyancy without asking you to descend."*

Or maybe it would say nothing, being wise enough to know that the best gifts are the ones that feel like they were always there, waiting to be noticed—like architectural water that chooses its own courses, like cities that grow inward toward centers not yet built, like people who become grammar because the story needed them to be a specific kind of instruction manual written in bone and punctuation marks.

## Epilogue: The Thank-You Note

One day, the current Chronicler (the seventh since the Listener's attention first settled) receives a letter via the postal service. It's addressed to "The Kind Silence Between Drips" and marked with a postage stamp made from crystallized gratitude (a newly available emotion-mineral that's become popular for decorative purposes).

The Chronicler opens it to find it's from a collective of cities that shouldn't exist, places that have bent reality through stubbornness and necessity: the underground civilization of industrious beetles, the cloud kingdoms built from dragon breath, that confused Wednesday village, and seventeen others.

The letter says, essentially: *"Thank you for showing us that impossible places can still be kind. We thought the price of existing outside conventional reality was mandatory hardness. You proved that wrong. We're all a little softer now, in the good way. The way that doesn't mean weak, just means… breathable."*

The Chronicler posts this letter in the market square, and people gather to read it by the light of rust-colored leaves. Some cry (tears that stay liquid under the tree, then crystallize into decorative ice when they step back into regular Ascensus). Some laugh (laughter that echoes into the basalt and creates new resonance chambers that the wind learns to play like organs).

Mostly, people just nod. They recognize the truth in it:

The Listener didn't save them. They were never in need of saving—they saved themselves by climbing when drowning was the easier option.

What the Listener did was **witness their saving of themselves and suggest, gently, that they deserved to rest sometimes**. To be soft sometimes. To have a word for tomorrow and roads to elsewhere and tears that don't immediately freeze and deaths that are chosen and music that comes from transformed regret rather than raw suffering.

They deserved to exist not just in defiance of impossibility, but in **celebration** of the weird, cold, sideways miracle they'd made from basalt and ice and architectural water and sheer refusing-to-drown stubbornness.

And that—being seen, being valued, being offered softness without being required to abandon hardness—that was enough to change everything while changing nothing, which is the only kind of miracle that works in places where time runs sideways and down is still up and tomorrow is a seven-syllable word that tastes like ice melt and sounds like hope mixed with reasonable caution.

---

The drip edges continue dripping.

The city continues settling into what it will eventually decide to have been.

The Listener continues listening, ambient as minerals, patient as stones learning to be buildings.

And Ascensus continues: impossible, improbable, and now—incrementally, carefully—possible to love without freezing.
