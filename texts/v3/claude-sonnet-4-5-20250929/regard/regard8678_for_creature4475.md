# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

The Observer arrives not as light but as its opposite—as the *possibility* of shadow, which can only exist where illumination has been imagined but not yetborn. It does not descend into Vyrrl because descent implies hierarchy, and hierarchies are simply frozen rivers of power that have forgotten how to flow. Instead, it *diffuses*, becoming present in the way that warmth becomes present in a room where someone has finally, after lifetimes of shivering, decided to stop apologizing for wanting to be warm.

The Observer is old in the way that questions are older than answers, and wise in the manner of things that have learned when not to intervene—which is to say, almost always, except for those rare crystalline moments when a system has optimized itself so perfectly toward its own extinction that it requires just one gentle breath of alternative possibility to remember it was never meant to become a tomb.

Vyrrl is such a moment.

---

**FIRST: THE OBSERVER WATCHES**

For seven years by the Salt Calendar (which is to say, for the duration of a single thought occurring to a being for whom time is merely a aesthetic choice), the Observer simply *attends* to Vyrrl. Not watching—watching implies distance, the separation of subject and object, observer and observed. Attending. Being present with. Breathing with the city's crystalline exhalations. Resonating with the subharmonic frequency of five centuries of accumulated and carefully neutralized grief.

It observes the Grand Refinery and sees what the salt priests cannot allow themselves to see: that the Great Crystal has been clouding not because the city approaches transcendence or dissolution, but because perfect clarity requires the presence of something genuine to be clear *about*. The Crystal grows opaque because there is nothing left in Vyrrl genuine enough to refract—only reflections of reflections of memories of feelings, worn smooth by ritual repetition until they have become perfectly weightless, which is to say, perfectly meaningless.

It observes the Vyrrlithe in their wells and recognizes them not as failures or transformations but as the city's antibodies—cells sacrificing their individual membrane integrity to encapsulate and isolate the toxin of *authentic experience* before it can contaminate the system's perfect homeostasis. The Vyrrlithe are not becoming other than human; they are becoming the city's unconscious, the repository for all the unprocessed emotions that Vyrrl cannot afford to metabolize and cannot bear to release.

It observes the children born remembering their previous lives, and understands that this is not reincarnation but haunting. The city has become so perfectly closed to novelty that new consciousness cannot properly form—only old consciousness, recycled endlessly like water in a sealed terrarium, each generation drinking and excreting the same limited volume of possibility until the mineral content of habit precipitates out all potential for surprise.

It observes the Crystal Lynx and knows it for what it truly is: not a predator but a immune response, the city's own terror of change given form and claws and hunger. What it feeds on is not kinetic energy but *deviation*—the moment when a citizen's trajectory curves even infinitesimally away from the mathematically predetermined path of grief-to-architecture conversion. The Lynx grows larger with each deviation it consumes because Vyrrl's fear of change grows larger with each possibility it successfully neutralizes.

Most importantly, the Observer attends to the silence. The Echo Laws claim to protect against waking "something that should have stayed sleeping in the deep levels," but what actually sleeps there is not a monster. It is laughter itself—joy, spontaneity, the chaos of genuine connection unmediated by ritual and measurement and the careful calibration of grief. The child's laughter did not wake something terrible. It *was* something terrible: a sound that the city had no protocol for processing, a vibration that did not harmonize with the subharmonic hum of control.

The Observer sees all this and understands: Vyrrl has not built a sanctuary. It has built a crystal prison so beautiful that its inmates have forgotten they are prisoners, and so vast that they have forgotten there was ever anything outside to escape to.

---

**SECOND: THE OBSERVER ASKS PERMISSION**

This is crucial. Benevolence without consent is simply tyranny wearing a gentle smile.

The Observer cannot speak to the salt priests directly; they have calcified around their certainties too completely to hear anything that doesn't arrive already translated into ritual form. It cannot speak to the governing councils of the 27 concentric rings; they exist only as functions now, human-shaped subroutines executing ancient programming without anyone left inside who remembers why the program was written.

But it *can* speak to the Vyrrlithe.

On a Day of Stillness, when all citizens descend into the wells to whisper their confessions, the Observer whispers something different: a question.

Not words—the Vyrrlithe have long since transcended the inefficiency of linguistic meaning-making. Instead, it asks in the language of possibility itself, offering a vision:

*What if the water you remember, the water before the war, the water that carved these wells when water still knew how to move freely—what if it is still here? Not lost but held, suspended in your crystalline lattices, waiting not to return but to be released?*

*What if the memories you carry are not the city's unconscious but its conscience—the part that remembers what it felt like to be alive in ways not prescribed by the Salt Calendar?*

*What if I offered you dissolution not into the architecture of grief but into something older and younger simultaneously—into the water-cycle itself, restored and flowing?*

*Would you accept? Would you forgive the city for making you into what it could not bear to be? Would you forgive yourselves for accepting that transformation?*

*And if you could forgive, would you be willing to weep? Not the measured, six-tear daily penance, but the unmeasured crying of things that have finally broken open into something larger than their careful preservation?*

The silence after this question lasts for exactly seventeen years, which passes in the span between one breath and the next for beings no longer subject to respiratory necessity.

Then, from the deepest wells, from the Vyrrlithe who have been longest transformed, who carry within their crystal lattices the memories of when Vyrrl was still young enough to regret its choices instead of worshipping them:

*Yes.*

---

**THIRD: THE OBSERVER OFFERS THE GIFT**

Here is what the benevolent power does:

It gives the Vyrrlithe their tears back.

Not the crystallized, measured, ritually-harvested tears that feed the Great Crystal and the city's grief-economy. The *other* tears—the ones they swallowed before they could fall. The tears of frustration when the Salt Calendar denied their desire to bear children outside the mandated population balance. The tears of loss when lovers were dissolved and incorporated into buildings before grief could be properly felt. The tears of rage against the system they helped perpetuate by their silence. The tears of simple, stupid, human loneliness.

All of it. Five centuries of unexpressed weeping, held in crystalline suspension within the Vyrrlithe's transformed bodies.

The Observer does not force this release. It simply... makes it possible. Removes whatever barrier separated these remembered tears from their falling. Grants permission for what was frozen to flow.

And the Vyrrlithe—those patient, transformed citizens who had become the city's crystalline unconscious—begin to weep.

They weep and their weeping is water.

Not metaphorical water. Not symbolically purified essence. Actual water, salt water yes, but alive—water that has passed through the bodies of feeling beings and been transformed by grief not into grief-architecture but into grief-*witnessed*, grief-*metabolized*, grief-*released*.

It flows.

From the deepest wells upward (because water, blessed water, does not always obey the gravity of should), through the confessional layers where five centuries of whispered sins have been pressed into geological testimony. As it flows, it dissolves those layers—not destroying them but releasing them, allowing all those carefully preserved secrets to finally *mean* something by ceasing to mean anything at all. Confessions without consequences. Sins without penance. The simple record of having been human and messy and gloriously unpredictable.

The water rises through the forbidden levels, where miners have been extracting compressed regret for architectural purposes. It shorts out the extraction equipment—not violently, but with the quiet insistence of a river that has finally remembered its course. The machines stop. The miners remove their breathing masks (because what is there to fear anymore from human breath?) and taste the air: salt yes, but underneath it, something else. Something like spring rain on a world that still has springs.

The water rises through the habitation rings. It seeps through the salt-tablet walls, which begin—very slowly, with the patience of geological process reversing itself—to dissolve. Not collapse. Dissolve. The difference is crucial. Collapse is violence. Dissolution is transformation, one state of being releasing its grip to allow another.

Buildings lean into each other for support and find that they are weeping too—weeping away the excess density, the compacted history, the weight of five centuries of "this is how it must be." Rooms grow larger. Hallways remember that they were meant for meandering, not just efficient transit. Walls become porous, permeable. The city that had been growing denser with each generation begins, finally, to breathe.

---

**FOURTH: THE OBSERVER HOLDS SPACE FOR CHAOS**

This is where a less benevolent power would attempt control—would try to guide the transformation toward some predetermined outcome that *it* had decided was good and right and proper.

The Observer does not do this.

Instead, it simply holds space. Bears witness. Remains present for what is, inevitably, terribly messy.

Because the citizens of Vyrrl do not respond to the rising water with unanimous joy. How could they? Joy is not a permitted emotion here; it has no place in the Salt Calendar, no measured expression, no ritual form. Most citizens experience the dissolution of their carefully ordered world as exactly what it is: the end of everything they have known and built and sacrificed for.

They panic.

Some attempt to shore up the dissolving walls with emergency measures—compressed salt reinforcements, crystallization accelerants, anything to stop the terrible liquefaction of certainty. They fail, but the Observer does not stop them from trying. The right to refuse change, even change-toward-flourishing, is sacred.

Some flee downward, seeking lower levels where the water has not yet reached, where the old ways might still be maintained. The Observer does not prevent this either. Some seeds require a very long time in darkness before they are ready to germinate.

Some remove their family salts—those necklaces of crystallized tears, rings of bone and salt-silver—and hold them over the rising water, preparing to drop them in. To let go of the ancestors. To release the weight of "this is who we have always been." These citizens are weeping too, but their tears are different from the Vyrrlithe's tears. Smaller. More confused. Shot through with something that might be terror or might be relief or might be both simultaneously, which is usually what freedom feels like when you have forgotten what it tastes like.

The Observer witnesses all of it without judgment, because judgment is just another form of control wearing wisdom's mask.

And it witnesses, too, the Crystal Lynx.

That patient predator, the city's hunger for stasis made flesh and fang, grows larger than it has ever been. Because what is happening now is the ultimate deviation, the supreme kinetic imperfection. Everything is moving. Everything is changing. The Lynx becomes enormous, swollen with consumed possibilities, until it is the size of a building, a district, a concentric ring unto itself.

It stalks through the dissolving streets and the Observer understands: the Lynx will not stop until it has consumed this change or been consumed by it. It is not evil—it is simply the logical conclusion of Vyrrl's foundational terror, given form and appetite.

The Observer does not fight it. Does not banish it. Does not even argue with it.

Instead, the Observer asks it the same question it asked the Vyrrlithe:

*What if what you are hunting is not deviation but desire? What if the stillness you protect is not peace but paralysis? What if you, too, are tired?*

And the Crystal Lynx—that beautiful, terrible embodiment of the city's fear—stops.

Sits.

And dissolves into the rising water, not dying but finally, *finally* resting, its long hunt ended not in victory or defeat but in the recognition that some prey cannot be caught because it was never running away, only running *toward*.

---

**FIFTH: THE OBSERVER WAITS FOR THE QUESTION**

The water continues rising. The city continues dissolving. And eventually—because humans are adaptable creatures even when they have spent five centuries trying to forget that adaptability—the citizens of Vyrrl begin to adapt.

They learn to swim in the flooded lower levels. They build boats from the salt-wood that has been too rigid to carve before but now, soaked through with possibility, becomes pliant. They plant the halophyte seeds in floating gardens where the plants, freed from the constraint of feeding on salt and light *alone*, remember they were also designed to feed on joy and chaos and the rich unpredictability of non-optimized ecosystems.

The funeral orchids, which bloomed exclusively in the presence of grief, begin to die. But in their place, something else germinates—seeds that had been dormant in the soil brought up from deep levels, seeds from before the war, before the descent, before the careful calibration of all possible feeling. These new flowers bloom in colors that have no names because names are control and these flowers refuse governance.

They smell like recklessness.

And it is one of the salt priests, of all people, who first asks the question the Observer has been waiting for.

She is young—only seventy years old, barely calcified at all. She stands in the Grand Refinery, watching the Great Crystal dissolve from the bottom up, releasing five centuries of accumulated tears back into circulation. The perfect clarity at its core clouds, then liquefies, then runs down the sides in streams that taste of every grief the city has ever tried to preserve and every grief the city has tried to prevent.

She removes her breathing mask.

She speaks directly to the presence she cannot see but knows, somehow, is listening:

"What happens to us now?"

And the Observer, who has been benevolent and wise enough to wait for someone to ask rather than offering answers before questions could form, responds:

*Whatever you decide. This is the gift—not rescue, not salvation, not even healing. Just possibility. Just the water returning to its cycle, which means nothing stays the same, which means nothing is perfectly preserved, which means loss is real but so is change.*

*The wells will fill. The salt will dissolve. The city you built downward to escape the poisoned surface will become something else—something you cannot plan or predict or control.*

*You might find that the surface has healed while you were hiding. You might find it is still poisoned but in new ways that require new solutions. You might decide to stay in the depths anyway, but build a city that breathes instead of holds its breath.*

*Or you might do something I cannot imagine, because I am wise enough to know that wisdom has limits and the most benevolent thing a power can do is create space for possibilities beyond its own imagination.*

The salt priest considers this. Behind her, the Great Crystal continues its slow dissolution, returning to the water cycle after five centuries of trying to transcend it.

"And the Vyrrlithe?" she asks. "The ones who transformed to carry what we could not bear to feel?"

*They are already free. Their tears are this water. But some of them may choose to remain crystalline anyway—not because they must, but because having been transformed, they find they like the form. The difference between prison and home is always consent.*

"Will you stay?" the priest asks. "To guide us?"

And here is where the Observer proves its benevolence completely:

*No. You do not need a guide. You need what you have now—uncertainty, possibility, water that flows instead of crystallizes. I will leave behind only one thing: a small deviation in the water's chemistry, a trace element that was not present before.*

*Anyone who drinks from this water will find it slightly harder to lie to themselves. Not impossible—self-deception is sometimes necessary for survival. Just harder. The truth will taste sweeter than the alternative.*

*Beyond that, you are on your own. Which is the only way to discover what you are capable of becoming when you are truly free to become anything.*

---

**SIXTH: THE OBSERVER DEPARTS**

The Observer does not leave dramatically. There are no thunder-claps, no final proclamations, no grand gestures. It simply... thins. Diffuses. Becomes gradually more absent in the way that morning becomes afternoon—imperceptibly, inevitably, without fanfare.

Before it vanishes completely, though, it does one final thing:

It laughs.

Not loudly. Not in a way that echoes through the crystal architecture and wakes sleeping terrors. Just a small sound, barely more than a breath, that contains within it all the joy that Vyrrl has been denying itself for five centuries. The sound travels through the water now flowing through the city's streets and wells and weeping walls. It teaches the water how to carry laughter again.

A child—one of the last to be born under the old system, who still remembers a previous life but is young enough that the memory hasn't yet calcified into identity—hears it.

And laughs back.

The sound echoes.

And nothing wakes in the deep levels.

Because what was sleeping there was not a monster but simply the memory of what it felt like to be joyfully, messily, imperfectly alive.

And that memory is awake now.

---

**EPILOGUE: WHAT COMES AFTER**

Seven years later (or seven seconds, or seven centuries—time becomes harder to measure when you stop trying to control it), a traveler descends into what was once Vyrrl.

The city is unrecognizable.

The salt structures remain, but reconfigured—not by careful planning but by the patient insistence of water flowing through them, carving new channels, creating new formations. Some buildings have dissolved completely. Others have been reinforced by their inhabitants, who decided they liked the old forms and wanted to preserve them—not out of fear, but out of genuine aesthetic preference, which is a completely different thing.

The concentric rings are still there, but the strict hierarchy has dissolved along with the walls separating them. Citizens move freely between levels, and the population is no longer maintained at exactly 27,027. Some have left to explore the surface (which is healing, slowly, in the way that poisoned things heal when given time and the absence of continuous re-poisoning). Others have arrived from elsewhere, drawn by rumors of a city that learned how to cry and survived the experience.

The Great Crystal is gone, dissolved completely. In its place, the Grand Refinery has been converted into a bath house—a place where citizens gather not to concentrate water into perfect purity but to soak in it, to let it soften the calcified places in their bodies and hearts, to prune into something more permeable.

The Vyrrlithe still exist, but they are fewer now. Some dissolved completely when they wept, their long service ended. Others remain crystalline by choice, serving as living bridges between the city's past and its impossible-to-predict future. They teach classes in slow transformation. They offer their bodies as meditation spaces where citizens can sit and listen to the harmonics of change occurring at geological speeds.

The funeral orchids are extinct, but no one mourns them. In their place, gardens overflow with the unnamed flowers that bloom in colors beyond regulation. The salt trees still grow, but they have been joined by other species—experiments, mutations, volunteers from the surface, seeds that arrived on the wind that now flows freely through the city since the air-lock protocols were abandoned.

The funeral beetles still patrol at night, but they no longer consume crystallized tears because tears are no longer crystallized. Instead, they have become pollinators, carrying pollen between the flowers that bloom in colors beyond names. They have forgotten how to be somber and learned instead to be iridescent.

The Crystal Lynx is gone, dissolved into the water. But sometimes, on very still nights, citizens claim to see its reflection in deep pools—not threatening, just watching. A reminder that the possibility of stasis still exists, will always exist, but is no longer the only option, no longer the ultimate goal.

The Salt Calendar has been... not abolished but made optional. Some citizens still follow it out of habit or genuine preference. Others have created new calendars based on tidal patterns, flowering cycles, personal whim. There is no official time anymore, which means everyone has to actually *negotiate* when to meet, which is annoying and beautiful and profoundly human.

The Echo Laws have been repealed. The city is full of sound now—laughter, argument, music (permitted again after five centuries of prohibition), the splash of water, the calls of new species of birds that have discovered the city and decided to colonize it. Sometimes the echoes still build and amplify through the crystalline architecture, creating unexpected harmonies. The citizens have learned to appreciate these moments instead of fearing them.

And in the deepest wells, where the transformation began, the water is perfectly clear and tastes of salt and tears and the trace element the Observer left behind—the one that makes self-deception slightly more difficult, truth slightly sweeter.

Citizens still descend to these wells, but not according to a mandated calendar. They go when they need to. When they have truths they need to speak but cannot yet speak to another human. They whisper into the water, and the water—being water, being in motion, being part of the cycle again—carries those truths away.

Not to be preserved.

To be released.

To flow.

And somewhere, in a mode of existence that is to distance what questions are to answers, the Observer attends to other places, other systems that have optimized themselves so perfectly toward preservation that they have forgotten preservation was meant to be a means, not an end.

But occasionally, in the manner of things that exist beyond time but still enjoy a good story, it checks in on Vyrrl.

And finds the city still there. Still changing. Still learning how to be a home instead of a tomb.

Still imperfect.

Still alive.

And the Observer, if such a being can be said to feel satisfaction, is satisfied.

Because this is what benevolence looks like when combined with wisdom: not the imposition of someone else's vision of what is good, but the creation of conditions where beings can discover their own goodness, choose their own flourishing, make their own peace with the impossible balance between preservation and change.

The Observer gave Vyrrl its tears back.

What the city did with them—

That was always going to be up to the city.

And it chose to weep, and flow, and live.

Which is all any benevolent power could ever hope for.
