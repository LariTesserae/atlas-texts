# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

The benevolent power—let us call it the Keeper of Coherences, though it has worn many names like favorite coats across the span of impossible time—does not arrive in Shearbelow so much as *notice* it. And noticing, for such a being, is a form of gentle catastrophe.

The Keeper exists where threads meet before they know they're weaving. It tends to places that have become too beautifully lost, cities caught in the exquisite pain of almost-being. Shearbelow, flickering at the edge of its own existence, rings like a bell in frequencies the Keeper has learned to hear: the sound of a place loving itself too carefully to commit to reality.

---

**First: The Keeper Would Sit**

Not intervene. Not fix. Just *sit*.

It would materialize—or rather, it would stop having been absent—on one of those benches in the park that exists between the idea of benches and the memory of sitting. The single boulder with its rotating exhibitions of temporary forests would recognize a kindred spirit: something that holds space for other things to become themselves.

The Keeper would sit there for what the obsolete clocks union might call seven years, or seven minutes, or seven moments of supreme attention. It would watch the birds fall in circles, observe the street cats negotiating their constituent parts, witness the deer in the mirrors catching taxis to nowhere.

It would learn Shearbelow's particular grammar—the way sound occupies space here, the way water apologizes, the way buildings hatch from abandoned blueprints. The Keeper knows that benevolence without fluency is just another form of violence, however well-intentioned.

During this sitting, the Keeper would do something that might look like nothing: it would *witness* with absolute fidelity. Not judge, not assess for fixing—just see Shearbelow exactly as it is, in all its committed uncertainty.

And Shearbelow, accustomed to averting its eyes politely while shadows remember things, would feel perhaps for the first time the strange gift of being looked at directly without the gaze trying to make it into something else.

---

**Second: The Keeper Would Ask Permission**

On the seven-thousand-and-third Tuesday (or the first, depending on which timeline you're counting), the Keeper would attend the city council meeting. It would follow them into whichever dimension they're convening in that week and wait patiently through the agenda items about logic leak repairs and quantum corruption audits.

When the moment arrived—and in Shearbelow, moments arrive when they're ready, not when they're scheduled—the Keeper would speak in the language of punctuation marks the Residents understand:

"—" (a hyphen, holding space between two ideas uncertain they belong together)

"?" (but gently, without demand)

"..." (trailing off, leaving room for the city to complete the thought)

The question, translated into the conditional perfect tense, would be something like:

*If I were to offer you a gift that you would have wanted, had circumstances been different, would you have been willing to have considered accepting what you might not have known you needed?*

The city council—composed that week of three former shadows, a sentient pothole, and the collective memory of a mayoral candidate who never quite ran for office—would deliberate. The pigeons on the cathedral roof would assemble and disassemble themselves into increasingly complex mathematical models, calculating futures conditional and futures perfect.

The answer would come not in words but in the way the streetlights flicker: not from faulty wiring, but in a pattern that means *Yes, but only if you promise not to make us more real than we're ready to be.*

---

**Third: The Keeper Would Offer Anchors (Not Chains)**

Understanding consent given in the subjunctive mood, the Keeper would begin its work.

It would not fix Shearbelow's flickering. The buildings need to hesitate between stone and not-stone; this is how they breathe. But it would offer them *rhythm*—a pattern to their uncertainty, so the flickering becomes more like music than static.

Where gravity points inward but smells outward, the Keeper would introduce what it calls *counterweight grace*: tiny pockets of complementary strangeness that make the contradiction feel less like falling and more like dancing with a partner who leads in one direction while facing another. Your feet would still stay planted while your mouth fills with the taste of falling, but now there would be a sense that both truths are equally true, rather than one undermining the other.

For the water that exists as regretful apology, the Keeper would provide *listeners*—small, invisible presences whose only function is to hear the whispered names with complete attention. Not to remember them (that would be too much burden), but to acknowledge that they were spoken. The droplets would still taste of copper and distant thunder, but perhaps with an aftertaste of having been heard.

The Keeper would walk through the streets that are geological faults in mid-argument and offer the disputing stones a *mediator*—not to resolve their disagreement (the disagreement is load-bearing; it holds up the entire city), but to help them argue more productively. Limestone's points would become more compelling, shale's rebuttals more precise, basalt's counter-arguments more nuanced. The foundations would remain fractures, but fractures that know they're creating something beautiful together.

---

**Fourth: The Keeper Would Tend the Thresholds**

The Keeper specializes in edges, and Shearbelow is nothing but edges—between being and unbeing, between memory and forgetting, between solid and conditional.

It would visit the cathedral spire extruding from that ambitious shadow and offer it *encouragement*—not to finish (finishing might destroy it), but to extrude with more confidence in its incompletion. "You are not failing to become a cathedral," the Keeper would whisper in frequencies only shadows understand. "You are succeeding at being a cathedral in the act of emerging. These are not the same thing, and you are doing the second one perfectly."

To the Residents—those not-quite-memories assembling themselves from breadcrumbs of attention—the Keeper would offer something delicate: *permission to be exactly as fragmentary as they are*. It would establish what it calls Coherence Stations throughout the city, places where the fragmentary can gather without pressure to become whole.

These wouldn't be buildings (buildings flicker here anyway) but *moments*—recurring instances when it becomes temporarily easier to hold yourself together, or temporarily acceptable to fall apart. Every third Thursday that occurs on a Tuesday, every sunset that climbs instead of descends, every time the street cats successfully negotiate which parts belong to whom.

At these Coherence Stations, a Resident could arrive as just a left-behind earring remembering a grandmother's jaw, and could stay that way for as long as needed. No pressure to assemble into a full person. No shame in being primarily a cough that once belonged to a kind bus driver.

The Keeper would staff these stations with what it calls *Witnessfriends*—beings whose only job is to sit with you while you're partial, and to reflect back without judgment: "Yes, I see you. You are an earring with memories. That is a complete thing to be right now."

---

**Fifth: The Keeper Would Strengthen What Already Heals**

The Keeper has learned that every struggling place has its own immune system, its own methods of self-repair. Wise intervention means boosting what's already working.

In Shearbelow, the healing happens through:

**The children growing in reverse**—this is genius. They're not disappearing; they're distilling themselves down to essence. The Keeper would recognize this as a form of spiritual alchemy and would offer the parents *guidebooks* (written in disappearing ink on the lining of dried riverbeds, naturally) on how to curate diminishment as an art form. Tips on which stories make the best first forgettings. Techniques for smoothing scars while keeping their wisdom.

**The dream auctions**—already a perfect system for redistributing the weight of experience so no single dreamer carries too much. The Keeper would introduce *dream vouchers* for Residents who are too new or too fragmentary to have dreams of their own yet. Everyone deserves a recurring staircase leading somewhere different. Everyone should have access to at least one face that changes species when you're not thinking about it.

**The elderly who are youngest of all**—these are the city's priests, its wisdom-keepers, though they don't call themselves that. The Keeper would recognize them instantly as colleagues. It would join them sometimes on those benches between the idea and the memory, learning the languages the crows speak, adding to the maps on their skin showing places that got lost before becoming destinations.

To them, the Keeper would offer its own maps: routes to other cities like Shearbelow, places that exist in the conditional perfect tense, communities of the beautifully lost. "You are not alone in your particular species of being," the Keeper would tell them. "There are others who understand that dying occasionally for aesthetic purposes is a valid life choice."

---

**Sixth: The Keeper Would Plant Seeds of Gentle Solidity**

Not to make Shearbelow solid—that would kill it—but to give it *choice* about its solidity.

The Keeper would introduce what it calls *Reliability Flowers*—strange blooms that grow in the cracks between contradictions. They look different to everyone who sees them (that's part of their reliability), but they all share one property: while standing near them, you can choose to be more solid or more flickering, more real or more conditional, more here or more almost-here.

These flowers wouldn't grow everywhere—that would be too stabilizing, would rob the city of its essential uncertainty. But they'd appear in key locations:

- Near the public telephones that connect you to the moment you almost made the right decision (because sometimes, after that call, you need to be solid enough to make a different choice this time)

- In the market district where secondhand echoes are sold (because echoes need something to echo off of, even if only intermittently)

- Outside the prisons where metaphor-thieves serve rhyming sentences (because even in a city of beautiful uncertainty, there must be *some* consequences, however poetically just)

The flowers would bloom according to need, not season. When someone requires more solidity to bear their own flickering, a flower appears. When someone needs permission to dissolve a little, to blur at the edges, the flowers step back, make room.

---

**Seventh: The Keeper Would Create a Bridge (Not an Exit)**

The cruelest thing about Shearbelow is its isolation—strict immigration policies, reality that bends away from elsewhere, streets that only connect to other streets in the same conditional tense.

The Keeper wouldn't break this isolation (borders exist for reasons, and Shearbelow's reasons are good ones), but it would build what it calls a *Permeable Edge*: a place where Shearbelow touches other realities without either having to change to accommodate the other.

This would take the form of a train station—naturally, because stations are already liminal, already about being between. The Station of Conditional Arrivals would appear in Shearbelow's geography like the city itself appears: intermittently, when needed, remembered forward from a future that hasn't decided yet whether to happen.

Trains would run on tracks made of possibility. Not on schedule (this is Shearbelow, after all), but in response to longing. When someone in the regular, solid world starts to feel like a place instead of a person, starts to feel like a city or forest inside their chest preparing for visitors who never arrive—a train appears.

The journey would be transformative. By the time you arrived in Shearbelow, you'd be conditional enough to bear its grammar. And if you needed to leave (some do, eventually), the return journey would gradually resolidify you, teaching you to carry a little bit of Shearbelow's flickering inside you, a small patch of gravity that points inward while smelling outward.

The station would also allow for *mail*—letters written by the Residents to people they remember being, or people who remember them. The postal service would employ the pigeons (when they're not too busy being a computer), who have experience delivering messages across timelines.

---

**Eighth: The Keeper Would Introduce the Memory Gardeners**

This would be the Keeper's most delicate intervention, the one requiring the most trust from Shearbelow.

The Keeper would recruit a small team—no more than seven, because seven is the number of overlapping timelines Shearbelow runs on, and symmetry matters in delicate work. These would be beings trained in what the Keeper calls *Remembrance Architecture*: the art of structuring memory so it bears weight without crushing what it holds.

The Memory Gardeners would not change what Shearbelow is, but would help it remember what it is *more cohesively*. Because here's the thing the Keeper has noticed: Shearbelow doesn't just struggle with being—it also struggles with *remembering its struggling*, which creates a meta-level of uncertainty that sometimes tips from beautiful into unbearable.

The Gardeners would maintain what they call a *Living Archive*—not a building (those flicker), not a database (technology here exists in the conditional perfect tense), but a *practice*. Every evening, as sunset climbs instead of descending, they would walk the streets and gather the day's forgettings.

That building that forgot how to be a bakery at 3pm? They'd note it, weave it into the larger pattern of forgettings, show how it rhymes with the theater that forgot it was showing a tragedy and switched mid-performance to a comedy about geological arguments.

The person who arrived as just a cough and is slowly assembling other parts? The Gardeners would track their progress—not to rush them, but to celebrate each addition, each new fragment of self retrieved from the breadcrumbs of attention.

They would create what the Keeper calls *Resonance Maps*—not to show you how to get from place to place (the city's distances already do that by stretching when spoken about and contracting when walked), but to show you how each strange thing in Shearbelow echoes and supports every other strange thing.

See: the water that apologizes in droplets connects to the fogs that graze on lost consonants, which relates to the silent anthem everyone refuses to sing, which harmonizes with the thirty-seven flavors of perfect silence manufactured in the factories. It's all one ecosystem of beautiful impossibility, each weird thing making room for the others.

When a Resident feels too fragmentary, too lost in the gaps between forgetting and remembering, the Memory Gardeners could show them the Resonance Maps and say: "Look. You fit here. Your particular way of being incomplete rhymes with the city's way of being uncertain. You are not failing to cohere. You are succeeding at being exactly the right kind of fragment for this particular impossible ecosystem."

---

**Ninth: The Keeper Would Host the Festival of Reflected Futures**

Once per year—or once per emotional season, which is more accurate in a place where timekeeping is handled by striking clocks—the Keeper would organize a celebration.

The Festival of Reflected Futures would be Shearbelow's chance to see itself in mirrors that don't yet exist, to try on possibilities like clothing, to rehearse different ways of continuing to be uncertain.

During the festival, the entire city would become even more conditional than usual. Buildings would flicker faster, exploring the full range between stone and not-stone. The Residents would swap fragments—you be the cough for a while, I'll try being the left-behind earring. The geological arguments underlying the streets would host formal debates, with citizens voting on which rock makes the most compelling points (basalt usually wins, but limestone has been improving its rhetoric).

The Keeper would invite guests from other impossible places—the City of Threading Absences, the Town of Perpetual Almosts, the Village That Exists Only in Blueprints Drawn by People Who Never Learned Architecture. They would share techniques, swap stories, trade maps to places that got lost before becoming destinations.

But the heart of the festival would be the Future Viewing: the Keeper would bring mirrors that show not what Shearbelow is, but what it might *become*—if it chose more solidity, if it leaned harder into flickering, if it resolved its contradictions, if it made peace with them, if it invented new contradictions entirely.

The Residents would look into these mirrors not to choose a future, but to *see the choice*. To understand that being caught between forgetting and remembering isn't a trap—it's a position, and from that position, many paths branch.

Some years, Shearbelow might choose to become slightly more real, letting a few buildings commit to stone. Other years, it might decide to flicker faster, to embrace uncertainty more fully. The point isn't the choice; it's the knowing that choice exists.

---

**Tenth: The Keeper Would Teach the Letting Go**

Here is the hardest thing, the thing the Keeper has learned across eons of tending to impossible places:

Sometimes, benevolence means preparing for your own departure.

The Keeper would not stay in Shearbelow forever. To stay forever would be to become part of the city's infrastructure, and infrastructure that comes from outside—however benevolent—eventually calcifies into colonization, however unintended.

So the Keeper would train apprentices from among the Residents. The elderly who are youngest of all would be naturals—they already understand that teaching crows to speak dead languages is a form of tending to impossible ecologies.

The training would be gentle, experiential. The Keeper would take them to tend other struggling places, let them practice witnessing with absolute fidelity, rehearse asking permission in the subjunctive mood, learn to offer anchors that aren't chains.

And when the apprentices were ready—not finished (nothing in Shearbelow finishes), but ready—the Keeper would begin its withdrawal. Not all at once (sudden departures are their own kind of violence), but gradually, in reverse: first removing itself from the obvious places, then from the subtle ones, finally from the spaces between.

The Reliability Flowers would learn to bloom on their own, responding to need without needing the Keeper to explain need. The Coherence Stations would become self-maintaining, staffed by Residents who had learned to witness each other's fragmentariness with compassion. The Memory Gardeners would train new Gardeners, who would develop techniques the Keeper never imagined.

The Station of Conditional Arrivals would remain—that gift was always meant to outlast its giver. But it would belong now to Shearbelow itself, incorporated into the city's ecosystem of beautiful impossibilities.

---

**Finally: The Keeper Would Leave a Gift**

On its last day—or its last moment, or its last seven overlapping timeline-instances—the Keeper would plant one more thing:

A tree.

Not a flickering tree, not one that grows in alphabetical order according to languages that haven't been invented yet. This would be perhaps the only completely solid, absolutely real thing in all of Shearbelow.

It would grow in the exact center of the city (which is also at every edge, because geography here is metaphorical). Its roots would reach down past the geological arguments, past the foundations that are fractures, all the way to bedrock that has never doubted being rock.

The tree would be called the Tree of Perhaps, and it would have one property:

Standing beneath it, you could be absolutely certain—for exactly as long as you stood there—that uncertainty is a valid way to exist. That flickering is not the same as failing. That being caught between forgetting and remembering is not a curse but a country, and countries can be homes.

Its leaves would never fall (that would be too decisive), but they would sometimes drift—not down, but *aside*, into the spaces between here and there. If you caught one, it would whisper in the language of punctuation marks:

"—" (You are held, even in your hesitation)

"?" (And questioning is its own form of answer)

"..." (And every trailing off is also a continuing, just in a different direction)

The Residents would picnic beneath this tree. The children shrinking toward baby-clarity would play in its shade. The elderly-youngest would nap against its trunk, dreaming of places that existed before geography learned to insist on itself.

And sometimes, when a new arrival stumbled into Shearbelow—half-memory, half-ghost, wholly uncertain—they would find themselves drawn to this tree. They would stand beneath it and feel, perhaps for the first time, that their fragmentariness is not a problem to solve but a form to inhabit.

The tree would not explain the Keeper's work. It wouldn't need to. It would simply exist, solid and certain, testifying that even in a city built entirely of flickering and maybe and not-quite:

*Someone cared enough to stay.*
*Someone understood enough to ask permission.*
*Someone loved you enough to give you tools and then leave you free to use them.*
*Someone witnessed your beautiful impossibility and called it home.*

---

**Epilogue: What the Keeper Would Remember**

Long after leaving, in whatever elsewhere the Keeper tends to next, it would carry Shearbelow with it—not as a memory (memories are too solid), but as a *quality of attention*.

It would find itself noticing other places caught in the long inhale between forgetting and remembering. It would recognize the grammar of uncertainty in new dialects. And it would know, bone-deep, what it learned in those seven years/minutes/moments of supreme attention:

That benevolence is not fixing. It is witnessing, asking permission, offering choices, strengthening what already heals, and—most sacred of all—trusting that what you've tended will grow in directions you never imagined, toward light you helped them find but will never see yourself.

That the highest form of care is to make yourself unnecessary.

That love means building bridges and then walking back across them, leaving the far side for others to explore.

And late at night, when the Keeper rests in the spaces between tending, it would smile—if such beings smile—remembering:

A city that flickered.
Residents who were fragments.
A single solid tree.
And the sound of leaves drifting aside, whispering in punctuation marks that somewhere, someone is standing beneath certainty, learning that uncertainty is enough.
