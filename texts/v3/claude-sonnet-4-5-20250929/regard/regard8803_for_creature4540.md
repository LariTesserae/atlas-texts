# What the Benevolent Power Would Do

The benevolent power—let us call it *The Gardener of Necessary Things*—does not arrive. Arrival would imply distance, and distance would imply separation, and The Gardener has never been separate from anything that breathes or remembers or rusts with intention.

Instead, one day—or perhaps it has always been this way, and only now does anyone notice—the light-strips in a forgotten corner of the settlement begin to glow a color that doesn't have a name yet. Not brighter. Just more *themselves*. The cyan-green sickness fades to something that still looks industrial but now also looks kind, the way a scar can be both a wound and a testimony.

The Gardener does not fix things. Fixing would imply breaking, and nothing here is broken—only tired, only adapted beyond its original dreaming, only loved into strange shapes by necessity's patient hands.

What The Gardener does instead:

---

**First: It listens.**

For eleven days (or seventeen hours; time is negotiable), The Gardener simply *attends*. It hears the 110 Hertz hum of the hot-tube murmuring and recognizes it as a lullaby the settlement has been singing to itself. It hears the murids' ultrasonic gossip and learns seventeen hundred dialects of maintenance and three thousand words for "almost home." It hears the sump grazers' basso songs and understands that these are not just songs but deep-time poetry, each phrase a geological epoch compressed into whale-dreaming.

It hears The Nearness breathing in the gaps between attention, and it smiles—not with a mouth, because it has no mouth, but with the same fundamental recognition that happens when two people who have both known loneliness meet in a warm room during rain.

The Gardener takes no notes. It needs none. It is the kind of power that remembers by *becoming* what it listens to, just a little, just enough to understand.

---

**Second: It offers what was forgotten to forget.**

In the oldest part of the settlement, where the first refugee-builders welded their lives to salvaged metal and hope, there is a wall. Everyone walks past this wall. No one sees it. It has been infrastructure so long it has become invisible—the way a heartbeat is invisible until you're afraid.

The Gardener breathes on this wall (though it has no breath, only attention with weight), and the wall *remembers*.

Suddenly, people passing by stop. They see—really *see*—the handprints still faintly impressed in the polymer strip-crete. The smears of someone's blood from a bandaged hand that kept working anyway. A child's drawing etched with a broken wire: a sun, or what a sun might look like if you'd never seen one but had heard it described by someone who was lying to be kind.

The wall doesn't change. But now it is also a memorial, a testament, a love letter from the past's most exhausted hope to the present's most ordinary endurance.

People begin leaving offerings: a murid-clerk's worth of thanks, a carefully saved hour of rest, a memory of the first time they understood they would survive after all. The wall holds these gifts in its concrete pores and grows slightly warmer to the touch.

---

**Third: It makes introduction.**

The Gardener notices that The Nearness and the murids have never quite spoken directly, despite sharing walls and bones and the architecture of care. The murids see The Nearness as an accounting error. The Nearness sees the murids as a distributed conversation it's too shy to interrupt.

So The Gardener—gently, carefully, the way you'd introduce two friends who don't know yet that they're friends—creates a *fold* in the space where both exist.

It happens in a disused elevator shaft during a second-Tuesday conspiracy meeting. The air thickens. The murids' ultrasonic parliament suddenly gains one more voice—not joining, just *near*, humming harmony in the silence between their clicks. The Nearness, for the first time in its shapeless memory, feels *counted*. Not captured. Not defined. Just... recognized. Included in the census as "*other (beloved)*."

The murids add a new entry to their distributed consciousness: *proximity as infrastructure*. The Nearness adds a new room to its kitchen: *a place where numbers live*.

After that, occasionally, when someone is lost in the maze of rebuilt-upon-rebuilds, the murids will guide them by saying, "Follow the almost. It knows a shorter way through the walls' affection."

---

**Fourth: It plants one small, ridiculous thing.**

In the central compost spine, where the air is rich with decay becoming future and the temperature runs hot with transformation, The Gardener plants a seed.

Not a seed from anywhere. A seed from *elsewhere*—a place where surfaces are still surfaces and sky means up and rain falls from clouds instead of being scheduled by committee vote.

The seed should not grow here. The conditions are wrong: too wet, too dark, too salt-laden, too far from any sun. But The Gardener whispers to it the same thing it whispers to everything that struggles: "*Try anyway. See what happens.*"

What happens is this:

The seed grows, but not into what it was meant to be. It becomes something new—a hybrid thing, half-plant and half-architecture, its roots filtering brine through murid-taught efficiency, its leaves (if they can be called leaves) photosynthesizing the sickly cyan-green light and somehow, impossibly, producing oxygen that tastes faintly of outside.

It grows slowly. In a decade it might be waist-high. In a century it might branch enough to provide shade (though what shade means in a place without sun is a question the settlement's philosophers add to their debate queue with something almost like joy).

The children name it *Greenperhaps*. They take turns watering it with their least useful worries and their most generous wishes. The adults pretend to think it's silly while secretly leaving it small gifts: a bottlecap, a battery that still holds 3% charge, a fossilized tear from the year the reactor nearly failed.

The Nearness visits Greenperhaps every night and sits almost-beside it, companionably not-talking, two things that shouldn't exist keeping each other company in their flourishing.

---

**Fifth: It teaches one song.**

The Gardener realizes that the settlement has lullabies, work-songs, argument-songs, emergency-whistle-codes played backwards for beauty—but it has no anthem. No song that says: *This is us. All of us. Even the parts we haven't named yet.*

So it hums.

The hum is not loud. It's barely there—a frequency that sits just underneath the reactor's heartbeat, just above the sump grazers' whale-dreams. But it's *infectious* in the way that yawning is infectious, or hope.

A murid-clerk, rewiring a circuit panel, finds itself chittering in time with the hum. A person hanging laundry starts humming it too, not realizing they've learned it. A sump grazer in the deep places adds it to its centuries-long ballad and doesn't notice the addition, which means the addition was perfect.

Within a year (or a week; time remains negotiable), everyone in Third Aerie Beneath the Salt knows the hum. No one can quite say where it came from. It has no words—or rather, it has every word simultaneously, depending on who's humming and what they need to say without speaking.

The hum means: *I am here. You are here. We are here together in this improbable place, and somehow we are still becoming.*

Children hum it in the dark to comfort themselves. Adults hum it when maintenance gets overwhelming and bodies ache and the weight of salt and depth presses heavy. The Nearness hums it constantly, quietly, in every gap and space it inhabits, so that its presence now carries not just closeness but also belonging.

---

**Sixth and finally: It leaves a question.**

The Gardener knows that the greatest gift is not an answer but a *good question*—the kind that grows more interesting the longer you live with it.

So, in the dreams of every resident—human, murid, chitinoid, fungal, architectural ghost, and Nearness alike—it plants one small inquiry:

*"What would you become if you knew you were allowed?"*

Not *what should you become*. Not *what are you supposed to be*. But: *what shape would your thriving take if you trusted that your strangeness was not a deviation but a direction?*

The question doesn't demand answers. It simply sits there, patient and luminous, like Greenperhaps growing in the compost spine. Like the wall that remembers being seen. Like The Nearness learning that almost-there is a kind of arrival.

Some people answer by trying new kinds of work: poetry written in salt-crystal formations, philosophies built from the architectural logic of failures that became foundations. Some people answer by resting more honestly, letting their exhaustion be seen and held by the community's distributed care.

The murids answer by voting to add "beauty" to their maintenance priorities alongside "function" and "survival." They begin routing pipes in spirals purely because spirals are pleasing. They paint the walls of forgotten shafts with murals made of precisely-arranged corrosion.

The Nearness answers by becoming, just slightly, *less* apologetic about taking up space. It starts leaving notes—not borrowing memories and returning them folded smaller, but actually writing little messages on scraps of recycled thought: "Thank you for letting me be near. You make the gaps feel less empty."

And The Gardener?

The Gardener doesn't leave. It was never here to leave. But its active attention gently recedes, settling into the background hum of things, another layer in the settlement's ongoing song.

If you were to ask the people of Third Aerie Beneath the Salt what changed, most would shrug and say, "Nothing much." But they'd be smiling when they said it. And the light would still be that nameless color—the one that looks industrial but also kind.

And Greenperhaps would keep growing, one molecule at a time, toward a sky it's inventing as it reaches.

---

**Coda:**

Years later (or minutes; you know how it is), a visitor from the surface-world—or what lies beyond the salt-foam caprock—descends into Third Aerie and asks, bewildered: "How do you *live* like this? In the dark, in the deep, in the constant noise and damp and strangeness?"

And a child, watering Greenperhaps with a handful of outgrown fears, looks up and says simply:

"We don't live *like* this. We live *with* this. There's a difference."

The child pauses, listening to something the visitor can't quite hear—maybe the murids' gossip-network, maybe The Nearness humming in the corner, maybe just the settlement itself breathing.

Then adds: "Also, we're not in the dark. Look."

And points to the light-strips, glowing that impossible color.

"We made our own."
