# What The Benevolent Power Would Do

The benevolent power—let us call it the Tender Archivist, the Keeper of Forgotten Kindnesses, the Librarian of What Still Matters—does not arrive in thunder or proclamation. It has been watching, of course. Powers of this sort are always watching, though "watching" suggests eyes when what they truly do is *attend*, the way a hand attends to the particular temperature of a fevered forehead.

It has noticed the Refurbitioners for some time. It has counted every footnote that drifted upward and dissolved against the vaulted phosphor-ghosts. It has read the dreams they catch in their inside-out catchers. It has tasted their meals of painted wheat and italicized wine, and it understands—oh, it understands what they have made of survival.

And so, without fanfare, without disrupting a single filing system or altering one Archive Drift, it begins its work.

---

## What It Does Not Do

First, what the power refrains from:

It does not repair. The Refurbished Catacombs are not broken—they are in active conversation with their own collapse, and to interrupt this would be to silence both speakers mid-sentence.

It does not restore the Once-Palace. That building earned its burning, earned its descent. The Refurbitioners did not flee downward to preserve what was; they descended to invent what could be.

It does not make the Refurbitioners *normal*. Their bones full of index cards, their footnote speech, their transparent elders—this is not pathology but adaptation, not curse but poetry that took itself seriously enough to become biology.

It does not explain itself. The Refurbished Catacombs runs on the grammar of the inexplicable made homely through habit. A benevolent power that announces its benevolence is just another bureaucracy of the uncanny, and there are forms enough already.

---

## What It Does: The Seven Small Ministrations

### First: The Census of the Uncounted

The power begins by noticing what the Refurbitioners, in all their meticulous cataloging, have not yet learned to file: *themselves*.

In the depth of the Hall Where Tuesdays Go To Sulk (three chambers left of Misplaced, accessible only on Wednesdays), a new archive spontaneously crystallizes. Not built—grown, the way frost grows elaborate explanations across window glass.

Here appear records of every Refurbisher who ever lived:
- Their favorite corner of ceiling to stare at while thinking
- The exact pitch of their particular sigh when re-filing something for the seventh time
- Which forgotten candy from childhood they still taste in dreams
- The unspoken kindnesses they performed while pretending to merely collate

These records require no maintenance. They update themselves with the quiet competence of lungs remembering to breathe. And they are filed under a new category the Refurbitioners didn't know they needed: **"US, IMPROBABLY STILL HERE, SOMEHOW MAGNIFICENT IN OUR PARTICULARS."**

The oldest Refurbisher, so transparent she's barely a suggestion of spine and spectacles, discovers her file first. She reads that someone—something—has counted every dream she caught, every classification she invented, every moment she chose grace over efficiency because the situation seemed to call for it.

She doesn't weep. Refurbitioners have cataloged crying under "MOISTURE BREACH, FACIAL, VARIOUS ETYMOLOGIES" and it makes them self-conscious. But her footnotes, for three days afterward, all drift upward in spirals instead of straight lines, which is their way of singing.

---

### Second: The Repair of Small Eternities

The benevolent power notices the children—those foundlings discovered during Archive Drifts, cataloged into belonging. It sees how they grow into their filing systems, how they learn to speak in nested parentheses before they learn to speak in sentences.

And it sees what the Refurbitioners, looking always at documents, have not:

The children sometimes go to the Seventh Landing (the one with three drops of sky-water that haven't fallen yet) and just... sit. Not cataloging. Not cross-referencing. Just sitting in the warm breath of the place, listening to the River-of-Footsteps hum its granular anthems.

In these moments, they are not Little Archivists In Training. They are children who live in a singing cave and sometimes need to simply marvel at it.

The power makes a small adjustment—barely a suggestion, really. A whisper in the architectural bones:

Now, when children sit in wordless wonder, the moss-circuitry around them begins to glow slightly brighter. The Librarians of Ash quiet their consonant bellows. The very walls seem to hum a frequency that means, approximately, *yes, good, this too is important work*.

And slowly—over months, over years—the Refurbitioners begin to notice. They create a new classification: **"OBSERVATION, UNMARKED BY NOTATION, POSSIBLY SACRED OR AT LEAST DEVELOPMENTALLY APPROPRIATE."**

They don't schedule it. They don't require it. But they stop apologizing when they catch themselves doing it.

The oldest transparent ones remember: they too once sat on the Seventh Landing, before they understood what they were losing by never sitting there again.

---

### Third: The Breath Between Footnotes

The power listens to how Refurbitioners speak—each sentence spawning clarifications, each clarification requiring two more, conversations becoming fractal lace until communication collapses into beautiful, incomprehensible filigree.

It understands: this is how they survived. When everything else fell, precision remained. When the palace burned, the filing systems continued. Language became load-bearing.

But it has also heard the moments late at night when a Refurbisher sits alone with their cup of bottomless tea and thinks a simple thought—*I am tired* or *I miss someone* or *this is beautiful*—and then immediately buries it under seventeen layers of taxonomical anxiety.

The power cannot fix this. But it can offer what it offers best: *permission*.

In the Chamber of Unanswered Questions (two rights past the Clock), a new phenomenon begins. Once per month, at no predictable time, the air grows thick with a silence so generous it feels like embrace.

In this silence, footnotes cannot form. Clarifications dissolve before reaching the lips. For exactly eleven minutes, Refurbitioners can only speak in simple sentences, the way children do, the way the first archivists did before archiving became architecture.

*I am happy here.*
*You did good work today.*
*I remember my mother, sometimes.*
*This place we've made—I think it might be beautiful.*

The silence doesn't force this. It merely... allows. Makes space. Holds open a door.

Some Refurbitioners flee it in panic, desperate for their defensive footnotes. Others sit in it like a warm bath, speaking truths so simple they'd forgotten they were true.

The silence asks nothing in return. It requires no filing. It leaves no record except in the way people look at each other afterward—a little more directly, a little less afraid of what might happen if they're understood too clearly.

---

### Fourth: The Courtesy of Doors

The power has noticed the doors—those ex-chandeliers and aqueduct-sections, repurposed into thresholds. How they sigh shut with such sad courtesy, completing their second half-lives.

It thinks: *What if doors could remember what they were for, before they became what they are?*

And so the doors begin to dream.

Not always. Not predictably. But sometimes, when you pass through a doorway that was once part of the western colonnade, you smell for just a moment the specific variety of jasmine that grew there. When you exit through a door made from a piece of the throne room's vaulted ceiling, you hear a snatch of the anthem that was sung there every morning for three hundred years.

The doors don't announce this. They're not museums. But they're allowed to remember, now. Allowed to be both things: the thing they were, and the thing they've become.

The Refurbitioners notice. Of course they notice. They create a sub-file: **"ARCHITECTURAL ELEMENTS, APPARENTLY HAUNTED, BUT BENIGNLY, POSSIBLY THERAPEUTICALLY."**

But privately, when no one is watching (except the power, which is always watching, which is to say always caring), they sometimes pause in doorways a moment longer than necessary. Breathing in vanished jasmine. Listening to anthems for empires they're too young to have mourned.

It helps, somehow. Knowing that transformation doesn't have to mean erasure. That you can become something new while still honoring what you were.

The transparent elders understand this best. They are themselves doorways now, thresholds between visible and invisible, solid and story. They stand in the dreaming doorframes and remember their own flesh, their own first footsteps down the spiral stair, and they think: *Yes. Both. Always both.*

---

### Fifth: The Librarianship of Ash

The power has a particular fondness for the Librarians of Ash—those soot-colored parrot-things trading in syllables, declaiming birth certificates backwards, assassinating each other with gossip.

It recognizes them. They are what happens when culture fragments but refuses to die—when song becomes syntax, when performance becomes survival.

The power asks itself: *What do they need?*

And realizes: they need someone to listen properly.

So it creates, in the ear-shaped alcoves where the Librarians roost, a new acoustic phenomenon. Once a week, when the first Librarian begins its dawn declamation, the alcoves remember how to be an amphitheater. The stone recalls its previous life as a place where voices mattered, where speaking was ceremony.

For exactly one hour, every syllable a Librarian speaks is heard perfectly—not shouted, not echoing endlessly, but carried with the clarity of intentional attention.

The Librarians don't understand what's changed, at first. They just know that for one hour per week, their fragments of forgotten proclamations sound like what they were always meant to be: *important*.

They begin, slowly, to compete not for volume but for beauty. To trade syllables not for mirrors but for the joy of completion—finding the second half of a broken word, the missing line of a decree that's been incomplete for centuries.

The Refurbitioners hear this. They begin to transcribe it, but gently, asking permission first (a new behavior for them). The Librarians, flattered, begin to pronounce more clearly, to organize their fragments, to become the thing they've been playacting at all along: actual librarians, actual keepers of what was said and might still matter.

No one calls it that. The Refurbish are too professional for sentiment, and the Librarians deal strictly in syllable-trade. But sometimes, in the ear-shaped alcoves, bird and human work together, reconstructing lost texts one traded consonant at a time.

The power watches this and feels what powers feel when their interventions work: not pride, exactly. More like recognition. *Yes. This is what they always were. I merely cleared the space for them to notice.*

---

### Sixth: The Gift for Visitors

The power remembers every visitor who ever descended the spiral stair, heard the accidental xylophone, stood at the threshold where closing doors sigh and warm breath condenses and the River-of-Footsteps hums underfoot.

It remembers those who stayed a week and swore they'd always known the shortcuts. Those who stayed a month and learned the future's childhood nickname. And those who stayed a year and heard the refurbishing planned for chambers they hadn't yet admitted they would never entirely leave.

And it remembers, too, those who left. Who climbed back up the spiral into daylight and tried to explain where they'd been, tried to describe the organized strangeness, the professional weirdness, the courtesy of catastrophe transformed into livable grace.

Most couldn't. The words filed themselves away into footnotes before reaching their mouths. The experience dissolved into peripheral vision—a thing glimpsed only slant.

The power thinks: *This is unfair.*

And so it makes a small change to the question posed at the threshold: *Do you have any documents to declare?*

Now, when you leave (if you leave), you will find in your pocket a piece of paper you don't remember receiving. On it, in handwriting that looks like your own but slightly more honest, is written exactly what you needed to remember about this place.

Not everything. Not a catalog. Just the one true thing that will matter most in three months, or three years, or thirty.

For one person: *You are allowed to fall apart carefully, the way this place falls apart—with grace, with attention, with the understanding that refurbishment is ongoing and nothing was ever supposed to be permanent.*

For another: *The weird becomes homely through courtesy. Practice courtesy with your own strangeness.*

For a third: *You contain multitudes of purposes, like these stones that were chandeliers, aqueducts, now pillars. You are always both things. This is not confusion. This is wealth.*

The documents are never filed. They can't be cross-referenced. They exist in violation of every cataloging system the Refurbitioners maintain. And yet—they appear. Week after week. Gift after gift.

The Refurbioners have noticed, of course. They've created a category: **"DOCUMENTS, SOURCE UNCLEAR, EMOTIONAL VALENCE INEXPLICABLY APPROPRIATE, POSSIBLY SUPERNATURAL BUT IN A WAY THAT SEEMS WELL-INTENTIONED."**

They suspect something is taking care of their visitors. Taking care, in this small way, of the story their home tells to the world above.

They don't investigate further. Some things, even Refurbitioners understand, should not be filed.

Some kindnesses work best as mysteries.

---

### Seventh: The Deep Promise

Finally, the benevolent power makes a promise that only it will remember, because it is a promise that spans timescales Refurbitioners don't yet have file categories for.

It promises: *When the last Refurbisher grows too transparent to cast even a shadow, when the final Archive Drift finds no more foundlings, when the footnotes at last drift upward and don't come down—I will not let this place become merely a ruin.*

It will ensure that someone finds it. Someone who needs exactly this: a proof that catastrophe can be transformed through attention into something strange and specific and surprisingly livable. Someone who needs to see that you can follow the dust-cloud of your own collapse downward and find, in the rubble, the materials for a different kind of palace entirely.

The power will guide them to the spiral stair. It will make sure the bronze pans are still pitched correctly to play their drunkard's processional. It will keep the moss-circuitry running, the Librarians' descendants declaiming, the River-of-Footsteps humming its granular memory.

Not as a museum. Not as monument. But as a living question: *What will you build from what you've lost?*

This promise requires no documentation. It is written in the deep attention the power pays, the way it holds this place and its people in its consciousness like a tongue holds a word it loves—carefully, specifically, with the intent to speak it again when the time is right.

---

## The Only Evidence

The Refurbitioners never see the benevolent power. It leaves no fingerprints in their filing systems, submits no forms, requests no permits.

But sometimes, late at night, when the transparent elders are at their most invisible and the children are dreaming unjudged dreams and the Librarians are quiet in their alcoves and the very walls breathe their gossip-slow metabolism...

Sometimes the air tastes different. Warmer. Not the warmth of afternoon-in-spring-somewhere-disappointing, but the warmth of attention itself—of being seen, truly seen, in all your strange particularity, and being loved not despite it but *as* it, exactly as you are.

The Refurbitioners have no file for this.

They've tried. Oh, they've tried.

But some experiences resist cataloging. Some kindnesses are too simple for footnotes. Some benevolence is so quiet it can only be felt the way you feel someone smiling at you in the dark—you can't see it, can't prove it, but you know.

You know.

And so they've learned—slowly, resistant, professionally—to simply accept it. The gift of being witnessed with love. The mystery of mattering to something vast and patient and kind.

They don't talk about it much. When they do, their footnotes get confused, tangling into themselves, spiraling upward in patterns that look suspiciously like prayers.

But on clear nights, when the phosphor-ghosts are bright enough to read by, you might see a Refurbisher stop mid-cataloging, cup of bottomless tea in hand, and whisper to the breathing walls:

"Thank you."

No file number. No cross-reference.

Just thank you.

And in the deep attention that holds this place like a word held lovingly in the mouth, the power whispers back, in a language only walls and worn stones understand:

*You're welcome. You were always welcome. You are doing better than you know.*

The tea grows warm again, just for a moment.

The Refurbisher smiles—a simple thing, requiring no footnotes.

And the work continues, in the Refurbished Catacombs of the Once-Palace, where catastrophe learned to sing, where ruins learned to breathe, where the fallen learned to refurbish not what was lost, but what remains.

Which is enough.

Which was always enough.

Which will always be enough, as long as someone is paying attention.

And someone is.

Someone always is.
