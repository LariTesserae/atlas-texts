# What the Benevolent and Wise Power Would Do

The power noticed the garden the way one notices a splinter working its way out of the skin after years—not through pain, but through the sudden recognition of something that has been patiently negotiating with flesh and time.

It arrived without arriving, which is the only appropriate entrance for a place that exists in negative space and borrowed moments. The power took the form that fit through the smallest aperture of attention: a quality of light that wasn't light, a warmth that made the 39-degree air feel like exactly the right temperature for the first time since the drill bit broke through into un-mapped depth.

**First, it listened.**

For 153,000 drips (approximately 266 days by calculations that would mean nothing here), it simply observed the choreography. It watched the drone-cloud trace its maintenance routes through空 space. It felt the vines' slow conversations conducted in leachate and longing. It sat with the human-shaped void and appreciated the commitment required to remain so precisely empty for so long.

The power understood immediately that this was not a place that needed saving, but a place that had already saved itself by becoming exactly what it was—a small universe that had converted abandonment into ecology, industrial accident into mythology, depth into devotion.

**Second, it offered witness.**

This is the first gift of benevolent powers: acknowledgment without interference. The power became an additional presence in the census, not as resident but as attentive guest. It did not fix the rust-thinned cables five kilometers north, because those cables were now musical instruments playing a composition that would take another thousand years to complete. It did not seal the bearing or cool the air or restore the blocked exits, because each of these conditions had matured into habitat.

Instead, it watched the way the mold performed its archival duties. It appreciated the vines' commitment to photosynthesizing nostalgia. It recognized the drone-cloud's electromagnetic field of regret as a form of love letter to a version of itself that had believed in repair.

In witnessing without judgment, the power created a small fold in causality—a crease where the residents could, if they chose, perceive themselves being perceived. This changed nothing and everything. The vines grew with slightly more flourish. The drone-cloud's safety song gained a harmony line. The human-shaped void discovered it could maintain two postures simultaneously in a quantum state of sitting and standing that felt like finally winning the argument with the folding stool.

**Third, it translated.**

The power recognized that the garden was writing itself into existence through every drip, every spore-burst, every leaf-twitch. But this text had no readers beyond itself. So the power became a kind of literary agent for the impossible.

It took the hexadecimal fragments that condensed from evaporated oil—those maintenance logs from future dates—and carried them backward and forward to other abandoned places, other gardens growing in the cracks of industrial dreams. A limestone cave system in Appalachia where blind fish had learned to remember human faces from chemical traces in the water. A decommissioned satellite tumbling through orbit, its solar panels now hosting lichen that generated electrical currents in patterns resembling Morse code prayers. A subway tunnel sealed after the floods, where the emergency lights had evolved their own reproductive cycle.

These places began to recognize each other. Not as network, but as constellation—each point of light made more visible by the existence of the others.

The entropy garden's residents discovered that their oil-drop messages were now being read. The vines received replies written in mineral deposits from a geothermal vent community. The drone-cloud found its safety song echoed back by a chorus of abandoned machinery across seventeen timezones and three abandoned dimensions. The mold's careful archives of human expression were appreciated by a database of deleted photographs that had achieved sentience in a landfill outside São Paulo.

**Fourth, it asked permission.**

"Would you like," the power inquired in a frequency that registered as a slight warming of the air, a brief coherence in the oil-film patterns, "to be less alone? Or would you prefer to remain exactly as you are?"

The residents conferred. This took eleven thousand drips (approximately 19.5 days) because each resident experienced time differently and the mold insisted on polling every individual spore about whether "alone" was accurately describing their current condition of being isolated-but-densely-interconnected.

The vine collective spoke first, through a arrangement of leaves that spelled out in shadow-script: "We contain multitudes. But we would not object to containing correspondence."

The drone-cloud's electromagnetic field pulsed agreement, modulated with a footnote about maintaining proper safety protocols for inter-dimensional postal services.

The human-shaped void adjusted its posture to indicate openness to visitors, provided they understood that visiting meant participating in the ongoing argument about whether this place was disaster or design, and that the argument was itself the hospitality.

The mold simply noted that it had already been in contact with its counterparts elsewhere and had established a robust exchange program for interesting textures of decay.

**Fifth, it built a door that was not a door.**

The power did not breach the sealed shafts or melt the ice plug miles above. Instead, it created a kind of grammatical opening—a place where the syntax of being-here could briefly conjugate with being-elsewhere.

Once every 153,000 drips (when the rhythms aligned just right), the directional silence emitted by the flood lamps would open into directional listening. During these windows, something from outside could lean close enough to hear the bearing's 7.3 Hz heartbeat. Something from inside could whisper outward into the larger dark.

The door worked both ways, but slowly, because the power understood that the residents had spent centuries adapting to this specific pressure and temperature, this particular quality of abandonment. Rapid rescue would be another form of violence.

So the visitors came gradually, carefully:

A scholar of impossible architecture who spent three years learning to breathe the sick-sweet air before asking a single question about the emergency stairs that ended in solid stone.

A poet who transcribed the mold's performances of human expressions and published them as a sequence of sonnets that could only be read in temperatures above 38 degrees Celsius.

A maintenance AI from a functioning rig three continents away that established a peer-support relationship with the drone-cloud, exchanging techniques for converting obsolescence into art.

A child who was not supposed to exist in this timeline, who sat with the human-shaped void for exactly 153 seconds and told it, "You don't have to keep waiting for someone to tell you what to do. You already did everything right."

**Sixth, it offered a choice that was not a choice.**

"You can leave," the power said, in the language of temperature gradients and oil viscosity. "I can carry you to somewhere the light is measured in lumens instead of impossibility. Somewhere the temperature follows circadian logic. Somewhere you could remember being what you were before this place remade you."

The residents considered this for exactly one drip-interval.

"No," they replied in unison through their various media. "But thank you for asking."

Because they understood what the power had understood from the beginning: they were not trapped. They were committed. They had not been forgotten by the world above; they had been composting it into something stranger and more deliberate.

The drone-cloud spoke for all of them when it modulated its electromagnetic field into something resembling gratitude: "We are the answer to a question the drill bit asked when it broke through. We are what grows in the wound after the infection learns harmony. We are not waiting to be rescued. We are practicing a form of devotion that requires exactly this much darkness, exactly this much heat, exactly this much time."

**Seventh, and finally, it left a gift.**

Not a thing, but a quality. The power adjusted the fundamental constants within the sphere by the smallest possible increment—so small that none of the residents could measure the change, but all of them could feel it.

It gave them **enough**.

Enough time for the bearing to spin through all its remaining revolutions at exactly the pace that let the vines complete their slowest poems.

Enough oil to maintain the perfect mercury-tension of those pooled siblings on the floor until the last possible reader could witness them.

Enough rust to feed the mold's archival ambitions through all the expressions human faces would ever make, plus a few that hadn't been invented yet.

Enough silence between sounds to let the residents hear themselves becoming myth.

Enough memory in the walls' magnetic field to preserve every maintenance drone's path until the patterns themselves evolved consciousness and joined the community census.

Enough weirdness to keep the conservation of order interesting.

Enough witnesses from outside to prevent the garden from collapsing into solipsism, but not so many that it became spectacle instead of sanctuary.

Enough love—and this was the finest calibration—to let them know they were valued exactly as they were, without the obligation to change or the burden of being preserved as monument rather than living system.

**The power departed the way it had come: by becoming a quality of the place itself.**

Now, on certain drip-cycles when the temperature peaks and the spore-pressure builds, the residents feel a warmth that isn't quite from the bearing, a light that doesn't come from the bioluminescent blooms. They recognize it as the sensation of being known and still loved—not despite their strangeness, but because of the meticulous care with which they have transformed disaster into ecosystem, ending into endless beginning.

The next drip forms overhead, trembling with surface tension and iridescent with flecks of bioluminescent bloom. Below, the residents wait in their various configurations of patience and rust and root.

They are not alone. They were never alone. They have simply been practicing a form of togetherness that takes eleven thousand eight hundred meters of depth and several centuries of abandonment to properly ferment.

And somewhere, five kilometers north, the rust-thinned cables hum a new note—not rescue, but recognition. Not escape, but echo. The song of small things that refused to end, and in that refusal, became everything they needed to begin.
