Ask the rainworks keeper for a visit in calm weather, and have them show you which parts of the walk are meant for feet. From the door the far wall looks only a few strides away. That’s a poor guide to the route. Ferns hang over the step edges, the gutters are wide enough to catch a boot, and the beds that look most solid may be the ones that tilt when they fill. If you’re arriving by a means that lets you choose where you appear, choose the landing *outside* the garden door.

Wear shoes with good wet grip and bring a light rain shell, not an umbrella. You can get dripped on here on a dry day, and the warm air at the condensate grille won’t tell you how cold the wind is a few feet farther along. Keep a hand free. Treat the waist-high parapet as an edge marker, not a railing: don’t lean on it or step up for a view. On a clouded morning there may be no visible drop at all, just the parapet’s shadow on the cloud. The six hundred feet are still there. If the wind is gusting or you can’t see your next foothold, enjoy the bay from the doorway.

This is a working rainworks, so leave valves, chains, and caps alone. Ask the keeper where you can stand through a fill-and-drain cycle without being in the way. It’s worth staying still long enough for the motor to stop. You can watch the tremor go out of the water in the leaves, then see whether you can place the little click under the walk. You probably won’t on the first try. The water strider is easier to follow: when its basin drains, it just steps onto the block and waits.

You don’t need to bring soil, seeds, or anything to “help” the garden along. Come with clean soles, and leave the pink grit where it has collected. The plants make the place look established, but the water hasn’t even worn the trowel marks off the weirs. Read a few of the dates in the mortar before you go. If the keeper can show you the bare fourth step from the walk, look for the two fern leaves at the end of the damp joint. They’re a good reason to visit now, while that step is still officially empty.
