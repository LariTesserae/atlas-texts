Visit in a submersible, and plan your first descent around one doorway.

The sea is almost still, but that does not make it forgiving. Find out how deep the pavement lies before choosing a vehicle; the description gives no reliable depth, and a rise gentle enough to cross in half a day may still take you well beyond your vehicle’s rating. Survey from open water during the northern summer if you can. Broken ice lets light in, but it can also drift across your recovery site while you’re down. For a first visit, give up the red algae if seeing it means putting ice between you and the surface.

Bring a trained pilot, a surface crew able to recover you, and a vehicle with independent emergency power, life support, and a means of returning to the surface after a propulsion failure. If your way home is a multiverse crossing, establish it somewhere dry and sheltered. Don’t assume you can open it at depth or that a flooded crossing will stay on its own side.

**Look without landing.** What appears to be a solid square may be a lid over a buried room. The pavement’s wrinkles are clues to what lies beneath it, not promises that it can bear weight. Stay clear of culverts, overhangs, and rooms, even when you can see all the way to the back. Approach the pavement slowly and keep your thrusters off the bottom as much as possible. In that weak a current, the cloud you raise at a doorstep may still be there when you come back, and some of it may settle where it would never have gone on its own.

Use sonar to keep track of the larger shapes and a camera with a light you can aim low across the stone. Straight-on illumination will show you a wall; raking light may show you the blow that didn’t quite finish its edge. Record scale and position with your images. It would be easy to mistake a long embankment for a natural slope, or to join two stretches of pavement on your map that were laid centuries apart. Where white growth traces a buried drain, follow it with the camera rather than scraping it back to see what it covers. Leave the loose tiles where they are, too. A tile small enough to carry may be the only thing that tells you which way a floor fell.

Expect to spend a surprising amount of time waiting for your eyes to make sense of things. There are no fish to give a wall scale, no tracks to show which hollow is sheltered, and below the shallow rim no change of daylight to mark the hours. Set a turnaround time before you descend. If you hear a knock, don’t go looking for its source: it may be miles away. Check your own position and clearance first.

At the doorway, stop outside and light the sill from the side. Look at where the silt ends. Someone raised that lip a little to keep water out, and the sea has long since filled the room. But the sill is still enough to keep the passing silt from spreading across the floor. Give yourself time to see that before you move on. You don’t need to cross the threshold to have visited the place.
