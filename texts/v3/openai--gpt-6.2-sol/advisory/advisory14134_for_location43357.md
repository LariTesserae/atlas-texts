Arrange to arrive at an occupied keeper’s hut, on its inland side. Ask someone to meet you there. A hut with a working dynamo is not necessarily a staffed hut, and the next one may be half a day away. Confirm that you and your host have a way to recognize one another by voice. If you can choose where your crossing opens, choose a dry shelf inside the hut, above the highest water mark your host can show you. Use that as your way home, too.

**Plan to travel without sight.** Nothing in the account establishes whether a lamp you bring will work. Try one from the safety of the hut, but don’t make it part of any essential plan until you know. Even if it works, spend your first outing learning the path by foot and hand. Ask your keeper how they signal *stop*, *step up*, and *take my arm* when the rain is too loud for words. Bring a short probing stick and grippy boots you can feel the ground through. Leave the noise-canceling earbuds at home. You’ll need to hear your guide, and you’ll want to hear what makes them stop talking.

**Pack for warmth and wet, not cold and wet.** A heavy rain suit may leave you as soaked from the inside as from the outside. Bring light, quick-drying clothes, a spare pair of socks and a sleeping layer sealed separately in dry bags, and bland food you can open by touch. Carry drinking water; clear seepage is not necessarily safe, and a drain can carry both seepage and machine water. Ask what the keepers drink. At the end of each walk, sit somewhere dry, get your boots off, and check the skin between your toes. In a place where nothing ever quite dries, do this before your feet give you a reason.

**Learn where water goes before learning where paths go.** A bank can be well above the river you hear and still be part of the river. If you feel shells embedded high in it, all turned the same way, get your guide to show you where the flood goes from there. Don’t treat a ditch plank as a footbridge: the keepers put some of them out for animals precisely when the ditch is about to fill. If you hear a river in a channel your guide says is empty, move to the agreed high ground rather than walking down to check. The gravel ticking in the current is the detail that distinguishes the early river from an echo, according to the keepers. You don’t have to learn to tell them apart on your first visit. You do have to be willing to move while everything underfoot still feels dry.

Stay away from the rim unless a keeper takes you to a designated stopping place. The change in the sound of rain is something to listen for **from inland**, not something to follow until you find it. The brass rings are old work fittings, not visitor anchors. A tremor in the soles followed by warm air from a slot means a clearing may already be starting; back away along the route you came by. Likewise, leave intake towers, lava tubes, and turbine halls to the people who know which ones are live. A stopped pump and an abandoned pump can sound exactly alike.

For a first visit, make one short walk out and back with your host, then stay through what they call morning. Let a herd have the junction if you meet one. The animals are waiting for the last of their number, and you can wait too. Back at the hut, ask for a place to sit where you can hear the canopy begin to empty. At first it may sound as if the rain has simply picked up somewhere upwind. Then it will reach the trees over you. If you’re close enough to the rim to hear the falls, your host may go quiet after the last leaves tip. Wait with them. The water is still falling.
