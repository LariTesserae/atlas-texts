Arrive inside a submersible, not in the water. A mile down, the pressure is roughly 160 atmospheres, and the water may be only a few degrees above freezing. For an ordinary human, this is a visit in shirtsleeves inside a warm, one-atmosphere cabin. Use a crewed submersible rated comfortably beyond the actual depth, with a surface support team. If your way between worlds lets you choose where to arrive, choose the surface. The valley itself is no place to find out how accurately you can pick an arrival point.

**Plan to lose the view.** A floodlight pointed straight out of the viewport will light every flake of mica and scrap of shell between you and the bed. Lights mounted well off to the side, aimed across the ground, will give you a better chance of seeing the ridge itself. Let the pilot navigate by imaging sonar, depth, and acoustic tracking rather than by the window. The hills are so low and rounded that even in clear water you may not notice you’ve crossed one. If the view turns to mud, don’t chase a clear seam: it may be gone before you reach it.

Make the first pass high enough to get a sense of the relief and the current without stirring up the bed. Then pick one ridge and watch it from standoff distance, keeping the thruster wash off the slope. There’s little reason to land. The thin skin of mud can blot out your view even where the bed beneath it is hard, and the bare crest is not necessarily a better place to set down: the sponges are tucked just below it on the sheltered side. Work out your turnaround time in advance, leaving enough battery and life support for a slow departure. A long wait for the water to clear should be something you can afford, not something you need in order to get home.

Dress for sitting still in a cool cabin. Bring a dimmable light for your notes, and give your eyes time to adjust before asking for more light outside. If you want to listen, bring a hydrophone and record during a quiet drift, when the pilot can safely ease off the thrusters. From inside the hull you’re more likely to hear pumps and fans than the tap of a shell.

The best part of the visit may be spending ten minutes looking at the same patch of ground. A white rim appears as the mud thins; a brittle star gets one arm free. Then the whole slope goes out of sight. When it comes back, the ridge looks exactly as it did before, but the sponge is buried again.
