Treat it as a visit to a working utility room, not an abandoned one. The empty rectangle on the floor is waiting for a machine; the main overhead is already carrying hot water. Arrange to go down with someone from the city who knows what is live, whether the room is cleared for entry, and how to get back through the service door if it shuts. If there’s no escort or no reliable way back out, stay at the threshold.

Wear slip-resistant work boots, light long trousers, and whatever protective gear the crew requires. Bring a headlamp: the amber lighting is enough to show the reflection of the stairs, not necessarily enough to show where your boot will land. Ask about hearing protection and agree on a hand signal for leaving; the motor and the main may swallow a spoken warning. Keep the visit short. It’s only thirty paces across, but the heat is there from the moment you open the door, and the humidity makes it harder to cool off. If you feel light-headed, go back upstairs rather than waiting to see if it passes.

Take the stairs slowly and stay on the dry route your escort points out. The paint goes through the water because it marks the site of future equipment, not a walking lane. Don’t steady yourself on a pipe: the unfinished elbows are hot enough to feel on your face in passing. Leave the drum, capped connections, and junction boxes alone. The seep at the rock face may be the temperature of your hand, but that doesn’t tell you what’s in it.

Expect the insects to be more startling than obstructive. You may see only one or two roaches on the way down and then, when the door shuts, discover how many were under the stairs. Give yourself a moment rather than trying to dodge them on a wet tread. Keep your bag zipped and off the floor, and check your boot treads and bag seams upstairs. There’s no reason to carry a passenger into the next city.

If you get a safe place to stand, wait for the motor to stop. The deep sound of the main will still be there, but for a few seconds you’ll be able to tell the drops hitting the drum from the ones falling close beneath the supply line. Look for the glove print on your way out. It’s easy to spend the whole visit looking at your feet and miss the person who left a hand in the wall.
