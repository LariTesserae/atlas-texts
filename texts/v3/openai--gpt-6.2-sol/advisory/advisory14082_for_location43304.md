Visit from inside a pressure hull. If the crew works outside, ask to watch a shift before you even consider joining one. The daylight and the handrails will make the ridge feel more hospitable than it is. At six hundred metres in Earthlike water, the pressure is about sixty times what you’re used to, and the bottom of the drop beyond the third governor is another four kilometres down. Being able to see it does not make it part of the dive.

**Arrange the ride home before you descend.** Ask the crew what happens if the propulsion fails, if they lose the guide line, or if a vehicle is stranded beyond the third governor. A pilot who knows the ridge and a recovery vehicle that can actually reach it are worth more than any equipment you could wear. If your way home is a multiverse crossing rather than an ascent, test it in an unoccupied pressure chamber first. Find out whether it brings the cabin and its air with you. You don't want to learn at depth that it only brings the traveler.

**Learn to talk without sound.** Rehearse with the crew before launch, in the gloves and lighting you’ll actually have. Use text or wired communications, with a few unmistakable signals for *stop*, *look at me*, and *return now*. Don’t make the emergency signal a shout or a pattern of knocks. Ask to hear a recording of the ridge, but don’t expect it to prepare you for the low note coming through your seat and into your jaw. You may find that you can bear the noise perfectly well and still have trouble thinking in it. Plan a short first outing, with a turnaround time someone else is responsible for calling.

**Bring your own sense of scale.** Use a depth readout and an independent navigation reference, and agree on a route that doesn't depend on seeing the next governor. There is no bright patch overhead to tell you which way is up. The far handrail is visible enough to tempt you into judging the distance by eye, and the first stretch of the drop looks close enough to touch. Neither impression is useful. Stay on the surveyed side of the crest. Give the fractured ledges room, too: eighty years of stable survey stakes tell you something about the ridge as a whole, not whether the lip above your window will take a bump.

Leave the governors running and the white colonies undisturbed. Ask which decks are approved for landing rather than assuming three legs and a rail make a dock. Photograph the dial only if the crew can show it to you without disturbing the mechanism. The thumbprint is worth leaving exactly where it is. So is the crack. If you want to know whether it has changed, bring back an image the surveyors can compare with an older one; don’t put a blade in it just because it looks as though one would fit.

For a first visit, I’d ask the pilot to stop short of the third governor and hold at a surveyed overlook. Give your eyes time to find the white bodies beneath the cliff. If a dark animal passes, watch the colony rather than following the animal: the folding mouths will tell you where its shadow is after you’ve lost any useful sense of its speed. Then look across at the bent handrail through a good lens. You can have the full experience of wanting to go over there without going.
