Arrive on a falling tide, with several hours of daylight left. Arrange to meet someone at the freight exchange or another staffed building on higher ground. Ask them which roads stay passable through the next high tide, where you can cool down, and how to get back there if your first route floods. The six steps at the old pump house tell you what once counted as high ground, not what counts now.

**Pack for heat even if the sky looks like rain.** Wear light clothes and closed shoes with good grip on wet concrete. Bring drinking water, a snack, and a waterproof pouch for your map, phone, and whatever you need to get home. A loading canopy is useful in a shower, but don’t count on it as a place to cool off. The yards are so broad that a building can look close and still take a long, hot walk to reach. If you start feeling dizzy or nauseated, turn back to the staffed building rather than resting under a hot canopy and hoping it passes.

**Walk the dry route, not the shortest one.** An empty turning circle is still a turning circle: give freight vehicles room and don’t assume a driver can see you against the glare off the pavement. Don’t wade across a flooded lane. A film of water can hide a drain, a broken edge, or the point where the apron drops into a channel. If water starts to cross your way back, take the higher route while it’s still dry. And leave the service tunnels to the crews. A running pump tells you that water is being moved, not that the space below is safe; a pump going quiet is no invitation to investigate.

For the visit itself, ask whether there’s a public overlook or a safe place to stand clear of the working yards. Give yourself time there. The first thing you’ll notice is how much pavement there is; the second may be that a painted stripe has sprouted legs. You don’t need to pry at a joint or pick up a shell to see what lives beneath the slabs. Watch the edge of the water, especially where it crosses white paint. Then, as the tide falls, look out toward the new mud for birds.

If you stay until the roller doors open at dusk, settle your return route before you settle in to watch. That warm light can make a warehouse look like the nearest refuge, but it may be across three freight lanes and a stretch of pavement you can no longer cross. The city is easiest to appreciate when you’re not asking it for a shortcut home.
