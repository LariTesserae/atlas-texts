Visit the high country as a day trip first. Go with a companion, come up in the morning, and turn back while you still have enough daylight to descend slowly. The plateau will look easier than the climb. In one sense it is: there is almost nothing to step over. But an hour of bracing against gusts takes more out of you than an hour of ordinary walking, and you may not notice how tired you are until you get behind a wall.

**Give the rim a wide berth.** Pick an inland route before you start walking, with enough room that a stumble in any direction would be unremarkable. Don’t use the cliff as a handrail for navigation. The ferns and grass show what the air was doing a moment ago; they don’t tell you what it will do when you cross the next strip of stone. If a gust makes you take an involuntary step, get low and wait rather than trying to walk through it. If you want to watch the evening drops rise, watch from well inland. There is no need to stand over the pool they came from.

**Pack for sun, spray, and flying grit at the same time.** Wear grippy shoes, close-fitting eye protection, and a light windproof rain shell with a hood you can secure. Bring sun protection even if the valley was overcast. Keep straps tucked away, and put anything you’ll need often in a zipped pocket. A rain lane may soak one arm and move on before you’ve got your jacket out; the more useful reason to wear the shell is that it keeps the wind from stripping heat off whatever got wet.

Carry your own water and enough food to eat during a delay. Don’t count on an afternoon shower to refill a bottle, or on a hollow being as full in the morning as it was the night before. The cup in the shelter is evidence that somebody knew how to catch water here, not that the water is safe to drink. On a first visit, leave the local water, plants, and snails alone.

**Work out how to get back when you can’t hear each other.** Agree on simple signals for *stop*, *come back*, and *get low*. Stay close enough to see them without having to turn your back on where you’re putting your feet. Record the ascent exit as soon as you reach the top. Note where your route crosses the quartz and what lies on either side; the white band is a good reference, but half a mile of white band is not a waypoint. Carry a compass and a route sketch even if you have a navigation device, and verify that your instruments work in this world before relying on them. Don’t let a green patch set your pace. On that flat pavement there’s very little to tell you how far away it is.

Treat the old shelters as bonuses, not destinations you have to reach. Check the slabs and any surviving roof from outside before settling in. A sound wall can give you a place to drink and eat without fighting your own sleeves, but a roofless enclosure is not a lightning refuge, and split wood held down by stones is not necessarily a sound roof. If thunderstorms are likely, postpone the visit rather than counting on shelter up there.

The best first stop is somewhere inland where you can sit out of the gusts and still see a stretch of open pavement. Give yourself time to watch a shower cross the quartz. When you stand up again, you’ll have a better measure of the place: not how far you can see, but how far you’d willingly walk back into that wind.
