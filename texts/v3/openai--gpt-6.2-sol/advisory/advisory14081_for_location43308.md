Go to look at the saddle, not to stand on it. Thirty fathoms is about fifty-five metres: a serious depth even in still water, with a sound bottom and a dependable way back up. Here the bottom sheds flakes, the lee pockets move, and a pebble can be swept along at full speed without getting any closer to the far side of a cleft. I would make the first visit by camera, from above and well off to one side.

Choose your lookout by watching what the mountain throws. Some flakes disappear into the deep; others turn up overhead with the light on the wrong side. A spot directly above the saddle is not necessarily out of its reach. Watch several of the great sweeps from a distance before bringing a vehicle closer, and mark where the returning debris first comes into view. If you can’t find a place that stays clear, settle for a distant view. Bring a camera you can bear to lose, and don’t go after it if it vanishes.

For a later visit, use a rated observation submersible with an experienced crew. Keep it clear of the rock: no landing, no mooring to the ridge, no slipping behind a wall to get out of the current. Bring two independent clocks and compare the elapsed time aboard with the time recorded by your surface crew. Agree in advance on a turn-back time. If the clocks disagree, the view changes without the vehicle moving, or the low note starts coming through the seat and into your ribs, leave. You don’t need to wait for a second sign.

The place will offer you small, persuasive reassurances. A warm seam will look like somewhere to rest a hand. The overhang will go dry long enough for you to hear a grain of grit land. A fish will hold still where your pilot can barely keep the ridge in frame. None of those things tells you it’s safe. You don’t know what’s in the vent water, the dry interval is shorter than the time it takes to get out from under the ledge, and you never see where the fish comes out.

Bring a good lens for the limpet. You may spend the whole visit watching its foot come out and feel for purchase, and that would be a good visit. Leave the six walls as you found them. Someone may already have run the experiment you’re most tempted to try: putting one more thing on the saddle and seeing whether it comes back.
