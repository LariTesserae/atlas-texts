Arrive in the morning by the long road. The north gate is a shortcut, not a sensible first entrance: the shelf on the other side is colder, and the threshold doesn’t agree with either street about which way is down. Once you’ve dropped off your luggage, ask someone to walk you through it. Zip your pockets first. For your return journey, confirm the route with someone who has walked it recently. A road here can grow out of use in the most literal sense.

**Bring your own water.** The plateau’s ability to live without it tells you nothing about yours. Find out how the town supplies human visitors before you count on a refill, and carry enough to get back if there isn’t one. Verify that the air and elevation are tolerable for you; being above the dust fronts doesn’t mean you’re above the dust. Bring close-fitting goggles, a particulate mask, and boots with good grip. The pollen is fine enough to sit in the leaf joints and abundant enough to be mistaken for soil. You don’t want your first encounter with it to be a deep breath taken while kneeling down to admire the ground.

Give yourself a morning to learn where to put your feet. The gray flats look like a single surface from standing height. They aren’t. Follow the worn streets and step where your guide steps; don’t test a lifted edge with your weight or put a finger in to see how far it goes. In town, a vein that runs through your room may run through the next three houses as well. Treat the walls as living, shared property: no nails, no carving, no souvenir flakes. If you come across two repairers sitting quietly on either side of a crack, take the long way around. They may be doing something rather delicate without appearing to move at all.

**Spend your first late afternoon in town.** Ask your host which shutters to raise and when. Your instinct will be to shut out the knocking, but closing the room at the wrong moment can leave you with the sound all night. Earplugs are worth packing for the sharp local knocks; don’t expect them to do much about the note coming up through the floor. Watch the grazers from a doorway. When the note deepens, you’ll see why the hunters have waited. It’s a poor time to be out on the flats relying on a companion’s shouted warning, quite apart from what the animals make of visitors.

After dark, take a headlamp and keep to a street you learned by daylight. You can watch the stems open from town; there’s no need to walk toward the red one at the rim. The winged thing is likely to pass before you see it. Let it. If the leaf beneath you lifts and shows a violet line, don’t lean into the opening for a better look. Step back onto the worn path and watch it settle. That small movement under your boot will tell you more about where you are than the view from the rim did when you arrived.
