Make your first visit an out-and-back along the dry half of the service road. If the bench is within an easy walk of your arrival point, make it your destination. There’s more to see from that seat than you’ll have time for, and it’s one of the few places where you can put your weight down without first having to look for eyes.

**Pack for a warm walk with no resupply.** Bring drinking water, sun protection, closed shoes with good grip, a light rain shell, and a headlamp. The water looks fresh, but you have no idea what’s in it or what the machinery does to it. Carry a watch and a way to retrace your route that doesn’t depend on local signals or on the lifting numbers being unique. Photograph junctions facing the way you’ll see them on the return. If you came through a doorway, confirm that you can open it from this side before you lose sight of it.

**Don’t count on the stillness.** The hanging drops, the banking flock, and the animal caught above the weir all suggest that something has interrupted motion. The electrical note in the pump hall suggests that whatever happened hasn’t affected everything alike. Take two photographs of the same droplet several minutes apart if you’re curious, but don’t put a finger under it to see what happens. Even if nothing moves during your whole visit, that tells you very little about what will happen when you lean on a rake or step into a sluice. Stay out of the machinery and off the roofs. At the inspection shaft, look at the samples in the rail; you don’t need to descend to appreciate the depth.

**Look twice before touching anything.** The first look will tell you that a wall is ribbed. The second may tell you where the hind feet of one animal rest on the back of another. Give wall faces, bearings, and sheltered undersides more room than you think they need. Leave the apparently empty shells where they are. On the wet side, shallow doesn’t mean safe: a film of water can make a hard surface slick, and that sharp ceramic curb is exactly what you don’t want to land on. There’s no reason to wade on a first visit.

The hardest thing to prepare for may be the lack of sound. Your own shoe scuff could be louder than a whole visible rain front. After a while you may catch yourself waiting for a wingbeat or for the drop in the shaft to strike something. Set a turnaround alarm before you sit down. Then let yourself sit. The bench is warm; the road stripe is wet on one side and white on the other. Look at the flock directly, then find it upside down in the bead at the end of the seat. You can have a very good visit without ever finding out what happens when that bead reaches the edge.
