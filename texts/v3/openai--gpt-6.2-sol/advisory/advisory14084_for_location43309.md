Arrive at a staffed tram platform, with a return passage you can open from the platform itself. Don’t arrange to leave from an address you haven’t visited yet. Here, a street can take longer to walk on the way back without either end having moved.

**Plan your first day around one district.** From the western platforms you can see enough of the city to mistake visibility for access. The white stair might be perfectly sound and still put you down on the wrong side of a very long walk. Ask the station attendant which routes have been reliable *today*, where you can wait indoors, and how to recognize an out-of-service crossing. Keep a list of staffed stops rather than relying on a map drawn to scale.

**Cross on blue.** Blue tells you the far curb is as far away as it looks. It doesn’t tell you the way is clear, so look before stepping out. Amber is not a shorter opportunity for a person who can move quickly. Watch the beetles go. If the blue doesn’t come, ask for another route rather than trying to time the foundations by ear. The notes overlap, and the doors lifting on their hinges tell you what the street is doing, not what it’s about to do.

Bring hearing protection you can wear for hours, and agree on hand signals if you’re traveling with someone. The foundations are too widely spaced for getting away from the nearest works to be much of a strategy. Book an interior room if you’re staying overnight, and ask to stand in it through a full cycle before you pay. A room can be quieter than the street and still have a bed frame that hums.

Wear grippy, stiff-soled shoes and bring drinking water and a light rain shell. The fresh stone has sharp edges, and what looks like a flat strap across it may be a living root. Step over the roots rather than on them; where they’re too thick to do that easily, take the paved way around. Late in the afternoon, expect slick stone near the rail gutters even if you’ve walked all day without feeling a drop. A bridge isn’t necessarily shelter from rain here.

Spend that dim part of the afternoon in a public garden gallery. It’s a good place to stop trying to get somewhere. You can watch the leaves turn toward the damp and, from a safe distance, see the water gather under the rails. Where the moths hang thickest, give the low branches a wide berth. The animals running under them are hunting, and you don’t want to be the thing they have to dodge.

If you stay for the grazer crossings, ask where people watch from. Pick a place off the open stone and away from the narrow planting strips the herds disappear into. A herd can look loosely scattered and still be moving along a well-used route, with young following adults through gaps you might otherwise take for an invitation to pass. In the morning you can look for the green seeds they left behind. That’s a better time to see exactly where they went, without being in their way.
