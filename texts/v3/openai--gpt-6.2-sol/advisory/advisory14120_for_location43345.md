Go with someone from the shift crew, and arrange the ride back up before the freight lift goes down. Forty paces is a short walk, but it’s the whole street: the stair to the lower market is still a chalk line across the back of a bench. Ask whether the works are due to come fully on during your visit. If the masons are waiting to see what the rock does under load, come back after they’ve had a look.

Wear sturdy boots and trousers with close cuffs. The stone flakes at the foot of the wall are fresh enough to snag a loose hem. Bring water and a small lamp of your own. The work lamps are bright enough to make the white upper windows look inhabited, but there’s nobody behind those panes to notice if the lights go out. Ask your guide what hearing protection to wear and how they’ll signal you to head back. The motors can be running when the street sounds almost still.

The heat at the lift will probably be the first thing you want to get away from. Don’t take that as a reason to hurry farther in. Give yourself a moment to see how you feel, and go back up if you get lightheaded or nauseated. Don’t count on the cool strip beneath the pipes as a place to rest; it’s barely wide enough to stand in, and the bench at the end is too hot to sit on. Keep to the bottom level. The iron stairs may look like part of the street, but they belong to the machinery.

If you’re carrying a parcel, get the address and the name of the person taking it before you descend. There are only two numbers, and you can read both without finding anyone to answer a knock. If you’re only visiting, ask your guide where you can stand out of the way for a minute. Leave the broken tile where it is: someone put it there so a person reaching for the light switch wouldn’t tread on the ferns. If the machinery happens to go quiet, stay put long enough to hear a drop land. Then take the lift back up. You’ll notice the air upstairs more than you did on the way in.
