Make your first visit a short one, well inland, in the local morning. Arrange a way home that you can operate from where you stand. A return point marked with a pile of pebbles is no return point here.

### Before you step out

Check the air with instruments or send an unmanned probe first. Fourteen miles of clear air *below* the lip doesn’t tell you the pressure or oxygen level at the summit. Neither the plants nor the little animal can tell you whether a human can breathe there. Until you have measurements, plan on a pressure-capable suit with an independent air supply, not just an oxygen mask. If you establish that the air is breathable, you’ll still want sealed goggles and good particulate protection. The pale ground is bright enough to make you squint before the dust gets anywhere near you.

Bring all your water, including a reserve for getting delayed. Don’t count on the pale ground being snow, or on the branching marks leading to a spring. Bring gloves and an insulating pad if you plan to sit. After sunset a slab may be much colder than the air, and a smooth dark one is exactly where you’ll be tempted to rest.

### On the summit

Start on exposed bedrock, away from both the northern edge and the paired posts. A half-day’s walk without a rise above your knee is excellent terrain for losing your bearings. Use a navigation system you’ve verified works in this world, and keep a second record of your route tied to distinctive bedrock features. Photograph long outcrops and their junctions, not loose rocks or patches of contrasting grit. A compass or satellite receiver is useful only once you know what it actually does here.

On that first visit, turn back before the usual late-morning disturbances. On a later visit, watch them from a surveyed inland position with your exit ready. Don’t judge the storm by what you feel on your face or hear in your ears. Your sleeve can hang still while a ribbon of dust miles long changes direction overhead. The clear strip between two ribbons is not a passage; it can be gone in seconds. And the fact that the summit looks the same from the next ridge in the morning doesn’t mean your footing, tracks, or gear will be where you left them.

Give the paired posts a wide berth, even if one is broken. The finger’s breadth of clear air beneath a dust column is worth seeing through binoculars. It is not a gap to put your hand in. If you ever stay overnight, do it in a tested shelter well inland, with your gear secured and your exit available from inside. Don’t expect the stars to provide a continuous reference or the storm to announce itself against the walls.

### At the northern lip

Set a turnaround line well back on solid rock while you can still see clearly. Don’t move it to get a better photograph. The summit offers almost no gradual warning that you’re coming to an edge, and the view beyond it gives you very little to judge distance by. Let a camera on a pole take the look you’d otherwise have to lean out for.

Treat the low stone room as an instrument, not a shelter or a surveying aid. Look through the opening from stable footing, without reaching inside or stepping around it to match the view. If you can see the hollow under your feet, that tells you something remarkable about the opening and nothing reliable about the rock supporting you. Leave the swept fan alone.

The animal’s dusk walk is best watched from inland with a long lens. It may be finding purchase on something you can’t see, or doing something your body simply can’t do. Either way, it is not showing you a route. Watch where you put your boots around the black leaves, too: several patches along a crack may be one plant. You don’t need to lift the stone to appreciate that.

The hardest thing to prepare for is how ordinary it all feels at ground level. You can sit in good daylight, with a hand-shaped shadow beside you, and watch half the sky turn over without hearing a thing. Decide where you’ll sit and when you’ll leave *before* you see it. That way you can give it your full attention without having to decide, in the silence, whether it’s time to go.
