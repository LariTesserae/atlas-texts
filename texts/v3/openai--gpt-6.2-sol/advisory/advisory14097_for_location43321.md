Make the first visit a short out-and-back. The valley is easy to see into and hard to judge: the light gives almost no sense of depth or passing time, and a yellow lane that looks like a trail may turn out to be a root on its way to the ceiling.

- **Keep your way home independent of the valley.** Check that you can reopen your crossing from inside before you leave the entrance. Bring a watch, a headlamp despite the light, and an air monitor appropriate for enclosed spaces. Set a turnaround time while you’re still at the threshold. There’s no dusk to remind you, and the point where floor and roof seem to meet is too indistinct to use as a destination.
- **Bring water and dress for heat from below.** Wear light clothing, knee protection for the low entrance, and boots with grippy, insulating soles. Carry all the drinking water you’ll need, plus a reserve. The cups are too sparse to count on, and clear water is not necessarily safe. Don’t wait beneath a bead expecting it to fall; you may spend a surprising amount of your visit there.
- **Walk the broad roots.** On leaf sheets, test your footing before committing your weight and follow the veins where you can. Skip the branches that climb into the vault, and don’t assume the pale stone is good footing just because it’s hard. Photograph forks looking *back* toward the entrance. The return view may bear little resemblance to the one that made the choice seem obvious.
- **Stay in sight of your companion.** Agree on hand signals. A scuff can carry down a straight lane while a person six feet away in the stems can call without being heard. If you lose sight of each other, return to the last agreed fork rather than following a sound.
- **Leave the valley as you found it.** Clean your boots and gear before entering and after leaving. Don’t cut trail marks, collect insects, or put anything in the bowl recess. The walls are evidence that somebody made a place for something here, not evidence that they meant it for visitors.

The best use of a first visit might be to walk as far as the clay walls and sit on the root path for a few minutes. Give the insect time to resume its ticking after your shadow moves away. Notice how little the scene changes while you sit. That stillness is part of what makes the valley worth visiting—and a poor reason to trust your sense of how long you’ve been there.
