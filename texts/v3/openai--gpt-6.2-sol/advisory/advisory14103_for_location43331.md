**Arrange to arrive in the square, and arrange to leave from the square.** It has a broad, level floor and room to turn without catching anything on an eave. The ends of the street are lovely places to visit once you have your bearings, but bad places to discover how much you rely on a horizon. If your means of crossing worlds requires a dry threshold, test it somewhere else first. These forty doors are not forty chances to find air.

Eighteen fathoms is about thirty-three metres. For an ordinary human, this is a cold-water dive near the deep end of recreational diving, not a long swim down from a boat. Go only with the appropriate deep- and cold-water training, a competent buddy and local guide, and surface support. Have the water and surface conditions checked, and plan your gas, no-decompression limits, and ascent before you go. If the point of the visit is to spend an afternoon in the market, take a pressure-rated observation craft instead. You don't want to spend your whole allowance of bottom time getting warm enough and settled enough to look around.

**Dress for standing still, not for the swim down.** The sunshine in the square tells you where the light is good, not where the water is warm. The cold comes over the north summit and is easiest to miss while you're busy. Agree on a signal for getting cold and use it while your hands still work well. Ask about the sea above even if the street looks perfectly calm: the town can sit in almost motionless water while surf is still breaking hard overhead.

Ask your guide where a visitor may put a hand. A worn doorpost may be a customary handhold; a pale stem between two roofs may be next year's wall. Keep your fins up over the gutter, and don't put a finger into a joint to see what lives there. Give the houses the same courtesy you'd give a house on land: don't look through a freshly cleared window just because there's no glass. And don't swim through an open door on the assumption that you can turn around inside. A room can be both a private home and an overhead hazard.

Spend the first minute in the square doing very little. A blow you feel before you hear it, followed by several roofs creaking in turn, needn't mean anything has broken. Ask your guide in advance what sounds are ordinary and what would make them leave. The pouring-gravel noise behind the market is worth finding: once you see the fish at the paving, you'll be able to pick it out from the rest of the town.

If you can make a separate dusk visit, do. Stay in the open and watch one threshold rather than following a light from hole to hole. The square will remain almost empty while the edges of everything become busy. Bring a light for the return, but you may see more of the town's evening life if you don't keep it trained on the walls.

Don't plan to swim down to the older street. The lower stairs are a sight you may be lucky enough to see from above, not the second half of a route. And don't test that new handrail. It has grown to exactly where a person would reach for it, which is what makes it so hard to leave alone.
