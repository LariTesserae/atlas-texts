Pack for a cold, damp hill town, but plan your days around a fact no ordinary hill town has to contend with: a street can be open to sound and still be closed to feet.

Arrive in daylight and book your first night in the settlement where you come in. From the powerhouse you can count all eight settlements, which makes the city look like something you could get across before supper. Ask your host how long it takes to get there *by the route people are using this week*. Carry the current tram and lift timetables on paper, and find out when the last return runs before you take an afternoon trip. Leave yourself a spare day. This is a poor place to make a connection with six minutes to spare.

**Ask for a traveler’s map, not a surveyor’s map.** You want to know which roads have continuous pavement and which crossings are open to pedestrians. At the eastern end of Foundry Street, the bus shelter across the gap is a particularly convincing invitation to keep walking. Stop at the pavement edge. If you’re visiting the people on the far side, ask where to board the freight lift and whether you need someone to meet you. On a wet evening you may hear a car cross the missing stretch. That’s worth standing still to listen to; it isn’t a reason to take a step.

Bring boots that grip wet stone, a hooded shell, warm layers, a headlamp, and more dry socks than seems reasonable. Keep one change of clothes sealed in your bag. The handrails are wet underneath even when there’s been no rain to speak of, and a jacket you hang up in the evening may still be clammy in the morning. Give yourself an easy first day at altitude, especially if you’ve come from sea level. The cable stations will still be there tomorrow, and there’s no advantage in discovering halfway up a steep lane that you’ve overdone it.

Ask when the rolling mill starts. Have earplugs handy if you’re staying close to it, but don’t wear them on tram platforms or in loading bays. The first blow can make you start even indoors. If you’re holding a cup, put it down and feel the sound come back through the floor. Afterward, when the valley goes quiet enough to hear claws on a zinc roof, you’ll understand why people leave a pause in a conversation instead of trying to talk over the mill.

For a first outing, take a tram somewhere you can get lunch and come back without changing lines. Watch for hares in the ballast from the carriage, not from the track. Leave the fern-covered maintenance walks to the crews, and give the goats the lift ramp when they’re coming down in file. Late in the day, find a public spot with a view of the roost. The windows may already look black when the starlings turn and catch the sun. It’s a good thing to see early in your visit, before you start thinking of the dimness as bad weather.

If you go up to the municipal stair, take a sandwich and sit where the children sit, back from the last tread. You don’t need to lean out to appreciate seven missing steps. And if you want to hear the stream, go on a Thursday and ask the bridge keeper where you can stand. This year you may not need to kneel. Listen for the stones, then go back while you can still see the path. The keeper will be there next Thursday whether the water has come into view or not.
