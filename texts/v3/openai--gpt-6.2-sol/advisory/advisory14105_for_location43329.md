Visit in daylight, after a dry spell if you can. Ask whoever lets you through the gate two things: where visitors are welcome, and what tells people it’s time to leave. Learn the way back to the gate before you go looking for the pump house. You can see clear across the ward in the right season, but seeing where you want to go is no guarantee there’s a dry path to it.

Wear closed, grippy shoes you don’t mind getting wet. Bring drinking water, a light rain layer, and a dry pouch for whatever gets you home. The puddles are clear enough to invite inspection, but the paving under them has settled unevenly. A green patch may be a mat over a drain rather than solid ground. Use the paths people keep open with shears, and don’t leave your bag in one of the gaps: that gap is somebody’s front door, or the only place the washing gets sun. If you stay overnight, ask for a hook indoors. Even the handles of a handcart are wet by morning.

The pump’s normal work leaves plenty of water in the ward. A puddle isn’t an emergency, and a pause in the pump isn’t necessarily one either. Ask a resident how to read the water mark on the brickwork. If water is rising over the path you came in on, head back while you can still see your footing. Leave the drain grates and electrical equipment alone. At the mat works, wait to be invited inside and take earplugs if you’re offered a tour. Save your questions for outside; there’s a reason people stop talking as they pass the doors.

Leave time to stand in the narrow quiet place between the works and the pump house. On a dry day, you can hear water ticking off the awning there. After the last shift, if you’re still welcome to linger, watch the heap of reed trimmings from a little way off. The bird seems to prefer an empty yard. And on your way out, look for the iron rings along the wall. They’re an old path’s worth of height below where you’d expect them to be. The ward changes slowly enough that no one has bothered to move them, and fast enough that a green tip you didn’t see yesterday can catch your eye today.
