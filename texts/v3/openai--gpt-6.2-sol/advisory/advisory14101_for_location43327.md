Go with someone who walks the basin every day. Ask them to meet you on the ridge, where you can see the town you’re aiming for. The view will make the way look obvious. It isn’t: two stairs can cross without meeting, and a level walk can take you past the roof of the house whose door you’re looking for. Ask for directions by landings and handrails, not by distance. “Down to the warm grille, then left at the enamel tub” is something you can use.

Wear boots with good grip on smooth metal and bring sunglasses. The light is strong enough to tire your eyes even in the shade, and a single wet seam may be all the warning you get that a landing is slick. Pack water and lunch rather than counting on finding either between the ridge and town. The saucers under the pipe joints are for drips and finches. Bring a warm layer for standing about: the exhaust grille may be pleasantly warm, but don’t take that as a promise about the next terrace. A small pack you can keep close to your body is better than a shoulder bag that swings when you turn.

The main thing to learn is that **quiet does not mean stopped**. A walk can feel as still as an empty station while the weights beside it are moving. Stay on the public side of the railings, and don’t put a hand through a gap to steady yourself. Let the hare have its shortcut. It knows where its hips end and how long the weight takes to come down; you know neither. If you want to see the bolts at the bottom of the old inspection shaft, ask whether there’s a place visitors can look from. There’s no need to lean out for a better view of what isn’t there.

Allow a whole afternoon for the first crossing, with no appointment on the far side. On the bridge by the warm grille, walk at your usual pace. If the middle weld takes a long time to come up, don’t speed up to make the crossing agree with what you saw from the ridge. If you’re walking with someone, cross together rather than sending one person ahead to see how much farther it is. On your first day it’s worth coming back by the same route. You’ll recognize the chipped weld; you needn’t expect to reach it in the same number of steps.

Before you set out, ask which columns are carrying the country and whether a transfer is in progress. If it is, get that day’s route from someone who has actually walked it. Don’t assume a familiar stair still meets its landing neatly. And if a drop hangs just above an empty saucer, leave it alone. Find a place to stand where you won’t block the walk and wait a little. A finch may come and show you why nobody has moved the saucer.
