Pack for a warm, wet place, but prepare as you would for a town whose map was printed before the bridges went in. The city is only nine days old. By the time you arrive, a stairway that went nowhere yesterday may be somebody’s front entrance. Ask before you follow anyone up it.

**Arrive in the morning, with a room already arranged.** Ask your host for the address *and* something to recognize from the street: a blue towel on a rail, a chair turned backward on a balcony. A building can have several addresses and only one household. From the middle of those avenues, you’ll have a long walk if you choose the wrong door. Have your host name a second meeting place on finished pavement in case the first one has become awkward to reach.

**Bring shoes that grip wet paving, a small flashlight, and more water than you think you’ll need.** Wear light clothes that dry quickly and carry a spare pair of socks. The dim sky doesn’t mean it’s cool, and a little rain can leave a shallow puddle in your way for hours. The showers off the tram wires before dawn are worth hearing from a window; the bowls people set out tell you how little water you can expect to collect. Bring something to eat, too. There is bread, but getting it depends on when the green basket comes down.

**Use the routes an ordinary body can use.** The yellow cloth marks sharp edges; it doesn’t make them safe to lean on. A stair can be sound all the way to the top and still have no landing. Pay for your loaf from the pavement. If the baker steps out of her second-floor window and puts her foot down beside you, take her advice about where to look as a description of what works for *her*. Don’t try it. Likewise, a tram bell is a reason to look up, not a reason to step off the curb. Wait until you see the car. If you need to be somewhere at a particular hour, ask which stops have actually had a tram that day.

Stay on the upper walks near the river and the drainage courts, especially toward evening. A dry grate is no promise that a court will stay dry; the water comes from beneath it, quietly. If a heron turns up, watch from where the neighbors watch. You’ll see what it came for without making it leave.

**Save the eastern woods for a second day.** The surveyors count their stakes every morning for good reason. Ask one of them whether the brick path has been walked since the last rain, and if you can, go with someone who knows both ends. Don’t take a stake for a boundary just because it was one yesterday. The hollowed bricks are the route to follow, not a guarantee about how far you’ve come. If you reach the cistern and your companion is unsure of the way onward, go back together. There’s no need to make the first visit the one on which you find out how much larger the woods can get.

Leave some time unplanned. You can spend a pleasant morning waiting for bread and learning which windows open first. If you wake before dawn, listen to the water dropping from the wires a block at a time. Later, when you pass the yellow cloth again, look at the little leaves coming through it. The most useful question here is often not “How old is that?” but “Was that there yesterday?”
