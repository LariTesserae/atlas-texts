Arrive in the morning, with dry socks in a waterproof bag and a place to sleep already agreed upon. From across the ravine you can see nearly every roof; once you’re among them, you may not be able to see the house next door. Ask your host to meet you at the entrance to town. On the way in, learn the route by doorways and corners rather than by which stairs are dry. Before you go out again, ask which way the water is running now.

Dress for a day spent *almost* getting wet. A hood matters even when the sky is clear: some of the roofs will drip directly down your collar. Bring gloves you can grip wet stone in, boots with good wet-rock traction, and removable ice grips. Keep one set of clothes dry for sleeping. Don’t count on being able to dry the other set by the fire; fuel is dear, and there may barely be room for the cook beside it. Bring sunglasses and wear them above the roofs. Take them off when you duck inside, and give your eyes a moment to find the floor. A small headlamp will earn its keep in rooms where the ceiling is brilliantly lit and the threshold is invisible.

On the stairs, look at the next tread, not the flight as a whole. The deep hollow is where generations of feet have gone; the shallow one is where the water usually goes. Neither tells you where to put your foot *today*. If a stair starts carrying water, backtrack rather than trying to keep to its glazed margin. In a sudden hailstorm, wait under cover with your host’s leave. When the sun comes out, check the footing again. A goat stepping out onto a newly exposed ledge is evidence that the ledge will hold a goat.

Bring food that doesn’t need much cooking, and ask before drawing drinking water. There’s water everywhere, but that doesn’t make every gutter a tap. Ask your host what would be useful to bring from below; a little of something they actually need is better than a bulky gift they have to find room for. Handle borrowed bowls with both hands. If someone serves you in a bowl stitched with copper, don’t ask for a better one.

Go to the meeting-house early enough to catch the cistern’s ripples on the ceiling. Then, if someone is going up to the blue pool, ask to walk with them. The pool is where people go to find out what needs seeing to. If it shows an unfamiliar roof, don’t set off to find the roof yourself, and don’t lift a drain slab to see whether the fish have come. You can be useful without knowing what the pool is doing: offer to carry a lamp or help lift a flour bin. Let the people who know the house decide whether the cellar door ought to stay open.

If you came through a crossing you’ll need to use again, don’t leave it in a sealed passage or a room that merely *looks* as though it has never flooded. Keep your return kit packed and above floor level. The lesson of the cellar isn’t that you should be afraid of warm rain indoors. It’s that the people here knew how high to put the flour, and you don’t.
