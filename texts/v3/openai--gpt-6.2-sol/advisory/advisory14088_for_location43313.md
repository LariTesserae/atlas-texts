Arrange to come in on the freight lift with the maintenance crew. Ask for a gallery visit, and have someone show you the way back to the lift before you go any farther. From the gallery you can see the trays fill and empty, pick out the green in the silt baskets, and watch the beads travel along the ceiling. There’s no need to go down onto the enamel. The raised seams look like paths when they’re dry, but they’re only dry between cycles. Half a mile is a long way to be from a stair when the pumps turn over.

Dress for standing still in moving, near-freezing air. Wear a windproof outer layer, warm gloves you can close around a rail, and waterproof work boots with good wet grip. The felt on the rails is there to spare bare hands from cold metal; don’t count on it being dry. Take a helmet and headlamp even if you mean to stay under the working lamps. The lamps light the trays much better than they light the stairs, and it takes a moment to notice how much of the apparent light below you is reflection. Bring hearing protection and agree on a few hand signals with your guide before you leave the lift. You won’t be able to finish a question over the answering gate.

At the south end, ask your guide where to stand *before* the first roar. You may hear water come in, run out, and taper to a trickle while the tray below still has dry footprints in it. It’s the end of the sound that’s most likely to fool you: everything in you says the event is over. Stay where you are. The crew knows the cold that comes ahead of the actual water, but by the time you feel it in your teeth, you should already be somewhere you can watch the fill without moving.

Give yourself a full pump cycle in one spot. On the first pass you’ll mostly hear machinery. On the next, you may be able to separate the stroke you feel through your boots from the gate you hear across the valley, or pick out the single high note of a hook rounding its wheel. As a tray empties, watch the screw heads for a shrimp; it’s easier to see by what it does than by what it looks like. Turn sideways at the mineral lip if your guide takes you down that stair. The crew has been doing that rather than breaking it off.

Make the last working lamp your turnaround point unless the crew takes you farther. The works beyond it are unfinished, not abandoned; the darkness doesn’t mean the water stops there. You can stand in the light and listen. When the chains change speed, there’s a brief chance to hear an unused bolt hole fill, spill, and start filling again.
