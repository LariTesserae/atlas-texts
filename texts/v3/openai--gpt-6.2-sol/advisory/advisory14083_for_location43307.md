Go in the morning, and make your first visit a short one. The basin is the size of a chapel, which makes it look as if you could learn your way around it in a dozen steps. You probably can. What you can’t learn that fast is where the next step will be safe.

**Dress for both sides of the entrance.** Wear boots with grippy soles and enough stiffness to protect your feet on sharp stone. Bring a rain shell for the approach, then take it off once you’re inside if you’re getting hot. Carry drinking water, a sun hat, and sunglasses: the glare off the wet slabs is bad enough that you may miss a hairline crack right where you’re about to put your foot. Keep your hands free. A slab that looks dry may just be one the water hasn’t reached again, and a wet handprint disappearing tells you more about the heat than about the footing.

**Make sure you can leave before you go in.** If your way home is a door or a jump point, put it on stable ground on the rain side, well back from the drop. If you’ve brought a companion, have them wait there on the first visit. Stop at the entrance step and look back from inside: learn what the exit looks like through glare, not just what the entrance looks like through rain. You can enjoy the trick of holding one hand in each kind of weather without leaning out over the lip.

**Take the step and the bench as evidence of a previous visitor, not as a promise.** The cool gray material has outlasted at least two fractures in the floor. That doesn’t tell you what will happen if you sit on it, and the floating chip doesn’t tell you what will happen if you fall. Don’t touch the fragment or wedge anything into the gap. Watch the water fall upward from where both your feet have ordinary weight. Likewise, don’t lean on the ferns: some of those pale roots are lying bare across the rock, and a clump that looks substantial from above may give way under a hand.

Give yourself time to stand still. The little brown animal is easier to see if you aren’t trying to follow it. Watch for the dry footprints, and when the ticks come, look at the tracks rather than toward the sound. The crossing is already over. That’s a good habit elsewhere in the basin, too: don’t use silence as proof that nothing has moved.

On the first visit, **be back at the entrance before the evening rise**. If you want to stay for it on a later visit, watch from the rain side, well back, and only if you can see the deepest seam from there. The pane stands quietly long enough to make waiting beside it seem reasonable. But once it leans, the basin goes from a collection of thin films and little channels to a warm rush across the floor. It carries grit over the lip. You don’t want to find out which route it takes by having to cross it.

Bring back a sketch if you like. Mark where the fern tips meet the pane, or draw the hanging chip twice to show how slowly it turns. Leave the stone, roots, and water where they are. The best thing to bring home is the memory of those six or seven ticks arriving after the animal has gone.
