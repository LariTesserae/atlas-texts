Come in the last hour before dawn, if you can. You’ll have the best chance of seeing the copper-backed leaves turn and the first beads of water gather on them. Don’t mistake the hillside cooling for the room cooling, though. If the rail is warm at the hatch, it’s already telling you something the air outside won’t.

**Treat the hatch as the entrance to a damaged utility chamber, not a greenhouse.** The rock has moved recently enough that the broken edges are still sharp, and the return line is still carrying water. Before going in, find out who maintains the line and whether anyone has inspected the chamber since the floor shifted. Have someone qualified check the structure and test the air; a quiet room full of healthy leaves isn’t evidence that it’s safe to breathe. If you can’t get those checks, make the hatch the end of your visit. Don’t descend alone, and have someone stay outside who knows when you’re due back.

Bring a headlamp and a spare light, boots with good grip, and water to drink outside. Keep one hand free on the steps. The amber lamp is a poor guide to the floor: you can be almost under it before you realize it’s on. Once down, look back and fix the route to the steps in your mind. Stay on the sound side of the split. Don’t put your weight on the tilted slab, even if it looks settled, and leave the gauge, drain cock, and pipe alone. The water is moving whether or not you can hear it.

Make the first visit short. In that heat, sweat can run down your arms without doing much to cool you. Turn back while the climb still feels easy; if you start feeling light-headed, nauseated, or unusually tired, leave at once. Don’t plan to spend the night below waiting for the leaves to turn. Wait on the hillside and go down near the end, if the chamber is safe to enter.

At the far wall, resist the invitation to put a finger behind the new leaf. It hasn’t hardened, and the gap is the newest break in the room. A brief light from the side should let you see the older leaves pressed against the rock without moving anything. That’s the detail I’d go for. The leaves under the lamp can tell you where the light is; these have been growing into a place the light has never reached. You don’t need to peel one back to bring that sight home.
