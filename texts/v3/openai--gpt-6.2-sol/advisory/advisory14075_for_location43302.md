Arrange to visit at noon, and ask the washhouse keeper to lift the tile for you. Go first as a spectator. The opening makes the room look like a shallow basin, but the stair doubles back out of sight and its bottom tread is underwater, a little below head height. There’s no pocket of air at the far end to come up into. For an ordinary traveler, the view from the opening is the visit; don’t make it a breath-hold dive.

Bring a small waterproof light, shoes that grip wet tile, and a dry cloth for your knees. Have the keeper show you where to kneel and make sure the lifted tile is secure. The feeder is loud enough that you shouldn’t count on being heard from inside. If anyone offers to take you down the stairs, ask whether the room can be drained and the inflow shut off first. Lowering the gate in the evening quiets the feeder, but it doesn’t empty the chamber. Don’t test the pull at the outlet slots with a hand or foot, and leave the little fan of sand alone: it’s material the water has carried out of a joint. Point it out to the keeper if they haven’t noticed it.

At noon, leave your light off at first. Give your eyes time to pick out the pink clay under the white slip and the shadow each tread lays on the next. Ask someone in the yard to set a bucket in the settling pool, then lift it again. From above it’s an ordinary bit of washhouse work; from the hatch it changes the apparent size of the room. Follow a loose green thread if you’re lucky enough to see one. It takes its time crossing the wall even while the feeder makes the whole place sound in a hurry.

Come back at dusk if the keeper doesn’t mind. Stand clear while they lower the gate. The knock stops before the light goes, and the water stays where it is. That’s worth seeing from above: a room that can go quiet and dark in front of you without getting any shallower.
