Pack for a short mountain walk in wet weather, even if all you mean to do is cross the town and buy lunch. Wear shoes with soles that grip wet wood, bring a warm layer and dry socks, and keep your hands free. A small pack is better than a shoulder bag on the stairs. Carry drinking water: the channels look clear, but they run through a town full of ducks, fish baskets, and busy kitchens. Ask at the bakehouse where people fill their cups.

**Look at the weather uphill.** The rain that matters may fall where you can’t see it. The shower over the ridge can be over before the water reaches the market. When the note under the planks deepens, stop somewhere out of the way and see what the stallholders do. If someone pulls the pin on a bridge, let them swing it in. Don’t try to get across ahead of them, and don’t go down to the water to help with a branch unless asked. The people doing those jobs know which steps will still be there under the spray.

Before you descend into the market, pick out the far-bank overhang and the stair through the alders. Ask someone whether the high path is open and which way to go if the bridges are drawn in. Fifty paces across is a misleading measure here: the way back may be on a different level from the way in. If your means of getting home depends on returning to a precise spot, choose one on the mountain trail above town, not on a bridge or a scrap of beach. Even on an uneventful visit, the beach may not be there when you come back.

Walk at the pace of someone carrying a full bowl. Give people room at the ends of bridges, and don’t assume that a rail continues to be a rail just because it began as one. It may turn into somebody’s counter. A dry-looking landing may be a potter’s yard. Ask before taking a shortcut through either. If you need to ask directions, do it away from the drop under the market; you may hear the answer perfectly well two stalls farther up.

Leave yourself time to sit. From the entrance it looks as though you ought to be able to see the whole town, but most of what’s happening is below your line of sight. Get something hot and find a place the stallholder is happy for you to stand. Watch how people make room for a laden neighbor, a carpenter with a rung, or a swallow coming through low. After a while you’ll be able to pick out those small sounds and movements against the water. That’s worth staying for. Just leave enough daylight to take the high stair at your own pace.
