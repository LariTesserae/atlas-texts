Pack for a cold, wet walk. Plan your route as if you were taking a small boat out: know how you’ll get back, and don’t count on being able to walk straight to shore.

**Make your first visit from the alder margin.** Arrive early, after a period of steady weather, with a companion and an exit on unmistakably firm ground. Leave your route and a turnaround time with someone who’ll remain there. If you travel by portal, set the return point on that same dry ground, not on a tussock that happens to be dry when you arrive. In country this flat, an inch of rising water can take away a path without making anything look particularly flooded.

Wear waterproof boots with good grip, rain gear you can walk in without overheating, and warm layers that still insulate when damp. Bring a dry change in a sealed bag, drinking water, a headlamp, a first-aid kit, and a walking pole. You’ll want the pole for the ordinary hazards: branches that roll underfoot, sedge mats that give way, and clay that offers almost no purchase once you’ve stepped down onto it. Probe the next patch of ground from a stance you can retreat from. If the footing is doubtful, don’t test it with your weight. Don’t wade the river.

**Treat the double water as a boundary, not a crossing.** The two surfaces may be close enough for roots and silt to pass between them, but that tells you nothing about what will happen to a boot or a hand. If a stone appears in two places and both images cast shadows, don’t use either image to judge depth or footing. Watch from the bank with binoculars. Likewise, you already know that touching a beating pool stops it and that the missed blows come back in a rush. There’s no need to find out what happens when you interrupt a larger pool, or when your boot takes longer to pull free than your finger. Leave the moss rims intact; you can read the visible waterlines without peeling them back.

Pick out several landmarks on the way in and turn around to see what they look like on the way out. A split alder crown will serve you better than the edge of a pool. Carry a compass and an offline map if one exists, but don’t assume a mapped channel is still where it was last spring. The traveling silence might help you follow the river from a distance. It’s a poor thing to steer by: it moves, and you can interrupt the sound without moving the river at all.

The best first visit would probably be a short walk and a long sit. Bring something dry to sit on and give yourself enough time for the rain to stop and the trees to go on dripping. From a firm patch among the alders, you might watch a heron bring up one fish from a strike that sends out two sets of ripples. That’s worth the trip on its own. Head back while you can still tell a living reed from a dead one at a glance; by the time you need the headlamp, you’ll want to be on ground you already know.
