Plan to visit at noon, but find your footing before then. The cleft is a white seam from below, and the moment it becomes interesting is exactly when you’ll be tempted to lean in. Pick a stance you can hold without bracing a hand against the lip. Bring water, sun protection, a helmet, and whatever gear the approach itself warrants. If the only way to see in is to hang over the opening, settle for seeing the seam.

Bring a small camera with a zoom and a notebook. Leave the sample bags at home. The basin has never had so much as a proper gust through it; your sleeve brushing the rim might be the biggest weather event it has ever experienced. Keep your breath back from the opening and don’t steady the camera on the rock. The heat smells like bruised leaves, but smell won’t tell you whether the air coming out is safe to breathe.

Start with things you can count: four plants, four shadows, four threads. If you hear the click, note whether you saw the leaves turn or only noticed afterward that they had turned. Don’t touch a thread to find out whether it’s live, and don’t lower anything past the lip to find out how deep the basin goes. The two glints are worth seeing precisely because they make such an ordinary thing as judging distance stop working. You don’t need to get closer for that.

Save some time for the walk back down. Mark a safe place on the approach where you can see the face to the left of the cleft, and wait there in the late afternoon if you have enough daylight. Sketch the shadow before it grows its second lobe. Then leave without going back up to check whether the leaf has caught up. You came to see a mountain drawing wire; you may go home thinking instead about a little patch of shade that had no business being there.
