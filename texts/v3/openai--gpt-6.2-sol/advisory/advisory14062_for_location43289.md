Visit the basin from the surface first. Ask the pump crew to take you out at noon, when you can see the long blue ridges and the shadows of gulls crossing them. Eight miles is an easy distance for the eye here. You won’t feel how far it is until the boat has been running for twenty minutes and you’re still looking at the same seam.

**Plan your visit around the gates, not the daylight.** Get a current operating chart and speak to whoever is on shift. Ask which reaches are open to boats, when they’ll close, and where you can land if the return route is cut off. The evening shutdown is not a slack tide: closing a gate uphill can set a reach moving that has been still all day. The freight-train sound is worth hearing from a safe berth, but it isn’t a warning you can navigate by. You may hear a gate you can’t see, and you won’t know from the sound alone which way the water will go.

Take a locally operated boat with a radio, a proper cold-water life jacket, and clothing chosen for immersion rather than the temperature on deck. Keep a dry change and a hot drink aboard. The temptation will be to trail a hand over the side in the beautiful clear water; do it briefly and you’ll understand why you dressed that way. Give intakes and gates their full exclusion zones. Don’t use the little steep waves to judge where an intake’s pull begins. By the time the surface shows you anything, you’re already looking at water in motion.

**Don’t make your first visit a swim.** A ridge a few fathoms down looks as inviting as the bottom of a pool, but there’s nowhere to stand up, get warm, or walk home. A grate with kelp on it is not a handhold, and a joint wide enough for a seal is not a passage for a person. If the operators offer an escorted dive in a reach formally isolated from flows, that’s a different outing, for trained cold-water divers. Otherwise, lower a camera from an approved observation point. It will show you the board grain in the concrete and the crabs coming back to their squares after your shadow has passed.

Bring binoculars. They’re as useful for reading a joint number as for watching the seals. And give yourself time to sit somewhere out of the traffic and listen. The pump note will disappear from your attention long before it disappears from the basin. When it finally stops, you may hear bolts ticking in their collars and think the place has gone quiet. That’s a good moment to stay exactly where the crew put you. Somewhere beyond the next ridge, the water may just be starting to move.
