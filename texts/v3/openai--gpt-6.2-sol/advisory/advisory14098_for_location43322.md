Bring your own air and your own way home. Do not count on a doorway for either.

The best first visit would be a short one, on the largest square in the morning, with your return crossing in the open and an attendant on the home side. You could spend the whole visit there. From the square you can see the shadows of the mountains on the underside of the sky band, and watch the band travel without taking the shadows with it. You don’t need to go to the parapet to see the most remarkable thing in the city.

**Before you cross.** Find out what the air can support with an instrument, not a breath. If you can’t test it from the home side, go in with a pressure-capable suit, independent breathing gas, and a reserve sufficient to get you out at a slow walk. The description gives you thin air, not a reliable estimate of how thin. Take cold-weather layers, a sealed visor, gloves you can work in, and boots that grip powdered stone. Carry water. The absence of frost and damp is a reason not to expect any there, not a reason to leave yours behind.

More important than the suit is the arrangement for leaving. If your means of crossing can bring you home directly, test it from just inside the city before going farther. If it requires returning to a fixed opening, keep that opening in sight and have your attendant confirm it from the other side. A chalk mark will tell you that you have found the same piece of stone; it won’t tell you that the stone still leads to the same place. If the crossing can’t be checked or held open, watch from home.

**On foot.** Read the city as terrain, not as architecture. The worn public steps, the benches, the little recesses just inside the doors: all suggest a place where other people have already worked out how to get around. Here, even the wear can mislead you. The old hollows remain when new treads appear between them. A count made on the way down is an observation, not a promise about the way back. Make your first turnaround while you can still see the whole route, not when a timer tells you that you’ve used half your air.

Agree on hand signals and a lost-contact rule. Don’t rely on a shout reaching the next court. If a dust front begins erasing buildings across the valley, head for the crossing while your route is still visible. Don’t duck into a house to wait it out. A room may be perfectly still while its door changes what it opens onto. The dust is dangerous less for what it does to the room than for what it does to the way out.

Give the cliff landing a wide berth. That parapet looks exactly high enough to lean on, which is a good reason to decide in advance not to lean on it. Don’t drop anything to judge the depth, either. You already know the bottom need not be beneath you. Give the gray animals much the same courtesy you give the edge: watch from a distance, and don’t put a finger into a joint to see what they’re finding. An animal that lies still for an hour is easy to forget when you turn to look at something else.

**Leave before the roofs go dark.** The evening view through the narrow house would be the thing I’d most want to see, and the thing I’d plan a separate visit around. It offers an unusually persuasive invitation: a lit room, another square, steps that look as if people have used them for years. If you ever do watch it, watch from the street with your return already in hand. Pay attention to the dust at the foot of the far stairs. It keeps going when you can no longer see where it goes. You should be on your way home before you’re tempted to follow it.
