Arrange to meet the attendant **on the road**, not at the pump-house door. From outside, it looks like a little grove you could cross in a minute. From inside, it’s a series of places where you can put one foot, or turn one shoulder, or let another person pass if one of you backs up first. Ask him to show you where he walks *now*. The old handrail posts still look like directions, but they belong to a path that isn’t there anymore.

Wear grippy boots you don’t mind getting wet, long trousers, and a light layer you can take off at the road. It’s warm under the leaves. Leave the big pack behind; a small bag held close to your body won’t catch on everything when you turn sideways. Bring clean boots rather than carrying soil from your last world into this one. Bring drinking water, too. The discharge looks clear, but nothing about its clarity tells you what’s in it, and the little basin under the gutter is better watched than dipped into. A headlamp will help if you want to see the old chalk mark from the entrance, though in the afternoon you probably won’t need it for walking.

Put your weight on the broad crowns of the roots, where years of feet have flattened them. The pale edges are new growth, not extra footing. Don’t grab the iron posts to steady yourself unless the attendant says they’ll hold, and don’t duck off the path to investigate the green door. It really is a door, and it really is opening, but the step beneath it no longer takes you there. If you need a hand free just to get in, you brought too much.

Bring earplugs if you’re going to spend time beside the motor. Have your conversations on the road or come around to the attendant’s side of the house; the machinery will swallow a shouted question at exactly the moment you want an answer. The check valve’s clack and the answering rattle of the roof are ordinary sounds here. So is the needle’s position just short of the red line. Look at the photograph if you’re curious, but leave the valves and switches to the person who knows the pump. Don’t ask him to shut it off just so you can hear the hollow.

If your visit happens to coincide with a bearing check, stay where you are when the motor goes quiet. The leaves won’t lift immediately. You may think you’ve missed it, and then the first row by the door will come up. Listen for the wet-rope sound at the roadward edge. You can hear it without putting a hand into the gap. On your way out, crouch on the road and look back for last summer’s chalk. It’s an unusually good thing for a multiverse traveler to see: a mark made to tell someone where the ground stops, still perfectly legible after the ground has turned it underneath itself.
