Pack for a cold, dry hike on a sunlit plateau. The hardest thing to believe, once you’re there, will be that a place with that much water can leave you thirsty.

**Make the first visit a short out-and-back.** Arrive early, well inland, and mark your arrival point before you walk. If your way home is a fixed crossing, save its coordinates if your instruments work there, but also take bearings to nearby outcrops and photograph the approach from several directions. Use the far notch to orient yourself, not to judge how far you’ve walked. Pick a turnaround time and set a vibrating alarm. Six or seven miles of open ground looks like a morning’s stroll until you’ve walked half of it and the far edge hasn’t visibly moved.

**Bring all your water.** Carry enough for the planned walk and a delay. The wet seams and hanging beads are evidence of moisture, not a supply. Leave the rain pans to the animals; even if you can treat the water, there may not be enough to collect. Wear boots that grip wet stone, layers that still insulate when damp, and a windproof shell. Bring sunglasses and sun protection: cool air won’t do much about glare off pale rock. A dry sit pad will make lunch considerably more pleasant.

**Agree on how to talk before you arrive.** Go with a companion if you can, but don’t count on hearing them from even a pace away. Use simple hand signals for *stop*, *come back*, and *look at me*, and agree that if you lose sight of each other, you both stop rather than fan out to search. Bring a slate or a phone for anything more complicated. Don’t count on a whistle, an audible alarm, or a shout to warn you of trouble. If the roar is uncomfortable, use hearing protection; you aren’t giving up much useful information by putting it in.

**Give the rim a wide berth.** The rounded lip may make the transition from level ground to drop harder to read, especially in glare or on damp stone. Walk parallel to it from well inland, not along the line that gives the best view. Bring binoculars so you can watch the birds bank without leaning out to see where they nest. Don’t follow grazers to the brink just because they look at ease there. And if the green band appears, watch it from where you already have good footing. The fact that it moves with you is interesting; it isn’t a reason to move closer.

Once you’ve found a dry place to sit well back from the edge, give yourself time to do nothing. At first you’ll probably keep turning toward the roar, expecting some change that tells you where the water is. There may be no such change. Watch a patch of apparently empty slope instead. Once one striped back moves, you may find you’ve been looking at a whole herd for ten minutes. That’s a good first visit. You don’t need to cross the shelf or see the bottom.
