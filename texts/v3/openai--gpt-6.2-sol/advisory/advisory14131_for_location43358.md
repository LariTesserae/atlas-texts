Visit it as a snorkeler first. The best view of this country may be from the surface, with your face in the water and the whole height of the mountains falling away beneath you. You don’t need to reach the bottom to feel how high up you are.

**Put your doorway above water, and give yourself somewhere dry to come back to.** Nothing in the description suggests a usable shore. Even the highest crest is mostly submerged, and a row of shoots is no place to stand. Bring a stable float or boat with a boarding ladder you can use in fins. Have a companion stay aboard on the first outing. Before you get in, check that the air is breathable, that the water is safe for skin contact, and that both you and your gear float as expected. Don’t infer any of that from a green leaf or a copper-backed fish.

Bring a mask, snorkel, well-fitting fins, and a thin protective suit. The water may feel lovely when you first slip in, but an afternoon of barely moving can chill you. The suit will also spare you some scrapes: those fins of rock are sharp, and the light makes the stone look close enough to touch well before it is. Set a return time, carry a whistle, and keep your float in sight. In water this clear and quiet it would be easy to spend an hour going a little farther without ever feeling that you’d left your starting point.

**Swim around the forest, not through it.** A crown that almost disappears edge-on can be twenty paces across. Look down before you lower your legs, and turn well clear of the branches so your fins don’t sweep through them. The overlapping crowns in the ravines may look like inviting swim-throughs, but they take away your straight route to the surface. Treat the cool spill at a gully mouth as a boundary on your first visit. You can see it tugging the fronds together from the warm side. If you later want to see the lower forest, arrange a proper buddy dive with familiar equipment; don’t improvise a breath-hold descent after a fish.

Go in the morning if you can, and make two passes over the saddle. On the first, the meadow will look flattened, its clear tips lying along the blades. Come back in the afternoon by the same route, and look at the scarp rather than at the grass. The shadow will tell you how much the meadow has risen. That seems to be the pace of the place: if you watch any one thing for a minute, almost nothing happens. If you go away and return, a whole slope has changed.

Leave the chalk-colored mouths alone, and don’t use the red root pads as handholds. If you visit the highest crest, approach from the open-water side and stop before your wake reaches the shoots. You can lift your face out of the water there and see the little leaf in the same air you’re breathing. Then put your mask back under and look down the slope. That short change of view is worth the trip: above water, scarcely anything marks the summit; below it, an entire country begins.
