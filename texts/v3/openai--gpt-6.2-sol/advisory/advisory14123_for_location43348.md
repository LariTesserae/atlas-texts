Bring someone you trust to hold the lamp at the head of the six steps, and bring a light of your own. A small headlamp is better than a lantern you have to carry sideways. Wear shoes that grip wet stone, leave your pack upstairs, and agree on a signal before you go down. A raised hand can mean *I’m coming up; keep the stair clear*. Don’t count on being able to explain anything from the bottom. The person above may see you speak and assume you’re remarking on the pipes.

Ask whoever looks after the cellars whether the stair is still sound and the air below has been checked. The city gave up drawing water here because the way out got narrower, not because the spring failed. That’s a good reason to pay more attention to the stair than to the water. Go down one at a time, with nothing hanging from your belt. If you have to force your way past the narrow part, turn back while you still have the choice.

At the foot, put both feet on the dry patch before you try to turn. Someone went to the trouble of leaving you that much floor. Stay there. There’s no need to put a boot in the gutter or bring your face down to the gap to hear the fall beneath the stone. Give yourself a little time to get used to the noise. The odd thing is not just how loud it is: you can pick out a single drop striking clay while an entire sentence disappears. Step up one tread if you want to tell your companion about it.

If you bring water, bring it from upstairs. You can admire the clear cord without knowing what’s in it, and there’s no good reason to try carrying a sample out through a stair that defeated the city’s water jars. Keep your hands off the soft salts. If the little white animal appears, hold your light still and let it go where it was going.

Before you leave, lower your beam to the black stone. At first you’ll see the white line and the edge of the falling water. Move the light a little and you’ll see the lip of one stone standing just proud of its neighbor. That’s the thing worth going down for. You don’t need to wait for rain or pick a season. The person who set that stone couldn’t have known you’d be coming, but you can still stand where they stood and see exactly how much higher they left it.
