Arrive by the saddle in daylight. The town starts at the pale street stones, not at the houses. Follow those lines where the paving gives out, and don’t assume that an empty court is a shortcut. Some hold water just below the paving, and a patch of ferns can hide a deep seam in the rock. If you want the view from the opposite ridge, ask which path people use. Fresh-looking rockfall is a good reason to stay on the path even if the locals tell you the ridge has looked that way since their grandparents’ time.

Wear boots with a good grip on wet stone. The broad stairs look forgiving, but the middles of the blocks are worn smooth. Bring a light rain layer and something warm for the return: the saddle may be sunny while the route below it is already in cloud. Carry your own drinking water and enough food to get back out. Water coming straight from a wall looks inviting, but ask what people drink before filling a bottle. Don’t wash at the cistern or step into the spillway. That quiet sheet of overflow and the silt the gardeners lift out are both doing work.

Ask where visitors are expected to wait. A court several times larger than the nearest house might look like a public square to you and be somebody’s working yard. The cistern steps seem a good place to sit if you’re invited; leave room for people carrying bowls and baskets. Before dusk, settle where you’re sleeping or start back. Bring a headlamp, but don’t count on lit windows to guide you: from any one house, there may be very few others in sight.

The silence may take more getting used to than the terrain. Back home, you might listen for birds to judge the hour or take insects over water as a sign that a pool is ordinary. Here, neither will help. You may hear a bowl set down across a court and then nothing for several minutes. There’s no need to fill the gap. If someone has time to talk, ask them to show you how the water leaves the town. You can see the spillway plainly and still miss it working.
