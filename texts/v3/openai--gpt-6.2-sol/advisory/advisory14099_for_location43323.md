Prepare to visit the pocket without putting your body into it. The root and the rain have ways in; you don’t. Don’t widen the seam or pull the root aside. Even if you could shrink, a single bead of rain could cut off your way back. If your means of crossing worlds lets you open a viewing window independently of a doorway, use that. Otherwise, settle for what you can see from outside.

**Bring magnification, not more light.** The chamber is already bright enough. Set up a camera that can watch the roof and floor together, and give it a fixed reference for orientation. Near the seam, a shadow can point toward the ceiling; near the root, it can outlast the thing that cast it. If you lose track of a probe, look for the probe itself, not its shadow. And leave the loose clay scales in place. A wall that looks bare from your side may be the roof of a crowded room.

**Plan to watch for several days.** Bring a clock that stays with you and keep separate notes on what happens inside and when you see it. Record the rain click and the first appearance of water as two events; don’t use one to date the other. If you want to catch a springtail jumping into the following morning, keep the camera on the landing place after the animal disappears. The centipede seems to know where to wait. You’ll learn more from watching it than from trying to follow the jump.

If you can watch only one wet morning, frame the growing mineral tube and the clay fold opposite it. The little animal crossing between them is evidence that *it* has something to put its feet on. That isn’t evidence that a probe will find the same support. Keep yours out of the gap. The same restraint applies to the sand-grain roof: the husk underneath might be a meal, a burial, or something you don’t have a word for. You don’t need to lift the roof to make the visit worthwhile.

**Make the blister your turning point.** Its opening has room for one mite, but the tracks inside lead apart for a long way. That tells you something about how much room there is beyond the opening and nothing about how to come back. Watch it edge-on. If you send a camera in and it goes out of sight, don’t send another after it.

The easiest mistake would be to decide, after ten minutes, that the chamber is empty. Give your eyes time to learn the dry clay. A footprint drying around a springtail that hasn’t made it yet is easy to miss if you’re waiting for something bigger to happen.
