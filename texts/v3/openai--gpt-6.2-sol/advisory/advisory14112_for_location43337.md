Arrive in the morning, and make your first appointment at a door cut into the old black stone. A door on a freight deck may be exactly where your host said it would be and still not be there when you come back. Ask for directions from the stone up: “From the sixth step, which way to the bathhouse’s high door?” Above the Seventh is a neighborhood, not an instruction to climb. If the warm drain is already spilling over the sixth, take the long way.

**Pack for warm water and loud streets.** Bring shoes with wet grip and closed toes. Ankle-deep water can hide a missing tread, and ordinary-looking gutters sometimes have eels in them. Put your papers, money, and whatever gets you home in a waterproof pouch on your body. Keep dry socks in it if there’s room. Bring earplugs for the market and a pencil and paper for when you need to ask a question there. The noise will reach you through the paving even with your ears plugged. At a public counter, put your bare palm on the worn brass plate and wait. The clerk will come through much more clearly than anyone shouting beside you. The first time, it may feel as though someone has spoken from inside a back tooth.

**Learn how to get indoors before you learn how to get across town.** Ask your host to show you a high door and a route to it that doesn’t depend on a lift or a moving platform. Then ask where to go if that door is already cut off. A dry street under blue sky can fill before rain reaches you; the people adjusting the fall don’t have to wait for the weather. Around midafternoon, stay off freight platforms unless you’re prepared to finish the trip wherever the porters do. If the porters sit down on their cart handles and unwrap lunch, take that as a better estimate of your arrival time than anything on a sign.

If you’re caught by a rise, follow people heading for a high doorway rather than people making a dash for the opposite curb. The bathhouse is a good place to wait even if it becomes an island. Take the towel when it’s offered. You don’t have to buy a bath to explain why you’re there.

**Don’t judge the water by its looks.** In the municipal galleries, a channel can look uphill and carry a stone briskly downhill. At street level, watch what the water actually does around a post or a wheel, and don’t step into a current to find out how strong it is. Leave the old pools alone except where they’ve been incorporated into a public bath. At the freight station you can watch the pale animals from the landing: wait for a lift to pass and look for the antennae. There’s no need to drop anything. Likewise, don’t brush the green off a wall to see what’s underneath. It’s copper, not moss, and you’ll be looking at the mark on your sleeve for the rest of the day.

Give yourself an afternoon with nowhere you have to be. Find a public parapet on the common stone, out of the way of people working, and look along a street rather than across the roofs. You may see rain a long way off and assume you have the rest of the day before it gets to you. Ask someone who works outside what they make of it. They may know where it’s been.

Before you leave, go back to the sixth step at high water and watch from the dry side. By then you may have seen a market move with lunch still on the pans, or come back to a familiar corner and found the street draining the other way. The warm water will still find that same nick in the stone. It’s a good thing to know about a city before you try to know the whole of it.
