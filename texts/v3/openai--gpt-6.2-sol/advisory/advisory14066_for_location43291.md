Go at your ordinary size first. Find the washhouses, ask a keeper where you can crouch without getting in anyone’s way, and watch the last stair through a morning’s work. The keeper may not know there is a room under it. They will know when the stair takes weight, how long it stays down after rain, and which tread lets the warm water through. That’s the information you need.

Bring a waterproof kneeling pad, a hand lens, and a small light you can aim along the underside of the stair. Leave the probe behind. The fresh split makes a tempting place to rest a fingertip, but there isn’t much clearance even when the stair is open. Nor should you wedge it open: the movement you’d be preventing is part of how the room works. If you want to hear the grubs, put your ear to the wood from beside the stairs, out of the path of the pails. You’ll hear more there than you would inside.

If you mean to go in, rather than look in, arrange to return to full size somewhere **beside** the stair, not underneath it. Your way back should work without your having to reach the entrance again. Go on a dry day after the morning traffic, with someone outside who can warn you when a person approaches. Even then, plan a short visit. At that size, the main danger is not the hunter in the root. It’s an ordinary person coming down to do the washing. The footfall reduces the clearance and sends water through at the same time. The springtails can let go and be carried out; don’t assume you’ll fit through the gap they use, or that you’ll like where it empties. The sedge is no sure refuge either: it grows along the part that comes down.

You can see a great deal without entering. Pick one feature—the drop at the split, say—and wait. You may never see the strand, but you can watch the drop move sideways. The pale strip on the floor looks like a path until you’ve watched it long enough to notice where the springtails stop. And if you come back after a few wash days, look for the distance between the root tip and the eggs. The animal that laid them is gone, but you can still see what it was counting on.
