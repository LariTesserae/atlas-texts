Visit from the edge, in daylight, with a way home that does not depend on walking back to the place where you arrived. The plateau looks like country you could cross in a day. It may not be country you can cross at all.

Dress for standing still in cold water while the wind blows. Bring waterproof boots, dry socks and insulation sealed in a dry bag, a windproof shell, gloves you can work in when wet, and a broad-footed walking pole. The pole is for testing the next step, not the far side of a pool. There is no reliable shoreline: by the time the grass looks aquatic, you may already be standing in water. Bring your own drinking water. A clear pool, a still pool, and a pool whose plants have shut down are three different things, and none is a promise of potability. If the air is as thin as the elevation suggests, acclimatize before trying to walk any distance.

**Spend your first outing learning what counts as a return.** Go with a companion and make a short out-and-back while the weather is settled. Test whatever brings you home before you need it. A compass may tell you which way you’re facing; it cannot tell you how much country lies between you and the exit. A tree getting larger is not proof of progress. If you find yourself behind a flock that has been facing you the whole time, stop. Don’t try to correct the discrepancy by walking faster. And don’t follow a bird onto a reflection. You have not brought its feet.

Pick your weather by looking beyond the rain. That strip of flattened grass may tell you more about what just passed through than the clear sky above it. Turn back early rather than waiting to see which way a storm moves. There is almost nothing to shelter behind, and the ground itself is among the things the storm can pick up. Don’t count on tent stakes in sod or on a tree as an anchor. If you can arrange it, sleep off the plateau. A campsite that stays dry through the afternoon is not necessarily above water, and a tree a mile away may belong to the grove directly beneath your bedroll.

Give the groves room when the cold comes on. The first low sound is a poor guide to where the water will come out; you can hear it across miles of living wood. If you hear the stems knocking, don’t go looking for a closer vantage, especially along the western edge. A pause may mean something is stuck, not that the grove is finished. Leave the thin ice alone, too. The dark spaces around the grass are a good reason not to mistake a continuous-looking surface for a continuous one.

I’d make the first visit very small. Find a place near your tested exit where you can stand dry, turn off anything that hums, and wait. You may hear birds eating before you can find them. You may watch the lanes of silver mites hold their shape for a while after whatever sustained them has stopped. There is enough to see in one shallow pool. Save the tree on the horizon for a visit when you no longer feel obliged to reach it.
