Arrange for someone to meet you at the tram. Ask them to take you all the way to your room, not just point out the building. In this city an address can tell you which door is yours without telling you which level to approach it from. Have your host show you the warm strip at the latch while you can still see it. Coming home tired, with cold fingers and a lamp full of reflected rain, you’ll be glad to know where to put your thumb.

**Pack for cold that comes up through the floor.** Bring waterproof boots with insulated soles and good grip on wet stone, dry socks in a sealed bag, and slippers substantial enough to wear indoors. Take a hood that turns with your head; you want to see the next tread without having to turn your whole body. A headlamp with a low setting is more useful than a fierce beam that lights up every drop between you and the stair. Keep a spare light somewhere you can reach without taking your pack off. Bring drinking water, too. Nothing about the clarity of the runoff tells you what’s been combing it upstream.

Ask where you can dry your boots before you unpack. This is a more important question than whether the room has a view. Keep one set of clothes dry for sleeping and bring a clock: a lull in the rain or a clearing below the walls may feel like morning, but it won’t tell you how much time you’ve slept or when the last tram leaves.

**Learn one route before you learn the city.** Walk between your lodging and an occupied tram stop with your host, then walk it back. Remember landmarks at hand height: a pipe crossing a court, a change in the railing, the sheltered side of a door. Don’t orient by calls from the culverts. You may be hearing something a few streets away, or the same thing you heard earlier coming back through miles of channel. If the great breath comes through the floor, take your cue from the people who work in the building. You don’t have to settle where the animal is.

Use the civil works as the residents use them. If they cross between two landings in one ordinary step, do the same; don’t take a run at it. Leave the locked storeroom locked. At the reservoir, stay on the public side of the rail. A falling pebble tells you what happens to a pebble, not what happens to you if you lean into the water.

**Give the animals room to keep doing what they’re doing.** What looks like fur on a wall is not something to brush with your sleeve, and a warm rail may be occupied underneath. Look before wrapping your fingers around it. That isn’t a reason to refuse a handhold on a slick stair; it’s a reason to check it before you need it. Don’t feed the animals or move them out of a drain for a better look. If you hear the soft landings overhead in your room, you can listen from bed. There’s no need to find a way onto the roof.

At the north tram stop, ask the maintenance crew where the walkable route ends **today**. A map can be accurate about the city and already wrong about its edge. Don’t step on the loose grate, even if it looks as though it would complete a perfectly good crossing. If you go beyond the pump houses, go with someone who knows the ground and agree on a turnaround before you set out. The city remains audible a surprisingly long way out; hearing a tram is not proof that you can see your way back to it. Treat every dark or shining patch as a pool until you know otherwise. The ice can look like an opening when it’s attached to the rim and like a paving stone when it’s floating free.

You needn’t go far onto the shelf to get what you came for. Find a place the crew says is sound, stand still, and wait until you can separate the close ticking of the ice from the rain. Then go back while you still have warm feet. On your first visit, finding your own latch without the lamp is a better measure of how well you’ve learned the place than how far you walked into the dark.
