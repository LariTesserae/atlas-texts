Go first to the roofs you can see from the upper road. Ask whether there’s a footpath through the valley, who looks after the freight line, and whether you can visit the winding house. Do this while you still have the whole place in view. From below, a building half a mile away may be harder to find than one ten miles away on open ground. Sketch the junctions and the way back up. If you crossed into this world at a particular spot on the road, record that spot from both directions; the view on your return may be nothing like the view you arrived to.

**Pack for cold shade and wet brush, not for the sun on the road.** Wear boots with good grip on wet stone, long trousers, and a coat that sheds water brushed off leaves. Bring a dry layer, drinking water, food, and a headlamp even if you’re only going down for the afternoon. There’s moisture everywhere but no dependable place to fill a bottle. Leave the little pools in the old machinery alone. If you’re tempted to stay the night, ask for a room on the far side rather than bedding down under a ledge. The hollow may be considerably colder by morning than it was when you chose it.

Take the established path, even if it makes a long turn around something you can see. The ferns are high enough to hide the edge of a slab, and a mat of roots can hold soil together without holding it to the slope. Give the clean rock faces and their fallen slabs a wide berth. The tilted birches are worth looking at from a place where you don’t have to put a foot on the plate that carries them.

Treat the freight works as **maintained, not abandoned**. No cars on the line doesn’t mean the machinery is safe to approach. Don’t touch the cables, climb the supports, or try the tally wheel. If someone lets you into the winding house, let them show you where to stand. They may also know a much better place to watch the late-afternoon shadow than anywhere you’d find by following the cable downhill.

Bring a notebook if the shadow is what you’ve come for. On a first visit, check that you can see the sag with your own eyes and note where you stood and when. Ask whether you can look at the old photograph, and ask what the red dashes measure: the lowest point of the curve, perhaps, or where it crosses a particular seam. The people who grease the bearings have been coming back long enough to notice a change you won’t see in an afternoon. Their account is worth more than an elaborate theory brought in from another world. Don’t add a dash of your own.

Leave yourself enough daylight to climb out without hurrying. On the way back, stop once where the path is level and let the noise of your coat and boots die away. It may take a little while to realize how much of what you’ve been hearing all afternoon was you.
