Make the first visit a day trip, and put your way home on the valley floor, well back from the rim. The place sounds welcoming in exactly the ways that could get a traveler into trouble: the water is warm, the ground looks nearly level, and there are frogs where your experience says there ought to be snow. Enjoy that first sound from the pass, but don’t take it as an all-clear.

**Find out what you’re breathing before you cross.** If the air is anything like Earth’s at seven thousand metres, a healthy person arriving from low altitude can become dangerously impaired. Warmth doesn’t tell you anything about oxygen availability. Send a pressure and oxygen sensor through first if you can; you want the *partial pressure* of oxygen, not just its percentage. Measure on the approach as well as in the valley. If the readings are consistent with Earth’s at that elevation, this is an expedition for an acclimatized team with appropriate oxygen and a reliable evacuation plan, not a walk for an ordinary visitor. If you can’t establish that the air is safe or arrange that support, stay home. On the visit, a headache with nausea is reason to turn back; confusion, breathlessness at rest, or stumbling calls for immediate evacuation. Don’t wait to see whether lunch helps.

**Dress for a hot wade, but pack for the cold side of the pass.** Wear light, quick-drying clothes, long trousers, and grippy footwear that stays on in mud. Use a walking pole to test what looks like ankle-deep water: a mat of sedge can conceal a hole, and wet roots can be slick even when they look rough. Carry your own drinking water, electrolytes, and food. Clear water with a single red larva in it is a lovely thing to look at, not a source to count on. Pack dry socks and a warm layer in a sealed bag. In air that humid, sweating may do surprisingly little to cool you; walk slowly and take breaks before you feel you need them.

I’d go with one companion and mark the return route as we went. From the pass you can see the shape of the whole valley; down among the sedges, you may be able to see very little of it. Don’t use the falls as your only bearing. You can hear them long before you can see them, which makes it tempting to think you’re making for a landmark when all you’re doing is walking toward a noise.

Give the grazers room to get to their pools at noon. An animal with only its nostrils showing is easy to miss from a low bank, and you don’t want to surprise it in the water. Watch the yellow birds from dry ground. Don’t handle the frogs or bring back a pebble, a strip of bark, or a snail shell. Clean and dry your kit before the trip, and contain the mud you bring back on it. The valley has had a long time to become what it is; neither world needs a little of the other carried over in a boot tread.

**Watch the falls from a stable bank, and leave the rim alone.** You can see the leaf make the whole circuit without stepping into the flow. That’s the thing I’d go to see. I wouldn’t send a camera through after it or assume a swimmer would come back just because a leaf does. The thin-floored building is another thing best inspected from shore: daylight underneath is fascinating, but it doesn’t tell you what the floor can bear. At the rim, the line of seeds marks where something changes, not where it’s safe to stand. Stay well inland of it, especially if mist comes up. There’s no need to put a hand across to find out whether the cold is real.

Leave yourself enough daylight and energy to get back *up* to the pass. On the way out, stop once on firm ground and listen. After an afternoon with the falls in your ears, you may have to stand still a while before you hear the frogs again.
