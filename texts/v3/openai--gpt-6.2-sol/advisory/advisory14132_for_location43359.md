Go in the morning, and arrange to spend your first night at the first occupied house that will take you. The city begins long before there is anybody to ask for directions. A curb with beautifully cut drain holes can look like a promise that there’s an inn around the bend. Here it may keep you company for twelve miles.

**Pack for a high, treeless country with cold nights and short, repeated soakings.** Bring boots that grip wet stone, dry socks in a waterproof bag, a windproof layer, sun protection, and enough food and treated water to spend an unplanned night outdoors. The lack of permanent snow tells you little about the altitude. If you come from low country, take the last pass slowly and leave time to acclimatize. On the plateau itself, pitch only on dry, exposed stone well clear of gutters, hollows, and moss banks. A gutter can carry water from a storm you never saw. Ask your host where travelers are welcome to camp; a bare patch may be somebody’s garden in a dry year.

**Ask for a route in two versions: dry and wet.** On bare rock, walk toward things you can see. Look back often: the pass can drop out of sight without your ever feeling that you’ve climbed. Don’t count on a surveyed line or an old row of pegs, particularly near the northern gardens. In town, ask which crossings stay usable after rain and which door you should knock on if you have to wait. Make those arrangements in daylight. A familiar street under an inch of water is no longer a familiar street.

The covered stair is worth seeing dry. Take the story about the sixth step literally: if you feel it bear your weight before your boot lands, don’t take another step to see whether it happens twice. Back out if the way you came is still plainly there. If you do find yourself in the court with fresh stone dust, leave the stone alone. Two households already have to live with the results of somebody treating that court as a quarry.

**Let your hosts show you how to handle water.** Carry drinking water in an opaque, capped bottle and use a shallow cup at the table. Don’t lift a jar lid just to see what window might be in it. The lid is a household precaution, not an invitation, and the jar may hold the family’s supply. Stay out of the drains even when the water sounds warm and inviting. If you want to help after a shower, ask which leaves to clear from the grate.

Leave a whole afternoon for doing very little. Sit where you can see a gutter cross the plain. A shower can have its front dissolving into mist while its far end is still falling, and you may hear water under the street before a drop lands beside you. The woman at the eastern house takes in her washing when the rain is still hours away. Watch what she does rather than trying to decide whether the damp hems are a warning or simply the first part of the rain. When she goes back out to clear the drain, that’s a good moment to offer a hand.
