Prepare to visit the tooth, not to enter the room. The best place for an ordinary human to be when the grass loop draws tight is back on the ridge, with both feet on ground they already know will hold.

Approach in settled weather with a companion, cold-weather ridge gear, and a turnaround time that leaves plenty of daylight for the descent. The opening is on the uphill side, but that does not make the snow above it safe. Treat the lip as an overhang: don’t step onto it, dig into it, or lean over it to find the slit. Work from established footing off to one side. If the snow hides the opening completely, let it. You can come back another day. A storm may show you the water’s curved surface, but that is a reason to be farther away, not a weather window to aim for.

The room’s most misleading feature is that you can see across it. Kitchen-window light, knife curls on a peg, a little green scrap: all of that gives the eye the scale of a place you could reach into. But there’s no breathing space, no standing room, and only one way out, through a slit whose clearance you can’t count on. The stone is changing around it. Even if you had breathing gear and could fit through, the clear middle of the room tells you nothing about what a blow would do to you. Watch the animal if you’re lucky enough to see it. It’s already across before the grit starts to move. You won’t get a useful warning from the grit.

Bring binoculars that focus close, or a camera with a zoom, and use the daylight that’s already there. Pick one detail and give it time. The edge of the little stair is better than the whole wall: you may never catch a flake fastening, but you can look away, look back, and find that a step is gone. Leave the pegs and the grass alone. They’re the clearest evidence that somebody has been here before, and they tell you almost nothing about how that person got there, or what the room was like when they did.

Don’t put a fingernail into the seam to hear it. The unnerving part of the visit is worth having without that test: you may watch the loop snap taut and the grit jump, and hear nothing but your companion’s buckle tapping in the wind. On the way down, turn once and look back. It will look like dry white stone again.
