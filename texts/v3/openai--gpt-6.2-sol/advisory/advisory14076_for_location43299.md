Arrive above the town, not in it. Ask your crossing to put you in clear water over the middle of the ridge, in a small pressure-rated cabin with its own air and a way home that works from inside. The forty fathoms in the description take you only to water that *looks* like daylight; they don’t tell you how much farther it is to air, or even whether there is air overhead. Don’t make surfacing your emergency plan. And don’t open a hatch because you see someone outside carrying groceries.

From the cabin, the town should be easy to read. Follow the roofline rather than the streets: the roofs run down both sides of the ridge, and the broad bare bench marks the north end. Below the gardens there’s very little to give you a sense of scale. The white road is especially deceptive. It keeps looking like a road after it stops being maintained, and it points toward a valley floor you can’t see. Save it for a second visit, if someone local offers to take you.

If you want to go out among the houses, arrange it in advance. Find out what the water is, what pressure you’ll be working at, and whether your equipment is suited to both. An ordinary human should expect to need a properly supported diving operation or a one-atmosphere suit, not a mask and a tank borrowed from a friend. Have your host meet you in open water. A doorway here marks a change in temperature, not a change from wet to dry. You can spend a whole afternoon indoors without ever coming up for a breath.

The hardest part of getting around may be learning to stop. Watch the market for a while before you enter it. People clear a low wall and catch a post without thinking; you’ll be thinking about the post just as your feet swing through somebody’s lunch. Keep your gear close, clip down anything you’d mind losing, and ask which posts a visitor can take hold of. The leaves over the lanes are trimmed for the people who live there, not for someone with a valve sticking up behind their head. Take the steps if they suit you. The fact that nobody else bothers doesn’t make them ceremonial.

Ask your host how they prefer to get your attention, and agree on a few signals before going out. Don’t tap on a wall to announce yourself: the knock may turn up in a house you’re not visiting. Leave the bronze bell alone. It’s for someone who is lost, and ringing it to hear how far the note carries is a good way to make the whole town stop what it’s doing.

Bring a gift that can be handed over underwater. Something dense and washable in a tied pouch will travel better than anything wrapped in paper. If you’re invited to help in the gardens, ask for a job rather than pulling up a shoot to show willing. You may be most useful sitting on the wall. Ask about the young shoots at supper, but don’t count on being able to eat where your hosts eat; bring food you can manage back in the cabin.

Go to the new square with someone who knows where the boundary rope was last year. Keep your hands and equipment off the soft edge. A shoulder print makes the stone look inviting to lean on, but remember what happened to the basket. Don’t anchor there, even briefly. At the well, stay where your host stays. There’s no reason to lower a light or a line into it to see how far it goes.

If a white grain comes up while you’re there, give yourself time to watch it. Most of what you’ll have done all day is learn not to drift: not over a stall, not into a roof, not downhill along a road you can’t see the end of. Here is one small thing you can let go by. You don’t have to follow it to find out where daylight begins.
