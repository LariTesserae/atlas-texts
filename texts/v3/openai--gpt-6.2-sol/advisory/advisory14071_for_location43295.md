The first thing I’d pack is a way home that doesn’t depend on finding the shore. There isn’t a shore here in the ordinary sense. A dry passage between two pools can disappear under an inch of water, and the dark strip you’ve been walking toward for an hour may turn out to be sedges standing in yet more water.

**Give yourself a short first visit.** If you can choose where to arrive, pick a broad shelf of exposed stone near the uppermost visible line of husks and shed wings. Those old lines are useful evidence of where water has stood, not a guarantee that it won’t go higher. Check that you can breathe comfortably and that your return mechanism works before you wander. Save the arrival point on whatever navigation instrument works in this world, and take a few photographs looking *back* toward it as you leave. Don’t count on footprints. If reopening the way home requires a dry spot, be especially conservative about how far you go.

**Dress to get wet, not to stay dry.** For a warm day I’d wear light, quick-drying clothes, closed shoes with grippy soles, and thin socks that keep grit from rubbing my feet raw. Bring a brimmed hat, insect protection, and a light rain shell, but put your dry clothes, first-aid kit, and return gear in waterproof bags. Carry all the drinking water you’ll need. Clear water with minnows in it is lovely to look at and tells you almost nothing about whether it’s safe to drink. Tend to small cuts and blisters early; a whole afternoon of wet grit can make a minor rub the biggest thing you think about.

**Walk by the ground underfoot, not by the view.** A light walking pole will tell you more than your eyes about the next step. Probe where mud meets stone and where a mat of vegetation hides the bottom. A shelf that feels solid and warm at noon may be slick once the rain comes. Never commit to a long crossing on the strength of what the far end looks like. In this country, water doesn’t have to get deep to make trouble. It only has to cover the few inches of relief that distinguish your route from everything around it.

**Let the weather set the length of the visit.** A shower can erase the horizon and drown out every other sound. Watch the water against a fixed edge of stone when you stop; it’s easier to notice it creeping up there than across an entire flat. If separate pools begin joining, or the way back starts to lose its definition, turn around while you can still recognize it. Treat thunder as a reason to leave promptly if you can: the plain offers almost no shelter from lightning. I wouldn’t camp out on it on a first visit. A place can be dry enough to sit on without being high enough to sleep on.

Once you have a reliable way back, there’s no particular reward for covering distance. Sit on a dry shelf and let your eyes adjust to the small changes. You’ll see the minnows hold their place in water that looks still. After a shower, you can hear which clump of sedge starts the frogs off again, before the whole plain takes up the call. The longer you sit, the more distinct the terrain becomes. Just remember that learning to see it and being able to cross it are two different things.
