Make the first visit a look from the threshold. The hollow is about the size of a chapel, but you should plan the trip as if you were entering a crawlspace: you’ll be stooping, you’ll lose sight of the way out, and you can’t count on the walls to hold your weight. Go farther only if there’s something you need to see that you can’t see from outside.

**Keep the way out independent of the hollow.** Leave your return gate on stable ground beyond the living grit. If you cross the threshold, leave a companion there and agree on a turnaround time and a signal you can see. A shout or knock might carry a surprising distance through the leaf without telling anyone where it came from. Use a light or a line tended from outside; don’t drive anchors into the rind. The leaf that looks like three separate pillars on your way in may look like one continuous wall on your way back.

**Dress for getting cold while standing still.** Wear waterproof boots with good wet-surface grip, dry insulation, and gloves you can work buckles in. Bring a helmet, a headlamp, and a spare light you can reach without taking off your pack. Keep the pack small enough that you can turn around under a low fold. If you don’t know whether the air is breathable, check it from outside with equipment suited to that world. Don’t use the feeding animals as evidence that the air is safe for you. If you can’t establish that it is, stay at the threshold.

**Treat the floor as a living surface, not as ground.** Avoid the black pools, the root fringes, and the fine seams. A bulging patch may be thick rind, or it may be a skin over moving water; the description gives you no reliable way to tell by looking. Don’t stamp to find out. If there’s no route you can take without testing the floor with your weight, you’ve reached the end of the visit. And if you want to hear the clicking through the wall, listen from where you’re standing. You don’t need to lean against the leaf or put your face next to it.

It may take a minute to get used to how loud the place is. A drop can sound like a board falling, and something feeding a room away can sound as if it’s under your boot. Give yourself a quiet minute at the entrance to learn which sounds repeat. You’ll also be tempted to switch off your lamp when a bud brightens. Keep it within reach if you do. The little patch of afternoon is worth waiting for, but it isn’t a skylight, a source of warmth, or a clue to the way out.

**Stop well short of the two lips at the back.** There’s no need to imagine them snapping shut. They’re already close enough that reaching through would put your hand somewhere you can’t see or easily withdraw it. The water you hear falling beyond them tells you almost nothing about the space a person could fit into. Watch the grazers through the hanging water and leave the next room to them.

On the way out, check your boot treads and cuffs before stepping through the gate. The angular grains look like dirt, but they’re the material the hollow builds with. Leave them on their own side. A good first visit might amount to ten minutes at the entrance, one frozen bead splitting on a root tip, and no footprints at all.
