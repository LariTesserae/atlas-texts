Make your first visit a daylight excursion from the rim. Bring all the water you’ll need to get back, and leave while there’s still enough light to see where you’re putting your feet. Fifteen miles of canopy looks wonderfully legible from above. Once you’re under it, you may have trouble seeing which side of a tree a sound came from, let alone which way the valley runs.

**Keep the way home simple.** If you come through a portal, put it on stable ground well back from the rim, rather than beside some splendid black outcrop. Verify that you can reopen it from this side before you descend. On the first trip, pick a stretch of forest you can leave by climbing back to a broad section of rim, not by finding one particular crossing. Take a compass and a paper sketch, check that the compass behaves before trusting it, and look back often enough to learn what the route looks like in reverse. Set a turnaround time that leaves room for a detour. A cleft that a herd crossed yesterday may not be crossable today; don’t make it the one thing between you and home.

**Dress for what you can’t see under the leaves.** Wear boots with stiff, grippy soles and good toe protection, long trousers, and gloves tough enough for accidental contact with a sharp edge. Bring a walking pole to test the depth of litter in front of you. Don’t haul yourself up by hanging roots: the clay they’re holding may be all that’s left of the ground they used to stand in. Pack a headlamp and a spare light even for the shortest outing. After dark, stop before trying to make sense of a gap. Shine the light into it from where you stand rather than reaching forward. If the apparent gap turns out to have ribs, back away and give whatever owns them room to move.

**Carry water, not just a way to treat it.** Take enough for the walk in, the walk out, and a delay in warm, humid air. The spoonfuls in leaves and root forks are hard to find and already have regular visitors. A squall might leave you plenty to look at and very little you can collect cleanly. Bring a filter or other treatment as a fallback, not as permission to go in light. Take food you already know agrees with you, keep it sealed, and save the local nuts for the apes.

**Treat noise as poor evidence of distance.** Take a companion if you can and stay in sight of each other; agree on hand signals before going down. In the loudest stretches, pause at places where you can see a little way in more than one direction. If you feel a heavy footfall, don’t wait to hear the next one. Step out of the line of travel if you can see it, and don’t retreat into a slot with no second exit. Give the apes a wide berth, including the one that has apparently put down its stone and gone to sleep. That stone is scarce enough to carry all night. It isn’t a souvenir.

The rock’s trick with voices is worth hearing, but it makes calling to a separated companion unreliable. A reply can be close and the person who made it can still be across the clearing. If you lose sight of each other, go back to the last agreed meeting point if it’s safe to do so; don’t scramble toward a voice that seems to be coming from the foot of a slab. Nor should you count on a pair of stone faces carrying sound on the way out just because they did on the way in.

**If rain catches you, stop trying to make time.** Get out of the clefts and shallow drainage lines, and look for firm ground away from fractured rock and dead limbs overhead. An overhang isn’t automatically safe just because it’s dry. Wait for the falling debris and the rush of animals to ease before moving, then check the route again. Don’t follow the shine of water in low light: what looks like a stream might be a string of pools with sharp dry rock between them.

I’d save dawn for a second visit and watch it from the rim first. Go down once you can see the ground, and stop at the first pool you can watch without standing on an animal path. You don’t have to cover much of this valley to see what makes it strange. Six inches of dry stone is a serious crossing for the beetle. Give it time to decide what to do, and leave yourself enough daylight to be just as choosy about your own way home.
