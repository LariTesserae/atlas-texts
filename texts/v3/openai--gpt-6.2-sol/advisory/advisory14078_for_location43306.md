Bring your own water, your own light, and a way home that works from the street. Plan to spend your first visit at the threshold. You can hear most of what makes the garden worth visiting without putting your head below street level.

**Go early, and mark the way back.** The city is low enough to look navigable from outside, but once you’re in it, a passage can turn into a room without anything you’d recognize as a doorway. Don’t count on a stair leading up or a courtyard having a second exit. Record your turns or leave removable markers. Wear sturdy shoes, bring eye protection for the grit, and carry enough water for a wrong turn in the afternoon heat. There is nothing here to refill a bottle with.

**Treat the garden as a confined space.** The western slot and the hole under the threshold move air, but only when the city’s drafts cooperate. A combustible vapor seeps from the seam, and you don’t know what else is in it or what collects near the floor when the wind drops. The pine smell belongs to the torn leaves; its absence tells you nothing about the air. No flame, no ordinary flashlight or other electronics in the opening. If you mean to go inside, come back with someone trained to assess confined spaces, instruments and lighting rated for flammable atmospheres, and a rescue plan. A dust mask won’t make the air safe. Don’t shift the narrow-set brick until you know whether it holds anything up.

**Give yourself time to listen.** In the afternoon the leaves sound like somebody folding stiff paper in the next room. Then the oven bricks start up. The note may be startlingly loud for a place with no moving creature in it, and the break in the sound does not mean the wind is done. If the approach stays safe and you can spare the time, wait outside until the slot goes dark. The floor will still be warm. In the quiet before the draft reverses, you may hear a seed hit the dish and roll.

Leave the oil and seeds where they fall. The polished side of the hollow tells you where someone used to put a hand, but not what they knew about the oil or how long they could safely stay. The best thing to take home is the sound of that last seed: after the others stop at the threshold, it goes on ticking around the curve the wind has worn.
