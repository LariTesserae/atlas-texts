**Treat it as an overhead dive, not a garden with six floors of water above it.** The distance to the surface is the least useful number in the description. What matters is how far you have to go *sideways* before you can go up. Arrive at the surface or at a verified dry staging point, never by a jump straight into the chapel. Find a guide who knows the tower. If you aren’t trained for confined overhead diving, send a camera in and watch from above. Being a good swimmer won’t help you find a door behind a curtain of leaves.

Before you enter, have the guide establish which entrance is actually passable in both directions. The eastern stair may be the easier one to see, with every tread picked out in translucent leaves, but that doesn’t make it the easier one to climb. Check that you can turn around in your gear without backing into a tray or wrenching a root out of a screw recess. Bring the breathing redundancies, lights, and continuous route back to open water that you’d use for any overhead dive. Bring close-fitting gloves, too: you’ll be using your hands near sharp glazed lips and stamped steel. Leave the dangling camera strap behind.

Inside, expect to go at the speed of putting things down. Lay a broad leaf gently against its neighbor, pass it, and let it go. Don’t gather several at once; you won’t know which stem you’re pulling on. Keep your fins off the porcelain. The room is small enough that you might expect to take it in with one sweep of a light, but you’ll get it in pieces: a weld, the red underside of a leaf, a lifting eye at the foot of the stair. The vast view out the western window will come as a surprise after so much close work.

Use your depth instrument and your route out to tell you which way is up. The plants are giving perfectly consistent directions, but to a different building—the one specified by the assembly plates. The place to see that is at the glass. Find two consecutive numbers, one on either side, and look at what the plants have done with the gap. A photograph that includes both plates will be more useful afterward than a sample. Clean your gear before you come and after you leave; in a place where a fallen leaf can keep its shape for weeks, don’t assume that whatever you bring in will quietly disappear.

Ask about the afternoon heating cycle before you descend. If there’s a place to wait without blocking the return route or hovering over a grate, be still there when the flow eases. Don’t spend your return reserve waiting for it. You’re looking for the first loose ribbon tip to rise, not for the sunlight itself. And leave the yellow rectangle clear even when you can’t see it. The nicest part of the visit may be discovering that you’ve been looking at the same patch of floor all along.
