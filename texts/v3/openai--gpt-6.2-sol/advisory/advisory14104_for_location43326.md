Go in the morning, while daylight still reaches the ferns, and plan to spend most of your visit on the steps. The room is small enough to see from there. What takes time isn’t crossing it; it’s learning to see what’s already moving.

**Before you descend.** If you’re arriving from another world, set your return point out on the slope, well back from the limestone lip. Make sure you can get back without relying on a device you’ve left below. If you don’t know whether the air is breathable, check from outside before putting your head under the shelf. The frogs tell you the room suits frogs, not that it suits you. Check the recent weather, too: a clear morning after rain can still be a wet morning underground. Don’t go down if the steps are slick, the rock looks freshly fractured, or getting back up would mean hauling yourself by a root.

**What to bring.** Wear grippy closed shoes and light clothing that covers your knees and forearms. The wind on the slope may make you dress too warmly for the room; leave a dry layer outside for your return. Take drinking water and two small lights. Use one on its lowest useful setting for your footing, then let your eyes adjust. Start with clean soles so you don’t bring soil or seeds from another place. Leave the bulky pack outside: under that ceiling, turning around with it may do more damage than walking straight ahead.

**Where to stop.** The last sound step is a good seat if you can sit without blocking anyone’s way out. Don’t use the three upright stones as handholds or put a foot in the dry triangle behind them. That dust is one of the few places in the room that keeps a record of who’s been through. Leave the cups alone, too: don’t dip a finger in, top one up, or move a frog for a better look. If the only way farther in is to part the hanging roots with your hands, you’ve gone far enough.

Give yourself a few quiet minutes. The crickets may back away at your first footfall, and it’s easy to mistake their retreat for stillness. Pick one small patch—the rim of a cup, the underside of a root, the tip of a fern—and watch it rather than sweeping the walls with your light. If you’re still there when the seam of daylight climbs off the bush, watch the drops appear at different heights. Then turn your light on *before* you stand. The bottom steps won’t have dried while you were looking.
