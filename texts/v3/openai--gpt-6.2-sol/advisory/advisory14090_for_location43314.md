Bring earplugs, a bottle of water, and someone who knows the stair. The person you want is a neighbor who uses the passage or a mason who checks the roots. Ask them to show you how they lift the bottom step and how they keep it up. The thumb hollow looks like a good grip until you try it. You don’t want to discover how little purchase it gives you with your other hand under the stone.

Sit on the landing for a minute before opening it. With your feet flat, you may feel the tremor and still swear it’s traffic. Put the earplugs in *then*. The change when the step comes up is likely to be more startling than the volume itself: what sounded like something passing overhead turns out to be right beneath your knees. Agree on a hand signal for closing the step, since you may not be able to hear each other. If a root gives way and the roar drops out, stay still. The answering slabs are the one thing you can’t count on hearing twice.

Go at noon for a first visit. You’ll be able to see the pale oil in the thinnest stems and pick out the oven and the cupboard by their shadows. Look from the landing rather than lowering your head into the opening. The fans of leaves make a convincing false floor from above, but there’s no reason to trust the middle with so much as your weight on one hand. Secure anything you’d ordinarily set beside you—a phone, a lens cap, your water bottle. Don’t drop a pebble to see how far down it goes. People have evidently been able to live with not knowing.

Leave the leaves and the crack marks alone. The nut smell isn’t an invitation to taste anything, and the shining tracks are a better thing to find than a blind animal you’ve waited in its way to see. If a mason comes by, give them the landing. Ask which mark they checked last time. Then ask them to show you the old floor. It’s one thing to be told that roots can make limestone ring; it’s another to stand beside a mark halfway up a wall and realize where the person who scratched it had their feet.

If you come back after dark, don’t expect cool stone. The brick keeps the day’s heat, though the leaves turn edgewise and the garden sounds like a cart that never quite goes by. Lower the step gently when you’re done. Leave the passage clear for whoever comes next to sit down and knock the salt out of their shoes.
