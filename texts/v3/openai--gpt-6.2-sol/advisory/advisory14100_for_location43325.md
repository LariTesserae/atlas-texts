Come in the season when the old paths show. Give yourself a morning to learn what one looks like: a run of shorter plants, not necessarily a gap in the green. Look down it from a crouch. You may find you can see its course quite plainly at knee height even when, standing up, you can’t see the person twenty paces ahead of you. A long swell that looks like an easy walk before breakfast may take you until noon. Plan the first day as an out-and-back, and turn while you can still see the taller crowns.

**Bring equipment for being warm and wet at the same time.** Wear light, quick-drying clothes, grippy boots that drain, and gaiters to keep stems and grit out. Bring a spare pair of socks and a dry sleeping layer in a sealed bag. Two trekking poles will earn their weight, as will a headlamp you can reach without opening your pack. The cloud may come down gently enough that you don’t notice how dark it’s got until you stop to find something. Carry drinking water or a reliable way to treat it; clear brown water is not necessarily clean, and getting to it may be harder than it looks.

**The surface is not a reliable guide to what’s underneath.** The most dangerous crossing may be the one that doesn’t look like a crossing. Stop if the ground flexes under a light step, if you hear water without seeing it, or if the leaves continue to dip in a line after you’ve stopped moving. Back away along known footing. A doe can spread her weight across a mat that won’t hold a person with a pack, and a pole that meets roots tells you little about what lies beneath them. Detour to a crossing you can actually inspect, or turn back. Don’t make the success of the trip depend on finding a way over.

Bring a compass and a waterproof notebook, and keep a record of turns and elapsed walking time. Test any navigation or return device at your arrival point before trusting it farther afield. If you can choose where to place your return point, put it on verified firm ground above the wet hollows. A stone enclosure can make a good check on your route, but note its shape and which side you approached from; another low wall over the next swell may look much the same. Deer trails are worth noticing, not trusting.

If you stay overnight, choose your site before the afternoon cloud forms. Look for firm, slightly raised footing and check where water could collect as the beds fill. A rooted trunk may have built a dry strip along one side of itself, but walk its edge before committing to it. Bring a shelter that doesn’t depend on stakes holding in wet roots. The dry space beneath an upturned root plate will be tempting. Leave it to the animals: you can’t readily tell what’s holding that ceiling up, and the fact that it has stood for years doesn’t make it a safe roof.

If “high enough to have clouds beneath it” also means thin air, take the first day slowly. The warmth and the gentle grades can make it easy to mistake altitude symptoms for ordinary tiredness. Don’t press farther upland with a worsening headache, nausea, or unusual breathlessness; use your return route.

Take some time to sit on firm ground and let things come close. You’ll hear more once you stop making the leaves move. The little walls are worth looking at without clearing the roots from their joints, and a rubbed corner will tell you more about the stone than pulling up a whole patch of growth. On a clear morning, watch a cloud shadow cross the swell you mean to walk. It won’t give you a distance, but it may show you that what you took for one rise is two. That’s a good thing to learn before you set out.
