Bring what you’d take for a day on a high, wet moor, plus enough to spend the night if your way back closes. The place will look like a city just when you’re tired enough to expect city conveniences: a street that goes through, a dry doorstep, somebody within earshot. It has none of those.

Start with a short visit from the ascent. Mark the point where you came out of the blue bank on an offline map, and carry a compass; don’t make your return depend on recognizing a particular court from the opposite direction. On the way in, look back at each important junction and note a higher alternative to any crossing that depends on a low sill. If there’s no such alternative, make that crossing your turnaround point. Tell someone when you expect to be back. The plateau is broad enough to disappear into without ever feeling that you’ve gone very far.

Wear grippy boots and bring waterproof layers, warm clothing, spare socks in a dry bag, and a headlamp. A walking pole is useful for checking whether a shallow pool has a solid floor *within reach*, but not for feeling your way across a dark seam. Step around those. A seam the width of a stride can hide several levels of stone, and the shelf that keeps the rain off a lower passage tells you nothing about what will bear your weight above it. Keep well back from the plateau edge, especially in poor visibility. If your ascent has brought you high enough to leave you unusually breathless or headachy, shorten the outing rather than counting on the gentle terrain to make it easy.

Watch the ridges as well as the sky overhead. A shower you never stood in can fill the court you meant to cross, and the water can keep rising after the shower is out of sight. Don’t count on hearing it coming. If a crossing starts to fill, backtrack while you still can; don’t follow a lower passage just because it’s dry at the entrance. For a rest or an emergency bivouac, choose a broad, stable bench above the spillways, away from fissures. A sheltered strip under a lip may be pleasantly dry in the rain and still be a bad place to sleep.

Carry drinking water. The pools are clear enough to make kneeling for a drink seem natural, but clarity doesn’t tell you whether unfamiliar water is safe, and ordinary treatment won’t remove every dissolved contaminant. Bring food, too. There’s little to forage, and the willow and sedge have precious few places to grow. Walk on sound bare rock where you can rather than cutting through those pockets of grit.

Leave time to stand still somewhere safely above a court. The most useful thing you can learn on a first visit is how one basin hands its water to the next. Watch a shower cross a distant ridge, then see how long it takes for the thin fans of runoff to reach the flats. On the way home, the first sign that your morning route has changed may be nothing more than a fresh wet line on a pale wall.
