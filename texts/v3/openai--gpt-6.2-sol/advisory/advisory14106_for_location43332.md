Visit on a dry morning, before the ovens are fully hot. Ask the baker to show you down. The stairs are still swept, the baskets and jars are still in use, and the drain is still cleared when it stops. This is a working room, even if nobody happens to be working in it when you arrive. The baker will know which bit of floor you can borrow.

Wear closed shoes with good grip and clothes that let you turn sideways without catching a hem. The middles of the stair treads are worn into bowls, and the brick below can be slick even when there’s no water running. Carry a small light so you can see where one low terrace drops to the next, but keep it pointed down. Leave a bulky pack upstairs, especially if it’s what you use to travel between worlds. If you can choose where you arrive and depart, choose the street, not the cellar: twelve paces sounds like plenty until you see how little of it is free floor.

Listen for rain. The rattle in the stack of pots is your warning while the floor still looks dry. If you hear it, go back up rather than stepping down to watch the first brown sheet come in. You can watch from the doorway if you’re not in anyone’s way. Don’t drink from the pans, and don’t put a hand behind a basket to steady yourself. The house snake has a good reason to stay out of sight; give it no reason to move.

Once you’ve found a place to stand, stay there a little longer than feels natural. The street opening gives you light without much of a view, and the vault makes it hard at first to tell what’s overhead and what’s right beside you. The knock of the kneading trough is a good sound to start with. Wait for it to stop. You’ll hear the crickets differently, although they haven’t changed their pitch at all.

If you can come back on a dry evening, watch the little hollows instead of the drain. That’s where the sparrows will come to drink. Don’t top up a pan, prop the broken one more neatly, or turn the barley jar so you can see its green side. The room doesn’t need putting right for you to get a good look at it.
