Plan a short daylight visit from a fixed arrival point. The plain looks easy to cross because it is flat, but almost every step takes you over a rounded lip of limestone into a shallow channel. From standing height you can see for miles without seeing what lies in the next runnel. A pale ledge is not a reliable foothold, even if you’ve watched it for a minute and it hasn’t moved.

Before stepping through, check the air at the arrival point rather than assuming that rain and familiar-looking rock mean you can breathe there. If the air is safe, bring boots with good wet-rock grip, gaiters, gloves for an accidental fall, and a trekking pole for balance. Plant the pole only where you can see bare stone; don’t use it to find out whether a shape is alive. Carry all your drinking water. The pools under the overhangs are occupied even when they look clear, and the black grit is no cleaner for being dry. Cover cuts before you go, keep wet gloves away from your face, and bag muddy gear before bringing it back into your living space.

**The important route is the one back.** Mark the arrival point with a raised beacon you can retrieve, and record your outbound track with a device that doesn’t depend on local satellites or a familiar magnetic field. Set a turnaround time that leaves plenty of daylight. Walk with a partner if you can: one person can watch the next few steps while the other keeps track of the route. Favor visibly bare ridge tops, but don’t assume they’ll stay clear when the wind starts dropping food. If the only way onward is across an animal or through a channel you can’t inspect, turn back. There is no reason to count on a large animal moving aside, or on it staying put while you step over it.

Carry a headlamp and a spare even for a daylight trip. A light held low across the rock can reveal a gap beneath what looked like a solid ledge. Use your ears to notice changes, not to locate a safe crossing: the bellow may come from below your line of sight, and the smaller rattles can continue inside a body that has gone quiet. Rain makes the footing worse at exactly the moment the plain starts to sound less crowded. If you’re on a verified patch of bare rock when a shower arrives, stop there rather than hurrying toward an overhang. The shelter you’ve spotted may already have something under it, waiting for the same drip.

For a first visit, there’s more to see from one good stance than on a long traverse. Bring binoculars or a zoom lens and watch a single runnel from the rim. You may see the little animals hurry *toward* a descending edge, or a pool turn white for an instant under one drop. If you want to hear the rain quiet the plain in bands, do it close to your way home. The silence isn’t the animals leaving. It’s them opening their joints to drink.
