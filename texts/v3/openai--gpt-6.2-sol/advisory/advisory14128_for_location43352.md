Pack for a warm city that never quite dries. The rain you can see may be a thousand feet below you; the drop that lands on your neck has been gathering on a trolley wire since dawn.

**Bring shoes you trust on wet stone.** The old stairs are hollowed by feet, and a tread that looks level from above may be slick and cupped where everyone steps. Take the handrail, especially on the way down. Wear light, quick-drying clothes and bring a brimmed hat: you’ll want it for the sun on the outer tramline as much as for the drips. Keep your papers and anything electronic in a dry pouch. A heavy raincoat is likely to spend most of the visit rolled up under your arm.

**Book a room on an upper terrace for your first night.** Ask whether it faces a pump, a sorting shed, or a landing ledge. The calls from below can come through the floor as much as through the window, so earplugs may only do half the job. Ask for directions by terrace as well as by street name. A tram can pass practically over your lodging without offering any way down to it. On your first outing, find the stairs or inland tram stop you’ll use to get home before you wander down to the canals.

**Treat the water as habitat, not as a public bath or a drinking supply.** Ask your host where to fill a bottle, and try the local harvest cooked at a busy shop rather than tasting anything straight from a sluice. The sluice is worth a look from a public walkway: in a handspan of water you can see what most of the city lives on. Watch where you put your hands under bridges at dusk, when the big shells reach out to feed. And if a brown animal stands up on the embankment, give it the whole way to the canal. Don’t count on the others shifting aside to give you an escape route.

**Ask about the basin level separately from the weather.** A sunny afternoon tells you very little about whether the riverside track will be running next week. If the shopkeepers are clearing lower storerooms, take the hint: move your own things uphill and learn the inland route. There’s no need to leave town just because the herds are coming. It’s a procession, not an emergency, provided you watch it from where the city has made room for you. Buy lunch, take a stool under an awning if one’s offered, and let the animals set the pace.

On a clear afternoon after rain, ride the outer tram and look for the far wall of the basin. Then, on the way back, stop in one of the tiled stairwells and listen. After an hour of pumps and wheels and calls you can feel through your shoes, it’s a surprise to discover how clearly you can hear one drop hitting a sill.
