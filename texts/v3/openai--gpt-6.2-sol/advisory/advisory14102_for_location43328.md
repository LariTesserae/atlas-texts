Visit in the morning, from the lower end, and plan to spend most of your time on the dry side of the bedrock sill. From there you can watch the marsh and the glacier without having to get across either. For a first visit, make it a half-day outing. If you can choose where you arrive, choose firm ground below the sill, with a retreat that doesn’t depend on the pass staying visible.

**Dress for a day that never quite dries out.** The cloud doesn’t have to turn to rain to soak your cuffs, and you’ll get cold quickest when you stop to watch something. Wear a windproof waterproof shell, warm layers, gloves you can handle wet buckles in, and sturdy waterproof boots with soles that grip wet rock. Gaiters will help with shallow sheet flow; they won’t make the marsh walkable. Pack dry gloves and an insulating layer in a waterproof bag, along with food, drinking water, a headlamp, and an emergency shelter. If you’ve come straight from low elevation, take the first uphill stretch slowly. Breathlessness out of proportion to the effort, or a worsening headache with nausea, is a reason to descend, not to sit in the cold and see how it goes.

**Navigate by the rock, not the water.** Take a compass and an offline map or other navigation aid that works in this world. If there’s no reliable map, record your outward route and look back often enough to learn what the return looks like. A pool edge can move while you’re standing beside it, and yesterday’s dry strip may be carrying today’s meltwater. Favor broad, dry bedrock over loose rubble. Test doubtful footing with a pole from a stable stance; a slab can look as neatly fitted as a paving stone and still rock under your weight. At the sill, watch especially for clear ice where seepage runs over the stone. It can look no different from a wet patch.

Set a turnaround time before you leave, with enough margin to retrace the route slowly in cloud. Don’t revise it because the opposite slope looks close. If the cloud starts erasing the landmarks you need for the return, go back while you still have them. If you must enter and leave by the pass, save the trip for a settled weather window rather than counting on an evening crossing. The geese can climb straight out of the valley; you can’t.

**Give the glacier and the marsh room.** Don’t approach the ice front, step onto ice at the margins, or follow a meltwater route into a hollow beneath rubble. The little wave arriving after the sound from the glacier tells you that even a pool well away from the ice can feel a disturbance. Watch from firm ground above the shore, not from a low spit that gives you only one way back. Keep off the skim ice and the apparently solid silt at the margins. Neither has to be deep to soak a boot or put you down hard on sharp stone.

Bring binoculars. They’ll do more for this visit than an extra mile of walking. Settle somewhere that doesn’t make the geese stop feeding, and give your eyes time to separate the waders from the stones. You can watch the black sand collect behind a rock and the pale silt drift on past it. The valley looks uneventful at a distance because so much of what’s happening is only a few millimetres high. Pick one patch of shore and stay long enough to see it change.
