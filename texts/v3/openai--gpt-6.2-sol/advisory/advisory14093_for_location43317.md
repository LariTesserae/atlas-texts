Plan to arrive by the old trail in daylight and spend the first visit on the near shoulder. Thirty paces is a short crossing once there’s a pass. Until then, it’s thirty paces of ground that can look finished before it takes weight. If you need to be on the far side by nightfall, take another route.

Wear a hooded shell, boots with good wet-rock grip, and a dry layer in a sealed bag. The water is warm, but you’re still high on a ridge and you can get thoroughly wet in half a minute. Don’t shelter under a lip just because the drops are going up: the ledges are where the beads gather, and the flat animals are already using the undersides. Wait well back on old ground. Bring a headlamp even at noon. A low beam across the floor will tell you more about a seam than a bright beam aimed straight into the black stone.

Watch the posts from the old trail. Their alignment is a good reason to stop and listen, not a signal to cross. The change from hollow drumming to a hard clatter tells you that rain has found stone somewhere; it doesn’t tell you there’s stone all the way across. If you go with someone, agree on a hand signal for *stay there* before you spread out. During a downpour you can see somebody perfectly well and have no idea they’re calling to you.

The evening procession is worth staying for if you have a safe walk back. Watch where the little animals put their long forefeet, and especially where they go around. The patch with the late reflection is easier to notice once you know where not to look for a foothold. But a dozen animals crossing in single file proves only that the strip can hold a dozen small animals, one at a time. Come back in the morning and ask someone who uses the pass whether people have started crossing. If nobody has, don’t make your own weight the test.

Leave the cup and cap on the hook. They’re somebody’s things, and in a lull the cap dripping into the cup is one of the best ways to hear how small the saddle really is. You don’t need to cross to have visited it. From the old shoulder, you can watch a moth’s shadow pass over the grass, then look up and find the moth itself walking on the water.
