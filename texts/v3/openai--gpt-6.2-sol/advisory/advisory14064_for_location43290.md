**Plan to spend your first visit on one shore, through one rise and fall of the water. Don’t try to cross the plain.** The water that covers your boot soles is not, by itself, the danger. The danger is that getting back may take an afternoon on ground that can change while you’re standing on it.

Put your arrival point on the landward side of the farthest flood mark you can identify, on ground that doesn’t move with the hammering. Check that your way home still works there before walking out. If you can’t find such a place, observe from the threshold. A shelf with a good view is not necessarily high ground; the water can go around it and leave you a long walk from the nearest dry footing. Bring a companion and, on the first outing, have one of you stay at the exit. Agree on a return time by the clock. Neither a shout nor a whistle is worth much over that mill noise.

Dress for getting wet even if you mean to stay dry. Wear insulated waterproof boots with good grip, and carry dry socks, spare footwear, and a windproof layer in a sealed bag. Bring your own drinking water and something warm in a flask. Use hearing protection that still lets you hear a person close by, and have a visual signal for turning back. A headlamp is useful even at noon: when the whole west brightens, it still won’t light the hole in front of your boot.

Walk slowly enough to look at where the *next* foot will land. The white calcite beds are loose, the polished shelves are slick, and a smooth dark patch can be the mouth of a well. A walking pole can help on a shelf you can see clearly, but a solid tap doesn’t tell you that the plate will stay put. Give moving seams a wide berth. Don’t tug at the copper, even where it looks like a loose scrap. One end is still passing between stone plates.

Watch the seams behind you as often as the ones ahead. The black lines widening on your return route are your cue to go, not to see how fast the water comes. Give channels and broad animal shadows room, including shadows with nothing in them yet. If one settles over your route, go around by known ground or turn back. You don’t know whether the animal will arrive by way of the channel you can see, or whether that channel will still be shallow when it does.

**Don’t step over the trough.** The two banks disagree about what’s below, and a boot can fill without being visibly immersed or punctured. A dry pole or a tossed stone won’t settle which conditions will apply to you halfway across. Look from well back on the bank you came by. If a boot fills anywhere on the plain, get back to stable ground, change into your spare, and check your foot for cuts or numbness. Leave the grit and whatever came in with it on that side of the exit.

The evening pause is worth hearing from shore. You may see the little combers fold all at once and then open in a line from the west. Don’t take their reopening, or the first retreat of the water, as an all-clear. A channel too shallow for a large animal in the morning can carry it away by evening. And if a stranded animal leaves behind a wet ridge that goes on breathing, give the ridge the same room you gave the animal. There’s no need to find out which of them the plain considers still present.
