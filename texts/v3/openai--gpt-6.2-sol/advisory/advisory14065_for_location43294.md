Bring something to kneel on, a layer with sleeves, and enough time to look twice. The stone is hard even where it fits an elbow, and the cold at the opening will surprise you if you’ve come down from a street full of noon light. A folded scarf will do for your knees. Keep it clear of the sill: what looks like a patch of gray dust may be seven moths.

Go at noon on your first visit. The shaft is narrow, but the whole white city seems to help light it. You won’t need a lamp. It may take a little while for your eyes to stop asking for the brightness of the street. Let them settle on something easy—the worn groove in the sill, or the rim of the basin—and then look into the joints between the stones. The room is about the size of a bed, but the square opening gives you only one angle at a time. Moving your head an inch will show you more than reaching in.

Plan to stay outside. You can see the pale places where the leaves touch without parting them, and there’s no need to find out what’s inside a shell by picking it up. Don’t bring water for the plants. A dry basin is ordinary here. If you’re curious about how the garden drinks, come back a day or two after rain and look at the edges, where the moss meets the water. The change happens slowly enough that you might see a darkened stone on one visit and water on the next. You won’t hear it arrive.

The mallets above will make it hard to believe at first that anything down here is moving. Watch one small patch until you know it well enough to notice a difference. A moth may turn out to have been there the whole time. The white-legged creature may cross into the light and go backward, and you may spend the rest of your visit looking for it without seeing it again. That would be a good visit. There’s no need to make anything come out.

If your travels let you return years later, take a little sketch rather than trying to memorize the view. Mark the hinge hole, the worn side of the basin, and the fork where the green meets its shadow. Compare the sketch afterward. When you first kneel down again, give yourself a moment to see where your elbow goes.
