Pack for a cool, damp hill town where you may have to walk several wards to ask a question. Bring boots that grip wet stone, a warm layer, a rain shell, a headlamp, and enough food and drinking water for the visit. Don’t count on buying lunch just because there’s a market, or on finding a dry seat just because a wall feels warm. Try the bench above the roofs first.

**Arrange your way out before you go down to the stream.** If your crossing can be reopened, agree on a return time with someone on the other side, using their clock. Bring a watch and a paper map, and note the turns on your way in. Don’t use a puddle drying, the ovens cooling, or a patch of sun reaching a stair as a timer. There’s no reason to assume your own watch will stop; there’s also no reason to assume the town’s ordinary signs of passing time will tell you what you need to know.

Stay on the stairs and paved streets for your first walk. A slab that still fits the mountain neatly isn’t necessarily well attached, and the shelves over the stream are a poor place to find out how readily the rock breaks. The stream is so quiet you may be tempted to judge it by sound. Look at the footing instead. Leave the covered drains to the owls, give the cattle room, and ask before drawing from the cistern.

The thing to get used to is that **the town persists because people do things, not because nothing happens.** The woman clearing the drain knows exactly what her work is for. The new root doesn’t undo the dry steps the children will have that afternoon. If you offer to help, ask her what needs doing; don’t set about making the drain permanently rootproof. Likewise, don’t pull pottery wedges out of the paving as souvenirs. Somebody needed those.

Spend part of your visit sitting still. Watch a single reach of the stream before walking to the next. Listen long enough to hear a bird twice. Ask a resident when the bread comes out rather than whether it always has. You’ll learn more from the answer, and you may get bread.

On a first visit, leave yourself plenty of time to get back uphill. The house beyond the green shelves looks like somewhere you could walk to before supper. It may be. But you can see its washing from the bench without being able to tell whether it’s moving, and that’s a good reminder of how little a clear view tells you about the ground between here and there.
