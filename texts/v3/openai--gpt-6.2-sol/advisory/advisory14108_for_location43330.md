Visit from the air first. If you can’t hold your position above the valley and leave without touching down, choose another destination. The remarkable thing here isn’t the drop from the rim; it’s that a place well back from the rim can become an edge in a few seconds.

**Bring your own air and water.** Clear air tells you nothing about pressure or breathability. Take a pressure suit with a closed breathing supply on the first visit, and measure the atmosphere before considering anything lighter. The warmth underfoot is no guide to how warm you’ll be standing still. Take insulation for the air and boots with heat-resistant, cut-resistant soles for the ground. Carry water you can drink without opening your helmet. There’s no reason to count on finding any here.

**Make sure your way home doesn’t depend on the floor.** If you travel by gate, open it aboard a hovering craft, not on a promising slab. If you travel by craft alone, keep someone at the controls while anyone is outside. Carry independent lights and a rangefinder; the red band may be useful for looking at the valley, but it’s no good as a horizon or a compass bearing. A ridge can take it away while you’re looking at it. Don’t plan a walking route home by memorizing seams or stacking cairns.

Spend the first visit watching one patch of floor from above. Use a fixed camera if you have one: watching through a moving window, you might mistake the rise of a shelf for a dip of your own craft. Don’t expect the valley to announce a movement before you see it. That little clack could be the end of something you watched fall a long way off. It’s a poor cue to turn around, and a worse cue to step back.

If you do land on a later visit, make it a short stop with the craft ready to lift. Stay off the fitted plates, however much they look like pavement, and well away from cuts and overhangs. Even a solid-looking slab is only a place to stand for the moment, not a refuge. Leave the four-stone arrangements alone. Photograph one in place if you can do it safely. You don’t need to decide whether someone set it there, and moving a stone would erase some of the little evidence you have.

The hardest thing to prepare for may be how ordinary it feels between movements. You’ll be standing in the dry, with warm feet, looking across open country. After a while you’ll want to switch off your lamp, take off a glove, or walk a little farther for a better view. Set a departure time before you arrive. The view worth coming for is the new reach of stone catching the red light and then going dark. You can see that perfectly well from above.
