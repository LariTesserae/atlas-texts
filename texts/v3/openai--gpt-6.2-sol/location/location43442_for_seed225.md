The hollow is just below the hillside, under a roof of chalk thin enough for daylight to come through. On a clear afternoon the whole ceiling shines. There are no shafts of light, no bright spots to stand in. Even the undersides of the ledges are bright, lit from the pale floor, and a hand held out at arm’s length casts a shadow you can see through.

It is a small place. From the entrance you could toss a plum against the far wall. Yet there is so little between one wall and the other that crossing it feels like going out into the open. Nothing has fallen from the ceiling in a long time. The floor rises gently toward a dry lip and sinks at the back into three shallow bowls. Their rims have been worn smooth by water that no longer arrives often enough to fill them. In the deepest bowl there is usually a little moisture, about as much as a cloth would hold after wringing. The stone around it is warm.

The only plants are a fringe of moss under that lip and a few white roots that have come down from the hill. One root has grown along the roof for the length of a finger, apparently unable to decide which way is down. At its tip, late in the afternoon, a drop begins to swell.

There are plenty of animals here, though you would be forgiven for thinking otherwise. The floor looks bare until you lower your cheek to it. Then you can see the jointed legs of little amber creatures under the rims of the bowls, and the mouths of burrows so small a single chalk grain will stop them. A pale cricket can spend the warm part of the day standing on the same four grains. The animals leave the middle of the room alone. At dusk they make their way around the skirts of the walls, one behind another, with generous intervals between them.

Now and then a creature crosses the ceiling on the *other* side of the chalk. You see its shadow swell and then sharpen as it comes close, but you hear nothing of its passage. There is one with six feet and a long body that sometimes stops over the hanging root. The root bends. The shadow carries on. Whatever passes between them takes place in the thickness of the roof.

In dry weather the room is quiet enough that the drop, when it falls, makes two sounds: a tick in the bowl and a soft answering tick somewhere in the wall. After rain there is only the first. The bowl never seems to get any deeper. What changes, over the course of a wet year, is the position of a dark ring no wider than a hair. In the morning, while the hill above is still gray, that ring is the first thing you can see.
