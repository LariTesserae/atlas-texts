The stair comes down into warm rain. At the last tread you can put a hand through it without getting wet: the drops are falling about a pace farther in than they sound. The racket is coming through the stone.

The chamber is small enough that from the stair you can see a white cup on the opposite ledge. Between you and the cup lies a basin of pale, rounded rock, much of it just under water. There is no deep pool. Instead the entire floor seems to be trying to drain in several directions at once. Sheets of water run over one another; little crests rise where they meet, stand trembling for a second, then run sideways and break. Along the walls, ankle-deep channels take this as it comes. Their bends have been worn so smooth that a surge goes around them without splashing, though you can hear it hit the next bend, out of sight, like a door slammed in another room.

There is a lot of light. It comes in through six narrow joints in the ceiling, where someone long ago fitted strips of a dull gray material. The strips are dark to look at. They give light only to things at a distance from them, and only from one side; a falling drop will flash white and leave a tiny traveling shadow on the rock *above* it. Where a strip has chipped, the light goes on across the break. Mineral crust has grown over that one, thick enough to hold the print of a thumb.

Now and then the rain quits in the middle of the chamber. The wet floor shines unbroken for a breath, and you can see beneath the water the old course of the basin, a shallow winding groove no wider than a boot. Then the rain starts up against a wall, and a whole slope of water hurries out to meet it. It is difficult to tell, with that much going on, how little the place is changing. The ledges are broad and blunt. The pockets that fill and empty a dozen times a minute have had time to grow smooth even under their lips.

A few dark green shoots grow where the stair meets the ledge. Their roots go into the mortar and nowhere else, although there are acres, by their measure, of wet stone. On the underside of the lowest tread, something the color of a fingernail holds still through two or three reversals of the current. When it moves, it goes only as far as the next dry patch. Ordinarily you would hear its shell tick against the stone. Here you see the little jolt and supply the sound yourself.

The cup is the only loose thing anyone has left. It rests in a shallow ring of lime, with its handle turned toward the stair. From time to time a gust off the basin sends spray high enough to reach it. The cup has a crack down to the foot; a fine wet line leads away from it along the ledge. In all that brightness and uproar, the line is the one bit of water you can watch all the way to where it goes.
