At noon the upper valley is hot enough that the rain feels cool for only the first drop. After that it is the same temperature as the leaves. You can watch a shower cross the whole basin, whitening a distant terrace, darkening the next, then disappearing into the glare without ever reaching you. Five minutes later water wells up through the floor at your feet.

There is no rock here, though the ridges have faces as sheer as a fresh quarry. The pale stuff in those faces is the inner wood of plants that grow flat upon one another. On a young break you can see their layers, green at the edge and cream within, and the little wet sockets where the next layer would have fastened. Even at the bottom of the deepest cleft there are only older roots, compressed to a brown substance that takes a polish from running water. Everything is alive to some depth. Where that depth ends, another kind of life begins.

The valley is built almost entirely out of floor. Great flat leaves join at their margins and thicken into terraces hundreds of yards across. The joins are easy to pick out after rain: two rows of raised green stitches with a dark line of water between them. Uprights are rare. A few stand far out on the terraces, each carrying a single crown of leaves high enough that its shadow is something you can see approaching for a long time before it reaches you. There is so much unoccupied space that a new upright, even a small one, changes the look of the valley from miles away.

Nothing made the terraces but the plants, yet it is hard not to think of them as works under way. A new span reaches across a cleft in paired tongues, each curled back at the end until it touches the other. By evening the tips have taken hold and the span is bearing its first load of rain. Elsewhere a gutter ends in midair, pouring warm water down a wall that has only just grown high enough to need it. The following day a lip has turned out beneath the fall. It catches about half. The other half still goes over with a noise you can hear from the opposite side of the basin.

The basin is high enough that its outermost terraces look down on clouds. Their undersides drip into bright, apparently empty air. At that edge the green is so thin that daylight comes through it, showing the branching veins and, now and then, a clot of water moving slowly toward a point. Inland, the heat collects in the sheltered angles. Pools there are shallow and nearly clear, with tiny red leaves spread flat across their bottoms. When a pool drains, the leaves fold in on themselves. A dry patch can go from red to gray while its reflection is still trembling in the water below.

There are no wings over the pools, no small disturbances at their edges. Most of the noise comes from water, and most of the rest from things giving way. A joint opens with a pop that carries in the clear air. A whole row of joints follows it, not quite at once. Sometimes a terrace tilts very slowly, and its water runs off in a widening sheet while the roots beneath it stretch into view. A fall on the far side of the valley can be almost silent where you stand; a minute later, the basin sends back a low, surprisingly intimate thud.

After a particularly hard shower, a long stretch of the western rim lets go. For a while there is an unobstructed view into the country below, ordinary blue distance with clouds throwing shadows on it. The cut is sharp enough to cast a line like the edge of a blade. Already, in the sunlight along that line, the opened layers are swelling. Small leaves lift out of them, lying horizontal over the drop. They are very far apart. Rain lands on the first of them and holds there, a single bright bead on a floor that goes no farther.
