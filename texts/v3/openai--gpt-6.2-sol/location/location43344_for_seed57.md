There is a garden under the lowest step of the drowned house. You could cover its entrance with the heel of your hand. The house is gone above the second course of stones, so there is nothing to tell you that you are looking at a step until you feel the shallow wear in its middle. The garden is underneath, where the stone broke across a bed of black clay when it settled. The break is still sharp enough to cut a root.

It is cold in there, though no colder than the water outside. The difference is that outside the water moves past you, and here it seems to stay in contact with the same bit of skin. There is no air. The roof is wet all the way up.

From the opening you can see perhaps the first finger’s breadth. Liverwort lies flat against the clay, its overlapping lobes the green of the inside of a split stem. Farther in, the growth thickens into pale, jointed things, with crowded whorls of leaves scarcely wider than hairs. They grow from the walls and the underside of the step as readily as from the floor. There is room for water between the leaves but not for a leaf to turn over. When a stem dies it keeps its place until the stems on either side have grown into it.

The stone is older than anything under it. A little mortar clings to one end, and in the mortar is the corner of a red tile, set there flush to make the step sit level. The clay below is raw. Along the break you can still make out the fine parallel ridges left when the two faces pulled apart. Silt has not softened them. In this much water you would expect a steady fall of silt, but the few grains that come through the entrance lodge on the first leaves and stay there.

The garden has a far wall. You can touch it without putting your wrist through the opening. It feels close and faintly gritty. But a root growing straight toward that wall does not reach it. There are roots in there older than the flood, roots with generations of dead side shoots combed into their length, all still growing toward that same little patch of grit. If you put a finger against the wall and follow a root backward with your nail, you come to its beginning almost at once. The root takes much longer to come to your finger.

Once in a while a white shrimp edges out among the leaves. Its shell is clear at the joints. It feeds by passing a stalk between its mouthparts, drawing the stalk through over and over without taking a bite you can see. It can spend the whole afternoon at this. At last it backs into a cleft too narrow for the thickest part of its body. The leaves on either side do not move.

Sound seldom comes from the garden. What you hear, with your ear to the step, is the rest of the flooded ground: a pebble knocking over in the open water, a slow scrape as something distant drags along the bottom. Now and then, after a long quiet, there is the sound of water falling from a height into water. It is a small sound, a single drop. There is nowhere underneath the step for it to fall from.

In the season when the water outside clears, a little more light gets in. The outermost leaves put out clear tips, and for a few days you can see the red corner of the tile without touching it. Deeper in, there are leaves folded around buds that have not opened. Some are folded so tightly that the seams have grown together. One has opened against the far wall, where no light reaches. If you run your finger along the grit, you can feel its edges. It has made a place for itself there by growing thinner and thinner, until there is scarcely anything of it but the two surfaces, one against the stone and one against the water.
