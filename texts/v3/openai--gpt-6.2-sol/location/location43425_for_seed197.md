The town takes up about as much ground as a farm pond. You can stand at one gate and see daylight through the other. Between them lie six streets, a square, and a row of narrow houses with no backs. Everything is alive, down to the greenish curb that lifts a little in the afternoon and settles again after dark.

There is almost nothing in the streets. The houses are hollow, too. All that growth has gone into making surfaces: the thin, hard floors, the walls with their soft inner bark, the canopy drawn tight over the square. Where a wall bends out of the way of a doorway, you can follow the grain around the bend and see that nothing was cut. There are no joints. In one house a stair climbs the wall in seventeen shallow folds, arriving at a little landing with an excellent view of the house across the street. The landing has worn smooth under the feet of animals that no longer go there.

It is warm enough that the damp never feels cold. Rain comes briefly, sometimes in full sun, and stays in the town long after the low country around it has dried. It beads under the eaves and darkens the bottoms of the walls. The square slopes almost imperceptibly toward a drain shaped like a closed eyelid. When the water is deep enough, the lid opens. For a few seconds you can see pale root tips combing through the runoff. Then the square is empty again except for a stain with a crisp, advancing edge.

The noise is unbearable. The sensible thing would be to get indoors, but the walls are what make most of it. Each street has grown to a different pitch. Water runs down chambers in the wood; ribs under the pavement flex against one another; broad leaves slap flat against the canopy and are torn free by the wind they make. Now and then a fruit splits and the whole square takes up the report. You can shout into the ear of someone touching shoulders with you and know by their face that they heard only the town.

The animals go about in the little shelter of its rhythms. Along the western street, at each lull between two enormous knocks, small gray lizards cross from one wall to the other. Look down the street at the wrong moment and there are thirty of them in the open, all running. Look again and there is not one. Bats sleep by the hundred in seams no wider than a thumb. A long-legged bird hunts in the drain, drawing up one foot at a time so carefully that the water does not register its departure. There are tracks all over the wet thresholds. There is rarely anything standing in a doorway.

At noon, an unremarkable light falls into the square. You can see brown patches in the green paving, the minute serrations along a leaf, a fly washing its head. You can also see both gates. That is the best time to notice the trouble with the streets. Take the short one toward the far gate and you pass the square on your left. Turn back without turning any corners and you pass it on your left again. The bird in the drain is still drawing up the same foot. The fly has finished washing.

This has been going on for a long time. At the bend where the short street comes back wrong, the curb is worn into two parallel grooves, one for each direction of travel. After rain, a thread of water follows each groove. Both threads run downhill. They meet under the same sill and go on, separately, into the roots.
