At the height where snow ought to begin, there is a shore.

It runs for perhaps six miles around a shallow basin, though there is no lake in the basin, only bright shelves of slate and a scatter of pools. On a clear day you can see the whole farther shore, including the white stones that mark a stair cut into the headland. Everything below the rim is lost in cloud. Stand at a breach and look down and you may see, through a moving hole in the whiteness, another range of mountains with weather of its own.

The slate is wet even on its undersides. Water beads along the edges of the shelves, swells, and falls with a hard little tick. A great many of those ticks make up the sound of the basin. Over them comes the bellow of water going through a slot in the western wall. It wells out of one crack and disappears into another less than a yard away. You can feel that short fall through the soles of your feet a long way off.

The sun is fierce here, but the rock stays cold. Wherever a shelf shades a pool, a thin pane of ice reaches out from the shaded bank. When the sun gets under it the pane breaks loose and turns slowly, trailing its little combs of air. There is no green under it. Even the oldest pools have bare bottoms. Their colors come from the stone: black where the slate is whole, pale gold where it has gone to flakes, a milky blue where something has ground it finer than flour. A few pools have rims of soft white mineral, built over many years and worn through at the low end. The overflow is already building the next rim down.

At intervals along the shore are low walls, hardly knee-high, each with a smooth stone set crosswise at either end. Their builders had a good eye for a stone that would sit without rocking. Here and there a wedge is still in place, hammered so far into its seam that only the blunt end shows. One wall shelters a dry patch no bigger than a coat. Another runs straight into a pool and can be seen continuing across the bottom, its capstones bright and sharp through four feet of water.

The stair on the far side is harder to account for. From across the basin it descends from the ridge in fourteen distinct steps. At its foot, where the first tread ought to be, there is a clean vertical face. Put your hand to that face and the next step is under your heel. You can climb all fourteen. At the top you are back on the shore, a little farther along, and the water you knocked from your boots is still falling past the place where you started. The steps are hollowed in the middle from use. On the third there is a shallow round cup, just the size of a thumb, where someone used to set a hand down.

Most of the basin goes on in a more ordinary way. Frost puts a finger into a split and loosens a slab; the slab falls and for a few winters makes a new note in a stream. Fine grit gathers in the lee of the walls. Cloud comes up the windward face and spills through the gaps, and for ten minutes the world is close and dripping. Then the cloud shears away and every wet surface throws back the sun at once.

Late in the day the pools begin to rise. You can watch the water creep up the mineral rims, though nothing flows into them. The little western fall grows louder. At a certain level the pools join by narrow channels so shallow the water scarcely covers the stone. Seen from the headland, they make a single shining figure across the floor of the basin. Seen from below, through a gap in the clouds, the same water hangs in long threads from the underside of the mountain. Some of the threads reach right down into the weather. The rest end in bright beads that shake in the wind and do not fall.
