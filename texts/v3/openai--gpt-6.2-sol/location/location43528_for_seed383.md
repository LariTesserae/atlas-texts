The city is brightest at the bottom.

From the stair that comes down through the bedrock, you can see almost the whole of it: a pale plain seven miles long, with houses set far enough apart that each has its own patch of brightness. The ceiling is low at the edges and too high in the middle to make out. Nothing shines down from it. The light comes out of the worn pavement, and on a dry day you can see the undersides of lintels more clearly than their tops.

There is no soil. The plain is made of mica and a white stone that takes a polish from bare feet. Far from the streets it is already polished, though no one goes there now. The old river did that work. You can follow its loops by the way the light falls sideways from the shallow banks. In one bend stands a district of roofless houses, each with a round hole through its back wall where the current used to pass. The holes have lost their tool marks. In places the walls themselves have worn so thin that a hand on the other side shows through, pink at the knuckles.

Rain seldom reaches this depth as rain. It comes through seams in the ceiling and hangs there until the drops are too heavy. A single drop can fall all morning’s distance and strike a dry square at noon. The stone goes dark where it lands. For an hour afterward the whole square smells of a cup that has just been rinsed. Water for drinking is kept in deep jars; water for washing collects under stairs, and children know which steps to skip by the chill around their ankles.

The houses are small, but the city was laid out for distances. There are avenues, bridges over channels dry for generations, a market with numbered stalls, and public stairs broad enough for fifty people abreast. At most hours you can cross the market without passing within a stone’s throw of anyone. Tools hang outside the workshops: antler picks, wooden wedges, copper blades rubbed almost to nothing. A mason can spend half a day fitting a sill, then look up to find that the house opposite has moved.

That is a property of the plain. It is not the bedrock that moves, but the broad plates of pavement laid over it. They travel in fits, some scarcely the width of a thumb in a year, others the length of a street between one meal and the next. Nobody builds across a seam. Doorsteps are loose, so they can be taken in. A house may belong to the potters’ district for three generations and arrive one morning among the people who carve bowls. It keeps its old address until someone has time to cut a new one.

From above, when several plates are moving, the city looks frantic. Courtyards rotate. Alleys narrow to black lines and open elsewhere into squares. People run out carrying stools, ropes, bowls of dough. A woman on a moving terrace holds out a cup to someone standing still, and for a moment neither can judge where their hands will meet. Yet from the stair you hear only an occasional knock. The plates ride on beds of stone flour, ground finer than ash. Below the noise of sandals and the clack of a dropped lid, their passage is felt mainly in the soles of the feet.

The animals are quicker to notice it. Pale jointed things live under the paving and come out by the hundred when a seam opens, their backs brilliant and their legs almost invisible against the light. They race across an empty square like a change in the grain of the stone. Along the old riverbanks live soft, six-legged grazers that rasp the faint brown skin from damp rock. There is hardly enough of that skin to see, except where something has eaten a path through it. The grazers leave clean, shining trails. At dusk they gather beneath the bridges, not for shelter from weather but because the darkness there is deep enough to sleep in.

Dusk is uneven. The white stone spends its light faster where it is wet, and after a rare season of seepage whole neighborhoods go dim while the dry avenues remain bright. On those evenings the city looks inhabited chiefly in the dark places. There are people sitting in doorways, eating or mending sandals, with their feet out in the light. Far off, beyond the last houses, a strip of the old riverbed slides past the foot of the stair. Its banks are so rubbed down that you would not know them for banks but for the little pools that travel with them, each carrying, unbroken, its reflection of the stone below.
