At the head of the valley there is a sill of white stone. Water crosses it in a sheet so thin that you can see the marks of the hammer underneath. Below, the valley opens for several miles, bright from rim to rim. There is nowhere to put a foot that somebody has not made level, or deliberately left sloping.

The country is high enough that the water stays cold through the brightest part of the day. It lies in shallow trays, in the joints of the paving, in thumbprints left in the clay caps of walls. Long slopes are tiled with overlapping flakes of slate. Their exposed edges are still keen, and a few have the pale bruised places where a wedge took them off the parent slab. Nothing has had time to round. On the undersides of the overhangs there are wet beads that grow almost imperceptibly until two touch and fall as one.

From the upper rim the place looks open. Once down in it, you find it has scarcely a vacancy. A lane is also a drain, and the drain is crowded with stones set on edge to keep the water from hurrying. The steps are hollowed at alternate ends to carry the overflow from one level to the next. Between two flights of steps, where there might have been a patch of waste ground, there are hundreds of little clay dishes. The dishes nearest the wall are deep and nearly black; those at the outer edge are scarcely more than saucers. They hold the same rain at different temperatures. On a clear morning the little ones are empty before there is any visible change in the large ones.

Everything you can see was laid by hand. Even the long ridge across the valley is a stack: broad footings below, baskets of chips tipped out and packed into the middle, closely fitted capstones above. There are no arches wider than a person can reach. Wherever a wall grows tall, it grows thick enough to walk on. The largest stones have shallow sockets in their sides, in pairs, where the lifting poles went. Here and there a socket has become the mouth of a seep. The water comes out perfectly clear, though the stones around it are orange.

There is no green in the valley. A powdery bloom beneath the old waterlines looks soft enough to be alive, but comes away in dry grains and will not return until the next time the water drops. No wings cross the bright floors of the basins. At midday you can see down through six inches of water to the little flakes that broke off when the basin was made. They still lie where they fell.

For so much water, there is very little sound. The builders gave it surfaces to cling to. It rounds a lip and runs along the underside before it finds the next wall. A few channels cross the lanes on narrow stone bridges; if you put an ear to the side of one, you can hear the thread going over. The far end of the valley makes a dry tick now and then. It is a row of empty jars with lids that shift a little as the air inside them warms.

Near evening, the lower courts are already in shadow when the water in them turns white. The light comes down by reflection from the high trays, which are still in full sun. For a while you can see the underside of every stair in a court more clearly than you can see the stair itself.

It might take a week of rain to send water over the sill at the bottom of the valley. Before that can happen, each shallow place between here and the rim has to take its share. Some are so small they hold only the skin of a drop. Some are rooms without roofs, with steps descending from all four sides. When the last room is full, water moves across a strip of paving that has been dry for months. The first thing it reaches is a low stone bench. The people who laid the paving gave the bench four feet instead of a solid base, and the water goes quietly between them.
