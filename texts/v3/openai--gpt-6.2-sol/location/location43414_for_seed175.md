High on the shoulder of the mountain, there is a hollow no wider than the spread of a large tree. From below you would pass it off as a dark seam in the rock. From above you can see that it holds water, though nowhere in it is there enough depth to lose sight of the stone.

The stone has worn into broad, shallow steps. Water covers the middle of each step and leaves the edges dry, so that the pale rims look almost chalky in the afternoon light. Where the water spills from one level to the next, it does so from underneath. The lips are dry on top. Put a finger to the underside of one and a thread of warm water will run down your wrist. Take your hand away and the thread draws itself back against the rock.

There are only four or five places where plants have taken hold. Each is extravagant. Ferns lean out of a joint in the wall, their new fronds yellow and translucent; beneath them, in a patch scarcely bigger than a hand, the moss rises in little separate cushions, each with a dark well between it and the next. A low tree grows out of the highest crack. One of its roots has followed the rim of a basin for so long that the stone has a shallow channel in the shape of the root, even where the living wood has drawn away. Between these few green places there is a great deal of bare rock.

In full daylight the hollow feels warmer than the open slope. Damp comes off the stone without forming mist. There is a smell of leaves that have lain wet a long time and, close to the wall, the faint mineral smell left when a wet pebble dries in the sun. The leaf litter is undisturbed. Nothing has nibbled the soft fern tips. A leaf that falls into a pool may lie there until its flesh turns clear and the veins come apart.

It takes a while to hear the water. What you notice first, if you sit down, is that you can hear the cloth at your knees when you shift. After a few minutes there may be a small tick from the wall, the sort of sound a seed makes when it lands on a dry leaf. The drop that made it has fallen into a pool behind the ferns. By the time you find the pool, the surface is still again.

The lowest basin reflects a notch in the far ridge that you cannot see from anywhere inside the hollow. At first you account for this by moving your head. The ordinary reflections slide as they should: a fern goes out of sight, the lip of the basin comes in. The notch stays where it is. Late in the day its left side takes the sun, and after the hollow itself has gone into shade you can still see, at the bottom of an inch of water, the light picking out individual ledges on that distant face.

By morning the water will have risen enough to cover a little more of the pale rim. By afternoon the rim will be dry again. There are older rims farther up the wall, softened almost past recognition, with ferns growing from one and no mark of green on the others. The hollow has been deeper, and it has been smaller. For now, at the warmest hour of the day, there is room to sit on the dry stone with both feet flat and watch a submerged leaf let go of a single vein.
