The town fits inside a cleft high on the mountain. From the opposite slope you would see only a dark seam in the snow, widening a little at its lower end. Inside, there are streets, courts, overhangs, and enough doorways to suggest a much larger place. You can walk from one end to the other in the time it takes your hands to get cold.

Everything you touch is growing. The pavement is made of roots pressed flat against one another, their upper surfaces worn smooth in the middle of the lane. Walls are pleats of living wood, thick at the bottom and translucent at the newest edges. Under the eaves, small leaves grow with their pale undersides turned out, so that the sheltered parts of the town are brighter than the streets. There is stone underneath. Here and there a fresh corner of it has pushed through, sharp enough to catch a sleeve, but already hair-fine roots have found the fracture behind it.

The town is built down as much as up. A courtyard serves as the roof of a long, low room, and its outer steps turn midway down to become the lintel of another. In places the buildings meet overhead. You pass under a braid of trunks and find a gutter running along the inside of the braid, out of sight until the water drops from a pointed leaf at the far end. There are no fittings anywhere, no nails or hinges. Some passages have been kept open for so long that the trunks swell around them in rounded collars. Others have closed to the width of a hand.

Water is seldom more than a finger’s depth here. It collects in the hollows between roots and stands so clear that the green at the bottom seems dry until a drop strikes it. At dusk those little pools freeze from the rims inward. Deeper in the walls the water goes on moving, fed by a seep in the cleft that never quite freezes. Now and then a drop falls through two or three stories and lands on a leaf with a sound out of proportion to the quiet.

Above the roofs, where the slope is exposed, the wind runs hard enough to carry snow uphill. Very little of it comes down into the town. What does arrive gets caught in the upper branches and sifts into the courts long after the gust has passed. The snow in the darkest court is greenish underneath. You can see individual moss stems holding it clear of the ground.

There are few animals. A small brown bird comes in from the bare slope to work the seams in the bark, then leaves without calling. Under a loose flap of root skin there may be a cluster of black springtails, motionless until the warmth of a hand reaches them. The streets themselves are nearly still, though in a thaw you can watch a sheet of ice tip by degrees as the moss beneath it takes up water.

At the upper end of the town a stair leads to a landing scarcely large enough to turn around on. Beyond it the mountain shows its newest face: a fall of pale, angular rock with no soil yet in the joints. A stem has reached out from the landing and laid itself across the nearest block. For most of its length it has nothing to hold on to. At the tip, where water beads against the stone, it has put down three white roots.
