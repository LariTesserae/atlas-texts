The whole town fits inside a notch in the mountain. From one rim you can see the other through the alder tops, but the streets turn so sharply around the new ribs of stone that it takes half a morning to cross. The rock has not had time to go gray. Where it split, it is pale as the inside of a hazelnut; where a slab has fallen, its edges will still cut a shoe. The builders have left such slabs where they landed and made steps up one side and down the other.

There are eleven houses, a market, and a public stair broad enough for six people to climb abreast. Usually there is nobody on it. The town was laid out with the expectation of crowds, and even now a woman bringing a pot down to the cistern will keep to her side of the empty stair. The houses are roofed with split wood weighted down by stones. Their gutters are hollow stalks, replaced each autumn. Rain comes in short spells, sometimes not enough to darken the paving all the way across. The cistern gets most of its water on rainless nights, when beads gather on the underside of the roofs and find the stalks.

In the gaps between houses, the growth is luxuriant. A single clump of ferns can fill the space where a house ought to be. Alders lean over bare courts without quite meeting, their roots exposed in the fresh cuts of the streets. There is so little direct sun that the plants along the upper wall all incline toward the same pale strip of sky. In the morning the strip moves down the wall, lights the underside of a leaf or the wet lip of a gutter, and is gone before it reaches the paving. The air stays cool enough that a hand laid on a door latch leaves a patch of mist.

At first the town seems almost uninhabited. Then a paving stone lifts on the legs of a crab. What looked like the shadow beneath a bench thins out into a procession of black newts. Moths hang under the market tables in such numbers that their folded wings make a second, softer grain across the wood. High on the wall, animals with the long hind legs of hares step between ledges without jumping. They go down headfirst, feeling for each foothold with their ears.

None of this makes much noise. There is a dry tick when a moth opens its wings. A crab setting itself down sounds like someone putting a cup back on a shelf. Even a hunting bird, dropping the full height of the notch, is known chiefly by the sudden retraction of hundreds of little heads into the joints of the paving. The inhabitants talk at ordinary volume indoors. Outside they tend to wait until they are close enough to touch a sleeve.

The market is swept every morning, though it has not held a stall in years. Toward evening the animals begin to emerge from its uphill wall. They cross the clean square one by one, leaving between each body and the next an interval wide enough for a person to walk through. Nobody crosses during the first passage. At that hour the intervals are not always empty. You can watch the damp paving take the weight of a foot, then another, without seeing anything pass. The town has set a shallow bowl at the far side of the square. By dark it is usually empty, even when the night is too cold for water to evaporate.

Afterward someone turns the bowl upside down. The newts go back to the joints, the moths lift off toward the alders, and the woman on the cistern stair carries her full pot home. Behind her, from a crack too recent to have gathered moss, a root as white as a peeled twig inches out into the road. By morning there will be a stone to move.
