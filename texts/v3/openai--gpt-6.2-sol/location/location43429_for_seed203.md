Below the last bed of stone, there is a bright country with no ground.

Its floor is the upper face of a leaf. You can walk on it for weeks without reaching the margin, though in places it stands so steeply that the people who cross it cut steps. The steps are shallow and numerous. Cut too deep and the leaf will fill the wound with clear sap, which freezes harder than the little stone axes that made it. From a distance the old stairways are easy to pick out: they shine a slightly different white.

Above the floor, across miles of nearly empty air, hangs the underside of another leaf. Light enters the country there. It has no warmth and comes from every part of the ceiling at once, laying bare the smallest scratches on a tool. You can see a split in the far wall long before you can see the hill that hides its lower end. The light is worst on clear days, when there isn't even a strand of mist to give your eyes somewhere to stop.

Everything between the two leaves is growing, but there is very little of it. A root descends alone for six miles, coils once, and goes back up. At its lowest turn it carries a thimbleful of water. There are pale shoots along the horizon that look like a stand of grass until one falls and takes the better part of a morning to pass out of sight. No soil collects in the folds. The brown stuff that blows out of them is dry skin shed by the leaf, and where it lodges against a rib it puts out a fine green edge.

Water appears chiefly on things that are about to move. First a rib sweats; then the whole slope changes its inclination. The drops run together just long enough to make a narrow stream, which reaches the lip of the leaf and breaks into beads. The beads drift sideways in the cold air. Some freeze before they strike anything. Most never make a sound you can hear.

A great deal happens in that silence. Two slopes that faced one another yesterday may open overnight into a passage so wide it has weather at the far end. New ribs come up sharp enough to cut a hide shoe. Whole reaches of the floor roll under, exposing a damp, luminous surface on which there are no tracks. The changes travel unevenly. Sometimes you can watch a fold coming for hours and finish a meal before it reaches you. Sometimes a cup set on level ground slides away before you have let go of it. The country is too large to hear most of its own upheavals. Now and then a tremor arrives through your heels from a place you cannot see, and a line of frost jumps out of a seam.

There are shelters, though seldom where you need one. The best are made under overhanging ribs, with slats of shed leaf sewn together by split fibres. They have no hearths. People warm their hands around smooth pebbles carried next to the body, and sleep with their tools wrapped so that the handles won't freeze to the wall. At a fork in one of the old paths there is a low bench with a cup hollowed into either end. One cup is always dry. The other contains a wet ring and, on very cold mornings, a thin disk of ice that you can lift out whole. It is the only dependable water for a long way in either direction.

The animal most often seen is a grey thing about the size of a thumb. It has six soft feet and grazes on the clear rim of a healing cut. You may go a season without seeing one. Then, where a fresh seam has opened, there will be a dozen, widely spaced along it, each facing into the little current of air that comes out of the wound. They don't flee from footsteps. They stop feeding when the leaf begins to tilt, several breaths before you feel it yourself.

From a high rib you can sometimes see down through an opening into the country underneath. There is another ceiling there, and another brightness, with long bare surfaces receding at angles that make it difficult to tell which of them a person could stand on. Once in a while one has a dark speck on it that might be a shelter. You watch for smoke, though you know there won't be any. By the time you look up again, the edge nearest your feet has curled enough to hide it.
