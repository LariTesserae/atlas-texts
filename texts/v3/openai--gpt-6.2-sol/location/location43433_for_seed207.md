The hollow is just deep enough to kneel in and turn around. A seam in the limestone widens here, then pinches shut again, leaving a low room that has never had a floor level enough to stand on. The side nearest the seam is rough. Farther in, the stone has the worn contours of a bar of soap. Water once ran through it in earnest. Now, every few weeks, a bead gathers on the ceiling and drops.

There is hardly ever any light. By touch, the lower wall is soft with overlapping fronds, their tips folded inward. Roots travel across the ceiling in flat braids and disappear into cracks too fine to take a fingernail. If you bring a lamp, you can pick out three or four generations of leaves in the same handspan: new ones pale at the hinge, old ones dark as bottle glass, and underneath them a felt of leaves that have lost everything but their veins. In the middle of the hollow, where two shelves almost meet, the growth is thick enough to hold the shape of a hand after you take it away. Cold comes through the wet impression on your palm.

There is no trickle to follow to a source. The room gets its water in separate little allotments. The tips of some leaves are wet while their stems are dry. Where a drop habitually lands, the plants have left a bare patch around a shallow cup in the rock. For part of the year the cup is empty. In the coldest weeks, ice forms around its rim and catches the next drop before it can reach the bottom.

The only things put here are three ceramic squares, each the size of a thumbnail. One is wedged into a crack at the rear, one has gone crooked under the weight of a root, and the third lies face down in the leaf felt. They were made to store a tiny difference between one second and the next. The roots that touch them spend a little longer in a reaction that elsewhere requires sunlight. Nothing gets warm. Nothing lights up. The squares were buried with enough time in them to grow a garden considerably larger than this one, but the seam is stingy with water, and there is nowhere for the garden to go.

Nothing grazes here. There are no scalloped edges to the leaves, no holes with silk across them, no little heaps beneath the roots. Leaves come apart where they fall, slowly enough that the brown layer keeps a clear record of a particularly wet year: a finger’s breadth of larger leaves flattened one over another. Above that layer the leaves are small again. Most of the growing happens along an inch or two of wall where the ceiling still gives up moisture. The rest of the green persists on what it has.

You hear your sleeve brush the stone much louder than you expect. Afterward there is the faint irregular clicking of drops finding the cup, or no sound at all. From the seam, once the lamp is out, it is impossible to tell the hollow from solid rock. A folded leaf at the entrance is cold on one side and colder on the other. Over a winter, that is enough to turn it slightly on its stem. By spring its tip will be in a good place to catch a drop.
