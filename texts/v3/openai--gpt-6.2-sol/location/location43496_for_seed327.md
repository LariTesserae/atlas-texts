The meadow is no bigger than a chapel floor. It lies in a notch between two summits, so high that on a clear morning you can see the shadows of both peaks lying across the clouds below. On three sides the turf ends in a rounded lip of stone. On the fourth it runs a little way uphill into a cleft, where the rock is dark with seepage even after a week without rain.

The grass comes nearly to the knee. Under it are saxifrages, little ferns, and thick cushions of moss that hold the cold against your hand. There isn't a tree, or even a bush tall enough to catch a sleeve. The richness is all at your feet. Part the grass and there are the white husks of last year's flowers, a beetle turning itself over, and water moving between the roots. Stand up and there is almost nothing between you and the next mountain, which is far enough away that you can watch weather cross the gap without feeling any change in the air.

It is a loud place. Water works through a split in the ledge and falls a long way before striking anything. The sound of that first strike comes back up the cliff, hollow and remarkably distinct. When the wind gets under the lip, the falling water comes back too. For a minute or so the edge of the meadow is wet on both sides, and the grass nearest it inclines inland under what seems to be a small, perfectly ordinary rain.

The ledge itself looks as though it has had time to get used to all this. Its outer stones are blunt and pale, with shallow cups that hold grit and the occasional feather. A marmot has a door under one of them. Its alarm brings three or four heads out of the turf in quick succession, and then the meadow looks empty again. Choughs pass below it in loose companies, their calls audible before their red feet are visible. In the cleft, where the wind drops, bumblebees are busy among the low flowers. They disappear whenever a cloud comes up through the notch and start again as soon as the light reaches the ground.

Someone once made a shelter against the uphill wall, stacking flat stones to the height of a seated person's shoulder. One end has fallen. At the other, a roof slab still covers a dry patch just large enough for a pair of boots. Below it, the seep crosses the path in a little gutter worn into the rock. Most of the gutter is the work of water, but at the head there are three short peck marks where somebody persuaded it to turn.

By afternoon the cloud usually sinks away and the whole patch of grass stands in plain daylight. You can see the shelter, the marmot's stone, the wet path, and every place there is to go without putting a hand down. Then a bird tips over the edge. You can follow it for a long time, still descending, after it has become too small to tell from a fleck in your eye.
