From the road, there is almost nothing to see of the town. Low rims of masonry stand among the stones on the slope, with rope laid out to dry on them. Here and there a square of ground is missing. Look into one of those squares and you can see a woman crossing it two storeys down, a covered bowl in her hands. She passes out of the sun and into her house. The square is her front yard.

The town is being cut into a broad bed of pale, fine-grained stone. Its lowest rooms are only a little below the level of the fields on either side, but there is enough hill over them to keep them cool through summer. The builders leave the roofs where they can and take them away where they need light. At the larger courts you can stand in ordinary daylight and see all the way down a street, across another court, to the wooden ladders at the newest workings. At noon the white floors are almost too bright to look at. A few steps inside a doorway, you might have to put a hand out for the wall.

Nothing has had time to wear smooth. The stairs still have the shallow cups where a mason tested the stone with the point of his hammer. A door jamb comes away gritty under the fingers. There are houses at the western end with shelves crowded with bowls and old sleeping mats rolled up for the day; at the eastern end, the shelves have been drawn in charcoal on a wall that is still part of the hill. You can put your hand where somebody will keep the salt.

The streets are wider than you would expect from above. Two people can carry a door between them without turning it edgewise. It is the spaces off the streets that are packed: rooms behind rooms, storerooms over stairs, a baker's wood tucked into the wedge beneath a neighbor's landing. In one court, three households share a drain and each has put a little removable stone across it to keep the sweepings from passing into the next yard. There is just room for a stool by the baker's door, provided nobody wants to open the door all the way.

After rain, the hill gives up water for several days. It darkens a seam in a ceiling, gathers at the tip of a chisel mark, and falls into whatever has been put beneath it. A shallow groove along the main street takes the overflow to a settling basin. Most days the groove is dry enough to walk in. The basin keeps a thumb's depth of water over a layer of white mud; if you draw from it in the morning and leave it alone, it will be clear again by evening. The people who plaster the houses come late, when they can scoop the mud without anyone telling them to wait.

There is very little green. A tuft of grass grows in the grit at the head of the public stairs. Someone has set onions along the sunny edge of a court, and the people hanging laundry there have learned how far back to stand. Swallows sometimes come down as far as the big court, make a close turn over it, and go back out. The small moths that settle on the shaded walls are easier to miss. In the new rooms, where there is as yet nothing to eat and nowhere soft to nest, even the mice have not arrived.

A lot gets done without much noise. Doors are propped open while floors are swept. A bucket comes out of a light well, turning slowly on its rope. In the unfinished quarter a man taps a row of wedges, then waits with his palm against the stone. You hear the tapping clearly across one court and not at all across the next. After supper, when the hammers are put away, you can hear water strike the inside of a jar from the foot of the stairs.

Every few weeks the street reaches a little farther into the hill. There is usually a morning when the people at the last inhabited house find chalk marks across the blank wall opposite. They move their jars out of the way. The first baskets brought up are full of sharp pale chips; children pick out pieces flat enough to use as lids. For a while the opening is only deep enough to stand in. Then one day the crew takes away the stone above it, and sunlight lies on a stretch of floor that nobody has yet swept.
