The hollow is high on the mountain, though nothing in it gives a view. You come down into it between two shoulders of slate and find yourself on level ground, in a space you could cross in thirty paces if there were thirty places to put your feet. The walls lean far enough inward to hide the sky. In the middle stands a rowan no taller than a person, with roots that run all the way to the walls.

At first there is only the cold. Then, as your eyes adjust, one or two pale things separate from the dark: the underside of a fern, the flank of a snail, a strip of bare rock at knee height. The slate is worn smooth along that strip. Above it the stone flakes in thin, sharp leaves; below it you can lay your cheek against the rock without finding an edge. At some time there must have been a great deal of water here, moving fast. Now there is a great deal of water hardly moving at all.

It stands among the roots at the height of the moss. You can tell where it is by the things that will bear your weight and the things that look exactly like them but won't. Where a root goes under, it keeps its colour for a little way. Farther down there is no seeing, though if you reach in you can feel the root branching into finer and finer fibres, each hung with a soft fringe. The water on the walls never quite gathers into drops. It puts a shine on the little shelves of liverwort and leaves the upper ledges dry enough for spiders.

There are animals everywhere. This is the last thing you discover, and once you have discovered it there is almost nothing else to see. A patch of mottling on the rowan resolves into folded moths, the head of each tucked under the wing of the one above. The ferns have frogs in them. Each frond holds its frog close to the stem, where the weight doesn't bend it. Beneath the roots are crayfish, pale at the joints, with their claws laid out in front of them like stones in a stream. There is even a shrew on a ledge in the slate. Its nose is close to a slug, not touching it. In a fissure behind the shrew are the feet of bats, pink where they grip the stone. There is no spare length of fissure.

You can hear the sleeve of your coat drag against a fern from the other side of the hollow. That is how quiet it is. There is a smaller sound, which might be water passing under a stone, but if you go towards it you hear it just as well behind you. Outside, the mountain sometimes sheds a stone. You can hear it strike twice on the slope and then go a long way without striking anything.

Nothing here quite finishes what it is doing. The snail has one feeler out and one still curled inward. At the tip of a root, where the water ought to let go, a bead hangs long enough to show an upside-down fern. Put a finger to it and your finger comes away wet, but the bead is still there. The shrew is warm if you bring your hand close. You can wait beside it until your knees ache with the cold, and the distance between its nose and the slug will still be the width of a hair.

At the entrance lies a flat piece of slate with a comfortable edge for the hand. It would make a good scraper. There is a fallen branch with a fork just right for lifting thorn canes. Neither has a mark on it. Take the branch if you like; there is room to swing it once you're out on the slope. In the hollow all you can do is turn it end for end, carefully, among the ferns.

When you leave, the open mountainside seems bare in a way it did not before. There is grass, and there are stones, but you can see the ground between them. Wind moves the grass aside and for a moment there is even more ground. Behind you, in the cleft, the rowan has a single red berry low down near the water. A snail is halfway around it. You could find them both by touch now. You know exactly where they are.
