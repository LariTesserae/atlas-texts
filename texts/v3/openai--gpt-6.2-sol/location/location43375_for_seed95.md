The upland is warm enough for bare feet, though there are clouds a long way below it. From the rim you can see their shadows crossing the low country. Up here a shadow hardly seems to move at all. It takes most of the afternoon for the dark of a standing stone to cross the pale hollow at its foot.

There is room on top for a whole district: shallow valleys, long backs of stone, a basin wide enough that weather on its far side is a matter of conjecture. Almost nothing rises sharply. The highest ridges are broad and round, and the passes between them are scarcely lower. In places all that remains of a softer bed of rock is a band of reddish grit in a seam. You can follow one of these seams for half a day and find it at much the same height on every slope. Whatever wore the mountain down had time to finish. What remains is high because the country around it went on wearing away.

Rain comes occasionally, sometimes out of a sky that stays bright in all directions. For an hour the gray stone is plum-colored. Then the tops dry, and the damp withdraws under lips and into hairline cracks, where it can last a week. There is a little open water in the middle basin, no more than a string of dark patches among slabs. It is hard to tell from a distance whether you are seeing the pools or the shadows of stones. Strands of coppery grass grow around some of them. The other plants are small and widely spaced: a fleshy leaf tucked into a crease, a tuft so low that grit gathers over it and the green shows only when the wind lifts a few grains.

On first coming up you might think the place nearly empty. Then a patch of shade draws in its legs.

The animals are close together without quite touching. Under the slabs lie broad, soft-bodied things with translucent edges, fitted belly to back in the little room available. The rounded backs of the ridges carry drifts of ash-colored grazers, each with its muzzle to the stone. There is almost nothing for them to eat that an eye at standing height can see. If you crouch where a grazer has been, you find a clean strip the width of a tongue in a faint yellow bloom. The bloom takes a season to come back. The herds advance accordingly.

Above them hang small, pale-winged animals, hundreds to a slope. They face into the same mild current and make a single adjustment now and then, all down the slope, as if the air had run a finger along their wings. Most of the animals here make no call. On a quiet morning you can hear water drip inside a slab while several dozen bodies settle around your feet.

There are a few low rings of stone, seldom more than knee-high. From one ridge you can pick out three on the opposite hillside, with a great deal of bare ground between them. Nothing has been cut or fastened. At dusk an animal as broad as a table noses a loose stone into a gap, lies down against it, and goes to sleep. By dawn there are smaller creatures all along the inside of the ring, pressed into the arc of warmth. Some rings have sunk so far that only their uppermost stones are showing. They are still used.

A curious thing about the upland is that the little water it holds seems to remember its former depth. In the middle basin a pool may shrink to a wet patch no wider than a hand, but the fine-legged animals that feed there continue to wade at the old waterline. Their feet come down gently on nothing. They leave no tracks until rain fills the basin again. Then, for a day or two, you can see the same creatures for what they look like in water, their long feet opening into fans with each step.

Toward evening the light becomes level enough to show how much is moving. A low ridge acquires a second outline as the grazers raise their heads. The pale wings descend by degrees and come to rest on the warm stone. Far across the basin, a congregation that looked all day like a dark bed of gravel begins to spread, very slowly, into the ground around it. By morning it will have crossed a seam of red grit. From where you stand there is no sound at all.
