At the bottom of the city, behind a brick set on its narrow end, there is a garden no wider than a sleeping mat.

The garden lies below the floor of a dry basin, though not by much. A person sitting in it would have their head level with the street. The city rises from that level in courses of mudless brick: courtyards over courtyards, blind stairs, bread ovens with their mouths bricked shut. None of it is tall. The city got its size by going sideways, and when it needed another room it was as likely to roof a passage as to build a wall. This particular passage was roofed long ago, leaving a thumb-wide slot where the roof meets the western wall. A little brown light gets in. Mostly what comes through is heat.

There is no water in the basin, not even the trace that would darken a stone held overnight against cooler stone. The bricks were knocked from beds of soft mineral and fitted dry. Their exposed faces have worn down to the grain, but in the garden the corners are rounded and faintly glossy. Every afternoon a current of hot air comes down through the streets and finds the slot. It takes a pinch of grit around the room, rubs it along the same low course of brick, and carries it out through a hole beneath the threshold. The long shallow curve worn there looks deliberate. Perhaps the first builders cut a notch and the wind has been correcting it ever since.

Most of the room is bare. Halfway up one wall, however, a seam supports a black-green shelf of growth as thick as a folded blanket. Its leaves overlap in great numbers, broad at the base and drawn out to hair-fine points. They make no effort to reach the light. The newest leaves grow inward, into the darkest part of the seam, where the stone gives off a combustible vapor. The plants take it up through pale fringes and store what they can use as a clear oil. A torn leaf smells of warm pine pitch. Nothing comes to feed on it. There are no animals in the garden or in the city above it, no small tracks in the dust where a leaf has fallen.

For much of the day you can hear the leaves rearranging themselves. Heat loosens them. One point slips free of the next, and a whole handful slides with the sound of stiff paper being folded. Then the wind reaches the lower streets. Above the garden, it moves through a row of hollow bricks that once ventilated a common oven. One still has a plug of fired clay in it; the others take up the note around that obstruction. The noise is loud enough to feel through the heel of a hand laid flat on the floor. It lasts a while, breaks off, and resumes at a lower pitch as another passage warms. The garden is dim enough that these changes are easier to follow than the progress of the light.

Beneath the hanging leaves is a shallow dish chipped into the floor. It might be mistaken for a basin, but there is a ridge deliberately left across the bottom and a little pour-lip on either side. Fresh oil gathers on the ridge. When the floor is hot enough it creeps down one lip into a thumbprint hollow; as the stone cools, the next drop stiffens where it hangs. Whoever cut the dish could have taken the oil without touching the leaves. The hollow is polished on the side a hand would have approached from. On the other it still shows the little white bruises of a stone chisel.

By the time the slot goes dark, the oven bricks have fallen quiet. The floor keeps its heat. In the long pause before the wind reverses, a dry seed case splits somewhere under the leaves. Three or four hard seeds strike the dish, then roll out across the brick. Most will fetch up against the threshold. One goes on ticking a little longer, finding the worn curve along the wall.
