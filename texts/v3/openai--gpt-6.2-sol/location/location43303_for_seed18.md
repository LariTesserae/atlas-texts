The garden is one room, forty paces from end to end, hung so high on the factory tower that clouds pass beneath its floor. At noon the light is ordinary. You can see the pale stitching on the underside of a leaf, the dirt in a thumbprint on the door. It is only when you look down through the grating that the height becomes difficult to believe. The tower continues into a gray distance. On a clear day you can watch the shadow of a cloud travel down it.

There is no earth here. The fig trees stand in troughs of broken porcelain, and their roots have nosed between the pieces and polished them. Ferns grow out of braids of waste rope slung along the walls. A vine with broad, tender leaves has filled the old conduit racks so completely that the electricians, while there were electricians, ran the new conduit straight through the middle of the room. It hangs there still, crossing the open air at shoulder height. Nothing grows on it. The air above it is empty all the way to the roof.

The garden gets wet by degrees. Vapor seeps through a joint in the warm factory wall and condenses on the colder bolt heads. By afternoon there is enough to blacken the rope around the ferns. A drop occasionally makes it as far as the floor. The place smells of hot iron and the inside of a split fig. There has never been enough water to fill the little drain at the door, but over the years a shallow channel has worn into the enamel where the drops cross it.

Behind the wall, the blowers run. The sound is too loud to locate. Put a hand on a trough and you feel it there; lift your hand and your teeth are still ringing with it. At first the garden seems frantic because of the noise. Then you notice that a leaf you took for trembling is perfectly still. The great volume of air above the plants is warm and nearly motionless.

There are geckos behind the ceramic spacers, beetles under the trough lips, and small brown birds that nest in the bends of abandoned pipe. Most of the time you would swear there was nothing alive but the plants. A bird will appear on a handrail, and then, as your eye adjusts to the way it holds itself, you will find six more along the rail, all facing the same drain. Before a drop falls, every one of them opens its beak. You cannot hear what they do.

The factory stopped making things long ago. Its gauges have gone white, and the service walk outside the garden ends at a blank rectangle where a stair used to be. The blowers have outlasted the people who knew what they were for. Once in a great while they ease, not enough for silence but enough that a falling drop can be heard to strike the porcelain. In that interval the birds shut their beaks. Then the noise comes back, and the day goes on at the pace of a root widening a seam.
