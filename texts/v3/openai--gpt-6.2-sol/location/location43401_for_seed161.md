The town is forty paces across at its widest. You can stand in the middle and throw a date stone over either wall. The people who live there speak of the east and west ends as if they were a morning’s walk apart, and if you have a basket to carry between them, with all the steps and doorways and people who want to see what’s in it, they nearly are.

It occupies a notch at the top of a mountain. On both sides of the notch the rock falls away so far that the eye loses the slope before it finds the country below. The narrow ledge where the houses stand was once the floor of something much larger. There are old rounded hollows in it, big enough to lie down in, and seams of white stone polished flush with the darker rock. Whatever wore it down has been gone a very long time. The mason who sets a doorstep doesn’t try to level the ledge. He cuts the bottom of the stone to fit.

Houses go up three and four stories, though a story here can be little more than a shelf for sleeping. Their walls are thin slabs held edgewise in grooves, with dry stems tamped into the joins. The stairs make use of the mountain wherever they can. In one passage you climb a flight of stone steps and cross a kitchen on a plank, then go up another flight cut into the back wall of the same kitchen. The woman who cooks there hangs her ladles underneath the plank. You know to tread lightly over them.

There is no well. On the weeks when pack trains come, they bring stoppered clay jars and bundles of wood, and take away wool, salt, and the little hard dates that ripen against the upper walls. The jars go straight into the deepest rooms. Water is never set out in a bowl. If a jar breaks, whoever is nearest pushes a square of clean wool into the spill.

For all this the town has a leafy interior. Date fronds lie across the roofs, and grey vines crowd the stair rails. The plants occupy very little ground. Their roots follow warm cracks in the stone, some all the way down to beds of damp clay sealed beneath the ledge. Each household knows which roots it can cut. Where a wall has to come down, there may be an old vine propped on forked sticks for a year while the stones are dressed and put back around it. The leaves on the stairs are thick and not particularly soft. Brush them with a bare arm and you come away smelling faintly of lemons.

Direct sun reaches the town in a strip a little before noon. The rest of the day the high walls of the notch give it a brownish shade, warm enough that people sit barefoot on the steps after supper. The rock holds heat long after the air beyond the parapet has turned cold. At certain windows you can put one hand on a warm sill and hold the other out in the mountain air. Nobody leaves a loose shutter on those windows. The wind outside will work a wooden peg loose over the course of a winter, then take the whole shutter so quickly that no one sees it go.

Inside, it’s loud. The goats live on the bottom floors and call up the stairwells when they hear feed being cut. Small birds nest behind the slabs and quarrel in spaces too thin to get your fingers into. Children turn the hand mills in pairs. Up by the high gate a man dresses stone with a bronze chisel, one blow at a time, with long pauses in which his last blow can still be heard down in the houses. After dark the bats come out. There are enough of them to make the air at the mouth of the notch patter, though by then the stairs are quiet and you can hear a goat drawing hay through its teeth.

The town has a rule about making a new door: the threshold must come from an old one. There’s no shortage of stone; it’s a way of keeping track of doors that no longer open. At the lower end, where the last stair reaches the parapet, three thresholds lie flat in the paving. They’re worn into shallow saddles by feet that turned into houses long since taken down. People still step across them rather than on them. In the deepest hollow of one, a date seed has lodged against the wall. There is a little green tip on it this year. The sweepers have been going around it.
