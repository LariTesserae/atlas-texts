There is no shore to the lowland. Water comes up between the leaves wherever they part, but there is nowhere you could stand and say that the land ends here. The land is a floating thickness of roots, deep enough to carry its own weight on the water beneath it. In the dark, a little pressure from a hand brings the water level with the knuckles. Let go and the surface takes its old shape without a ripple.

The plants grow in tiers, although there is no great height to any of them. At the bottom are pale, corded roots as thick as a wrist; above them lie fans of overlapping leaf, and above those, at about the height of a person’s shoulder, are the wet undersides of another set of leaves. Between the tiers there is room to move. The openings run a long way at the same low pitch, then close where two leaves meet edge to edge. Their edges are keen enough to part a hanging drop. Nothing has rounded them. Even the oldest leaves have the crisp folds of something just unfolded.

The air is warm. A surface and the air immediately above it may differ in warmth by so little that only a film of dew gives the difference away. Here and there a cup of water hangs from the underside of a leaf. It is fed through the leaf and drawn back into it at the same rate. The cup never fills to the point of falling. There is no patter in all that wetness.

Some of the roots work in banks. Along a bank, one flattened chamber swells while its neighbor narrows, passing water from one to the next. The motion is hard to see even where a thin seam of green light shows through the root skin. Lay a hand on it and there is a steady pressure with a small, regular hesitation in it. A mile farther on, the same hesitation comes at the same moment. The banks take dissolved material from the deep water and deliver it to the leaves. What comes back is clear enough to look like a gap in the root. There are no housings around these works, no fasteners, no difference in substance between a valve and the supple wall that closes it. At the ends of some of the banks, fine threads grow into the water and hold an electrical charge. Cut off from the air by their own depth, they give off a little light. It goes no farther than the nearest root.

No animal passes through the openings. The soft leaves have no torn margins, and there are no little wells of footprints in the wet. Where a root wears thin from the passage of water, new tissue meets the thinning surface from within. A leaf gives up its outermost skin so slowly that the layer beneath is already wet before it is exposed. There is no litter. Even the youngest-looking folds might have stood here for ages, renewed along the same sharp line.

Over the lowland, the warm breath of the leaves gathers into a roof of cloud. It is weather on a continental scale, though from below it amounts to a darkness that never lifts and a faint downward pressure of damp air. Water rises through the open spaces and returns as dew. Far out, where the root mat is thicker, there are broader regions of descent. They have never traveled. The same leaves have always received them.

Once in a while there is a small sound like wet paper being pulled apart. It comes from deep down, where a pocket of air separates into two pockets and is taken up by the roots. The sound carries a surprising distance. Then there is only the pressure under the hand, if the hand is still there, and the warmth, which feels at first as though it must be coming from somewhere.
