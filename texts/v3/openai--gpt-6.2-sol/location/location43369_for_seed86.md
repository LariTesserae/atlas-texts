The basin is wider than the ridge that covers it. From the foot of the entrance slope you can see nearly a mile across, to a pale bank of broken stone. You cannot see the roof over the middle. Daylight gets in through a seam high in the western wall and is enough to separate the bank from the darkness behind it, though not enough to show whether what you’re looking at is a slope or the face of a cliff.

Most of the floor is dry. Dust lies in the hollows of the limestone and on the upper faces of fallen slabs. Where two slabs lean together, their undersides are wet and cold enough to numb the pads of your fingers. There is no stream. Water gathers at a few points on the roof, falls through a long interval of air, and strikes shallow cups in the stone. Each cup has a dark rim and a little clean gravel at the bottom. Between one cup and the next you can walk for ten minutes without finding a damp place.

The rock looks newly broken. There are white edges you could cut a hide on, and blocks with corners so exact that the eye looks for the rest of the block in the wall above. Sometimes it is there: a matching notch, twenty feet up. But there is dust even on the sharpest edges. Nothing has fallen recently. Nothing looks about to fall. At the entrance a little fern grows sideways out of a clay seam. Farther in there are three or four mats of moss where the roof drips within reach of the light. That is nearly all the green.

The noise is there before the bats are. On the way down it has the breadth of rain falling on a hillside. Out on the floor it resolves into little dry cries, some near enough to pick out and others coming from so far along the vault that they scarcely sound like the same animal. There are thousands of bats overhead, perhaps tens of thousands, but they hang in bands along cracks and ledges. Between the bands are great bare stretches of ceiling. One animal loosens its grip, drops, and is across the chamber before the eye catches up. A little farther along, another slips into the space it left. Under each roost is a low dark mound with a wide margin of clean stone around it.

Closer to the ground, the numbers are harder to take in. A bank that looks like fine gravel is full of beetles. Cave crickets occupy the underside of a ledge so completely that when you stoop to look, the ledge seems to have grown a fringe. Yet the next ledge is empty, and the next hundred yards of floor hold nothing larger than a shed wing. In a drip cup, pale little animals draw back under the gravel at the passing of a shadow. Wait a while and you can see their feelers come out again.

A loose flake of stone fits neatly between thumb and forefinger. It would scrape a skin or split a reed. There are a hundred like it on the slope, lying where they broke. No soot marks the low ceiling by the entrance, no stones have been rolled aside to ease the descent, and the soft clay under the fern has only the pinpricks of small feet.

Toward evening the seam in the wall loses what little color it had. The bats pour out through it; by then it is too dark to see them cross the basin, but for a time the air at the entrance smells warm. By morning they are back on the same ledges. The cups are neither fuller nor emptier, and the pale bank across the chamber is the first thing the returning light finds. There is a dry shelf well out from under the roosts where you can sit and listen to the whole occupied vault. At your feet, the dust between two stones is fine and level, without a mark in it.
