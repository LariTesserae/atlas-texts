The hollow is no bigger than a chapel. You can see the far lip from anywhere inside it, though there is no good place to stand. The floor is a skin of living moss drawn over a mesh of roots, and it gives a little even where it looks firm. The banks are sedge, their old blades folded flat beneath the new ones. No stone breaks the surface. If there was ever a stream here with enough strength to carry gravel, it has long since spread its load and gone elsewhere.

Almost everything that takes up space is green, but almost none of the space is taken up. From the lip to the floor there is a long, clear reach of air. A few alder branches extend over it without meeting. In ordinary daylight you can see the pale undersides of their leaves and, through the leaves, the unremarkable sky. Water collects at the bent tips of the sedge. There is hardly ever enough to fall as a drop; brush past, though, and by the time you reach the bottom your sleeves are wet and cold.

The hollow is loud. A pumping station on the far side of the flats runs day and night, out of sight beyond the alders. Its low note comes through the root mat more clearly than through the air. Above that, there are wrens working the banks, and in the shallow folds under the sedge a surprising number of frogs. When one stops calling, you can hear another at nearly the same pitch across the hollow. Beetles climb the stems that hold the water. A thrush can spend half a morning here, feeding within a few feet of you, and you may see nothing but the trembling of one blade at a time.

The banks are so mild that from above the depression scarcely shows. It has had a long time to lose its edges. What little earth slips down in a hard rain lodges in the moss before it can travel the length of a hand. The moss grows through it. An alder falls and the sedge covers the trunk, leaving a slight rise that lasts for years and then no rise at all.

Come back after a month of dry weather and the sleeve-level blades will be dry, but the moss will still darken under your heel. Come back in rain and there will be no pond. Water will be passing somewhere underfoot, too slowly to hear beneath the pumps and the frogs. Across the hollow the same low lip will be visible, with its sparse stems standing against the sky.
