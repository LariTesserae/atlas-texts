The town begins well before the first house. A paved footway crosses the saddle, and for half a mile the only sign of work is that the water on one side of it runs north and the water on the other runs south. The paving stops wherever the ground is firm enough to walk on. There, rows of pale stones mark the edges of streets that have nothing built along them.

It is warm on the saddle. Below it, cloud fills the deeper valleys, but up here the light is clear enough to pick out the fresh corners of the ridges. Whole faces of rock have come away along straight seams. At their feet the fallen pieces are still sharp, with pale undersides that look dry even when they are wet. Soil has gathered in some of the cracks, enough for a fern or a little stand of broad-leaved shrubs. Where the cracks meet, the growth is lush enough to hide the rock entirely. A few steps away there may be nothing green for the length of a street.

The houses stand far apart on the higher shelves. They are built of split stone, with reed roofs held down by flat slabs. Between them are paved courts several times the size of the houses, low-walled gardens, and steps wide enough for a dozen people abreast. From any one doorway it would be easy to mistake the place for a scattering of farms. From the opposite ridge, the alignments are plain: the courts lie one below another, and a stair leaving the uppermost square keeps its line all the way down to the communal cistern. The blocks along that stair are rough on the sides and smooth in the middle. On the newest ones you can still see the shallow cups left by a stone hammer.

Water is almost always within sight, though there is no big river. It comes out halfway up walls that look too thin to have anything behind them. It gathers in the hollows of the vacant courts and stands just below the paving. In the gardens, people raise the beds a handspan at a time, using silt lifted from the cistern with woven scoops. There are no high banks around the cistern. Its broad lip is set level with a spillway, so that excess water leaves in a sheet too thin to hear. Farther downhill it fans out among sedges and disappears without having cut a channel.

You can sit on the cistern steps for a long time before hearing anything. There is no wingbeat, no insect whine, nothing moving in the reeds except the reeds themselves. Occasionally a drop falls from a roof into a clay jar. Across the open ground someone sets a wooden bowl down on stone, a small, distinct sound with no other sound following it.

The valley looks recently made; the town looks as though it has had ample time to learn where to put things. Neither gives much impression of changing now. A damp line halfway up the lowest stair is as good a mark of the water level as it was last year. The paths go around the same pools, and the pools reflect the same breaks in the skyline. Toward evening, when the sun reaches under the eaves, the wet stone there is warm to the touch. A palm leaves a print on it for a moment. Then the rock darkens again.
