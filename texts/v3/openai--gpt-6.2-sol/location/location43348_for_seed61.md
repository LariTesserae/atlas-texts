Six steps below the lowest cellar in the city, there is a room just wide enough for a person to turn around. To make the turn you have to tuck in your elbows. The walls are crowded with the mouths of clay pipes, some no bigger than a reed, some wide enough to put a fist into. They come from different centuries of street. The oldest are square, made of four fired slabs pressed together while the clay was soft. Near the ceiling is a pipe made from the neck of a storage jar. Its handle is still attached.

This is the city’s overflow. Water comes out of only three mouths. From one it falls in a clear cord; from another it works its way over the lip and runs down the face of the wall. The third is under the floor, where you can hear it striking the underside of a stone. There is a shallow gutter around three sides of the room. Whoever laid it left a dry place for both feet at the foot of the stair.

The noise on that dry place is astonishing. You can shout up the steps and the person holding the lamp will see your mouth move. Take one step up and you can hear them ask what you said. It is hard to credit the amount of water with the amount of sound. Most of the noise comes from a thumb-wide gap beneath the floor where the water falls through into a second hollow. A low, continuous note comes up out of it. The smaller sounds, the ticks and slaps and little splatters at the pipe mouths, are perfectly distinct over that note, even though a human voice is not.

What light comes down the stair picks out yellow clay and the pale fur of mineral salts. The salts are soft where they have just formed and hard as crockery farther up. There is nothing green, not even in the seams where the mortar has fallen away. A white animal sometimes shows itself in the gutter: a flat thing, jointed, scarcely longer than a clipping from a fingernail. It can stand in the full stream without moving. When it goes, it goes up the wet wall, into a seam that looked too fine to admit even water.

Every stone that can be reached from the bottom step has been used. There is a thumb hollow in the jamb, a shelf worn into a projecting block for a lamp, a little round depression where people used to rest the heel of a jar while they got their grip. The water is clean down here. The city stopped drawing it from this room when the stair narrowed in a settling of the foundations. A person can still get down sideways, but a full jar won’t come back up.

After rain, the streets overhead run ankle-deep. In summer the public basins go dry and people lower cups on strings into the wells. Neither event makes any difference here. There is a white line on the black stone beneath the largest pipe, and the clear cord lands with one edge on that line. It lands there while the new market is being paved and while the paving is being pried up to build the new market’s replacement. With a lamp held close you can see that the line is actually a seam between two stones. Somebody, setting them by hand in the cold, left one a little higher than the other. Water spills over that tiny step, and beyond it the floor is smooth.
