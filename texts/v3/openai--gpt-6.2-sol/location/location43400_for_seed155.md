The city stands high enough that rain can be seen falling in the valley without any of it reaching the roofs. Up here the usual weather is a cool, bright overcast and a little water on the underside of things. By midmorning the handrails are beaded. The paving stays dry except at the joints, where the living edges draw together and hold a dark thread of moisture.

From a distance the plateau looks like worn stone. It has the long slopes and broad, unaccountably shallow basins of a range that has been eroding for an immense time. Up close there is no stone. The gray of the uplands is the exposed grain of an old plant body, polished by weather across miles of nearly horizontal growth. The youngest wood is underneath. In road cuts you can see it, pale and wet, quietly putting on thickness beneath surfaces worn smooth before the city was laid out. The surveyors find no original soil. Their deepest borings bring up soft white curls that green at the edges if left on a windowsill.

There are blocks, avenues, freight yards, a municipal market and two rail lines. The city has the plan of a much busier place. You can walk the length of a broad commercial street and pass a woman setting out six pears on a felt pad and nobody else. The shopfronts continue on either side, their glass-clear membranes slacker where the premises have been empty a long time. Some vacant lots are older than the buildings around them. They’re kept shaved to the grain, with a low curb to keep a wheel from dropping in. Nothing grows *up* in those lots. It all grows sideways, and when the light is right you can see the slow turn it takes at each corner.

The tallest structures are the mills on the western lip. They have flexible flywheels the height of a house and belts that need watering once a week. The presses make translucent sheet from layers the plateau sheds naturally in winter. A freshly pressed sheet will curl toward the mill where it was made; stacked in a freight car, a whole consignment leans a little west. Everything in the works that can be grown in place is grown there, including the bushings. When one wears oval, the fitters take the shaft out for a shift and bind a damp rag around the hole.

It is a loud place to be so nearly alone. The presses start before dawn, but most of the noise in the city comes from far below them. As the upland cools, broad layers slip against one another. A dry roll goes on for half a minute beneath the streets, loud enough to make conversation pointless. In the gaps you can hear the trolley wire hum and condensate drop into the thin-walled gutters. Once or twice a day a small bird passes over the freight yard. The pointsman claims it’s the same bird, though it has come over in both directions at once.

That sort of thing is commonest where the old grain has folded back on itself. On the lower tram route there are two stops four miles apart that share a platform. The station clock is bolted to the uphill end. It shows the right time from either stop, but if you go all the way around it you lose sight of it halfway, with your hand still on the back of the case. The conductor calls both names and leaves the doors open long enough for anyone who is unsure. People who commute that way learn which end of the platform smells of the mills.

Rain makes the layout more reliable. On a wet day you can see where each drop lands, and the streets seem obliged to keep their places. Water runs out of the high squares in shallow ribs, hesitates at the rims of the vacant lots, then sinks so evenly that there is no visible puddle a minute later. For the next few hours the city creaks. Doorways tighten. Little cream-colored leaves come out along the sheltered sides of the loading ramps, all at the height of a person’s knee. By the following afternoon most have been rubbed away by freight or folded back into the ramp.

Beyond the last signal mast there is another twelve miles of upland, open enough to see the weather arrive. The city thins to its service tracks and then to a row of clipped posts, each still attached to the ground it was cut from. Out there a shallow hollow holds a palmful of water all summer. You can kneel beside it and watch the surface shake under a clear sky. The mill shift has ended; the shutters are down. Somewhere under the bare-looking plain, a layer many acres wide is easing past the one below it, and the sound is still coming up.
