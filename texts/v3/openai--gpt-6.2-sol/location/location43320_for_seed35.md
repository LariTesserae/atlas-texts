The chamber is only twenty paces long, but it takes a while to see the far wall. Narrow blades of black stone rise from the floor and descend from the roof. They almost meet, and what looks like a wall from one position turns out, a step later, to be the edge of a blade you could put your hand around. The stone is warm. Its fractures are so new-looking that their silver edges have not yet taken the color of the rest of the rock. There is no loose stone beneath them.

Nothing has ever dripped here. The pale branching marks on the ceiling are where the rock split, not where anything ran. At the foot of the north wall a few rust-red stalks grow directly out of a seam. Each bears a single papery cup. There are more cups under the grating, all turned toward an electrical insulator that gives off just enough violet light to show the dust on its ribs. The cups on the far side of the grating are still there; they have simply grown flat.

A motor stands on three bolted feet near the middle of the chamber. Its shaft goes into the rock at both ends. Nobody built a housing for it, but somebody bent a bit of sheet metal into a shelter for the switch, and the shelter has a neat rolled lip that keeps it from cutting your fingers. There is a ceramic stool beside it. Those are the only things in the chamber that look as if they were put down where there happened to be room.

The motor is loud enough to make your back teeth feel poorly fitted. Beneath it is a slower thudding you can hear only with your mouth open. Most of the animals live down near the floor, where the air is no cooler but the thudding comes through the stone. They are thumb-long and jointed, the color of lamp smoke, with two white pads on their undersides. They put the pads against a blade and hold quite still. What looks at first like a thin deposit of ash is hundreds of their folded legs. There are larger ones up under the roof. You can make out the bellies of two or three pressed together in a crack no wider than one of them.

Every so often one of the little animals crosses the grating. There is a brief hard rattle, audible even over the motor. If you watch long enough you begin to know which one will cross next. It comes out from behind the left foot of the stool, crosses three bars, and vanishes under the fourth. In the time it takes your eyes to find it below, another has come round behind the stool. The bare patch on the grating never gets any larger. Neither does the fine red fringe of growth underneath. At one corner a stalk has come up through a bolt hole without touching the sides. Its cup is much too wide to go back through.

There is a handhold cut into the rock at the entrance. Standing there with your palm in it, you can feel a slight tremor that stops exactly at the threshold. The warmth stops there too. On the step outside lies a little fan of ordinary gray dust, with the marks of your shoes in it. Inside, where your first step ought to be, the black floor is clean.
