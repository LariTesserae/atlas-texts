Behind the tram depot there is a low square of ground the city has not decided what to do with. You can see from one end to the other: a brick pump house, three alder trees, an empty loading platform, and then the pale back wall of the laundries. At noon the whole square is in daylight. Only the space beneath the platform stays dark enough for the starlings to vanish into.

The ground is young. Last winter a retaining wall came away in one piece, exposing a bank of yellow clay with the straight, hard edges of a cut loaf. Pebbles still jut from it at angles that a few years of rain will round off. At its foot lie bricks with mortar on only one side. The older surfaces are harder to pick out: a worn curb in the middle of the grass, two iron rings set into a block of stone, a row of cobbles that goes nowhere since the wall fell. The alders have rooted along that row. Where the roots lift the cobbles, their undersides are damp.

It seldom rains long enough to make a puddle. Moisture gathers on the pump house pipes and drops into a strip of nettles, one leaf at a time. The nettles come up waist-high there; a stride away, the gravel is dry and loose. Ferns crowd the steps to the old loading office, and a currant bush reaches through the window where the glass is missing. Most of the square is open enough for the cool air to get at your wrists.

It is a loud place to find so nearly empty. The laundry machinery runs behind a blank wall, and when the big extractors come on, the iron rings tremble against their stone. Outside the square, tram wheels shriek around a bend you cannot see. There are minutes when you can watch a thrush singing from the platform rail without hearing a note of it. Then the extractors wind down and the thrush is still at it, and you hear the small scratch of a blackbird turning over a leaf.

A great many animals seem to live in the intervals between the few things standing. Bees enter the pump house through a seam above the lintel. Snails spend the day deep in the bolt holes of a discarded machine bed. A rabbit comes out under the depot fence in full daylight and sits on the warmest bit of rail, washing its ears. In late afternoon the starlings spread out over the bare gravel. They leave little distances between themselves, each bird walking its own patch, until a tram goes round the bend and they all lift at once.

The oddest thing is the echo from the broken bank. A sharp noise comes back from it as a pair of soft taps, the second so late that at first you take it for somebody indoors setting down a cup. The depot workers say it started when the wall fell. In summer, when the nettles are tall, you have to stand well out in the square to hear it.

Little else changes quickly. All spring a pale slip of clay hangs from the bank by an alder root. The machinery shakes it every working day. Birds land above it and kick grit down its face. By autumn it will have dropped, and the fresh patch behind it will be a little yellower than the rest. For a while you will be able to see it from the tram.
