The town occupies the top of a single tooth of stone. You can walk from one end to the other in the time it takes the sun to clear the opposite ridge, though you will have to turn sideways in places. The streets are narrowest where they cross the new fissures. You step over a black line no wider than your hand and see, a long way down, the sunlit wall of the mountain.

The stone is young. Its corners have not been thumbed smooth by wind or feet, and in the shade the broken faces still have a faint glassy shine. Whole shelves lean away from the summit without having fallen yet. Along one of them stand seven houses, each with its doorstep cut to a different angle so that the floors inside are level.

There is no water here. The low places are full of pale, loose crystals, and the broad steps at the center of town descend into what looks, from above, like a sunken pool. At the bottom, in the warmest hour, little animals lie flat among the crystals with their ribs spread. They are the color of bread inside, with black feet and thin, overlapping plates along the back. As the steps fall into shadow they climb out one by one. Their feet are soft enough that they can cross a drift of crystals without making it slide.

Most of the green grows overhead. The plants are rooted in the porous undersides of ledges and in the joints between house walls. They send down dense fans of blunt leaves, yellow-green where the light gets through and nearly blue where they overlap. Break a leaf and there is no sap, only a soft amber wax that takes the print of your finger. Each year a fan puts on a new blade at its lower edge. A doorway that was high enough when a house was built may now require everyone who lives there to duck.

The houses are made of flakes of the same stone, balanced in courses with their thick ends inward. There are no hinges or latches. A doorway is closed by easing a flat stone across it; at night most people leave a little space at the bottom for the warm-bodied animals to come in. The roof gardens and the upper lanes are one continuous surface. In places you can look through the leaves beside your feet and see someone below, setting out a meal on a sill. There is a public stair whose upper steps belong to nobody and whose lower steps are inside three different houses.

For so small a town it takes a long time to see how much lives in it. There are gray moths in pairs under the stair lips, their open wings fitted to the angles of the stone. There are six-legged grazers that strip the old wax from the leaves without harming the new growth. At eye height, if you stand still, a face will resolve itself in a wall: wide cheeks, folded ears, eyes the dull green of the leaf bases. The rest of the animal is in the wall. Generations of them have slept in the spaces left by the thick ends of the building stones.

At midday there is ordinary sunlight in the square, and warmth enough to feel through the soles of your feet. The air smells of hot mineral and the faint sweetness of the wax. A leaf occasionally unhooks from another leaf. From a room you cannot see comes the brief scrape of a bowl being set down. Even at the rim, where you expect the exposure to bring a rush of wind, there is usually only a movement of the fine hairs on your wrists. Far below, on the white plain, the shadow of the summit has a jagged edge. In the afternoon you can pick out the little projection made by the row of seven houses.

The mountain is still opening. It does so too slowly for anyone to feel, but along the main lane there are doorways with two thresholds, the old one wedged in the rock on one side, the new one set a finger’s width away on the other. People remember when particular steps were one stone. The plants do not seem to mind. Their oldest roots cross the fissures in dark, slack loops, and when the light goes out of a crack in the evening, the soft-footed animals take those loops down into the warmth.
