The city stands on a shelf so high that rain can be seen falling below it. From the outer tramline, you watch a shower cross the lower country in the afternoon sun, whitening the flats mile by mile. Up here the paving is already wet. It has been wet since before dawn, when mist condensed on the trolley wires and came down in large, widely spaced drops. The handrails are warm. There is no shade except what stone and iron provide.

The shelf is the worn rim of a basin wide enough to make its own weather. Its far wall is visible only after rain, and then only as a blue interruption beneath the clouds. The near wall descends in shallow steps, each broad enough to hold a district. The oldest streets follow the steps; later ones cross them on viaducts, sometimes passing so close over the tiled roofs that a passenger can look down through a skylight and see a family eating lunch.

Nothing grows between the tiles. There are no roots to lift a pavement, no leaves to fish out of the intake grilles. A century of foot traffic has hollowed the stair treads, and water has done much the same work on the exposed stone outside the city. Everywhere, sharp edges belong to recent repairs. The old cuts in the cliff have rounded until it is hard to tell which were quarried and which were made by water.

The wet stone is full of animals. Thumb-sized grazers work the copper-colored film off the public steps, leaving pale commas behind them. Under each bridge hang ridged shells as large as washbasins. Their occupants put out soft jointed limbs at dusk and spend the night picking insects out of the air. Along the sunny embankments, immense brown animals lie flank to flank, their nostrils opening at long intervals. When one gets up to enter the canal, the others lean into the space it leaves, without waking.

Most of what feeds them begins in the warm water rising through the basin floor. Bacterial films thicken over beds of porous stone, and small things rasp them loose. Larger things eat the small ones. At a sluice you can see several levels of this economy in the depth of a handspan: the film on the gate, the transparent mouths working over it, the narrow fish hanging sideways in the flow. The city takes its share farther down, where screens lift a continuous glistening harvest into the sorting sheds. Workers there can tell by the weight on a belt whether the night was clear on the far side of the basin.

It is loud without much happening. Pumps beat under the street. The sorting sheds rattle. Below the tramline, thousands of animals call from the landing ledges, and those farther down call at a pitch you feel through the soles of your shoes. A door shutting scarcely registers. But from inside a tiled stairwell, through an open window the size of a book, you might hear a single drop finding the same hollow in a sill over and over.

The city has learned not to hurry its water. A new culvert dug too steeply runs clean for a season, then silts at the foot and floods the workshops. The oldest drains scarcely seem to slope at all. Their stone covers bear a line of small brass studs, set there so a surveyor can check their fall. In some streets the studs are the only parts of the drains anyone has touched in living memory. Water slips through underneath and reaches the next district a day or two later.

Once in several years the basin rises enough for the great herds to reach the upper landings. Shopkeepers know the rise is coming weeks ahead: first a dark seam on the retaining wall disappears, then the bottom rung of a ladder. By the time broad wet backs show above the parapet, the lower warehouses have been emptied and the tram has stopped running on the riverside track. The animals take a month to file past. The shops stay open. People put stools out under the awnings and eat their lunches there, watching a procession whose far end is still somewhere below the weather.
