The upland is high enough to make weather without making clouds. Toward noon, broad sheets of cold air lift from the bright stone and travel east. You can follow their passage by the darkening of the groves, one after another, all the way to the limit of sight. Then the nearer groves turn silver again. Whatever passed over them has not yet reached the far end of the country.

The ground is white where the wind has polished it and violet where a slab has split. Most of the splits are old. Their edges have the soft, rubbed look of bones that have lain a long time in a drawer. There are escarpments a thousand feet high, but from the top of one you can seldom see the base of the next. Each drops away into a succession of pale benches, wide enough to have horizons of their own. At the foot of the cliffs, the finest grit collects in long scallops. It takes a season for the wind to move a scallop the length of a hand. Over the ages they have gone around whole mountains.

Nothing here has been wet in the lifetime of the landscape. The deep, branching gullies are the work of falling air. After sunset it pours off the heights, carrying a little stone, and in the morning the dust lies in bars on the inside of the bends. Some gullies are still deepening. Others have lost their headwaters to a wind coming down a different slope, and there the stone at the bottom has grown soft with leaves.

The groves are far apart, but there is nothing sparse about the inside of one. The plants grow shoulder over shoulder above a seam of black mineral. Low fans spread across the ground, overlapping so closely that even beneath a broken stalk you cannot see the soil. Above them stand the taller growths, with copper stems and folds of thick green tissue that are almost blue in shadow. These keep a clear, resinous sap that remains fluid in the bitterest cold. When a branch breaks, the sap draws out into a shining thread; a thread may hang all winter without producing so much as a drop. At the outermost tips of the grove, new leaves uncurl into the full glare, tender and pale as the inside of a nut.

You can walk for a day between groves. From a distance one looks like a dark mark no bigger than its own shadow. Nearer, on the bare ground around it, there are whole generations of shed scales, each turned hollow-side down by the wind. At the living edge the scales become scarce. Small grazers come out from under the fans to eat them while they are still pliable. A grazer is a broad, six-footed thing about the size of a hare, with a white back that catches the light a long way off. You might see three in the course of a summer. Mostly you find their marks: two rows of little brackets in the grit and, where one stopped to feed, a clean oval among the scales. The long-winged animals that hunt them are harder to see. Now and then a shadow slips over the fans and a whole margin of the grove goes still.

At midday the light is hard enough to hide the smaller contours of the country. The plateau looks almost level until late afternoon, when shadows gather in folds you could have sworn were not there. The sun can still be fierce on your face when the soles of your feet have begun to ache from the cold. In the lee of a slab you can hear the dry click of a fan closing. You have to wait a while for the next one. Even during the passage of the noon wind, the low, supple groves make very little sound.

The greatest changes are visible at a distance. One year, along a ledge on the opposite face of an immense valley, a grove turns the color of old brass. The following year there are only a few green islands in it. Its mineral seam has weathered through. In the next valley a newly exposed seam shows black for a while, then puts out a fringe so bright you can see it from the heights. The young grove is already dense by the time it is large enough to cast a shadow beyond itself.

Late in the day, the shadow of the upland reaches that valley floor. Up on the rim there is still an hour of sun. A narrow stand of fans keeps opening there, turning the last light over on its leaves, green on one side and silver on the other. Beyond it the country descends in terraces too broad to count. One by one they lose their color. The farthest is still white when the first cold dust begins to move at your feet.
