The city’s smallest watershed fits behind a stair tread.

The tread is on the uppermost public landing, where the stairs come out of the mountain and the streets begin. It has a square hole for a lifting key. Put your eye to the hole and you can see the underside of the street, the edge of the mountain, and the little distance the builders have left between them. At noon, daylight comes down a joint in the paving and reaches all the way to the back.

The mountain is black here, with pale grains in it the size of sugar. It broke along this face when the city was already standing. The engineers cut the street free of the moving rock and left the new face alone. Its edges are sharp enough to split a drop of water. Where a drop divides, one half falls straight into the gutter and the other clings, swells, and goes off by a longer route. You can tell which routes are oldest by the faint white deposits at their ends. None is old enough to have worn a groove.

It is well below freezing in the stairwell. The water is liquid all the same. Under the street, narrow bands of ceramic shift the freezing point wherever they touch the rock. They do it in sequence, drawing melt away from the foundations without needing to heat the mountain. A wet patch can advance across a ledge while the frost just beside it grows longer. At the bottom, where the ceramic stops, a pendant of clear ice takes the overflow. Every few minutes the gutter tilts by less than the width of a hair, and the pendant breaks off. The first time you hear it you think someone farther down the stairs has set a cup on a saucer.

For all the work being done here, there is very little to look at. Two supports cross the opening, far enough apart that neither throws a shadow on the other. A silver line carries water to the district below. Three survey marks on the stone are dated; the last two are almost on top of each other. There is no moss in the wet places, no root in the fracture. Once in a while a pale insect comes down through the paving joint. It spends a long time testing the dry side of the gutter with one foreleg. If it finds nothing, it goes back the way it came.

The pocket has weather of its own, made partly by the weather outside and partly by the city’s demand for water. In the early morning it clouds over from the bottom up. Later, when the kitchens on the north terraces draw from the line, the cloud thins and the far wall comes into view. On days when snow blows level across the roofs, fine grains fall through the joint and vanish on the wet rock. Sometimes one lands on the dry half of a ledge. Other grains bank against it until there is a drift no higher than a fingernail, with a steep lee face and a hollow underneath.

Through the keyhole, that drift is the only thing that looks soft. Above it, beyond the narrow strip of sky, people are crossing the landing on their way into town. Their weight passes from one support to the next. The supports give a little, the gutter keeps its slope, and on the black face a bead of water reaches a sharp edge and becomes two.
