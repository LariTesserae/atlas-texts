The cleft is small enough that a fallen fir could bridge it. None has. The firs grow back from the edges, where there is enough soil for a root to turn, and the cleft is younger than any of them.

It opened last winter high on the mountain, when a slab of the north face slipped a little way and stopped. The two walls still fit. A white vein of quartz runs down one wall and resumes, a handspan lower, on the other. Under the overhangs the fracture is almost blue. The rock there is sharp enough to take the tip off a fern without bending the stem.

At noon there is light at the mouth, but scarcely any at the bottom. The greenery belongs mostly to the upper walls, where roots exposed by the slip hang in thick, tangled curtains. Ferns lean out of the old soil into the new space. Their fronds knock together whenever the wind changes. Farther down, in the dark, there is only what has fallen: fir sprays still green, plugs of moss with stones caught in them, a whole shelf of sod tipped on its side. Its grass is slowly bending upward again.

The air is cold enough to wet the stone without a rain. Beads gather on the roots and travel along them to the fine ends. A little water sits in the deepest socket of the floor, its surface gray with stone dust. Below that socket the mountain falls away for nearly a mile. Here there is not enough water to run.

The noise comes in batches. Wind presses into the narrow upper opening and the cleft booms. Roots slap the walls. A chip comes off somewhere out of sight and skips down through the leaves, setting loose several more. Then the wind cuts out and, for a second or two, there is room to hear a single drop hit the pool.

On some nights a lower sound reaches the cleft from the works far down the valley. There is nothing of the works here—no road, no wire, no cut stump—but the crushers run after dark, and when the wind is right their steady beat is audible between the irregular blows of the rock. It is easy to mistake one for the other. The crushers keep their time. The mountain can be quiet for half an hour and then drop a ton of stone.

By morning a ledge that was bare the day before may be piled with green branches. They will stay fresh for a while in the cold. Higher up, where they tore loose, a strip of earth no wider than a sleeve is open to the weather. The surviving ferns are already brushing across it.
