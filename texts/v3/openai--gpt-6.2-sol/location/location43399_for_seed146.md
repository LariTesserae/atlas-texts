The cavern is warmest at the entrance. A hand laid against the wall feels the last of the day's heat coming down through the stone. A little farther in, that difference disappears. Floor, air, and ceiling have the same warmth. It is possible to sleep directly on the rock without waking cold.

The entrance is a slit scarcely wider than a shoulder. Beyond it there is room for a country. A lamp, lowered on a cord from the first ledge, lights successive outcrops without ever lighting the floor. The outcrops are black glass furred at their bases with white needles. Nothing has blunted their edges. Where two faces meet, the line is fine enough to split a hair drawn across it. The cavern opened all at once, along a weakness in the glass, and the two sides still fit. Somewhere above each prow of rock is the hollow it came out of.

There is no water. In the deepest basins lies a gray powder so fine that a foot sinks into it without a crunch. Set a bowl down in it and the powder climbs the outside in little branching lines. The bowl comes away with a white rim even if it has never had anything in it.

Most of what looks like gravel is alive. The commonest animals are jointed wedges, warm to the touch, with floury undersides and backs almost indistinguishable from broken glass. They pack themselves nose to tail in the cracks. On a wall that seems bare, a lamp held close will show hundreds of them, each with two small pads pressed flat against the stone. They feed where the rock bears its own weight. At the foot of a pillar, under a low shelf, in the thin neck between two hollows, they gather so densely that it can be difficult to see any stone at all. An unloaded chip will not keep one alive.

They move in turns. One lifts its pads, edges forward, and fits them into the places just vacated by its neighbor. The movement can travel the length of a ledge without altering its outline. There are larger animals in the powder basins, but the easiest way to find one is to sit still and wait for the dust between your fingers to draw slowly sideways. They are flat and many-legged, and pass beneath a resting hand without lifting it. The warmth they take from the fingers is scarcely noticeable. What gives them away is how long the little patch of coolness lasts.

Far below the entrance there are stone huts. They stand singly, often on shelves with perfectly good room for more. Their walls are dry-laid, the door lintels chipped square at the ends. Inside each is a raised bed and a shallow cup of hammered copper. The cup is not for drinking. Tap it with a knuckle and listen: if the sound cuts off at once, the pressure in the rock will take it, and it is safe to go on. If the cup rings, stay where you are until the ringing stops. It can take an afternoon. In the oldest huts the copper has been beaten thin around the rim by generations of people making this test as gently as they could.

There is weather here, though none of it stirs the dust. Broad changes of pressure travel through the rock, taking years to cross the cavern. On a clear shelf you can sometimes feel one approach as a succession of minute taps through the soles of your feet. The wedges begin to shift far ahead of it. Every patch that empties fills again behind its passage. A person returning after a lifetime would see the same dark bands of animals on the same pale seams, and could be forgiven for thinking that none of them had moved.

Deep in the cavern, when a lamp is put out, the eye keeps a little of the country: a ledge slanting down, the white foot of a pillar, the opening of a hut. These go one by one. The warmth makes it hard to tell how much space there is around the body. A wall a finger's breadth from the cheek feels no different from a drop a mile deep. The people who built the huts cut thumb hollows beside their doors, always at the same height. Coming in from the dark, you find the hollow first. Then, a handspan beyond it, there is room to step through.
