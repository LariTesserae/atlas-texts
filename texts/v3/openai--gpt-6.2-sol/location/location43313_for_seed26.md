The valley is six streets wide and has no streets in it. From the service gallery you can see all the way across, though the far wall takes a while to distinguish from the ceiling. Both are faced in the same blue-gray tile. The difference is that water runs down one and travels in bright, unsteady beads along the other.

They built the low ground first. Long trays of black enamel meet at raised seams, each seam stamped with the month it was laid. A person could walk dry-footed for half a mile on those ridges if the pumps were between cycles. When the pumps turn over, the trays fill in order, beginning at the north end. Water spills through the slots with a noise like a train coming in. For several minutes the whole valley looks flooded. Then the trays empty, also in order, and the reflection of the overhead lamps breaks into shorter and shorter pieces.

It is cold enough that the handrails have felt sleeves where a bare hand might need to close on them. Frost survives on the sheltered side of every bolt. Out in the open reaches, where there is room for an air current to get going, the chill moves ahead of the water: you feel it in your teeth just before the first tray fills. There is no smell of earth. There is wet felt, machine oil, and the faint sweet smell of new grout. Even the pale banks along the drainage cuts are manufactured, a rough cast tread meant to grip the feet of maintenance crews. Winter has already chipped a few corners off it. The chips are dazzlingly white.

The place is young. You can tell by the saw marks in the expansion joints and by the way the water has not yet settled on a route down the walls. Every week some narrow runnel gives up, and another starts two tiles over. Below the older leaks, a skim of mineral deposit takes the pattern of the tile and then makes its own. One has grown out over a stair in a delicate lip. The crew turns sideways to pass it rather than knock it off. So far it is the only thing down here that has made a roof for itself.

There are twelve lamps still burning down the middle of the valley. The others are switched off until the works extend that far. Under each working lamp is a silt basket, and in four of those baskets there is something green. A few grass blades lean toward the light so nearly flat that their tips rest on the mesh. Moss has got into a strip of felt on the lowest handrail. Once in a while a moth comes off the freight lift and spends its brief time here trying every illuminated surface except the lamp. The only animal that seems to belong is a small, nearly clear shrimp. You see one best when a tray empties: it will work its way upstream through water barely deep enough to cover it, going from screw head to screw head.

The machinery is mostly elsewhere. You hear the deep stroke of a gate through the soles of your boots; the answering gate, a quarter mile away, is loud enough in the air to stop a conversation. In between there is a steady overhead rattle from the return chains. Each chain carries empty hooks at long intervals. After a while you learn to hear a hook pass above you. It gives a single high note as it rounds a guide wheel, far up where the lamp glow thins out.

There is one thing the crew has never got used to. At the south end, the roar of the incoming water reaches the gallery a few seconds before the gate opens. You can stand there and hear a full tray draining, hear the last thin rush through the slot, and look down at enamel dry enough to show your footprints. Then the water arrives and runs through the whole performance again. People new to the works tend to step back at the first sound. The others wait for the cold.

Past the last light the valley widens. The seams diverge for a set of trays not yet installed; the pipes there are capped and still wear their paper labels. Water has reached that part anyway. Drop by drop it falls from a cable sling into an unused bolt hole. During the brief quiet when the return chains change speed, you can hear the hole fill, spill, and begin to fill again.
