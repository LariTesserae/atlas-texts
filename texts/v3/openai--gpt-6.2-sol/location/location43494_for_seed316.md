The plain is so flat that rain falling a hundred miles away can be seen to strike it. There is no bright line where the ground ends. There are only successive darkenings, each with its own weather.

Underfoot, the stone is warm and worn to the texture of an old tooth. There has been time enough to grind away the mountains, then to grind away the harder roots of the mountains, then to erase the beds of the rivers that carried them off. What remains is a low continent of stone on which water collects to the depth of a fingernail. A ripple can travel all afternoon without finding a bank.

Nothing here is vacant. In the space between two steps stand a film of brown water, the stipple of rain on it, the shadows of drops still falling, and a thin amber scum that gathers itself into wrinkles as the stone breathes. The air is crowded too. It is full of warm spray, veils of finer spray, and clear, elbow-shaped turns of wind that can be seen by the way droplets hang in them. Yet there is almost nothing green. Here and there a tuft of black rushes lashes the water flat. Around the roots lie empty seed cases slick as little tongues.

The noise is immense. Much of it comes from rain with an unbroken fall of miles, landing on stone all at once. Some comes from beneath. Long plates of the plain lift a hair’s breadth against one another, and a note passes through the soles of the feet. Far off, the surface buckles into a chain of dark hills. They run abreast for half the afternoon, with white water unrolling behind them. When they reach you, they are scarcely high enough to wet the tops of your feet. The stone has not moved.

There is no quiet interval in which to judge how dim it has become. The light has the color of a window with a wet hand held over it. Now and then a whole reach of the plain brightens from underneath, and for a moment you can see the round shadows of pebbles on the undersides of the clouds.

In a day's walk there might be one animal: a pale, soft-backed thing that waits with its mouth pressed to a crack. When the water changes direction it turns, slowly and with considerable effort, to keep its mouth in the same place. The crack has gone on ahead of it. By the time the animal has finished turning, the crack has come round again. Nearer dusk you may hear the hard clatter of wings, unexpectedly close, and see nothing but three brief hollows in the rain.

There are a few walls. They stand ankle-high, in loops or very short straight runs, made of stones a hand could lift. In their lee are things the general wear has spared: a white pebble with an edge, the hollow stalk of a rush, a pinch of dry dust. A great sheet of water crosses the plain and breaks over one of the walls. The dust stirs. The wall is dry on both sides.

At dusk a narrow margin of bare ground appears underfoot. You can follow it for a little while. On one side the rain is just arriving; on the other is water already trembling with the blows. The margin curves, and in the wan light you see that it continues as far as sight will go, carrying on through one storm after another. Farther along, against the pale strip of stone, a tuft of rushes leans in the rain. You recognize it by a broken stem. You passed it that morning, bent the other way.
