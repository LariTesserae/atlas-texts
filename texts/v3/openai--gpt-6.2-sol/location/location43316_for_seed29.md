High in the mountain, behind a face of black glass that looks solid from the valley, there is a crack as long as a forearm. It opens inward. At the mouth there is scarcely room for a grain of grit; farther in, the two walls stand a thumb’s width apart. This is the whole place. Its highest point is a little shelf crowded with pale crystals. Its lowest is a corner into which everything that dies eventually slips.

The crack is young. The walls still fit each other: every blister on one has a hollow opposite it, every knife-edge its matching notch. Nothing has flowed through to round the edges or sort the fallen grains. There is no mud, no stain left by an old pool. The air coming up through the mountain carries the dry bite of sulfur. Even at night, when frost whitens the exposed ridge, the back of the pocket stays hot enough that its inhabitants keep to the outer wall.

There is almost no light. For a few minutes late in the day, a glint gets past the mouth and strikes the crystal shelf. It shows how little empty space there is. Flakes of glass stand on edge between the crystals. Under every flake there are husks, eggs, or animals so flat they can turn around without lifting their backs. Some are no bigger than the dot left by a pencil. Their shed skins collect in the angles of the rock, and the smallest mites feed there. Nothing grows toward the light. When it goes, the animals carry on passing one another in the dark, folding their legs into grooves along their sides to make room.

The loudest thing in the pocket is the ore haulage below. The winch is far down the slope, out of sight of the crack and well below the frost line, but the mountain carries its load changes better than the open air does. When the loaded cars go over the head sheave, two loose flakes chatter against each other so hard they throw off dust. The animals flatten. Then the line settles into its long pull and they come out again. Where the flakes touch, there are two pinpoints of polish. Those are the only worn places in the crack.

The animals came from older seams. They eat what the air brings: fragments of insects blown against the upper face, fine dry scraps that work their way through openings too small for the insects themselves. An outer lip catches most of it. The lip is so narrow that a large mite feeding there can hold up traffic in both directions. Below it, the black glass is stippled with eggs, each set in the shadow of a grain. The hottest recess is empty of living things, but not bare. It is full of skins light enough to move when the air shifts, and too brittle to survive the journey out.

A busy day leaves the place much as it found it. The winch stops; dust comes to rest. A skin splits and a soft white animal takes the place of a darker one. Yet the opening is still being made. As the exposed face cools and the interior stays hot, the strain gathers at the blind end. One night, perhaps years from now, it will travel the width of a crystal. A grain now wedged so tightly that the haulage cannot shake it will drop. Behind it there will be a narrow strip of stone that has never touched air. It will be hot and dark and, for a little while, empty.
