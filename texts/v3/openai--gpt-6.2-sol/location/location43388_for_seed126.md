At noon the plain is so bright that the easiest things to see are the flaws. A gray crescent where two surfaces have worn against each other; a row of pits with one pit missing; a seam that runs straight for half a day and then bends, very slightly, to go around something no longer there.

The plain is low. From its highest ridge, which rises about as far as the roof of a house, you can see the white of it go on until distance turns it blue. There are darker flats in that blue, and beyond them other whites. None of it is bedrock. Even the fine buff dust that collects on the lee side of the ridge was made, though it has worn too smooth to say what it was made for. In a few places the surface has peeled back, exposing older surfaces underneath. They have curbs and expansion joints, just as the present surface does, but the curbs describe much smaller squares. The farther down you look, the more often there used to be corners.

There is no water. The shallow basins have never held it. Toward evening they fill with a violet gas heavy enough to displace the clear air, and the lowest ones hold their color until morning. A stone dropped into one leaves a pale channel that closes from the bottom up. There is no splash. On the warmer slopes, where the gas thins early, little stands of foil-leaf grow in the joints. Their pale roots fan out between layers of the pavement; their leaves are dark on one side and bright on the other. In the morning every leaf in a stand shows its dark face. By midday one or two have turned. It takes until late afternoon for the whole stand to flash silver, leaf by leaf, though there may be only twelve leaves.

From a distance the country looks empty. Kneel down and there is hardly an unoccupied inch. The seams contain beads, shutters, combs fine as eyelashes, dusty little stacks of translucent plates. Deep in a joint a comb draws back, letting a row of amber beads through; the last bead sticks, and the comb waits. Warm bands pass beneath the paving day and night. Dust above them sorts itself into ribs, and the ribs travel without any of their grains going very far. Over the course of a season this can carry the dust out of a basin and up onto a ridge.

There is weather on the scale of the whole plain. After several days of hard light, heat gathers over the western flats in a bank with a straight lower edge. The bank drifts east. Wherever its shadow arrives, the pavement gives up the warmth it has stored, and the air rises into the bank and makes it taller. By the time it reaches the eastern flats it can be seen from a thousand kilometers away. It goes by in almost perfect silence. For an hour the ground feels pleasantly warm through the soles, the light goes the color of unbleached linen, and every hollow fills at once.

A small animal sometimes follows that shadow. It has six wide feet and a back the color of the dust. Where the violet gas has just drained off a surface, it picks at the brittle fringe left along the high mark. It can feed for most of an afternoon in a hollow you could step across. Once in a while it lifts its head and stands quite still. The sound it is listening for, if there is a sound, is too faint to hear over the scuff of your own sleeve.

There are rules here that take a while to notice. On a clear day you can watch the shadow of a survey post and the shadow of your hand agree as the light moves west. Find the same post lying on its side, and it casts no shadow at all. The little hollow where it stood still goes dark at noon. Along some of the oldest seams you can see whole ranks of such shadows, each with a narrow foot and a broader head, crossing a place where there has been nothing upright for longer than the present pavement has existed. Dust moves more slowly through them. Foil-leaf does well at their edges.

At sundown the light comes in low enough to show how much of the plain is moving. Long strips of paving bow a finger’s breadth and settle; the warmer ridges let go of their dust. A dark basin in the middle distance acquires a bright rim, starting on one side and working around, as the shutters in its lip turn over. The last one leaves a small gap. The basin has a broken circle for most of the night. Just before dawn the gap closes. By then the animal has gone to ground under a loosened curb, with three of its broad feet folded over its nose.
