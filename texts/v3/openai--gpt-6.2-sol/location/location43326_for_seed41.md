The heat begins three steps before the bottom. Up on the slope there is wind among the dry grasses; here, where the steps turn under a shelf of limestone, the air is warm enough that a damp sleeve never quite dries. The room below is no bigger than a small chapel. From the bottom step you can see the opposite wall, though it takes a while to see what’s moving on it.

Most of the ceiling is low and heavily rooted. A fig growing on the slope has sent roots down through the limestone, and over a long time they have found the floor and thickened into pale, flattened pillars. Between them hangs a finer mesh of living root, close enough to brush your face. The green is all on one side, under a fissure that admits a slant of daylight for part of the morning. Ferns grow there, and liverworts, and a bush with six broad leaves, each turned separately toward the slit. Beyond the last leaf, the roots go on into the dark. Nothing green follows them.

There is no stream. Rain takes a day or two to work its way through the hill, and then the room gets wet from the top down. Beads run along the undersides of the roots until they reach a fork. Some fall into shallow cups in the rock; others strike the old leaf litter and are gone. The cups have thick rims, built up by the same drops that wear a little hollow in their bottoms. In dry weeks the smallest ones empty altogether. The deepest is about the size of a hand, and there is nearly always something alive in it.

Once your eyes adjust, it’s hard to find a patch of ground that stays still. Little crickets back into cracks without singing. A centipede threads the litter, sending pale springtails off in all directions. There are snails on the roots and flat, jointed things under the snails, eating the soft edge of an old leaf. Two thumb-sized frogs sit at either end of the deepest cup. One of them makes a short lunge; a cricket disappears, and the other frog, which has not moved at all, closes its eyes. The only sound is a drop landing on a leaf that is already wet.

At the foot of the steps, three stones have been set on edge to keep the slope from spilling into the room. They still do. A root has lifted the middle one just enough for beetles to pass beneath it. On the far side, in the dry triangle behind the stones, there’s a shallow layer of dust that has caught the tracks of a small animal coming and going by the same route.

By noon the bright seam has crept off the bush and up the wall. For a few minutes it catches the lower ends of the hanging roots. The drops show up one by one, at different heights, and you can see how much water is in the room without any of it running. Then the light leaves the roots. From the steps the place looks still again, except where a snail has reached the tip of a fern and the whole frond is slowly bending under it.
