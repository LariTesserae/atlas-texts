From a height it looks like bare country: tawny benches, ravines with sloping sides, long level distances broken by the occasional black spire. Then a wind crosses the nearest bench and the ground turns its pale underside to you. The whole slope is in leaf.

The upland is old enough that no one knows whether there is stone under it. Where the ravines have eaten deepest, the exposed strata are wood: red wood that powders at a touch, gray wood with the cold fracture of flint, a layer of white root felt in which the mouths of long-dead burrows are still perfectly round. The youngest layer is a skin of little jointed leaves. They lie flat to bear the sun, and a person can walk for days on them without passing anything tall enough to stand in their shade.

It is hot up here. The light is ordinary, which somehow makes the heat more impressive. At noon a shadow has a sharp edge and is only a little cooler than the ground beside it. There is hardly ever rain, but turn back the lip of a ledge and you can put two fingers into the damp. The plants keep their water on the underside of things. It collects overnight in blind cups, in the seams of the wood, in the soft roofs of hollows. Toward morning it falls from one layer to the next, a few drops at a time. A traveler may hear water underneath her bed and find nothing to drink there when she gets up.

For all that grows here, the country feels empty. A spire on the horizon may be the next spire for a hundred miles. These are the standing stems of plants whose leaves are the broad benches; some are hollow, and the wind that enters at their feet comes out far above in a different key. In the distance between them there is little to judge size by. A white scratch across a slope turns out to be a dry gully wide enough to contain its own afternoon weather. The cloud over it grows handsome and dark, drops six or seven streaks of rain that never reach the surface, and is gone before sundown.

The animals are largely responsible for the illusion of emptiness. Almost none of them stand above the leaf skin at midday. They are below, in the galleries, in the cool thickness of the benches, or clinging belly-up to the walls of cracks. You see a twitch, then a hundred feet of ground settle as a file of heavy grazers passes underneath. Sometimes one breaks through. It blinks in the brightness with leaves stuck to its muzzle, takes a bite of the lip it has made, and steps down out of sight. The hole stays open for a season. Smaller mouths widen it; tendrils hang into it. In a few years it is a place with its own damp, its own inhabitants, and a rim you can sit on without remembering that it began as an accident.

At dusk the animals come out in numbers that seem impossible for a country so open. The grazers arrange themselves on the inclines, nose to tail, following the narrow bands where new growth has softened in the cooling air. Thin-winged hunters pace them from overhead. The first flying insects cast individual shadows; a little later the shadows overlap and it looks as though another cloud has come up. Down in the ravines, the calling begins. It climbs out of several depths at once, out of throats and wing cases and chambers in the wood that amplify whatever is moving inside them. By full dark you can shout into somebody’s ear and they will turn to you only because they felt your breath.

There are a few hand-built shelters on the upland. Most are low hoops of twisted stem with mats pulled over them. The mats are alive and the hoops are not, and where the two meet you can see the green weave slowly taking the weight from the old frame. On one lee slope there is a little row of clay jars, their mouths covered with split leaves. Whoever set them there cut a thumb-wide gutter in the bench to lead the morning seep into them. The gutter has nearly grown shut. One jar still takes a drop every few minutes, and around that jar, in a span no wider than a hand, the leaf skin is a deep and tender green.

There is one hour before sunrise when the noise withdraws below ground. On the highest bench you can hear the last of the night’s water falling through the wood. The plain is quite still in the gray light. Then the sun gets under the edges of the leaves, and they begin to turn, not all at once but in widening patches, as far away as you can see. You can watch for a long time before the warmth reaches your own feet.
