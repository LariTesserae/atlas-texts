There is a little pass inside the mountain, higher than any pass on its surface. You reach it by a service lift, then through a door with a brush along its sill. The brush keeps the dust in. Beyond it, in a space not much longer than a railway carriage, two slopes rise to a saddle under a bright, ordinary sky.

The mountain is built, too, but here you can see how it was done. The slopes are laid in courses of pale refractory brick, with dark wedges driven between them to bring the faces to the right inclination. Along the crest, the bricks have been cut diagonally. Their edges are still sharp enough to catch a thread from a glove. Below the saddle lies a little fan of blue-black grit, each fragment angular and clean. It looks as though it fell yesterday. In fact, the pieces are keyed into the floor. The fan was delivered in three sections and assembled from a drawing.

It is bitterly cold. There is no frost. Nothing ever sweats on the bright flanges, and the shallow ditch that follows the nearer slope has never carried anything. It is there for a cable that was left out of the final installation. Looking down its length, you can see the flat white ends of the bricks and, every so often, a tuft of rust-colored leaves.

The plants are alive, though it takes the maintenance crew a long time to establish this. They grow in porous plugs fed from behind the slope, on a fluid that remains clear and mobile at temperatures that would split a water pipe. A leaf takes several years to unfold. It comes out at a low angle, doubles back, and lies against the plug. There is no smell. Between one tuft and the next there may be twenty feet of bare masonry. The spacing makes each plant conspicuous from the saddle, but from below you can walk past one with your hand an inch from it and never see it.

The noise is appalling. The pass is built over a bank of compressors, and the whole structure carries their note. It is too steady to hear as the work of separate machines. Open your mouth and you feel it in the roots of your teeth. Stand with one boot on the brick and one on the steel threshold and the sound seems to lean to one side. Yet there is no trembling in the leaves, and a loose washer laid on the saddle will stay where you put it. People have tried this often enough that there is usually a washer up there.

The sky is a deep panel of lamps behind ground glass. It gives a convincing impression of a clear morning at altitude, even down to the faint blue in the shadows. After a few minutes you begin to notice that you can walk all the way over the pass without bringing the far hillside into view. There is no far hillside. The last course of bricks meets the glass at eye level, and beyond it there is just enough illuminated depth for the eye to keep looking.

On the service side of the door, the place is called *Allowance*. The original plans required room here for the mountain to settle. It never did. At shift change, when the lift is slow, someone will occasionally come in and stand at the saddle. There’s a square of yellow paint there, just wide enough for two boots, where the surveyors were meant to set their instrument. From that square you can see every plant in the pass, and nobody waiting at the door can see you.
