At the upper edge of the rain, the ground is still deciding how to be ground.

A heel goes in the width of a fingernail and stops. What stops it is not stone but several thousand layers of leaf, pressed together while wet. The upper leaves are olive and black; deeper down they are cream, then clear. Through a tear in the surface you can see a bubble tilt slowly in its bed. It doesn’t rise. The bed grows around it, and by evening the bubble is part of the slope.

There is stone, though not much of it has seen weather. It comes up in long, sharp ridges, bluish on the sides and white at the tips. Wherever a ridge breaks through, little twin leaves already sit in the split. The rock has not had time to round off, nor the rain to dig itself a proper channel. Water goes wherever it can for half an hour, then the land closes that way and opens another. A brook runs straight down the flank of a hill, spreads into a sheet, and disappears under a band of broad, flat stems. Five miles away the stems give it back, a little colder and no lower than where it entered.

From a height you might take the whole country for a forest. Once down in it, you can’t find the point where the forest begins. There are trunks where the ground needs lifting, roots where it needs holding, leaves at every angle a leaf can occupy. The standing water is crowded with pale green growth right down to its cold bottom. Even the larger pools have roofs: floating leaves with their rims overlapped, lifting and settling as the water moves beneath them. No patch of mud stays bare long enough to take a print.

It is cool all day. Rain gathers on the undersides of things even when none is falling. The light comes through a high thickness of cloud and then through so many leaves that a fresh break in a stem, pale as the inside of an apple, is sometimes the brightest thing in sight.

And it is very loud.

At first the noise seems to be water, and much of it is. Every leaf that cups the rain has an edge that spills it. There are drops falling a hundred feet through layers of foliage and drops with scarcely an inch to go. Beneath them is a coarser sound, the splitting of wet fibers. The ground rises here and there in short increments, each with a crack you feel in your teeth. There are no calls in the canopy, no wingbeats, no insects to go quiet before a storm. The storm comes on top of a noise that was already too loud to speak through.

On the high flats, there is a kind of reed that bends in advance of weather. Not a minute or two in advance: in the morning it takes the shape the wind would give it at dusk. You can follow the bend from one stand to the next, across slopes the approaching weather cannot yet have touched. By the time the gust arrives, the reeds have lowered whole acres of their fine leaves and raised their stout ones. The rain passes through without flattening them. Farther down, species with no likeness to the reeds have made their own preparations. A seam in the ground will be widening to receive a flood while the cloud that bears it is still growing on the far side of the range.

There is nothing laid out in rows. Nothing has a polished face or a fastener. If this is engineering, it has nowhere to stand apart from the thing it works on. The small folds at the base of a leaf do something that a valley does at another scale: hold water out of its natural fall until something elsewhere is ready for it. Sometimes the holding happens without an observable barrier. A film of rain remains on the uphill side of a leaf, trembling, though the whole leaf hangs nearly vertical. At last it lets go, and across the slope other leaves let go with it. You hear the release coming, but there is no time to get clear of the deluge.

The heights keep their own weather. From the top of one raw ridge you can see clouds forming below in troughs of darker green. They rise until they hide the nearer hills, and beyond them another tier of land goes on in the same dim light. The farthest banks of cloud look still until you have watched them for an hour. Then you see one spilling over a rim so remote that the rain takes the rest of the afternoon to come into view.

Toward evening a long section of hillside lifts. The change is slow enough to miss if you watch the hillside itself. Watch instead the water caught among the leaves: here it begins to run left where all day it has run right. A little farther up, black stems emerge from the mat, uncurling from the base as the grade changes. They are not reaching for the light. They grow almost level for a while, through the flow, catching fallen leaves against themselves. By tomorrow there will be a low bank. In another season the bank may be high enough to catch cloud.

For now, from the ridge, all you can see of the new country is a pale line where the stems have turned their wet undersides to the sky. The water reaches it and makes a different sound. Then the line lengthens into the dark.
