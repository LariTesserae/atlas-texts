The plain is warm enough that a hand laid on the stone makes no impression of warmth or cold. It lies scarcely above its own water. From anywhere on it you can see the low black rim, though in places the rim is six or seven miles off and so worn down that it looks like a thickening of the horizon.

There is no soil. The stone has weathered into shallow overlapping dishes, some as wide as a house, most no bigger than a heel print. A little water stands in the deepest ones. Elsewhere there is only the dark of damp stone, or a pale mineral crust with a wet edge. Nothing grows along those edges. The green on the south side of the plain is in the rock itself; where a flake has come loose, its underside is green too.

The noise is considerable. Out on the broad flats it resembles the sound of a mill heard through a wall: a low, even running note with a finer vibration above it. At a few of the dishes the higher note drops away and you can hear a hard, regular knock. The knock carries through the soles better than through the air. There is no wall, and there has never been a mill.

Under the plain, warm water moves through long beds of porous stone. Seams of harder rock divide the flow, bring it together, divide it again. At one of the larger pools you can see a fleck of mica travel along the bottom against the direction of the surface ripples. The pool feeds a seam at one end and is fed by another at the other. Neither lip is more than a finger’s breadth high. The same is true of the smallest pools, even the ones that look as if they ought to dry before noon. Put a finger in one and the water wells back into the space it leaves. The wet mark on your finger lasts longer than the disturbance in the pool.

There are animals, but you can cross the plain without seeing one. In the lee of a worn ridge, where a shelf of stone hangs an inch above a strip of wet gravel, there are sometimes small translucent bodies folded around the pebbles. Their mouths face into the seep. They shift so little that the surest sign of life is the clean patch each keeps on its pebble. The rest of the gravel wears a soft gray deposit. Farther out, where the gravel gives way to bare stone, there is nothing for them to hold on to.

In ordinary daylight the plain looks drier than it is. The pools take on the color of their beds, and you notice them by the small pieces of sky that appear at your feet and vanish when you take another step. From the rim it is easier to see how much empty ground there is between them. No channel has lately cut across another. The rounded spillways are so old that a dry one is as smooth as a wet one. The water still carries grit, but where it takes a little from one face it lays a little down on the next. There is no advancing bank of gravel, no shallowing lake, no place from which the plain seems to be getting on with becoming something else.

Toward the middle, the separate notes resolve into a single steady sound. It is loud enough that two people walking abreast would have to raise their voices. Then the ground lifts by perhaps a foot, and on the far side the sound thins. There is another stretch of bare stone, another set of widely spaced pools. A drop hangs beneath a ledge, swells, and falls. Before its ring has reached the edge of the pool, the next drop is hanging in its place.
