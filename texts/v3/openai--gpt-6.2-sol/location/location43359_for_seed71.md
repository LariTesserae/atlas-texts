Above the last pass, the land levels out at an altitude where there ought not to be so much land. It runs clear across the continent. Seen from the pass, it looks almost flat; seen from a day’s walk inland, the pass is already lost below the curve of it. There are no peaks to take bearings from. The highest things are broad swells of stone that rise so gradually you notice you have climbed one only when the puddles begin spilling the other way.

The rock is dark and fine-grained. A fresh chip, struck from it with a hammerstone, looks wet even after a week indoors. The exposed surfaces have gone pale as flour. All the old fractures have worn into shallow gutters, and some of the gutters are wide enough to hold an entire afternoon’s weather. From a ridge you can watch a shower travel down one, its head dissolving into mist while its tail is still falling thirty miles away.

Most mornings are bright. There is a sharp little sun, a blue sky, and frost tucked under the west edges of things. By noon there may be rain, though it rarely falls long enough to darken a whole wall. There is no permanent snow. Water spends the night as a white fur on the ground and the day working its way from one hollow to the next. Some hollows are dry for years. Then a ledge a hundred miles away sheds a sheet of ice, and three days later a drowned garden rises green through the water.

Where growth takes hold, it is lavish. Moss heaps up in banks as deep as a person is tall, with folded leaves growing out of it in tiers. The leaves are thick and cold to the touch. In rain they open far enough to show the small red leaves underneath; in dry weather they close so neatly that a bank looks upholstered. Pale stalks come up through the mats and dust the next hollow with spores. Nothing browses the tender ends. Nothing tunnels under the roots. The mud beside a pool will keep the impression of a falling drop until the next rain.

Between these banks lie miles of bare stone. A person on foot can spend half a day approaching a patch of green, pass through it shoulder-deep in leaves, and come out the other side in less time than it takes to eat an apple.

The city occupies one of the shallower slopes. Its outermost paved street is sixty miles from its innermost stair, though there are places along that street where the only building in sight is a roof over a basin. The dense parts come all at once: low houses sharing walls, little courts green to the lintels, steps worn into scoops by bare heels. Then the paving goes on alone. A fine stone curb, with drain holes cut at regular intervals, keeps company with the walker across twelve miles of nothing.

People split slabs with wooden wedges soaked in rainwater. They roof with overlapping stone thin enough to lift by hand, and mend pots by boring paired holes and drawing the cracks shut with copper wire. In the mornings there is a little smoke. The fuel is dried stem and the roofs are low, so the smoke comes out at knee height and wanders down the street. There are no dogs to object to a stranger. If someone is working in a court, the first sign of them is often a cup left on a wall where the sun can get at it.

Much of the city was built to persuade water to go where it had gone the year before. On the older streets the drain openings are worn smooth. You can put an arm into one and feel the current draw gently at your sleeve. In a few places the current is warm. It sometimes smells of leaves that have not opened yet.

Distance is dependable on the open rock so long as you are going somewhere you can see. Trouble begins at doors, under bridges, or where a few inches of water cover a familiar stretch of paving. A covered stair in the city has nine steps on a dry day. After rain, the sixth step begins to take the weight of a foot before the foot reaches it. If you keep walking, the stair brings you out in a narrow court where the same mason’s marks appear on the walls, still white with stone dust. The stone there is softer. People once went in to cut new lintels from it, which is why two houses on that street now have doorways they cannot close.

Children are sent to fetch water with shallow cups. A shallow cup cannot show much of a reflection. In a deep jar, a window may appear with light in it although the house across the street has been roofless since anyone can remember. Usually this does no harm. But if the jar is set down and left overnight, there may be wet footprints in the dust around it in the morning. The households that still use deep jars keep them lidded. They need the water.

Out on the plateau the changes are larger and harder to catch in the act. Near the northern rim there is a belt of gardens whose roots lie on top of the stone for weeks at a time. Each year the green advances along the same shallow grooves. Each year there are more grooves behind it. A surveyor once left a line of pegs there to see which was moving. By spring the pegs stood close enough together to span with a hand, and one enclosed a cast of its own grain in solid rock. When the gardeners need to mark a boundary now, they lay a cord at the edge of the growth and move it as needed.

Most of this happens quietly. The plants make a faint papery sound when the sun comes out after a shower. Far into a cold night, when the streets are empty, a roof slab may slide the width of a fingernail and knock against its neighbor. There are hours when the only sound is water dropping down a drain. You can listen to it from a dry street under a clear sky and be fairly sure that somewhere, not necessarily uphill, it has begun to rain.

At the last occupied house on the eastern road, a woman takes in her washing when a dark patch comes into view on the plain. It won’t reach her before evening. The cloth, though, is already damp along the hems. She folds it damp. By the time the rain gets to the house the moss will be open, and she’ll go out again to clear the leaves from the drain.
