At the height where the valley ought to narrow to a pass, it opens into a shelf of black stone. The city occupies the shelf from lip to lip. On a clear day there might be a view of the lower country, but there are no days here, only intervals when the cloud drops below the retaining walls and the water can be heard falling all the way down.

The mountain is new. Its terraces have the sharp inside corners of things that have never been rained on, though rain has been falling for as long as anyone has lived here. Break a flake off a roadside outcrop and the surface beneath is already wet. The water seems to come from the stone as much as from the sky. It fills the seams in the pavements, hangs under the bridge decks in long trembling beads, and collects in shallow basins where the ice is clear enough to mistake for depth. There is no soil. Nothing roots in the mortar. Where the walls look furred, it is because a thousand small animals have hooked their feet into the pores and turned their backs to the runoff.

You can't see much of the city at once. A shutter opens across a court and for a moment you see three levels of balconies, a pipe thick as a house, and the wet elbow of a stair going up into nothing. Then the shutter closes. Most doors have no lamps. They have a thumb-wide strip of warm metal at the latch, and if you live here you learn to find it without looking. Cold comes up through the soles even in the houses; people sit with their feet on the lower rungs of their chairs.

The animals are everywhere. In the drains are pale, jointed things that stand crosswise in the water and comb it with their forelegs. In the outer courts, broad creatures sleep on their sides with their mouths open, letting smaller ones pick through the ridges of their gums. A handrail can be warm for twenty yards because something has wrapped itself around the underside. On the lowest roofs there are whole colonies of animals that nobody has seen from above. From a room directly beneath them you can hear the young being passed from one body to another, a succession of soft landings across the ceiling.

Once in a while the whole city hears a large animal breathe. The sound comes up through cellar floors and out of the mouths of gutters. There is some disagreement over whether it lives beneath the shelf or merely leans against it from the other side. The surveyors have a place in their records for the breath but have never agreed where to put the animal.

For all that noise, very little happens quickly. Rain drums on the same hollow steps all winter. A colony advances along a parapet by the width of a hand in a season, leaving a clean stripe where its bellies have polished the stone. The deep calls from the culverts seem to answer one another until you stay long enough to realize that the second call is often the first one coming back by a longer route. The city has built over channels that run for miles without coming to the surface.

Its civil works are stranger than its animals. At the end of one avenue, a reservoir lies on its side against the escarpment. Pebbles fall past it normally; the water rests against the cliff as if that were down. You can stand at the rail and hear little waves breaking vertically beside your ear. In the older part of town, where a stair would have taken up too much room, the builders gave the distance between two landings to an empty storeroom. You cross the gap in one step. The storeroom is kept locked: it takes nearly an hour to walk to the back wall and return.

But there are ordinary drains as well, and people go out in the cold to clear them. At the north edge of the shelf, where the last tram stops, a maintenance crew has laid a run of iron grates over a ditch that wasn't there the previous year. The rock is still coming up. You can tell by the way the new ditch ends in a blunt bulge, with water spilling over both shoulders. The crew has left the final grate loose, ready to be moved when there is somewhere to move it to.

Beyond the tram stop the buildings thin to a few pump houses, then nothing. Underfoot the shelf is smooth and hard, except where fine lips of stone have begun to rise around the pools. A person could walk out there for half an hour and still hear the city clearly: the water in its downpipes, the animals on its roofs, the knock of a tram crossing a joint. Out here there is another sound, faint but close, like a fingernail drawn around the rim of a glass. Ice is growing inward from the edge of a pool. By the time it closes, the water beneath it will have lifted the whole thin disk clear of the rock. There are thousands of them farther out, too dark to see, ticking gently against their rims.
