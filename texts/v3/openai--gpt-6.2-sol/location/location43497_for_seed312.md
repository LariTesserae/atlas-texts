The plateau is high enough to make its own weather and broad enough that the weather rarely crosses it in a day. A cloud may spend the whole afternoon over one district, trailing a little sleet, while two ridges away the sun warms the backs of people working on a roof. From the highest inhabited ledges there is no view down to the lowlands. There are more ledges, a pale reach of broken ground, and beyond that another line of mountains whose upper snow is visible long after their bases have gone into evening.

The rock still has corners. Where a slab has parted from a cliff, the two faces are so clean and similar that you can stand between them and see where a seam of white stone once ran straight through. In the gullies, fallen blocks have not yet worn themselves into gravel. They lie tipped against one another, with deep, dry spaces beneath. Soil gathers by the handful: behind a stone, in a hoofprint, under the lip of a step. A tussock in one of these pockets may be older than the house beside it. You can tell where it has crept outward over the years by the skirt of dead leaves it holds tight against the ground.

The city begins wherever a ledge is wide enough for two people to pass with loads. At first there might be only a fold in the cliff closed off with a wall. Then come houses with their rear rooms cut into the stone, and houses built against those houses, until the roofs are a second set of ledges. Some streets pass over three or four occupied rooms without any sign of them except for a little warmth at a smoke hole. In the older quarters a stair may serve as the front step to one dwelling and the back wall of another. People leave the outside edge clear for laden animals. If you meet a flock on one of those stairs, you wait in a doorway while the sheep come down, placing their feet with more care than you did.

Almost everything is built of stone that can be moved by four people and a rope. Larger pieces tend to be left where they fell. One serves as the back of a row of ovens; another divides a square, so that sellers on opposite sides can go all morning without seeing one another. The walls are fitted with hammerstones and wedges, their gaps stopped with clay and wool too short to spin. Good wood is brought up from below and used sparingly. The lintel above a busy doorway may be dark with the smoke of several houses that no longer stand. Ordinary doors are hide stretched on frames, stiff enough on a cold morning to rattle when lifted.

For all the trouble the cold makes, it is damp rather than snow that people watch for indoors. A white film forms on the underside of a roof and comes down by drops when the cooking starts. North-facing walls have blackened feet. Outside, a brief shower can darken a whole flight of stairs without wetting the dust beneath the overhang at the bottom. There are no streams to speak of on the inhabited shelves. Water travels along seams, showing itself as a bright inch of stone, then disappearing for the length of a house. Shallow cups have been cut wherever it reliably emerges. Some fill overnight; at others a child sets down a pot in the morning and comes back for it after the evening meal. You learn which is which. Nobody wastes a walk on the look of a wet wall.

There is more animal life than the poor grass seems able to account for. Most of it eats somewhere other than where you first see it. The sheep are fat from distant summer slopes; the little finches that line the eaves have come in from miles of seed-bearing scree. Pikas bring grass into the block fields one stem at a time. Hares flatten themselves in the shallow ground between tussocks and are hard to see even when there are six or seven on a slope. Beneath the lower roofs, where grain is stored, cats wait at holes polished by generations of mice. At dusk the cliff birds return. From across a ravine their numbers are apparent only when they bank and the pale undersides of their wings catch the light all at once.

It is a quiet city, though seldom a still one. Wool is teased on sunny steps. People carry clay in shallow baskets, holding them level as they turn sideways through the passages. A stoneworker's taps travel clearly along one alley and scarcely at all around its corner. On still days a person drawing a bucket three roofs below you can be heard to set it down, but a flock passing behind the wall at your elbow makes only a soft, protracted brushing sound. When the wind rises, work moves indoors rather than trying to speak over it. Most houses have a place near the entrance where you can sit in daylight with your feet out of the draft.

The city is dense wherever it has found purchase, but there is no continuous blanket of building. Whole reaches of the plateau stand empty between its districts. A paved stair may set out across bare country, descend into a notch, and rise again at a neighborhood a day's walk away. Each spring, people walk these long stairs to reset the treads lifted by frost. They bring wedges, food, and spare sandals. There are stretches where the same tread comes loose every year and stretches where the builders' chisel marks are still legible. The work proceeds a little farther some years than others. In a late thaw they may not reach the end at all.

Toward evening the stairs are among the last things you can make out across the empty ground. The rock and the walls are the same color, but the worn centers of the treads hold a little light. Seen from a roof, they give you the distance in a way the mountains do not: here a short pale run, there a gap where the path turns behind stone, then another run so far off that you have to wait for someone to move on it before you can be sure what you are seeing.
