There is no view from the top of the escarpment. There is a change in the sound of the rain. On the plateau it strikes leaves at every height, one shower becoming ten thousand smaller showers. Beyond the rim it falls through open air for so long that you stop hearing it before it reaches anything.

Here and there along the rim stand waist-high posts with brass rings. The rings are worn oval on the side facing inland. In the old days people tied themselves to them to clear the spillways. Now most of the spillways clear themselves. You can feel the clearing coming: a tremor through the soles, a warm breath out of a slot in the rock, then the deep, unhurried sound of a gate too heavy to shut quickly. For a while afterward the rain seems quiet. It hasn't changed at all.

The plateau is high enough to interrupt winds that have crossed half the world. Air comes up its flanks in wet folds and lies warm under the canopy. The rock is black basalt, though black is only a word carried over from places that have light. You know it here by the smooth shallow cups under your palm, by the grit where a root has got into it, by how long it keeps the heat of a passing flood. The rivers have occupied their beds for so long that even their floods have channels. A bank that seems improbably high will have freshwater shells set into it, all with their mouths pointing downstream.

Nothing shines. Not the insects, not the fungi, not the maintenance huts where the dynamos still turn. There is electricity enough to lift water a thousand feet through a buried main, but the huts announce it by a sour smell in the rain and a buzz in the fillings of your teeth. One hut may be half a day's walk from the next. Between them the industry is mostly underfoot: penstocks in old lava tubes, turbine halls behind waterfalls, lengths of ceramic pipe so thoroughly wrapped in roots that when one cracks it is hard to say which system has sprung a leak. At a drain you can put one hand in warm machine water and the other in cold seepage from the stone. Downstream of their meeting the snails grow large enough to knock together in the current.

Above all that, the forest is crowded. Ferns root in the wet felt on tree trunks; sedges grow in the forks; little trees grow on the bowed backs of larger ones and sometimes outlive them. The low leaves keep the touch of whatever passed last. Brush through after a herd and the foliage is warm at the height of your hip. The animals themselves are soft-footed, broad and low, with whiskers that trail along both sides of a path. They stop at junctions to listen, crowding one another until the last animal has caught up. In the crowns, things with dry wings make a noise like a deck of cards shuffled badly. The frogs call from pockets of water in the bark. When something climbs toward them, they go quiet in order, trunk by trunk, and start again below it.

There are nights when a river is heard in a channel it will not enter until morning. The people who tend the gates distinguish this from ordinary echo. An echo loses its small sounds first; the early river has them all, down to the tick of gravel turning in an eddy. Where it can be heard clearly, the keepers move their tools up a shelf and leave a plank across the ditch for the small animals. Sometimes the flood arrives without a sound of its own. By then the ditch is full of beetles riding the plank, and the keepers stand with bare feet in the water to judge when it has reached its height.

Far inland the escarpment ceases to matter. There are basins large enough to have winds that never reach the rim, and watersheds whose rain takes a season to get out. One can walk for weeks over ground that feels nearly level and still hear, through a well shaft, the water dropping farther than a stone could be followed. In some districts the machinery has been stopped for centuries. The forests there are not quieter. Hollow intake towers are occupied by nesting colonies; the old settling pools churn when the fish come up to breathe. Elsewhere a pump comes on so far away that the first sign is a shiver in a puddle, then another, then the slow tilt of the floating leaves toward a culvert.

Toward what the keepers call morning, the canopy begins to shed the night's rain. It does not do this all at once. Upwind, some small change in the air loosens the first drops, and the shedding passes from tree to tree until the whole warm country seems to be taking a step. At the escarpment, the last leaves tip their water over the edge. A long way below, after enough time to mistake the silence for an ending, the falls grow louder.
