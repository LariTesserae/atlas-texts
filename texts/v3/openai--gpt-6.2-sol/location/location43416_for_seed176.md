The marsh is no bigger than a churchyard, and it lies higher than any snow you can see.

From below, there is no sign of it. The mountain ends in two raw blades of stone with a dark seam between them. Climb through the seam and the ground gives under your boot. There is warm water to the ankle, and sedge so thick that the bent stems hold up the ones that have gone to seed. The far bank is a dozen strides away. Beyond it you can look down on the white backs of the peaks that hid the sky all morning.

The rock is young enough to cut the roots. Where a piece has broken off, the fracture is blue-black and faintly ribbed; a tuft of fern will grow on the broken piece before the exposed face loses its shine. Water comes out of the seam at several heights. The lowest seep smells of wet iron. The highest runs down the wall behind a curtain of little round leaves, which turn their dry sides to the sun even while their stalks are dripping. In ordinary afternoon light you can see all the way to the bottom of the pools. The silt there is pale and fine, except around the sedge roots, where it has baked into little rust-colored collars.

There is a pump house on the narrowest part of the rim. Its door is gone. A green enamel shade still hangs over the workbench, and under it someone has scratched a line in the wall to show how far the water once rose. The screws in the bench vise are greased. Through a slot in the floor, two pipes descend into the mountain and a third comes up warm enough to dry a sleeve laid over it. It takes a while to realize that the pump house, for all its quivering tin, is not where the noise comes from.

The noise comes through the soles of your feet. Somewhere under the marsh a big machine is running at a steady load. You can hear it even against the water spilling over the rim, which falls out of sight before it breaks into spray. At the lip, the overflow is scarcely wider than a hand. A blade of sedge caught there will bow and shiver for hours without being carried over. Stand back among the pools and the falling water sounds as if it ought to be emptying the whole place in minutes.

There are few animals. A single black fly works the sheltered side of the warm pipe. On some afternoons a bird comes up from below, almost level with the bank, and turns away without landing. The most reliable sign of life besides the plants is a little track through the bottom silt, visible in one of the shallow pools. It lengthens between visits. Whatever makes it works under the mat of roots, where there is room for hardly more than a finger.

The people who maintain the machinery have laid a plank from the cleft to the pump house. It has been moved uphill twice: you can see the old bolt holes, the lower pair under water and the upper pair just taking moss. The marsh is gaining on the path at about the rate the path wears out. On a still day the plank is warm, the leaves are wet, and you can sit there with your boots off and watch a drop gather at the tip of a fern. Below you a cloud crosses an entire valley. The drop is still there when the valley comes back into the light.
