The valley is high enough that most of its weather comes in level with the ground. Cloud slips over the western lip and travels along the floor in loose banks, wetting the stones without quite becoming rain. When it lifts, there is no corresponding brightening. The sky beyond it is the dull white of ice seen through ice.

A glacier still occupies the upper end. It has withdrawn from the rest so recently that the valley has hardly begun to collect its water into streams. Water spreads through the rubble in sheets a few millimetres deep, disappears beneath a fan of splintered rock, and reappears fifty paces away, welling up around stones that seem too heavy to tremble. At the lower end, a bedrock sill holds it back in a broad, shallow marsh. The pools have no banks to speak of. Their edges move with the wind.

Most of the exposed stone is sharp. Some slabs fit their neighbours so closely that you can follow a pale mineral seam across the gap where they parted. There are no rounded banks of soil yet, no deep channels. On the longest slope, meltwater is trying three routes down the same strip of gravel. Each afternoon one route takes almost all the flow; by the next morning a little ridge of needle ice has sent the water another way.

The largest piece of work goes on where the glacier meets the marsh. All day the ice delivers stone flour, fine enough to stay suspended long after the coarser grains have dropped. The pools do the sorting. Black sand collects in crescents behind the bigger stones; pale silt travels farther, settling in thin, level beds that a single windy afternoon can lift and lay down again. At night the shallows freeze from the edges inward. In the morning the new ice lies stacked along the shore in overlapping plates, each with its own thin load of grit. The valley is putting down a floor a fraction of an inch at a time.

There is remarkably little to see above that floor. A few mats of moss mark places where seepage lasts through the freeze. Sedge grows at the outlets, its blades short and dark, pressed nearly flat by the weather. Between those patches lie acres of wet stone with nothing taller than a loose flake propped against another. From the middle of the valley the far side looks near enough to walk to before the next shower. It takes half the morning.

The animals are easy to miss until the cloud moves. Then what looked like a low bar of gravel turns out to be sixty geese standing ankle-deep, all facing into the wind. They have come over the pass and are feeding along a few hundred yards of shore before going on. In the deeper pools, midge larvae hang from the underside of the skim ice. Small waders work the thawed margins, moving briskly between stretches of such complete stillness that you lose them against the stones. On the dry side of the sill a pika darts out to cut a stem, vanishes, and comes back for another. For most of the day almost everything alive in the valley is within a few steps of water.

It is mostly quiet. Close to the ground there is the faint ticking of grit carried under a slab. From the marsh comes the soft pull of a goose lifting its foot out of the mud. Once in a while the glacier makes a sound like a heavy door closing in another room. Nothing answers it, though a minute later a thin wave darkens the silt all around the nearest pool.

Toward evening the clouds descend far enough to hide the pass. The geese leave before it disappears, climbing in two ragged lines that come together only once they are above the valley. Below them, the small pools go dark one by one. The last to freeze is a narrow seep at the foot of the sill, where water keeps nosing through a crack and laying a clear skin over the stone. By morning it will have made a ledge. For a little while, that will be the shore.
