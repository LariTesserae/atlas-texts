The land is so low that a finger’s depth of water can change the color of half the horizon.

In the morning it lies in broad pewter sheets, broken by pale shelves of stone and long bars of rust-colored mud. There is no obvious shore. The water thins until the sky in it looks rubbed away, and then there is wet ground, firm enough to take a footprint but too fine-grained to keep one for long. Far out, a darker strip looks like higher country. It is a bed of sedges with water standing between the stems.

What rock is left lies almost flush with the plain. Rain has worked the softer seams down into runnels, none deep enough to hide a resting bird. The harder stone has the smooth, uneven surface of something worn for a very long time without ever being polished. There are no loose blocks at its feet. At noon the exposed shelves grow warm, and the little pools in their hollows grow warmer still. Minnows hold along the shaded side of a submerged lip, all facing into a current too slight to wrinkle the surface.

There is usually some rain within sight. You can watch it gray out the far flats while sunlight stays on the water at your feet. Here the showers arrive warm and strike hard. For five minutes there is nothing to hear but water hitting water, and afterward the frogs start up again, first from a few clumps of sedge and then from so far away that their calls merge into a single hoarse note. The plain can be loud for hours without there being much to see. A heron stands out in the open, its shadow shorter than its legs. Along a bare bank, small crabs go on lifting mud to their mouths.

Almost everything that grows here stays close to the ground. There are blue-green mats that darken when wet, tufts of wiry grass on the sandier rises, and an occasional low shrub with rain shining on the undersides of its leaves. The shrubs are far enough apart that each has its own patch of sky reflected around it. In the long gaps between them, insects hang over the water. A fish rises and the rings travel a surprisingly long way.

After a week of rain the shallow basins join. Fish pass over places where birds were feeding the day before. When the water falls, it leaves a crooked line of husks and shed wings on the stone. There are older lines above it, though scarcely higher, some bleached nearly white. Between one and the next is a strip of ground you could step across without noticing. For much of the year, that strip is the whole distance the water travels.
