From the pass you can see the whole valley, though you cannot see its floor. Pale green rises fill it from shoulder to shoulder, with long hollows between them where the light lies blue. The farther slopes have the same blunt profiles as the near ones. Whatever once wore the valley into this shape had a great deal of time to do it. The shape holds even when everything in it is moving.

There is rock under the pass. You can feel it through your heels in one or two places, but you would have to dig to find it. The gray surface that takes a boot print is a close crop of stems, each no wider than a pencil line, with last year's growth packed hard beneath. Farther down, roots overlap in plates the size of fields. Where the lip of a hollow has curled back, you can see their cross sections: white fibers, a thin wet seam, then the black, lustrous stuff made by centuries of compressed leaves. There is no dirt loose enough to blow away.

It is cold even in the sun. Moisture collects on the underside of things. Turn up a leaf and there may be six clear drops on it, all the same size, though the next leaf is dry. After a brief rain, water stands in cups at the joints of the taller plants. A cup tips when it gets too heavy, filling the one below; sometimes the whole hillside goes on tipping for hours after the cloud has passed. When the light is ordinary and bright, you can look straight down into a cup and see the little hairs at the bottom, bending where the water escapes.

Toward noon, a strip of the western slope puckers. Several acres of leaf turn edge-on at once. Behind them the stems bow, draw taut, and pull a lower mat uphill. Its leading edge disappears under the standing growth; its trailing edge comes up in soft white rolls. For a while the slope is all parallel motion. Then one roll takes a crease, the next three buckle against it, and the whole pattern breaks into smaller motions going every which way. The plants do this work slowly enough that you can watch a single stem draw tight, and fast enough that you can look up from that stem and find a different hillside.

On the coldest mornings the sun starts this process at the rim, a little at a time. By afternoon, with the whole valley warm enough to move, the work is on a scale that suggests a mill. There are banks of pale stems that rise and fall in succession; there are belts of leaf that seem to feed continuously into the dark beneath them. Nothing has been assembled. Each stem takes water into one side and lets it out of the other, and the pull passes down the slope. The folds are lined with felt. Even when a mat gives way across the width of a field, there is only a brief dry whisper, too far below the pass to be sure you heard it.

Once in a long while a small brown bird comes out of the leaves. It flies low across a hollow and lands on a ridge that is already sinking. It has time to put its head down twice before the foliage closes around it. There are no calls from the far side of the valley, and no buzzing near your face. If you stand still for a minute, the clearest sound is the fabric of your sleeve moving against your ribs as you breathe.

The best footing is on the rounded crests, where the roots have thickened in the same places for a very long time. The hollows are more deceptive. A dark lane may open at your feet and run for half a mile, with walls almost shoulder-high, and be gone by the time you have walked fifty paces. Sometimes an old seam opens and shows you a piece of winter: a length of cloudy ice with green tips pressed against it from below. The edges knit over the ice again before the day has warmed it.

By late afternoon a gray shower has crossed the far end of the valley. In its wake the leaves all show their slick undersides, and the long slopes, which looked soft enough to lie on in the morning, shine like something newly cut. Then the light comes back. The ridges are where they were. On the nearest one, a shallow print you left on your way up is full of water. As you watch, its edges rise, the water slips out, and the stems stand straight.
