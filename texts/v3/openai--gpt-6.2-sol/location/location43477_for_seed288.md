The city begins well below the last place the sun can reach directly. There is no gate. The passage widens, the ceiling lifts out of sight, and a dusty slope descends into a basin broad enough to lose its far side in the dark. Light comes down through a seam in the roof. It has already struck several walls by the time it reaches the basin, and what remains of it lies along the tops of the terraces. From the slope you can see pale horizontal lines, one above another, with a great deal of black between them.

What makes it a city is the way the ways through it fit together. Broad ramps carry traffic from the upper shelves to the floor. Narrow lanes feed into them from rows of shallow chambers. At the foot of each ramp is a bare oval where several routes meet and the travelers have room to pass. Nobody laid any of it out. The largest animals have been sleeping in the same banks of soft stone for so many generations that their hollows have met at the back and become corridors. The little ones keep the corridors clear by carrying a few grains out at a time. Their spoil has accumulated in low curbs along the lanes. Where two lanes join, the curb turns the corner with them.

The stone is so old that there is almost nothing left for age to do to it. The ledges have round noses. Former pillars stand waist-high in the floor, smooth as knuckles. Even the break where a whole quarter of the city slid down into the basin has worn to a long, easy incline. Animals cross it without changing their pace. On either side of that incline, the chamber mouths line up at different heights.

There is no pool at the low point, nor a line on the wall where a pool used to stand. The low point is full of flour-fine gypsum. Tracks keep their shape there until the next creature crosses them. The air is cool enough to stiffen the fingers and dry enough that a crushed leaf gives off dust before it gives off scent.

Yet in the few places where the reflected light falls all year, the growth is prodigious. Thickets of gray-green leaves overhang empty streets. Their stems are woody, packed close together, and the oldest have spread until they touch the undersides of the terraces above them. Seen from below, a thicket seems to have no soil at all. Its roots have entered a soft band in the limestone no thicker than a hand. Between that band and the next there may be a hundred paces of naked rock. The browsing animals know every descent between the patches. They leave long strips of the lowest foliage untouched where the footing is bad.

You seldom see more than a few animals at once. Then you sit still and discover how many you have been looking at. A ledge resolves into a row of folded wings. The pale grit at its foot begins to move uphill, grain by grain. Two blunt-headed grazers come out of separate doorways and take the ramp abreast. High overhead, where the ceiling ought to be, a colony shifts its weight, and a scattering of husks comes down through the faint light. There is room for all of them. A creature crossing the basin floor can be in plain view for a long time before it passes another.

The noise is tremendous. Air moves through a long fault under the western wall, and the wall gives out a rough, unbroken note. Farther in, the cries of the roosting animals come back from the roof with their edges rubbed off, so that even a pause in the calling goes on sounding. At a crowded junction you can put your palm to the stone and feel the low note in it, though you cannot hear the feet of an animal passing within reach. Down in the sleeping chambers the pitch drops. It is possible to go quite deep looking for a quiet room before you realize you have stopped expecting one.

Almost everything that changes here changes by a little. A lane rises as the chambers beside it deepen. One end of a thicket dies back; at the other end, a root finds the same seam a few inches farther along. For most of the year the narrow wash of light on the upper terraces touches only bare stone. Then, for a few days, it reaches the edge of the highest thicket. The leaves there are a paler green than the rest. By the time the light withdraws, the grazers have eaten them back to the stems.
