The pocket is no longer than a person lying down. There is no way into it large enough for a person, though a fine root enters at one end and a seam admits rain at the other. Between rains the chamber smells of cool clay. Its floor is dry enough to hold the jointed footprints of springtails, except along one margin, where the clay darkens to the color of a plum.

The light is bright enough to show the grain of every footprint. It does not come through the seam. Put a pebble beneath the seam and its shadow falls toward the ceiling; move it a little farther in and the shadow goes underneath it as it should. There is one place, near the root, where the shadow stays behind when the pebble is moved. The springtails avoid that place until the shadow wears out.

The roof is still forming. A thin lip of white mineral has grown around a drop of water, and when the drop grows heavy enough to fall, the lip keeps its shape. The next drop hangs a little lower. In this way a delicate, crooked tube is descending into the chamber. Across from it, a fold of clay rises from the floor. The two are nowhere near meeting, but on wet mornings a very small animal can be seen crossing from one to the other. Its feet make contact all the way across. There is nothing there that will take a footprint.

For most of the day the floor looks almost empty. The life is in the seams. Peel back one loose scale of clay and the space behind it is white with mites, their legs going at different speeds. A pale centipede works the damp margin, where it has learned to wait for springtails to land. The springtails sometimes jump into the following morning. The centipede waits there too. Once in a while one lands in the wet print it will leave on its next jump, and sits cleaning its antennae while the print dries around it.

There is very little green. A few leaves have opened on a seedling washed down from above, and their undersides are as green as their tops. The seedling keeps turning, slowly enough that it takes days to notice, unable to find a direction from which the light comes. Beside it, three grains of sand have been set upright in a line. A fourth lies flat across two of them. Under this little roof is a husk so translucent that the animal’s last meal, a thread of fungus, is still visible inside it. Whatever set the grains there has built nothing else.

Rain announces itself as a single click. After that the seam shines for a while. Water advances along the wall in separate beads, each drawing the clay darker around itself, and the mites come out to feed at the advancing edges. Sometimes the click comes after the wall has dried. Nothing in the pocket seems to mind the difference.

At the far end, beyond the reach of the root, a blister in the clay has lately begun to swell. The light is brightest there. Seen edge-on, the blister contains a narrow floor, a seam, and a drop about to fall. There is room in it for one mite. Several have gone in, and their tracks lead away from one another for a long distance.
