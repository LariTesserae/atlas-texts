At the bottom of the city, four streets end at the roof of a pumping station. You come down a short flight of steps to reach it. The roof is broad and almost level, paved in brick, with a low parapet on three sides. On the fourth, water from the street runs down the steps beside you. Nobody has bothered to give it a separate drain.

It is hot under the cloud cover. Rain has darkened the brick without cooling it, and the handrail is warm even where it drips. Across the roof, two exhaust stacks and a row of glazed inspection hatches leave a great deal of open space. In that space the rain gathers into shallow sheets, reflecting the blank undersides of the clouds. The only bright color comes from the planting beds along the parapet, where taro leaves lean out over the bricks. Each leaf can hold a surprising weight of water before it tips. After a shower, the beds go on spilling for half an hour.

The station is loudest at the steps. There the beat of the pumps comes up through the soles of your shoes, and a loose length of downpipe rattles against the wall. Farther out, the racket separates. There is the steady work of the motors below, the slap of water in a return channel, and, at long intervals, a bang from a flap gate out of sight. At the far parapet you can hear water draining through holes in a leaf. Nothing calls from the beds. No wings lift when a valve opens. An overflow pipe has been dripping into the same round basin for years, and the green film on it is whole except where the drops fall.

From the parapet you can look down into the works. The street walls continue below roof level, faced in the same brown brick, with old doorways bricked up to the height of the high-water line. Ferns grow from the mortar above it. Below it the corners have gone smooth, and the iron ladder has worn shallow sockets into the wall where its feet touch. Now and then a pump takes hold and the water draws away from the ladder, exposing another rung, then another, each with a mat of green laid flat along its top.

The depth gauges are ordinary enamel boards, installed in pairs. One hangs in the intake well and the other on the roof above it, beside a hatch that the maintenance crew keeps padlocked. The upper gauge has never touched the water. Nevertheless, when the level falls in the well, a wet line descends its face. The crew read whichever board is easier to get to. In heavy rain they use the lower one.

Late in the afternoon the motors change over. For a few seconds the roof stops trembling, and water that has been quivering in the hollows goes clear enough to show the sand between the bricks. Then the second motor starts. A gate opens beneath the far street; the channel rises, and the floating leaves turn and travel back the way they came. Up on the roof, where it hasn't rained for some time, water begins to come down the steps again.
