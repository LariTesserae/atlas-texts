The plateau is high enough to catch rain that never falls. Cloud touches the grass and leaves water on the uphill side of every blade. The taller plants comb it out of the air in long beads, which run down their stems and disappear into the ground. By noon the sky may be perfectly clear. If you kneel, the knees of your trousers will still come away black.

There is very little bare stone, although the whole country is stone close underfoot. Roots occupy the old joints in the rock; moss occupies the roots; tiny red-leaved plants grow in the moss with their stems laid flat, each leaf fitting into a gap between the others. Where a slab has split and tipped, its sheltered underside is furred with pale growth. A beetle can live its whole life on that underside and never cross a patch that has nothing growing on it.

From a ridge the plateau looks almost level. The next ridge seems near enough to reach before the weather changes, but between the two is a trough broad enough to hold a morning fog long after the high ground has warmed. Farther out are other troughs, each with its own clouds and each draining into a river that leaves the plateau by a different rim. It can be raining for a week in one of those drainages while the animals in the next are licking dew from stones. On a fine afternoon the light is ordinary and generous: you can see the gloss on a broad leaf at your feet and the white underside of a cloud a hundred miles away.

Most of the water travels out of sight. It follows beds of softer rock through the ridges and collects in chambers worn smooth over a very long time. In some of these, the rise of water compresses a pocket of air until it forces a passage through a flooded seam. The seam empties; the chamber fills again. Behind a particularly prolific bank of ferns, this happens every few minutes throughout the wet season. The fronds all lift a little, showing their undersides, and settle. Nothing nearby seems to notice.

The effect, across the plateau, is almost industrial. One bed of rock strains ochre out of the water; another carries it away in suspension; a mile below, an entire marsh lays it down in thin orange coats. There are colonies of black reeds that feed on the coats as fast as they form. At the edges of those colonies, the water comes out clear enough to show the smallest bones in the silt. There is no intake someone has cut, no wall holding a pond in place. Where a process is interrupted by a fallen slab, it resumes, sometimes decades later, around the ends.

Animals are everywhere, though at first you might count only the birds. Small grazing things with dark, slick backs feed under the taller grass. In the cool middle of the day they come onto the open shelves and stand in the sun, their wet coats taking on separate points of light. Hunting birds pass low over them on nearly motionless wings. Along the pools, little fish ease into the submerged grass when a shadow crosses; after it has gone they ease out again, the smallest first. Under loose flakes of stone there are cream-colored snails, often three or four to a flake, with the youngest tucked into the hollows left by the oldest.

For all that life, there is little to hear. Water running under turf makes a sound only where you put a foot down hard enough to squeeze it out. Grass bends against grass without a dry edge to rasp. Once in a while a bird calls from a hollow and another answers from surprisingly close by. Then there may be nothing for a long time but the faint ticking of drops falling from the lip of a rock into leaves already wet.

Toward evening the cloud starts to gather in the troughs. It covers a pool first, then the grazing shelves above it, then the lower half of the opposite slope. The animals don't climb ahead of it. For a while you can watch a dark back moving along the cloud's upper edge, disappearing as the animal puts its head down to feed. On the ridge where you stand, there is still enough sun to warm one side of a stone. Water beads on the other.
