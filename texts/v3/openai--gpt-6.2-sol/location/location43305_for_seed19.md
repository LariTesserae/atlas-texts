The plateau is high enough that you can see the tops of mountains a long way off and none of their sides. The sides of this mountain are missing from view too. At the rim, the ground goes straight from a pale, scuffed stone to blue distance. The light is ordinary daylight. It picks out a bruise on a fingernail as readily as it picks out a ridge sixty miles away.

Nothing here has been wet. The white in the hollows is not salt left by an evaporated sea, and the branching marks in the slabs were never made by streams. Break a slab and the branches continue through it, every twig pointing down. Once, perhaps, the whole mountain was soft enough to take an impression. It has had time to wear away almost everything that could tell you what pressed on it.

There are plants, or at least things that grow. A few black pads grip the stone where two surfaces meet. They are no thicker than a thumbprint. The larger ones have a fringe of little glassy cups, all empty. Kneel to examine one and you feel the cold of the plateau through both knees, even where only one touches the ground.

The animals are harder to miss. They stand so close together that from a distance their backs read as a second terrain, gently sloping where the stone beneath them is level. At first they look like lean, dun-colored cattle. Closer in, there are some with the pleated throats of lizards and some with long, bare forelegs folded up like carpenter’s rules. Small animals stand between the toes of larger ones. One has laid its chin across the lid of another’s open eye. None seems crowded. There is room to walk wherever you decide to put your foot, though you can never see the room in advance.

The whole herd makes less noise than a mouse in a wall. You hear a dry click now and then. If you wait for the next one, it comes from behind you; if you turn, it seems to have come from a long way down inside the stone.

Toward the middle of the plateau there is a low house with a door of stitched hide. The stitches were made with a bone awl, which lies on the sill beside a pebble worn flat on one face. The wall has been rubbed white at shoulder height. Inside, a shelf holds six shallow bowls. From the doorway you can see what each contains: a crooked tooth, a coil of hair, a red chip of stone, three black seeds, a smaller bowl, and the room you are standing in. The last bowl is chipped in two places. Through the larger chip you can see the soles of your boots.

Outside, a line of upright stones runs toward the rim. Their tops have worn down almost to the ground, but the marks cut into them are sharp. Each bears a sketch of a particular animal nearby, with its particular torn ear or bent leg. The stones are set much too close for graves. At the last one, the little drawing has no animal beside it. It shows a long head with its mouth held slightly open. Looking over the edge, you see that same opening, miles across, in the blue below. It takes a while to make out the head around it. Farther out, what looked like the tops of mountains begin to suggest other parts of the body.

You can spend the afternoon here, by your watch. The house will keep its short shadow, and the animal with its chin across the other’s eye will not lift it. Your knees will warm when you stand, and the dust lodged in your bootlaces will work its way out as you walk. If you come back to the house, the dust will be in your bootlaces again. There will be a clean place on the sill where you thought about picking up the awl. The awl itself will be there.
