The room is at the top of a stairway with nine hundred steps. It is also under a mile of water. The steps below it have worn into a long slope, but the last three are still distinct. Small white animals stand on their edges with their front legs hanging over, as if waiting for something to come up.

Nothing here is bedrock. Even the stairway was assembled out of slabs, and the ridge beneath it is a stack of baskets filled with fired clay. The baskets themselves are gone. You can see the weave in the clay where a corner has broken away. Whoever built the ridge had stone hammers and cord and a way of keeping a kiln hot for days. They made a great height out of things one person could carry.

The room at the top is no wider than a dozen paces. Its roof is held up by short pillars set so close together that there is seldom a clear view across it. A traveler feeling along one pillar will find a shallow groove, then the same groove on the next. They were molds for making cord the same thickness, or rests for thumbs, or merely places where the masons liked to put their hands. The water has had enough time to make all three explanations plausible.

It is warmest at the floor. A little warmth comes through the bricks, and the animals arrange themselves in layers above them. Flat ones lie against the joints. Jointed ones stand over those, their legs planted in the few bare places. Higher up, pale soft bodies hang between the pillars without quite touching them. When one descends, several others make room, each moving only as far as it has to. There is no green growth, not so much as a stain of it. What look like branching white stems along the ceiling are the jointed legs of animals sitting upside down.

For most of the time there is nothing to see. Now and then, one of the animals puts out a blue point of light. Its neighbors' legs interrupt the light, and by those interruptions you can tell how crowded the room is. Farther away, an animal may answer. The light arrives too late to illuminate the first one.

There is almost no sound. A shell shifted against a brick makes a small, dry click, improbable in so much water. Long afterward, there may be a second click. No gravel rolls down from above; no wash comes up the stair. The whole place changes most in the warm seam by the door, where a generation of blunt-mouthed animals scrapes a little from the mortar. What they let fall settles one step lower. In time, the last three steps will be a slope too.

The doorway once had a sill high enough that a person would have had to lift a foot over it. You can find the sill now by its warmth and by the way the animals leave a narrow lane above it, though nothing too large to pass between them has come through in a very long time. Beyond it the water falls away on both sides of the stair. A few of the hanging bodies drift out there, down to the level of the first step, and then rise again. They spend nearly all their lives within reach of the building. For a little while they hang where there is nothing to touch.
