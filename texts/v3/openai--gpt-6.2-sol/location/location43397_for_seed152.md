Behind the last house in the lane, two brick walls come almost together. From the lane the gap looks like a seam in the masonry. A little farther in, it opens around a patch of ground no wider than a kitchen table. The walls are the backs of houses on different streets. Their roof tiles lean toward one another without quite meeting, and through the opening you can see a pale strip of sky, wider at one end where a tile is missing.

The ground is new. A wall once crossed the gap at an angle, and its lowest course still lies half buried there. On one side is the hard yellow clay the builders cut into, with a square corner so clean that a fallen seed rests on it without rolling. On the other is a soft black mixture of brick crumbs, dead leaves, and earth brought in by ants. The two surfaces haven’t yet worn to the same level. After rain, the wet clay takes the print of a snail’s shell where the snail rests against it. There is no channel for the water to follow; each shower has to find its way across the ground afresh.

The town is low here, scarcely higher than the river in summer, though you can’t see the river from the gap. Its warmth collects in the brick. In the morning, beads of water stand on the mortar beneath the eaves. By midday the upper walls are dry, but the lower bricks stay dark enough that you can count the courses by color. A fern grows out of a joint at knee height. It has all the room it needs and sends its fronds nearly to the opposite wall. Below it a single broad-leaved dock has raised a low roof of its own. Between those two plants there’s a good deal of bare ground.

Much of what lives here is visible only when it crosses that open space. Ants come down the wall, disappear under the dock, and come out carrying pale scraps. A little brown spider waits at the edge of the old foundation. Woodlice spend the day fitted into the underside of a loose brick, their backs pressed together; toward evening they come out one at a time. After a shower, gnats gather in the dim strip of air below the missing tile. They rise and settle there for an hour without touching either wall.

A voice from the lane reaches the entrance clearly and then is gone. Inside, a drop can hang from a fern tip long enough to seem permanent. When it falls, it lands on a lower frond, which swings once and is still. Most of the changes are that small. A dock root eases a crumb of mortar out of its bed. A slug goes over the clean corner of the clay in the night. By morning there is a little soil where there wasn’t any before, and the seed, which has been lying dry since it fell, is damp on one side.
