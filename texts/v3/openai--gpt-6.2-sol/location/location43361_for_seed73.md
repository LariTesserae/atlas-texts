At the top of the pass, nearly seven thousand metres above the sea, the first thing you hear is frogs.

They live in the water that stands ankle-deep across the saddle, among sedges with stems as thick as fingers. The water is warm enough to take the chill out of a stone carried up from the last slope. There is no chill anywhere on this side of the pass. Heat rises from the wet ground, collects under the broad leaves, and comes back down in the drops that fall from them. Even in the open, where the wind has a clear run of twelve kilometres, it feels like the air over a pot just taken off the fire.

The valley is wide and, for most of its length, almost level. From the pass you can see the dark backs of feeding animals out on the reed flats and, beyond them, the pale line where the whole country ends. Clouds go by well below that line. A few drift up the outer cliffs, thinning as they climb, and briefly show through the gaps between the roots of trees growing on the rim. Far below, when the cloud opens, there are dry brown mountains with snow on their north faces.

Up here the rain has had a very long time to work. It has rounded the ledges and hollowed shallow cups in the dark rock. Some of the cups hold nothing but clear water and a single red larva; others have filled with black soil and grown little islands of fern. The oldest trees sit so low in their own banks of humus that their branches appear to come straight out of the earth. They shed strips of bark into the pools, where snails rasp away the soft underside and leave each strip floating with its grain picked out in white.

A fall at the far end of the valley makes most of the noise. You can hear it from the pass, though you can’t see it until you’ve gone halfway down. Closer at hand there are frogs, the hollow knocks of birds cracking snails on a stone, and the tearing sound the grazing animals make when they pull up a mouthful of sedge. The grazers are narrow-legged and broad through the ribs. Small yellow birds walk along their backs, stepping aside when a shoulder blade rises under the hide. At noon the herd goes into the pools and stands with only its nostrils and the tops of its ears showing. The birds stay aboard.

There are three buildings. Two are roofless, and have been for long enough that their walls hold the trunks of living trees. The third stands in the shallows on a floor no thicker than a plate. You can put your face to the water and see daylight under it. Inside, a recess in the wall contains a clean rectangle with four round marks at the corners, where something was taken away. Mud daubers have made use of the recess. There is nothing else to see.

The thing still working is out by the falls. It looks like a seam in the rock until a leaf goes over the edge. The water drops perhaps eighty metres, breaks into spray, and falls back into the valley from a shelf upstream. The leaf comes with it. From one bank you can follow the whole circuit in ordinary daylight, even see the leaf tuck under and spin where the returning water strikes. There are pebbles in the flow that have been going round long enough to wear down to beads. In a drought the fall gets thinner. It has never, so far as the old waterlines show, stopped.

Only at the rim does the heat give way. A hand held out over the drop feels cold on the fingertips while the wrist is still sweating. Seeds collect there in a ragged line, and almost none of them take on the far side. In places a root as thick as a thigh has crept up to the line and turned to follow it. You can walk beside one of those roots for an hour, with the fern canopy dripping on your left and five thousand metres of open air on your right. Every so often a frog calls from underneath it, and another answers much farther along.
