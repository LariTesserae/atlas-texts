At noon, seven thousand metres above the sea, the black stones are too hot to pick up. A little rain fell before sunrise. You can still find it by turning over a stone: the underside is cool and wet, and a dozen pale animals tuck their legs in at the sudden light.

The plateau is broad enough that from its middle you cannot tell which way the land falls. There are low rises, fields of fitted stones, and long shallow basins whose far shores look close in the clear air. Only at the eastern edge does the height become apparent. The plateau ends in a straight drop past bands of ochre and violet rock, and below those bands a river makes a small white turn. In the afternoon you can watch a thunderstorm cross the valley well below your feet. Up here your shirt dries while you watch.

The stones have been in place a long time. Their tops are varnished dark; their buried edges are rounded and white. In some fields they fit so closely that rain runs over them as it would over a tiled roof, though there is nothing underneath but older stones and a little red dust. Water collects in thumbprints in the softer rock. By midmorning the smallest hollows are dry, each with a clean ring of salt. The deepest hold water for three days, and their inhabitants make good use of all three.

There are few plants. A grey cushion no bigger than a hand might be the only green thing for fifty paces. But on the damp side of that cushion are mites, springtails, and small amber beetles feeding on a film that doesn't look like food until you see the tracks through it. Lift your eyes and the same traffic is going on across the whole basin. Black lizards wait with their bellies clear of the hot stone. Little dun birds walk the seams in the pavement, stopping now and then to knock a beetle against a rock. At the brink, swifts pour out of cracks that looked empty a moment before. They spend most of the day below the level of the plateau, where the insects rise on warm air from the valley.

The only building still in use is a low pump house with a sheet-metal shade over its door. It draws brine from a warm seam in the rock and sends it through iron pipes to three settling pans. From a distance the pans are easy to mistake for more stone fields; only when a valve opens does one of them take on a shine. The machinery runs for a few minutes at a time. Standing by the door you hear a belt slap and the thin rattle of a loose grille. A hundred paces away you know the pump has started because a line of birds lifts off the pipe.

There is work for one person here. In the late afternoon the keeper walks out with a scraper and clears the pale crust from the sluices. Birds follow at a distance. Beside the last pan stands a roofless room built from the same dark stones as the pavement. The keeper leaves a cup on its shaded wall. Even on days when no rain falls, there is usually enough water in it by dawn to rinse the salt off a pair of spectacles.

Toward evening the plateau cools unevenly. The bare rock is still giving back heat when the shallow basins fill with cold air. Their edges become busy: beetles come out from underneath the stones, lizards shift to the last warm slabs, and the birds work along the boundary until there is too little light to see what they are taking. For a while, crossing a basin, you can feel cold at your ankles and heat through the soles of your boots. Then the wind moves a little, and the two temperatures mix. Somewhere out on the pavement a stone clicks as it cools. From farther away comes another click, faint enough that you stop walking to hear it.
