The high country is under water. On a clear day there are forty fathoms of cold green above its passes, and from the lip of the plateau the bottom falls away so far that a stone dropped there is lost to sight before it has gone a tenth of the way down. The plateau itself is nearly level. A ridge that looks imposing from across the valley may turn out, on approach, to stand no higher than a shoulder.

Every inch of it was laid by hand. Even out on the flats, where there seems to be nothing but gray silt, a passing current will uncover the close-set ends of paving stones. The silt is their worn substance. There is no bedrock here, no outcrop incorporated into a wall to save labor. At the edge of the high country, where the whole thickness is exposed, one sees courses laid upon courses down into the blue. The lowest visible blocks are no rougher than the highest. There is nowhere to point and say: this much at least was here before.

A few things stand clear of the pavement: a stair broad enough for a hundred people to climb abreast, three piers with nothing on them, a long wall pierced at wide intervals by doors. Most of the stairs have worn into slopes. The doors are recognizable by the way the water goes through them. On the upstream side, suspended grit gathers into thin white sheets, one to each opening. From a distance they look like curtains being drawn through the wall.

The current seldom keeps its direction for long. Water from the flats pours over the edge, meets a colder flow rising out of the basin, and folds back across the plateau. At the junction a pebble may travel steadily uphill for a minute, stop, and drop into the same socket it came from. Whole acres disappear behind moving banks of powdered stone. A little later the water clears, and a low flight of steps appears where there had seemed to be only a plain. Nothing has risen. The current has swept the steps clean.

There is no weed to show how hard the water is running. Nothing green grows in the joints. But the plateau is thick with animals, nearly all of them the color of what they rest on. Flat silver fish stand on edge in the lee of a sill; seen head-on, a hundred of them take up less room than the shadow of the sill. Pale crabs hang beneath the old treads. When the current turns, they come over the rounded edges all at once and walk the other way, each testing for shelter with a single outstretched leg. Farther down the slope are colonies of small, clear-bodied drifters. They gather in the slow center of a whirl, feeding there until it breaks up, then scatter so widely that it seems impossible there were ever so many in one place.

For all that motion, it is mostly quiet. There is a dry little ticking when grit strikes a doorpost. Now and again a block shifts somewhere underneath, and the sound comes through the soles of the feet before it reaches the ear. Storms pass overhead as broad alternations of light and dark. The water can be bright enough to show the pale undersides of the fish while the pavement beneath them is already in night.

Along the western edge runs a shallow groove polished smoother than the rest. In several places there are two grooves side by side, and at one of the doorways they divide around a worn socket. It may once have been a threshold with a post in the middle. There are no hinges left, nothing to show what swung there or whether there was ever a door. When the flow slackens, the fish settle into the grooves, facing whichever way the water last came. Across the valley, the far pavement shows for a moment through the settling silt, as level and empty as a floor in a room nobody has entered yet. Then the water comes back through the openings, and every fish turns.
