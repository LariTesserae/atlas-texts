Behind a thumb-wide seam in the tower’s rain collector there is a warm ravine no longer than a hand. It lies above the cloud deck, though nothing that lives in it can see the sky. Its far wall is the back of a number on a service plate. Its near wall is a stack of black ceramic ribs, fitted so closely that a drop of water can touch six of them at once.

Nothing here was quarried. The gravel was printed round, the ledges were folded out of sheet, and the pale grit underfoot is what remains of a bearing that wore away centuries ago. The ravine was meant to be a straight drain. Now it has undercuts, shallows, and a plunge pool with the shape of an ear. Repairs have preserved the mouth and the outlet; between them, water has been left to decide.

It is deafening. The service plate hums at a pitch low enough to feel in the hinge of the jaw, and the tower’s pumps send a continuous hard chatter through the ceramic. The little falls would be inaudible on their own. Every so often a bubble lodges under the plate and changes the note. For the few seconds it takes the bubble to work loose, the ravine sounds like a much larger chamber with a door open at the other end.

Water gathers on the warm walls without cooling them. The collector was built to draw moisture out of air even when there was no colder surface to give it to, and its oldest panels still do so faithfully. Droplets swell in the same polished sockets, run together, and spill down the ribs. There is just enough light from an amber inspection strip to see this happen. Where it passes through a bead of water, a whole wall lights up; once the bead falls, all that remains is a dull brown line.

There are two little patches of green, both on the inspection strip. Everywhere else the life in the ravine moves. Pale springtails stipple the water so thickly that a falling drop opens a black hole among them. Midges idle in the lee of the ribs, close enough to brush wings. In the pool, blunt translucent larvae feed on the brown film that grows over the old bearing grit. A slender red larva hunts them from a lip in the ceramic. It waits there through three or four full spills, holding fast while the pool empties and fills, until something small comes by sideways.

At intervals the tower tests the collector. The walls flex almost imperceptibly, the running water gathers into a single trembling sheet, and for a moment the floor is bare. The animals in the pool go with the sheet; those in the cracks stay where they are. When the walls relax, water wells back through the outlet first, carrying grit and half a dozen bewildered larvae uphill into the pool. The springtails spread out again. From the service side there is nothing to see but a seam that needs wiping, already wet at one end.
