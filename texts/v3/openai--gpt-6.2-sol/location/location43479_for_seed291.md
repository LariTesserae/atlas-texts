The city is being built on a plain that is still soft at the edges. In the morning, beyond the last tram shelter, you can see survey flags standing in a gray, faintly steaming surface. By noon the flags have tipped and the surface has hardened enough for a fox to cross. It leaves no prints except where it pauses.

There is no river, although the city has bridges. Under them run belts carrying brick, furnace ash, coils of wire, and the occasional entire tree with its roots wrapped in sacking. Water is less conspicuous. It beads on the underside of the belts and falls at long intervals into the loading yards. There are puddles beneath the street clocks, none beneath the gutters. The cold is ordinary and persistent: the sort that gets into the bolt of a door and makes you think the door is locked.

From the top deck of a tram you can see almost the whole district. Nothing rises very far. Factories, housing courts, and freight sheds repeat out to the pale limit of sight, interrupted by broad raw lots whose distances are hard to judge. The view is open in a way the streets are not. At ground level every handspan has been claimed by a cable, a bracket, a nest, a notice, a length of pipe with a smaller pipe strapped to it. Ferns grow in the corrugations of loading doors. The warm side of each transformer cage is green with little round leaves. In the unheated courts, where laundry freezes on the line, currant bushes have worked their way through the slats of the benches. People pick the fruit without sitting down.

Most of the machinery is quieter than the animals. The stamping works make a soft, regular displacement in the air, felt chiefly in the loose hem of a coat. You can stand beside a wall behind which six hundred window frames are being cut and hear a starling turning a snail over on the coping. At dusk the starlings go down into the ventilators in such numbers that the factory roofs seem to drain.

The streets change quickly, but not all at once. You can finish a cup of tea while the building across from you is lifted off its foundation. Where it stood, a crew will be setting curbstones against a seam of black grit that wasn't there yesterday. At the next corner, a stairway descends to a cellar that has not yet formed. The bottom step meets a soft, cold material like the inside of an unbaked loaf. Someone has put up a rail. Someone else, anticipating a landing, has already left a bicycle there.

Children know that the new ground is strongest where plants have taken hold, though they disagree about whether the roots bind it or the ground sets because the roots expect it to. The works department has tried planting grass in the path of a proposed avenue. The avenue wandered three meters north of its stakes, taking the grass with it in a neat strip. Now the department waits for the weeds. In spring you can tell where a road is likely to go by the line of coltsfoot, its yellow heads all at the same low height, right through a jumble of pallet stacks and parked engines.

There is daylight enough to see these things plainly. No colored haze, no permanent dusk. On a clear afternoon the walls are buff and red, and the fresh aggregate shines like sugar where a truck has backed over it. You can look along an avenue and see, half a mile away, a woman holding a sheet of paper down on a zinc counter while the counter is wheeled out of a shop. The shop is closing, or moving, or becoming the entrance to something else. Whatever she's writing, she finishes it before she moves her hand.
