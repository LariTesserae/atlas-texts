The room is nine paces long if you take the outside route, eleven if you go between the machines. They were put in before the ceiling went on. You can follow the joint where the last slab was lowered; it crosses the room at a slant, ignoring the walls and everything the room has been used for since.

There is no exposed earth. The floor is tile, the walls are glazed brick, and even the low place where the water goes was made with a mould. It has the shape of a shallow spoon. For most of the week it holds a skin of brown water no deeper than the thickness of a fingernail. When rain reaches the building overhead, nothing happens here for hours. Then a drop falls from the head of a bolt. Another falls from the same bolt about a minute later. By morning the spoon is full. Nobody has found a wet seam above it.

The heat comes chiefly from three iron housings ranged along the longer wall. Two are still in use. The third has been empty for years, but it takes on the warmth of the others and gives it back so slowly that it is often the warmest thing in the room when the current is off. There is hardly room to put a hand between the housings. Behind them, pipes have accumulated in successive orderly rows: heavy ones on brackets, little ones hooked over the heavy ones, and a single copper line laid in the trough of a pipe that was split open to see what had stopped it. What had stopped it was a wad of linen. The linen is still there, hard and black, with one corner lifting whenever the current comes on.

In darkness the room seems larger from the doorway than it does from inside. There are too many things at shoulder height. You learn to pass the valve with your elbow down and to step over the low return pipe before your shin finds it. A pilot light at the back of one housing shines into its own metal hood. From the right place on the floor it shows a red dash no wider than a grain of rice. If the spoon is full, there are two dashes.

The oldest wear is on the tile beside the door, where people turn their feet to pull it shut. The glaze there has gone soft-looking. Elsewhere the wear has less to do with people. Condensation has eaten a little scallop out of each screw below the cold-water coupling. A bracket that has been under load for sixty years has bowed until it rests neatly on the bracket beneath it. On the big shutoff wheel, all but two of the spokes are rough with rust. The other two are smooth on the undersides, where hands can get a purchase without having to see.

There is a small green growth in the drain collar. It keeps to the side that receives light while the door is being held open for deliveries. The deliveries don’t take long, and the green ends well short of the far side. Once in a while a pale insect comes out of the collar and stands on the dry tile. It is about as long as the dash of light. It can stand there through a whole shift. The only other animals anyone sees are the thin spiders behind the terminal boxes; when a box is opened, they go farther in.

The machinery makes very little noise of its own. The faint ticking that starts after shutdown comes from a row of tin labels, each hung on a loop of wire. As the pipes cool they draw the wires tight, one after another. The last label hangs over the empty housing. Sometimes it doesn’t tick until the next shift, when someone has already come down and put a hand on the latch. By then the water in the spoon has shrunk away from the rim, leaving one more fine line inside it, slightly lower on the side where the floor has settled.
