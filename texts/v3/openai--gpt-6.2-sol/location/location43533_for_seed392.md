The plain is no higher than a doorstep above its water. You can see nearly to the far edge, where the white paving rises one course and the glare hides whatever lies beyond. Between here and there stand eleven gardens, each on a broad, low plinth. They are far enough apart that the shade of one never touches the shade of another.

The paving is shell lime rammed into reed molds. In places you can still see the knots in the reeds, and the corners are sharp enough to skin a bare heel. Nothing looks newly washed, though everything is wet. A bead hangs from the lip of every tile. The shallow gutters are full to a line one finger below their rims. Kneel and put your cheek to the pavement and you can feel the heat stored in it, the way you might feel it in the side of a cooking pot after the fire has been taken away.

Someone made even the ground beneath the ground. At the inspection pits you can count the layers: lime, split cane, black clay, inverted jars with their mouths bedded in pitch. There is no earth at the bottom of a pit, only another floor, neatly swept. The gardens grow in chipped crockery packed with leaf mold. Their breadfruit trees are lush enough to roof a house, and their vines have crossed the retaining walls and hung down in thick green skirts. In the open spaces between them, there is not so much as a weed in a seam.

There are animals enough to make the emptiness surprising. Under each plinth, terrapins lie nose to tail in the shade. Tiny fish hold over the red clay plugs in the gutters, all facing the same way, though a dropped leaf shows no current. Lizards occupy the grout lines. White birds stand among the breadfruit leaves, so numerous that what looks from a distance like a branch in flower turns out to have eyes. One opens its bill. The only sound is the dry click of its closing.

At the fourth garden you can borrow a dipper. It is a gourd with a cane handle, laid on a shelf at the height of a child’s shoulder. The water you take from the gutter leaves a hollow the shape of the gourd. A fish swims through the space where the water was, turns, and comes back. Only when you pour the dipper out does the hollow fill. You can pour it out anywhere. Even on the hot tiles, the water is gone before it strikes them.

The place is not still in the sense of being held fast. A terrapin will walk the length of your shadow. A bird will shift its feet to let you pass underneath. But stop anywhere and let your eyes settle, and there will be the same number of white throats among the leaves, the same number of dark shells under the plinth. The bead on the lip of the tile is always about to fall. You can wipe it away and count the seconds until you find yourself looking at it again. There is no moment when it returns.

Out near the middle stands a low wall made of fired bricks. Each brick bears a thumbprint, and no two prints are alike. In the hard light you can see the little ridges and the places where wet clay clung to the skin as the thumb lifted. On the sheltered side, an unfinished brick rests on a board. Its clay is soft. Beside it lies a lump of the same clay wrapped in a wet cloth, and beside that a cup for wetting your hands. Of all the water on the plain, this is the only cup with dust in it.
