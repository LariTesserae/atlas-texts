The hollow is high enough that clouds sometimes have a bottom you can look down on. It is no bigger than a grove, though there are only four trees in it. Three stand along the rim and one grows out of a split in the floor, its red roots visible through black water. From the lip you can see almost every part of the place. What you cannot see is what the water is standing on.

The rock looks newly broken. One wall is a single clean face of gray stone, with little white crystals still sharp enough to snag a sleeve. At its foot lie the pieces that came off it, each with a corner you could fit back into the wall. Ferns grow waist-deep in the joints between the pieces. Their undersides gleam with cold beads; if you brush through them, your trousers get wet to the knee. Out on the open slabs there is nothing but a wash of reflected sky. The green is rich and local. There is room between the clumps to see how little soil the hollow has.

It sounds like a factory heard from the other side of a hill. A deep, loaded turning goes on under the water. Over it comes a brisk series of impacts, followed by the hiss of pressure let off. The hiss is loud enough to interrupt a conversation. There is no iron in sight except for an orange stain in the rock, no opening from which the noise issues. Put a hand to the clean wall and you feel the turning slow under load, then pick up as something takes hold. Take your hand away and the wall is quite still. The creatures pay no attention to any of this except at the end of the hiss, when the little wrens all cock their heads.

There are a great many creatures for a place so nearly empty. Sedge moves along the pool in separate patches as the water voles go through it. Pale moths hang under the fern fronds, three or four to a frond, and on a flat stone the size of a palm there may be a dozen newts, their toes laid over one another. In the open, you might see only one animal at a time. Wait by the pool and a shrew comes out, crosses the gray slab, and disappears into a place where there is visibly no crack. A moment later you hear it rustling in the fern behind you.

Nothing here is held motionless. A wren picks a moth from a frond; you can see the powder on its bill. The frond springs up, and there is still a moth under it. The wren swallows. The moth has the same crooked edge to its left wing. It isn't always the wren that gets it: sometimes it makes it as far as the water, where a newt takes it, and sometimes it flies straight up past the rim. The hollow loses none of its moths by any of these means. The voles tug at the sedge without thinning it. Their wet tracks go dark on the slabs, in among tracks that have not dried since you arrived, yet the stone never grows any darker overall.

At the upper end of the pool, water seeps out of a seam and drops from a pointed shelf. You can hold your palm in the fall. The water is cold enough to make the joints of your fingers ache, and it runs freely down your wrist. From the far bank, though, you can see the space between the shelf and the pool, and the space is empty. The pool dimples all the same. In each dimple there is a little brightness, although the sky above is the color of unpolished lead.

After a while the turning underfoot becomes familiar enough that you hear the smaller things against it: a newt easing its belly off a stone, a wren wiping its bill on bark, the fine tick of water loading the tip of a fern. That last drop gets surprisingly large. The fern bends until the drop touches the slab below, and the water spreads into a thin bright film. Then the fern lifts, with another heavy drop already hanging from it. Beneath the film the crystal points are as keen as the day the wall broke, if there was a day when it broke.
