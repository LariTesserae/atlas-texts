Behind the pump house, where the concrete apron stops a handspan short of the marsh, there is a little piece of ground you can only see by standing on the drain cover. From anywhere else it looks like a shadow under the building. It is about the size of a kitchen table.

The ground came up in the spring. During a shutdown, warm water found its way under the apron and pushed a wedge of black clay through the gap. The wedge still has a sharp crest. On the pump-house side it bears the grain of the old formwork, including the whorl of a knot. On the marsh side it breaks into flat shelves, each with a bright seam of water at its foot. The lowest shelf is almost level with the marsh, though the two waters seldom touch.

It is hotter down there than it ought to be. The return pipe runs beneath the clay, and the concrete holds the afternoon heat well into the night. Droplets collect on the galvanized siding and travel down to the bottom lip. From the drain cover you can watch one hang there long enough to take on the blue of the sky. When it falls, the sound is lost in the pumps.

There are two pumps, and they are loud enough that a person on the other side of the building has to come around to speak to you. They run at nearly the same speed. Every few seconds their notes come together and the drain cover buzzes under your soles; then they draw apart and it settles. In the pocket, the water barely seems to notice. A thread of it runs along a shelf so slowly that the only way to be sure it’s moving is to pick out a fleck of rust and look again later.

Three rush stems stand at the marshward end. There is a green stain below them, and a little more green in the recess where a square bolt has fallen out of the wall. Nothing visits the water. The mud takes an excellent impression, but the only marks in it are the straight edge of a fallen washer and the pits the drops have made. Some of the pits have deepened until they meet underneath. A narrow bridge of clay can stand over one of these hollows for most of a summer.

At noon, ordinary daylight reaches all the way to the back wall. You can see pale grit in the black clay and the lettering on the washer, which came from something painted yellow. There is no steam to speak of, only a slight wavering close to the pipe. By late afternoon the light has withdrawn from everything but the crest. It makes that fresh edge look dry, although if you put a finger there you come away with a clean black line.

Once a year the pumps are stopped together. The sudden quiet makes the pocket seem farther from the marsh than it is. You can hear a drop strike the clay, and then another, striking somewhere lower. For an hour or two the shelves hold water without receiving any from below. The thinnest pools disappear first. In the deeper ones a rim of pale sediment is left at the old waterline, too fine to see until the pool fills again and the rim stays white beneath it.
