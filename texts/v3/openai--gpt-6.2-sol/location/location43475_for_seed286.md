The high country begins three hundred fathoms down.

There is no shore to it. The mountain rises from a depth at which the water is black, and for most of its height it is too broad for its slope to be apparent. Then the contours tighten. Long saddles separate one summit from the next, and the light strengthens until the water is the blue of a clear afternoon. It is cold even here. The highest ridge stands just beneath the surface, close enough that the underside of the water throws a second, inverted ridge across the sky.

Nothing in the mountain is stone. The pale faces of the cliffs are growth laid down so closely that a break exposes no grain to the eye, though a thumbnail drawn across it catches on the edges of a thousand years. Where the currents have worn through a face, the exposed layers are green, russet, and a yellow like the inside of a pear. All of them are alive. The oldest wood has no leaves of its own, but if you lay a hand on it you can feel the faint recoil of water being drawn through it toward the crown.

The crown is remarkably bare. A few dark blades stand out of the slopes, each tall enough to be lost in the distance above its base. Between them are leagues of open water. Seen from one blade, the next is a vertical smudge with nothing to tell you how far away it is. Then a shoal passing behind it vanishes all at once, and you get some idea of its width. Here the plants take up almost all the solid world and almost none of the view.

At first the noise seems to come from the ocean overhead. Down in the saddles it resolves into separate sounds: a hammering at long intervals, a drag like a belt running badly on a wheel, a low chord that comes up through the teeth. The mountain has the sound of heavy work. Under each saddle, hollow lobes a mile across pass water from one to the next. Their walls draw taut, release, and draw taut again. Along the ridge, thin leaves take a minute charge from the passing water; where thousands meet at their bases, the current is strong enough to light the sap a cloudy white. The narrowest ravines are deafening. In the wide places, away from the mouths of the lobes, you can hear individual fish biting at the wood.

There are a great many fish, though you can go a long way without seeing one. They favor the edges of things. Silver grazers hang head down just off the cliffs, trimming a fur that is too short to see except where they have eaten it. Long transparent animals lie crosswise in the current with their mouths open. In the blue water between the summits, immense, soft-bellied swimmers come to have their skins cleaned. For hours they hold themselves a handspan off the leaves, moving little fins that seem too small to matter. A stripped leaf has exactly as much living surface as it had before the swimmer arrived. New skin has come up beneath the old.

On the scale of a single blade there is no stillness. Across the high country nothing changes. The cliffs have the rounded lips of cliffs worn by water for a very long time, and the water is still wearing them. Each particle it takes is replaced from below. No lobe fills faster than its neighbor can empty. Nothing dams a channel; nothing opens a new one. At the western end of the range, far beyond sight of the crown, the pumps send up a cold return current broad enough to hold a low cloud in the same place over the sea. It is always raining there. The drops come down through the surface as little bright dents, and their noise, after all that hammering in the mountain, is almost too soft to hear.

From the highest saddle, looking down, you can see a pale patch on the next slope where a blade divides the current. Fish have polished the wood on one side and left the other untouched. Water pours off the lip of the patch and keeps its shape for a long way below it, a waterfall in an ocean, visible by the fine green meal it carries. At the foot of the fall there is no pool, only more clear water. The green disperses so evenly you cannot say where it ends. Far below, out of sight, the roots take it up again.
