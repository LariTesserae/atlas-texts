The land has barely got its shoulders out of the water. In the open reaches, low backs of black clay carry belts of sedge; between them lie warm, shallow sheets of water, furred at the edges with roots. The clay is raw enough to shine where a crab has pushed it up. There are no gullies. A crease that might someday gather enough runoff to cut a channel is already full of fine white roots, drinking from it on both sides.

It is hot even in the dimmest places. Under the broad leaves, where there ought to be relief, the air is warmer and closer, and a hand laid on the underside of a leaf comes away wet. Drops collect along the ribs and fall from the tips. Most strike another leaf before they reach the ground. Out in the open the sky is a dull pewter, reflected in strips where the water is too deep for sedge. Now and then one of those strips turns silver as a school of little fish rolls beneath it.

From a distance you can see a long way across the basin. Up close there is scarcely an empty inch. Snails are stacked on the lower stems, two and three to a leaf joint. There are frogs in the cups where the leaves meet, and smaller frogs on their backs. Wherever a patch of mud looks bare, it turns out to be covered with nearly transparent shrimp. Birds wade with their bellies clear of the water by the width of a finger. A large one lifts a foot and holds it there until a crab has gone under, then puts it down in exactly the same place.

The noise carries well across the open water. There is a continuous dry rattling from the insects, though nothing here is dry, and a layer of frog calls so low and close together that they sound like one throat. Wings slap leaves. Fish strike at the surface. Far out, in water smooth enough to hold the pale shape of the sky, something lets out a slow breath. A little while later the breath comes again from much nearer, without any sound of swimming in between.

With so much going on, it takes a long time to notice how little happens. A stalk goes down under the weight of a bird; by the time it rises, a second stalk has gone down farther along the bank. A fish empties a little crescent of water of its shrimp, and more shrimp drift into it from the roots. The clay shelf is nibbled away at the waterline and laid back down, grain by grain, just above the same line. You can watch a particular root all afternoon. Sometimes it is under water and sometimes it glistens in the air, but there is always one small bead of water caught at its fork. It is never the same bead.

At the far end of the basin the sedges thin out, and for a long way there is only water and a few naked humps of clay. On each hump, at about the height a wading bird could step over, the first leaves are opening. Behind them the older growth is so thick that its fallen leaves never reach the mud. From out there the whole low country looks as if it has just risen, with water still running off it. Along the nearest bank, a snail has made it to the tip of a sedge blade. The blade bends into the water. When it comes back up, there is a snail on it again.
