The chamber is no bigger than a bed, and the roof is low enough that you would have to lie in the water to see all of it. At noon it is brilliantly lit. A finger-wide crack runs up through the limestone to the surface, and for a little while the sun at the top of the crack strikes the pool at the bottom. The water throws the light back across the pale ceiling. Even the small recesses are bright, though nothing in them casts a distinct shadow.

It is hotter than the ground above. Warm water comes in under one wall, so slowly that the inflow is visible only where it folds a skin of mineral dust in on itself. The roof, cooled by its nearness to the open air, gathers drops. Most run along the slant of the stone and fall at the far end. One hangs from a sharp lip directly over the pool. It takes long enough to fall that you stop expecting it, and then the whole ceiling moves.

The chamber opened during the last wet season, when a seam gave way and a slab dropped the width of a hand. You can still match the points on its broken edge to the sockets above it. There is no mud to speak of, no moss at the mouth of the crack, no roots nosing through. In one corner, where a cooler seep keeps a shallow shelf wet, a pale springtail traverses the edge of a meniscus. It disappears under a flake of limestone. There may be others under there; out in the light, there is only the one.

The chamber first showed up on a survey made from far above the surface. The instrument could distinguish warm water from cold through solid rock and follow the slow exchange between them. On the survey it was the smallest of a string of cavities, notable mostly because its water sometimes ran uphill into the next one. That happens when the lower passages fill after rain. Here the only sign is a dark line climbing the wall and a few loose grains stirring in a crack. Nothing has been lowered into the chamber. No one has widened the opening.

By late afternoon the bright patch has crossed the ceiling and contracted to a bar along the broken seam. The little pool is still rising. It reaches a dry ledge the width of a thumbnail, spreads over it, and brings out a second set of fractures too fine to see in the dry stone. Then the light goes, and the water keeps finding them.
