The town is inside a tooth of rock at the top of the range. From the next summit it looks like a dark seam, no wider than a pencil mark. Even from the approach ledge you can miss the door. The door is a slab cut from the seam itself, and the only thing that gives it away is that the dust along its lower edge jumps at regular intervals.

Inside, the whole town is scarcely thirty paces deep, though no one has ever walked those paces in a straight line. Streets double back under themselves. Workshops occupy the undersides of stairs. A landing that serves as a kitchen in the morning becomes the loading floor of a bearing works at dusk. At the upper end, where the rock narrows, two rows of houses face one another across a gap small enough to pass a bowl through. The bowl comes back dry. Everything here does.

The mountain is young. Nothing has had time to round off its broken surfaces, not even the stones dropped when the first galleries were cut. A fresh chip has the dull blue of a knife blade; the older faces are almost black. There is no soil in the joints. On some walls a pale fringe of needle crystals grows out of the rock, and in the dimness a newcomer might take it for moss. Touch it and the whole fringe falls at once. It leaves a clean stripe on your sleeve.

It is cold enough that people put felt sleeves on door handles. At this height a pressure door at either end of a street matters more than a roof. You can watch the sky through thick inspection glass: violet above, brown along the horizon, with loose stone blowing off the neighboring peaks in level streamers. There is seldom a cloud. A storm here is a great deal of mountain in the air.

The little factories work around the clock. They draw wire, grind ceramic bushings, and turn out the thin spring plates that keep the galleries from splitting when the peak flexes. The big motors hang on slings so they won't shake the rock. You hear the tick of a misaligned belt, the soft rattle of parts going into a tray, a lift being called two floors down. For something so busy the place is remarkably quiet. When an entire row of machines stops, you don't notice the silence first. You notice all the animals.

There are hundreds of them in sight if you know how to look. Flat gray skinks sleep in the warm slots behind switchboards, with their tails sticking out like spare leads. Pale moths breed in the bins where the press cakes of meal are stored. The skinks eat the moths, and the town's nail-footed little birds eat anything the skinks leave. The birds almost never fly. They run up the wire partitions and hang there, head down, watching people work. When a motor is due to start, the skinks come out a second or two before the relay clicks. An apprentice who can't see a warning lamp learns to watch the wall.

The street plan is printed on paper small enough to fold into a shirt pocket. It has to be reissued every few weeks. The newest fractures can travel a yard during a shift, and a stair that met its landing at breakfast may be hanging an inch short of it by evening. There are adjustable treads for this, and bridges with one end left free to slide. More trouble comes from the storms. A gust hitting the shoulder of the mountain can set three levels swaying in different directions. Wheels roll off benches. The lift doors refuse to open. Someone sets their lunch on the floor and keeps eating while the fitter next door jacks a doorway back into square.

The darkest place in town is a recess behind the lowest workshop, where the excavators broke through into the original cleft. Nothing has been built in it. The opening is only as long as a hand, but if you put your cheek against the stone you can see straight down past the foundations, past the last ledge, to a sunlit slope miles below. From there the entire town is behind your back: the suspended engines, the houses with their facing windows, the moths rising every time a meal bin is opened. The view is so bright that when you turn around you can't see the workshop at all. You stand still until the small sounds give you its dimensions again.
