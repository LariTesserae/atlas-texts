At noon the plateau is bright enough to see through the ears of animals on the next ridge. The light comes white off the stone, red through the ears. Nothing about it suggests warmth. Where a herd has lain all morning, the frost beneath their bellies is still there when they get up.

The plateau is high enough to divide a storm without receiving much of it. Clouds pile against its western face and spend days there, shedding rain into country too far below to see. A little mist comes over the rim. It beads on the low, fleshy leaves and runs down their stems into the turf. Part the leaves and the soil is black and wet; lift a loose slab a few feet away and the dust beneath it is dry enough to blow into your eyes. In deep clefts, water has been dripping for centuries onto stone so cold that the drops freeze before they flatten. The ice grows in knuckled columns, each with a dark thread of rock visible inside.

The mountains are young. Some have yet to shed the thin plates that stand on their summits where the rock split. On a clear day those plates glitter one by one, all the way to the limit of sight. Below them the vegetation is luxuriant and almost entirely horizontal. There are groves you could cover with a coat: woody stems going back a hundred years, their crowns pressed together under a common lid of leaves. Ferns grow down into fissures rather than up into the wind. When a fissure opens in a hard winter, it reveals pale, perfectly formed fronds that have never had light on them.

Every grove is occupied. Pale grazers with shovel lips crop the crowns while smaller animals stand on their backs and pick through their wool. There are flies even in the hardest cold. They walk instead of flying, in files across the faces of the grazers, and bunch at the corners of the eyes to drink. In the open ground, flat animals the color of stone lie fitted so close together that the seams between their bodies look like cracks in a pavement. Occasionally one lifts itself. The others ease inward until there is no sign of the space it left.

From a distance the plateau sounds industrial. There is a low, loaded hum and, at intervals, a hard metallic knock. There are no buildings. The knock comes from animals underground, bracing their backs against the ceilings of shallow chambers and crushing stone between two ridged plates in their chests. They swallow one fraction of the grit and push the rest out through holes beside their mouths. On the surface, generations of this work have laid down gray fans of astonishingly even grain. Little rooting animals turn the fans over for the salt in them, and by evening there are hoofprints and beak marks where the fresh deposit was smooth.

The hum is harder to place. On bright afternoons, currents travel along the exposed ribs of ore. You can follow one by watching the animals: long guard hairs lift in succession across a slope, and a line of perched birds opens its wings without taking flight. The current passes on into another valley. The sound stays behind. Some of it is the ore vibrating; some is the answering note of animals with hollow breastbones. A narrow pass can be loud enough that you feel it in the roots of your teeth, though the moss at your feet does not stir.

For all the noise and the press of bodies, very little happens quickly. A herd crosses the white basin in the course of a winter. In the mornings its leading edge advances a few paces to reach fresh leaves, and by dark the animals behind have closed the gaps. Their trail, seen from a ridge, is an immense band of darker green: the cropped groves begin putting out new leaves while the last of the herd is still passing over them. The young are born on the crossing and learn the taste of stone dust before they see the far side.

Toward evening the light reaches farther under the leaves. A few small mouths become visible there, working steadily at the stems. Far out on the basin, a patch of ground that has looked bare all day catches the sun at a lower angle and shines. It is the backs of more animals, facing into the cold, each with a little meltwater held in the hollow between its shoulders.
