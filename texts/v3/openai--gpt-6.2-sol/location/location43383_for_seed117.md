At low tide you can walk the length of the settling yard in thirty steps. At high tide there is no yard, only three iron caps above the water and a brick hut with its door open. The hut is hardly higher than the rest of the place. Someone simply hung the door with enough clearance for the tide to come underneath it.

The yard lies under the shoulder of an old sea wall, where it gets an hour of thin daylight even in summer. It is paved with slate, though the joints have widened into channels and the channels into little pools. On a still morning the water in them looks black until you put a hand in. Then you see the pale floor, the fine grit moving over your knuckles, and half a dozen white shrimp braced against the current. The current is hard to account for. Two pools an inch apart may be draining in opposite directions.

Nothing grows upright here. There is no weed on the wall, no grass between the slates. In the wettest seams there is a gray film that lifts in one piece if you catch its edge. Underneath it live flatworms the color of apricot flesh. The shrimp eat the film from below, making round windows in it. Small eels spend the ebb in the drain holes with their heads just out. If a shrimp crosses open stone, an eel can take it and be back in its hole before the water closes over the mark. For a place so bare, there is very little unoccupied surface. Even the underside of a loose washer has a clutch of clear eggs on it.

The hut contains a pump that still runs. The motor is newer than the hut; its blue paint has only just begun to blister. It draws seepage out from behind the wall and sends it through a cast-iron pipe into the lowest pool, where the sediment can settle before the sea comes back. Most of the time you hear nothing from it. When the float makes contact, a relay clicks and a steady vibration comes up through the soles of your boots. A moment later a bead of water swells on the ceiling and falls. There is a tin cup on the sill for catching the drip, though no one has moved it in years and the drip now falls an inch to the left.

The place has worn into a shape that seems unlikely to change much more. The pipe has cut a shallow fan in the slate. The brick at the foot of the doorway is hollowed where people used to turn on their heels to pull it shut. Old bolt holes, no longer serving anything, give small crabs a place to wait for the flood. On the wall, four generations of water marks cross a single enamel sign. Only the word *BELOW* remains legible.

The first sign of the returning tide is a drop falling upward inside the glass gauge by the door. It rises slowly, keeping its round underside, until it reaches the top of the tube and is gone. The gauge has been disconnected for years. The crabs start out of the bolt holes then, and if you stand still you can hear their feet on the dry shoulders of the slate. The pump makes one last run. In the pool below its outlet, fresh seepage lies over the incoming salt water in a trembling layer. The shrimp hang at the boundary, all facing the same way.

Then water comes through the doorway in a sheet thin enough to see the brick through it. It is no warmer than the air, but it carries the smell of the open shore into the hut. For a little while the whole yard is clear and continuous, its steps and seams and small animals visible at once. An eel leaves its hole and crosses a stretch of pavement it could not have crossed a minute before. By the time it reaches the other side, the light has gone out of the water.
