Under the loading dock there is a piece of ground that did not exist last summer.

The factory poured a new apron over the old one, leaving a gap at the river end where the forms didn’t quite meet. Through that gap, sand has come up. It stands in little ridges no higher than a knuckle, sharp as the creases in folded paper. Black grains lie along their crests; the slopes are pale. From the yard you see a broken corner of concrete and, behind it, what looks like more concrete. Lie down and give your eyes a moment and there is a warm country under there, about as wide as a kitchen table.

It doesn’t get much rain. A pipe sweats above it, and the drops fall so far apart that the pit made by one has usually dried before the next strikes. Once or twice a day the river backs up the drain and darkens the sand from below. The wet line climbs without breaking the surface. Beetles walk ahead of it, then stop on the crests, all facing different ways. A few stems of grass grow where a washer has trapped enough silt to hold their roots. Their tips are blanched from touching the underside of the slab.

There is hardly an unoccupied grain. Mites work the dry slopes. Between the grains, close enough to see only when something moves, are springtails, clear worms, and the white jointed legs of animals that never come all the way out. A spider has strung a web across the open end of a hollow rivet. The web is always being repaired from the inside. When a beetle crosses the sand, three or four smaller creatures take shelter in the impressions of its feet.

A belt turns somewhere in the building. You can feel it through your elbows, though out in the yard all you hear is a loose length of strapping stirring against a pallet. At intervals the belt stops and a dull warmth comes down through the slab. The sand gives up a smell of wet brick. For a minute everything that can move uphill does. Then the belt starts again, and down they come, some of them walking over the backs of the slower ones.

The light is ordinary daylight, reflected off the white wall opposite. It shows each blade and discarded wing quite plainly. What it does not show is where the animals go during the shift change, when the delivery door rolls up and a band of direct sun travels the length of the gap. You can watch a beetle reach the band and continue into it. Its shadow goes on ahead, but the beetle is gone. At the far edge the shadow stops. The beetle steps back into view and begins cleaning its feet. The little ones do this too; on a clear afternoon the bright strip is full of moving shadows.

At the river end, a curl of blue paint serves as a roof. Under it are eggs, a husk, two pinheads of water, and the fine sediment sifted from the last rise of the drain. A ridge has begun to push the paint up. By tomorrow there may be room for something larger underneath, but this evening a drop is gathering on the pipe directly above it. The whole blue roof, seen in the drop, hangs upside down.
