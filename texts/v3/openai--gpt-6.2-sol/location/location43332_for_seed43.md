The room under the bakehouse is only twelve paces long, but there is no place to stand where something is not passing close to your feet.

You come down sideways on brick stairs. The outer ends of the treads still have the thumb marks of the people who made them; the middles have been worn into shallow bowls. At the bottom, the floor falls away in three low terraces toward a drain. Everything down here was carried in or shaped in place. Even the banks beside the drain, which look like gray mud, are old mortar, softened grain by grain by the passage of water. There is no exposed earth. Where the wall has lost a brick, you see the narrow end of another brick behind it.

In the morning there is enough light to mend a basket. It comes through a long opening at street level, strikes a limewashed slope, and spills across the room without giving away much of what is happening overhead. A passing skirt darkens the wash. A dog stops to sniff and makes a shadow much larger than a dog. Later, when the ovens are going, the ceiling warms and the room begins to smell of hot clay, old reeds, and the flour that has worked its way into the joints.

Most days there is no water running. A few drops hang under the stair, and the clay pans along the lowest wall hold enough for the sparrows to bathe in, one at a time. When it rains, the sound of water arrives well before the water itself. It rattles down a stack of fitted pots, each with its bottom knocked out, and comes into the room in a thin brown sheet. The first flow rolls husks and beetle cases down the terraces. Then the water clears, and you can see the old scratches in the drain where someone used to rake it out with a bone hook.

There are four places where anything green grows. Three are seams of moss around the pans. The fourth is a cracked jar under the light, with a little barley growing in the sweepings. Mice cut the shoots as soon as they are tall enough to bend over the lip. The jar has stood there so long that the pale side faces the room and the green side faces the wall.

It is a loud place, though hardly anything in it is large. Sparrows argue in the gaps above the limewash. Crickets call from behind the warm bricks, where you can hear them but cannot get a finger in. Pigeons on the street grating set up a drumming with their wings. The bakehouse kneading trough has one short leg, and each time it strikes the floor the sound comes down through the vault. After a while you can tell when the baker has stopped to flour her hands: the crickets go on at exactly the same pitch, but suddenly they seem farther away.

By midday the sparrows have thrown most of the loose chaff out of their nesting holes. It falls onto the shelves and the lidded jars, catches on the handles of crowded baskets, and fetches up in the corner where the mice come out. A small house snake spends the warm part of the afternoon behind those baskets. You sometimes know it is there because the mice, instead of taking the short way across the corner, run the whole length of the middle terrace in the light.

Someone still sweeps the stairs. The rest of the room is left alone unless the drain stops. A broken pan is propped level on a potsherd; a worn brick has been turned over to present its rough face. Repairs are easy to find by touch, harder by sight. Given a few years, the new surfaces take on the same polish as the old ones, and the damp draws its dark line across both. On dry evenings, when the ovens have been shut and the last light is low enough to show the floor grain by grain, the line ends a handspan short of the drain. The sparrows come down then and drink from all the little hollows the water did not quite manage to leave.
