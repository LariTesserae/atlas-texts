The town occupies a saddle between two summits, eighteen fathoms under the surface. At either end of its main street the ground falls away so steeply that you can hang in the water with one hand on the last doorpost and look down into blue for as long as your grip holds. The slopes below vanish in that blue. Now and then a fish comes up from beneath the town, turns on its side, and catches the daylight before anything else about it is visible.

There are forty doors, a market square, two stairways, and a fine deep gutter down the middle of the street. All of it is alive. The oldest inhabitants bent the growing stems of a jointed water tree around stone forms, lashing them with strips of their own bark. Where two stems touched, they eventually grew together. A house built this way has no seam between the wall and the lintel, and puts out buds along the inside of its cupboards. The forms were knocked out with cobble hammers centuries ago. You can still see their shapes in the rooms: the little hollow a chipped corner left behind, the rounded shoulder of a boulder that must have taken half the town to carry.

For all that growth, the town feels open. The square is a broad interval of green-lit water with a flat root floor. Houses stand well apart, connected at the eaves by an occasional stem that nobody has bothered to cut. In summer the stems put out pale leaves flat against the walls. The rest of the year the walls look like wet timber, except for the slow opening and closing of the pores. Someone keeps the windows clear with a flint knife. The cuts are small and neat and often quite fresh.

It is cool here in a way that takes a while to notice. From above, the afternoon looks warm enough: ordinary sunshine on the roofs, a bright patch in the square. Stay still in that patch and the cold works up through your knees. The current comes over the north summit, where there is still snow in the cracks of the rock. It takes most of a day to comb the leaves from one side of a stem to the other. Clay laid in the gutter in a child's lifetime is still there when the child is old, though by then it has been worked into a hard brown shine by the roots.

The town is loud. Along the exposed side of the saddle, the larger trees knock underground. A deep wooden report comes through the soles of the feet; a little later the roofs answer with creaks at different pitches. Prawns snap in the joints of the stairways. In a paved passage behind the market, hundreds of small fish pick at the paving in such close succession that it sounds like gravel being poured. Between these noises you can hear the surf breaking on the higher summit, well out of sight. Its sound is bigger after a storm has passed, when the water in the street hardly seems to move.

For much of the day you might see only three or four animals: a crab at a threshold, two fish keeping level with a roof. At dusk they come out of the fabric of the town. Eels unthread themselves from the gutter. Little orange fish back out of holes no wider than a finger. Snails cross the square in ragged lines, leaving trails that last till morning. The broad, empty spaces remain empty. Most of the animals keep close enough to the walls to touch them, so that seen from above the town seems to acquire a second, restless outline just inside its old one.

At the south end, a stairway goes down twelve steps and stops. Each tread is worn into a shallow bowl. Nothing has been built beneath it, and there is no landing. Once a year the water clears enough to see another twelve steps, much farther down, running back toward the mountain. They are the stairs of an older street, still growing. A pale new handrail has come up from the depths and turned, very slowly, toward the place where somebody reaching from the last worn step would want it to be.
