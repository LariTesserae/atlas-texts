From the low country the plateau looks like a weather front that never quite arrives. Its underside is a long blue bank; its top catches ordinary sunlight. You climb through the blue and come out on a pavement of gray stone with water standing in nearly every joint. There are no mountains immediately ahead of you. You are already high. The land goes on at this height for days, rising and falling by the depth of a house.

Only after a little while does it begin to look like a city.

Along the edge of the plateau, the stone breaks into blocks as large as rooms. Inland the blocks stand closer, shoulder to shoulder, with narrow passages where the joints have widened. Some passages open on broad, nearly square courts; others end at a waist-high sill with a sheet of water lying beyond it. You can walk a long way on these upper streets and never see the bottom of the next one. In places the stone has split again below the first split, and what at ground level seems a single dark seam is, from a distance, a deep stack of ledges, each holding its own shallow pool.

Nothing was laid here. There are no cut faces, though a fracture freshly washed by rain can look more exact than masonry. The likeness to a city comes from how much the stone has had to fit into the available space. Water has a route at the foot of almost every wall. A slab that tipped ages ago now carries a shallow runnel across a gap, watering a row of basins on the far side. Thin shelves stick out over lower passages, keeping a dry strip underneath even in rain. At a few crossings you can stand in daylight and look down through two or three levels of clear water, each flowing in a different direction.

The water is seldom deep. It is simply everywhere near the surface. Step onto what looks like dry grit and the print slowly fills from below. On the flatter shelves, beads of rain stay apart on the stone until a cloud passes and the beads acquire shadows. Then they begin to shrink. A few yards away, where a wall has not yet felt the sun, water is coming out of a pale seam in it and gathering on the underside of a lip. The drops hang a surprisingly long time. When they fall, they fall into mud.

That accounts for some of the quiet. The rest is the shape of the place. Wind combs the high surfaces, but at the bottom of a passage a thread of spider silk can hang straight across the way. Rain falling into the courts makes less sound than a coat sleeve brushing stone. In a sheltered hollow you can hear water going somewhere beneath your feet, though not well enough to follow it. Out on the flats, the movements of weather are mostly visible: a pool going dull all at once, a mile of pale roofs turning dark, a ragged shadow moving uphill against the slow fall of the land.

Very little grows where you first think it ought to. The black margins of the pools are bare mineral stain. Sedge keeps to small pockets of grit above the high-water mark, its blades short and often bent in the same direction. Faint orange lichens occupy the lips of dry overhangs. In one court, tucked close to the north wall, there is a patch of willow no higher than a boot, with old stems as thick as fingers running horizontally under the gravel. In the wet months, tiny crustaceans move through the pools. You generally notice them only after kneeling to drink, when a bit of what seemed to be the bottom crosses into the shadow of your hand. A bird sometimes comes low over the courts, drops out of sight, and rises two or three streets away. You may go the rest of the day without seeing another.

Seen from any one height, the plateau seems close-packed. Walk to the next height and whole districts appear that the first kept hidden: miles of upright stone, then a broad shallow marsh with barely anything projecting from it, then more stone rising in ranks through the wet ground. Toward evening you can follow a rain shower across all three. It fills the courts on the nearer ridge before it reaches the marsh; by the time the far walls darken, water from the first ridge is already spreading in slender fans across the flats. The storm may have gone on beyond sight before those basins finish overflowing.

The place has been in much this shape for a long time. The corners at ankle height are rounded, and the channels pass around fallen slabs so smoothly that it takes a moment to see which way the slabs fell. Still, the route you took in the morning may not serve in the afternoon. The change can be less than an inch of water over a sill. On one side, the court you came through quietly fills; on the other, a passage that held a pool all morning drains down to its pebbles. You cross there instead. The sun has reached the walls but not the floor, and on the pale stone beside you the old waterline is already drying.
