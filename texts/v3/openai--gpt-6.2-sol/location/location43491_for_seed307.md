The whole place would fit under an upturned hand. It lies inside a split in a mountain, a little below the highest point, where the rock is too new for the split to have acquired a name or even a stain. Outside, ice feathers the windward faces. Inside, the rain is hot enough to raise steam from itself.

Two black planes of glass stand a thumb’s width apart. Between them, a blade of pale stone rises almost the entire height of the cleft. The blade has the sharp, slightly ragged edge of something broken this morning. Nothing has fallen far enough to make rubble. Every splinter is still wedged near the place it came from, and water swells in the seams around them. Now and then a splinter turns a fraction. A new channel opens; an old one goes dry. This is as close as the place comes to having seasons.

The light is ordinary daylight. It comes through a notch above the blade and falls on its wet flank for several hours, plainly enough that you can see the small brown mineral grains in the stone. There is no green, not even under the lip of the warmest pool. What looks from above like a soft brown growth draws in its feet when the water touches it. The rock underneath is bare.

There are animals on nearly every surface and animals in the water between the surfaces. The most numerous are clear, flattened things with seven or eight feet apiece. They climb over one another without apparent haste, fastening each foot wherever they find a purchase: glass, stone, the moving back of another animal. In the deeper beads of water, white jointed bodies fold and unfold so rapidly that it is hard to see how many there are. Once in a while a black head noses through the mass, takes one, and withdraws. For a moment the entire bead clears around the place where the white body was. Then the others unfold into it.

The animals with jaws sometimes pick up a loose grain of glass. There is nowhere to put it down within the press of bodies, so they carry it until they come to a thread of water, then hold it in the thread and wait. Small swimmers snag on the grain’s corners. The animal eats them one at a time. When the grain has worn smooth, it drops it. The floor of the cleft is still too young to have caught many of these.

For all this, the pocket is almost silent. Most of the water never gets far enough from a surface to fall. It passes by contact, rounding the points of the splinters, slipping from a wet foot onto a wet back. There is an occasional tick. It is as likely to come from a jaw closing as from the mountain settling.

Near the base of the blade, the two walls lean close enough for a drop to connect them. On the near wall the drop is a skin of water, with a few feet moving under it. On the far wall it is deep enough to drown a swimmer. An animal crossing it does not change size. It simply has farther to go than there is room for. You can watch one walk in at noon and still see its hind feet an hour later, testing the same edge, while its head emerges into the daylight on the other side.

The young cross there in great numbers. They have no difficulty until the drop begins to thin. Then the ones nearest either wall hurry, and those in the middle turn over and lie still. When the bridge parts, the two wet surfaces draw back into their separate beads. The animals in the middle are nowhere on the dry stone. At the next joining they turn right side up and continue across. Sometimes there is a black head among them that was not there when the bridge broke.

Late in the day the light rises off the blade. It goes out of the shallower water first. The deep side of the bridge keeps a little daylight, even when there is no longer a bridge, and by it the animals go on reaching for the next foothold. Above them, ice begins to close over the notch. Below it, so close that you could touch both places with the same finger, another hot drop gathers.
