The plain is low enough that a change in the wind can move its shoreline half a mile. You can watch the water come: first the black seams between the stones widen, then the stones take on reflections, and then there is nowhere left to put a dry foot. The water is clear, barely deeper than a boot sole, and so cold that little fans of ice grow out from the stones while it is still moving. They break off and travel flat side up.

There is nothing growing on the plain. The green in the shallows is the color of stone seen through a particular thickness of water. Where a stone noses up into the air it is gray again. The land has been worn nearly level, but the last foot of relief is crowded with things: overlapping shelves, blind wells with lips as smooth as cups, beds of loose white teeth that turn out to be calcite. From any one of the shelves you can see a long way. It takes most of an afternoon to reach the next one.

The noise is that of a mill working at full capacity. A low turning sound carries for miles, with a steady hammering above it and, now and then, the high sustained note of something being drawn through a die. Nothing on the plain has been built. Where the water is shallowest you can see broad plates of stone slip beneath one another, their edges polished and wet. Along one edge a skin of copper, finer than foil, comes out in a continuous sheet. It buckles in the wind, goes under, and disappears into another seam. There are miles of these workings. A held breath is long enough to feel one course of blows cease and another take up under your heels.

The animals were hard to distinguish at first because they were everywhere. What looked like a bed of small pale pebbles lifts on jointed legs when the water reaches it. The creatures turn broadside to the current and comb it with the long hairs on their forelimbs. They catch metallic grit, translucent eggs, and one another’s young. Farther out, the big animals lie lengthwise in the channels with only their nostrils above water. When one rolls, the pale combs scatter over its flank. In the still pools behind them are tiny swimming things with dark stomachs. You can tell which ones have just fed: their stomachs flash when the copper sheet tears.

The light is dim and comes mostly sideways. A shallow flood can make the whole west brighter without illuminating the underside of a lifted hand. All the same, there are shadows at noon. Some are cast by animals that will not arrive until the water turns. The little combing creatures settle in these shadows and are caught there often enough that it seems a poor habit, though they may be getting something from the shade that they cannot get after the animal arrives.

Near the middle of the plain a trough runs straight for perhaps six miles. It stays empty during floods. Water hangs at either lip, bulging a little over the drop, with scraps of ice lodged in it. Looking down from the north bank, you see the trough full of deep moving water and the bellies of animals crossing overhead. From the south bank you see dry stone. At one place the trough is narrow enough to step over. On the far side your leading boot is dry and the other is full. The water in it contains grit and a small animal that would have had to come up through the sole.

Toward evening the hammering misses a beat. All across the shallows, the small creatures fold their limbs. In the quiet you can hear ice settling along the shore, each piece moving a little against its neighbor. Then a blow lands a long way off. The creatures unfold again, beginning in the west. By the time the line reaches you, the water has begun to go down. A broad-backed animal is left stranded on a shelf. It lies still while the little ones pick over it, then slides into a channel that wasn’t deep enough to hold it in the morning. Long after its wake has gone, a wet ridge on the shelf rises and falls where the animal had been breathing.
