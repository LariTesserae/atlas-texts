The whole reef would fit under a cathedral roof. It stands near the summit of a drowned mountain, well above the surrounding seabed and just far enough below the surface that the noon light arrives white rather than blue. On clear days you can see the water wrinkle overhead. Below the reef’s outer lip there is nothing to catch the light for a very long way.

The rock is young. Its ridges still have the thin, translucent edges of broken bottles, and where a slab has split, the two faces match down to the smallest tooth. Through the black glass run seams of chalk-white stone that the water has barely begun to widen. There is no kelp, not even a skin of green on the sunny ledges. A pale fuzz in the cracks turns out to be the legs of small crabs. When a shadow passes over them, they fold away and leave the rock clean.

A low thudding comes from inside the summit. There are six or seven notes to it, each steady enough that you can follow it through the others. Cold water descends through one set of clefts, water warmed in the mountain rises through another, and at the narrowest points the two flows make thin leaves of stone flex against their sockets. One of the leaves has a chipped corner. You can hear that one from anywhere in the reef: two clean knocks and a little scrape. Nothing here was made, but the place has the layered racket of a factory heard from the floor above, right down to the occasional hard report that makes every other sound seem briefly far away.

Most of the animals live within a body’s length of another animal. Flat white fish hang in the slots with their noses pointed into the flow. Limpets cover the most exposed edges, their shells overlapping, with shrimp tucked into the gaps beneath them. In the innermost hollow, a knot of transparent animals strains a cloudy suspension out of the rising water. There are so many of them that you can see the current by watching the dark meal pass from one clear body to the next. What drifts out the other side is nearly clean.

Despite the noise and the crowding, very little moves any distance. The rock leaves travel less than the width of a thumbnail. The fish hold their places all afternoon, correcting for each pulse with a flick of a fin. A long animal like a loose length of intestine takes days to cross a ledge. If the ledge ends before it has finished, the forward part waits in open water for the rest of the body to catch up.

At the lowest note, the water in one cleft goes milky. For a few seconds the fish there cast shadows upward. Then the cleft clears, the shadows fall back where they belong, and the chipped leaf goes on knocking. The shrimp nearest it are so accustomed to the disturbance that they use the pause after the scrape to pass from one shell to another.

At the rim the noise drops sharply. A hand laid on the last slab would still feel the strokes coming through the stone, but beyond it the cold water is almost empty. Every so often a crab loses its hold and falls past the lip. For a surprisingly long time you can watch it reach for the mountain.
