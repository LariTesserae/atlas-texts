The plain has only just come up out of the water. You can tell by the edges. A clay ledge no higher than a boot has a lip sharp enough to peel skin, and the little runnels below it meet at angles that the first hard rain will round away. There has not yet been a first hard rain, though everything is wet. Warm water wells through the clay and stands on it in films. Where the ground is firm enough to carry you, it sweats under your weight.

There is no light. The plain extends a long way in every direction, but you learn that from the absence of returning sound and from the old causeway, which runs straight for eleven miles without climbing higher than your knee. Every so often you come to a joint where one slab has tipped. Water laps over the low corner. Put your palm flat on the next slab and you can feel the pumps working somewhere far out in the basin: a short tremor, a pause, then a longer tremor. After walking for an hour you may find the same stroke still in progress.

Not much grows here. Reeds have taken hold around a few pipe collars, and their roots knock softly against the iron when the water lifts them. There is a tough, low plant with leaves that cup the seepage and hold it even when you turn the leaf over. Most of the mud has no roots in it at all.

It is full of animals. Little hard-shelled things work past one another in the shallows, so numerous that when you put a finger in the water you feel the crowd part before you touch the bottom. Flat fish lie on shelves of clay with their mouths in the current. Something broad and warm sleeps across the causeway in the deepest part of the basin. There is room to go around it, if you don't mind putting a foot among the fish. The animals seldom call. What you hear, mostly, is the occasional sound of a single mouth closing.

The pump houses are low brick buildings, miles apart. Even at the door of one you hear more water than machinery. The motors sit on cork beds; the pipes have been wrapped in felt that is always damp. Someone has greased the hinges recently. Inside, the handrails are warm, and there are enamel plates beside the valves, with the lettering raised just enough to read by touch. One says **RETURN**. The pipe beneath it goes down into the floor. When you lean against it, you feel water falling for much farther than there is any depth in the basin.

That is an ordinary difficulty here. The surveyors used to carry sounding lines until they found that a line could come up wet above the hand that was paying it out. Now the depth marks on the pump-house walls are dated. A mark at shoulder height might say *Thursday*, another at the skirting board *the following April*. The dates help with the pumps. They are no use if you are wading.

Distance is more dependable, provided you keep moving. If you stop on the causeway long enough, you may hear your last footfall ahead of you, on concrete you have not reached. Usually it is just the one. Farther out, where the clay is freshest, there are banks with pools on both sides that share a surface. A beetle can dimple the water on your left and the ripple will finish on your right. The fish keep to their own pools. You can feel them turn at the bank.

Nothing much comes of these irregularities from one night to the next. The broad animal in the basin moves a little in its sleep. A ledge acquires a bead of water that takes all night to fall. The pumps go on lowering the plain by an amount the gauges can register but a person standing in it cannot. At the far end of the causeway there is a ladder laid flat on the mud. The lowest rung is still under water. Someone comes out every few weeks to wipe the mud from it, so that when the water finally drops there will be a clean place to put a foot.
