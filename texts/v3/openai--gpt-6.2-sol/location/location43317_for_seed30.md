The pass is finished on both sides. Only the saddle is still coming in.

It is a small place, thirty paces between the old stone shoulders, and high enough that you can look down on the weather moving through the valley. Up here a different weather goes on. Warm beads gather on the undersides of ledges, creep to the rim, and let go upward. A hand held over a ledge comes away wet. Every so often enough water collects above the saddle to make a shallow, wavering ceiling. Then something gives, and for half a minute it rains very hard.

Three white posts stand around the new ground. One has a hook where people leave things they want on the way back: a string bag, a wet cap, a chipped cup. The other two have no obvious fittings. When they come into alignment, as seen from the old trail, a little more of the saddle takes weight. You can hear it happen. The hollow, room-filling noise of the rain changes to the clatter of rain on stone.

The new rock has no weather side. Its corners are as exact underneath as they are on top, and each pane of black mineral is clear for the first minute or two. You can look through it and see the next pane arriving at an angle. The seams shut with a report that sends the animals out from under the lip in a rush.

Until then you might think the place nearly empty. There is a tuft of wiry grass beside the cup, and on the far shoulder a little fan of bronze leaves, all turned to the wall. But the lip is crowded on its underside. Small flat animals hang there with their toes spread, drinking from the beads before they rise. On the new stone they travel in spurts, bellies lifted, testing each clear patch with one long forefoot. There are pale moths, too, which never settle on anything solid. They land on the water overhead and walk about on it upside down. When the rain comes they fold their wings and fall with it. The flat animals are very good at telling a falling moth from a falling drop.

Even at noon the saddle is dim. The old shoulders keep out most of the sky; what light reaches the floor has generally passed through water first. From the trail you sometimes see the shadow of a moth go over the grass, large and slow, while the moth itself is no more than a flicker above your head. The noise makes the space seem larger than it is. You can be looking directly at someone across the gap and not hear them call. Then the water lifts, the stone stops moving, and you hear the wet cap dripping into the cup.

Toward evening a strip of the new saddle runs all the way across. The animals find it first. A dozen go over in single file, making a little detour around a place that looks firm but still gives back their reflections a fraction too late. By morning there will be a trail there. For now, on the entire warm black floor, the only marks are where their feet have been.
