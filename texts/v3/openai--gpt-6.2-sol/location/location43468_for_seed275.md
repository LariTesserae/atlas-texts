The city is growing under an unbroken mile of stone. From the arrival platform you can see across it in ordinary white light: a freight line suspended over a pale basin, a row of unfinished towers, and, beyond them, a second basin opening under a rise in the ceiling. The lamps are spaced so evenly that distance, rather than darkness, hides the far districts. It’s cool enough to wish you’d kept your coat on in the lift.

The first thing you learn is not to judge where the plants are by what’s green. The pavement is a living rind, gray and finely stippled. The broad pale slopes are the undersides of leaves that haven’t unfolded. Even the dark material in the cuts beside the railway is compressed root, though it breaks into angular pieces and rings when struck. There are recognizable trees here and there, widely spaced, with narrow crowns. They stand out the way a fountain might in another city: an elaborate local expression of the material everything else is made of.

There’s no water in the basin. In the deeper cuts, where you’d expect the rock to sweat, it gives off a dust so fine it clings to the vertical faces. The plants circulate a clear oil that stiffens to wax on exposure. A torn leaf will lay down a little white seam before your eyes. Along the avenues, the horticultural crews cut drain slots by habit, even though nothing has ever run down them. Beetles use them to get across the road.

From a distance the center looks quite finished. There are stations with clocks, elevated junctions, factories with enormous louvered walls. Then you notice that the stairways on the east side of the central square end a foot above the pavement. The square has sunk a little overnight. Underneath it, a junction is putting out a second tier of platforms, knitting floor across an open space the surveyors marked only last week. Crews in quilted jackets go around with wedges and measuring rods. By evening they’ll have fitted temporary bottom steps. Nobody cuts back a public stair until they know where it means to go.

Industry takes up a great deal of ground and makes remarkably little noise. A mill has six bays and a door wide enough to admit a whole train, but its machines are bedded in thick root felt. Standing outside, you hear an occasional contactor click. Through the louvers you can watch black belts ferry lengths of fresh-grown beam to the planers. Farther out, a traveling crane follows the lip of the newest terrace, lowering electric lamps ahead of the road crew. The city has learned to light its outskirts before it builds them; otherwise the big leaves rise toward the center and close off the freight approaches.

Many animals live in the basin, though there’s room enough that you can walk a long way without seeing one. Flat-backed grazers come up from the median strips to shave the warm growth around lamp standards. The little gray hunters move between vacant lots, waiting at the open ends of curb channels. Once in a while a whole flight of winged things drops out of a ceiling fold and crosses the lit space above the tram wires. You can hear their wings only when they pass directly overhead.

At the far end of the line there’s a stop with no name. Beyond it the pavement gives way to broad, intact lobes, each the size of a building lot, with thin black seams between them. Nothing has been rounded off here. The slopes have edges you could set a ruler against. Survey flags stand in groups of three, and some of yesterday’s are already high enough to need a ladder. If you stay until the work lights come on, you can see the next street taking shape down in one of the seams. Its two sides are still touching in places. At intervals, a surveyor reaches in to pull out the strips of paper that tell her where they’ve begun to part.
