From the opposite rim you can see the whole city, including the undersides of some of its streets. It occupies the upper walls of a basin so deep that the floor is hidden by its own moisture. The highest roofs stand among bare peaks. Far below them, through rents in the white air, you can make out more roofs, complete with courtyards and dark doorways, on ledges that from this distance seem no thicker than the edge of a plate.

It is hot all the way up. On the rim, where there ought to be frost in the cracks, water warm enough to bathe in gathers around the roots of low, broad-leaved trees. Their bark comes away in wet red scales. Ferns stand shoulder to shoulder along the first descent; below them the vegetation hangs in long skirts over the walls, with pale new leaves folded in the shelter of the old ones. In the spaces between leaves there’s nothing flying. The slick mud at the edge of a pool hasn’t so much as a pinprick in it.

Once you’re in the city, you can see how much of it was made by arranging for things to grow against one another. There are no cut faces on the stone. Retaining walls are banks of rounded black boulders with roots fitted so closely around them that you can scarcely find a joint. Houses share those walls. Their outer rooms are roofed with the thick lateral growth of trees you can follow back uphill, sometimes for the length of a street, to the soil they stand in. In one place there’s a run of shallow doorsteps, each a little higher than the last. The ground outside is perfectly level. Whatever lives in those rooms, if anything does, must have wanted a different height of threshold for each one.

The noise begins well before the descent and is still startling between walls. Water comes out of the rock in sheets, striking the shelves below with a force you feel in your back teeth. There are also the deeper sounds of falls you can’t see, and a steady, almost granular clatter from the wet leaves. At some corners you can hear the little tap of a drop falling from a lintel, although someone shouting into your ear would make no difference. The tap is always there when you turn the corner. It doesn’t seem to matter how long you stood on the other side.

The streets are well drained, even those that cross open water. They have gutters and spillways, little raised crossings at the doors, wider channels down the middle where two lanes meet. You can follow one of these channels downhill for most of an afternoon and come to the lip from which its water falls. Across the gap, directly above you, is the crossing where you started. You recognize a white root exposed in the pavement. The fall passes it on the way down. Looking back along the street, you can see that there isn’t a foot of it you would now describe as uphill.

Daylight reaches nearly everywhere. In the deeper courts it comes off wet walls and has the greenish cast of light under a tree, but out on the terraces the sky is a plain, hazy blue. You can put your hand in the shadow of a leaf and watch the water run over your fingers. When you take your hand away, the thread of water is whole again. The stone beneath it has a groove as wide as your thumb, worn smooth, with no grit at the bottom. All the gutters are like that. It’s easy to believe these channels took ages to wear and difficult, standing over one, to picture it getting any deeper.

There is a broad square toward the lower end of the city, open on three sides. In its center a low wall surrounds a stand of glossy trees with roots submerged in clear water. You might go there expecting relief from the noise, seeing how still the pool is from the steps. Instead it’s the loudest place in the basin. Here you can hear every fall at once, including one from far overhead whose spray never arrives. Under the trees the water lies flat enough to show the pale forks of the branches. At the wall there’s a notch where the pool overflows. The water goes out through it in a smooth, thick tongue. Even this close you can’t see the height of the pool change.
