At the upper tram stop, the sun has warmed the handrail before the first car comes in. Swifts are going under the platform, flattening themselves for the gap between the floor and the cable housing. Stand at the rail and you can see the whole valley, including the underside of the terrace where your breakfast was grown. Far below that are three more terraces, white in the sun, and below those the roofs of the pump works. The works look close enough to walk to. It takes most of the morning.

There is no mountain inside the mountain. The ridges are hollow, with stairs and freight shafts in them, and the saddle between the two summits is a continuous pour of concrete. In the first years people used to date the different sections by the color of the aggregate. Now the windward edges have rounded, lime has filled the hairline cracks, and you have to go down into a service bay to see what any of it looked like new. Where runoff comes down the long stairs, the treads have worn into shallow spoons. The stairs alongside them, which carry all the foot traffic, still have square noses.

It is warm enough for figs at the top. Their roots go into deep troughs filled with crushed brick and compost from the lower gardens. The orchard keepers leave a hand’s breadth between the trunks and the expansion joints, a distance the trees have spent eighty years making inadequate. Beneath the figs are taro, ginger, and shade cloth gone green with moss. Ferns grow in the bolt cups on the outside of the terrace. From a distance their fronds soften the line of the parapet; from above, you can see how precariously each one is seated, with its little dark wad of roots and the bolt showing through.

Rain comes some weeks and not others. The air is seldom wet all the way across the valley. On a bright afternoon you can walk through the cool drip beneath a condenser bank, come out into dust and hot tile, and find the laundries two levels down hanging their washing in the same little breeze. Water is kept in shallow cisterns under the public squares. You hear it through the inspection grilles when a delivery cart goes over: a brief slap against the stone lid, then nothing. Most of the damp is in places no one meant to irrigate. A leaky shutoff has kept a strip of maidenhair alive behind the tram depot for longer than anyone at the depot can remember.

The valley is open to the sky and crowded all the way down. Bridges pass over gardens, gardens over workshops. On the far side, the roofs of a row of houses serve as the loading apron for a mill. A woman bringing in her sheets can look up and see a wagonful of flour go by at the height of her bedroom ceiling. The longest view is down the spillway, a brilliant, almost bare incline with a seam of green down one side. It takes a moment to notice the maintenance crew halfway along it. They are sitting in the shade of their own cart, eating lunch.

By midday the pumps and the mill are both running. Their sounds come up separately through the structure: the pumps in the soles of your feet, the mill in the balustrade if you rest an elbow on it. In the open air there are cicadas, tram bells, and the hard calls of the little parrots that chew the fruit buds. Geckos sleep behind the switchboard covers. A colony of bats hangs in the disused freight shaft, above a floor that the night crew sweeps once a week. Down at the settling tanks, herons stand on the depth gauges. The fish there descend from fish put in to eat mosquito larvae; nobody has stocked the tanks in years.

Very little moves quickly except the animals and the machinery. The tram makes the same bend every twelve minutes, but the fig branch above it has taken half a lifetime to reach the cable. Every winter a surveyor measures the distance between the two sides of the old saddle. There is a penciled column of readings in a cabinet at the upper stop, and room on the sheet for another thirty years. The people who live up there mostly judge the age of things by the shade. The bench where the tram driver eats her supper used to be too hot to sit on until dusk. Now the fig reaches it by four.
