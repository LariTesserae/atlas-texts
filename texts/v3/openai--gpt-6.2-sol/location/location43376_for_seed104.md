At the highest part of the drowned continent, the water is warmest. It comes up through the rock in broad, almost imperceptible sheets, lifting the leaves without turning them over. There is no summit in the usual sense. The old mountains have worn down to a tableland so wide that a current crossing it acquires a season. Yet at its edge the bottom falls away for miles. The plants along that lip lean inland. Nothing shelters them there; they are leaning toward the heat.

Most of the light comes through the leaves. They grow in tiers, the upper ones black-green and leathery, the lower ones thin enough to show the slow passage of sap. In the space between are veils of finer growth, and between those, grains of white mineral rising out of the ground. There seems scarcely room for water, though water occupies nearly everything. Even the rock is soft with it. A flake broken off the tableland shows the same dull brown all the way through. There has not been a fresh face of stone here for a very long time.

The seasons travel east. You can see one coming as a line where the forest lies flat for hundreds of miles. In its passage the oldest leaves let go, the warm seepage turns briefly cold, and the bare stalks put out clear bladders that pull them upright again. By the time the eastern rim begins to shed, the west has grown a whole new roof. Almost none of this makes a sound. A leaf will occasionally fold along its rib with a small, dry click, astonishing in so much water.

The plateau has its own weather above the forest: long, dim fronts of freshwater that do not mix with the salt. They go by like ceilings. Where one touches a high grove, the trees are visible twice, once rooted in the silt and once hanging down into it. That much can be accounted for by the bending of light. But as the front passes, the hanging grove sometimes stays. Its roots work their way into what was open water; fine sediment collects on the underside of its leaves. The original trees go on growing. In time the two stands are equally old, down to the healed split in a particular trunk.

On the leeward side of one rise are three low walls. Each encloses little more than the spread of a sleeping body. The stones fit closely enough to suggest hands, though their tops have worn lower than the moss around them. Inside the middle enclosure the silt slopes to a shallow round hollow. It fills every day and is empty again by dusk. Dusk cannot be seen from here, but the hollow observes it, even during those years when a freshwater front settles over the rise and will not move.

Animals are so scarce that the forest has no paths. Once in a long while a pale, jointed thing comes out of the vegetation and crosses a stretch of bare ground. It puts each foot down with great care. Before it reaches cover, the impressions at the beginning of its trail have filled, not with settling silt but with little upright fans of green. The creature does not eat them. Nothing follows it.

Farther inland, away from the warmer rim, the folds in the land become almost too gentle to see. You know them by what the water does. Over a shallow valley, drifting fragments speed up and disappear, then come into view a little farther on as whole leaves, still attached to their stems. At first this seems to happen only to small things. Then, after a long traverse of nearly level ground, you reach a place where a great eroded shoulder of the continent is being carried uphill in the current. It moves so slowly that a forest lives on it undisturbed. Below it lies the smooth bed it wore for itself over ages. At the upstream end you can just make out the first grains of stone coming together.
