The garden occupies a notch high in the mountain, a place no wider than a railway platform. One wall is the mountain itself. The other was laid up in blocks of a pale stone that has weathered more slowly than the rock it was built to hold. Both ends are open. Stand at either end and you can see straight through to daylight, though it may take several minutes before the leaves give you a clear view.

There is no water here. A boot scuff lifts a flour of mica that takes a long time to come down. Under the trees lie heaps of translucent husks, brittle even where they’ve just split. The trees are doing beautifully. Their trunks are the thickness of a finger and their crowns are extravagant, layered with broad green leaves that show tawny undersides in a gust. Break a fallen twig and it draws out into a thread of clear oil. On cold mornings the oil moves slowly enough that you can wind it around the twig again. The trees make it even in the middle of winter, which up here differs from summer chiefly in the angle at which the light comes in.

The noise is appalling. The first impression is of a train passing very close; then comes a succession of flat reports, and a much higher sound that makes the little bones around the ear ache. There is surprisingly little wind on your face. A seed husk can hang almost motionless at eye level while a branch beside it lashes hard enough to score the wall. The trees have grown around this discrepancy. Their trunks are swollen at points where nothing you can feel bears on them, and some of their oldest limbs have worn shallow channels in the air. You can find such a channel by holding a shred of paper out on a stick. The paper disappears. After a while it comes back along the ground, folded small.

There are three pieces of equipment in the notch. One resembles the rim of a sink set upright in the rock. Another is a black peg you could cover with a thumb. The third is somewhere beneath the paving; its position is given away by a square slab that shifts sideways a hair at noon. The apparatus takes some of the mountain’s weight off it. It does not take the weight *away*. Where the weight spends its time is an old question. The people who used to tend the place left a bench with its feet set very deep, and if you sit on it your knees occasionally feel the arrival of something many tons heavier than you are.

The animals mostly keep to the carved lip under the wall. Look into it and there are rows of copper backs, soft white feet, little rubbery mouths. They eat the oil from the trees and sleep touching, sometimes in strings a dozen long. In the open garden you might wait an hour to see one. Then a crown flips inside out and dozens come sailing from it, fantastically spread out, each animal trailing its own shadow on the pale stone. They make tiny adjustments with their hind feet. A few get all the way across the notch; most are overtaken by the restoring branches and caught again. Down among the roots the young go on feeding. The noise has never told them anything worth stopping for.

For all that goes on, it is an old, settled place. The floor has a shallow dish worn in it where loose stones have been going round for centuries. A fallen tree lies under the bench, polished on the exposed side and still shaggy underneath. Nothing has filled in the notch or widened it. In one corner a bush grows out of the joint between two blocks, and the same narrow gap that admits its roots gives them room to twist when the whole crown is snatched to the left.

Late in the day the wall puts the bench in shade. The air is cold enough that you want your hands in your pockets. From there you can watch the crowns break into separate green masses, scatter, and come together again. Between convulsions there is a great deal of plain blue air: above the bench, under the leaves, beyond the lip of the garden, where the mountain drops out of sight. For a second you can see a single animal crossing it, with all four feet extended and nothing at all beneath them. Then the sound hits, the wall shivers against your back, and the animal lands on the leaf it was aiming for.
