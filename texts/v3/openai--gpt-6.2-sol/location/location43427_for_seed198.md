From the upper tram stop you can see almost the whole valley, though you can’t see the bottom. It’s under the works. The two sides descend in steps of brick and poured concrete toward a long, level expanse of factory roofs. Across that expanse run belt galleries, hot-water mains, and streets carried on columns. The river is down there too. Once in a while, through a gap no wider than a doorway, you catch the dull green of it between its tiled banks.

It has rained recently, but it would look much the same if it hadn’t. Water comes out of the retaining walls at the joints, gathers along the underside of the handrails, and falls from one flight of steps onto the next. The public stairs have twin gutters: one for the rain, one for the seepage. On the older flights the two have worn together, leaving little islands of tread with the aggregate showing. The air is cool enough that you keep your coat on even in the glass passages. The panes are fogged at the edges and clear at face height where people have wiped them with their sleeves.

Almost everything that looks like a hillside is a roof. There are allotments on the cable vaults, a nursery over the switching yard, wet meadows on the deep decks where the reservoir used to be open to the sky. Alders grow in continuous beds along the outer parapets, their roots inspected from hatches in the galleries below. There is a great deal of green, much of it nobody’s doing. Ferns have filled the space between double walls; moss retains the outlines of signs taken down decades ago. Along the warm return pipes, gardeners grow beans late into the year. They tie the vines back at the crossings so the inspection crews can pass.

The valley is wide enough for weather to hide one district from another. This morning the far incinerator is missing from the view, but below it the yellow footbridge is clear from end to end. A freight lift comes up through the mist with a little square of wet daylight on its roof. It stops level with a warehouse floor, and the square vanishes.

There is not much noise. The plant machinery sits on its own foundations, apart from the floors people walk on, and the newer freight lines are enclosed. A lowering gate makes the faint rasp of a knife being drawn across a board. You hear water going down pipes in the walls. At intervals the tram rails begin to sing; then a car rounds the bend, quite close, with a face in every window.

For all that quiet, nothing stays put for long. A lane of empty carts accumulates under an awning, is coupled in a minute, and leaves behind a strip of paving dry enough to show its original color. A growers’ market opens along the reservoir stairs. Before noon its tables have been carried up three landings to make way for a crew renewing the expansion joints. Roof vents turn into the gusts and spill white vapor over the lower walks. On the flood terraces the river is let into one holding court as another drains. Workers drop grates over the channels, and people who’ve been waiting with shopping bags take the shortcut before the last of the water has run away.

The permanent things are easier to recognize by touch. The thick lip of the bridge coping has been polished by wet cuffs. The oldest brick piers have lost their corners to generations of turning carts. A set of iron rings in the tram-stop wall once held the ends of a hoist; now each ring holds a loop of hose, and the wall below is green. Whole buildings have come and gone around those piers without changing the way the water runs off them.

You can go a long way without seeing an animal. Sometimes a pigeon steps out from beneath a heating duct with the affronted look of a person disturbed on a break. In the reed beds on the reservoir lids there are small birds in summer, but today the only movement there is the wind laying the reeds flat in successive patches. From the far side they might be taken for water.

Toward evening the valley becomes easier to read. Lamps go on beneath the decks before they do in the streets, showing all the narrow floors between one broad surface and the next. What looked from above like a single dark hillside turns out to contain a laundry, two tram platforms, a fernery, and a row of kitchens. Above them the trees move in a wind that doesn’t reach the kitchens; below them the river moves in the dark. At the tram stop the rain begins again, lightly. For a while the only evidence of it is that the wet handrail, which had almost stopped dripping, starts to drip at both ends.
