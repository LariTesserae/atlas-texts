The city has a little room beneath it where the rain goes after it has finished falling.

You reach it by a stair behind the old tram terminus. At the bottom, the stair opens onto a brick quay about as long as a tramcar. Across the water there is another quay, narrower, with a bench and three enamel signs giving the names of streets overhead. One of those streets no longer exists. Water still comes down its pipe.

The basin is shallow enough that you can see the paving under it when the inspection lamp is on. The stones are set in fans, as they are in the older market squares, and their crowns have been worn flat by some use that preceded the water. Along the edges, lime has grown over the mortar in low, rounded lips. There is no clear line between the masonry and what has accumulated on it. A mason coming here to repair the place would have to decide which parts of the wall were his.

It is cool, but not with the moving cold of a tunnel. The air stays where it is. Condensation gathers beneath the bench and on the glazed handles of the sluices. In two joints below a ventilation grille, hart’s-tongue ferns hold out a few unblemished leaves. There is a little moss on the top step, where a streetlamp reaches it through the stairwell. Nothing green grows very far from either of these places.

There are, however, animals almost everywhere. The water looks empty until you stand still. Then freshwater shrimp become visible, first one under the quay and then a dozen, each facing into a current too slight to carry a flake of rust. Pale snails graze up the glass of the depth gauge. In the dry recess behind the bench, cave crickets stand so far apart that you could take each for the only one, but their antennae meet across the gaps. In winter, moths hang under the brick vault. Now and then one changes its hold, and a small patch of the ceiling seems to settle.

Two pumps occupy an alcove in the far wall. They are still in service. Their motors are painted the pale green once used for machinery in public buildings, and the name of the foundry is cast into each housing. At intervals a relay closes with the sound of a fingernail touching a cup. One of the pumps turns just long enough to lower the water by the width of the black line on the gauge. Then there may be nothing to hear for an hour except a drop falling off the underside of the bench. The traffic overhead is most noticeable when it stops: a fine tremor leaves the handrail, and you discover that you had been feeling it all along.

At the near end of the basin, below the lowest stair, there is a reflected window. It has six panes, one cracked, and sometimes the shadow of a branch crosses it. There is no window in the vault. If you put your hand in the water, the panes break up quite ordinarily around your wrist.

The old caretaker used to judge the weather by it. Not the weather above the terminus, which had a habit of being different, but the weather the room would have in a few days. When the panes went white, beads of water would begin to stand out on the enamel street signs. When the branch moved, the shrimp would leave the quay and collect in the lee of the submerged curb. Once, for most of a summer, the window was open a little at the bottom. The caretaker left his coat on the bench during those weeks. He said it was warmer down there, though the thermometer did not agree.

The stair door is locked now, except on inspection days. The attendant comes down, wipes the gauge with a rag, and notes the level in a book. From one visit to the next, the basin is nearly always the same. Then, leafing back through several years, she finds a photograph in which the bench has four legs clear of the water. She looks across at it. Beneath the seat, in the inch of water that covers the feet, there are shrimp feeding on the paint.
