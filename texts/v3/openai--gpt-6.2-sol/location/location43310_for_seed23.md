The town fits inside a fold in the mountain. From the far side of the ravine you could cover it with your thumb: a white spill of roofs, a black seam where the stairs go up, and a single blue pool at the top. From inside, there is no view of the whole place. A wall is always in the way, or a roof passes so close above the lane that you can reach up and put a finger into the water running along its underside.

It is very cold, and almost nothing is dry. Water comes out of the cliff a little warmer than the air and travels through the town in shallow stone gutters. It freezes at the edges, narrowing its own channels until it has to climb over them. A stair that was dry at breakfast may be carrying the whole flow by noon. The stairs are old enough for this. Each tread has a deep foot hollow and a shallower hollow to one side, worn by water taking the same detour for hundreds of winters.

The houses are built of split slate, clay, and the shoulder blades of large animals. The bones make good lintels in the smaller doorways; the broad ends sit deep in the walls. There is a little hammered copper in the town, much of it in the form of staples that hold cracked bowls together. A broken bowl is worth mending here. Timber has to come up from below the cloud line, and a person can spend half a day’s fuel firing a new pot. When a roof needs propping, the neighbors bring stone. Some of the kitchens have acquired so many pillars that the person tending the fire has to turn sideways to get to it.

Above the roofs the sun is almost unbearable. It shines on snow across the ravine and comes back up into the low rooms, where it lights the ceilings and leaves the floors dark. For a few minutes each morning you can see the little waves in the public cistern projected on the underside of the meeting-house roof. Then the sun clears the ridge and the cistern goes blank with glare. The only green in the square is a thumbprint of moss under the lip of the trough, where a goat cannot quite get its teeth.

There are animals everywhere. The goats stand on the roofs to lick the white salts out of the slate. Small gray birds nest behind the loose faces of the walls, sometimes three or four nests to a stone. Beneath the lanes, in the covered drains, are pale fish with broad heads. When a slab is lifted to clear an obstruction, they crowd into the opening so densely that for a moment the water seems paved with them. The wall birds come down to look. They do not take the fish; they take the little red things that come loose from the fishes’ gills.

For all this, the town is quiet. A goat landing on a roof makes a soft knock that can be heard two lanes away. Most of the water falls from a wetted surface onto another wetted surface, with hardly a splash. Even the wind, which tears long pennants of ice dust off the ridge, passes over the fold without getting much voice into it. You can watch a whole weather change from a doorway: birds gone in one sweep, the far ravine erased, hail bounding up off the roofs, every bright gutter going dark. A minute later the sun is back and the goats are picking their way out along ledges that were not there before.

The blue pool above the town is where people go to see what the water is doing. There is no shrine, only a stone bench with a worn place for each heel. On still days the pool shows the undersides of the roofs below it. Nobody objects to that. What they watch for is the morning when it shows a roof they don’t recognize. Then the households go down and check the empty rooms, the sealed passages, the old lanes built over by newer ones. Usually they find a slab shifted or a blocked drain running clear. Once, after a very bright night, they found warm rain falling in a cellar that had been dry in living memory. By evening there were fish in it. They raised the flour bins on stones, cut a step into the threshold, and left the cellar door open for the birds.
