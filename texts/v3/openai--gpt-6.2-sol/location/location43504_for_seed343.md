The mountains have worn flat without coming down. For a hundred miles their tops stand at much the same height, broad enough in places to lose a herd on, and between them the land drops so far that the shadows of the eastern walls do not reach the western walls until midday. There are no sharp summits left. Even the freestanding columns in the valleys have rounded heads. From above, the country looks like a plain with most of the plain missing.

You can walk all morning without putting a foot on stone. The turf is a mat of small waxy leaves, brown underneath and warm to the touch. In some places it lies on rock; in others it has grown across a fissure, and the difference is apparent only when your step makes the leaves tremble a little way ahead of you. Look down over a rim and the same green continues on the undersides of the ledges. Ferns hang roots-up from them. Broad, pale vines descend until their own weight tears them loose, leaving bare streaks that may take fifty years to close. Whole slopes have no soil except what the plants are holding between them.

It rains chiefly in the late afternoon, a brief warm rain with large separate drops. Water runs along stems and gathers wherever a leaf has folded against itself. There are beetles that can live out their lives in one of these folds. By morning, even after rain, there is seldom a puddle on the path. The only dependable open water is in the hollows on top of the oldest mesas, where the bottoms have been lined, over centuries, by the flattened husks of the plants that grew there and drowned. The pools are shallow enough to wade across and taste faintly of tea.

The air between the mesas looks vacant. A bird crossing it can be watched for twenty minutes. Yet at daybreak the cliffs begin to call to one another, or so it seems from the path. The first sound is the rasp of insects warming under the leaves. Then the ledge birds wake, and the long-bodied animals that graze head-down on the walls start calling for their young. The calls arrive from below and from miles away, some twice, some with the beginning lost and only the last note coming clear. By late morning you can shout to someone on the same patch of turf and see them turn because they caught the movement of your arm.

For all that noise, you may go half a day without seeing more than a bird. Then a shadow moves against the sun and what appeared to be an acre of gray foliage lifts from the opposite wall. Those are the sailwing colonies leaving to feed. The animals spread out as they cross; by the time the first reaches your side, the sky seems almost empty again. Underfoot there are burrowers eating dead roots, and under them the slender hunters that follow the burrows by touch. Sometimes a patch of turf rises and travels a few inches as a grazer works its way beneath it. You learn not to sit on a place just because it looks soft.

Along the rims are a few low huts made of living willow. Their builders bent the stems down and pegged them with horn until they rooted. A hut left untended grows shut, door first. There are stone cups set into some of the thresholds, and now and then a worn bone hook where a roof used to be. Paths survive better than houses. The grazers use them in the wet season, and on steep stretches their feet have polished old hand-cut steps. Nobody cuts steps all the way down a wall. The paths go sideways, sometimes for miles, to get from one band of pasture to the next.

At the end of a hot day the valley air rises and spills across the tops. You feel it first as a little warmth against your ankles. Seed floss comes up past the rim, then insects, then birds turning easily in the current. Far below, the first rain has already struck the leaves. You can hear it coming up the wall for some time before it reaches you. When it does, the sound of it is lost at once in the animals. Only the dark spots on the path tell you it has begun.
