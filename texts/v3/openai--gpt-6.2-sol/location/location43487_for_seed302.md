The plateau is so loud that the small stones seem to be keeping quiet.

It lies above the last soil, a broad shelf of pale rock with a darker range shouldering up behind it. On a clear day you can see from the first wet ledges to the far rim, where three notches give a view of blue distances below. There is nothing to interrupt the view, but almost nowhere to put a foot. The entire shelf is crowded with knee-high fins of stone. Some are no wider than a hand. They run in crooked ranks, part and come together, and stand so close in places that a skin of water bridges the gap between them.

The light is fierce and cold. Quartz grains shine through the water on the flats; on the steeper faces a thin wetting brings out bands of plum and green in the stone. There is no moss in the green. There is no root under any of the loose plates, and no dark accumulation in the hollows where the water rests. At the bottom of even the deepest of those little pools you can see clean grains of sand, each with its own fine shadow.

The noise comes from underneath. Water enters the plateau against the dark range, where it passes through a bed of broken rock and disappears. It keeps to that bed for most of the width of the shelf. The stone above it is jointed, and the joints open to the air in thousands of narrow mouths. At some of them you can look down and see nothing moving. At others there is a white flicker a long way down. Kneel at one and the roar is no louder than it was standing up. It seems that it ought to be possible to climb above it, but the highest ledges are as loud as the flats. From out on the lower slopes, looking back, you can hear it all at once: a single sustained note of water, with a hard rattling edge.

Very little of that violence reaches the surface. A bead gathers on the end of a fin and lets go; the next bead takes its place. There are low places where the rock gives under a boot, just perceptibly, and water wells up around the sole. Elsewhere a channel as fine as a scratch serves as the outlet for a pool the size of a room. It has served long enough to wear a lip into the pool's edge. The lip is rounded and intact. The water goes over it without a ripple.

Once in a long while a grey bird crosses the shelf, flying low enough to disappear between the fins. It may land on one of the bare points and stand with its feathers lifted at the neck. You cannot hear it leave. Other than the bird there are a few pale things in the deep pools, flat against the stone. You can spend a long time looking at one before it moves far enough for the patch beneath it to come into view.

Toward the outer rim the fins diminish until they are scarcely higher than the standing water. Here the whole shelf is visible behind you, mile on mile of little wet edges. Ahead, the ground falls away so cleanly that the eye goes first to the opposite mountains and only then down. The water does not pour off in a great fall. It comes out at many different heights, in short white threads that cling to the cliff and are lost in its cracks. Even here it is difficult to pick out the sound of any one thread. In the brightness they look scarcely substantial enough to wet the miles of stone beneath them. Yet every ledge down there is dark with water, right to the limit of sight.
