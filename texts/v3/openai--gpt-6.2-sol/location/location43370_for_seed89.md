The hollow is forty paces across, and its sill is higher than the snow on the next mountain. From outside, it looks like a seam in a tooth of black limestone. You have to lie flat to see into it. A warm draft comes out against your cheek, smelling of wet stone and the underside of a bird’s wing.

Inside, the floor slopes in a series of shallow bowls. Their rims have been rounded so thoroughly that even where the rock is broken it feels worn. They are mostly dry. In one, there is enough water to cover a knuckle; another has only a wet crescent where a drop has been landing since before the lower valleys took their present shape. There are small pale crusts along the old waterlines. In the deepest bowl, the bottom is black with the cases of hatched insects.

It takes a while to understand how much of the ceiling is alive. There is no one moment when the eye adjusts and shows you everything. A ledge that seemed solid divides into backs. The backs lift a little, settle, and become a ledge again. At the upper end, where the last of the daylight fails, thousands of little mountain swifts cling to the stone. Lower down are the bats, packed into cracks so narrow that what you see of them is mostly ears. The warmth along that wall is startling. The air a pace away is cool enough to dry your lips; near the crack it feels like air breathed through a blanket.

The noise goes on even when nothing flies. There is a brittle twittering overhead, a lower shuffling from the seams, and a sound like dry seeds poured slowly into a wooden bowl. That last comes from beetles among the fallen feathers. Whenever a swift changes its grip, several of its neighbors object, and the quarrel travels along the roof after the bird itself has gone still. The drip in the back can be heard only from beside it. At the entrance, the wind sometimes takes all the other sounds away at once. Then it lets them back.

Green things keep to a strip below the sill: a few pads of moss pressed nearly flat, a grass with three stiff blades, a lichen the color of old butter. On damp mornings the moss swells enough to hide the scratches of claws. By afternoon you can see them again. Nothing roots farther in. The brown strands hanging from the ceiling are fur caught on the limestone, and the soft white growth along the floor vanishes in dry spells and returns to precisely the same depth.

Water comes chiefly when a cloud settles over the ridge. There may be no rain anywhere below, but the sill beads over and the shallow bowls fill in order, each spilling by a notch into the next. The notches are scarcely wider than a finger. Most of the birds have never seen the last one run. In a wet year it makes a thin thread down the outside face, where the wind breaks it into droplets before it has fallen the length of the hollow.

At dusk the swifts leave first. They drop out of the seam rather than trying to climb into the wind, and for a little while you can see them banking below your feet, against snow that is still lit. Then the bats come out low over the sill. The hollow does not empty. Young birds go on calling in the roof, beetles work their way under the feathers, and now and then something small drinks from the knuckle-deep pool. Long after the opposite mountain has disappeared, the water there still holds a little of the sky.
