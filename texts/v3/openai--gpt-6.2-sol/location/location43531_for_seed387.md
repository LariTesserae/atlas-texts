Under the city, behind a row of brick arches that look solid from the service tunnel, there is a flooded room scarcely wider than a tram. It takes the spent cooling water from three blocks of factories. The water comes in through a slot at floor level and leaves through an iron throat in the roof, to join a culvert that runs all the way to the harbor. The city has grown over this arrangement without altering it much. In the ceiling, two generations of electrical conduit cross a much older pipe stamped with the name of a street that no longer exists.

There is surprisingly little in the room. A ladder ends two rungs down. Four brass bolts mark the place where a screen once stood. For the rest, there is warm gray water from floor to ceiling, and a distance of perhaps five yards across which nothing holds still.

The noise is deafening. You hear the low note of the outflow in your jaw before you can distinguish it from the racket of the incoming water. Now and then a pump somewhere up the line shuts off, and for half a second the chamber seems to fall quiet. In that interval you can hear a loose washer beating against its bolt on the far wall. Then another pump comes on, the washer disappears into the noise, and the direction of the current at your knees changes.

A single green inspection lamp shines through thick glass. Its light reaches the opposite wall only when the water is briefly clear of bubbles. The bricks there have worn into long shallow hollows, with the mortar left standing between them. At the level of the inlet even the mortar is gone, and the wall has the smooth, slightly concave surface of a basin used for washing over many years. There is no weed. A rust-colored film survives under the lip of the lamp, where the strongest current passes a finger’s breadth away. Once in a while a pale shrimp comes out of a bolt hole, walks a little way upside down, and backs in again. Its antennae stay out long after the rest of it has vanished.

Small things from the city pass through. A flake of blue enamel can go round and round beneath the lamp for most of a shift, while a steel nut dropped just an inch to one side is gone before it hits bottom. Someone once lowered a sounding chain from the hatch. The current laid it nearly horizontal. Its end tapped the same brick so often that there is a round white mark there now, in the one part of the room the water itself has hardly touched.

At the change of shift the water grows warmer. The roof trembles as the old pumps hand their work over to the new ones, and for a few seconds the two currents meet in the center of the room. Everything loose rises. Grit, bubbles, a curl of bright wire: all hang together in the green light until one current wins. Then the room empties itself again, and the pale shrimp puts out its antennae.
