At noon there is enough light to see from one end of the basin to the other. It comes through a long break in the roof, too narrow to show much sky from below, though the clouds passing over it can be followed by the movement of their shadows. The basin lies so far down that rain is often falling at both levels: the new rain through the break, and yesterday’s rain coming out of the stone.

The first thing the water touches is a steep belt of ledges. Some are pale where they have only lately split away from the wall. The edges are sharp enough to part a leaf drifting down against them. Below these ledges the ground opens into a shelf a mile or two across, crossed by shallow channels and so thoroughly grown over that it is hard to say where the land ends. What looks like a meadow from above turns out to be a hundred narrow islands with their sedges leaning together. There is water under almost every step, even on ground high enough to carry alders.

That is where the town is.

It was made by beavers, though very little of it looks like the work of one kind of animal anymore. The oldest dams are broad enough to have trees on them. Their roots take the place of branches that have long since rotted, and their toes are thick with rushes, which catch the silt that comes down in spring. Below the dams, new banks are going up in crooked increments. The tips of the alder cuttings are still orange. There are lodges with three or four submerged approaches, canals that turn neatly around outcrops, and little bypasses that carry overflow into ponds too small to see until you come right to their edge. In the upper reaches, where a fall of stone cut off the water last winter, a whole string of lodges stands empty. Voles have moved into the dry roofs. The old canals are full of young willow.

From the ledges you can see how crowded the place is. There is scarcely a foot of shore that does only one thing. A bank holds back a pond on one side and shelters a trout on the other. Above it, in the root plate of a fallen tree, a wren has put a nest where the rising water cannot reach. The tree itself is half sunk and soft enough that a beaver swims over it without breaking the surface. Small fish keep to the fine branches. Herons walk the tops of the dams, lifting each foot clear of the grass before setting it down.

For all that movement, there is almost nothing to listen to. The main flow leaves through gravel at the far end of the basin; it makes no fall. Drops patter briefly on the larger leaves, then the leaves tip and empty into moss. Sometimes a duck goes down a canal in a hurry and for several seconds you can hear the water brushing both banks. An alder gives way once in a while. Its crown hangs in the branches of the next tree, and the sound is over before the tree has finished falling.

The cold comes up through the water. Even in the middle of the day the mud under the sedges is cold enough to hurt a bare hand. Things grow close and low where the roof hangs down: liverworts over every joint in the stone, ferns flattened by the drip, pale runners of plants that flower only in the open part of the basin. Along the middle, under the break, the alders are tall enough to catch the light on their upper leaves while their trunks stand in shade. On clear afternoons there is a little warmth there. Midges gather over the ponds, and swallows come down from above and feed until the rock cuts off the sun.

At the far wall, the last inhabited pond ends abruptly in a new slope of broken stone. Nothing has grown on it yet except a thin green line marking where the water stood a week ago. The pond is higher now. At its upper end, fresh mud has been pressed into a gap in the dam, and water is beginning to run the other way, along the foot of the slope. By evening it has found a hollow among the stones. A few minnows are already in it, holding still where the current comes through.
