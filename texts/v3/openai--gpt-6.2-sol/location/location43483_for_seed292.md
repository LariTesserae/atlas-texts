The ridge is six miles long and has not broken the surface in a very long time. It rises out of a cold basin in a series of broad shoulders, the highest of them just shallow enough for kelp. On a clear day there is ordinary green daylight up there. You can see the small white scars on a seal’s flank as it turns. Off either edge the bottom falls away so steeply that, from the last shelf, you can watch stones go down until they disappear without ever seeing them strike.

The rock has had all its corners taken off. There are ledges as broad as streets with no lip to them, and gullies that fit the current as neatly as the inside of a pipe. The whole ridge might look soft from a distance if there were anywhere to stand far enough back to see it. In the shelter of the lower terraces, fine gray silt lies in deposits older than anything growing above them. A crab can put a leg through the top layer and turn up a band of black sand from a storm ten thousand years ago.

Up on the crown, the kelp grows so thickly that the upper blades lie across one another for acres. They don’t all move together. A swell lays the canopy flat while the water underneath is still running uphill; then the stalks come upright one row at a time, dragging long banners of bubbles out of the leaves. Mussels pack the cracks at their roots. In the lean of a boulder, soft corals and anemones leave scarcely a thumb’s width of bare stone. Here and there the green ends abruptly at a chute polished clean by the tide. Fish gather on either side of those chutes, working their tails, and cross in bursts. The seals wait at the downstream mouths.

It’s a loud place. The smaller stones make a busy clatter in the shallows, while the large ones knock at intervals you can’t predict. In a storm there’s a low, continuous roll from the surface, and beneath it the nearer, sharper sound of shell coming loose. Halfway along the ridge, at the head of a ravine, an old tide gauge is bolted into the rock. The cable that once carried its readings ashore still passes through a steel fairlead. When the current draws it taut, it hums. For the first hour of the flood tide you can hear that note from both sides of the ridge; at slack water even a few yards away there’s nothing.

There isn’t much else to show that people worked here. A yellow lifting eye, a length of armored cable with copper showing where the stones have worn through, two concrete feet from a frame that went over the side. The feet are still square underneath. Eels use the bolt holes. On the north shoulder there’s a shallow bore someone drilled and abandoned, perhaps because the crown of the ridge was too rough a place to lower a second load. Every winter it fills with gravel. Every spring an octopus clears it out.

The roughest days change the view almost by the minute. A hillside of kelp flattens and exposes pale stone you could have sworn wasn’t there. A bank of tiny fish breaks around nothing you can see and closes again farther down. Water coming up from the basin is several degrees colder; it carries a haze that spills over the shelves and puts out the daylight from below. Then a long swell passes, the haze lifts in strips, and shadows are visible again under the blades.

At the south end the ridge divides around a bowl wide enough to hold a town. Most of what the storms tear loose goes there. In the hours between tides, scraps of kelp descend through perfectly clear water, some of them still carrying snails. They come to rest on the silt with their torn ends curled upward. The next flood will take a few of them out again. The rest settle into a dark seam so thin that a crab crossing it leaves prints in the gray beneath.
