The marsh is no bigger than a kitchen table. It lies under the last landing of a steel stair in the pump house, where you can see it only by bending far enough to put your head below the handrail. At the foot of the stair there was once a door. Its threshold is still there, scarcely higher than the water.

There is no earth in the marsh. Under the sedges is a bed of crushed brick, and under that a shallow tray cast into the floor. The plants grow along the sides, where their roots can get into the joints. They are lush enough to conceal the tray's corners, but nothing grows in the middle. There the water is clear and the level floor shows through, pale as the inside of a worn cup. Here and there a black aggregate pebble rises out of the concrete. The pebbles used to stand proud enough to catch a broom. Now they are flush and glossy.

Daylight comes through the tall windows of the pump house and falls between the stair treads. It is ordinary daylight, good enough to see a hair caught in the mesh over a drain. The room is warm. You feel the warmth first on the railing, then in the air below it, where your shirt begins to stick between your shoulders. Water beads on the underside of the landing. Most of it runs to a low spot in the steel and falls from there onto the same broad sedge leaf. The leaf bows, empties, and lifts. Below it the brick grit has worn away to a little round hollow.

The noise is so great that at first it seems to leave nothing out. Three pumps run on the other side of the wall, and the wall carries their sound into the floor and the soles of your feet. If you speak, you can feel yourself speaking but cannot tell how loud you are. Yet the middle of the pool is nearly still. Look closely and there is a fine tremor along the reflection of a stair tread, too small to travel as a wave. A water snail, the only animal in sight, is partway across that reflection. Behind it is a clean track through a film you would not otherwise have noticed.

The pump house stands on a plain so low that the discharge pipes have to lift the water before it can go anywhere. Here, under the stair, it has only to cross the width of a hand. A little warmth comes through the wall with it. A little water goes out over a lip at the far end. No one has had to adjust the lip in a very long time. It was a saw cut to begin with; you can still see the last tooth mark above the waterline. Below the line the cut has rounded until it is difficult to say where the floor ends and the outlet begins.

Every so often a sedge goes brown and falls inward. By the time it reaches the middle it has softened enough to lie flat. There is almost no mud. The finest matter leaves with the overflow; what stays lodges in the roots at the edge, where the next shoots come up. The newest leaves are a bright, plain green. Behind them, on the wall, a band of old mineral stain records the same depth as the water now, down to the slight rise where it touches the steel.

From half a flight up, the marsh is gone. There is just the sound of the pumps and, in the light beneath the landing, the top of one leaf that never quite reaches the stair.
