The whole district fits beneath the old railway station. You can stand at the bottom of the ticket stairs and see, through warm green water, the pale rectangles of all three streets. The farthest ends at a brick wall. The nearest runs into a machine shop without any change in paving: the shopkeepers used to roll their work out into the street, and the rails for their carts are still there, worn shallow in the middle.

Nothing down here is quite ruined. The brick has rounded at the corners and the mortar has receded to the depth of a thumbnail, but the arches hold. A blue enamel sign hangs above a door whose wood has long since gone. The sign says ALTERATIONS. Through the door, a stand of ribbon weed grows from a cutting table, each leaf turned edgewise to the same nearly imperceptible current.

There is hardly any unoccupied surface. On one windowsill, a layer of black moss supports a layer of lime, which supports a second layer of moss so fine it looks like soot. Copper pipes wear thick green sleeves of growth except at their joints, where small leaks keep the metal bare. Ferns rooted in the gutters unfurl across upper windows. A person could pass easily down the middle of a street, yet there is nowhere to put a hand without touching several things at once.

The district is warm because the works still run. Beyond the brick wall, a flywheel turns in a housing full of oil. Along the street, broad leather belts have been replaced by chains, then the chains by shafts enclosed in ceramic tubes; the empty brackets of all three systems occupy the walls together. The machinery moves so slowly that the only way to catch it in motion is to pick a bolt and wait for it to come out on the other side. Heat gathers under each arch. In a doorway where two currents meet, it rests in a band no thicker than a bedsheet. You can put one hand in summer and leave the other in the cool.

Every few doors a lamp burns behind ribbed glass. There is enough light to read the raised numbers on the utility covers, but not enough to tell the color of a coat hanging in a shop. Shadows fall upward under the lamps. Once a day, when the station above lets a little daylight through its pavement grates, the shadows turn over. The weed takes another hour to follow.

Animals are scarce. A white snail lives on the face of a pressure gauge. It has cleared a little arc across the glass, from which you can see that the needle is still trembling. Sometimes a translucent fish comes out of the drain beside the machine shop and crosses the street. With nothing else moving at its speed, the crossing seems quite abrupt. Then the drain is empty and the street looks much longer.

You hear the works best from the ticket stairs, with an ear against the iron handrail. There is a faint knock, then a long interval in which you can hear the water moving inside the rail itself. A warm bead wells from a seam beside your thumb. It lets go and rises past the old fare notice, gathering nothing on its way, until it touches the underside of the landing. There it joins a pocket of clear water that has been growing, very gradually, for years.
