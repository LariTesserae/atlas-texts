The valley is so high that most weather passes beneath it. From its rim you can watch a cloud bank work its way up the outer slopes all night without reaching you. Only at the saddles does a little vapor get through. It lays a wet stripe across the stone, beads on the undersides of leaves, and is gone before it can gather into a stream.

There is considerable green here, though almost none of it rises above the ankle. The valley floor is a succession of broad shelves with deep, empty intervals between them. On the shelves, overlapping leaves make a continuous cover, thick enough to lose a hand in. There are black ferns curled under the cover and pale stems that go on growing beneath the oldest mats, where nothing has seen the sky for centuries. At the lip of each shelf the vegetation stops. Across the gap, sometimes half a mile away, it begins again at exactly the same height.

The rock has worn into forms that no longer seem to be changing. The same shallow basins hold the same skins of ice each night. Loose stones have found the hollows they fit. Yet there is a great deal of work going on inside the mountain. At irregular hours the leaves all along one shelf turn up their silver undersides, starting at one end and finishing at the other. A minute later, the shelf below does the same. You can feel the passage in your teeth before you see it.

There are no towers or cables, no road by which anything heavy could have been brought here. Still, the valley has the hours and habits of a factory. It draws a charge from the clouds below, stores it in seams of wet black mineral, and spends it in long, nearly silent discharges. Where two seams meet, the exposed rock is smooth and slightly warm. Small animals lie there in shifts. Each has six feet and a long naked tail; in the dark, their tails are easier to see than their bodies. They hold them out over the drop until a faint blue light collects at the tips, then tuck them under again.

There are more animals than the emptiness suggests. A shelf that has shown nothing but leaves for an hour may suddenly release a hundred gliders. They fall a long way before opening, so that at first they look like stones knocked loose by something out of sight. Their hunters wait below the point where the wings spread. Sometimes the only evidence of a pursuit is a patch of plants folding flat on the far side of the valley, followed much later by a soft scrape as the hunter comes home over the rock.

Daylight reaches the rim but seldom goes farther. For a few minutes it picks out the distant snow and shows how much space there is between one living surface and the next. Then the snow goes gray, and the valley is given back to its smaller lights. Near your feet, a drop works its way down a fern stem. It reaches the last curl and hangs there, trembling through two discharges of the mountain. By morning it has frozen in place. The fern puts out a root toward it.
