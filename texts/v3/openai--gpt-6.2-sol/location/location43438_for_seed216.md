The upland has no rock in it. What looks like a cliff is the exposed edge of an old floor, its layers of leaf and bast pressed so fine that rain runs down them in straight, dark strokes. The paths have the warm give of a cutting board worn thin by generations of hands. Even the pale grit that sticks to a wet heel is plant matter: little hard cells shed by the rushes bred to take the wear.

The whole country is built, but very little of it is filled. From a path no wider than a doorstep you can look through a thousand feet of warm air to the next inhabited surface, a cupped green terrace that catches the runoff. Farther out, long woven slopes hang at different heights without quite meeting. A rain squall can travel all afternoon among them, visible by the darkening of one rim after another. Sometimes you watch a shower pass underneath you and feel its warmth arrive a little later.

In the old districts you can see how the work was done. Someone split a cane with a sharp shell and wedged the halves apart with a peg. Someone else came back when the new shoots were supple and drew them through the gap. The peg sprouted. Four hundred years on, its crown shades the crossing, and its roots have swallowed everything but the little nick where it was trimmed to fit. There are no dead poles here. A pole that dies gets teased into mulch and packed into a joint where something living needs feeding.

Rain has made its own revisions. A stairway that once ran straight now bends around the groove worn by water falling from a higher stair. In places the foot treads and the drip hollows are equally deep. Water is rarely far away, though there are few pools. Lift a flap of bark and there is a clear bead behind it, trembling on a root hair. At the close of a warm day whole undersides begin to weep. The drops gather at the ends of leaves, hang until they are almost too long to hold together, then fall so far that you never hear them land.

Light filters down along the same routes. In the middle of the day, you can distinguish the greens; toward evening you tell one surface from another by what the wet does on it. The toughest walks shine in patches where bare feet have polished them. The loose fronds barely show at all. If you stand still long enough, the dimness resolves into animals. Flat brown frogs fit flush in the old peg holes. A flock of soft-winged birds pours out of the folded underside of a terrace and travels a great distance before the first one moves a wing. At dusk, long-toed grazers come out along the woven slopes to crop the tender tips. They keep far apart. Each has enough to eat within a few steps, and out in that much open air a raised head can be seen for miles.

There is a rule about going down. A drop can fall directly from an upper walk to a lower one, and so can a person. But a stair built between the same two walks will sometimes arrive at the lower one in a different season. The old builders tested the route with seedlings before laying the handrail. Where the test failed, they left the last step unwoven, so that the traveler had to jump. You come upon those gaps everywhere: a foot or two of empty air, often with a good stout handhold on either side. It is wise not to let the handholds grow together.

From the highest walk, on a dim, dry morning, you can see almost nothing between yourself and the horizon. Then a passing shower sets a thousand edges dripping and you begin to make out the country: not a continuous spread of green, but acre after acre of thin living surfaces, all carefully kept apart. Very far off, a storm has bowed one of the great slopes. The grazers have already moved to the rim. By the time the slope lifts again, the storm is watering a district you cannot see.
