The highest country in the sea is a set of broad terraces, one above another, with cold water over the last of them. You can swim out from a terrace and go a long way before there is anything beneath you again. The slopes are so great that the sinking of a dead leaf from the upper country to the lower takes most of a year. Yet the terraces themselves are almost level. Wherever a corner might once have been sharp, the water has worn it round.

All of it was made by trees. You can tell this at a broken parapet, where the ends of the roots turn back into the parapet instead of trailing into the water. A tree begins by growing outward along the flank of its parent. Its roots go down a little way, find the roof of an older tree, and spread along it. The lower roots thicken into piers; the upper ones meet and rub together until their skins wear off and they can grow into one another. Given enough generations, they make a floor. Then other trees build out from that floor. There is no earth packed between them and no stone underneath. In the deepest cut anyone has reached, the walls are still wood.

For all that, it is a bare-looking country. On a terrace a mile wide, perhaps three crowns rise above the floor. The rest of the forest is underfoot, alive in layers, and its leaves hang where the water can reach them: beneath cornices, inside the long joints of the piers, on the undersides of bridges. There are places where you can put a hand through a seam and feel a whole stand of soft new leaves on the other side. Out in the open water there is nothing to touch for a great distance.

Even up here there is no daylight. The leaves closest to the unseen surface are broad and almost black; those lower down are pale enough that for a moment you think you see them, then realize you are seeing a fish pass in front of them. A few of the fish carry a pinprick of light under each eye. They cross the open terraces alone. Close in, the animal life is thick. Shrimps stand in rows along the lower edges of the steps, facing out, and small eels lie two or three to a root hole. In certain galleries, every change in the direction of your swimming sends a different school brushing past your face. Then you round the end of the gallery and have a quarter mile of empty water to yourself.

The loudest places are not the exposed heights but the covered passages. A great deal of water moves through them, slowly enough that you hardly feel it against your cheek. Somewhere in each passage it has to pass between two old walls. The narrowest gaps give out a steady note that carries through the wood into the terraces on either side. Put your ear against a floor and you can hear several such notes at once. There is also a dry-sounding crackle, surprisingly near, from animals feeding on the tender skin of the roots. A large fish passing through a gap takes one note out of the chord. As soon as its tail is through, the note is back.

Here and there an entire span has died. None has fallen far. Its living neighbors have grown through it and hold it in place, even after the grain has washed out of the dead wood and left holes big enough to swim through. A new floor is always forming a little below an old one, and when they finally meet there is barely a seam. The passing water takes a little from the outer edge; the roots give back a little from within. The great ledges have neither advanced nor withdrawn for longer than the oldest living tree.

At the rim of the uppermost terrace the floor curves down, and the leaves begin on the other side. Fish come up over the rim, turn, and disappear under it. If you follow them, keeping one hand on the wood, there is a moment when the whole height of the country is below your feet and the floor is above your head. It is just as cold there as on the terrace. The same low note comes through the wood, a little clearer now, because you are on the side where the passage opens.
