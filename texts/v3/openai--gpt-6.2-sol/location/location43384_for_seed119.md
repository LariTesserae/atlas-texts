The whole marsh fits inside a low square of wall. At the threshold you can see the opposite sill, though not the floor between. There is a roof of overlapping clay slabs, propped on stone ribs, and along the join a finger’s width of sky. Even in the middle of the day the light has to come down wet walls before it reaches the water.

There is no earth here. The shallows are laid in fired brick; the little offshore bars are made of broken pots, deliberately stacked with their mouths facing upstream. What looks like a bank slumped under its own weight is a stair worn out of recognition. A few tufts of sedge grow from bowls sunk to their rims. Their roots go round and round inside the bowls and nowhere else.

Water seeps in through one wall and out through another. The inlet has been lengthened by wear into a slanting slot, so that the stream comes in sideways and runs halfway round the room before crossing it. Nobody alive remembers it running straight, though you can put your hand into the old channel and feel the chisel nicks under the water. At the far end, the current sorts fragments off the brickwork by size. The heavier ones lodge against the sill and make a little dam. The fine red grit goes on through. Every so often the dam gives way and the marsh drops the width of a fingernail. Then all the wet steps show again, with their edges shining.

At first the room seems almost empty. Then a snail lifts off the underside of a slab and falls with an audible tick. There are hundreds more under there, crowded onto the cool clay out of sight. Pale shrimp feed in the current beside the sill, facing upstream and slipping backward in unison whenever the water quickens. In the deepest gutter lives a flat fish the color of a thumbprint. It lies still while the shrimp pass over it, then twists so quickly that for a moment all you see is its clean white belly.

The frogs are loudest after a fall in the water. They sit inside the old pots and call into the hollows behind them. One frog can fill the room. Two calling from opposite banks make it hard to tell how wide the room is, or even which side of the threshold you are standing on. There is a jar somewhere in the wall that answers a little late. It answers the frogs, the slap of the sluice, sometimes a footstep; but a voice gets nothing from it.

Toward evening the strip of light climbs out of the marsh, leaving the upper wall faintly visible and the water black. This is when the smallest animals come off the bricks. Their backs catch what little light is left, and for a while the channel looks full of drifting grit. Look longer and you can see that some of it is going against the current. At the sill each creature has to cross a bare span of clay, scarcely the length of a hand. They go one at a time. Behind them, in the dark of the room, the frogs make a noise you can hear from well outside the walls.
