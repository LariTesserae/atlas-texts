The town fits inside the bend of a low stone bank. From the bank you can see every roof, though once you go down among them it takes a while to find your bearings. The lanes are barely wide enough for two people to pass, and where a roof has been weighted with particularly large stones, the house opposite gives a little at the shoulder to make room for the overhang. There are no yards. A woman cleaning a pot sets it on her doorstep and stands in the street to do it.

The stone is young. A winter flood tore the bank open within living memory, and the blocks that came out of it still have edges sharp enough to take skin off a knuckle. The lower courses of the houses are made from those blocks, fitted with chips and thin wedges of oak. Above them are walls of clay and plaited rush. In the cool daylight you can see which parts of a wall were patched by which hands: some smooth the clay with the heel of the palm, others leave four parallel strokes.

Water is never far enough down to forget about. It wells through the joints in the paving and runs along grooves pecked into the thresholds. Each household keeps its own few feet of gutter clear, but all the gutters belong to one system, and children learn its branches the way they learn who is related to whom. At the bottom of the town, a slab with a hole in it lets the water out into a shallow reach of the marsh. When the marsh rises, the hole lets it back in. People put their baskets up on pegs and go on with what they were doing.

Little grows inside the bank. Two tufts of sedge have rooted in the steps to the landing, and under a communal bench there is a pale moss that comes away like wet felt. A snail sometimes crosses the lane there. Its silver track lasts until the next seep reaches it. Outside, the marsh is mostly open mud and standing water; a bird can walk a long way in plain sight. When one calls, people look up, not because the call is strange but because there have been so few calls all morning.

The town is quiet enough that a loose paving stone is known by its note. Even the work of shaping stone is done in short spells: three or four blows, then a pause while someone turns a block or feels for the new edge. Most of the sound comes from water falling distances no longer than a finger. In close rooms it is possible to hear it falling in the house next door.

There is one oddity about the seep. For a little while before rain, it runs uphill in the shallowest grooves. It does not climb steps or fill vessels; it simply takes the wrong fork at certain junctions and wets a doorstep that ought to be dry. Nobody has found a use for this except the pot-cleaner, who brings her pot in when the water touches her left heel. By the time the rain begins, the grooves are carrying water the usual way again, and the whole packed town is at work beneath its roofs, passing the weather from one threshold to the next.
