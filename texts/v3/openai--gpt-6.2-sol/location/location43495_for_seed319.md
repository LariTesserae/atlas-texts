The city occupies an old shelf in the mountainside. From the valley it looks like a retaining wall with a skyline; from the shelf itself you can walk half an hour on level ground and forget there is a drop. Then a cross street ends at a waist-high parapet, and below it the roofs of another district shine through the warm haze. Beyond those roofs lie the settling ponds, pale green and rectangular, and beyond the ponds the valley opens far enough for the next ridge to have weather of its own.

It rains often, but the streets are usually wet before it rains. Water comes out of the mountain at the backs of buildings. It beads on the mortar, gathers at the bottom of stairwells, and passes under the pavement in stone channels that were there before the pavement. Most doorways have a little bridge in front of them, just wide enough for a pair of feet. On newer streets the bridges are cast iron; on the oldest they are single slabs worn hollow in the middle. After a shower you can tell which water fell from the sky because it runs brown for an hour. The water from the mountain stays clear.

There is a great deal of city here and not much crowd. A broad avenue will have an occupied tea shop, seventy yards of locked enamel doors, then a municipal washhouse with every window open. The tram stops under roofs long enough to shelter four cars, though the tram has only one. Seen from above, much of what looks like building turns out to be empty floor: loading courts, obsolete platforms, the fenced outlines of tanks. The open spaces reflect a plain white afternoon light. In the shade under the loading roofs it is warm enough that a wet shirt never quite cools against your back.

The sound is extraordinary. Below the shelf, water passes through the old powerworks, and the powerworks and the mountain make a noise together that neither would make alone. The lowest note comes up through the paving; the higher part pours out of the grilles along the foot of the retaining wall. At the tram stop, people lean close to speak and give up if the sentence is going to be long. The tram itself arrives in near silence: you see the wet wheels turning, and a spoon walks to the edge of its saucer in the tea shop, but you don't hear the car.

Inside certain buildings the sound all but disappears. It has nothing to do with the thickness of the walls. The washhouse goes quiet between the second and third basins, in a strip so narrow a tall person can stand with one ear in it and one ear out. A woman who works there takes her lunch on a stool in that strip. She can hear the paper come off her sandwich. The position of the stool is marked on the floor, not with paint but with three worn circles in the tile.

Almost nothing grows higher than a boot. Rust-colored moss takes the undersides of the doorway bridges, where it can stay wet without being scoured by rain. A few wiry plants live in the joints atop the parapets, all bent toward the city. In the warmest drains there are little translucent snails; the people who clean the channels lift them out with a spoon and put them back when the work is done. A bird on a roof is enough to make someone stop and look.

No one expects the mountain to fall. Chunks do come off, but rarely, and the scars are dull by the time anyone thinks to point them out. The real wear is lower down, at the height of wheels and hands and running water. Where a drain crosses the old market, it has eaten deep enough into the bedrock that the stallholders need a hook to retrieve a dropped coin. The drain is older than the market. It probably began as a groove on an exposed slope, before the first retaining wall made that slope into a place where you could set down a table.

Toward evening, in the topmost settling pond, a maintenance worker lifts a gate by one tooth. You can watch the pond for twenty minutes without seeing the level change. By morning a band of pale mineral will show around the entire basin, the width of a thumbnail. There are bands like it on the walls all down the valley. Some are level, some have tilted with the ground, and some run straight into the foundations of houses where people are getting up, setting cups on tables, and putting a hand to the cupboard door to keep it from rattling.
