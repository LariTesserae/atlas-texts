The town is built along the highest ridge in the valley. There is water over the ridge, water over the tallest roofs, and another forty fathoms of it before the light begins to look like daylight. From the upper terraces you can see the roofs descending on either side, then the gardens, then the pale backs of ridges no one has built on yet. The valley floor is too far below to see. At the edge of town a white road goes down toward it, keeping its width long after it has lost its paving.

The mountain is new. It has long faces of green-black stone with hardly a nick in them, and seams of soft white stuff that takes the print of a thumb. The masons cut their blocks from those seams with shell wedges. A cut left overnight will sometimes close far enough to hold the wedges fast. In the older streets, where the paving has been down for three or four generations, dark points of native rock have begun to come through the joints. Children use them as footholds. The council has agreed to leave them until they lift a whole slab.

The water is warm enough that you notice a change of temperature only when you pass a doorway. Most houses are cool inside, roofed with thick mats of living weed laid over ribs of stone. The weed puts out broad bronze leaves that hang level in the still water. People trim them above the lanes, where a long leaf can catch a basket or brush the meal out of somebody’s hands. Down the sunny slope the same plant grows in terraces, the roots bound to low walls with plaited cord. Its young shoots are eaten. Its old roots make a good broom. When a bed is ready to be pulled, the whole household goes out to do it; the roots have usually got into the wall by then, and someone has to sit on the top course while the others tug.

For a town with so many adjoining walls, it is remarkably open. You can take the steps or leave them. In the market, people come and go over the low partitions, catching themselves on the stall posts to stop. The weights on the balance scales are pierced so they can be tied down. There is a bronze bell at the lower gate, but its note travels so well through the ridge that it is rung only when someone is lost. From an upstairs room you are more likely to hear the small knock of a pestle from two houses away, carried through the continuous stone of the foundations.

Few animals come up this high. Now and then a translucent fish hangs under the eaves, just where the trimmed leaves end. There are burrows the width of a wrist in the white banks outside town, and occasionally a fresh fan of grit below one, though no one seems to have seen what lives in them. The gardens go for years without losing a leaf to anything but a knife. When a shoal does come over the ridge, people stop along the lanes to watch it pass. The fish cast faint moving shadows on the paving, even at noon.

At the north end the ridge rises into a broad, unroofed bench. The water there is a little warmer. Each year the people who keep the bench clear find a new length of stone beyond their boundary rope. They move the rope out, scrape away the first threads of weed, and measure the addition in handspans. At the far edge, where the rock is still soft, it takes the shapes of things that rest against it. There is a flawless impression of a knotted rope there, and beside it the shallow print of a shoulder. A mason once left a basket overnight and had to unweave it to get it back.

They are laying out a second square on the bench. It has a well by custom, though there is no need to draw water, and the well is the only part they have finished. It is a round opening in the new stone, lined a little way down with blocks. Lean over it and the warmer water comes up against your face. Sometimes a white grain rises out of it, pauses at the lip, and goes on past you toward the dim surface. You can watch it a long time before you lose it.
