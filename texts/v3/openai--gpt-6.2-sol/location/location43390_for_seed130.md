There is no earth beneath the water. In the shallows you can see the joints: narrow black seams between slabs, each slab wide enough to have carried a street. The edges have worn down until the seams are the only straight things left. In places even those are gone. Water passes from one slab to the next without a ripple.

The plain is low enough that a rise of two steps changes what you can see. From the top of an old loading platform, the roofs of three pumping sheds come into view, each a mile or so from the next. Their roofs are barely higher than the platform. Between them stand isolated pieces of taller buildings: the bottom of a stair, a doorway with no wall above it, a row of glazed pillars with iron running out of them in thin brown stains. Whatever stood here has worn away almost to the level at which it was built. The bolts in the pavement have broad, smooth heads. Rain gathers in the impressions left by washers.

It is dark enough that a pool can look like an opening in the floor. The distinction usually becomes clear when a light comes on in one of the sheds and travels a little way across the water. These lights are the color of heated wire. You can sometimes make out rain in them, though most of the wet seems to come from nearer at hand: beads swelling on a handrail, a slow seep through the side of a column, droplets on the *underside* of every projecting lip. The cold is steady. A hand laid flat on a slab leaves a dry print that closes from the edges in.

The noise is tremendous. At first it seems to come from the sheds, but when you get close to one you find you can hear its own machinery quite distinctly: a belt slapping at regular intervals, a little knock when a valve seats. The larger sound comes through the floor. Something underneath the whole plain is moving water in quantities the surface gives no hint of. One stroke is long enough to walk twenty paces in. At the end of it there is a moment when you can hear the rain, and then the next stroke takes the rain away. People once put felt in the hollows of the railings to stop them singing. You can still find a tuft of it here and there, black and hard as a seed.

Things grow on what the machines bring up. There are short rows of pale stems along the warm side of a discharge slot, and flat green skins on the ceramic where a leak has kept the same course for years. They are widely separated. In a long walk you might pass two patches of moss and a single plant with leaves large enough to turn over. There are no bites in the leaves. The puddles have no small movements of their own. When something breaks the surface it is a bubble from a joint, or a curl of scale lifting off the metal beneath.

The plain does not flood evenly. Every few hours a shallow reach goes dry in the middle first, leaving a margin of water standing along both sides. The exposed slabs shine, then dull as they cool. Far off, another reach fills without any visible inflow. Marks cut into the sides of the sheds record levels much higher than these, though the upper marks are so rounded that they collect no dirt. Nothing now seems capable of reaching them.

At the far end of the platform there is a flight of stairs with only three treads left. The lowest is level with the plain. Water creeps over it as the pumps come onto their next stroke, and for a while all three treads are visible by the faint light of a shed. Then the light goes out. The water keeps climbing, too slowly to see in the dark, until it touches the toe of your boot.
