The valley is still deciding which side is down.

Along its western rim, a dropped stone falls out of sight. Along the eastern rim it hangs for a season or two, then comes to rest against the underside of a ledge. The old footpath keeps to the middle, where things generally fall at your feet. It is marked with flat white stones, each with a shallow cup pecked into it. You can follow these cups by touch when the light fails. None has ever held water.

It is very high country. From the rim you can see three more valleys, each pale and enormous, and beyond them a black plain so far below that the peaks rising from it look like grit. There are no clouds to give a sense of the intervening distance. The small light in the sky lends a little color to things close at hand: purple to a fresh break in the rock, green to the thick leaves. In the distance even the green places look like holes.

They are lush where they occur. A single bed of growth might fill a hollow fifty paces across, its leaves stacked so densely that the lower ones grow almost white. There are broad fans with ribs you could split for basket splints, and small folded leaves that smell of struck flint when bruised. Underneath is no soil, only the fine, cold flour of the new stone. The roots go into it as flat black ribbons. At the far side of a bed you can sometimes see them emerge from solid rock without a crack around them, and go in again a little farther on.

Between beds there may be half a mile of bare mineral. Nothing crosses it except a shadow, if there is one. There are no insects under the leaves, no droppings on the high shelves. The curled husks you find in the hollows were made by the plants themselves. Split one and it is green all the way through.

The noise is worst on the bare ground. It is too loud to shout over, though it has no direction and raises no dust. The valley is making stone. A new face may take a hundred winters to come out of the slope, but you hear the whole thickness of it at once: a continuous low tearing, with a higher note that makes the small bones behind your ears ache. Put both hands against the ground and the sound stops. You can hear your sleeve move. Lift one finger and the valley comes back at full volume.

There are two shelters on the path, low walls of fitted stone with roofs of leaf ribs. In the farther one someone has left a hammerstone and a deep basket. The basket is worn through at the bottom. The shelter is too low to stand in, and its builders made a bench wide enough to lie on, with a slight rise at one end. On the wall facing the bench are three parallel scratches, then two, then one. The same marks are on the outside of the wall, but on that side they can be seen only while the wall is between you and the light.

Beyond the second shelter the trail ends at a raw lip. You can see its continuation well out over the empty valley: six or seven white stones in a mild curve, each cup worn smooth by hands. On some visits there is ground beneath the first stone. On others there is less ground than you remember. The custom is to set a marker only where the rock will bear your weight, and the cups are cut after the markers are laid. No one has found a useful exception to that rule.

Near the lip, a bed of green is putting out a new leaf. Its tip has unrolled to the width of a thumbnail; the rest is still tight and pale. If you sit beside it all day, you won't see it move. But when you get up to go, there is a shadow under the tip that wasn't there before, falling clear of the ledge onto something that has not yet come into view.
