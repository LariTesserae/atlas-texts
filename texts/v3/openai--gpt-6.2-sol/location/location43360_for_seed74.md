The ward is six streets wide and lies low enough that, from the last intersection, you can see the fields on either side of it. Rain darkens the pavement without quite making puddles. The pavement is alive: a close-grained mat of roots that has thickened over generations into something like cork. Where the delivery carts used to turn, it has a sheen. In the hollows left by their wheels, small leaves lie flat with their pale undersides up.

There are few trees, or few things you would stand back and count as trees. Most of the plant life is horizontal. It makes the roads, the banks, the long low walls of the workshops. Between buildings there is a great deal of sky. Even in the narrowest street, you can step away from an eave and feel ordinary daylight on your face. The cool comes chiefly from below. Put a hand on a sunny wall and it will be warm at the surface, cold in the deeper cracks.

A foundry once stood at the center of the ward. Its loading yard is still the largest open space, and the root pavement there has preserved the two grooves of a weighbridge long after the bridge itself rusted away. On the north side stands a working pump house. Its cast-iron motor turns twice an hour, drawing seepage from beneath the yard and sending it uphill the modest height of a doorstep. From there the water travels by itself, through shallow gutters grown from the same stock as the walls. It reaches the houses in order. You can tell how far it has got by which thresholds are dark.

The buildings look trimmed rather than ruined. Window sills have rounded off where people rested their arms. A stair behind the pump house has lost an entire tread, not to collapse but to the slow widening of the one below it. Every spring someone saws a finger’s width off the doors. The offcuts are stacked in the dry goods shed, where they keep trying to leaf in the dark.

There is motion almost everywhere, though little of it catches the eye directly. A parapet that shed its rain inward yesterday sheds it into the street today. Leaf buds along the tram bed open in the wake of the morning sun, while the older leaves fold back to give them room. By dusk a hairline break in a gutter may have closed enough to send the evening water down a different street. The pump keeper makes a round after the last run, moving a few slate weights from lip to lip. Left alone, the ward would still drain; it would simply change its mind about which houses ought to stay dry.

Once in a while a wren comes in from the fields. Its voice is startlingly loud. The motor, by contrast, is more easily felt than heard: a faint tremor in the soles, then a pause while the water climbs. In that pause you can hear drops falling inside the walls. Stand there long enough and one of them will reach the street, travel a yard or two along a seam, and disappear into a surface that a moment later looks quite dry.
