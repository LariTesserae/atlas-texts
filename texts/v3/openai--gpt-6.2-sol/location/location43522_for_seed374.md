The city stands a handspan above the water. That is enough. Its whole extent, from the close-packed houses to the last low walls on the plain, has been built to that measure. At the doors a single step brings you out of the warm shallows and onto dry clay. Then, every dozen doors or so, the pavement dips and the water takes the street back.

Beyond the city there is very little rise in the land. You can see a long way, though there is seldom enough light to see clearly. The sky is a pale grey without a bright part. Far out on the flats, a line of dark objects looks like another district until one of them puts its head down and the others follow. They are large, flat-backed animals that stand in the water with their bellies just touching it. Where they have been feeding, the surface is full of little rings.

There is nothing green. On the open ground, white silt lies in slabs with wet seams between them. Some slabs have buckled and show an underside as smooth as the inside of a bowl. Black stone comes through in places, with edges sharp enough to cut a bare heel. Neither the silt nor the stone has had much taken off it. There are no gullies, no banks worn back into shelves. Water has found its level here without finding much of a way downhill.

The houses are low and thick-walled. Most are made of clay pressed around fragments of shell, and if you put your hand on a wall you can feel the shell holding the day’s warmth a little longer than the clay. The builders work with cobbles, wedges of bone, and lengths of braided hide. A heavy roof is made in short spans: stone laid from wall to pier, then from pier to wall. Under one roof you may pass through six rooms without ever having space to spread both arms. Then a doorway gives straight onto the flats, and there is nothing in front of you for miles but shallow water and the backs of animals.

The noise is worst in those little rooms. All along the street, blunt-headed amphibians have wedged themselves into the joints at the foot of the walls. Their calls are low enough to feel in the hollows behind your knees. Above them, small hard-shelled creatures make a dry clatter from the undersides of the roof stones. There are so many of both that the ear cannot separate one call from the next. In the open, the sound spreads out; under a lintel it comes at you from either side and from the stone just over your head. People speak close to the cheek. At a distance, they hold up what they want to talk about.

The animals eat one another, and nearly everything eats the soft amber film that forms where warm seepage comes up through the mineral floor. You can scrape a patch clean with your thumbnail. Sometimes a creature no bigger than a fingernail is already working at the other end of the patch, drawing itself along on a broad translucent foot. In the deeper street crossings there are fish. You mostly see their mouths, opening at the surface beside the ankles of people waiting to cross.

Things are always being made, but the city does not grow. A woman kneels on a threshold and presses clay into a place where someone’s heel has loosened it. Two doors down, a man lifts a roof slab with a stone lever so a child can push a fresh shim under it. The silt that comes up between the paving stones is scooped into bowls and carried to the walls. Taken together, this work keeps every passage just wide enough and every threshold just high enough. There are corners that look newly finished and have looked newly finished as long as anyone can remember.

Toward the outskirts the houses stand farther apart. Between them are bare squares of wet clay, remarkably level, on which another hundred houses might be set. Nobody has built there. The animals cross these squares every evening, laying one set of tracks over the last until all the impressions are the same depth. Water gathers in them overnight. In the dim morning there are a hundred little reflections of the sky, with the unmarked clay between them still warm.
