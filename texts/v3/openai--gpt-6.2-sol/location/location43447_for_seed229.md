The grove fits inside the broken crown of a mountain. You can walk its entire rim in the time it takes a grasshopper to work a seed out of its husk. From up there you see six trees, three low walls, and a great deal of bare stone. Then you climb down among the trees and discover that each one is a thicket big enough to get lost in. The foliage starts at knee height and goes up in overlapping shelves, broad purple leaves over little bronze ones, with strings of pale seed bells dangling in the gaps. The trees do not touch. Between them are wide lanes of warm, empty rock.

There is no water here, even under the stones. If you break a twig, a clear amber paste pushes out in a short curl and stiffens. On windless days the leaves can be heard taking up the oily vapor that rises through hairline cracks in the floor. They swell a little as they feed. The skin of an old leaf has the grain of a thumbprint, and scales of it come away on your fingers, fragrant as orange peel. It is dim enough beneath the canopies that you find yourself locating each tree by smell before you can make out its trunk.

Wind arrives from below. It comes up the outside of the mountain and folds over the rim, sometimes from three sides at once. Whole shelves of foliage tip upright. For a moment there are openings all the way through a tree, and you see the small animals in it hanging on: the plump seed-eaters with their four black feet, the long paper-colored things that run on the undersides of branches, the grazers with faces like split walnuts. Then the leaves bang flat again. A gust can empty a tree of animals and fill the next one. Crossing the open stone, they cast a multitude of little shadows in a light scarcely strong enough to cast yours.

The loudest sound comes from the seed bells. There are hundreds on a branch, and each has a loose stone inside it that grows there. In a steady wind you can hear the difference between trees: one knocks like tools in a basket, another has a high, cracked note that cuts through everything. When the gusts collide, the notes come apart into a din so complete you can watch a creature cry out at your feet without hearing it. Once in a while the stone answers with a low report felt through both heels. Nothing breaks. The floor has had a long time to find a shape it can keep.

One of the walls is only two courses high. It follows no boundary you can see, but in its lee there is a handspan of still air where the finest dust has settled into soft parallel ridges. Someone has left a hammerstone there, cupped on one face by years of striking. The seed-eaters bring their hardest husks to that spot. You can see where they brace them against the wall and work at the seams. Bits of shell lie everywhere on the sheltered side; on the other there isn't so much as a grain.

Late in the day the vapor ceases to rise. The leaves tuck their edges in, row by row, until the grove looks smaller and the bare lanes wider. This is when the grazers come out in numbers. They stand well apart, each facing a crack, and wait for the first brittle curl of the evening’s growth. On the rim, an animal with a long loose tail leans out over the drop. The wind has gone on roaring below, and the tail streams straight up. Down among the trees there is just enough quiet to hear the grazers bite.
