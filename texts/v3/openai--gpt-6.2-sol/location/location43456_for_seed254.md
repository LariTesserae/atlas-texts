The whole district fits beneath one railway yard. From the foot of the service stairs you can see its far wall, though you have to know which of the dark verticals is a wall and which is a row of planting beds seen end-on. At street level it would be half a block. Down here it has a main road, two numbered lanes, a pumping station, and a footbridge that saves you perhaps twelve steps.

Warm water comes out of the brickwork. Along the oldest wall it seeps through the mortar in beads that join and run down behind the ferns. Along the newer wall it comes through a single joint in a clear sheet. Both walls have gutters at their feet, but the older gutter has worn to a shallow curve and carries water more quietly. Someone has painted a white line above the highest recorded level. The line disappears behind the moss and comes out again on the door of the switch room.

There is hardly a bare surface in the district except where something regularly touches it. The stair rail is bright at the turns. The treads have a clean crescent on their outer edges where boots go to avoid the wet center. Between the lanes stand long, deep beds of sedge, their roots knitted into a mat thick enough to hold the gravel in place when the beds are flooded. Ferns grow in the gaps below the pipe brackets. Liverwort covers the sides of the settling tanks down to the waterline, where it gives way to a dark fringe of algae. Even the brick piers appear to have been made green all the way through, until you find a corner knocked off by a handcart and see the dry red inside.

The grow lamps hang low, just above the beds. They light the plants well enough and leave the walkways dim. Between one lamp and the next, you pass through the shadow of leaves you could reach out and fold in your hand. The planting is dense enough that a dropped washer may not reach the water. It lies caught among the stems, shining for a day or two, then tarnishes. There are no flies about the tanks, no snails on the wet brick. You can lean on the footbridge and watch the same patch of water for several minutes without seeing anything move except what the current moves.

It is a loud place. Two old pumps run side by side at slightly different speeds, and the difference comes and goes in a beat you can feel through the bridge deck. Water drops from the upper tanks into the lower ones with a continuous sound like a load of gravel being poured. At the switch room a loose conduit rattles whenever the bigger pump takes up its load. The lanes bend around the tanks, and in one of those bends the noise falls away enough that you can hear drops striking individual leaves. A step farther on, the pumps drown them out again.

Nothing here looks recently overgrown. The beds were built wide enough for the sedge to bow without blocking the lanes, and where a fern does hang across the road, its outer fronds are blunt from brushing against passing coats. The channels have been patched in several generations of cement. In places the water has worn away a patch and returned to a groove it cut in the brick before the repair. The district changes at that pace. A bed thickens enough over a few years to send more water down one side than the other; someone clears the inlet, and the old channel fills again.

At the foot of the stairs there is a little landing with a bench too damp to sit on. From there the place seems much larger than it is. The lamps over the far beds look like lights down a street in rain, and the water on the main road catches each of them in turn. Going back up, you lose the view well before you lose the sound. Near the top, where the air cools and the concrete dries, the two pumps are still beating against each other under your feet.
