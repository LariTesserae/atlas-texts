The high room is eleven paces long. You enter it sideways, through a joint between two blocks, and for the first few breaths you think there is machinery behind the walls. The noise is that great. It fills your mouth. If you put a thumbnail against your front teeth you can feel it there, though the floor under your feet is perfectly still.

The room is the top of a tower, and the tower is made all the way down. There is no hill inside it. Through the entrance joint you can see, far below, the tops of other courses projecting a little from the shaft. The masons laid each course inward by a thumb’s breadth until there was scarcely enough tower left to put a room on. The last course is the room.

Very little of it is available to stand in. The floor rises in low shelves, and the shelves carry ranks of short clay pipes whose mouths come almost to your knees. Between the pipes are wedges, and between the wedges are thin leaves of beaten copper, and the remaining spaces were thumbed full of a black paste that has long since become as hard as the clay. There is one crooked way through this, paved with bricks laid end up. Their exposed ends have worn into shallow cups, but there is nothing in the cups. Not dust. Whoever last swept the place did not leave so much as a broom straw.

The warm air comes up through the pipes. It is not a breeze you can feel on your face. Hold a hand over a mouth, though, and the sound drops just enough to disclose the next pipe, a finger’s width away, making nearly the same note. There are hundreds of them. Some have a broken edge that looks like damage until you find the same break on every seventh pipe, taken out with three careful blows. At the far end the pipes turn into the wall, where the mouths are upright slits you could slip a copper leaf into. Those make the low sound. It is hard to credit such little openings with a sound that seems to require the whole height of the tower.

Light reaches the room along a narrow passage in the roof. You can trace it from one pale brick to the next as it turns two corners, each brick a little less bright than the one before. On the floor it is just enough to show the color of the clay, a dull red with white grains in it. A patch that might be lichen is part of a mason’s palm print. The heel of the hand went in deep; the fingers merely brushed. Here and there a thread of copper has darkened to purple. That is all the color you get.

There is no loose edge left for the air to worry at. The sharp work wore away so long ago that it is difficult to tell a worn surface from one that was made round. Even the narrow path has the look of something finished rather than something used up. If a person used to come here to tend the pipes, they could have sat on the low shelf by the entrance. It has a flat place large enough for that. From there, with your head against the warm jamb, you can look out over the tower’s diminishing ledges and see how high you are. Then you turn back and the whole place fits within the reach of a thrown brick.

You cannot hear your footsteps on the way out. You know you have crossed the room by the little changes underfoot: the cupped bricks, then a smooth one, then the raised seam at the door. There is a stretch in the entrance joint where the high notes go away and the low note remains. It is the first quiet you have had since you came in. It is still too loud to speak over.
