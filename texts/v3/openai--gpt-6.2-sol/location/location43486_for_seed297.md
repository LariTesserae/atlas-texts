The plain is low enough that a ridge the height of your ankle can hide its far side. There are miles of such ridges, knife-edged folds in a pale mineral crust, with dark vegetation packed into the level spaces between. From a distance they look like lines drawn through the green. Up close, each edge is serrated with tiny clear teeth. Nothing has worn them down.

The country has the look of land newly exposed, though there is no former sea to account for it. There is no water under the crust, either. Break a leaf and the two halves remain dry. Inside are thin overlapping plates, green on one face and coppery on the other; tilt them in the sun and the green travels inward through the leaf, disappearing into layers too fine to pull apart. There are broad leaves, jointed canes, and low branching growths so thick that you have to part them with both hands. They have no scent until rubbed, and then smell faintly of a warm coin.

The light is ordinary. It warms the back of your neck. You can pick out individual stems a long way off where the sun strikes them from the side. Here and there the vegetation gives way to acres of bare crust, cream-colored and very bright, and there are dark groves beyond those openings, and further openings beyond the groves. Nothing interrupts the view at the height where a roof would be.

It takes some walking to notice what the plants are doing with distance. Two stems may stand a finger’s width apart, with an entire thicket visible through the gap. Go around either stem and the thicket is not behind it. Look through the gap again and there it is, down to the little patch of cream earth at its center. If you reach between the stems, your hand casts a shadow on that patch. It does not touch the ground.

This is how the plain holds so much growth without shading itself out. Some leaves have their undersides turned toward open ground half a mile away. The smallest veins do the same work as the stems, admitting light from places with nothing but more leaf on either side. There is a precision to it that you would call engineered if there were so much as a discarded thing on the plain. But there are no seams in the crust, no cut ends, no place where the arrangement begins. Even a chip you pry loose with your heel has a second, much farther edge visible through its thickness.

The loudest sound for a long while is the chip coming free. The plants scarcely rustle. Once, in the lee of a fold, a gray animal the size of a hare steps out far enough to put its narrow forefeet on the bare mineral. It pauses and raises a flap of skin along its flank to the sun. There is a faint tick as the flap closes. Then it goes into the leaves, showing you how easily something that lives here can find room to pass.

Toward what ought to be evening, the low sun is still where it was when you arrived. It already stands at an angle that gives everything a good long shadow. The warmth has not deepened or ebbed. You come across a cane bowed almost double beneath a shelf of leaves, and under it a perfectly clean space where there ought to be a litter of old ones. The cane isn’t struggling to lift itself. Nothing is waiting to fall.

On the way back you find the chip lying where you left it, beside the small white notch. You can still do things here; the plain need not undo them. It will keep the notch as readily as it has kept the fold, with all its clear teeth. Through the chip’s far edge you can see a sunlit patch among the stems. After a moment you recognize the patch by the gray animal’s two little prints, sharp at the toes, in the dust.
