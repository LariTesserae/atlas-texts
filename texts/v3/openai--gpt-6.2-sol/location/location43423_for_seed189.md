Under the roots of a tree that has not yet come up, there is a room just large enough to lie down in. No soil shows on its walls. The floor is the flat of a single leaf, folded under at the edges, and the ceiling is a brown sheath with a green seam that has been opening all afternoon. Where the seam parts, the new edges are sharp enough to catch on skin. Nothing has lived here long enough to wear them smooth.

It is warm as the inside of a sleeve. Most of the leaf is dry, but in one corner a bead of water hangs from the ceiling. From time to time it falls, hits the floor with a crack like a split branch, and hangs from the ceiling again. There is no wet spot beneath it. A little farther along, where nothing ever drips, the floor is damp.

The room is almost entirely empty. Everything alive in it is busy being a boundary. There are no shoots reaching out into the middle, no strings of root to duck under. Even the leaf veins swell downward, out of sight. Once in a long while a pale mite comes around the fold of the floor. It walks a short way out, stops, and feels ahead with its first pair of legs. Then it goes back. There is nothing visibly wrong with the ground it refuses.

The light would be unremarkable in a kitchen at noon. You can see the fine hairs on the brown sheath and the reddish crescent under a bruised thumbnail. But when a fold passes across the ceiling, it is the far side of the fold that goes into shadow, whichever side you stand on. The afternoon advances by these little passages of shade. For three or four of them the room smells of cut grass, though nothing has been cut. Then it smells of the warm inside of a pea pod.

Growing makes a tremendous noise down here. The seam unzips a finger’s breadth with a succession of dry reports. The floor buckles in the corner and lets out a low note that stays audible after the leaf lies flat again. Somewhere close beyond the wall a root pushes through something tough. You can follow its progress by ear until it seems to be coming straight through the room. Nothing crosses. The sound continues on the other side.

If you put your palm to the wall you can feel, through the living thickness, the grain of the dirt outside. In one place you can feel a pebble with a chipped edge. Leave your hand there long enough and the pebble warms. On taking your hand away you find a shallow impression of its chip in the wall. By evening, the impression has opened into a tiny green mouth. It takes in the room’s one drop of water, and for the first time all day there is a wet spot on the floor.

Aboveground, a little hump in the dirt splits. Two seed leaves come up with their backs pressed together. Below, the room gets longer by exactly the distance between them. For a while there is room to stretch your feet without touching anything.
