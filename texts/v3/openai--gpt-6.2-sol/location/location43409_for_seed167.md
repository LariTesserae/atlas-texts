The town occupies a shallow fold in the seabed. From one edge to the other the ground rises no higher than a standing person, but the streets follow every inch of that rise. They bend around beds of shell, draw together at the mouths of narrow gutters, and spread into broad courts where the current crosses the floor without lifting the silt. Beyond the last houses there is a mile or more of open mud. The water is warm there, too. Nothing in the town holds heat that the rest of the sea does not have.

There is no light from above. Once in a long while something small gives a blue flicker, and for that instant a wall looks white or a passing flank looks silver. These glimpses seldom join up. The wall is known by the way the current thins beside it, the fish by the pressure of its passing. In the tightest streets, a large animal rounding a corner sends a soft push of water ahead of itself. Creatures in its path tuck their feelers in before it arrives.

The houses are made of shells, though not shells brought from anywhere. The town began where colonies settled on one another. Each generation grew at the lip of the last, making eaves, low vaults, and, where two fronts met, little rooms with openings at both ends. Only the outermost finger’s breadth of a wall is alive. Behind it lie hundreds of old chambers, some packed with fine sediment, some still hollow and occupied. There are no right angles, but there are addresses of a sort. A shrimp returning from the flats passes several hundred openings that would fit it before backing into its own.

On the side that takes the current, the corners have worn round. The lower passages have grown wider over the years, hollowed by grit and by the bodies of animals traveling the same routes. Higher up, young shell closes lanes a fraction at a time. A passage may carry traffic for generations before narrowing to a slit through which only a ribbon worm can pass. The large animals turn aside a little earlier every year. Eventually their detour is the main street.

Almost nothing grows here that could be called a plant. A few pale threads trail from cracks, at first easy to mistake for roots; touch one and the whole length snaps into a burrow. The open courts look empty between visits. Under a skin of mud, though, brittle stars lie with their arms folded, and small clams feed through paired holes so close together that a broad patch of ground breathes as one. When a ray comes down to feed, the holes close ahead of it. They open again behind its tail, beginning at the far edge of the court.

Most of the work of the tide is done quietly. A loose valve taps against a wall until the water turns and seats it firmly in the silt. A crab feeling its way along a roof puts one leg through and spends a long time getting it out. In the deeper rooms there are fish that make a low sound at intervals. A fish several streets away will sometimes answer, but by then the first has moved on, and for a time the two calls proceed down different streets.

At the turn of the tide, the gutters bring in what has fallen through the water above: soft scraps, empty eggs, a few grains of sand from a coast no creature here has visited. The shell mouths open along whole blocks. For a little while it is hard to find an unoccupied surface. Then the flow steadies, the larger animals go back out over the flats, and the town seems to empty. Inside one of its oldest walls, a brooding shrimp fans her eggs. It takes most of the slack tide for the little cloud she raises to work its way through the wall and out into the street.
