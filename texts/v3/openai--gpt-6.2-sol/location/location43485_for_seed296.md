From the lower country, the mountain looks flat on top. This is true for nearly eleven miles. There are no pinnacles up there, no young rock breaking through. The highest ground is a series of broad, shallow dishes in which rain has been collecting, briefly and in almost the same places, for longer than there have been rivers below. The stone at the lip of the plateau has worn into a curve. You can stand on what feels like the shoulder of a hill and look down through two distinct layers of weather.

The town occupies the second dish from the west. You see its extent before you see a house: a mile of wet roofs laid close as scales, a few factory chimneys with nothing coming out of them, and the raised beds of a freight railway going dark into the grass. There is room on the plateau, but only here can foundations reach sound stone without going through forty feet of peat. In some lanes, opposing gutters overlap. A person on an upper floor can set a cup on the sill across the street, though the two buildings belong to different districts and it takes ten minutes to walk from one door to the other.

It rarely rains for a whole hour. More often the cloud settles low enough to snag on the grass, and for a while everything with a tip has a drop hanging from it. Water travels a remarkable distance without falling: along the undersides of rails, through the woolly stems of the terrace plants, down chains hung from the eaves. By afternoon the streets may be dry in the middle and still dripping at the edges. Outside town, where the dew collects in hollows no wider than a hand, the peat heaves and lets out a bubble. A dry week will leave these little pools black and empty, with the print of each sedge blade preserved on the bottom.

The vegetation is lush to the knee and, in sheltered places, to the chin. There are trees, but their trunks lie close to the ground and send branches uphill under the moss. In winter you can follow the green humps of a single tree across three terraced gardens. In summer there are so many leaves between the paving blocks that the older lanes look solid until you step into them. The street crews cut a passage through twice a year. They work with short knives, feeling ahead with their boots for the edges of the stone.

The factories make felt, paper, and a dark green glass that looks black until it breaks. They tend to be busiest on dim mornings, when there is enough condensed water in the cisterns to run the washing floors. At the paper mill, belts turn behind tall windows clouded white at the corners. It is possible to watch everyone on a shift move at once and hear only the wet slap of one belt whose seam is coming loose. The machinery stands on beds of old felt, replaced from time to time by the factory next door. Farther out, where the railway crosses the open peat, a loaded wagon makes a deep yielding sound that goes on for a little while after it has passed.

There are few animals. A pair of black birds lives by the freight sheds, and weeks can go by without both being seen on the same day. There are small pale moths in the roof gardens. On warm evenings they come out while there is still work going on below, holding to the sheltered side of the chimneys. Once in a while an animal comes up from the lower country. Its tracks seem enormous in the thin mud. People stop to look at them on the way to work; by the time they come home, the last print is usually full of water.

The plateau has one peculiarity that makes the surveying difficult. After a spell of low cloud, the shallow pools sometimes take a day or two to find their level. A pool may stand a finger's breadth higher on one side than on the other. It isn't the wind: the pollen on the surface stays where it fell. The millwrights make their levels with oil, and the oldest streets have drains on both sides. Children sail paper boats uphill in the gutters until the water remembers itself. Then all along the lane, one iron grating after another begins to tick.

Toward evening the cloud often lifts off the town without lifting farther out. The whole length of the plateau comes into view: the railway bending between the dishes, the far roofs with their rows of little tanks, the dark ground putting on its shine again. There is a brief light in which you can see water moving everywhere. It is creeping under the grass, spilling from one garden to the next, being lifted in cups on a chain to the top of the mill. At the rim, a thread of it leaves the stone. You can follow it for a few feet. Then it goes into the weather, and there is no sound from below.
