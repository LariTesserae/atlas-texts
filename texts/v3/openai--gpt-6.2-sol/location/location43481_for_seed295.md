The ridge is made of pots.

The largest are taller than a person, pitched on their sides, their mouths fitted into the shoulders of the next row. Smaller ones fill the gaps. Generations of roots have worked through the clay, but where a tree falls and lifts its bed with it, there is no rock underneath. There are more pots, packed with leaf mold, down to the dark arches that carry the ridge from one end of the valley to the other.

It is warm up here, even at night. The terraces keep the heat of the day and give it back against your calves as you walk. Ferns lean out over the high steps; their tips are wet though it hasn't rained for a week. Water collects on the undersides of the arches and falls, a drop at a time, into shallow dishes worn smooth by tongues. Between the dishes the clay is dry enough to powder underfoot.

You can stand at the upper edge and know the valley is open without seeing across it. A bank of frogs starts up far below, and the sound comes back from the opposite terraces a little thinner. Then the nearer frogs answer and there is no judging distance at all. Crickets fill the spaces in the call. Bats pour from the jars overhead, their wings making a soft, continuous sound like hands brushing a wall. On the broad stair, animals are going both ways: small, quick feet on the risers, the deliberate knock of hooves, a heavy body in the ferns waiting for you to pass. There is barely enough light to see where the leaves part. The place is deafening, and most of what moves through it cannot be seen.

Whatever the pots were first made to hold, they have mostly held animals. The narrow-necked ones have nests wedged just inside the lip. In the wide ones, generations of roosting birds have left enough soil for little warm-rooted trees. Beetles live under the flakes of clay that come off in a dry year. A good wet year brings moss over the flakes and joins them to the wall again. There are no doors to shut, no lights along the paths. Even the old stair stays open chiefly because so many feet take it.

Over a lifetime a terrace might sag the width of a finger. A root thickens in a seam and lifts one jar just enough for rain to reach the jar beneath it. Things that lived in the dry cavity move a little higher; things that need damp settle in. From the upper stair, after a long while, you can pick out a shallow spill of stars reflected in the lowest dishes. One reflection goes out, then the next, as something walks the row to drink.
