The plain is low enough that a fallen tree can turn the rain aside for fifty years. On one side of an old trunk the soil is black and tacky, with a fringe of sedges; on the other it is pale clay, hard enough to ring under a hoof. Farther out, where the trees give way to grass, these differences are visible as long, irregular bands. They mark the former courses of water that seldom runs now.

Most of the year there is no standing water to speak of. There is a little in the deep hoofprints under the figs, a little in the forks of the figs themselves. You can put your hand under a shelf of bark and bring it away wet, though the open ground is dry to the depth of a finger. When rain comes, it is warm and brief. The leaves go on dripping for hours after the sky has cleared. In the morning, dung beetles roll their burdens over clay that has already turned to dust.

It is hot well into the night. Under the canopy the darkness is nearly complete, and out on the grass flats the stars give enough light to locate the nearest trees but not to tell a path from a strip of bare clay. A herd crossing a flat shows first as an absence of stars at ground level. Then there is the smell of warm hide and torn grass. The animals walk for a long time, close enough together that you might expect a continuous drumming. What carries is a scuff, a breath, the little crack of a stem, and then another scuff some way off. The ground takes most of it.

By day the country is open enough to see several miles, but there is almost nowhere unoccupied. Weaverbird nests hang from the outer branches of the thorn trees. Lizards lie along the branches above them, spaced just beyond one another’s reach. Each patch of shade has its residents, and the shade moves slowly enough for them to follow it without getting up all at once. At the edge of the grass, ants carry the broken parts of a beetle into the ground; on the same patch of earth, a wasp digs a hole and a browsing animal lowers its mouth to the leaves. When the animal moves away, the ants are still carrying the beetle.

There are no roads or fences. Only when the air settles after midnight does a low mechanical note reach the plain from beyond its farthest trees. Some nights it has a second note beneath it. You can lose both by moving a few paces into the grass. Whatever runs out there runs until morning; here, the nearest thing to a regular shift is the hour just before dawn, when the bats come back to the figs and the first birds begin to move inside the leaves.

The banks of the old channels are rounded, with roots exposed so long that their upper surfaces have gone gray. A flood will come again, though perhaps not this year. It will lift the grass and lay it flat, fill the hollows, and carry clay into places that have been dry long enough to grow trees. For now, a thread of damp earth under the sedges is all that joins one bend to the next. The animals know where it is. In the darkest part of the night they come down the old banks, nose the leaves apart, and drink from holes that look empty until the mud around their feet begins to shine.
