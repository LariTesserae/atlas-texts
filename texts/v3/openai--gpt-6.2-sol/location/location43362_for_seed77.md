For most of a morning after rain, the plain is two colors. The broad surfaces dry to the color of flour; the seams between them stay brown. By noon the brown has narrowed to a line fine enough to lose when you stand up. Kneel again and there it is, still wet, with a bead of water moving along it at a pace you can just detect by holding a finger beside it.

The plain is made in stretches the size of fields. Each stretch has a very slight crown, enough to shed water but not enough to see from the ground. At the joins, one surface sometimes stands a thumb’s width above the next. The edges are square and tender. Grains come away if you rub them. There has not been enough rain yet to round these steps, or enough wind to carry their fallen grains far from where they fell.

What makes the ground lives mostly out of sight. A pale crust grows along the wet underside of each plate, laying down mineral in thin, close sheets. The loose powder exposed at an unfinished edge is its waste; farther in, the sheets are hard enough to ring faintly if struck. When a plate gets too broad to bear its own weight over the moist bed, it settles. Its margins tip up a little. Water gathers against them at the next rain, and the crust begins a new sheet there. Even the bed under the plates is made of older sheets, soaked and crushed by the ones above. The deepest material ever exposed, in a recent slump, showed the same fine layering as the fresh edge beside it.

There is very little to interrupt the view. A dark patch half a mile away might be a stand of thin jointed stems, or merely a place where the surface has not finished drying. The stems favor corners where three plates meet. Dust accumulates there in a wedge, and their roots hold enough of it to make a little soil. Some stands have lived long enough to leave a standing straw from the previous season among the green stems. There is no movement in them when the wind stops. Nothing comes down to the water.

The heat builds evenly until late morning. It is much the same heat on the ground as at the height of a face, and there is nowhere to step out of it. Yet at the bottom of a seam barely deep enough to take two fingers, the wall can be cool. A little farther down, where the opening pinches shut, it stays wet through all but the longest dry spells. This is how the crust survives afternoons when even the shallow pools are gone and the stems have folded their narrow leaves.

The plain is broad enough to raise its own clouds. Early in the afternoon they stand at intervals along the horizon, small and separate. Later some of them join. You can see the rain beneath them, a gray foot that widens and moves sideways, wetting ground that might stay dark for days while the place where you stand gets six drops. Under a passing cloud the light goes flat, and for a moment it becomes possible to see how many plates there are. In full sun their edges disappear in the glare. Near evening, when each raised lip casts a shadow, the pattern goes on farther than the eye can follow.

The loudest thing here is rain at the beginning of a shower, when the drops strike dry mineral one at a time. There may be a low ticking later as the cooling plates contract. Most of the changes leave no sound at all. A seam opens during the afternoon. Water slips into it after dark. By the next morning the water is gone from sight, though it will go on feeding the pale crust for weeks.

In the lower country, where several crowns drain toward the same place, a little runoff has begun to follow yesterday’s route. It crosses a plate on the diagonal, ignoring the joins, and ends in a shallow pool with no outlet. There is a fan of white grit at its mouth, coarse at the tip and nearly smooth at the far edge. The next rain may deepen the channel enough to keep it. Or the plate may settle and send the water elsewhere, leaving the fan high and dry. For now, it is the only patch of ground in sight that water has shaped more than once.
