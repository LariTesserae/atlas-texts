At the bottom of the service stairs, the heat is enough to warm the backs of your teeth. There are forty-three steps, then a landing, then four more steps that were cut after the landing wore through. The last one is green. From there you can see most of the chamber, though there is nowhere you can stand and see the whole floor.

It was once a condenser room. A cast-iron pipe still enters high on the east wall and makes a long, almost graceful turn before disappearing into the stone opposite. Below it are two brick piers and the square of paler rock where a machine stood. Someone has left a porcelain cup upside down on one pier. The handle has been enclosed by a fern root without being broken. The other pier is worn to a shallow saddle, as if for years somebody sat there to eat lunch.

There isn't enough water to drip steadily. It gathers first on the underside of the pipe in a row of bright, widely spaced beads. Most of them shrink away in the heat. A few grow heavy and fall onto leaves already dished by generations of falling water. Under those leaves the soil is black and cool enough to put a finger into. A handspan away it is dust. The plants go to considerable lengths to cross that handspan: fine roots run inside the mortar joints and through the hollow legs of a workbench, while thick, jointed stems arch over the dry ground without touching it. Some of the ferns have been growing in the same place so long that what looks like a low bank of earth is entirely their old roots.

A caged lamp at the foot of the stairs throws a dull apricot light. The plants nearest it are a deep, ordinary green. Farther in, even under the pipe where the leaves are thickest, they look gray until you turn one over. The undersides are dark red. If you keep your hand still beneath a frond, you can see that red by the frond's own light. It isn't enough to find your way by. It is enough to see the creases in your palm.

The noise comes from behind the west wall. It's a low industrial roar, with a rough edge that rises every few minutes and makes the cup on the pier tick against the brick. You have to raise your voice to speak to someone on the stairs, but at the far end of the chamber there is a recess where you can hear a bead of water let go. The roar continues there; it simply seems to have no bearing on what happens in the recess. The old workers knew about it. The wall at shoulder height is polished on both sides of the opening, and there is a little shelf cut just deep enough for two cups.

Nothing comes to drink. There are no gnats above the damp places, no threads across the corners, no white husks in the leaf litter. A fallen frond keeps the shape it had in life until another grows against it. Then the old frond folds along its ribs and settles a little farther into the floor. The surface underfoot has been made that way for a long time. In the dry gaps you can still see the original paving, its corners rounded, the joints widening in the direction of drains that have long since filled with roots.

Once in a while the pipe cools. Not much: you could still burn yourself on it. The beads along its underside get larger, and for an hour or two the leaves receive more water than they can hold. It runs down their stems to a shallow basin in the floor, where a level black pool forms. The pool reflects the pipe, the brick piers, and the ceiling overhead. It does not reflect the lamp. In its place there is a pale patch of daylight with the shadows of grass moving through it.

By the time the pipe warms again, the water has retreated to the lowest part of the basin. The last of the daylight goes with it. For weeks afterward the only sign that there was a pool at all is a dark line on the brick, just above the roots.
