From the upper road you can see the whole valley, including the roofs on the far side, though once you go down into it you can seldom see more than twenty paces. Alder grows shoulder to shoulder on the slopes. In the hollows the ferns are taller than a person, and their new fronds are so pale that they look sunlit even under all the other leaves. It is cold enough in the shade to keep your coat on. Out on the road the light is fierce, but it does very little to warm you.

The valley has not had time to soften. Where the vegetation breaks, the rock stands in long, clean faces, with ledges as thin as shelves. At their feet lie slabs that have fallen and not yet broken up. You can fit some of them back into the cliff by eye. Roots follow the seams for yards before finding a place to go in. Along one slope, a whole stand of birch grows at an angle, its roots still holding the thin black soil to a single tilted plate of stone.

There is no river to speak of. Rain comes once or twice a month, usually at night. The rest of the time moisture gathers on cold things: the undersides of slabs, the iron posts of the old freight line, the porcelain bells on the power poles. At noon you can find dust on the road and wet moss three inches below it. Water collects in the bolt holes of discarded machine beds. Each hole has its own small garden, a green collar with a dark center that holds a piece of sky.

The works are spread out. A brick winding house sits on the western shoulder; a corrugated shed shines among the trees half a mile below it. Between them, two cables cross a wide reach of open air. There are no hanging cars, but at the lower terminal a brass tally wheel advances by one tooth every few days. The shed is still swept. Somebody has set the broom on two nails to keep its bristles out of the damp. Through the window you can see a row of heavy motors, each on its own concrete plinth, and behind them a strip of sunlight moving down a wall painted the green of hospital furniture.

What you hear most often is what you have just done: a cuff catching on a leaf, grit shifting under a heel. Stop and it is quiet enough to hear a drop strike the hollow rail. There are no clouds of insects in the bright openings. Once in a while you find a snail on the warm side of a post, or the print of a small cloven hoof in the mud beneath a seep. A bird crosses the entire valley without calling. You can follow it almost to the other side.

In the late afternoon, the shadows of the freight cables fall across a bare white face of rock. One of the shadows has a sag that the cable above it does not. An old photograph in the winding house shows the line before it was tightened, and the curve is precisely the same. Each year the shadow draws a little straighter. The people who come up to grease the bearings have marked its progress on the rock with a series of small red dashes. The oldest dashes are disappearing under the first growth of lichen; the newest are still sharp enough to show where the brush ran dry.
