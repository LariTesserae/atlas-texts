At the bottom of the water there is a high country.

The mountains rise out of a basin so deep that their lower slopes have never been green. Up here, along ridges that come within a few arm lengths of the surface, afternoon light is strong enough to show the rust in the stone. You can look down the length of a valley and see individual pale trunks on the opposite slope. Above them, the surface runs nearly level from peak to peak. In a lull it reflects the forest upside down, with a slit of sky where the angle changes.

The stone is young. Along the crests it stands in thin, sharp fins, some black on one face and ocher on the other. Great wedges have split away without falling far; you can follow a white seam across the gap between a boulder and the face it came from. There is little gravel. In the clefts, where you might expect a wash of sand, there are pads of red roots with tiny green leaves growing straight out of them. The leaves are so close-set that the roots look upholstered, except at the growing edge, where each leaf has room to open.

Much of the forest grows sideways. Gray trunks come out of the slopes at right angles and divide again and again, carrying their crowns well clear of the rock. A crown can be twenty paces across and no thicker than a hand. Seen edge-on, it scarcely interrupts the view; from above, it is a solid patch of vivid green. In the ravines the crowns overlap by the dozen, with enough space between them for long amber fronds to hang. Water passes so slowly through these layers that a loose flake of bark can take the whole bright part of the day to sink from one crown to the next.

On the broadest saddle there is a meadow. Its grass has clear tips, swollen with sap, which lie flat against the blades in the morning. They fill as the light strengthens and lift the grass until it stands waist-high. By dusk the tips are empty again. The change is too slow to see in any one blade, but late in the day the shadows of the meadow reach halfway down the scarp, over stone that was lit at noon.

The water is warm even in the shade. Only at the mouths of the deep gullies is there a coolness, a narrow spill that can be traced for some distance by the way it draws the hanging fronds together. There is almost no sound. Now and then a pebble shifts under a root and makes a dry little tick that carries all the way across a hollow. Animals are scarce enough that the forest offers no general impression of them. In a whole valley there might be one fish, copper-backed, resting in the light between two crowns. On a shaded wall, chalk-colored mouths open a fraction as the cool water goes by. They are easy to mistake for scars left by fallen leaves.

Below the last trunks the slope is bare again. The boundary is surprisingly exact: one branch in full leaf, another a little farther down with nothing on it but buds. Those buds may wait years. The rock is still rising, though not quickly enough for a fault to look any different from one season to the next. On the highest crest a row of green shoots has reached the surface. They lie there with their tips bent back into the water, all but one. That one has opened a leaf in the air. It is smaller than the leaves beneath it, thick at the edges, and already drying in the sun.
