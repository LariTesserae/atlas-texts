The wettest place in the ridge is a room no wider than a freight elevator. It sits high inside the north abutment of the pumping station, behind a door that opens inward because the service passage is too narrow to stand aside. There is no exposed mountain here. The floor, the slopes, even the little ledge on which water gathers before it falls are cast concrete. A seam left by the formwork runs through the ledge and makes two waterfalls out of one.

The room is new enough that its corners have not softened. Water spills over them in sheets so thin they seem to continue the planes of the concrete into empty air. Below, the sheets break on a black steel grate. You can see through it to a sloping floor crossed by three shallow channels. Each channel carries a different amount of water, and the middle one sometimes runs dry for half a minute. When it fills again, the advancing edge picks up a line of gray dust left by the builders.

Nothing about this small drainage would account for the noise. The main discharge pipe passes behind the far wall, and the wall gives back its full length of thunder. There is a higher sound where a valve is throttled, and every so often a motor comes up to speed beneath the floor. You see the nearer waterfall split and rejoin without hearing any difference. If someone comes to the door, you know it by the change in light. There is no use calling to them.

A service bulb behind wired glass lights the lower half of the room. The ceiling is only a little above reach, but you have to stand for a while before you can make out the drops hanging there. The air is cool enough to take the warmth out of your hands on the rail. Now and then a damper opens somewhere up the ventilation shaft and brings down a breath of outside air, colder still. For those few seconds the bulb clears. Then the glass clouds over from its edges.

At the foot of a ladder, in the shelter of the lowest rung, a patch of green has taken hold. It is about the size of a thumbprint and looks black until the water thins over it. There is another patch on the rubber door seal, where a crease holds grit. Nothing moves between them. The little rafts that catch on the grate are shavings of sealant, not leaves.

The pumps change duty at intervals. The old motor winds down while the next is already running, and for a few seconds the two beats pass through one another. Water shivers off the rail. The channels below the grate keep their separate courses. Only after the new beat has settled does the middle one begin to fill, as though the room were farther from the machinery than the thickness of a wall.
