The plain is low enough that no part of the city overlooks any other. From the western platforms you can see twenty kilometres of roof gardens, red survey lamps, and unoccupied air. A single white stair descends from a roof to the ground without coming near a wall. Beyond it, a district of green terraces stands nearly edge-on to the light. The next district is far enough away for its windows to look like a cluster of eggs.

The land came up recently. Along the tram reservation, black slabs still have the sharp lips they broke with. Weather has made almost no impression on them, though roots have gone straight into the seams. A tree can stand taller than a station canopy here with its whole root system visible, the roots running over bare rock in flattened straps until they find one of the warm cracks. Cut a strap by accident and it bleeds a little clear water, then folds its edges in.

It rains briefly, sometimes only under a bridge. Most of the damp arrives toward the end of the dim afternoon, when the tram rails cool and beads gather along their undersides. Water travels the length of a rail without falling, joined bead to bead in a trembling line. The lowest blocks have gutters for it. In the unpaved squares, broad leaves tilt as the damp comes on, and the soil beneath them receives enough to stay dark all year. You can smell it from well out in the open: warm earth and the bruised skin of green fruit. Between the squares there are long reaches where nothing interrupts the stone except the occasional dry husk of an insect.

The city is deafening across its entire extent. A descending note from the ground sets the handrails thrumming; before it has finished, a higher one starts. They come from foundation works so widely spaced that you can walk half a day without seeing one. Their job is to keep the distances between certain addresses fixed. Left alone, a street on this plain will slowly acquire more middle. Both ends stay where the surveyors put them. A person walking the street simply takes longer and longer to arrive. The foundations correct for this in pulses, and in the instant between two pulses all the doors on the street lift slightly on their hinges.

At the public crossings, instructions are given by light. A blue strip means the far curb is as far away as it looks. There is an amber phase, seldom seen, during which small things may cross. People waiting at the curb watch beetles go ahead of them.

Some of the gardens are elaborate enough to have street names of their own. They make a heavy canopy over the galleries, with pendulous orange blossoms that open into the walkways in the absence of anyone to prune them. Everything that flies seems to know where that canopy begins. Moths gather beneath the leaves in such numbers that their folded wings look like a second growth. There are slender, hot-blooded animals that hunt them by running down the undersides of branches. At the garden’s edge a hunter will stop with its head and forefeet out over the plain. Nothing lies ahead of it for a kilometre or more. It turns back into the leaves.

The larger animals make crossings. They come out at the darkest hour, when the survey lamps are the brightest things in sight: herds of pale-backed grazers, widely spaced, going from terrace to terrace. The young put their feet in the prints of the adults even on rock that takes no print. Once across, the herd vanishes into a strip of planting no wider than a station platform. For a while the trees move hard enough to show the yellow undersides of their leaves. At ground level there is the occasional close shock of a hoof over the continuous hammering of the foundations. Then the leaves settle. On the open stone, even after the damp has dried, you can follow the crossing by the small green seeds carried out in the animals’ feet.
