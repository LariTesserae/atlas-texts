The pocket is below the floor of a quarry, behind a slab that looks too thin to have anything behind it. You turn sideways to get in. Once inside, you can stand upright, but only in the middle, and if you stretch out both arms you can put a hand in each of two entirely different thickets.

There is no dampness. What looks like a waterline along the wall is the pale edge of a layer of mica. The floor is loose salt, warm through the soles, and the dark furrows in it are not channels but shadows. The stone has the look of something split this morning. A flake no thicker than a fingernail projects from the wall at hip height; nobody has broken it off.

The plants grow in three places. Each growth is lavish enough to conceal the rock behind it, but between them there are yards of bare wall. The leaves are broad and feel like soft leather until you bend one, when it creases with the sound of thin copper. Down their undersides run white veins full of dry, loosely packed grains. A broken stalk shows the same grains, finer toward the tip. At the foot of each thicket, the roots spread out over the rock in flat overlapping fans, leaving a little clean space around every sharp projection. Nothing has had to force its way through anything else.

The light comes from a patch of stone low down, about the size of a hand. It is just enough to show that the innermost leaves are red. Higher up they go black, and the few pale animals among them seem to hang in empty air. There are plenty once your eyes adjust: little jointed things with blunt faces, one to a leaf or fork, and a larger animal tucked under a root with all but the last two rings of its tail in shadow. None turns to look. You can go out and come back and find the same small foot planted on the same vein.

The sound is tremendous. At the entrance you could take it for wind in the quarry, but in the pocket it is a close, steady clatter, like a basketful of fired beads being poured onto stone. There is no pause for the basket to empty. Put your ear next to a leaf and the leaf is quiet; put it to the floor and the floor is quiet. The noise is loudest in the clear spaces between the thickets. You have to shout to be heard across one of them. Outside the slab, even with your head still in the opening, you can hear someone scuff a boot at the far end of the quarry.

Someone has put a low stool just inside. Its legs are three unequal pieces of root, trimmed with a flake of stone, and whoever fitted them found exactly the place on the uneven floor where the seat would be level. Beside it is a shallow dish with a little heap of white grains in it. A single grain lies on the rim. There is room to sit without touching a plant. From there you can see a young leaf, still folded, held clear of all the others on a long bare stem. It is always the leaf you look for first when you come back.
