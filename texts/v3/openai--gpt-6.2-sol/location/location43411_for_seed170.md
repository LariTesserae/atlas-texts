At noon the plateau looks like a place you could walk across. The light is ordinary and quite exact. It picks out mica in the gray shelves and shows where a piece of black glass has sat long enough for the surrounding stone to wear down, leaving it on a little pedestal. The far rim is twenty miles away. Between here and there, there is practically nothing to put a foot over. The heat comes through the soles.

It has not rained here, even in the oldest account the rocks give of themselves. Their hollows were made by dry grit, rolled in place by winds too weak to lift it. Some of the hollows are deep enough to stand in. At the bottom of each lies the round stone that made it, fitted so well that a fingernail won’t go between. From a distance the hollows look like a scattered flock of shallow white bowls. There is no stain in any of them. There are no roots in the cracks.

The noise is loud enough to make conversation impractical. It comes up through the plateau in a register that makes the teeth feel loose, then breaks into the hard, evenly spaced knocks of a machine taking a load. You can walk for miles looking for the source. There are no buildings, no exhaust stacks, no cables laid across the stone. At the western rim an exposed bed of quartz makes contact with a bed of dark metallic rock. The seam parts and closes by about the width of a hair. At each closing, a row of blue arcs runs away along the cliff, and somewhere below, a great weight advances one notch.

What the plateau manufactures is animals.

The first one you see may be standing so far off that you mistake it for a chip in the edge of a shelf. It has six narrow legs and a low body with the dull finish of cast iron. Under its ribs, broad plates slide past one another without touching. It takes a step. The knock you heard an hour ago fits the step perfectly, down to the small second knock as the rear foot comes down.

After that you begin to see the rest. They are not hidden underground or camouflaged against the rock. They occupy the distances between things, and there is far more of that country than the eye first allows. Look between two stones a stride apart and you may see, a long way down the interval, a procession of pale jointed backs. Look directly at either stone and the procession is gone. In the wide gap between the plateau and the range across the basin, animals are walking in such numbers that their overlapping calls make one steady note. From the rim you can see daylight between every pair.

Nothing grazes. The long-legged ones put their feet down on the warm faces of the shelves and stand for days while the plates under their ribs swell apart. At night the plates contract. Smaller animals come out to feed on the thin chips shed at the edges. They leave paired rows of tooth marks on the hotter stones. The old tooth marks have been polished smooth by grit; the new ones are sharp enough to catch a sleeve. This is one way to tell that things are still happening here.

At the north end of the plateau there is a slope down which a loose pebble will roll for three or four feet before stopping. If you walk down after it, it will be behind you. The animals negotiate the slope without difficulty. They step wide around the place where the pebble ought to be, although many of them were crossing here before you dropped it.

By late afternoon the air is no cooler, but the black glass has begun to tick. The shadows show features the glare concealed: a broad, shallow trough going east, a low ridge with its crown worn flat, the long parallel furrows of traffic. One animal in the trough has lifted its forefoot. If you stay until sunset you may hear the little second knock, though the foot will still be up when it is too dark to see. Beyond it the plain lies open all the way to the rim, and the sound of feet coming down goes on and on.
