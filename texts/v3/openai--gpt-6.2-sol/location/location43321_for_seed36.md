The valley is bright all the way to its floor, though there is no opening overhead. The roof hangs low at the entrance, close enough to touch with the back of a hand, then draws up into a vault several miles across. Broad leaves lie flat against it, their pale undersides giving off a steady light. Where two leaves overlap, the lower one shines just as brightly as the upper. You can stand in the shade of a root and see the fine hairs on your arms.

There is almost nowhere to put a foot that isn’t alive. On the slopes, short, closely packed stems take your weight the way a brush takes the weight of a palm. In the hollows they give way to tough sheets of leaf, each with its own slight pitch. You learn to follow the veins for footing. The paths are clear enough to see from a distance: long yellow lanes threading the dark green. Up close, they prove to be the tops of buried roots. At a fork, the smaller lane runs uphill and joins the roof.

Here and there the vegetation parts around a knob of new stone. The rock is pale and blunt, with a slick skin that looks as though it ought to be soft. It is warm and quite hard. Nothing has worn a groove in it. Even at the foot of the slopes there is no gravel, no bank of sand or fallen leaves. The valley has the finish of something just out of a mold. At its far end a ridge rises waist-high out of the floor and stops, with the same unbroken skin across the place where you expect a fracture. It looks unfinished from either side.

The heat comes up through the soles of your feet. Most of the leaf surfaces are dry and faintly tacky, but water stands in the cupped joints of the larger stems. These little basins are spaced far enough apart that you can cross a dry acre and come upon a thimbleful of water at knee height. In a few places the cups are broad enough to show the white roof in reflection. Beads hang from the undersides of the leaves above them. A particularly large bead, drawn almost to a point, looks ready to fall. It looks that way on the walk back, too.

There are three low walls of fitted clay along one of the root paths. They never stood high enough to keep anything out. One has a recess lined with the smooth inside of a split stalk, just large enough to set a bowl in, though there is no bowl. At the last wall someone left a thumbprint in the clay. A pale root crosses it without entering the hollow. There is room for a little more root, and it is hard, looking at that bare fraction of the print, not to supply the rest of its growth yourself.

Sound carries along the open lanes. A scuff at one end of a straight path comes back thinly from a long way off. Step sideways into the stems and a companion can speak from six feet away without being heard. Once, beside a cup of water, there is a dry ticking. A small brown insect is working its mouth against the rim of the leaf. It stops when your shadow reaches it, and for a moment the whole valley seems as quiet as that one cup.

From the highest place on the path you can see the lanes running out in several directions, some for nearly a mile, before the foliage closes over them. In the distance the floor and ceiling meet so gradually that there is no line to tell you where the valley ends. You can walk a long way toward that meeting and pass under leaf after leaf still folded down the middle. The heat holds. The light holds. In the narrow space inside each fold, the new green is almost white.
