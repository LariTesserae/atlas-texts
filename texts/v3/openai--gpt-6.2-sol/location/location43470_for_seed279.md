The flats are no more than a few feet above the water beneath them. In some places the distinction is a skin of black stone, thin enough that you can see a pale seam opening under your heel. Elsewhere the stone has buckled into rows of sharp little ridges. There are thousands of these, none much higher than a knee. From one end of the flats you can see clear across to the other, yet there is hardly a yard of empty ground.

It is almost always too dim to see whether rain is falling. You have to hold out a hand. More often the wet is coming up from below: beads well out of a fresh fracture, gather along its lip, and drop back down through another crack. The stone is young enough to have edges as fine as broken crockery. Its broad faces are dull gray, but a new break shows a dark green that looks, at first, like something growing. There is nothing growing. Even the sheltered side of a stone, where you might expect a little softness, is bare and cold.

The noise is astonishing. It starts well before the flats come into view, a heavy, continuous spilling sound, as though a river were going over a ledge. Once you are out on the stone you can hear the individual drops in it. Some fall a finger’s breadth, others a long way; some strike water, and some hit the undersides of thin slabs that ring at several pitches. At a dry place you can stand with both feet on solid ground and have to raise your voice. There is no river. You can come back after a night away and find the same bead still fattening on a point of stone.

Here and there someone has laid three or four slabs across a fissure. Most of these crossings are still good. One leads to a low enclosure with an opening on the side away from the prevailing rain. Inside, at just the height of a seated head, the sound drops away. The wall is no thicker there than anywhere else. On the floor is a shallow hollow polished by a long succession of heels. You can sit in it and hear the small intimate scrape of your sleeve against your ribs, while through the opening the whole plain goes on pouring.

There are animals, though you could cross the flats many times without seeing one. The clearest sign is a paired track in the pale sediment at the bottom of a pool. The tracks come out from under a shelf and end, always, a little short of the open water. Once, waiting there, you might see a flat, nearly colorless body go over the stone beneath the pool. It carries a pendant of air under its belly. Where it passes, the silt does not stir until a moment later, when a fine white line lifts into the water and hangs there.

In the far north the ridges peter out into an unbroken sheet. Water lies on it so shallowly that its surface seldom ripples, and the sky reflected there is brighter than the sky overhead. The new stone begins at that edge. It comes up in narrow strips, each one leaning against the last, with water caught between them. A crossing laid a lifetime ago is still in place, though the fissure beneath it has widened enough to show daylight at both ends. In another lifetime a slab may fall. The people who laid it left a spare on the bank. It is wet all over, even underneath.
