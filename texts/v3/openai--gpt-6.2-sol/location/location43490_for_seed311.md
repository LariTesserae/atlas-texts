At the bottom of the municipal swimming pool, behind the grille marked RETURN, there is a neighborhood six inches wide.

The pool has been closed for years, but the neighborhood is new. Its curbs are sharp enough to shave a ribbon from a fingernail. White ceramic apartment blocks stand on a level bed of poured glass, with narrow streets between them and a transit loop underneath. Everything is underwater. The water is cool enough to ache in a tooth. It fills the lift shafts, the mail slots, and the tiny hollows under the stair treads where the ceramic has not quite set.

There is daylight down there. The neighborhood borrows it from the shallow end, where a strip of sky lies across the old depth markings. A municipal lens folds that strip through the grille and unfolds it over the rooftops at something close to its original brightness. On cloudy mornings the whole district looks like a town waiting for a bus in bad weather. When someone walks past the pool, the shadow of a shoe crosses three blocks.

It is remarkably loud. Lift doors clap. Water bangs through the storm mains. The transit loop carries no passengers, but its cars come through every forty seconds with a whine that sets the bathroom mirrors humming. What they carry is room. The buildings go up faster than the neighborhood can make space for them, so every morning the transit authority brings in a fresh allotment of distance and distributes it among the streets. You can watch two walls draw apart just far enough for a drainpipe to straighten between them. By evening, somebody has built another wall.

Nothing swims here. There are no shells on the concrete, no eggs tucked into the grilles, no quick scattering of bodies when a door slams. A little green grows where the borrowed daylight is most reliable: three narrow strips on a school roof, and a tuft on a parapet that bends flat every time a tram goes by. The grounds department has laid out a park, but its trees are still upright slivers of clear glass. Beneath each one is a square of dark soil held in place by the pressure of the water.

Sometimes a real storm passes over the pool. The first drops arrive in the neighborhood as round shadows, running down the fronts of the buildings. Then the old drain takes a mouthful of rain, and the streets all lean in the same direction. For a few seconds you can see how recently the place was made: the bright seams, the scored edges, the clean grit shifting at the foot of every wall. Then the pressure equalizes. Somewhere behind a row of apartments a loose tile keeps ticking, long after the rest of the district has straightened up.
