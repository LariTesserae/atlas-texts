The town is high enough to look down on the weather, though there is nothing in the weather that could wet it. Pale fronts of dust travel below the rim of the plateau. From above, you can see where they meet, how one folds under the other and goes on in the opposite direction. Nothing falls out of them. There has never been a rain gutter in the town.

At first the plateau looks almost bare. There are a few upright forms, each standing a long way from the next, and a low dark town gathered around a shallow depression. The intervening ground is the gray of old paper. Kneel and look along it, though, and the gray resolves into overlapping leaves. Each is several paces long and no thicker than a thumbnail. Their free edges lift in the wind, admitting a line of violet light before settling again. The dust in their joints is pollen. The shelves below the rim are living wood laid down in laminations fine as silk. On the underside of the plateau, where no light has reached for an age, the plant is still growing.

It grows without water. Break a leaf and the fracture is dry and faintly warm. For a moment you can see the two halves continuing to exchange color across the break. Separate them farther and the color stops. On cold mornings the town’s repairers go out looking for breaks that have stopped answering. They carry no glue: they sit on either side of a seam and hold the edges within a hair of one another until the color starts again.

The buildings were trained out of the ground, and you can tell the age of a street by how much of the original leaf remains in its walls. In the older quarter the veins run uninterrupted through three or four houses. A cupboard may be the back of a neighbor’s stair. There are broad public rooms with nothing in them but a rib curving from floor to ceiling, polished at shoulder height by centuries of people taking the short way around it. The newest houses stand farther apart. They have doors on all sides, in anticipation of streets that have not yet arrived.

Some of those streets are already in use. The old road-makers learned to grow two surfaces that shared a boundary without sharing the ground in between. You can step through a gate on the north side of town and come out on a shelf a mile below it. The shelf is colder by several degrees, and the air coming through the gate can glaze the lintel with a white, dry secretion. A loose button dropped on the threshold will fall sideways and roll back to your feet. Larger loads go by the long road. Nobody likes to hear a laden cart strike a seam.

It is a loud place to have so much room in it. Late in the afternoon the plateau contracts, and the long leaves begin to knock against their beds. Close by, the sound is like someone beating a rug. Farther out the knocks run together, and a single note comes up through the soles of your feet. The note changes as the shadow of the rim crosses the town. Shutters go up rather than down at that hour: a closed room can catch the note and keep it until morning.

This is when the animals come out in numbers. All day you might see only one, a narrow six-legged thing standing against the sky so steadily that you take it for a seed stalk. At the first knocking, hundreds step out of the leaf joints. They have pale bellies and slate-colored backs; they cross the open ground in widely spaced files, stopping wherever the leaves lift to take what has gathered beneath. Behind them come the soft-footed hunters, whose broad ears fold flat when they run. The hunters wait for the deepest part of the afternoon note. In that interval the little grazers cannot hear one another stamp a warning.

For all this movement, the country has almost lost its relief. The ridges beyond town are the worn shoulders of folds that once stood higher than the plateau does now. They have been rubbed down by wind and by the passage of the leaves themselves, each generation sliding a little over the last. In a road cut you can put your finger on a line of black seed cases extending straight into the wall. A second line, a handspan below it, was laid down under a different sky. The living plant has kept both. It has also grown across most of the old road cut. The town now takes the bend without knowing there was once a straight way through.

After dark, the upright forms on the flats turn out to be flowering stems. They are so far apart that no two seem to open together. One shows a dull red interior, then another answers from near the rim. The grazers go quiet. Something winged leaves the town roof and crosses the intervening dark, too low to silhouette against the sky. You hear it pass, and for a while nothing else nearby moves. Then a leaf lifts under your feet, opening just enough to show the long dim line of the ground beneath it.
