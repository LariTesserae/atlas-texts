The bottom of the sea is paved.

Not evenly. A slab may be as broad as a table or small enough to lift in one hand, and where the paving settled over buried walls it runs in long, low wrinkles. But wherever a current has swept the bottom clean, there are joints. Follow one and it meets another. At the foot of a rise, the stones have been laid edgewise to take the weight. There is no exposed bedrock, no bank of native gravel. Even the white clay in the hollows came out of something: rub it away and you find the rim of a fired tile or the weave left by a reed mat on the underside of a brick.

The sea is cold from top to bottom. Near its northern rim, in summer, enough light gets down through the broken ice for red algae to grow on the upper courses of the walls. Below that narrow belt there is no day. The lushest growth is lower still, where water seeps through the paving. It is not the remains of a drowned garden, though in a lamp’s small circle it can look like one. Cream-colored threads hang from the lips of the drains. Rust-brown films buckle into folds as thick as a thumb. Bacteria take their energy where the mineral-rich seepage meets the seawater, and over centuries their growth has filled some courtyards nearly to the sills. The older layers are black and close-packed. The new growth lifts from them in soft white tassels.

Nothing grazes it. The mats have no bitten edges, the silt no tracks. Under a ledge, where a tuft has grown long enough to touch the floor, its tip has taken hold and started another patch. Most of the floor is bare between these seep gardens. The larger ones mark the buried drains so faithfully that you can follow a street by its growth long after the street itself has been worn level.

There are higher places, but you would barely call them hills. For half a day you could cross a rise without losing sight of the same pale seam below you. Then the whole slope turns out to be the side of an embankment. Near its crest, in a recess the water has spared, there are little overlapping scallops where somebody dressed the stone with a hammerstone. The last scallop is shallow: one blow more would have brought the edge flush. The next block was fitted to it as it was.

No single pattern holds across the basin. In the west the courses are narrow and fitted dry; farther out, squat clay piers stand in rows with nothing left on top of them. A newer pavement crosses both, though “newer” here means only that its foundations cut through theirs. There are enough such crossings to make it seem less like a work anyone finished than a succession of places people kept making underfoot. They used stone, clay, cord, and timber. The timber survives as dark stains under the heaviest blocks. The holes for the cord are still there.

The water is almost still. The tide takes hours to reach the far end of the basin, and by then has scarcely lifted it. A thread of growth stirs in a culvert. A skin of silt peels off a doorstep; some of it settles on the step below, some goes on through a doorway. A slab occasionally gives way above a buried room. The sound travels much farther than the falling stone, a low knock carried through miles of cold water to places where nothing moves in answer.

At a doorway the old builders raised the sill a little, as builders do to keep water out. The room behind it has been full for thousands of years. Yet on the outside, where a weak current runs along the wall, the silt comes up to the top of the sill and stops. Inside, the paving is clean almost to the back.
