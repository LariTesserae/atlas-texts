At the back of a warm tidal flat, a shelf of limestone overhangs a basin no wider than a cottage. The ceiling is high enough to stand under at the mouth. Farther in, it comes down to within a handspan of the water, and the last of the daylight shows how thoroughly the space is filled. Low stone ribs divide the basin into cups. Chalky ledges project over the cups, their undersides crowded with blunt mineral teeth. Wherever two ledges almost meet, a thread of water hangs between them.

The flat outside is only a little lower than the basin. There is no fall of water to announce it. Seepage comes through the back wall and leaves through a broad sill, over which it passes in a film too thin to see unless you put a finger in it. The lip of the sill is rounded and brown, the color of a river pebble that has been handled. Every cup is full to its own lip. You could bail one out with a bowl and watch it fill again, though it would take a good part of an afternoon.

Under the shelf the air is warm enough that the stone feels neither warm nor cool at first touch. Then the water finds the creases of your knuckles. There is a faint smell of wet lime. No leaf has blown this far in, and there is nothing green on the walls. The darkest patches are brown mineral stains, each beginning at a narrow crack and broadening below it. Near the mouth, a few small snails occupy a seam just above the water. Their shells are the same gray as the rock except where the wet has polished them.

Someone once laid three flat stones at the entrance so that a person could kneel there without sinking into the mud. Two remain level. The third has settled at an angle, and water stands in the shallow hollow where a knee used to go. There is also a groove in the sill, chipped with a pointed tool. Perhaps it was meant to draw off water for a jar. The water goes over it as readily as it goes over the rest of the sill now.

A drop strikes the roof of a submerged ledge, making a small, close sound. After a while it happens again. From outside comes the occasional soft collapse of a bubble in the mud. The light changes more than anything in the basin does. In the morning it picks out the rims of the cups; later it reaches beneath them and shows the pale gravel at the bottom. By dusk there is only a dull strip across the sill, and in it the water going out looks exactly like the stone staying where it is.
