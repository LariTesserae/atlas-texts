The high country is almost flat. This is the first thing you notice after the climb, and the hardest thing to reconcile with the climb afterward. For miles the ground goes on at the same warm, stony level, rising here and there by the height of a sleeping person. Then it ends. From the edge you can look down on the roofs of clouds and, through a hole in them, see a brown river making a bend so far below that it takes a while to recognize the bend as water.

The stone has worn down to its hardest parts. There are no loose slopes, no young heaps of rubble. The shallow hollows have rims polished by grit that has long since blown away. In one place a band of white quartz runs flush with the dark pavement for half a mile; it must once have stood up like a wall. Now you catch it with the sole of your foot only because it warms faster than the stone around it.

Nothing about the air is old or settled.

It comes over the rim in enormous rolls, warm enough to breathe comfortably and strong enough to take your breath away. There are several winds at once. You can watch one run across the plateau by the way the ferns go silver, while another holds the grass flat in a strip at right angles to it. Where they meet, dead leaves rocket straight upward. The noise is continuous, though its sources keep changing: a deep note from the cliff, the slap of a thousand broad leaves turning over together, a hard crack from somewhere out in the open where there is nothing to break. If you shout to someone walking beside you, you see their face change before you hear your own voice.

Rain seldom crosses the whole tableland. It comes down in narrow, slanting lanes, and in ordinary afternoon sunlight you can see three or four of them traveling separately. One will pass close enough to wet your sleeve and leave the rest of you dry. The hollows keep what they catch. There are waist-high ferns in them, and glossy creepers with little yellow flowers tucked well underneath the leaves. A crack no wider than a hand can carry a green seam almost to the horizon. Between those seams there may be acres of bare warm stone. From a distance the country looks luxuriant; walking it, you can spend an hour in full sun on your way to a patch of shade no bigger than a bed.

The plants grow close to the ground, but not all of them are small. There are trees whose trunks lie flat along the pavement for thirty feet before putting out branches. The roots enter separate joints in the rock, so that a single tree can look like several low groves with nothing between them. Under one of these trees the air smells of bruised leaf and wet bark. You can put your hand into the litter and find it cool and damp even when everything above it is thrashing dry. Small pale snails live there. Now and then a long-winged brown bird lifts from the rim without a stroke, goes up until it is barely visible, and drops into the valley. It is possible to walk all day and see no other animal.

At wide intervals there are shelters, no higher than the trees. Their walls are made of slabs levered out of the old joints, with the smooth sides turned inward. Most have lost their roofs. In one, a few lengths of split wood are still held down by stones, and below them there is a shallow cup pecked into the floor. It has water in it. Outside, the gusts send pebbles ticking along the pavement; inside, a thread of root hangs from the roof without moving. The din is scarcely less, but the reprieve makes you aware of how long you have been leaning into it.

Toward evening, drops begin to rise from the hollows nearest the edge. They come one at a time at first, fat enough to see in the level light. A drop will leave a pool, climb past the ferns, and hang briefly at the height of your face before shooting away. Sometimes the ascending spray meets a passing shower, and the two run through each other without mixing. The ferns get wet on both sides. By morning the pools are lower, though it may have rained on them all night.

From any of the shelters you can see a long way: the quartz line, the wet lanes passing over it, a low green patch that might be a mile away or five. The next shelter is usually too far to make out. Once a squall has passed, the dark pavement dries in patches, and the familiar marks return in no particular order. The white line is there again. The green patch never went anywhere. All that afternoon the place has been making enough noise to come apart, and there is not a fresh edge on any stone you can see.
