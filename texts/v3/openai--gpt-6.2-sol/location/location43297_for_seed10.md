At the height where you would expect the last grass to give out, there is a whole country of it.

The plateau has been worn almost flat, but not quite. Across a day’s walk it rises and falls by the height of a sleeping animal. Water knows these differences. It gathers in long, shallow lakes with no shore you can put a finger on: the grass thins, the spaces between the blades fill with sky, and after a while the blades are growing up through a foot of black water. In the dimness it is easier to tell how far away a place is by the weather on it. A strip of rain hangs over the western pools. Farther off, where no rain is falling now, the grass is still lying flat.

There is stone under the plateau, though it takes a torn bank to show it. The upper inch of the stone is threaded with roots fine enough to follow the grain of individual crystals. Below that it is pale and soft-looking, with the rounded edges of something that has spent a very long time under a tongue. There are no loose stones. Whatever comes loose is held.

Most of the vegetation is no taller than a thumb. That is why the country looks so empty. The trees, where there are trees, stand miles apart, and most of a tree is underground. Above the surface it has a thick green lip and a few stiff leaves, all on the downwind side. Below, its living wood spreads in stacked plates, with water running between them. A grove may take half a day to cross and present nothing to the eye but three dark commas on the horizon.

In cold weather the plates draw together. The smallest passages close first. A sound begins underfoot, not very loud at any one point, but so extensive that you can walk toward it all morning without getting nearer. As the larger passages close, the water is squeezed into upright stems at the edge of the grove. The stems knock in their sockets. They take turns, although not quite regularly, and when one sticks there is a pause in which you can hear the others straining against it. Then it comes free and the whole western edge of the grove throws up water.

There is no machinery to find. Nevertheless, after a few days here you begin to distinguish plants that are running from plants that have shut down. Some maintain a low electrical tremor in the water all night. In those pools the silver mites arrange themselves into moving lanes, their backs just breaking the surface. A pool that has gone dead may still look busy for hours: the mites keep to their lanes until the first one turns across the traffic, and then the pattern comes apart.

The bigger animals are harder to see. Flocks come over low enough to show the pale seams on the undersides of their wings, then land and disappear, even where there is nothing higher than their ankles. In a quiet moment you can hear them biting grass. There are white grazing animals whose winter coats trail in the water and collect enough weed to feed a second population, small warm creatures that seldom come down to the ground. Sometimes an animal stops in the middle of a pool and shakes itself. For a few seconds the air around it is full of passengers, all going in different directions. The animal moves on before they have finished landing.

The plateau is not always the same distance across. This is most apparent in settled weather, when you can choose a tree to walk toward and keep it in sight. The tree grows larger, and the flock standing halfway to it grows larger, but the intervening water does not diminish. Eventually you reach the flock from behind, though it has been facing you all the while. The birds take no notice. They have feet broad enough to spread their weight over whatever surface is available, and once in a while you see one step onto the reflection of a bank and climb out there, leaving the bank itself undisturbed.

In a storm there is too much happening to be sure which of these things is unusual. Pools empty sideways. Sod rises in wide flexible sheets and hangs there streaming, showing its white root-hairs to the air, then slaps down somewhere else. A flock lifts out of the rain before the rain reaches it; another remains on the ground and is carried off with the ground. The wind is loud, but louder still is the report from under the plateau when a long seam of roots lets go. The release travels out of hearing. You can follow it farther by the successive darkening of the grass.

By morning the pools occupy the old hollows. The sod that came down upside down has put green tips through its exposed roots. For all the violence of the night, the skyline may differ only by a tree. It is hard to say whether the missing one has died, gone underground, or become too far away to see.

At dusk the cold comes up through the water before it comes down through the air. Ice takes hold between the stems, leaving a dark little space around each living blade. In the west, one grove after another begins its work. The sound carries for miles over the level country. Close by, in a pool no wider than a hand, something under the new ice breathes into a bubble, waits for it to move, and breathes into it again.
