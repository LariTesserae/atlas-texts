The country is so low that a fallen alder can divide it into two watersheds. On one side of the trunk, rain works its way through several miles of reed bed to a broad, ink-colored river. On the other, it collects in shallow dishes worn into the clay. The dishes have rounded rims, thick with moss. When they overflow, they do so at the same little notches they have used for centuries.

Underfoot there is a close succession of things: sedge roots, wet leaf skins, the buried ends of branches, gravel fine enough to shift under the pressure of a thumb. Farther out, the marsh opens into dark reaches of water. You can see a long way without seeing much sky. The clouds are low, and the light they let through is just sufficient to distinguish the green of a living reed from the green on a dead one. Water beads along the underside of every branch. Even when the rain lets up, the alders go on dripping for hours.

The loudest sound is a continuous beating, like surf against a hollow bank. There is no bank high enough to account for it. Where the sound is strongest, the water may be scarcely ankle deep. Kneel there and you can hear individual impacts, each with a soft crumbling aftersound. Put a finger in the pool and the beating stops. Lift it and the pool makes up the missing blows in a quick, untidy succession. The sedges shake. Then the old pace resumes.

Nothing here looks made. That is easy to believe on the drier ground, among the alders with their split crowns and shelves of pale fungus. It is harder to believe out on the flats, where the water sometimes lays a second surface underneath the first. Silt passes between the two. Roots grow into the interval and come out washed clean, without losing the fine hairs at their tips. Along the margin, the same grain of quartz can be seen from above the water in two places at once. Both places cast a shadow. Over a winter the shadows wear a small fork in the clay.

Whatever keeps the two surfaces apart can make adjustments finer than a cell wall, yet it never holds the marsh to a fixed shape. In a wet year the interval widens under the weight of rain. One of the old channels will fill, and by spring the sedges along it will have leaned far enough to root on the other side. The change is slow enough for moss to follow. At the edge of a pool you can peel back a mat of it and find obsolete waterlines, one beneath another, each edged with the brittle tubes of creatures that no longer live there.

There are few animals to see. A heron may stand in the open for most of an afternoon, its feet lost in the brown water. When it strikes, the head goes down once and the ripples spread from two points. It comes up with one fish. Small snails graze the submerged alder bark, leaving narrow tracks through the green film. Now and then a vole swims between tussocks, pushing a little heap of water ahead of its nose. On the farther shore it spends a long time combing its whiskers.

At the end of the rain, the clouds lift enough to show the whole breadth of the lowland: miles of wet crowns and reeds, open water dull as the backs of leaves, and here and there the pale fans of trees that fell before the alders around them sprouted. The river is almost invisible from a distance. You can follow its course by the places where the beating goes quiet. The silence moves downstream at the speed of the current, taking a bend a little at a time. Behind it, in the shallows, the interrupted blows begin again.
