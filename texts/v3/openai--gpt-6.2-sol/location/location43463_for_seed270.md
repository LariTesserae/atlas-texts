Under the abandoned weighhouse there is a room no bigger than the bed of the truck that used to park above it. You get in sideways through a gap where the foundation has drawn away from the rock. A strip of ordinary daylight comes down through the old scale pit. In it you can see the heads of three bolts, a square of cream enamel, and so many animals that at first the floor appears to be running downhill.

There is almost no downhill left. The room occupies a hollow in a bed of dolomite worn low before the first walls went up. The ridges in it are broad enough to rest a wrist on. The shallow bowls between them hold dust, shed skins, and little heaps of enamel worn off the weighhouse fittings. Nothing grows here. Even the dark edge along the foundation, which looks like a line of moss from the entrance, is a mass of tightly folded wings.

The air is cool and dry enough that a dead thing keeps its shape until something takes it apart. It takes less than a minute. Small jointed creatures work their way under a fallen wing while larger ones are still dragging the body. There are hunters no longer than a grain of rice and pale animals as long as a finger that go through the melee on stiff, widely spaced legs. Their feet come down in the few patches of bare stone. You can watch one hesitate with a foot lifted while the patch it meant to step on fills up.

Behind the weighhouse, a screw conveyor still carries crushed stone from one shed to another. The screw is worn; once per turn it knocks against its casing. Each knock travels through the foundation and comes out in the little room with a hard, bright edge. There is also a steady noise like paper being crumpled. When the conveyor stops, the paper noise goes on. Most of it comes from a crevice behind the enamel plate, where thousands of narrow bodies are trying to pass in opposite directions.

The animals do not live on stone. The conveyor brings them a little food from outside: broken insects, flakes of grease, the remains of whatever nested in the quarry spoil. The abundance in the room is largely an abundance of young. They spend their first days packed into seams too fine for the hunters to enter, and leave in great numbers through a crack at the far end. The room would empty in a week if that were all that happened. But the warm casing above it is where the adults come back to lay. The few that miss the crack and emerge into the weighhouse can be seen on the white wall after a shift, sitting quite still with their wings spread, easy to mistake for chips in the paint.

At noon the light reaches a shallow bowl in the rock. Its near side is empty and its far side is crowded. There is no barrier between them. As the light moves, the crowd gives ground by the width of a leg at a time, and for half an hour you can see the bottom of the bowl. The stone there has the soft finish of a thing handled for years. There is a bolt at its center, or what looks like a bolt until you notice that the sides have worn away and there is nothing left for a wrench to catch. By one o’clock the bowl is full again, and the stone is cool enough to put your hand on, if you can find a place to put it.
