The heights begin with a shelf of pale stone six or seven miles across. You can see the far edge from almost anywhere on it. It looks close enough to reach before noon, right up to the moment you pick out a herd of animals on the intervening ground and realize that the little dark marks you took for their shadows are their young.

At the edge, the stone turns down. It has been turning down for so long that there is nothing ragged about the drop. The lip is blunt as the rim of a bowl, and below it the walls fall in great overlapping planes, each paler than the last, until the glare off the rock and the brightness of the air are the same thing. There is no bottom to be seen. On the opposite side, a range of detached summits stands at eye level, too far away for any but their broadest features to show. One has a shallow notch. One looks as if it ought to have a notch and doesn’t. Between them is an amount of sky you have to turn your head to take in.

The sound is enormous. It is not the wind on the shelf; the tufts of grass are hardly moving. Something much lower down is making it. At a few places along the rim you can see white water standing out from the wall in long, slanting streaks. They vanish into the brightness without striking anything you can see. You might imagine that the sound would be louder there, but it is every bit as loud a mile inland. You can shout into the ear of someone walking beside you and watch them wait for you to begin. Once you have been up here long enough, shutting your eyes makes the place seem smaller: the roar is too even to give you any distance at all.

For all that water below, there is very little to drink on top. A hand laid flat in a seam comes away damp. Under an overhang the stone may be black with moisture, with a bright bead hanging at its lowest point, but the dust directly beneath it is dry. There are shallow pans where rain collects, most of them no deeper than the print of a heel. The little grazers know which ones last. They go down on their front knees to drink, exposing a patch of nearly white fur at the throat. Even in full sun it is cool enough that the damp from a rock will stay in the seat of your clothes for half the morning.

The animals are easy to miss until one moves. Then there are striped backs all over a slope that seemed bare. The grazers are small, with blunt muzzles, and crop the stiff grass down to its silvery bases. What looks like grass from standing height often turns out to be a colony of low plants with overlapping leaves, each leaf fitted close over the next to keep the wet in. Lift one and something with many legs folds itself into the space it occupied. Farther out, birds come and go from the cliff by the hundreds. They rise singly, at great intervals along the rim, and only gather once they are over the drop. From above you see the plain brown backs of their wings. When they bank, the undersides flash white. Their young open bright mouths in the recesses of the cliff. From ten paces away you can't hear them.

There is no trail around the shelf, though there are plenty of places where you would swear a trail ought to be. A scatter of flat stones makes a comfortable line across a wet patch; beyond it you find that the entire slope breaks into flat stones of just that size. At the foot of a low outcrop lies a piece of dark rock with a good edge and a hollow where a thumb fits. You could carry it all day. There is no mark to tell you that anyone ever has. The only hollows worn by bodies are oval beds under the lee of the stone, each with a few short hairs caught in it.

In strong daylight a dim green band sometimes appears across the open air below the rim. It seems to lie a little way out from the wall. Move along the edge and it moves with you, crossing a blunt buttress or a streak of falling water without losing its shape. The grazers show no interest in it. They have a way of coming right up to the brink and eating there with their hindquarters turned toward the drop, as though the useful distinction were between ground with grass on it and ground without.

Nothing here is still, exactly. Water goes over; wings turn; a shallow pan dries around the edges. But even the dry pans have old dark rings showing just how far the next rain will fill them. The birds settle in recesses whose thresholds are white with the use of generations. Come back after a long absence and you're likely to look for the wet seam where you remember it, and find it by touch before you find it by sight. The far notch will be where it was. The sound will have started well before you got there, and there will be no moment at which it gets louder enough for you to say you've arrived.
