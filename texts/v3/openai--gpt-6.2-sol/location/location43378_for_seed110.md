The pocket is high inside the mountain, a little below the summit and well above the last grass. It is no bigger than the space beneath a table. You enter it feetfirst through a slant in the rock, and for a moment, before your heels find the floor, you can feel the warmth coming up your trouser legs.

There is no light. After a while you stop turning your head to get a better look. The floor is two broad shelves of stone with a seam between them. Each shelf has a wet, almost greasy smoothness, but the edges of the seam are sharp. They fit one another so closely that you can follow a little tooth on one side and find its socket on the other. This is not a hollow water has worn. It is a place the mountain has only just made room for.

Water is everywhere, though there is hardly enough depth to cover a knuckle. It lies between the shelves, beads under the roof, and fills the small hollows your fingers keep mistaking for holes. The air is as warm as the stone. Nothing dries here; neither does anything drip. You can put your palm to the ceiling and take it away wet without hearing a drop fall. In the stillness, the little clicks of your swallowing seem to come from somewhere on the other side of the wall.

There is quite a lot in reach. A pebble is pinched halfway out of the seam. Behind it is a soft mineral paste, fine as the clay that gathers under a potter’s fingernail. Three roots come down through a crack no wider than a hair. They end bluntly a finger’s breadth above the water. There is nothing leafy, and only one animal that you’re likely to meet: a pale jointed thing, smaller than a grain of rice, under the flake where you first put your heel. Disturb it and it folds. Wait, and it opens by degrees, without going anywhere.

Someone has set a shallow clay cup on the higher shelf. Its rim is uneven from being pinched, and it is steadied underneath with two chips struck from the same dark stone. Beside the cup there’s a thumb-wide patch that feels different from the rest of the shelf. It took a long time to rub it smooth. Whoever did it stopped just short of the edge, leaving a thin, keen lip.

There is an odd thing about the seam. Draw the water away from it with the edge of your hand and for a moment you can feel both sides dry. Then they are wet again, all along the length you touched. There is no little advancing edge of water. The cup, if you dip a finger into it, does exactly what a cup should: its level sinks a little, and stays there.

On the way out you may have to lie still while you find the slant with your feet. With your cheek against the lower shelf, you can feel how far the warmth extends into the rock. It isn’t a warmth anything in the pocket is giving off. It is the temperature of the mountain at that point, and the water, the cup, and the three short roots have all had time to come to it.
