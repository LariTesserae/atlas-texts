At the landward edge of the marsh there is a strip of gravel just high enough to stay out of the water. It runs for miles, though you can rarely see more than a hundred yards of it. The reeds lean across it from both sides. They are green down to the roots even in the cold, with last year’s hollow stems packed tight among the living ones. Where the wind lays them flat, you can see out across the basin: black pools, low islands of willow, a long pale line of broken water. Then the reeds stand up and the view is gone.

It is too dark to tell a cloud from the ground until something moves against it. The water gives what little light there is back to the sky. The plants keep it. On the lee side of a willow you can put your hand into foliage so thick and cold it feels at first like a bank of wet earth. Rain beads on the upper leaves and gathers underneath them in drops big enough to strike the water audibly, when there is a lull.

There are not many lulls. A swell comes across the shallows, lifts six acres of rooted vegetation, and breaks under it. The reed beds go off like roofs coming apart. Loose stones roll in the channels, a deep and surprisingly even sound beneath the slap of water. Wind is the highest note, except when a flock of ducks gets up. They rise in batches because the birds at the far end of a pool cannot hear the ones at the near end take flight. From the gravel you see the successive runs of white underwing without hearing a thing.

The country has only lately been dry enough to walk across. The banks are sharp, cut into gray silt with the fine roots sticking out unbroken. There are places where last season’s surface, complete with dead grass and mouse holes, stands at shoulder height above a new channel. Elsewhere the water has made land overnight by dropping a rack of willow branches across a shoal and filling it with gravel. By morning there will be wagtails on it. By the following night it may be moving downstream. The marsh is lush in part because it has so little shade that stays in one place.

Scattered through the basin are small square houses, most of them no bigger than a room. From a distance they pass for pump sheds. Up close there are no pipes, no vents, no cables running out into the marsh. Each stands on three clay feet, with room underneath for a fox. In the oldest one somebody has left a broom, and the floor around it is dry.

The houses keep the basin from settling. They do not hold the ground up so much as pass its weight around. The instrument in each house was set down when the land was still under water, and the houses were built over them later, to keep people from tripping on them. Out in the marsh you can sometimes see a line of willows all dip at once, clear across the basin, as a load passes beneath them. Nothing happens inside the houses that you can hear over the weather. There is a little glazed dial by each door, marked in millimeters. When the needle goes all the way around, it means a place somewhere else has sunk by the thickness of a fingernail and this part of the world has risen to meet it.

The animals seem to care mostly about what the water is doing. Voles hole up in the hollow shoulders of the gravel strip, sitting close enough to the path that their tunnels show through between the stones. In the quieter pools there are shoals of small fish under the flooded grass. When a bank falls, herons come into the new shallows almost at once. They stand with their necks drawn in against the wind and wait for the mud to clear.

Toward morning the dark thins enough to show how wide the basin is. Two of the little houses stand miles apart on what looks like level ground. Between them a fresh channel is opening. At first there is only a wet crease, then a black thread of running water; in less than a minute it has sides, and little blocks of turf are turning over in it. Farther down, where the flow spreads and slows, a low bank takes shape out of the same mud. An otter climbs onto it while it is still forming, shakes once, and slides in on the other side.
