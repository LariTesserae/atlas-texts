The ward is six streets and a pumping hall, all under water. A low curb runs around it, marking the old municipal boundary. The land beyond is so flat that on a clear day you can see the curb’s far corner from the near one. On most days you can see the next doorway.

There was never much height here. The houses are two stories at most, and the pumping hall makes room for its largest machinery by going down. Its roof is level with the sills of the upstairs windows across the street. In the little space between them hang the remains of a distribution network: power cables, ceramic insulators, loops of pipe put in whenever a house needed more heat than the original pipe could give it. A person crossing that space would have to turn sideways. The water fits through without disturbing a thing.

The current runs strongest at ankle height. It has taken the sharp edges off the paving stones and opened shallow channels around the iron feet of the public benches. Under each bench there is a patch of finer sediment where the current divides and leaves a little of what it has been carrying. Elsewhere it takes just as much away. The patches have kept their shape for years.

It is cold enough for ice to grow on the undersides of the eaves when the water freshens in winter. The ice holds the grit that was passing when it formed, grain by grain, while the water goes on moving below it. In late winter the eaves look as if someone has laid strips of dirty felt along them. There are plants in the ward, though not many: a black fringe on the glazed bricks above the old boiler vents, and a few stiff blades in a window box where a broad pane still gathers the faint daylight. Once in a while a little translucent shrimp comes out of the box and walks all the way around the window frame. There is another frame a handspan away, but it has never been seen to cross.

Nothing in the pumping hall has stopped simply because it is submerged. The electrical cabinets are filled with oil. The switches are operated by floats in closed tubes, and the old dial faces are legible through thick green glass. What the pumps move now is a pocket of slightly warmer water between the bottom of the hall and a row of buried tanks beneath the street. At certain hours one can watch the mortar joints darken in succession as the warmth passes behind them. The pumps are mounted on stone sunk deep into the mud. You can stand beside one and hear nothing; if you lay a hand on a stair rail two rooms away, there is a faint tremor in the little bones of the wrist.

The ward has one peculiarity the builders knew about. A light carried past an open door will illuminate the room as it is. A light left in the room will, after a while, begin to illuminate the room as it was earlier that day. The delay is seldom more than an hour, and usually all that changes is the drift of silt. You can see a soft fan of it spreading over a tiled floor and put your hand through the place where it ought to be without raising a speck. The hall’s inspection lamps are therefore mounted on short swinging arms. Even now, once a day, a rod connected to a float tips each lamp forward and draws it back.

Toward evening there is a brief interval when the water above the ward moves one way and the water in the streets moves the other. The loose cable ends lift. Fine grit comes out of the keyholes and the slots of the letterboxes. An upstairs curtain reaches out through its window until it touches the wall opposite, then folds itself back into the room. Down at the boundary, a flat white stone rolls over the curb. By morning the current will have brought it back.
