The plain is so level that a wall the height of your knee can hide the country behind it. From the top of one of the older walls you can see the city laid out in the ordinary yellow light of afternoon. There are roofed courts, blind alleys, long ridges pierced at their bases, and small clearings that look, from here, as if somebody had swept them. Beyond the clearings there are more roofs. The farthest ones are blue with distance. A dust front is moving along that part of the plain, taking half the afternoon to cross a stretch of city you could cover with your thumbnail.

There is no stone in the walls that a person couldn't lift. Most of the city is made of sand carried a grain at a time, mixed with chewed stalks, and packed into place by pale insects that work on the inside of whatever they are building. At a broken wall you can see their galleries, some no wider than a blade of grass. Leave the break alone and by morning there will be a thin, lumpy partition across it. The older galleries underneath are left where they were. Some still carry traffic; others hold husks and dead roots and the dry bodies of beetles. The broadest avenues are the newest. They are the strips the colonies haven't reached yet.

The ground under all of it is black lava, spread out in overlapping sheets. Its edges are still sharp. In places a sheet buckled as it cooled and left a low fold with a little shelter on its lee side. That much relief is enough to start a district. Dust lodges there; seeds follow. Farther out, a new sheet has covered the old ground, and you can walk a long way on bare rock before coming to a fold with three plants in it and the first thumb-high turret. The turret has no opening yet. At its foot the insects have cleared a patch of basalt so clean you can see the tiny pits left by bubbles in the stone.

From above, the settled plain is greener than you would expect. There are no pools, and the channels between the walls are full of husks, not mud. Rain comes in a few short bursts in a good year. It slips through the cracks before the sun is out again, and the plants that survive here have roots down in those cracks and thick, cool leaves that can hold what they take up for months. On the older roofs the leaves overlap. Trailing stems reach across the narrow lanes and root on the other side, so that what looks like one broad patch of scrub from a distance turns out to have a whole neighborhood underneath it. You can put your hand into that shade and feel the difference at the wrist.

In the open, lizards wait at the mouths of passages the insects use to carry out rubbish. Little ground birds work the seams of the roofs, stepping carefully where the sand is new. There are beetles puffing dust out of their burrows, and small tawny snakes that spend the hot part of the day laid along the curve of a wall. So much is moving that if you watch for it you can scarcely find a patch of ground to rest your eyes on. Yet the plain is quiet enough to hear a bird drop a seed. The walls take the wind apart at ground level. Above them, it goes on for hundreds of miles, carrying fine pale grit to the newest black rock.

Late in the day the warm lanes empty and the shaded lanes fill. Insects come out in loose files to cut the fallen leaves. A lizard crosses a court it wouldn't venture into an hour earlier. Far off, the dust front passes over the last roofs you can make out, and for a while there is no telling the inhabited ground from the bare plain. When the light clears, you can see a line of little green patches well beyond what you had taken for the edge. There are no walls around them yet.
