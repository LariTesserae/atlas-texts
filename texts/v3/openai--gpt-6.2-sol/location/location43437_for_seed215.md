From the last bend in the climbing road, you can see the city and the weather it makes. The city is a scattering of dark blocks on a high shelf. Its weather is a line of low cloud that starts above the roofs and runs east until it joins a storm too far away to hear.

The road comes up through bare rock. There are no gullies yet, only shallow folds with water standing in them. In places the rock is so fresh that its broken faces have a dull blue shine. Water seeps from the joints and freezes on the lower edges in clear, thick lips. By noon the ice is dripping; by three, where the road turns into shade, it has begun to set again. The puddles have dark bottoms and almost nothing in them. The first soil on the shelf collects in the lee of loose stones, enough to stain a glove, not enough to take the print of a boot.

The city has streets, tram stops, a hospital, and five municipal districts, two of which are still mostly survey stakes. A person can walk a long way between buildings without leaving a named street. The buildings stand close in little groups for shelter, with vacant ground between the groups to give the water somewhere to go. Their walls are made of the local stone, cut in broad slabs and left rough where no one will brush against them. At street level there are boot scrapers, lights over doorways, and damp wool hung in the windows above the bakery. On a clear morning the sun reaches all the way to the back of the bakery and shines on the flour bins.

Farther out, the city is making another piece of shelf. There are no cranes. A row of waist-high ceramic posts stands out on the raw ground, each with a small black number on it. Between the posts, the weight of the rock can be shifted without anything being removed. Over a winter a new bench rises by the height of a stair. The work goes slowly because the water has to be given time to find its way through. At intervals a surveyor walks the line, kneels by each post, and enters the depth of the nearest pool. If a pool falls too fast, she puts that section back the way it was. In one of the older districts, a spring that disappeared during construction came up in a tram shelter six years later. The city moved the stop. The shelter is still there, full of clear water, with the route map bleaching under its glass.

There is very little green on the open shelf. In summer the sedges make short black-green runs along the seep lines, and there are a few low willows with most of their wood underground. Yet by midmorning nearly every pool has something moving in it. The stoneflies arrive in such numbers that their shed skins catch in drifts under the footbridges. Small gray birds run out from the bridge abutments to take them. Beyond the last tram stop, pale-backed grazers follow the wet ground over the rise. The spacing makes them look scarce until you watch for a while: more keep coming over the same rise, and farther off, where the land looks empty, the same traffic is going on. Fresh droppings and last winter’s hair have begun to fill the hollows the first rain could not find. A fox works the edge of the herd, stopping now and then to listen with one forefoot raised.

Much of this happens without any sound carrying to the road. The trams make a soft rush on the straight stretches. You can hear water under a slab if you stand still above it. When a flight of birds comes down on a pool, the noise of their wings is distinct for a second and then there is only the drip from the roadcut again. In the afternoon a squall crosses the vacant districts, wetting one side of every post. Ten minutes later there is ordinary sunlight on the houses and snow still falling on the far side of the shelf.

From the upper end of town, the land looks nearly level, although it is high enough that newcomers stop for breath on the stairs. There is no view down into the country it rises from. The edge is several days away, and between here and there are shelves broad enough to hold weather systems of their own. In the evening, bands of rain show gray against the clear stretches, sometimes three or four abreast. The city turns on its streetlights, including a long row where the street itself has yet to be laid. Along that row, the thaw water has lately begun to run in the same course two days in succession. It has carried a few grains of sand as far as the third light.
