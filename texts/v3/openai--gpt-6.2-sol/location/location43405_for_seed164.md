The plateau is higher than most clouds, but it is wet. Water stands a finger’s width below the turf. Kneel and put a hand down and it wells up around your knuckles, cold enough to ache. You can walk all day without crossing a stream. Where the ground breaks off, the water comes out of the cliff in a fine horizontal seam and hangs there as mist. From the far side of a gulf, the seam looks like a line someone ruled across the rock.

The plateau is young. Its edges are still sharp enough to take threads from a sleeve. There are miles of bare stone between the wet places, black slabs with pale undersides where they’ve split. A cleft can be narrow enough to step across and deep enough that the daylight in it goes blue. Then, without any easing of the slope, you come upon a level acre of green so thick that a boot put down among the leaves disappears. There are no flowers. The plants grow broad, overlapping blades and carry their new growth underneath, in the warm fraction of a degree afforded by older leaves. Nothing has ever eaten a hole in one.

There is a city at the top of the escarpment. It takes a while to recognize it as a city, because its buildings are so far apart. The first thing you see is a railing, then a paved square with a drain down its middle. Across the square is a pump house. The next building is eleven miles away, but the curb goes all the way to it, with a shallow ramp at each of the intersections. Street names are cast into iron plates. Some of the streets end at the lip of the plateau, where the last pair of curb stones frame a view of weather passing far below.

The pump house is loud. So is the building eleven miles away. The rock carries the low notes better than the air, so that you hear a motor through your heels long before you can see the roof that shelters it. Above that sound is a dry clatter from the insulators on the power lines. The wires cross miles of open ground without a sag you can make out. At a certain distance from a tower, the two sounds cancel, and you can stand in a little patch of quiet and listen to water coming back into your footprints.

Through the pump-house windows you can see the flywheels turning, the gauges trembling, droplets running down the intake pipes. A drop falls from the elbow of one pipe onto the bare steel floor. There is no mark where it lands. There never will be. The bearings are not being worn away; the oil is not growing dirty. Out along the escarpment, water goes on emerging from a crack whose edges it has never rounded. The place makes a great deal of noise in the course of remaining exactly as it is.

The light is ordinary. Under it you can see small, neglected-looking things: a green bead of water in the slot of a screw head, a row of office windows with the blinds set at slightly different heights. In one window there is a chair with a view down the avenue. From that chair, on a clear day, you could watch the avenue diminish to a pale thread and pick it up again on the opposite side of a cleft. Beyond that are more miles of stone and turf, another line of towers, and, just at the limit of sight, the bright roof of a building that belongs to the same street.
