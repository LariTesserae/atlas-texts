The plateau is green all the way to its edges. There are no trees on the skyline, and for the first mile from the pass there is nothing taller than a man’s knee. What looks from above like grass is a continuous skin of sedge, moss, and little tough-leaved shrubs, drawn over the shallow swells of the rock. In the hollows, water stands level with the leaves. You can put a boot down on what seems to be dry ground and watch a bright inch of water rise around it without a splash.

The rock underneath is young. At the pass, where frost has lifted a corner of the turf, you can see the hard blue surface, still ridged from cooling. There are no rounded stones in the pools and scarcely any soil. Roots run sideways in the joints, braided into pads thick enough to lift in both hands if you could find a loose edge. Most of the plateau drains through those pads. At the western rim they hang a little way over the rock, dripping from thousands of pale root tips.

Beyond that rim the water falls. From the middle of the plateau you can’t see the falls at all, only their white vapor rising above a low swell of sedge. The noise is tremendous. It has no single point of origin you can turn toward: the rock carries the low part and the air carries the rest. A person walking twenty yards ahead can shout without your knowing it. Yet the grass beside your boot barely stirs. A drop on the bent tip of a leaf swells for a long time before it falls.

The light is fierce enough to make the standing water hard to look at. Clouds come up the east face and spread across the plateau in scraps; each scrap takes the shine off a band of ground as it passes, and for a few seconds you can see the purple stems under the sedge and the tiny red leaves of sundews laid flat on the moss. Then the glare comes back. Even at noon the water is cold enough to ache in the knuckles.

There are two buildings, well apart. One is a corrugated hut at the pass with a white door and a porch just wide enough for a pair of boots. The other is a generating house set low against the west rim. Its intake is marked by three black posts in a pool, and its power line drops straight out of sight over the edge. From outside, with all that falling water, you wouldn’t know whether the turbines were running. Inside, a spoon laid on the sill turns a little at a time until its handle rests against the glass.

People cross on narrow panels of steel grating laid end to end. In places the moss has come up through every square, and the path is visible only because its green is a shade darker. A keeper comes out in late summer to raise the panels. He levers each one up, puts a fresh block beneath it, and moves on. There are older sections beside the present route that you would never pick out unless the sun caught a rusted corner.

Once in a while a pipit lands on an intake post. Its throat works, but you hear nothing. There are flies in the warm lee of the hut and almost nowhere else. No fish come up past the falls. The pools are full of stems, right down to the bottom, and when their tops die back they settle in among the living ones. Season by season the hollows get shallower. The deepest of them still holds a clear patch the size of a room. At its edge, new sedge stands in the water in a single loose row, with bright gaps between the shoots. Next summer the gaps will be narrower. From the pass, nothing will seem to have changed.
