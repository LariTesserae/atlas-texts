The marsh is built into the thickness of a wall at the top of the mountain. From the stair you can see a wet line between two courses of brick. Put your eye to it and there is a bank, a bed of reeds, and a little bluff of orange clay with the weaver’s thumbprint still in it. The whole place is thirty handspans long. Below it, on the other side of the wall, clouds are breaking against peaks that took nine days to climb.

It is hot in the wall. The bricks were stacked before they cooled, and water drawn through their pores comes out warm enough to steam. In the dimness the reeds are a deep, glossy green. There is scarcely room for a drop to fall without striking three leaves. Roots have filled the earthen jars set out to contain them; they lift the jars by slow degrees, and the water runs off the tilted rims into other jars. Even the mud is made. It was kneaded from brick dust and last year’s reed mats, pressed into braided trays, and carried up the last flight of stairs one tray at a time.

Nothing has worn smooth yet. Each runnel has a sharp lip where a split stick drew it through the clay. The first pool is still taking the shape of its basin. At its far end a woman lies on her side on the stair, fitting another piece of bank through the opening. She pushes it home with the heel of her hand. In the marsh, the new ground arrives all at once, with six shoots already leaning over the water. A drop that had been falling past the old edge lands on one of them.

The noise is astonishing. Reed stems knock hollowly together. Water goes down the throat of a jar in gusts, as though someone in the wall were trying to say a long word between breaths. A clay lid jumps and settles, jumps and settles. Outside, these sounds seep through the joint as a single low note. Inside, you can pick out the faint tearing of a root tip pushing through a mat. Every so often all the water stops, and the reeds go on knocking for a beat too long.

There is one animal anyone has seen: a pale snail on the underside of the bluff. Its trail leads out across the surface of a pool. From low down, with your cheek against the brick, you can see the little dents its foot has left in the water. They have been there since morning. The snail itself is no farther along than it was then, though its trail has grown by the width of a finger.

Near dusk the woman takes away the wedge that holds the opening wide. This is when the evening rain begins in the marsh. It rises out of the pools and spatters the roof, and the reeds bow upward under it until their tips touch the brick. A little escapes onto the stair, where it falls in the usual direction and freezes before it reaches the next tread. Tomorrow she will scrape it into a bowl and bring it back. They have only so much water up here, and there is another bank to make.
