The highest pond on the mountain is inside a bolt.

The bolt is as wide as a house, though the pond would fit in a washbasin. It lies behind the threads, in a recess left by the casting. The rest of the mountain is cast, too: twelve thousand metres of ribbed iron rising from the plain, with lift shafts in place of ravines. The lifts still run. Twice a day the bolt takes their weight, and the pond, which has no surface, changes shape.

Everything in the recess is underwater. The water is warm enough that the little glass shrimp gather where the cooler feed pipe comes through the wall. They hang there upside down, combing their antennae. A mat of broad green leaves grows from the gasket at the opposite end. It is lush in the way a plant on a windowsill can be lush: all that green pressing out of one small place, and nothing growing for a long way around it. Between the leaves and the pipe is a stretch of clear water that the shrimp will cross one at a time. A flat, nearly transparent fish waits halfway across. You can see yesterday’s shrimp inside it.

A service lamp shines through a thumb-sized pane. Its light is ordinary and white. By it you can see that the iron has worn almost level with the ceramic washers. The maker’s stamp survives only because the bottoms of the letters were cut deeper than the rest. Silt collects in them. There is no ground here that was not first a part number, though the silt has been rubbed so fine and carried around the recess so many times that nothing in it could be sorted back into what it was.

It is loud. A return valve knocks somewhere below, and the flooded bolt carries the sound so well that it seems to come from the pane. When a lift is on its way up, the leaves lie down in the current, all but one whose stem has grown through a washer. That leaf snaps back and forth. The shrimp draw in their antennae. The fish moves a little nearer the gasket. When the lift stops, warm water comes through a seam in the ceiling and a few grains rise out of the old lettering. They take most of the interval between lifts to settle again.

On the far side of the pane, beside the service lamp, there is a strip of sky. Sometimes the highest clouds pass so far below it that their shadows cross the plain without touching the mountain. Nothing in the pond responds to those shadows. Late in the day, though, sunlight reaches the pane at an angle the service lamp cannot. It picks out a second mat of leaves growing flat against the ceiling, their undersides thick with tiny snails. For a few minutes, looking in, you would think the whole pond hung from them.
