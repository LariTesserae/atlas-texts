Above the last grass there is a cleft no wider than a house. Its rim is one of the highest points in the range, but down inside it there is no view at all. From the floor, the sky is a kinked line that disappears if you take two steps in either direction. For most of the day the cleft is as dark as it is at night. The difference is that at night the animals near the opening stop making room for one another.

The mountain is young here. The two sides of the cleft still fit: a blunt projection on one wall faces the socket it broke out of on the other. Fresh stone smells faintly of struck flint even when nothing has struck it. There is no sand underfoot, only flakes that lie on their broad faces until a foot turns them edge up. The little moisture there is comes from clouds passing over the rim. They leave beads on the upper rock, and the beads travel down the seams for hours. By the time they reach the floor they have gathered enough dust to be gray. There is a shallow cup of water at the far end. A rain can fill it, but most rains go straight over.

At first the noise might be mistaken for water. There is a steady rush with small hard ticks coming out of it. Stand still for a while and the ticks separate: claw on stone, bill on bill, the dry disengaging of a wing from the wing pressed against it. There are birds wedged into the wall in rows where the wall appears to have no ledges. There are pale many-legged things underneath the birds, moving through the warm refuse and coming out in such numbers that a fallen feather travels uphill. Moths hang on the underside of other moths. In the low places, where the rock is cool enough to hold condensation, small soft animals drink off one another’s backs. Very little of the floor is bare. Some of what looks bare is waiting for you to move.

The only green is a thumbprint of moss well above the water cup. There are two wiry plants at the lip, each with leaves on just one side where it can get light. Everything else is fed from farther down the mountain. The birds leave through the slit and return with seeds, insects, bits of fur. No one watching from below would guess how many go in. Against the immense face of the mountain each arrival is a speck that vanishes, and the departures happen one at a time.

Sound behaves oddly near the bottom. A call made close to your ear can seem to come from well inside the rock, and sometimes it does: there are continuations of the cleft too narrow for anything with shoulders. Other calls are taken up by the animals themselves. A particular three-note cry will pass down the wall long after the bird that started it has tucked its head away. In cold weather the last note occasionally goes unanswered. The gap it leaves is more conspicuous than the cry.

Near the water cup there is a patch of wall rubbed smooth at about the height of a hand. Nothing was built here. A small dark animal with a blunt muzzle comes out from under a flake, feels over the patch with its forefeet, and selects a chip from among several lying there. It uses the chip to lift a skin of ice off the water. When it has drunk, it sets the chip back on the dry side, where it will not freeze in place. Much later another animal comes, feels among the same stones, and chooses a different one.

In a whole season the light moves far enough down the wall to touch that smooth patch once. The birds nearest it draw back, exposing the little oval hollows where their bodies have kept the stone dry. For a few minutes you can see how close the walls are, how much of the depth you had given the place was darkness, and how many creatures are fitted into the space that remains. Then the lit edge climbs out of reach. The birds settle, one after another, with the sound of someone slowly shaking out a heavy cloth.
