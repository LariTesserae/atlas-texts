The summit is thirty fathoms underwater. It is still a summit: on three sides the rock falls away into water so deep that the little light there seems to come from above and below at once. On the fourth side a fresh black ridge runs to a second, smaller peak. Between the two is a saddle no wider than a chapel floor. This is where the storm lives.

The rock is young enough to cut. Its broken faces have the greasy shine of split flint, though here and there a seam of pale stone shows through, pink at the edges in the dimness. Water comes boiling out of those seams, pleasantly warm. In the quieter pockets it is possible to put a hand flat against the stone and feel the heat. There are not many such pockets, and they move.

It is deafening. The noise is not merely in the water; it comes up through the mountain. Two notes predominate, one low enough to make the ribs hurt, the other the pitch of a small stone rattling in a clay cup. No stone could rattle that loudly. At intervals the two notes coincide and the saddle stretches. A cleft widens without its walls moving. A loose pebble takes a long time to pass between them, though the current carries it fast.

There are six low walls on the saddle, built without mortar from slabs thin enough to lift by hand. They enclose nothing. On the lee side of one, three pale fronds grow out of a thumb-sized patch of grit. The current flattens them, springs them upright, then flattens them the other way. Farther along is a bowl in the rock with seven black limpet shells in it. One is occupied. You can tell because every so often a whitish foot extends under its rim, feels the stone, and draws back. The shell has made more progress across the bowl than the other six have made in years.

A great deal happens here without leaving much to look at. Water shears into broad, clear ribbons that strike the ridge hard enough to take flakes off it. The flakes whirl into the open water. Some go dark as they recede; others are suddenly visible again, far above, with their undersides lit. Once in a while the entire saddle is swept clean, and in the next rush every loose thing comes back except one. The little walls may be what someone built to find out which things would stay.

Near the smaller peak, the current lifts a long skin of water from the rock. For a moment there is a dry overhang beneath it. A dark stain on the ceiling begins to pale from the margins inward. A grain of grit falls, and in all that din you hear it strike the floor with a small, exact tick. Then the water comes down. The stain is dark again.

Rarely a fish crosses the saddle. They are small fish with blunt heads, and they do not come in schools. One will reach the ridge and hang there, facing into the tumult without moving its fins. Its gill covers work gently. Then the ridge behind it tilts through an angle the ridge in front of it does not, and the fish is gone. A little silt slides down the rock after it, taking the ordinary way.
