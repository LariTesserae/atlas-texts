The high country is green all the way through, though there is seldom enough light to tell. Break a handhold from the side of a ridge and you find no stone in it. The outer inch is black and hard enough to take a polish; beneath it are pale fibers running straight down into the mountain. A fresh break smells faintly of cucumber. By morning it has drawn a skin over itself, and after a winter there will be a little pointed bud where your thumb was.

The ranges are young and very sharp. Their crests look broad from across a gulf, but in places you can straddle one, with a long fall on either side. They are still putting on height. The great upright stems, crowded together at their bases, part as they rise, and the spaces between them are often wider than the stems themselves. There are hollows large enough to have a cloud in them without the cloud touching either wall. Once you get down off the crest there is no ground to speak of, only the next root, the next union of branches, and here and there a floor of old leaf ribs that will bear you if you step close to its edge. Loosen one of those ribs and it falls a surprisingly long time without hitting anything.

It is cold at every height, but the cold changes character. On the crowns it dries the skin and makes the leaves ring against one another. A little lower there are bands of wet air that you can walk into and out of in a dozen steps. Hoarfrost grows sideways on the stems at the edges of these bands. When the air shifts, the frost softens and there is a brief, hard rain, loud enough that you have to put your mouth against a companion’s hood to speak. Twenty paces farther on, under an overhang of living bark, the dust hasn’t been wet in years.

Most of the water never gathers into a stream. It beads on the undersides of leaves and moves along the veins to the stem. At certain joints a cup the size of a room holds a few inches of black water. Small, flat animals live there. At the first tremor of rain they climb out and stick themselves in a close row on the rim, all facing inward. The drops knock minute white insects from the foliage overhead, and the animals snap them up as they come to the surface. In a downpour a cup can tip far enough to empty itself. The animals cling on while it rocks back into place, then go down to wait in the mud.

There are hours when the whole range is noisy and nothing near you moves. A gust working its way up a distant slope will set thousands of stiff leaves clattering in succession; you can follow its progress long after it has gone too far away to feel. Split stems report like struck wood. The opening and closing of the big joints makes a lower sound that is easier to feel through your heels than to hear. A traveling herd adds a fine, fast drumming to all this. The animals have wide feet and low bodies, and when they come to a gap they stop so abruptly that the last few walk over the backs of the first. They can stand there for half a night. Then one finds a hanging spray strong enough to take its weight, and the whole herd goes over by the same route. In the morning the spray is stripped clean except for the leaves the first animal stepped on.

The few shelters are tucked beneath forks. People make them with wedges of split root and lashings of wet fiber, never with a peg driven into anything living. Living wood pushes a peg out, usually just enough to make it useless and not enough for you to notice. An old shelter can look snug from the path and have a foot of open air between its back wall and the stem it was built against. Travelers hang a notched sliver at each fork they use. They leave it dangling even when it would be easier to tie it flat. In the dark you can hear it tapping, and on a still night you can reach along the branch until you find the notches. One means the branch goes through; two mean there’s water. A smooth sliver means somebody once thought there was a way down.

There is a season when a whole face of the range turns its new leaves edgewise. For several days at a time, around noon, a little grey light reaches into the interior. Things usually judged by touch or by the delay of a sound can be seen at once: the fine hair on a root, a shed animal skin snagged on a twig, the next ridge rearing up miles away out of the same hollow. From a shelter you can watch loose leaves falling between the two. Some descend until they are too small to see. Others catch in a current and pass upward again, past the shelter and past the crest. The light goes before the last of them has passed, but you can still hear them brushing the tips of the new growth.
