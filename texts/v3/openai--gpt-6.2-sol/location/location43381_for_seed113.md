The city ends one floor below its lowest street, in a room no wider than a small railway station. All its avenues have a last drain here. Their names are stamped into the lips of the gutters, where they can be read only by kneeling: Lime Street, the Parade, Little Orchard. In a hard rain you can tell which streets are paved in stone and which in brick. The brick streets run red for the first few minutes.

It hasn’t rained today. The walls are wet anyway.

The room is newly made. Along the back, where the builders left the rock exposed, each ledge has an edge sharp enough to pare a fingernail. Water beads there, fattens, lets go. Most of the floor is dark ceramic, laid in long, slightly tilted slabs with no grout between them. At intervals the seams draw apart by the width of a hair. The water lying on the slabs lifts in sheets and slips down into the openings without a splash. When the seams close, the floor is dry and warm for about a minute. Then moisture begins to gather again.

There is a great deal of city in the walls. Behind one thin panel, thousands of valves turn to admit or refuse fractions of a millimetre of water. A band of cloudy glass carries the weight of the buildings overhead, passing it sideways into the bedrock. At dusk, when the tram depot fills, the cloud in the glass drifts a little to the west. Yet the room itself is almost empty. There is a bench bolted beside the entrance and a square bed of ferns at the far end. Nothing interrupts the distance between them. From the bench, that small patch of green seems farther away than it is.

The ferns grow lushly in grit dredged from the drains. There are no worms in it, no flies under the fronds. There are no chewed edges on the leaves. New growth comes up pale and clenched, opens in the warmth, and darkens until it is difficult to distinguish from its own shadow. On the underside of one frond a row of drops hangs so evenly that it looks like part of the plant.

The noise comes in from elsewhere: tram wheels on the curve above, a long rush from a public bath emptying, the rattle of a loose cover in a street that can’t be seen. Sometimes the rock takes up a note and holds it after the machinery stops. Between those sounds the little drops on the ledges are quite distinct. You can hear two fall at once, one close by and one across the room.

The lights are set into the floor, and most of their light travels just above it. They pick out the bases of things: the first inch of a wall, the ends of the bench legs, the faint green stems below the mass of fern. Higher up, there is only enough light to see the ceiling shine. Late in the evening, water from the bakery on Lime Street makes its way down. For a few minutes the warm room smells of wet flour. Then the floor opens, the smell thins, and a drop at the tip of the longest frond grows heavy enough to bend it into the light.
