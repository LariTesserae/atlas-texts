The valley is higher than the mountains you can see from it. They lie below its western lip, their tops showing through a dimness that might be cloud. There is no corresponding view to the east. The ground slopes gently that way for six or seven miles and then stops at a line as level as the water in a bowl. Pebbles are worked into the turf along that line. None has fallen over.

It is cold enough that the turf hurts to kneel on. There isn’t much of it: a few black pads of sedge, a woolly growth that fits itself into cracks in the slate. Most of the valley is bare stone, worn smooth in long diagonal strips. Once, ice went through here. It left a pair of low ridges and a scattering of boulders with their pale undersides facing up. The ridges have slumped to the angle they can keep. Nothing looks freshly broken.

There is moisture wherever one stone overhangs another. Under some ledges it beads on the ceiling; beneath others it darkens the dust without making mud. In the shallowest part of the valley, water stands among flat stones a good distance apart, all at exactly the same level. The pools are connected somehow, although the intervening ground is dry. At the edge of one, a skein of fine brown roots hangs down from a single living blade. You can see each root distinctly, even in the poor light.

The noise is tremendous. At first it seems reasonable that there should be a waterfall below the lip: a whole valley’s drainage going over in one place. But it is just as loud on the far slope, and louder still inside a little roofless shelter halfway up. The shelter is made of the same slaty stone as everything else, three walls and a broad sill, with a finger’s width of green on the inward side. If you rest your hand on the sill, the heel of it goes numb from the cold. There is no vibration.

In the shelter you can hear the valley at a scale you can’t see. A rush of water, a clatter like small stones being dragged on a bed of larger ones. Separate from that, very close, the scratching of something finding a foothold. A pale bird stands on a boulder forty paces away. You can watch it pick its way to the end of the boulder and back, testing the surface with one foot. The scratching has nothing to do with its steps. It goes on for a little while after the bird has tucked its foot into its belly, then stops.

There are other traces of habitation, though none quite makes a route to the shelter. Two stones have been stood on end to make a passage barely wide enough for the shoulders. A flat slab has a hand-worn edge. On the back of it is the same worn edge, with the thumb hollow in the same place. The slab is thin enough that the hollows should meet. You can put a thumb in either and feel solid stone.

The light never improves. You get used to it and begin to distinguish things that seemed at first to be variations in the dark: a line of small animal droppings under a ledge, patches of mica, a second shelter across the valley. No smoke or warmth is to be expected there. Still, its opening faces this one, which makes it easy to sit on the sill and look at it for a long time. The sill takes the weight off your feet. Somebody chose its height well.

After a while you may see a drop fall from the ledge beside you. It strikes your sleeve and soaks in. The bead overhead is still there, the same size, and there is no wet track between it and the edge. This can happen often enough to soak a shoulder. It never amounts to rain. Nor does the water in the low places rise, or the roots reach any farther into it. The valley allows these little events without acquiring a history of them.

Toward the west, the roar is so wide that you can speak at an ordinary pitch and feel, from the way your throat moves, that you are making words. You can’t hear them. The mountains below have snow caught in their gullies, and you can follow the gullies down until the light fails. Nothing up here runs into them. Even the loose flakes of slate at your feet lie with their long edges across the slope, as if they had already slid as far as they were going to.
