At the height of the city, the mountains cast their shadows upward. You can see them on the underside of a dim band in the sky, every nick and saddle faithfully reproduced. The band drifts from west to east. The shadows stay where they are.

The city occupies three ridges and the space between them. From a distance it has the ordinary shape of a place that grew too large for its site: close-set houses on the good ground, narrower houses on the slopes, stairs wherever two streets could not be persuaded to meet. There are little courts behind the houses and broad public steps worn hollow in the middle. None of it was built. A seam in a doorstep can be followed down the face of the cliff for half a mile. The same seam runs through lintels, party walls, the worn lip of a bench. Whatever made the rock made these distinctions in it, down to the shallow recess just inside each doorway where a person might put something they meant to take out with them again.

There is nothing in the recesses.

The stone is a pale, cold gray with a skin like the inside of a broken cup. No frost gathers on it. In the deepest shade there is no damp. The basins at the foot of the ridges are white with powdered feldspar, and a fall of grit down one of their slopes leaves a dark track that takes most of the day to pale. This is how the country weathers. It has had time to lose its sharpness without losing a single street.

The stair down from the largest square is thirty-one steps long in the morning. Late in the day it is longer. The extra steps work their way into the flight one at a time, leaving the old hollows where they were. A person standing on the seventeenth step will find that the square has drawn away overhead without feeling any motion underfoot. Across the valley the opposite ridgeline does something similar. A pass opens in it, then a whole descending range, faint with distance. Sometimes the sight of that much country lasts only long enough for a stone to fall through it.

Little of this makes a sound. The air is cold and so thin that a pebble kicked across a court can be felt through the sole of the other foot after it has passed out of hearing. Once or twice a day a front of dust crosses the valley. It is visible chiefly by what it hides: first the low annexes on the far ridge, then the tall blank houses, then the returns of the streets. Where the front reaches a doorway, the dust goes on through, but the doorway may come out of it facing into a different room. The rooms themselves are seldom troubled. Fine material lies undisturbed in their corners in layers the color of old ash.

An animal comes into the square after the dust. It is gray on top and translucent along its belly, with six feet spread wide on the pavement. It examines a joint between two stones and puts its whole weight on one side of it. A narrow gap opens. Something no larger than a fingernail darts out and gets as far as the next joint before the gray animal takes it. Then the animal settles flat. In an hour it has moved only its eyes. Far down the ridge, another of its kind crosses an entire avenue without passing within sight of the first.

The public steps end at a broad landing above the white basins. There is room there for hundreds of people to stand, and a low parapet at just the height a crowd would lean on. Below it the cliff goes sheer for so far that the change in light becomes more apparent than the rock itself. The bottom is not always under the top. Toward evening it can be seen off to one side, with the cliff’s fallen stones arranged neatly at its foot. The stones are old and rounded. Several are the size of houses.

Darkness comes first to the high roofs, where there is no sky left between them and the mountain shadows. The streets below keep a little light. From the landing you can watch it retreat along an avenue on the third ridge, doorway by doorway, until it reaches a narrow house at the end. For a while the house is lit all the way through. Its back room opens onto another square, at an elevation from which the three ridges look low. There too the steps are hollowed in the middle. A thin line of dust descends them, reaches the last tread, and goes on descending after the view has closed.
