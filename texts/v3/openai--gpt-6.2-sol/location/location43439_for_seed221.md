The smallest wetland on the mountain is inside the head of a rivet.

The rivet is a little larger than it needs to be. It holds a seam in the uphill wall of a pumping station, well above the last open water. From the service walk you can see the other rivets in the row, each with its small white collar of frost. This one has a hole where the maker’s stamp should be. Look into it. The far wall is almost too close for your eye to find, but after a while you can make out a shore.

There is no soil. The bank is folded nickel, the flats are ground glass, and the pale bluff on the other side is the end of a ceramic spacer. At its foot lie seven angular stones, cast with the bluff and still joined to it by little webs of porcelain. The country has been given everything it needs to begin eroding except the ability to lose anything. Where a real bank would have slumped by now, the mold seam is still sharp.

It is very cold. Nonetheless, water wells up through the glass in sheets so shallow they turn the flats black without hiding the scratches. A meniscus runs the length of the bluff like a second, finer seam. There is ice where the nickel curls out over it. The two meet along a line that has not moved in all the years the station has been here. Hollow copper fingers, warm at the tips, keep the line where it is. They reach in from the rest of the machine, which is dark from here except for one amber inspection lamp. Most of its light goes into the ice.

At first there seem to be three animals. There is a white one nosing the underside of the bank, a clear one braced in the current, and something with a jointed tail at the waterline. Then the white animal folds down and you see the twenty or thirty it was standing on. The clear animal is scarcely thicker than the water around it, but its gut is full of smaller animals whose legs are still going. Under the overhang, in the dry space the width of a thumbnail paring, there are rows of mouths taking air. Every usable surface is in use. There is a black tuft on the northern bank that really is moss. A second tuft, farther up, turns around while you watch.

Put your ear near the rivet and the noise drives you back. The pumps contribute a low, even stroke, but the shrill part comes from the shore. The animals have hard little plates that knock against the glass when they feed. The bluff rings with it. Against that racket there is a sound like somebody slowly tearing a bedsheet, which is the rush of water through a slot too narrow to see. Now and then you think the noise has let up. It is only that you have stopped being able to hear one of its pitches.

There are no seasons in here, nor any hour at which the bank is less crowded. An animal dies under the nickel and something the same size backs into the opening before the water can take possession of it. The moss holds three green tips above the flood; under the flood its dead tips are taken away as fast as it makes them. A scratch on the glass is always bright at the wet end and dull at the dry. Were you to come back with a photograph taken during construction, you could match the shore to it exactly, down to the wet bulge beneath the smallest stone. The animals would all be different.

From the walk, the rivet looks slightly damp. The station keeper never wipes it. There is a note to that effect in the maintenance book, beside an old drawing of the seam. No reason is given. The drawing shows the hole, and, with a care the rest of the drawing does not receive, the little white lip that keeps the water from spilling out.
