The valley is underground, but it is not a hollow in the rock. It is a hollow in a plant. The far slope and the ceiling are continuations of the same folded leaf, and the long bare reach between them, which from a distance looks like a salt pan, is where the leaf is making more of itself.

It does this noisily. A white seam runs down the middle of the pan. Every few breaths a length of it unbuttons, and a curl of pale tissue pushes up through the opening, flinging brittle scales to either side. Animals come running before the scales land. Some eat them; others snatch them out of the air and wear them, wedging the curled pieces between the ridges of their backs. A particularly large scale can set off a pursuit that crosses the entire pan. The tissue left behind is dry and warm to the touch. You can press a thumbnail into it and watch the dent move sideways as the leaf grows.

There is no water here. Split a stem and its cut faces shine, but nothing wells out. The shine is a thin, clear wax, soft in the heat of the plant and hard wherever a draft reaches it. On windward slopes, the youngest shoots grow with one side furred and the other side glazed. The little animals that feed on them turn each shoot over first, looking for the softer side.

In the middle of the valley there is enough room to see weather coming. A gust lifts the flocks out of a grove, turns the undersides of ten thousand leaves toward you, and stops at the foot of the opposite slope. The grove keeps turning after the air has gone still. Some of its leaves are animals that let go only when their neighbors do; some are leaves that tear themselves loose and take root where they fall. From a low ridge you can watch a strip of woodland lose its canopy, cross a clearing, and settle on the other side. It takes most of an afternoon. The trunks stand bare until the next growth comes through, and for that brief interval you can see the marks of teeth running in spirals all the way to their tips.

The light is ordinary enough to judge the color of a stone by, if there were a stone. It comes through the thinnest folds overhead, broad and cream-colored, with no bright point to look away from. Things cast soft shadows. Under one fold it is possible to see the dark shapes of animals walking on the other side. A procession passed there recently, leaving prints that have begun to swell into buds.

Nothing has worn smooth. The cliffs have sharp, unfinished corners; the gullies are splits forced open from underneath. The finest dust is waste from things that grow, not from things that crumble. In the deepest split, the walls have not yet learned which of them is the floor. They put out shoots toward each other. Animals run along both faces, dropping from one to the other when the gap gets narrow enough, with a small hitch in their gait as they turn over.

There are a few shelters high on the valley wall. Someone made them by pegging living ribs together with wedges of hard thorn. The roofs have since pulled tight enough to sing. Below the oldest shelter, a stair cut with a hand adze rises out of the slope and stops at shoulder height: the bottom three steps have grown more in a season than the top six. A new first step has been cut beside it. The chips are still lying there. They look like the chips left by any carpenter, except that the larger ones have put down roots.

Even up at the shelters the noise is too great for speech. The nearest sounds are quite distinct: the batting of wings against a stem, the rasp of two animals trying to get past each other in a narrow fork. Behind them is a continuous dry concussion as the broad leaves take the weight of what travels over them. Now and then an entire slope seems to cough. The inhabitants put a hand on the wall to find out whether that one came from below.

At the far end of the pan, a ridge has been rising all day. Its crest is still white. Animals crowd along both sides, watching for the next split, their backs nearly touching. A few have climbed to the top and are walking along it with exaggerated care. Then the ridge leans, not far, and they all step downhill together. For a moment there is a clear strip of ground where none has stood before. In the even light you can see that it is already green.
