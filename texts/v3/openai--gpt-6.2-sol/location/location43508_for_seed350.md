The plain is underwater, but it has not been underwater long enough to become a seabed. There are still corners on the stones. A whole reach of black pavement has lifted in the middle and stands on edge, its painted line unbroken. Beyond it, fresh ground goes out in low, broad steps toward a darkness that might be five miles away. There is nothing high enough to get a view from. The tallest thing for miles is a maintenance shed, and its roof is six feet above its doorstep.

It is warm at every depth. Warm water comes up through the joints in the ground, and warm water comes down in sheets from what ought to be the surface. Where the two meet, the suspended grit changes direction so abruptly that it seems to strike an invisible floor. Then the floor tilts. For a few seconds the entire plain is full of falling grit, all of it falling sideways.

Everything grows. Stiff red stems have come up through the asphalt while the broken edges are still clean. Their leaves are as wide as doors, with pale ribs you can see through when a light goes by. The leaves overlap in six or seven layers, each moving at a different speed. Clear roots fill the space underneath. Here and there a current folds the growth flat and exposes an acre of new stone, bare except for the roots combing over it. By the time the stems stand up again there are green points in the stone. There are no bites out of the leaves. No eggs in the folds, no small thing waiting for the current to let up.

The machinery is mostly underground. You come upon a flywheel turning in a slot scarcely wider than itself, or a row of ceramic insulators sticking out of a bank of fern. One shed contains a switchboard and nothing else, not even the wall the switchboard was mounted on. Miles away, a pump with a brass nameplate is bolted directly to the rock. The nameplate is bright. Whatever the machines were built to do, they are doing it at a scale the few little buildings do not account for. When a contact closes in the shed, a broad strip of vegetation bows down clear across the plain.

The noise is continuous and much too loud to locate. A low pounding comes through the bones of the face. Over it, relays clatter, stone breaks, and water goes through narrow places at a pitch that hurts the teeth. Sometimes the pounding stops, and the relief is so great that it takes a moment to notice the leaves are still being driven flat by each stroke. The sound comes back all at once, including the strokes you missed.

There is a road between the shed and the pump. Going one way, you pass a concrete post with a blue glass reflector. Going the other, you pass the same post three times. The first reflector is intact; the second has a little white star in it; the third is a bare socket with a fresh chip in the concrete below. A chip of just that shape hangs in the water beside the first post. You can close your hand around it. This doesn’t seem to settle anything.

Most of the light comes from work lamps set low to the ground. They still shine straight ahead even where the posts have been pushed over. In their shallow beams you can see new banks thrust up, thin as knife blades, and split to admit water. The plain changes faster than the lamps can be moved. One shines into a cut that has been filling and reopening all afternoon. There is a patch of yellow leaves at the bottom. Every time the cut closes, the leaves vanish between two clean faces of stone; every time it opens, there are more of them.

Far out, a pale front is moving in. At first it looks like the only calm water in the place. Then it reaches the outer beds, and row after row of broad leaves turns its white underside toward you. The front crosses the road. The lamps go dark one by one, although you can still see their beams laid out on the ground. Under the roar comes the hard click of a contact closing. In the maintenance shed, the only dry thing for miles is the dust on the inside of the gauge. Its needle is coming up slowly to **NORMAL**.
