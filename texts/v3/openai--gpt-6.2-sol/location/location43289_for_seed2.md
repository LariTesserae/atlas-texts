At noon you can see the next ridge, eight miles away. It is the blue of a hill seen through summer haze, although the water here is cold enough to ache in the knuckles. The ridge looks solid until a school of herring turns in front of it and the silver runs, unbroken, between the uprights.

Nothing here was raised by the earth. The ridges are the folds of a steel floor, stiffened with concrete underneath. From one crest to the next the floor drops in long, flat pitches, each poured against boards whose grain is still legible. In the valleys are expansion joints wide enough for a seal to swim down. The metal has not had time to round off or take on much color. Where a pipe crew cut through a partition, a fringe of bright burrs still catches the light.

The whole basin lies below the old shore, though a few fathoms of clear water are all that cover its highest ground. Above a ridge the surface is close enough for the shadow of a passing gull to cross the concrete. Down in the valleys it takes the whole morning for the sun to reach the floor. There are lamps there, ordinary white industrial lamps, in reflectors the size of kitchen tables. They show the floor, the numbered joints, and a little of the water immediately above them. Another lamp is visible in the distance long before you can see what it is fixed to.

There is very little to interrupt a crossing. You might swim for an hour past the same seam, with the same flat gray on either side and the same bright roof of water overhead. Then the seam ends at a cast-iron grate, and there are crabs in every square. They drop through as your shadow passes. A minute later their claws appear again, feeling along the edges. Kelp grows in a narrow strip wherever the grate traps enough sand to hold its weight. The rest of the floor is too new and too clean. In a hundred miles there is not a meadow, but there are fish enough to turn a valley dark.

The pumps are a long way off. You hear them everywhere. The low note is so steady you stop noticing it until a station shuts down and the bolts ticking in their collars become audible one by one. Then a gate opens somewhere else. Water striking its far side makes a sound like a loaded freight train going over a bridge. The sound arrives well ahead of the current. By the time the kelp lies flat, the herring have left the grate and the seals are waiting over the lee slope, where the small fish have to cross open water.

On the scale of the basin these openings and closings make weather. Water let in from the north is clear and cold; water returned across the southern shallows has had all day to warm. Where the two meet, a wavering boundary travels down a valley, lifting crumbs of mortar and the fine shells of dead plankton. Sometimes it hangs in one place for hours. Looking down through it, you see the floor very plainly, then not at all, then plainly again. Miles away, where an intake draws the warm layer down, the surface puckers into a field of little steep waves. A boat crossing from one side to the other would seem to enter a wind that wasn’t there.

Toward evening the uphill gates close in sequence. There is no single moment of quiet. One part of the basin drains into another; a reach that has been still since dawn begins to move. The last sunlight picks out a clean white line along a ridge where the paint crew stopped. Beneath it, on water that will take most of the night to reach the next valley, a scatter of newly hatched fish drifts over concrete with nothing on it yet.
