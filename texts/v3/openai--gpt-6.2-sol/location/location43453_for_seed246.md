At the top of the pass there is a town with nothing standing in it.

You come into it between two long banks of turf. The lane is broad enough for three people to walk abreast, though in places the grass leans so far in that all three would get wet. At the first turning there is a little square, then a narrower lane that goes behind the square and gives onto six plots of nearly equal size. You can see the whole town from there. It would take ten minutes to walk around it, even stopping at the corners. Beyond it the plateau goes on bare for miles.

The mountains here are old enough to have lost their points. From the valley they look high and severe; up among them the ground rises and falls as gently as a blanket over somebody’s feet. The rock comes to the surface in broad gray patches with soft edges. Rain has worn the softer veins out of it, leaving seams deep enough to take the tip of a finger. On the exposed ground those seams hold a little grit and last winter’s dead grass. In the town they hold soil black enough to stain your hands.

It’s cold even in full sun. A shower comes over at noon, and for a few minutes every plot has a pool in its lowest corner. Then the sun is out again and the water is gone from all but two of them. Down at ankle height the town is lush: tight green sedges, moss with red stalks, a kind of creeping willow whose trunks lie flat under its leaves. The willows grow thickly on the banks but never across the lanes. Turn over a branch and you find fine white roots growing from its underside. Turn it back. On a clear afternoon the wind can dry those roots before the next cloud arrives.

The loudest thing is under the square. Water drops through a crack there and falls a long way before it hits anything. In dry weather you can hear the individual drops; after a shower the falling water makes a steady racket, like hard rain on a roof. You can have the sun on your face and shout to be heard by someone on the other side of the square. Twenty paces down a side lane the noise cuts out. There you hear the willow leaves tick against one another, and, if you stand still long enough, something small working its way through the grass. Once in a while a pipit comes down to feed along the wet margins. It hops over the lane in a single flight. There’s nowhere higher than your knee for it to land.

On the downhill side, three lanes end in a low grassy step. The middle one has moved since the last frost. You can tell by the torn roots at its edge and by a strip of pale stone newly exposed in the lane. There’s no masonry beneath the sod. There are mats of living roots, mats of dead ones, and thin gray layers of silt laid down between them. When the soil freezes it lifts the banks a little at a time. When it thaws, water takes the loose earth out of the lanes and leaves some of it on the inside of each bend. At one corner a bank has begun to grow across an old turning. The rain is already opening another just uphill.

From the far side of the pass, where you can see the plan best, there’s scarcely anything to cast a shadow. The plots look like rooms because the grass inside them lies flat and the grass at their edges stands up. The lanes meet at angles that seem perfectly ordinary until you try to follow one with your eye. Then you notice how many of them turn just in time to miss a patch of bare rock, how the square has spread to take in a second drain. Below it, in a plot no bigger than a kitchen, the last of the noon rain is still standing. All afternoon the wind pushes it from one side of the plot to the other. By evening a rim of ice has formed at both ends, and there is still a little water moving between them.
