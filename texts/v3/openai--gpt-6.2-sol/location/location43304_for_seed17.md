The high country is six hundred metres underwater.

It rises out of a basin so broad that its farther wall has never been seen from this one. Even here, on the crown of the ridge, there is no shoreward feeling. The water overhead is cold and clear and lit to an ordinary daytime brightness. You can see the pale backs of your gloves and the individual grains in a piece of broken stone. Look up, though, and the blue goes on without a bright spot to tell you where the surface is.

The ridge is young. There are long black faces where the rock split, and ledges thin enough to let blue light through at the edges. Nothing has rounded them. At the foot of a cliff lie three slabs that fit the gaps above them so exactly you can tell which fell first. No silt has gathered in the joints. There is no weed, no fur of growth on the sunny side of a stone. The only soft-looking thing on the whole slope is the water, and when you put a hand through it the cold finds the seams in your glove.

The noise is tremendous. There is a low note you feel first in your teeth, and over it a continuous sound like sheet metal being dragged across a concrete floor. It does not come and go. There is no surf down here, no wash against the ledges. A dropped washer falls straight down, turning slowly enough for the stamped number to come into view twice before it strikes. You don't hear it strike. If someone takes your elbow and shouts into the side of your helmet, you feel the tug at your elbow.

At intervals of a hundred kilometres or so, a machine straddles a fissure. Each has the same small deck, the same porcelain insulators, the same three legs sunk into the basalt. Stand on the deck and you can feel that the machine is running, but its parts turn too slowly to account for the noise. The old maintenance drawings call these installations *governors*. What they govern is nowhere specified. Inside the nearest one, under a hinged steel cover, a needle holds at the midpoint of an unnumbered dial. There is a clean thumbprint in the grease on the hinge. It was there when the present crew first came down.

There are animals in surprising numbers, though you can travel for hours without seeing one. They gather under the outward lips of the cliffs, where rows of flat white bodies hang edge-on to the open water. From above, a colony looks like one pale scratch. From below, there are thousands. Their mouths open in sequence along the rows, taking something too small to see, or too clear. Once in a while a dark animal the length of a railway carriage comes level with the ridge. The white bodies fold as its shadow passes, then open in the same order. Out in the basin the animal grows smaller for a long time. You can still see it after you have lost any sense of how fast it is going.

The survey stakes say the ridge has not shifted in eighty years. The cold at the top agrees with the cold at the bottom to the last decimal the instruments can give. Even the noise, recorded a generation apart, rises and falls by the same few fractions of a note. Yet there is a place, just past the third governor, where a crack runs across the crest and stops halfway through a ledge. The two sides have opened just enough to admit the blade of a knife. Looking at it, you have the distinct feeling of having arrived a moment after something happened.

You can follow that crack to the edge. Beyond it the land drops steeply for four thousand metres. In the clear water the first hundred look near enough to touch; after that the scale gives out. On the opposite rise there is a single white speck. With a good lens you can make out a deck, three legs, and a handrail with one corner bent inward. There is nothing between the two machines but water, and the full weight of the sound.
