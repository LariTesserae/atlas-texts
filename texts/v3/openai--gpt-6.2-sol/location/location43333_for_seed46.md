High on the sunward side of the mountain is a cleft just wide enough to put a hand into. From below it looks like a white seam in the rock. Only at noon, when the sun reaches the back of it, can you see that it opens downward into a little basin. The heat rising out of it smells of split mica and bruised leaves.

The basin is barely three feet across. Its floor is new enough to have a skin. Under that skin, black mineral presses up in blunt knobs, not yet decided into crystals. There is no sand. The pale grains at the edges are still attached to the rock, each on a hair-thin stalk. Nothing has come through to knock them off: no rain, no roots prying at the seam, not even a gust with room to gather itself.

Four plants grow there, each far enough from the others to cast its whole shadow on bare ground. They are luxuriant out of all proportion to their number. Their leaves overlap in cool green tiers, broad at the base and soft at the tip, and the newest ones are almost yellow in the sunlight. The leaves are dry clear through. Where one has split, the two sides of the tear bristle with fine copper-colored filaments. A grain moves along one of these, hesitates at a fork, and takes the lower branch. It will reach the stem sometime after dark.

Under each plant, the mountain is drawing wire.

There is no spool and there are no rollers. A red thread comes out through a hole in the stone, lengthens a fraction, and hangs in the heat. All four threads lengthen by the same amount. At long intervals there is a click like a contact closing in an empty factory, and every leaf turns a little on its stem. Then the basin is quiet again. There are no insects to pick through the folds of the leaves, no old nests, not a single thing that has come here to eat.

You can follow a thread down with your eye, but not all the way to its end. Somewhere below the floor there is more room than the mountain has in it. This is easiest to see when the sun is ordinary and bright, and nothing else about the place asks to be doubted. The thread catches the light once just under the lip, then again at a distance at which the whole mountain ought to be a blue shape on the horizon. The second glint is no smaller than the first.

An old leaf lies flat between two of the plants. The rock is growing over it from one side. From the other, the leaf is still unfolding. In the middle there is a narrow strip of green that neither process has reached, and across it runs a vein in which the grains are moving uphill, one at a time.

By late afternoon the cleft is white again from below. On the mountain face, a few inches to the left of it, the shadow of a leaf remains in full sun. Over the next hour it acquires a second lobe. There is no leaf in the basin with that shape yet.
