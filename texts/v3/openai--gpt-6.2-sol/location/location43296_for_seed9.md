The garden takes up one bay at the top of a rainworks. From the door you can see its far wall, though you can’t walk straight to it: the floor rises in three wet steps, each crowded with leaves, and between the steps are gutters wide enough to put a foot in by mistake. Beyond the waist-high parapet there are six hundred feet of open air. In clear weather you can see the roofs of the lower works. This morning you can see the shadow of the parapet on a bank of cloud.

There is no soil. The banks are made of porous red blocks, fitted together with a thumb’s width between them for roots. The deeper beds are packed with pumice fired on the floor below. Somebody has scratched a date into the mortar at the head of each channel. Even the oldest date is from this year. Water has yet to round off the sharp lip of a weir; it goes over in a thin, straight sheet that keeps the little notches left by the trowel. In the bottom basin, a fan of pink grit has gathered. It fits under a fingernail. This is all the ground the place has made for itself so far.

The plants make it look much older. A climbing pepper has gone twice around the pipe it was given and now reaches for the chain on the inspection hatch. Ferns shoulder out over the steps. In one corner, where warm condensate enters through a grille, the moss has grown thick enough to carry the imprint of the grille’s screws. Stand there and the warmth comes up through your trouser legs. A few feet away, air off the height moves cool across the backs of your hands. The light is ordinary late-morning light, good enough to read the lettering on a valve. When rain passes through, the lettering is still legible under the water.

Most of what happens here makes almost no sound. Under the slatted walk a float comes up, a counterweight descends, and a whole bed of reeds tilts a little as water enters it from below. Farther along, another bed is draining. A root that was submerged a minute ago hangs white in the air, drawing its last drop down to a point. At intervals a motor starts on the other side of the wall, and the water standing in the cups of the leaves shows a fine tremor. Then the motor stops. There is a click from the counterweight, never quite where you’re listening for it.

On a dry afternoon the parapet sometimes rains inward. Its hollow coping takes the overflow from the upper beds, and the little holes on its underside were drilled at a slant to keep the water from going over the edge. Young vines have found those holes. They loop up from the floor and put their growing tips into the dark, so that the coping now drips in some places and in others sends water back down a stem. One vine has reached a bolt and split neatly around it. There is a small green leaf on either side of the bolt head.

Animals are scarce at this height. There’s a single water strider in the bottom basin. When the basin empties, it steps off the retreating surface onto the red block and waits. Now and then a swift comes level with the parapet, close enough for the dry sound of its wings to carry across the bay. It’s gone before anything below it has moved.

At the far end there’s a fourth step, still bare. The blocks there are pale and warm, with the lifting marks chalked on their sides. A capped pipe sticks out above them. Nothing is meant to grow there until the pipe is opened, but water has been creeping sideways along a mortar joint from the next bed. You can follow its progress by the darkening of the red. At the very end of the joint, where the mason wiped the mortar smooth with a finger, there are two unfolding fern leaves, each no bigger than the date scratched beside them.
