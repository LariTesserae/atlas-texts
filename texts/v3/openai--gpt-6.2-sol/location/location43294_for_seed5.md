At the bottom of the city, three steps below the lowest street, there is a garden the size of a bed. You can see it through a square hole in a wall, but only if you get down on your knees. Most people take the hole for an old drain. It was a drain once; you can still follow the groove left by water in the sill, deep at the center and polished on either lip.

The city is built of white stone on a plain hardly higher than the flats outside it. At noon the walls throw light into one another, all the way down the narrow streets, and some of that light reaches the garden through a shaft no wider than a hand. There is no direct sun. The far wall is nevertheless bright enough to show each black spore on the underside of a fern. The cold is just as thorough. Put your hand inside and the backs of your knuckles chill first; by the time you draw it out, you can feel the warmth of the street on them.

There is scarcely any soil you could lift with a spoon. The roots are in the joints of the masonry, or wound through old lime, or laid flat against the rock in brown mats. Over those mats grow mosses with stems long enough to comb, and over the mosses lie the stiff, glossy leaves of a little evergreen whose trunk, thick as a thumb, takes four turns before reaching the light. Not a leaf has much room. A fern frond goes up the wall on one side and comes down the other side of a projecting stone. Where two leaves press together they are pale, almost white. If you part them, you find a bead of water, though the sill under your elbows is dry enough to dust your sleeves.

The builders left a thumb-deep basin below the old drain. Its rim is worn down on one side, from the years when someone must have dipped a cup there. Now it is ordinarily dry. After a night of rain the upper stones darken, one at a time, and for several days there is a little water in the basin. The moss draws it off at the edges. Nothing falls far enough to make a sound.

For the first while it seems there are no animals. Then a gray patch on the sill separates into seven small moths. Behind them, where the stone has scaled away, the entire cavity is fitted with snail shells, some occupied, some full of eggs laid by other things. The snails crop the moss along the same narrow margin; you can see the old feeding line beneath the new one. Once in a while a white-legged creature crosses the exposed stone, stops where the light is strongest, and backs into the crack it came out of. There is always more life visible after you hold still, even when none of it appears to have moved.

In the street above, people set wedges with wooden mallets and bore holes in lintels with sand and a bow drill. Down here the tool marks are nearly gone. A stone pierced for a hinge has a root passing through the hole, and the root is now wider on either side than the hinge could ever have been. The little basin is the only thing in the garden that still declares what it was made for. Even the mortar has settled into rounded ledges that catch leaf mold. The street could be repaved, and has been, without anyone putting a hand through the opening.

A child can find the garden, grow old, and come back to the same green fork in the same forked shadow. The fern will have shed its fronds many times. There will be a fresh snail in the recess where the old one was. The likeness is exact enough to make the visitor doubt, not that the garden changes, but that they remember it at all. Then they put an elbow on the sill and find, without looking, the one place where the stone does not hurt.
