The hollow is no wider than a stand of thirty trees, though there are only four trees in it. You come upon it just below the crest, after a long climb through dry grass. The air at the lip is warm and moving; two steps down, it is warmer and still. From the upper end you can look straight across to the far rim, and beyond it to the tops of clouds passing below the mountain.

Water comes out of the uphill wall in hundreds of places. No one thread of it would fill a cupped hand, but together they make a noise like rain beating on broad leaves, loud enough that you have to raise your voice. The wall is old gray stone, rounded even in its recesses. Water follows shallow grooves across it, hesitating at the same little ledges where it has hesitated for centuries. Where the grooves come together, the stone is stained the rusty orange of a split apricot.

The floor is mostly bare. This is what makes the growth so startling: a dozen paces of pale, wet rock, then a fern bank up to your waist. The four trees stand well apart, each rooted in a pocket where the stone has weathered to clay. Their branches are loaded with moss and small glossy plants growing in the forks. There is plenty of daylight between the crowns. The wet leaves flash white when the wind reaches down far enough to turn them, which it does once or twice in an afternoon.

At the low end, the water spreads to the depth of a thumbnail and disappears into a seam. A few green blades stand in the sheet, so far apart that each has its own reflection. A small brown frog lives there. You might see it on your first visit and not on your next ten. Its call is a dry wooden knock, nothing like the sound you would expect a creature that small to make. There is never an answering knock.

If you spend the afternoon beside the seam, you may notice that the falling water occasionally goes quiet. The drops keep striking the fern leaves. Threads still run down the face of the stone. In the silence you can hear the grass on the crest rubbing in the wind. Then the water sounds again, all at once, at exactly its former loudness. There is no corresponding change in the wall or in the little sheet at your feet.

Nothing marks the way in. There is, near the lip, a piece of slate that fits the hand and has an edge sharp enough to cut a stem. If you pick it up, the stone beneath is dry in the shape of it. By the time you leave, the patch will be wet. The larger changes take longer: a root pries a flake loose, the flake settles into the seam, and over many summers a trickle is persuaded to take a different route. From the crest the hollow looks much as it always has. Only one fern, halfway down the wall, begins to grow on the other side of its crack.
