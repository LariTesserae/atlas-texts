The town stands high enough that the stream below it is visible in three separate reaches. Between them it passes under shelves of black rock. From the upper streets you can see the little bends in the water but hear nothing of it. Even down on the stepping stones, there is only a thin ticking where a thread of water enters a hollow.

The mountain looks freshly broken. Its ledges have square noses, and some of the larger slabs still fit their beds so neatly that a mason could take their measure without getting up from his lunch. There is good soil, but not much of it. It collects behind walls and in seams across the slope, where it grows enormous ferns, sour grass, and low trees with waxy leaves. On the open rock between these patches you can walk a long way without brushing against anything. The air is cool. A wall can be warm enough to sit against on one side and beaded with water on the other.

There are streets, drains, stairs, a market, and a whole run of public ovens, though you can cross several wards and meet no one. In places the houses have been built two deep against the cliff, with a passage between the front row and the back. Elsewhere a paved street goes on beyond the last house. It has a gutter, a mounting block, and sockets for the posts of awnings that were never put up. The paving is fitted with small stone wedges; at a bend you can see where somebody ran out of wedges and used the thick rims of broken pots.

Animals make more use of the town than its inhabitants do. Hares sit under the market benches. A small herd of shaggy cattle keeps to the grassy strip between two flights of steps, leaving the steps themselves to the people. There are owls in the covered drains, and on bright days the court of the cistern is full of lizards. Little brown birds forage in the fern beds. Their calls are so brief and widely spaced that it is possible to hear one and spend a long time deciding whether you heard it at all.

Things happen here, but they do not add up to change. Rain falls, though the soil is no wetter at the end of a shower than it was at the beginning. The ferns put out new fronds; underneath them are just enough old fronds lying flat to keep the bed the same height. The stream carries a little grit. If you watch at the first bend you can see the grains roll out from beneath a pebble and go downstream. At the last bend there is a crack from which the same pale grit works its way back up.

The town has its part in this. A woman takes the lid off a drain with a wooden lever and pulls out a mat of roots. She lays the roots on a wall for the birds. In the time it takes her to replace the lid, a thin white root has found its way across the bottom of the channel. She checks that the lid sits square, shoulders her lever, and goes home. She has not wasted her morning. If she did not clear the drain, the steps would be slick by the time the children came down them. This has never happened.

Above the roofs, the road crosses a bare shoulder before dropping into the next ward. There is a bench there, out of the damp. In one direction you can see the ovens and their little wood yards; in the other, a mile of rock and green shelves, with a single house beyond them. A cloth has been spread to dry on its parapet. From the bench it is too far away to tell whether it is moving.
