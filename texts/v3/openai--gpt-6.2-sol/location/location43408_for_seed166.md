The hollow in the limestone is about the size of a chapel, though there is no roof to distinguish its water from the water above. On three sides the rock rises in pale, rounded shelves. On the fourth it dwindles into a sill low enough that, from the middle of the hollow, you can see the empty seabed continuing beyond it. The floor is a single spread of gray sand. Near the walls you can see where it thins to individual grains caught in the pores of the stone.

There is enough daylight to tell gray from brown. The water takes the warmth out of both. At the upper rim, a few strips of brown algae lie flat against the rock; lower down there is only a green stain on the ledges that face upward. Nowhere is there a shell, a track, or the little heap of clean sand that would mark the mouth of a burrow. A dark spot on the floor, which at first looks like the shadow of something suspended above it, is just a piece of flint showing through.

Far off, machinery is running. The sound reaches the hollow as a low, even note, without any of the clatter it might have in air. There is no cable crossing the sill, no pipe laid along the rock. The machinery has no visible part in this place. It is simply one of the things the water carries, like daylight, from somewhere too far away to see.

The limestone has lost its sharp corners, but it has kept its small distinctions. One shelf overhangs the next by the width of a finger. A seam runs level through two walls and disappears under sand before it reaches the third. Where water once came over the sill hard enough to move gravel, it scooped a shallow crescent out of the inner slope. The gravel is still there, each pebble sunk to its widest point. There is almost no current now. Even the narrowest ends of the algae stay down.

It takes a while to notice that the hollow has a far side. The water between is clear enough that nothing announces the distance until you compare the pits in the rock at your feet with the pits across the sand. They are the same size. On the far wall, the smallest ones are just at the limit of what you can see. The low note goes on, and the sand beneath them stays smooth.
