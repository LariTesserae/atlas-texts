The new ward is a block and a half wide. From the tram you can see both ends of it: the brick arch where the old city stops, and the fence where, a hundred and twenty paces later, the old city starts again. The tram runs around it. There is no great drop or rise to account for the detour. The ward lies a little lower than the surrounding streets, on what used to be the floor of a municipal reservoir. On still days it is colder down there by several degrees.

Rain seldom reaches the paving. There are too many balconies, stairs, ducts, and undersides in the way. Instead, a day or two after rain, a seam in a wall darkens at waist height, or a drop falls from the underside of a handrail. Along the lowest curb are shallow rectangles of standing water, each the size of a letter. In the morning they have skins of ice. You can put a boot through one without getting your sock wet.

Almost every surface down there was made by someone, but the stone is still coming in. It is pale gray, with an open, sugary grain. It pushes up through the tramless street in broad, unfinished slabs, taking the cast of whatever it meets. Under the oldest stair you can see the impression of the wire basket a workman left there two winters ago. The basket itself is still in use. So far there is no soil except what blows in from outside. Moss has established itself in the rubber track of a loading door that has never closed properly. A thin stand of grass grows under the public telephone, just beyond the reach of people’s shoes.

The builders put the upper stories in first. They are narrow red and cream buildings, each with its own drains and service stairs, closely packed and perfectly ordinary in the midday light. The dimensions of the lower stories are still being settled. A grocer on the third floor has two delivery doors. One admits a trolley from the alley; the other admits the same trolley from the landing below, with its front wheels already wet. The delivery driver uses whichever door is nearer. On the invoice there is a box for where the wheels got wet, which nobody fills in.

There are municipal instruments under the paving, though the limit of their work is hard to place. In the survey office a brass strip on the floor marks the length of the ward. Once a year a woman comes down from the city and walks the strip in stocking feet while an apprentice walks the ward outside. They start together and knock at the ends. In the first year the apprentice was back in time to hear the woman finish. Last year he brought a sandwich. No building has moved with respect to its neighbor. The street numbers are all still in order.

For the most part it is easy to live here. The light is good enough for sewing beside a window. You can smell hot bread over the colder smell of wet concrete, and on some afternoons there is a faint laundry warmth in the passage behind the grocer. The buildings hold sound badly. A spoon dropped in an upstairs kitchen comes through quite distinctly at one paving joint and nowhere a step to either side. People who want to talk privately stand in the middle of the street.

A blackbird comes down from the old city in the late afternoon. It works the line of the curb, turning the little pieces of ice with its bill. There is usually nothing underneath. Sometimes there is a strand of red worm in the meltwater, though nobody who has lifted a slab has found enough earth to keep a worm alive. The bird spends perhaps five minutes in the ward and leaves by the same route, through the open square in a fire escape. After that there is very little movement. A drip fattens under the rail. Someone at a lit window leans over to see if it has fallen.

At the far fence there is a place where the paving ends in a soft, square edge. It had that edge last winter, too, a little farther back. Behind it, in the new stone, is the clean impression of a row of boot heels. Ahead of it there is nothing yet that will take a print.
