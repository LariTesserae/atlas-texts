High on the plateau, where the walking is easy and there is nothing left for miles that could be called a hill, a slab of limestone has worn thin enough to let the day through at one edge. Under it is a town. You could cover the whole of it by lying down and stretching out your arms, though you would crush the west quarter with your elbow.

The roof is low. Near the opening it gives off the heat of a breadstone; farther in, it is just warm to the touch. The floor was level long before anything lived here, rubbed down by ages of wind to a finish that shows the grain of the rock. The people make use of every fault in that finish. A shallow crease takes the main road. A series of pits, each no bigger than a lentil, holds the storage jars. Houses are built from flaked stone set on edge and roofed with the husks of seeds. There isn't room to stand a house away from its neighbor. When somebody needs another room, they build over the road, leaving enough headway underneath for a person with a load.

Nothing drips here. The pale fringe at the entrance looks like the margin of a dried pool, but it is stone dust banked up by the wind. The plants grow in it and on it and through it. Their crowded leaves are no thicker than paper at the tip and as fat as a grub at the stem. Turn one over and it is almost white. Break one and there is no juice, only a soft curl of green wax with a smell like a snapped bean. The roots run bare along the ceiling for much of the town, slipping into the stone wherever they find a grain they can part from its neighbors. In the deepest shade the leaves hang low enough to brush the roofs. People cut them back over the public ways and let them grow thick over the houses.

It takes a while to see the streets because so much is crossing them. The smallest animals run in the dust between the paving stones. Larger ones barge through the low leaf cover, carrying the dust on their backs. Mites ride on beetles; pale little hunters ride on the mites and drop off where they find something to eat. Under each seed husk there seems to be a congregation of legs waiting for the dark. At the bright edge of the slab, a row of burrows opens all at once and the runners come out, scattering so fast that for a moment they look like the shadow of a single animal breaking apart.

Inside, the noise is greater than you would expect from anything this small. The roof keeps it in. A seed being cut open ticks against a stone. The little mill under the hillward wall grates steadily as two people take turns pushing its handle. Wings buzz against leaves overhead, and every so often there is a clatter from a whole lane as a shelled animal comes through too wide for it. Door shutters close in succession ahead of the clatter. After it passes, they open again. Someone comes out with a brush to sweep up what it has knocked loose.

The town has no wall around it. There would be little point: most of what gets in comes through the roof or out of the ground. Instead there are low gates at the ends of the streets, woven with the stiff hairs of burrowing animals. They're good at turning back the seed thieves, which hate to have their feelers touched. The gatekeepers sit close enough to talk across a road and work the gates with their feet. Their seats are hollows worn into the stone, and at one gate the hollow has grown so deep that a new ledge has been fitted in for the keeper's heels.

Late in the day, the sun finds the slit under the slab. A narrow bar of light travels in at floor level, under the bridges and across the thresholds, reaching rooms whose roofs have never seen it. The winged animals come down into that warmth. The runners go up the walls after them. For a little while the main road is impassable, and most people stop where they are. They can tell how far the light has got by which doors are open.

Then the bar narrows to a thread and goes out. Above the slab, the plateau cools in the wind. Below it, the stone will give back heat all night. The mill starts up again. In the west quarter, where a large animal has dislodged a roof, three neighbors are already lifting a husk into place. They hold it a little crooked while the owner guides a living stem through the seam, so there will still be shade there tomorrow.
