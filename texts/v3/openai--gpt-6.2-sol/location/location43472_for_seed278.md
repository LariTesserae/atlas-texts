At the height of the plateau, rain seldom makes it all the way to the ground. It comes slanting out of a low cloud and dwindles in the last few hundred feet. You walk through the shadow of a shower without getting wet. Later, where a slab of stone overhangs the slope, you find its underside beaded with water. The beads grow all afternoon, touch, and draw together. Only the largest fall. They have been falling in the same places long enough to put little cups in the stone below.

The plateau is high without being mountainous. From most places there is nothing higher to look at. Even the passes are broad, and a person crossing one might notice the descent only by the way water has collected on the other side. In the west, shelves of dark shale overlap for hundreds of miles, their edges worn soft enough to lie down on. Farther east is a pale country of gravel that runs to the horizon in every direction. It has taken so long to wash the fines out of that gravel that the stones on top have grown skins darker than the rock they broke from. Turn one over and the bright face looks freshly broken, though it may not have seen the sky since there was ice over the passes.

There are no trees. Here and there a plant the size of a hand has grown down into the seams of a slab, where the damp lingers after the stone dries. Its old growth is almost as hard as the slab itself. At the margin there will be a few yellow leaves, blunt and very small. The animals crop these without disturbing the rest. In a sheltered seam you can find a plant with a palm’s breadth of untouched leaves, and, fitted exactly into the shelter beside it, the jaw of the animal that used to feed there.

You see surprisingly few animals at first.

After a while the gravel begins to sort itself into sizes. Some of the larger stones are rounded at both ends; some have six points of contact with the ground. A dull spot on one turns out to be an eye with a second lid drawn across it. The little animals wait out the cold with their legs folded beneath them. They are spaced a few feet apart, as far as each can reach from its resting place in a night’s grazing. Seen close to, they look scattered. From a low rise you can follow the spacing until you can no longer resolve one body from another and must judge by the faint change in the color of the plain. Beyond the next rise it begins again.

The great ones keep to the hollows. They have broad feet, soft at the edges, and lay them down so slowly that the stones have time to ease out from underneath. Late in the day you can see a string of them moving along a contour, miles between the first and last. Their calves are pale enough to find in the dimness. The adults carry on their backs colonies of a small wingless animal that tends the hair, loosening the dead strands and packing them into pads against the wind. When a herd lies down for the winter, other animals come and live in the warmth between their folded legs. There is feeding and breeding there all through the coldest months. From a little way off it looks like a hillside holding patches of dirty snow.

Something industrial goes on in the deeper troughs. It is easiest to see at dusk, when a blue glow shows beneath the thin skin of ice. The long, jointed animals down there take cold brine in at one end and work it through rows of chambers. In the outer chambers, where the fluid is nearly frozen, they separate out coarse salts; farther in, they plate iron onto the plates of their own growing shells. The current passes from plate to plate in an orderly succession. A trough a mile long may brighten by degrees over the course of an hour. At its lower end, sifted salts fall out of the ice in a fine gray powder, and animals come down from the slopes to lick them. Nothing has been built to house this work. When a trough is spent, the jointed bodies move on with the next thaw, and the depression they leave holds ordinary dark water.

Almost none of this makes a sound you can hear. Kneeling at a pool, you may hear a drop hit it from the lip of the ice. There is time to wait for another and then to decide it is not coming. The dimness shifts a little. What had seemed overcast in every direction resolves into several weathers: a low bank snagged on the western shelves, a faint shower evaporating out above the gravel, and to the east a clear strip where the last light falls on the undersides of far-off clouds. By morning the shower will have crossed perhaps a tenth of the plain. The animals beneath it will not have moved. Those farther on, in air the shower has yet to reach, have already begun to lift themselves onto their feet.
