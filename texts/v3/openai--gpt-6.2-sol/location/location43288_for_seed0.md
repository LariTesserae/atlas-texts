The valley is only a little lower than the country around it. By day, from the rim, you could see from one end to the other: fifteen miles of close green crowns, interrupted here and there by a ridge of black stone sharp enough to show through the foliage. At night the nearest leaf is difficult to distinguish from the far wall of the valley. You reach for what looks like a gap and put your hand flat against something warm and ribbed.

The stone is younger than some of the trees. It came up through a level forest, raising whole stands at a slant and taking their soil with them. Trees that had grown side by side now live on opposite faces of a cleft. Their roots hang down into it, still holding lumps of the same old clay. The new ridges have edges that will cut skin; where a boar has rubbed against one, coarse hairs stand out of the stone. In the low places there is a great depth of leaf mold, but scrape it aside on a rise and you find bare rock, dry to the touch and still giving back the day’s heat.

Water is hard to come by until rain. There may be a spoonful in the heel of a leaf, or a mouthful down between two folded roots. Small animals know these places and wait beside them. A tree frog can spend an entire evening with its belly over a cup of water too narrow for it to turn around in. Under the broadest leaves the soil stays powdery through all but the hardest showers. The air smells of crushed stems, warm hide, and, wherever the ground has opened recently, the faint struck smell of stone.

There are more animals than there seems to be room for. Moths sleep in layers along the undersides of branches; the bottom ones have to let go before the rest can fly. The litter shifts around the feet of a feeding herd as beetles and pale, jointed things climb out of its way. Even places that look unoccupied prove to be in use. A patch of dry ground beneath an overhang is where the long-legged birds go to lose their ticks. Before dawn, little striped animals come to eat the ticks and leave new tracks over the birds’ tracks, so neatly that the two sets seem to belong to a single creature with a very peculiar gait.

Now and then one of the apes carries a stone down from a ridge to open nuts. Good hammerstones are scarce in this valley of fresh, angular rock. An ape may carry one in the crook of its arm all night, setting it down within reach even when it climbs a tree to sleep. By next evening there will be a few shells lodged in a root fork and nothing else to show where it sat.

The noise is worst on the level ground. Frogs call from cavities underfoot; high in the crowns, insects produce a sustained note that makes the teeth feel cold. Flocks crossing above the trees are audible as flocks only when they turn together and the wingbeats arrive all at once. Big animals travel surprisingly near without being heard. You can be standing in the full uproar and feel a hoof come down through the sole of your foot before you know anything has approached.

Some nights a squall comes along the valley without crossing either rim. The first heavy drops knock insects out of the canopy. Fish no longer than a finger spill from holes in the roots and work their way through the thin wash on the forest floor. In the half hour they have, they feed, and some reach another pocket of water. The ones that don’t draw birds down out of trees that were shaking too hard a moment before for anything to perch in them. Dead limbs fall. A running herd hits a cleft it knew how to cross yesterday and splits around it. By the time the last rain reaches the ground, the broad leaves overhead have already tilted back into place.

Of all this, the rock is the slowest thing, though it too is moving. Each fresh break leaves two faces that fit. For a while, a call made beside one can be heard close to the other, even if the pieces have slid well apart. The small burrowing animals seem to know about this. A mother under a slab will call once, softly enough that you can barely hear her, and her young will answer from a seam across the clearing. Then the ground settles another fraction, or roots work their way into one face of the stone, and the arrangement fails. For nights afterward she goes on calling in the same place.

Near dawn the squall has left a string of shallow pools down the middle of the valley. In the dark they might pass for one continuous stream. A beetle comes swimming to the edge of one, tucks its legs under, and waits. The next pool is six inches away. Between them, already dry, is a little ridge of stone with no mark on it at all.
