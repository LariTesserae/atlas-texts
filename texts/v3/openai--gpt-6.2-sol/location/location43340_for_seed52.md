The city is nine days old. The trees are older than anyone can agree on.

It occupies a low plain between two dark ridges, though from the middle of it the ridges look no higher than the banks of a ditch. Most of the ground is level enough that a little rain goes nowhere. It darkens the paving, collects under the leaves of the broad roadside cannas, and waits for the warmth to take it back. A river crosses the western districts in a channel too new to have a mud line. Here and there a root dangles over the water, white at the tip, not yet long enough to touch.

There are buildings all the way to the ridges, but they stand far apart. Each has several addresses and generally only one lit window. From a distance you can tell which are inhabited by the little interruptions in their outlines: a chair on a balcony, a towel over a rail, a pane propped open with a spoon. The avenues between them are extravagantly wide. In the southern districts, where nothing has yet been assigned to the medians, figs and climbing beans have taken up whole acres. Beneath the fig trees it smells of warm bark and the fruit that splits before it falls.

The city was laid down in one piece. In excavations you can see the folded lower stories still opening. They are made of a gray material with sharp black edges, and the work crews wrap the edges in yellow cloth so people won’t cut themselves. The stairs are safe as soon as they appear, but the landings sometimes don’t arrive until the following morning. There’s a bakery on the second floor of a building whose stairs are still waiting for a landing. They lower the bread in a green shopping basket. At closing time, the woman who runs it comes down by leaning out of her window and putting a foot on the pavement. She says the trick is not to look at the street until you’ve put your weight on it.

The light is poor even at noon. No clouds obscure the sky; it simply has very little depth to it, like a ceiling painted blue a long time ago. In the wettest courtyards, the undersides of leaves shine more brightly than their tops. Work is done by this light. People bring their mending out under the big taros and turn the cloth over to see the stitches.

Most things here happen too far away to hear. Once in a while a fruit strikes a roof. The little tram announces its arrival with the sound of someone taking a glass off a shelf. There are empty stations where the announcement sounds and nothing arrives; these are stops the tram made before there was a city, and it has not yet been persuaded to give them up.

Animals are scarce enough that a heron in a drainage court brings people to the windows. It stands there all afternoon with one foot raised, watching a dry grate. By evening there are three children watching with it. The court fills from below after dark, very slowly, with water that smells of river stones. The heron takes something small and silver from between the bars. The following evening the children come back, but the bird has moved on.

Toward the eastern edge, where the avenues run straight for several miles, there is a stand of trees the builders left untouched. The surveyors have put their stakes all around it. They go out every morning and count the stakes, because the stand is larger on the inside after rain, and sometimes one of them turns up a considerable way into the woods. The oldest path through is paved with bricks worn hollow by feet. You can follow it past a cistern, a low wall, and the foundations of a house. Then you step out onto an avenue whose curbs are still sharp enough to peel an orange.

In the hour before dawn, the warmth lets up a little. Condensation beads along the tram wires, and lengths of wire let go all at once. You can hear the brief showers passing from block to block. A few people set bowls out, though there’s seldom enough to cover the bottom. By the time the bakery window opens, the paving is nearly dry. Only the yellow cloth around the unfinished stairs is still wet, and where it touches the ground, small green leaves have begun to grow through it.
