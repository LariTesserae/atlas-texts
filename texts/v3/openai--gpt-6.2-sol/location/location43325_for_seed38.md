The country is high enough to have clouds beneath it, but there is no longer an edge from which to see them. The mountains have worn down to a broad upland, gently creased, with a few long swells that take half a day to cross. On a clear morning you can watch the shadow of a cloud travel over one of these swells and disappear into the next. It is the easiest way to see how far the land goes. Otherwise there is very little to judge distance by. Nothing stands bare.

The ground is made of plants. There is stone underneath, but even in the beds of streams it is difficult to reach. The finger goes through the little round leaves, then through a warm mesh of roots, then into an older mesh that comes apart like wet cloth. Ferns grow out of the reclining trunks of other ferns. Some of those trunks are still alive and put up a frond here and there, far from the place where they first fell. The taller growth is spaced widely enough to walk between, though from any one spot the intervening beds of sedge and broadleaf make the country seem densely occupied. You can see a great way at the height of your knees and almost nowhere at the height of your eyes.

It is warm without being bright. Most of the light comes through a low sheet of cloud and then through two or three thicknesses of leaf. Where a leaf has been torn, the light on the ground is only a little paler. The upland makes much of its own cloud: water rises from the warm beds in the afternoon and collects so low that the taller crowns disappear. By evening it is falling out of them again. It seldom falls hard enough to make the ground run. Rather, the ground grows fuller, and water shows between the stems in places that were dry an hour before.

There are rivers, though you can pass over one without knowing it. The roots on either side have grown together above the channel, and plants with no roots in the earth have settled on that crossing. A doe with small, spreading feet can walk its whole width without sinking. When she stops, the tremor she has made goes on ahead of her. A little row of leaves dips, rights itself, then dips farther away. Underneath, in clear brown water, fish hold still with their noses to the current.

The animals are plentiful and for the most part close. There are snails in the folds of new leaves, frogs in the cups of old ones, and little soft-bodied birds that spend the warmest part of the day down among the stems. You find the places where a herd has lain more often than you see the herd itself. Each hollow holds a little water and smells of bruised parsley. Now and then a bird makes a single wooden note. There is a long enough interval before the next one that the note seldom seems like part of a call.

On some of the swells stand low walls made of rounded stones. They enclose very little: a space the size of a bed, or a patch of nothing distinguishable from what grows outside. In a few places a flat stone has been set across two uprights. The joints are full of roots as thick as wrists. These are the best places to see what the upland is made of beneath its green, for a deer will sometimes rub the corner of a stone clean. The stone is pale and fine-grained, almost sugary. A clean corner seldom lasts the season.

The strangest thing here is how long a fallen tree goes on making a clearing. While it stands it has scarcely any crown; after it falls, the roots lift a great platter of growing ground with them. For years there is an opening beneath the tilted earth, with grasses hanging upside down from the ceiling and rain climbing their blades by degrees. Small animals sleep there in the dry. By the time the platter has settled flat again, the trunk has rooted at several points along its length. What began as a single tree may end as a low ridge a hundred paces long, with water on one side and dry footing on the other.

There is a season when the water withdraws a finger’s depth from the upland. Old paths come out then: not tracks worn into soil, but narrow runs of shorter plants, crossing the flats in lines too deliberate to be streams. Some lead to the little walls. Others go on over one swell and the next. For a few weeks you can follow them without wetting your ankles. Then the beds fill, the taller leaves lean in, and there is nothing to mark the paths but the way the deer go.
