The pump house is the only thing in the hollow that stands on its own feet. Even those are hard to see. They are four short brick piers, and the roots have grown around them in such close-fitting sleeves that the brick shows only where someone has scraped a place to set a jack.

From the road, the hollow looks like a low grove, perhaps half an acre, with a corrugated roof in it. Inside, there is room enough to stand upright and, in one place, to take three steps without turning sideways. Everything else is occupied. Ferns grow out of the forks of alders; finer ferns grow in the brown felt around the feet of those ferns. The floor is made of roots that have flattened under years of traffic. They are firm at the crown and soft along the edges, where the little white tips are still putting on length. There is soil somewhere beneath all this, but even in the ditch beside the road, where the county cut straight down, the exposed bank is a section of living wood in dozens of shades.

It is warm in the hollow. Ordinary afternoon light comes down through rents in the canopy and catches on drops, on the pump house window, on a strip of orange paint somebody put on a valve handle. You can see the water without seeing much of what holds it. It stands in the cupped junctions of branches and runs, in threads, down bark that looks quite dry from a step away. Close to the pump house a row of broad leaves bends under the drips from the gutter. Each takes a long while to fill, then tips its load into the next. The last leaf hangs over a shallow depression worn into a root. The depression is as smooth as the inside of a spoon.

The machinery is loud enough that two people on opposite sides of the house have to come round to the same side to talk. There is a low electric note and, at longer intervals, a clack from a check valve. A sheet of roofing rattles in answer. The discharge goes under the road in a pipe wide enough for a child to crawl through, though nothing comes out at the far end but a thin spill of clear water. There is no pond to drain. The intake draws from between the root layers, and the water comes back as slowly and evenly as it is taken. On the wall inside, above the modern motor, there is a porcelain gauge with a red line painted on it. The needle is normally just below the line. In the oldest photograph tacked up by the door, the original motor is already rusty, a man stands beside it in shirtsleeves, and the needle is in exactly the same place.

There used to be a catwalk from the road to the door. Two iron handrail posts remain, neither at quite the right height. The present path follows a root that grew along the underside of the catwalk and kept its shape after the grating fell away. Off to the left, another root rises into a step that leads nowhere. A few feet above it, deep in the leaves, hangs the door it used to lead to, complete with the bottom hinge and a small tongue of green paint. The tree holding it has put on enough girth to swing it open a little farther each year.

Almost nothing moves away when you approach. Once in a while there is a snail, its shell the same pale brown as the wet brick. A wren sings from the roadside in spring but is seldom heard inside. Around the warm housing of the motor a mat of tiny round leaves has spread across the concrete, except for a hand’s breadth that the attendant keeps clear with a putty knife. The leaves there lie flat and tremble while the motor runs. When he shuts it off to check the bearings, they remain flat for perhaps a minute. Then they begin to lift, one row at a time, starting at the door.

In that short quiet you can hear water dropping from the gutter and a finer sound underfoot, like somebody rubbing two lengths of wet rope together. It comes from the roadward edge, where the floor is still growing. The attendant puts a chalk mark there every summer so he knows where to cut back to. Last year’s mark is easy to find. The bark has rolled it over onto the underside, and if you crouch at the entrance you can see it in the narrow space between the living floor and the road.
