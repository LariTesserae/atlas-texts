The drowned pump room is small enough that, from the doorway, you can see the pale rectangle of the opposite wall. You cannot always see what is between. The water carries a fine gray powder that settles in the lee of things and lifts again whenever the machinery starts.

There is no rock here. Under the powder are blue floor bricks, laid on edge and worn deepest along a route no one has walked for centuries. The banks on either side of the central channel are poured concrete. Where the concrete has worn away it shows not earth but a second, coarser pour, full of white ceramic chips. Even the rubble is manufactured: lengths of iron pipe lying in the channel, a dished inspection cover, a row of pale knobs that were once the heads of bolts. The current has rounded the broken edges of the cover until it looks like something you could put your hand on without getting cut.

Two pumps used to stand in the room. One is gone, leaving four low feet and a clean-sided hole where its intake descended. The other is still there, a dark waist-high casing with a seam around its middle. Nothing in the casing moves. The working machinery is farther down the line, beyond the north wall. At intervals its stroke comes through the old pipes, and the whole room gives out a booming note. The note is much larger than the room. A loose length of conduit clatters against the ceiling afterward, three or four times, depending on how long it takes the flow to steady. In the lull you can hear grit going over the bricks.

A grille high on the south wall admits a little daylight from a flooded service shaft. By the time it gets here it is green and weak. There is a fringe of short algae immediately below the grille, flattened in one direction by the flow; farther down, there isn't enough light. The floor stays cold even on days when the shaft delivers a thin pulse of warmer water. That pulse passes near the ceiling and is gone before it reaches the channel.

At first the spaces between the machinery seem empty. Look under the pump casing and there are prawns standing on the underside, picking at it, their feelers hanging down into the open water. Tiny fish shelter inside a vacant gauge housing. There are enough of them to fill the entire dial, and when they come out they do so in a narrow stream through the hole where the needle spindle went. Mussels occupy the joints in the bricks, leaving the broad faces almost bare. A pale eel lies down the empty intake with just its head showing. It does not stir at the stroke that sends all the little fish back into the gauge.

The room has a rule the animals seem to know: when the north pipe knocks twice, get off the floor. The second knock brings a low rush through the channel. Powder rolls up over the brick banks; the prawns lift their legs and hold by their last few toes. For a moment the old walking route shows all the way across the room, gently hollowed, with a little extra wear where a person would have turned to reach the pump. Then the water slows. The fish venture out, the powder comes down, and the turn in the path is the first part to disappear.
