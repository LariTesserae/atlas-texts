The entrance is halfway up a slope so broad and worn that it seems level until you stop to catch your breath. There is no peak above it, just more of the same tawny stone, warm enough to sit on in shirtsleeves. From here you can look down on the tops of afternoon clouds. You can also kneel, put your head through a crack no wider than your shoulders, and look down on a tree.

The tree grows in a hollow about the size of a kitchen. It is the only one. Its trunk rises crookedly from a bed of black grit and puts out an extravagant crown of glossy leaves, some big enough to catch on your ears as you climb in. A fern occupies the far corner, and a few ropes of wiry grass hang from a seam overhead. Between these patches there is bare rock. You can see nearly every inch of it in the ordinary daylight that comes through the crack: shallow scallops left by water, one quartz vein worn flush with the surrounding stone, the dull shine where generations of small feet have taken the same turn. There is no loose rubble. Everything that could come loose did so a very long time ago.

It is loud enough inside that at first you cannot tell what is making the noise. The tree seems to have a motor in it. Then one of its leaves turns over and you see that the underside is crowded with little green crickets, each rocking hard enough to move the leaf. There are crickets on most of the leaves. There are also pale lizards lying along the branches with their toes hanging down, and red mites traveling in such numbers that the bark appears to redden in strips. At intervals a lizard puts its head beneath a leaf. The singing stops there, leaving a little gap you can actually hear. A minute later the gap closes.

The hollow is not wet. A hand set flat on the floor comes away dusty. But the air is warm and close, and now and then a bead forms on the tip of a grass blade. The animals know which blade. Up to a dozen of them will wait below it without drinking, heads cocked, until the drop falls and bursts on the stone. Then the whole patch is busy with tongues. Afterward you could lie down in the same spot and not get a damp elbow.

Someone set three white pegs in the floor under the tree. They are blunt and unmarked, no higher than the first joint of a finger. One has been polished by lizards using it to get up onto a low branch. Another stands in the path of the falling drops, though they never hit it. As they approach, they bend off to one side and go on falling toward the wall. A loose feather does the same thing. Put a finger there and nothing pulls on it, but the nail feels briefly cold.

Toward the end of the afternoon a cloud draws up over the slope. Outside, it leaves a few dark spots on the rock, too far apart to touch. Inside, the roots of the tree begin to shine. Water comes out of the quartz seam in beads and travels along the exposed roots, even where they arch up out of the ground. The crickets fold their legs. For perhaps ten seconds the room is quiet enough to hear the water. Then the lizards begin to bark, not from the branches but from the bare walls, where dozens more have come out headfirst from cracks you hadn’t noticed. They drop into the tree a few at a time, and it shakes under their weight.

Back on the slope, two steps from the entrance, you can hear your own sleeve brush against your coat. The crack disappears when you stand up. The rain spots are already paling, except for one just under the lip, where a lizard has come out and laid its whole belly over it.
