The city stands on a ledge halfway up a mountain so broad that, from the city, it looks like the whole world tilts. The upper slopes are warm and ivory-colored. Below, long aprons of gray grit run out into a basin in which there is no water, and apparently never has been. Nothing in the basin has the branching shape of a thing made by running water. The gullies are straight, following flaws in the rock, and the narrow ridges between them have been rounded by a wind that has been going the same way for a very long time.

On a clear afternoon you can see the shadow of the mountain go out across the basin. It cuts off the light on one white outcrop and then another; between these events there is time to finish a meal. The far side of the basin has its own weather. Great sheets of copper-colored dust lift there and travel north for weeks. By the time one reaches the city it has usually thinned to a faint discoloration of the afternoon, and people going home from work notice it mostly because they can look west without squinting.

From below, the city is easy to miss. There is a fine dark line where the ledge overhangs, then another line a little farther up, and what looks like a mineral stain between them. These are two freight tracks and six stories of brick. The streets run inward as often as they run uphill. You can enter a public square through an ordinary door and find the square roofed with a single slab of mountain, the daylight coming from one side. The outer streets are paved in a glassy black slag that has worn to a comfortable dullness. At the crossings the rock shows through, cream-colored and faintly rippled underfoot.

The industrial quarter occupies a recess where a great piece of the slope came away before anybody lived here. Its furnaces run in long heats. A red inspection lamp burns above a loading door for eleven days; on the twelfth the door opens and a carriage brings out a single piece of ceramic the length of a street. Most of the time, standing outside, you hear the click of the cooling shutters and the dry whisper of dust against your cuffs. The great return cables go humming into their housings without seeming to move. If you lay a coin on a railing there, it walks slowly to the edge.

There are animals almost everywhere, though a newcomer can spend half a day on the ledge without seeing one. The little flat ones are the color of old mortar and fit into the joints between bricks. In the late afternoon they come out by the hundred and settle on the west walls with their pale undersides exposed. Higher up, jointed animals as big as freight carts work their way across the open slopes, grazing on a violet crust that forms where two kinds of stone meet. They leave wide, shallow bites in it. A bite takes thirty or forty years to fill, which is about how long it takes a herd to come around the mountain again. There are no leaves or roots anywhere. The crust grows at its edges, and a stretch untouched for a century is still thin enough to scrape away with a thumbnail.

The animals that live in town know the shift bell. At its first stroke, broad shells begin to appear over the lip of the stonework below the canteen. Workers knock the crumbs from their lunch tins onto the pavement, and the shells come up on small black legs to eat. On a rest day, when the bell doesn’t ring, they arrive anyway, in twos and threes, somewhat later.

There is one peculiarity of the height that no one in the city thinks to mention until a visitor is startled by it. Loose things get lighter as they rise, but only when they are out of contact with the mountain. A block weighs the usual amount on the quarry floor. Sling it clear and a person can turn it with one hand. Set it down on a shelf a mile higher and it takes its weight again. This is why the oldest houses, up near the bare summit, have such enormous lintels. It is also why dust sometimes hangs above a street in the exact shape of an animal that has just shaken itself. The animal walks away. The dust takes a little longer.

At dusk the city stays warm. The kilns and the sun have been putting heat into the same stone all day, and people sit in the doorways far into the evening, looking out over a drop too great to see the bottom of after dark. Now and then a signal comes on far below, where the freight line rounds a spur. It is a modest amber light; you might take it for a lamp in a house across a narrow street. Then a second light comes on, in answer, and the interval between them gives you some idea of the distance.
