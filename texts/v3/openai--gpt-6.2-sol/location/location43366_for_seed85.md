The town is submerged on the highest shoulder of the mountain. Its lowest doorstep stands above the old snow line, though there is no snow now, and no surface visible from the upper windows. The water is cold enough that after a while the stone feels warm by comparison. It fills the houses, the gaps under the doors, the little stamped hollows where the house numbers used to be.

There are nine buildings, with room enough between them to set down nine more. They make a town because of the things they share: a tram loop, a public stair, a conduit wide enough to walk through, and a square with a slight fall toward a drain. The drain has water on both sides. In the smallest house, someone fitted a shelf into the turn of the stair. You can pass a hand along its underside and feel where they took an extra shaving off the wood to clear the banister.

The mountain is younger than the town. It came up through the foundation slab, lifting the whole district without spilling a tile. Outside the retaining wall, long black faces of basalt meet at edges as fine as the edges of broken crockery. There is no silt in the joints. Nothing grows on the stone, not even a film. On the uphill side, the last flight of the public stair ends a finger’s width short of the rock. It has never quite touched.

Inside the tram shelter there is a strip of light about as bright as a watch dial. It is enough to read the stops if you put your face close. Three of them were elsewhere. Their names are still on the strip, and under each name is the distance to the next stop: zero. Step off the curb there and you feel, for the length of a stride, the easy downward pull of a street that isn’t under you. The paving on the far side of the loop is the same paving you left. There is a chip in one tile that you can use to check.

A few pale animals live in the conduit. You can find one by putting an ear to a pipe and waiting for the small, distinct tick of a shell against the inside. Away from the pipe you won’t hear it. The water in the square is so still that a bubble dislodged from a sleeve will rise to the height of the second-story windows and stay there, with the dark roofs curved around its rim. Whatever holds it up there does not prevent you from reaching in and breaking it.

At the outer rail there is a view straight down. The rail is the ordinary height for leaning on, and its top bar is slightly flattened where people used to rest their wrists. Below it, the basalt falls out of sight without a ledge. Above it is the same dark water. It takes some time at the rail to understand how high you are; the best way is to go back to the square and look at the stair. Whoever laid it cut the uphill treads deep and the downhill treads shallow, so that a person coming home tired could take the steep part in shorter steps. The mountain still stands at exactly that pitch.
