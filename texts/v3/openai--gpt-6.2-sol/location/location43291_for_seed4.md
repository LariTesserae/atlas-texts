The city has a low place no wider than a thumb. It lies under the last stair to the washhouses, where the living wood has lifted from the living wood and left a little room between. In a hard rain the stair settles and the room disappears. When the rain lets up, it opens again, cold enough to ache in a finger.

There is no soil in it. The floor is the pale underside of a root. A blade of sedge comes through at one end and, finding no light to turn toward, grows along the ceiling. Everything else is wood or leaf or the fine white felt a willow puts out when it has been kept wet too long. Yet the room feels bare. Three pillars cross it, with long reaches of nothing between them. At the far end a drop hangs from a freshly split edge. The split has not had time to darken.

Above, the stair belongs to a whole district of stairs. Generations of washhouse keepers have bent willow shoots into treads and pegged them down with thorns. There are water lanes roofed in dock leaves, doorways kept open by putting a shoulder through them every day, and a public well that has to be trimmed in spring so the bucket can get past its lip. The city owns a few iron knives, mostly worn down to hooks. A person repairing a step is as likely to bring a cup of water as a blade.

The little room has more animals in it than the district has people. Most are inside the pillars. The wood is so thoroughly inhabited that if a grub stops feeding, the one in the next gallery can be heard going on. There are mites in the water hanging off the ceiling and smaller things traveling on the mites. Along the floor, pale springtails keep a clear strip between two feeding grounds. Nothing enforces the boundary except a hunter that comes out of a slit in the root. It crosses the strip so fast that its arrival is known chiefly by the space it leaves behind.

Almost none of this makes a sound in the room. The chewing is loud only if you put an ear to the stair. Now and then a drop detaches and doesn't fall: a strand too fine to see takes its weight and draws it sideways, slowly, into a cleft. Something there drinks the drop from below. In winter, when the drops freeze, the same strands bear small crooked beads. Animals pass underneath them for weeks without touching them. Then a thaw takes all the beads in a single night.

The oldest thing in the room is not more than a season old. The stair split in autumn under a woman carrying two full pails. The new planes of wood still meet at angles sharp enough to hold a foot by one claw. Already a root tip is advancing across the widest interval. It grows most on wash days, when warm water spills through the treads; afterward the chill comes back up from the lanes and the tip goes still. There are eggs laid at the place it will reach. The animal that laid them could not have walked across.

Toward morning somebody steps down to open the washhouse. The ceiling descends. Water runs in a sheet between the pillars, and the springtails let go. For a moment the whole population of the open floor goes through a gap at the bottom of the stair. When the weight lifts, the room is empty except for a single animal hanging from the sedge blade. It waits through the next footfall. Then it lowers a pair of feelers into the dark where the floor has come back.
