Two floors below the tram terminus, the city has a little square. It is thirty paces from the shuttered bakery to the stairs, if you go straight across. Nobody goes straight across. The middle belongs to a bed of ginger, three distribution cabinets, a drinking fountain with no handle, and the iron legs of a machine whose working parts are on another floor.

The square is hot enough that the handrails feel like someone has just let go of them. Water gathers on the cold supply pipe overhead, but only at the joints. A joint may hold its drop from breakfast until the lamps come on. Beneath each one there is a different arrangement: a chipped bowl, a tuft of fern, a square of paving gone black. The rest of the floor is dry and pale. In front of the bakery you can still see the chalk marks the masons made when they laid it.

The city broke into this pocket of rock only a few years ago. Behind the newest row of shops, the excavation face is sharp enough to snag a sleeve. Roots have got into it all the same. They come down the saw cuts in flat white ribbons, passing behind the brackets for the electrical conduit and emerging under the shop awnings. Up near the ceiling, broad leaves overlap like roof tiles. There is so little light under them that the cobbler keeps a lamp trained on his own hands.

The heat comes from a bank of municipal pumps beyond the west wall. You would expect a racket. Stand still, though, and all you hear is the occasional click of a relay and the thin settling noise of the ginger leaves. The pumps make themselves known through objects: a key left in a lock will turn a fraction toward the wall; spoons laid on the bakery counter migrate, over a week or two, until their bowls hang over the edge. In the window of the closed ticket office, a dead moth has been inching uphill for as long as the cobbler can remember.

Most of the square's addresses are service doors. There is a door for the tram brakes, a door for the bakery flue, a door scarcely wider than a book for the weights that keep the stairwell gates from slamming. Each has a number and a little clear space in front of it. The gardeners are strict about those spaces. Everything else is given over to leaves. A woman coming down to read a meter has to put her clipboard on her head and use both hands to part the foliage.

Once, a lizard lived in the drinking fountain. People still leave the basin clear, though nobody has seen it since the fountain was disconnected. At the hour when the trams stop running, the square's three lamps dim in order, from the stair to the bakery. For a little while the only light is the green pilot on the pump cabinet. It shows the underside of one ginger leaf and the drop hanging from its tip. The drop is there in the morning, too, a little farther down.
