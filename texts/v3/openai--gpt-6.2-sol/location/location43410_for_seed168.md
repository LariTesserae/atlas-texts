The plateau is high enough that the lowlands have the color of the sky. You can follow its rim for miles by the pale faces of the broken rock, each one clean-edged from top to bottom. There is no apron of rubble below them. In places two faces stand a hand’s breadth apart, and the little knobs on one fit the hollows on the other. The ground looks as if it has only just opened and has not yet decided how far to go.

Nothing here has ever run downhill. The long trough in the middle of the plateau is not a riverbed; it is where two slabs failed to meet. Its floor is dull pink salt, warm through the soles of your boots. You can cross it without leaving a print. There are no soft places to look out for and no darker seam under a stone. Even the shade has the dry warmth of the inside of a cupboard.

The green begins some distance up the opposite slope. From across the trough it looks like a continuous wood, but the crowns are so wide, and stand at so nearly the same height, that they hide the empty ground between them. Under one crown you can spend twenty paces walking through the overlapping shade of thick leaves and then another two hundred in full sun before you reach the next. The leaves are green all the way through. Break one and the two halves show a fine, pale grain, with no bead of moisture. What the stems carry is a light oil. In the low warmth of the afternoon it gives the groves a smell somewhere between crushed bay and a new roll of rubber.

From a ridge you can see that each grove sits over a dark spindle in the rock. Some spindles protrude where the slope falls away, exposing rings of graphite and bright metal. They turn. The whole bed of stone conducts a steady current through them, and at the foot of each tree a collar of amber tissue grows close around the turning mineral. There is nothing resembling a cable or a housing anywhere on the plateau. Yet for miles, under the salt and the open ground, the same arrangement repeats. Stand with your heels on a bare spindle and you can feel the tremor in your teeth. Take one step off it and there is only the sound of your clothes.

There are flowers deep in the crowns, broad ivory cups that open onto nothing. No wings pass between them. At the base of a few trees you can find the beginning of another grove: a folded green blade rising through a hairline in the stone. There are no midsized trees. The blades stay folded, the flowers stay open, and the salt keeps its sharp little ridges even where it lies loose on a slope.

Toward evening, the far rim grows distinct as the light reaches under the crowns. For a while the whole width of the plateau is visible between the trunks. It is farther than the green had led you to expect. The last tree stands on a wedge of rock split nearly free of the rim, its roots laid bare along the fracture. One root crosses the gap. There is room to put your hand beneath it, and the stone on either side is still sharp.
