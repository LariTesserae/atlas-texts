The rain is warm enough that it leaves no sense of having been rained on. It falls out of a bright sky in wide, discontinuous strips, crossing the plateau one after another. You can watch a strip darken a mile of pale stone without ever reaching you. Afterward the stone shines, and water comes up through the joints for hours. The rain is only part of what keeps the plateau wet.

The land stands high above a lowland that is seldom visible. At the rim, white vapor pours over the edge and thins enough, now and then, to show ledges far below. Inland there is almost no slope to speak of. The rock has worn into broad dishes with low divides between them, each dish holding a finger’s depth of clear water. The divides are old and rounded, easy to cross even barefoot if you go early. By noon the exposed stone burns. The pools are not much cooler, but you can stand in one without having to move from foot to foot.

There is no green at any distance. A dark fringe around a pool resolves, as you come nearer, into tiny terraces of manganese. The rust-colored fans below the seeps are mineral too. Yet a pan that looks empty from ten paces may have hundreds of animals in it. Most are flat and pale, with jointed legs tucked out of sight beneath their shells. They graze on the colorless bacterial film along the bottom, leaving clean tracks that intersect without quite following one another. When a narrow shadow passes over them, they stop. The shadow belongs to an animal that fishes on four finely spread feet, bearing its weight on the surface. It can stand there through an entire shower. Only when it strikes do you see that its feet have broken through.

The town occupies the watershed between two of the larger dishes. It has streets, addresses, a pumping works, and surprisingly few walls. The buildings stand on ceramic piers, with whole blocks of empty paving between them. Nothing has been built in those spaces for generations. They are the streets the water takes when it is too deep for the culverts. On an ordinary afternoon you can walk straight across town; after a hard rain you go by the upper galleries, past kitchen doors and the backs of the meter cabinets, while warm clear water moves through the intersections below.

From the rim the pumping works looks like a row of low white sheds. Its bulk is underground, where the heat is steadier. The flywheels run in flooded housings, and the footings are bedded in thick pads that have to be renewed every few years. On the gallery above the works you can feel a running pump through the soles of your shoes, but what you hear is more likely to be a drop falling from the gallery rail. The town was built to spare its joints. Anything that rattles on a dry morning will work itself loose by the end of the wet season.

The best shade is under the return-water bridge. Animals gather there in the middle of the day, some carried in on the current and some arriving overland on legs long enough to keep their bodies off the stone. Their abundance is easy to miss from above. Look down from the bridge and you see three or four dark backs, well separated. Descend the steps until your eyes are level with the underside and the whole length of it is occupied. They cling there belly-up, packed around the sweating pipes. When the pumps change over, a cool pulse of water goes through the return, and the animals nearest the outlet let go. Those upstream edge down to take their places. This happens several times an hour without a cry or a splash you could hear from the street.

Late in the day the western dishes begin to empty. There is no visible channel between them and the rim. Water draws down around each pebble and leaves it shining, then goes on down through the stone. For a little while, all across the open ground, the stranded shell animals walk toward deeper pools. Their legs are much longer than they looked in water. Some have a considerable distance to go.

From the upper gallery you can follow their progress until the light gets too low to pick them out. Farther off, a shower reaches the rim. The vapor there grows white and thick, erasing first the ledges and then the edge itself. At your feet the paving is still warm. Water beads along the seams, and the first of the animals coming out of the western dishes step over them into town.
