The garden is inside a tooth of the mountain.

The tooth stands high enough that the plain below it has lost its features. From the summit you can see the shadow of the range laid across the salt, but not the foot of the slope on which you stand. The entrance is a seam behind a shoulder of stone. You have to take off your pack and turn sideways. For a little while there is rock against both your breastbone and your spine, and then there are leaves against your face.

It is not a large garden. If you could see through it, you could stand at one end and read the stamping on the machinery at the other. But there is no seeing through it. Fernlike things occupy the low angles of the rock, each frond fitted to a different irregularity in the stone. Thick black leaves hang edge-on from the ceiling. Between them grow upright fans with the dull green of old copper and a pale, furred growth that fills the joints down to their last hairline. There is no earth. Where a shelf has broken, you can see the rootwork holding the pieces in their original relation, with a thin dark gap between them.

You would expect the leaves to be wet. They have that heavy shine, and some curl down into points so precisely shaped you can almost hear the drip. Touch one and your finger comes away cool and powdered. The shine belongs to plates in the skin of the leaf, like the shine on mica. Inside the stems is a bitter oil that stays fluid at the temperature of the room. A broken stalk, visible beside the entrance, has not spilled. The oil stands flush with the break, with a tiny grain of white grit suspended in it.

The room is loud. From outside you hear nothing, then the passage opens and there is a note so strong you feel it in your back teeth. Under it is a coarser noise, like a fan turning in a metal housing. There is a fan, in fact, or something built on the same principle: a waist-high iron drum bolted to a ledge, with inspection holes and a belt wheel outboard. The wheel has a nick in its rim. You can watch that nick for as long as you like. It stays at the top.

The noise comes out of the stone around the bolts. Lay a hand on the housing and it feels no different from the ledge. Open your mouth and the sound changes; put your pack down and a thin upper note drops out. The garden will accommodate a visitor that far.

There are three other things somebody made: a bracket with no lamp in it, a short length of handrail, and a ceramic plate screwed to the floor. The handrail is worn to a comfortable oval at the bend. The plate has lettering, but the raised strokes have the same soft contours as the grit around them, and you can make out only the spacing of the words. Nothing suggests a town or even a second room. Whoever came up here to fit the drum had space to kneel in front of it. That space is still there, a shallow bare crescent bounded by leaves.

The light comes from a fault no wider than a fingernail at the back of the ceiling. It is gray at the fault and green by the time it reaches the floor. It does not cross the room as the day advances. You can go out onto the summit and watch the shadow of the mountain shorten by miles, go back in, and find the same small lobe of a leaf lit to its tip. Below it the rest of the plant is almost black.

Nothing in the garden is young, though there are tips as tightly rolled as new growth. Nothing is dead, though half the overhead leaves look scorched. There are no husks, no webs, no eggs under the leaves. In the narrow space behind the drum, a pale shoot has gone right through the eye of a washer. Its stem swells on both sides. The washer is not bent, and there is no scar where the metal touches the stem. It looks as though the two have had all the time they needed to arrive at that fit, and will never require any more.

If you stay a while, the noise begins to suggest things happening just out of sight. A shift ending, perhaps, or air being driven through a long duct. You can get down on the bare crescent and look under the drum. There is no duct. There is a little more of the black foliage, the square ends of the bolts, and, on the underside of the ledge where no light reaches, a row of leaves so closely set that their edges share a single silver line.

Coming out, you find there is wind on the mountain after all. It moves the loose strap of your pack. You may have to stand there for a moment and watch it do that.
