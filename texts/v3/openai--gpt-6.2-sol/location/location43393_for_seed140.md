The hollow is small enough to cross in eighty steps, if you know where to put your feet. It lies high on a mountain that has lost its peak. There is no rubble, no sharp stone. The rock bends over the rim and down into the hollow in one long, worn curve, like the inside of a bowl rubbed for centuries with a thumb.

It is absolutely dark. There is no seam of sky overhead, no gleam on the wet rock. The first thing you learn about the shape of the place is that it is mostly empty. A cough goes a long way before anything sends it back. Then, walking with a hand out, you touch a wall of warm leaves.

Everything grows at the edges. Ferns hang in successive tiers from a ledge you can reach only by standing on your toes. Their lower fronds lie flat on the floor, thick enough to kneel on. There are woody vines with bark as soft as soaked paper and little jointed plants that draw their leaves together when a drop hits them. At the center of the hollow there is scarcely a stem. The floor there is bare stone, warm through the soles, with a film of water so shallow that a footprint is dry for a moment after you lift your foot.

The water makes most of the noise. Somewhere high on the wall it runs through a narrow split, and the split gives it a steady, resonant note. Nearer the floor, drops fall from leaf to leaf. When one of the broadest leaves fills, it tips all at once, and the splash is loud enough to make the animals stop. Then they begin again: tree crickets rubbing in uneven unison, frogs with short calls like knuckles on a door, something heavier nosing through the fern litter. There are a great many animals here. Almost all of them can live in the depth of a hand laid flat against the wall. The middle belongs to whatever flies, and even there you feel a wingbeat only now and then.

Three low structures stand apart on the bare floor. Each has a seat, a shallow cup, and a rim worn smooth by hands. They may not have been made to sit on, but there is nowhere better to rest. Water gathers in the cups without dripping into them. Put a finger into one and you can feel a slow upward current; put the other hand underneath and the stone is dry. The cups keep the water a little cooler than the air. Once in a long while, all three empty at the same instant. A moment later, rain begins in the ferns. Nothing falls in the middle.

The hollow changes less in a lifetime than it does in the few seconds after one of those rains. A brook’s worth of frogs calls from a ledge no wider than a doorstep. A vine lowers itself under the weight of water and brushes the back of your neck. The rock gives off a smell of warm clay, though there is no clay left here. Then the drops space themselves out. The vine lifts. You can sit for a long time with your feet on the wet stone, listening to the water in the split, until it seems less like water coming down from somewhere than a sound the mountain has been making since it was higher.
