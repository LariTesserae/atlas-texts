At the marsh’s lowest edge, under a raft of pennywort, there is a room not much wider than a washbasin. You find it by parting two leaves. Nothing changes about the light when you do: it is ordinary afternoon light, warm and a little gray, with enough of the sky in it to show the color of things. The surprise is that there is a floor, and that the floor, though it looks like worn brown stone, is alive.

It is the exposed back of a root. The marsh has been rubbing water over it for so long that its grain shows only where the water hesitates. At one end there is a shallow bowl with a lip as smooth as the edge of a spoon. A film of water passes over the lip and disappears between two folds of the same root. The far wall is a sheet of upright moss, nearly black at the bottom. Five pale stems rise out of the bowl, with enough room between them for each to cast a separate shadow. Every surface belongs to a plant, but very little stands up into the air. Most of the room is open.

It is loud enough that you have to lean back out of it to hear the marsh.

Under the floor there is a steady, low churn. Over it comes a brisk series of clicks, then a rush much like air let out of a pipe. You can put a finger on the root and feel the clicks arrive through it. On the underside of the pennywort raft, where the stems join, translucent bladders fill and flatten in sequence. Water rises inside one bundle of stems and returns down another. Here and there a folded leaf admits a bubble with a hard little knock. There is no single loud thing. The sound is what all those small movements amount to when they run together, and the low roof sends it straight back down.

The animals keep mostly to the edges. A snail takes a long time to cross the bright underside of a leaf. In the bowl, minute crustaceans back into the current and let it wash their legs; their eyes are easier to see than their bodies. A frog no bigger than the end of your thumb sits in a fold of the moss, with one foot resting on the head of another frog. When a beetle surfaces, it brings up a pocket of air so cleanly silvered that for a moment it is the brightest thing in the room. There are dozens more beetles under the root. You can see their feet through a slit, all working, though from above the floor seems vacant.

After rain the whole marsh stands an inch higher. In here the bowl is still full to its lip. In dry weather, when the mud outside draws back from the sedges, the pennywort lowers with it and the same five stems stand in the same shallow water. Their tips are always blunted at just the height where the snails can reach them. One has a ragged edge today. Another is growing up through its old sheath. Given a week, you could watch every stem in the bowl be replaced without seeing the room look different.

Let go of the two leaves and they settle without quite touching. Through the gap you can still see the strip of water slipping over the brown lip. From a step away, the churn is gone; there is only a little rattle in the pennywort, a sound you would put down to a wet leaf rubbing against another wet leaf.
