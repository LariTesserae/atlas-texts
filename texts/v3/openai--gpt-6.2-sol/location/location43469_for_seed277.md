At the outer edge of the plateau, warm air comes up from a depth no falling stone has ever made audible. It smells faintly of pepper and wet iron. A little of its moisture beads on the lip of the escarpment, where the rock is cool, and runs back onto the high ground. For several miles inland you can follow these narrow damp tracks. They have no banks. They darken the stone, wander through the grit, and end.

Most of the plateau is too dark to see even when nothing stands between you and the sky. Sometimes a cloud moves and a few stars appear. They tell you which way is up but nothing about the country ahead. The country announces itself underfoot: broad shelves with rounded edges, a fine gravel that yields only on the slopes, hollows worn so shallow that you notice them by the warmth of the air at your ankles. The plateau is wide enough for a rainstorm to spend its whole life out there without touching either rim. Afterward, for a day or two, animals a hundred miles downwind can smell where it fell.

There is very little to eat on the surface. A tough, low plant grows at the ends of the damp tracks, putting out three or four leaves and then waiting, sometimes for years. Turn one of its leaves over and there is usually somebody underneath: a flat amber grazer, a clutch of eggs in a felt of old roots, a pale animal that eats the felt and leaves the eggs. On the open stone, life is more widely spaced. A herd may take half the night to pass without two animals coming within reach of each other. They are warm, long-legged creatures with soft feet and a habit of stopping all at once. The little hunters that follow them have learned to stop a moment later, so that the last sound in the dark is always theirs.

Farther in, the stone begins to work.

A deep seam of porous rock takes in warm vapor over an area the size of an inland sea. Where the seam pinches, the vapor gathers behind flexible mats of root and mineral fiber. The mats bow, seal, and eventually give. One after another, over distances of miles, they let the pressure through. Near the surface the pulse passes into beds of metallic grit, which separate by weight as they shiver. Lighter grains creep out onto the flats. The heavy grains sink into cracks, where wet colonies bind them into dark nodules. In time the nodules grow against the crack walls and choke off the flow; the vapor finds a looser seam and the whole slow manufacture moves elsewhere.

There are no towers over these workings, no cut steps down to them. You can cross an active bed and hear nothing but grit caught in the fold of your boot. If you sit with your palm flat on the ground, though, you may feel a stroke, a pause, then two strokes so close together they seem to come from the same place. Animals with feet broad enough to feel this come when the bed is about to vent. They stand over the fissures and feed on a brief rising wash of winged things. The ones they miss disperse across the plateau. Some land on a wet leaf; some fly until their wings wear through.

By the next still interval the grazers have moved on, and the ground feels like ground again. In one of the shallow hollows, rain has left a pool not much wider than a hand. Five or six animals will drink from it before the warmth takes it, each approaching from a different direction, none meeting another. When the pool is gone, the last to arrive will nose along its rim and then set off across the dry stone toward a place where it rained two days ago.
