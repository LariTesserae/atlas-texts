The plateau is high enough that its outer walls descend for half a mile before they reach another surface. There is no view from the edge. You can put a hand over it and feel heat rising along the wall, and every so often a flake comes up out of the dark, turning slowly, though it looks much too heavy to float. It is a bit of leaf. Given time, it will come to rest on the underside of something and grow there.

Everything on the plateau is made of plants. The paving is end grain. The walls are leaves laminated under pressure, their veins still widening between the layers. Wherever a load has to turn a corner, a stem has been trained around the angle and left to thicken. Even the dust is alive: under a lens its grains put out little square feet and fasten themselves to one another. There is no soil. A root that bores through the paving finds older paving, and under that the compressed crowns of the trees from which the first paving was cut.

The main avenue is nearly two miles long. It is open in the sense that there is nothing overhead. Underfoot there is scarcely space to set a heel between the ribs of the pavement, the little projecting buds, and the low belts that carry material from one factory to another. Some of the belts run straight across the avenue and some tilt up and pass through the walls. At the intersections a runner grows across one belt, borrows enough material from it to harden, and becomes a bridge for the next.

There is no water in the conduits. What runs in them is a clear oil that will soak into living wood but beads on a piece of wood that has died. It gives the place its smell: hot husks, with a bitter note where a fresh pipe has been cut. The factories work by sending the oil through successive beds of leaf. In one bed it gives up enough heat to make the leaves curl; their curling drives a shaft. In the next it takes up the compounds from which the leaves make more shaft. There are places where you can tell how long a machine has been running by the thickness of the part that moves.

You see very little of this. An electrical fault sometimes puts a thin blue line along the teeth of a gear, and for a second there will be a whole foundry in view: roof trusses, hoppers, a row of pale trunks squared off where they enter the press. Then the line goes out. The heat is a much better guide. A wall that has stood all night is hot at shoulder height and hotter at the base. Newly extruded pavement is cool for a few seconds, cool enough that the soles of your feet can follow it in the dark.

For all that machinery, you can hear a dry seed roll across the avenue. The bearings grow into the wear on them. The presses are bedded on layers of broad, tough leaf that take the shock and pack down another fraction. Once in a while something makes a sound like a knuckle against a door. You wait for the second knock and there isn't one.

The ground can change quite violently in the interval. A factory lifts off its bed as roots swell under one end of it. Its delivery belts keep running at the new angle until their loads begin to slide backward; then little catches come up out of the belts. The gutter beside you seals itself from left to right. Across the avenue a wall goes to pieces so neatly that its individual leaves stay upright for a moment, balanced on their edges. By the time they fall, tendrils have already come out to sort them. Nothing comes out to see what happened. There are no animals, and the plants do not leave vacancies where animals might have been. Even the gap that opens under a rising slab is full of pale filaments, drawn out finer and finer as the slab goes up.

From above, if there were light to see it, the district would look almost level. It has taken a very long time to get that way. Whole tiers of buildings have been worn down by the passage of freight, and each time a tier wore through, the freight began to travel over the one beneath it. In a deep cut near the rim you can put your fingers into the rounded sockets of former upper stories. The newest shop in that cut uses one of the sockets to hold a warm roll of stock. Its leaves have grown a little lip to keep the roll from working loose.

The oldest feature is a curve in the avenue. There was once a tower inside the curve. The tower is gone, down to its roots, and there is no difference now between the material where it stood and the material around it. Still, every time the avenue is torn up, the belts relay themselves around that spot. Tonight one of them overshoots. It crosses two others, bunches up against a press, and sends a pile of cut stems spilling soundlessly into the dark. For a while you can feel the error propagate underfoot, a sequence of small lifts coming one after another. Then the pavement cools in a long arc, and you can walk the old way round.
