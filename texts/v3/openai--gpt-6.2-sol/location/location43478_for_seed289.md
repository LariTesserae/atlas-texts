Under the hill there is a room no larger than the space beneath a bent knee. Nothing leads to it. The roots stop short, the fissures pinch shut, and the burrows go elsewhere. It is a room in the sense that, here and nowhere immediately around it, the stone has an inside.

The light is fierce. It comes from the broken-looking edges of things that have never broken: little white ridges on the walls, the lips of hollows, the fine raised seams on the backs of the animals. Where two surfaces touch, the line between them is brighter than either surface. Even the smallest animal has to tuck its eyes under a flange of its own shell. From under those flanges they look out into a country of afternoons.

There is very little water. The floor is dry enough to keep its powder, but in three low places the ceiling wears a skin of moisture. The largest of these hangs in a shallow dimple. It looks like the first drop of a leak, though there is no wetness above it, nor any stain on the ground below. A frond no longer than an eyelash rests its tip against the drop. There are seven fronds in the room, all pale green at the tips and almost black where they disappear into the stone. They are sufficiently far apart for each to have a name, if anything here were in the habit of calling to anything else.

The animals are not so readily counted. The walls are tiled with shoulder, shell, folded leg. To see the floor you have to look through a transparent animal that lies flat on it and has six smaller animals fitted beneath the rim of its body. In the middle, a multitude of chalk-colored backs makes what at first appears to be a low outcrop. Each back has a small hollow just deep enough to receive the chin of its neighbor. Near the wet ceiling there are a few lengths of woven husk, set crosswise over narrow gaps. One is a bridge no wider than a hair, with an animal on either end and another underneath, holding it up.

It is loud enough that, were there room for a person, speaking would be useless. The sound is like a bucket of gravel tipping out onto a hard floor, except that it never gets to the last few stones. Much of it comes from a pale shelf at the rear, where a seam of young rock stands open. Elsewhere under the hill the stone carries old scratches and the dull surfaces left by long pressure. This shelf has neither. Its two halves have the tender, granular finish of something just about to part. You can see a grain poised outside the seam, with brightness all the way around it. The noise of its falling fills the room. The grain stays where it is.

Nothing here is waiting for the noise to stop. The bridge has its full burden; the little eyes have found their shade. At the uphill end, where there is scarcely a fingernail’s breadth of bare wall, the rock is beginning to take the shape of another hollow. At the very lip of it an animal has set down one foot. The foot fits the place so exactly that it is hard to tell whether the animal has found a foothold or the hill has begun, very carefully, to make one.
