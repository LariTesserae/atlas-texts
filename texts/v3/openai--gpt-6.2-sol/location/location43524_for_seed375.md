The town is nine streets wide. From its eastern gate you can see the western gate, and beyond it the pale rind of the plain. Nothing lies very high. Even the tallest houses, which have six floors and no stairs, stand in a shallow depression. In the dimness the whole town looks as though it has been pressed into a block of grey salt and the block lifted away.

The streets are precise. They narrow at the corners to make room for triangular courts; there are little recessed places where a person carrying something wide could stand to let another pass. Yet there is nothing to carry and no one to let pass. The houses are hollow, right down to the thin partitions between rooms. A sill has the thickness of a fingernail. Where one has broken, the fracture is black and glossy. No grit has gathered on it yet.

The town is still coming up. Along the north edge, foundation lines appear in the afternoon: first a darkening in the plain, then a crease, then two faces of clean mineral meeting at a right angle. Meanwhile, at the south edge, whole rows slowly draw apart. A party wall becomes two walls with a narrow passage between. What appeared to be a cupboard at dawn has a street entrance by dusk. There is no rubble. The new surfaces are colder than the old ones, and for a while you can tell which rooms were made that day by holding your hand near the doorway.

Most of the light comes from the plain. Far off, it is just bright enough to show through several houses at once, a stack of windows ending in a small grey square. There is no sun you can point to. At intervals the square vanishes, then reappears a floor lower in every house along the street.

Plants grow where the town pulls apart. They are low fans of smoke-coloured leaf, with white roots that grip the exposed corners. The leaves close over grains of salt. By the time they open again, the grain is gone and a transparent rib has lengthened a little. In some alleys, generations of these ribs make a loose tracery from wall to wall. It hangs at shin height. Nothing grows in the courts, though they seem to have been laid out for gardens.

Once in a while a flat, many-legged animal crosses a room. You see it best from the adjoining house, as a slight occlusion moving across that last grey window. It gets to the edge of the floor and waits there. When the wall opposite comes close, it steps across into the next building. It can wait the better part of a day. The smaller animals live between the plant ribs, and you may spend an hour looking directly at one before it unfolds a leg.

There is air, and it is cool enough to ache in the teeth, but it carries no dust. You can watch a whole frontage change without hearing it. Your own steps are audible only when you stop: a few small ticks come out of the ground behind you, not always the same number as the steps you took.

Near the western gate there is a short street with doors all down one side and a blank wall down the other. As the light fails, the wall begins to withdraw. The first thing it reveals is a strip of pavement, then a row of low thresholds. By morning the doors will have doors facing them. For a little while there is just enough room to stand between the two rows, with the plain visible at either end.
