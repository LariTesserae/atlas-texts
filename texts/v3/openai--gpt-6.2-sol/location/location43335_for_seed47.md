At the top of the continent there are pebbles with no underside. Pick one up and the surface that lay against the ground is as pale and smooth as the surface that faced the sky. The wind has turned them for so long that there is no customary way for any of them to rest.

The ground is white in places, though there is no salt in it. It is mostly feldspar worn to the grain, with long exposures of a darker stone that takes a polish from dust. The summit is broad enough to lose sight of its edges. You can go half a day on foot without coming to a rise higher than your knee. Then, at the northern lip, you look down through fourteen miles of clear air and see the shadow of the continent lying across a lower range. Those mountains have snow-colored summits too. They are as dry as this one.

There has been no open water here in the lifetime of the present rocks. The branching marks on the slopes record the slow withdrawal of minerals from one another, a process that has left some ledges striped and others freckled like old porcelain. In the lee of a stone you may find a patch of black vegetation no wider than a thumbnail. Its leaves lie flat, overlapping at the tips. At noon they separate just enough to admit light to the pale tissue below. Each patch follows the lengthening crack in its stone, and the oldest have been divided so often that what looks like a scattering of separate plants is still one individual, joined underneath.

The daylight is ordinary. A hand casts a hand-shaped shadow. The white ground makes you squint. It is cool enough that you keep your sleeves down even at noon, and after sunset the polished rocks grow cold before the air does.

Late in the morning the first disturbance usually comes up from below. It is visible well before it reaches the lip: a chain of dust plumes rising out of the lower ranges, each taller than a mountain and narrow enough to see daylight between it and the next. At the lip the plumes come apart. Dust shoots across the summit in ribbons several miles long. One ribbon goes east, another west, with a strip of perfectly clear air between, and then the strip folds and both ribbons go straight up. These changes take seconds. By midafternoon the whole northern sky may be in motion, with patches of blue opening and shutting deep inside it. You can watch from a slab in full sun, your sleeve hanging still.

The silence is most apparent when a storm passes directly overhead. Your eye insists on a noise to go with a mass that size turning over. Sometimes a grit strikes another grit close by, and you hear that.

There are buildings here, though not enough of them to make a road worth laying. The one nearest the northern lip is a waist-high room with a slanted opening and no space inside for a person. Its walls are made of the same dark stone as the ledge. Look through the opening while walking past and you see a little more of the far ranges than you should: the backs of ridges whose near faces are visible above the roof. At a certain angle you can see the shaded hollow immediately beneath your own feet. The floor inside the room is swept clean in a fan that ends precisely at the outer wall.

Farther inland stand pairs of narrow posts. Some are just stumps. On days when the plumes reach high enough, the space between an intact pair admits a descending column of dust. The column stops a finger’s breadth above the ground. It spreads there, turning slowly, until the sky clears. The posts are often hundreds of miles apart. Whatever the pairs were set out to do, they were set out across the whole continent.

A small animal comes to the lip at dusk. It is the color of the unpolished stone, and you are more likely to see its tracks than the animal itself: four dots, a long space, four dots again. It feeds beneath the black leaves. When it moves between plants it goes in a shallow leap, drawing its legs in as if to pass through a narrow gap. Once in a while one leaps out over the edge. It drops a little way and stops, its feet working on something too fine to be seen. Then it walks down at a slant, pausing here and there to put its head low. Under the lip, inverted in the shade, there are more plants than there are on the summit.

After dark the turbulence continues. You can follow it by watching the stars. Several disappear together, and a moment later they are back while a different patch goes out halfway across the sky. By morning the loose stones have changed places. A white hollow has filled with dark grains; a low slab that had been buried all summer is bare. From the next ridge the summit looks exactly as it did yesterday, and from the lower ranges no one could tell whether the night had been stormy or still. The little animal’s tracks will last until noon in the shelter of the room. There is one set going in and another, more widely spaced, coming out.
