The city stands on a coastal flat so low that a flight of six steps once counted as flood protection. You can still find those steps at the old pump houses. Their treads are hollowed in the middle, and the lowest one stays wet even on days when the tide never reaches it. Water comes up through the mortar. It shines for a while on the stone, then runs back down.

From the roof of the freight exchange, there seems to be very little city below. The streets were laid out for long vehicles, the turning circles for longer ones. Between the buildings are hectares of dark paving, with weighbridges and loading islands here and there. Most of the buildings are broad and only one or two stories high. The space above them belongs to the sky, though there is seldom much to see in it. Warm cloud comes in off the sea and lies low over the flat. At midday the lamps under the loading canopies are still on, their weak amber light plainly visible from the opposite curb.

There is no shade worth seeking. A canopy keeps the rain off, but it has been taking heat from the machinery all morning. Out in the open, the air is nearly as warm as the water in the gutters. A rain shower darkens the concrete without cooling it. For a few minutes afterward you can smell the different surfaces: tar at the expansion joints, iron at the drains, the clean chalky smell of a wall where the rain has washed away the salt.

Nothing grows in the joints. The lowest ground is flushed with brine twice a day; the higher yards were built on crushed clinker and have been swept for generations. There are places where you might expect a tuft of grass, particularly around the foot of a disused lamp, but the tuft turns out to be a gathering of shed crab shells, bleached gray and caught by their spines.

At first the yards look empty. Then a little water slips over a painted lane marker and the lane marker comes apart.

The crabs move with the advancing film, hundreds of them across a single loading apron. Most are no bigger than a thumbnail. On wet concrete their backs are the color of the concrete; where they pass onto white paint, you can see every leg. The older ones live down in the expansion joints. A gull lands beside one of these joints and waits, looking absently toward the warehouses. Then it runs three steps, jabs, and swallows. Farther out, where the storm channel widens into the harbor, mullet turn in the warm shallows, showing their sides a few at a time. The food that sustains all this comes in from offshore: plankton in the tidewater, scraps off the fish quay, the fine organic mud that settles wherever a current loses speed. By morning the animals have worked over the newest deposit and left the old surface showing.

Much of the industry is below the level of the street. Behind a low door marked with a depth gauge, a pump draws water out of the service tunnels. You can put a hand on the wall and feel it running. There is little to hear unless you stand at a discharge gate, where the water falls a short distance into the receiving channel. The gate is old enough that the stream has worn a shallow bowl in the concrete beneath it. Barnacles crowd the sides of the bowl, but its center is bare. A maintenance crew replaced the gate last winter and fitted the new lip directly over the old one. The water falls where it always did.

Toward evening, the tide turns and several things happen well apart from one another. The pump on the inland side cuts out. A line of shorebirds comes down onto a mud shelf that wasn't there an hour ago. In the freight district a roller door goes up, and warm yellow light reaches across three lanes of deserted pavement. Someone inside rolls a cart over a seam in the floor. The sound carries all the way to the channel, a single hollow knock, and then there is only the water again.

By then the apron is empty, too. All those crabs have gone into the joints. The last light lies along the wet edges of the slabs, outlining the old work, and under each of those bright lines the city is full.
