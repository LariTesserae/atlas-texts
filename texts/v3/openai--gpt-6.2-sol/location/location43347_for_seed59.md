The town occupies a fold in the mountainside about the size of a cathedral. From the trail above, you can see straight across it to the far bank, where three alders lean out of the slope and show the dark undersides of their roots. You can’t see the bottom. Ferns rise through the footbridges, willow crowns spread under them, and every gap in the leaves shows another set of leaves at a different depth. Now and then someone in the town opens a shutter and you catch the pale rectangle of a room.

It’s cool enough that the people selling things keep their hands tucked in their sleeves between customers. The rain has stopped, but on the lower streets it hasn’t made much difference. Water comes out of the uphill bank in a dozen places, wells through the moss along the walls, and drops from the willows in spurts when a bird lands. It’s clear water, with a faint tea color in the deeper channels. In the open, the light is ordinary midmorning light. It picks out a red cup on a windowsill and the steam off a woman’s hair as she comes out of the bakehouse.

You enter on a walkway of split alder uprights driven into a mat of roots. The treads have been worn scooped in the middle. Where one has gone soft, somebody has laid a fresh one over it and left the old wood in place; in the space between the two, a wren has a nest. Most of the town has accumulated that way. A handrail becomes the sill of a stall. A stall grows a loft. Two neighbors lash their eaves together over the lane and hang their washing from the joint. There’s a little stonework where the current is strongest, but the bulk of the place is wood, wicker, bark, and roots, with clay worked into the cracks. The willow revetments are alive. Every spring they send up shoots into the kitchens, and every spring the householders cut them back, except where someone wants shade or a new hook for a basket.

The whole place is loud. The largest of the channels drops only the height of a person, but it drops into a hollow under the market and makes the planks hum. Above that are the geese, the mallets, the stallholders calling across the gap, and the little slaps of fish taking insects off water you can’t see. A pair of boys drive ducks up a ramp; the ducks come down by two other ramps and have to be fetched out of a potter’s yard. Something in the sedge gives a long, rising whistle. A woman answers it before she realizes it isn’t her daughter.

There are animals at every distance. Flies walk on the close side of a pane of horn. A shrew travels the length of a wall without once coming out from under the leaves, betrayed by a moving tremor in the sorrel. Snails crowd the underside of a chopping bench, their shells dusted with flour. In the pools below the houses, frogspawn clings to the stems of last year’s reeds; the new reeds are already shoulder high on a child. In weather like this the swallows hunt low, passing so close through the market that people lift their bowls to let them by, the way they do for someone carrying a load.

The oldest thing here is the bend in the water. On the outside of it, the foot of the bank is polished gray and bare. On the inside, the current has been laying down grit for so long that a row of alders now grows where the oldest houses stood. When someone digs a posthole there, they sometimes bring up a blackened peg or a piece of pot with its rim still smooth. Otherwise there’s little to tell you how long anyone has lived here. The roofs are always being patched, and the work is never all at the same stage. A carpenter fits a rung with an iron drawknife while, under his bench, the rung he replaced goes on feeding a white fan of fungus.

Toward noon a shower passes over the higher ground. For a while nothing happens in town. Then the sound under the market deepens and three people get up at once. One pulls a pin and swings a bridge against the bank; one lifts a basket of live fish out of a side channel; the third goes down two steps with a pole to free a branch caught at the sluice. The branch comes loose all at once. It turns broadside, sprays the shoes of everyone at a dumpling stall, and vanishes under a floor. By the time the bridge goes back, fresh grit has covered the little beach where the ducks were standing.

On the far bank, barely fifty paces from the first walkway, you can put a hand on dry stone under an overhang. From there the paths are easier to make out. They follow the higher roots, and where two roots part, the streets climb apart with them. A narrow stair threads up through the alders to the mountain trail. All the way up it you hear the water, but the other sounds drop away by degrees, until the last thing you can make out is somebody below calling for a duck that has got onto a roof.
