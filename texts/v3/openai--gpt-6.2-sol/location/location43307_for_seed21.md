The rain stops at the lip of the basin. You can put one hand out over the drop and feel it striking your palm while the other hand, held behind you, dries in the heat.

The basin is no bigger than a chapel. It sits high enough that the clouds below it have shadows of their own, blue hollows moving over a white floor. Its walls are black stone, fresh and sharp where they broke apart. Nothing has had time to round the edges. Water wells up between the slabs and spreads over them in sheets so thin you can see the mineral grains underneath. Where it reaches a crack, it draws back a little before going in.

There are only five or six clumps of green, but each is extravagant. Ferns shoulder out of the seams in tiers. A plant with broad, pleated leaves holds a whole afternoon's rain in its folds, then tips one leaf and pours it into the next. Underneath, in the shade, new roots run pale and bare across the rock. The open stone between the clumps is hot enough that a handprint in the wet vanishes while you watch.

Someone built a step at the entrance, and farther in there is half a bench. Both are made of a gray, faintly gritty material that stays cool. The bench has no legs. A corner has broken off, and the fragment hangs a finger's width below it, turning very slowly. Water gathers on its underside and falls upward into the break. The stone around the bench has split twice since it was set here; you can follow the old fracture and the bright new one with your eye. Whatever holds the bench in place seems to have taken no notice.

At midday the basin is full of ordinary sunlight. The wet rock hurts to look at. Now and then a little brown animal comes out from beneath the ferns and crosses a long stretch of bare slab. Its feet make no sound, but they leave dry tracks. A moment later, when the tracks fill, the whole crossing is audible: six or seven small ticks in quick succession. By then the animal is gone.

Toward evening, the water in the deepest seam begins to rise. It doesn't spill. It lifts as a single narrow pane, carrying silt and the husks of tiny insects, until its upper edge stands level with the bench. The ferns nearest it have grown sideways, putting the tender ends of their fronds in its path. For a while you can watch the leaflets drink without a ripple. Then the pane leans, breaks, and runs across the floor in a warm rush. That is the loudest thing the basin does all day. The water takes a little black grit over the lip, and below, in the rain, there is no sound of it landing.
