The whole ward fits inside a bend in the floodwall. From the gate you can see the pump house at the other end, though in summer you have to know which patch of green to look at. Its roof is lower than the top of the wall. So are the roofs of the six houses, the old mat works, and the little brick substation that supplies them all.

The ground is so nearly level that water takes most of a day to cross it. After rain it lies warm and clear in the wheel ruts. Look down through it and there are no stones at the bottom, only fragments of leaf and the pale ends of roots, some as fine as threads pulled from a hem. The old paving is still there in places. It has settled into shallow bowls, each holding a different little stand of plants. A brick loosened for a pipe repair comes up with a brick-shaped pad of roots underneath it.

There is a surprising amount of daylight. The ward has few tall trees; most of what grows here wants the light for itself, and spreads sideways to get it. Cane crowds the windows at waist height. Ferns fill the gutters. The drain beside the works has a mat of pennywort so thick that when the pump starts you can watch the whole surface draw tight against the grate. Along the houses, trained vines make a continuous awning over the footway. The gaps are kept open with shears: one for every door, one for the meter reader, and a wider one where the afternoon sun is wanted on the washing.

The mat works is loud enough that people stop talking as they pass its open doors. Inside, rollers feed wet reed through in broad, evenly spaced bands. There is a sharp knock at the end of each pass, and beneath it the steady noise of the drive. The pump is deeper and less distinct. You can hear it through the handrail on the footbridge if you put your ear to the steel. Between the two buildings there is a narrow place where both sounds fall away. Water ticks off an awning there, even in dry weather. It collects overnight on the underside of the sheet metal and feeds a dark stripe of moss on the sill.

Not much stirs in all that growth. A snail sometimes gets as far as the white line on the substation step. A small brown bird comes down into the yard after the last shift, when the reed trimmings have been swept into a heap and there is nobody about to see what it finds. On hot afternoons the leaves nearest the exhaust fan flutter continuously, while leaves a hand’s breadth away hang still.

The pump keeps the ward dry to a mark on the brickwork, which is not the same as keeping it dry. In the older kitchens the floor is one step higher than the front path; in the newer ones it is two. Along the floodwall there are iron rings at the height where the path used to be. No one has moved them. The present path has built up over the old one by degrees, with reed waste, repair gravel, and soil lifted out of the drains. A handcart left on it overnight will have dew on the handles and fresh green tips showing between its spokes. The tips were there yesterday, too, but yesterday they were below the rim.
