The plain is so low that a hand laid flat on the ground can stop a stream. The water gathers against your little finger, hesitates, and goes around your knuckles. Farther out, the same stream is half a mile wide and no deeper. In the dim light it is hard to tell where it ends. The exposed stone shines almost as much as the water.

Nothing grows on it. There are no stems to give the wind away, no moss in the cracks, not even a green stain on the sheltered side of a boulder. Yet the plain is full of animals. You notice this first by looking into a pool and waiting for your eyes to adjust. What seemed to be a paving of overlapping pebbles are flat, jointed creatures huddled with their breathing edges outward. A shadow passes over them and the entire paving comes apart. The pool looks empty for a moment. Then, one after another, the pebbles set themselves down again.

It is difficult to keep watching because of the noise.

Under the plain are long cavities that fill and drain with the tide. Nobody has found the sea that supplies them. On the flood, the rising water drives cold air out through a few hundred holes in the stone, some wide enough to swallow a house, others narrow enough to whistle. The nearest hole gives a low, unbroken note that you feel in the backs of your teeth. Farther off are reports like heavy doors slamming, and behind those a high, wavering sound whose source is impossible to place. There is almost nothing on the plain to interrupt sound. You can see a white jet rise four miles away and have time to watch it fall before the concussion reaches you.

The plain has not always been flat. In places the wet stone shows the cross sections of folded beds, dark bands that turn on themselves and disappear beneath shallows. The folds are enormous when you follow them on foot, but they give you nothing to climb. All their height has been taken away. Even the gravel has had the corners worn off it. A chip struck freshly from the bedrock is startlingly pale; it looks like something brought here from another country.

The oldest shelters are easy to miss for that reason. Their stones were gathered, not cut, and are the same dim gray as the ground. A shelter is usually three courses of wall around a hollow with a slab across the top, just high enough to sit under. On the landward side, people have worn thumb-sized hollows into the entrance stones. You put your hand there to steady yourself on the way in and find the marks without looking. Inside, out of the direct wind, you can hear water ticking from the ceiling into a horn cup. You cannot hear the person next to you unless they put their mouth close to your ear.

There are fish traps at intervals, low crescents of loose stone that a person could build in an afternoon. Some have been tended so long that the slabs of bedrock on either side are worn smooth by bare feet. The fish are pale, almost translucent, and travel in the seepage ahead of the tide. They can cross a surprising distance on a film of water. At the traps, long-legged animals wait motionless with their spatulate bills resting on the surface. They hunt by touch. A person approaching in the worst of the noise can get near enough to see the little beads of water trembling along a bill, but one careless step sends a ripple ahead, and every head lifts.

The shallows sustain all this without a leaf. Deep below, where warm seepage meets the cold tide, pale crusts feed on dissolved minerals. The flood tears loose soft grains of them and carries them out. Small mouths are waiting everywhere. For an hour after the water turns, even the underside of a floating sliver of shell will have swimmers clinging to it. Then the plain drains, and what looked like a crowded world contracts into seams and pools, leaving acres of bare rock between them.

At the lowest water the blowing holes fall quiet in succession. This is when people carry baskets from one shelter to the next, or kneel at a trap and move the stones back into place. From a distance there is often just one person in sight, bent over, their reflection reaching almost to your feet. The sky never quite clears; toward evening a strip of it goes the color of old pewter, and the light on the ground improves just as the light overhead fails.

Then comes the sound that the shelters are built for. Not the first great blast, but a soft, close clucking, like water disturbed in a cup. It means the tide has reached the little holes beneath your feet. You have time to pick up your basket. Looking back from the shelter, you may still see the dry route you took across the plain, every rib of stone showing through the shallows. For a little while it looks perfectly walkable. Then, all along it, the water begins to leap.
