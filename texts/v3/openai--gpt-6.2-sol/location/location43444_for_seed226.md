The town is small enough to see across and high enough that nothing can be seen below it. On a clear day the last paving stone looks out on a white floor of cloud. Grass grows between the stones, but the joints are still sharp. The grass was there first.

There are six streets, a cistern, and a square with a stone bench along one side. None of the streets has a house. A few have doorsteps, set at intervals against the bank, and one has a gutter lined with thumb-sized pebbles. Whoever laid them sorted the pebbles by thickness, not color, so the gutter runs straight through little patches of pink and green. Beside the cistern lie two wooden mallets under an upturned basket. The work has reached that stage at which the town already has a front and a back, a good place to sit out of the wind, and a corner everyone cuts, though there is still nowhere to sleep.

The ridge itself is newer than some of the work. At the uphill end of the square, a pale lip of stone is coming out of the turf. It has lifted one end of the bench by the width of a barley grain since the bench was set. The masons have scratched a line across the lip and another across the bench so they can keep an eye on it. Elsewhere the new stone comes up in smooth, blunt folds, with black soil caught in the creases. There is no scree. Even the little chips beside the mallets have corners that can cut a bare foot.

Everything low to the ground is green. Ferns lean into the streets from both banks, and in the shallow plots behind the doorsteps grow thick beds of sorrel, moss, and a sort of glossy mountain mint that smells strongest when cold. There is hardly a tree. Above the ferns the place is open from end to end; a person at the cistern can watch a person on the far stair stop to retie a sandal. A fine gray spider has put a web across one of the doorways. The next sign of an animal is a set of small wet prints on the cistern rim, close enough together that whatever made them must have stood there awhile.

Water keeps the whole place loud. It comes out under the uphill bank, cold and clear, and runs in quantities scarcely deeper than a fingernail through the gutters. The noise begins where the gutters enter their covered drains. The passages underneath are much larger than they need to be, and the water strikes the stone somewhere out of sight with the broad, steady sound of rain on a roof. At the square, people standing an arm’s length apart have to raise their voices. Take three steps up onto the turf and the noise drops away. There you can hear drops pattering off the underside of the mint leaves, though the sun is out and has been for hours.

Toward evening the cloud below the town rises just far enough to wet the bottom stair. It seldom gets farther. The stair has nine treads so far, each wide enough for two people to pass with baskets, and ends on a shelf of clean stone. In autumn there may be room to cut a tenth. For now someone has set a shallow clay cup on the shelf to catch the water that beads along the edge. By morning it will be full. From the square, where the drains are loudest, no one will have heard it fill.
