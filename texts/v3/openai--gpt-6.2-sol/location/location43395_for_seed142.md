The city stands so high that there is no higher ground from which to look down on it. A road comes up out of the dark for the last few miles, makes one unnecessary turn, and levels off among the freight yards. The turn once took it around a hill. The hill is gone, but nobody has moved the road.

Up here, the land is warm. You can sit on a loading step in the dark and feel the stone through your clothes. Nothing gives off enough heat to account for it: not the sealed furnaces, not the buried mains, not even the generating house with its one live switchboard. At plate joints and in the bottom of old drill holes, the rock is exactly as warm as it is on the open plain. There is no dampness at any depth anyone has reached. The city has pipes for powdered lime, for air, for current, and for the slow return of worn bearings to the works. It never had a pipe for water.

Most of the city is the space between its buildings. A foundry occupies one side of a square big enough to take a whole train; on the other side is a row of six houses, each with a number stamped into its iron sill. Then there are acres of pavement before the next address. In the factory district the roofs are so low that a person standing on a wagon could put a hand on the gutters. The machinery descends instead, through floor after floor of warm, accurately cut rock. Some of the engines are still turning. You know them by the faint tremor in the bolt heads. Put your ear to a casing and you can hear the teeth meeting, one at a time, with a long interval between.

There are lamps at the crossings. Their light is just sufficient to show the brass curb studs and, if you kneel, the dust piled against them. It does not reach the opposite curb. From far out on the plateau the city looks like a handful of dull points at different heights, although the ground between them is almost perfectly flat. The oldest cobbles have worn down to the level of the mortar. The oldest mortar has worn down to the level of the bedrock. On some streets the only distinction between the two is that the rock will not take a nail.

Now and then a small jointed animal comes up through a cable slot and stands in the road. Its belly is pale and soft; the rest of it looks like a series of black thimbles. It feeds on the grease that extrudes from the axle boxes at the yard, and it can go a very long way between axle boxes. Sometimes it spends an entire watch beside a curb stud, feeling the brass with one foreleg. Nothing hunts it that anyone has seen. In the houses, even the dust is clean mineral dust. It smells faintly of struck flint when a door brushes it aside.

The municipal survey gives every street two lengths. One is the distance a wheel measures; the other is how far a sound will go. The measurements agree in the newer wards. In the old freight square a dropped wrench can be heard quite distinctly beneath the north awning and nowhere in between. The night porter there has learned to leave his chair under the awning. He can hear a visitor set down a bag at the far gate, though he cannot hear the visitor crossing the square. He waits until they are close enough to touch before he speaks.

Beyond the last houses are empty sidings, then shallow cuts where the sidings used to be, then bare plateau. No fence marks the end of town. For a while you can follow the route by the feel of the paving through your soles. Farther out, the paving and the plain feel alike. If you stop there and wait, the city gets neither brighter nor darker. The little lights hold their places. Under your feet the warm stone is worn smooth, though there is nothing now, not even a wind, to wear it.
