By midmorning the plateau is warm enough to sit on. The wind has come up from the low country, carrying the smell of wet stone, though you can see all the way down to the river and there is no rain between here and there. The river is a brown thread at this height. The water that matters up here is in the rock.

It darkens the undersides of ledges and collects in shallow bowls with pale mineral rims. There are bowls no wider than an eye and one broad enough to lie down in, all polished to the same dull finish. They hold very little. A long-legged animal tests three of them before it finds one worth folding itself down to drink from. When it gets up, it puts each foot back in the print that foot made on the way in.

From the rim you can take in most of the country: the three shelves of the plateau, a valley floored with loose yellow stone, and the rise on the far side where a solitary white building faces back toward you. The shelves are old enough that even their fractures have rounded edges. At the heads of the gullies, stones that once tumbled down are fitted into the clay like knuckles. Little grows in the open. There are patches of wiry grass, copper at the tips, and a kind of low thorn whose leaves lie flat against the ground, leaving the branches bare. Wherever two slabs lean together, something green has generally found the inch of shade.

The longer you look, the more animals there are. The dark marks on the opposite slope resolve into a herd moving uphill with several body lengths between neighbors. Tiny pale things are working their way around the dew bowls, eating whatever the night left on the rims. Out over the valley, broad-winged gliders hold so still in the rising air that the easiest way to find one is to look for its shadow. Now and then a shadow folds to a point and goes down among the stones. Everything near the place it lands stops moving; twenty yards away nothing takes notice.

Very little of this reaches the ear. You hear grit turn under your heel, and once, close by, the dry click of an animal shutting its mouth. A warm gust passes with scarcely a whisper. There is almost nothing tall enough for it to catch in.

The white building across the valley has a door, or something that looks like one. Late in the morning the door opens and sunlight comes through it at the angle of early dawn. You can watch that light travel down a slope already bleached by noon. It picks out the westward faces of stones that, a moment ago, had their backs to the sun. At the lower shelf it stops in a straight line. The animals know the line. They gather on the lit side, nosing at hairline cracks that begin, one after another, to bead with water.

There are two smaller works farther down, easy to mistake for boundary markers. One stands in the bottom of a gully that has long since worn away on either side of it. The other is a waist-high loop of gray material through which you can see the valley on a cloudy day, even when there is not a cloud above you. The cloud shadow inside the loop moves at the pace of a walking animal. The valley outside it stays fiercely bright. A thorn has grown partway across the opening, and its leaves are wet on the cloudy side.

By afternoon even the larger bowls are dry. A herd comes along the middle shelf and passes them without pausing. Farther on, where the ledge appears to end, the first animal puts its foot down on warm air and finds purchase. The others cross at the same unhurried pace, the young close behind the adults. You can see the yellow valley floor between their legs. On the far side, where their feet touch stone again, the ledge is worn into a shallow ramp. No one has had to cut steps.
