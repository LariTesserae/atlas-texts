The grove grows on the inside of a broken summit. From above it is a green thumbprint in white stone; from within, it is a room about the size of a cathedral, with one wall missing. Through that opening you can see the shadow of the mountain lying across a second range. Nothing at the opening gives you a sense of the drop. A blade of grass hangs over it, steadily dripping.

The stone is new to the air. A slab that once roofed the hollow lies far below in three pieces, its upper face already grey, its broken faces still the colour of milk. Up here the fracture runs straight through pebbles and fossils alike. There is a shell half in the wall and half in a loose block on the floor. Neither half has worn enough to keep them from fitting.

For all the green, there is hardly any soil. The willows grow flat against the rock on plaits of exposed root. Their little leaves turn silver side up when air spills over the rim, and a whole bank of them can change colour without making a sound. Ferns occupy the seams. There are cushions of moss so thick you could sink a hand into them, but between one cushion and the next there may be twenty feet of bare stone. Most of what the hollow contains is air. You can stand in it a long time before finding a place soft enough to sit.

It is cold even at noon. Sunlight comes down almost white, showing the veins in the fern fronds and the dirt beneath your fingernails. Every so often a scrap of cloud catches on the upper rim. For a minute you cannot see across the hollow. Then it lifts, and everything that stood in it is wet on one side. The beads on the willows run down the stems into the moss. Only at the lowest corner does water collect, in a shallow basin where the rock shows pink through it. The basin has a lid of ice in the morning and sometimes acquires another before the afternoon is over.

At first there seem to be no animals. Then the bare wall begins to resolve into ledges. On one of them are six small, pale goats, lying with their legs tucked under them; on another is a row of birds with black feet. A seventh goat comes down a crease too narrow to see from the floor. It pauses to eat the green tips off a fern, dislodges a chip of stone, and stands chewing while the chip ticks twice on its way down. The sun brings beetles out from beneath the willow mats. A bird drops among them, and the beetles vanish; the bird leaves, and they come out again, one by one, from a dozen different cracks. There is nearly always something happening, but rarely anything loud enough to hear from across the room.

Someone has laid three flat stones across the basin so that a person can reach the wall without wetting their feet. In a notch there, sheltered by a projecting lip, is a shallow cup chipped from dark rock. Its rim is smooth on the side nearest the stepping stones. A little farther up, a strip of rawhide still binds two willow stems where somebody bent them aside to pass. The stems have thickened around it. There is no house, and nowhere level enough to build one without covering the best of the green.

Late in the day the shadow of the rim crosses the floor. The animals move ahead of it. A goat gets up and stretches, and the ledge where it lay is briefly darker than the stone around it. The birds shift to the opposite wall. For a while, all the life you can see is crowded into a bright strip no wider than a hand held at arm’s length. Then the strip climbs beyond the roots, and the hollow goes blue. High above it, the broken edge of the summit is still in full sun. From the basin comes one small click as the ice takes hold.
