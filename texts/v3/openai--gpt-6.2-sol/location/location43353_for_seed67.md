The plain is low enough that a curb can have weather on one side and not the other. On the wet side, a film of warm water lies over the paving. On the dry side, the paving is warm and pale and shows the parallel marks of the cutter that finished it. The curb itself is black ceramic, sharp along the top. There are thousands of miles of this edge, with interruptions for stairs that descend three steps into water no deeper than the first step.

Nothing here has worn down. Bolt heads stand proud of their plates; the casting fins have not broken off the pipe elbows. The water looks as though it fell in the first rain after the forms were stripped. Some of the biggest slabs still carry their lifting numbers, legible through the shallows. Follow the numbers far enough and they run out of digits and begin again with a little drawing of a hand.

There is no ground under the paving. Where inspection shafts open, you can see floor after floor of glazed vaults, their pillars widening toward the bottom of sight. One shaft has a sample of each floor material set into its stair rail: ceramic, steel, a brown resin with the finish of a good tooth. The samples are all the way up at daylight, where they can be examined without having to go down. Far below them, a drop of water hangs from the corner of a beam. It has the long neck of a drop about to fall.

The light is ordinary enough to read by. Warm banks of cloud extend along the low roofs, and beyond them are dry, sunlit provinces of cream-colored aggregate. This is the scale on which the plain has weather: you can spend a morning looking at a rain front whose far end is still below the horizon. The rain is visible, each bright thread separated from the next. At the edge of the front there is a white stripe across a service road. Half the stripe is wet.

The animals are most apparent on the sheltered side of that road. At first the drainage wall seems to have an elaborate surface, ribbed in some places and quilted in others. Then there are eyes. Flat animals lie belly-down against the ceramic, their hind feet folded over the backs of the animals below them. Pale, jointed creatures occupy the gaps under their chins. There are little ones in the hinge of a larger one's jaw, where a spoonful of water has collected among the teeth. The larger animal has one eye above water and one below; the upper eye reflects the stripe on the road, the lower a row of tiny feet.

Once you have seen the animals in the wall it is difficult to find a place without them. They pack the clearance beneath the pump housings. They hang in pendant clusters from bridge bearings. Out in the sun the basins are full of broad-backed swimmers, fitted together so closely that the thin water between them makes a pattern like the joints in the paving. An animal with a long neck has reached over three of its neighbors to put its mouth against a drip lip. Its mouth is open just enough to show the soft gray pad inside. The drop touches it but has not spread.

There are no roots prying up the slabs, no green at the wet seams. What looks like moss on the warmer conduits is a crowd of animals with fine fingers. From close by you can see that each is holding on with only two or three of them. Elsewhere, on a hump of dry paving spacious enough to have been a town square, thousands of white shells lie directly in the light. Most have occupants. Some have been outgrown, and the creatures beside them are larger by exactly as much as you would expect. A few are still halfway out, pleated and damp where they haven't yet expanded.

Everything necessary to move water is present: impellers, sluices, screens with rakes poised above them. The pump halls are long and low, their roof vents repeating until the perspective squeezes them into a solid band. There is a faint electrical note inside, so even that you notice it only when you step behind a thick pier and lose it. That and the pressure of warm water against hollow panels account for nearly all the sound. The rain makes none. Nor do the crowding animals, though several have the stretched throats and raised heads of animals calling to one another.

From the top of a pump hall the whole place looks busy. Water is breaking over a weir. A sheet of spray leans out of a cooling yard. In the sun, above the service road, a flock is banking, the near wings dark and the farther ones transparent. The longer you look, the more you can pick out: a separate feather bent at its tip, a flaw in the glass of a signal lamp, a small animal at the weir with both front feet clear of the water. You can go down and look from beneath. The feet have pads, and one pad has a pale scar.

On the service road there is a bench facing the wet half of the stripe. Its seat has been molded with a slight dip so that it will drain toward the ends. A bead of water rests just short of one end, holding the road, the rain and the underside of the flock in a clear, upside-down view. The bench is warm all the way through. There is room to sit without disturbing anything.
