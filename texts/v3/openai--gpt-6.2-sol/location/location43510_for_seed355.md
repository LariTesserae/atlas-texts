The town is low enough that the marsh comes in without making a shore. Water lies between the paving stones, covers the bottom step of each doorway, and fills the broad, shallow depressions where houses used to stand. At the edge of town, the streets continue for a while as bands of firmer mud. You can follow one barefoot long after you’ve lost the walls on either side.

There is no hour at which you can see the whole place. On a clear night, a little light comes off the cloud cover, enough to distinguish the water from the darker walls. Mostly you find your way by touch. The brick is warm and the mortar has worn back into seams deep enough to take a finger. At the corners the brick itself has rounded off. Even the chips in the paving have gone smooth. Nothing here has been broken recently.

It is hottest just after the rain. The courts give back the heat they took in during the long dry intervals, and water condenses on the underside of the arches. Drops gather there for hours before one falls. You can hear it two streets away. There are no frogs, no insects at the waterline, nothing nesting in the joints of the brick. A few salt grasses grow along the tops of walls, their roots descending through the mortar toward rooms that have been wet for a very long time. In the deeper courts, pale films spread over the water and part along the paths of the drops. By the time the next drop falls, the first opening has closed.

It was a substantial town. The drains run straight for hundreds of paces. There are public stairs shallow enough to climb with a full jar, and a marketplace with stone benches on three sides. But the buildings stand far apart now, and in the dark the empty lots are hard to tell from the open marsh. Past the market you can walk for ten minutes with one hand on a wall so low it seems to enclose nothing. Then the wall takes a turn and you find a doorstep, and beyond it the warmth of a room.

Water does not keep a common level here. In the market it stands ankle-deep on one side of a doorway and at the height of your waist on the other. There is no step between them. The surface runs level in both places and meets the jamb without bending. If you pass through, the water doesn’t pour after you; you simply find it higher on your body. The old builders seem to have known about this. The plaster on the two sides is worn to different heights, and on the deeper side someone set a bench into the wall at just the right depth to sit with your feet in the water.

Farther out, the streets don’t always agree on what lies between them. Two run parallel from the market to the marsh. Walk down either one and you pass the same three cross lanes. Take the middle lane from the west, and you cross it in six strides. Take it from the east, and there is a whole residential quarter in the crossing: bare courts, a cistern, the low wall of a garden. The cistern is fed by a single clay gutter that projects from a house with no roof. In rain, it runs for a little while before the rain starts.

The town changes most in the hours when it seems absolutely still. Water creeps up the pores of the brick and darkens it above the reach of any flood. Silt works its way down the drains a grain at a time. On the flats, shallow sheets of rainwater draw together and move inland, leaving a skin of salt that the next rain takes away. Now and then a whole court drains between one visit and the next, and the floor proves to be set with small blue tiles. They are almost impossible to see. You can feel their edges with the soles of your feet.

At the outermost houses, there are steps with neither walls nor roofs behind them. The marsh covers the bottom tread and leaves the upper ones warm and dry. Stand there long enough and you can hear rain falling out on the flats, a soft, even sound with no wind in it. It approaches until the step beside your foot begins to tick with drops. Then it moves on into town. The place where you’re standing stays dry a little longer, though the water has already begun to rise around it.
