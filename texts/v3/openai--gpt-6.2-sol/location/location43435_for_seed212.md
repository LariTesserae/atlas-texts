At noon you can see the whole town from the lip of a leaf.

It lies low and spreads for miles. There are squares, long streets, courts within courts, and an outer ring of closely spaced uprights beyond which the plain goes on in a single bright sheet. Nothing was quarried to make the town. The squares are places where a broad leaf has put out no veins; the streets are the creases between leaves. The uprights have soft white tips. They are taller than any of the roofs and still pushing up.

The leaf you stand on is warm enough to feel through your feet. Its edge, which appeared blunt from below, is keener than a fresh split in wood. There is no soil under it. If you work down through a seam, past the packed fibers with their little windows of amber sap, you reach another surface, paler for having never seen the sun. Even the black stuff at the lowest level is alive. It parts with a noise like wet cloth and begins to close before you have withdrawn your hand.

Water is scarce enough here that a drop can have a history. In the shaded courts it condenses along the underside of a rib, travels as far as a joint, and hangs there through the warm part of the day. A root tip may grow the last inch to meet it. Sometimes a whole street turns over in the instant before contact, and the drop falls a long way through hot air and lands on a face that has never been wet. There it leaves a dark mark with a surprisingly sharp edge.

The noise is appalling. No birds are calling over it, no insects filling the gaps. There are no gaps. The big sound is the tearing of sheaths as new growth shoulders through them, a rip that can run the length of a district. Under it is the clatter of dry seed cases, the knock of stems swollen tight against one another, and the low continuous note of air forced out of hollows that are getting smaller. Two leaves come together across a court and the report hurts your teeth. A thin green filament goes on growing across the opening as if nothing has happened.

From above, what looks like a regular plan is the result of countless such collisions. A stem forced sideways by its neighbors will run straight for hundreds of yards, roofed over by the bases of the very growth that bent it. Where several meet, there is room enough to stand in the sun. Those places have the proportions of public squares. You half expect to find their edges worn smooth, but there are no worn edges. At one corner the pale cuticle has not yet darkened. A lane that looks old because it is narrow may have been squeezed into existence since morning.

The sun behaves ordinarily. A blade held up to it makes a shadow. The difficulty is that the shadow does not always fall in a place that belongs to the blade. In one court you may look down at the clear silhouette of a crown and find, by walking through its branches, that it is the crown on the far side of town. At the end of a street you can see the plain, with the light falling across it from the left. Go to the mouth of the next street and look back: there is the same strip of plain, and the light still falls from the left. A split stem with a bead of sap on it is visible in both views. The bead is falling in one and not yet loose in the other.

Plants take advantage of these discrepancies. A creeper can enter a crease near the middle of town, come out at the perimeter, and put down feeding roots there while its leading tip is still making its way through the crease. The strain shows in its leaves. They are small and folded at first, then huge for three or four joints, then small again. Sometimes it draws so hard on a seam that two quarters of the town touch. For a while you can cross between them on a strip of green no wider than your foot. When the strip breaks, there is a crash from deeper down, as if the quarters had been holding up something neither of them could see.

Toward evening, the outer uprights begin to unfold. Each has been carrying a long blade rolled tight enough to look solid. As the blades come free they catch the warm air and lean inward, one after the next. For a little while the whole town is roofed. Light comes through in patches of yellow and dull red. Drops gather quickly in the new shade. The nearest one trembles, and the roar drops just far enough that you can hear it hit.

By the time the blade underfoot darkens, a white tip has already pierced it from below. You can watch the rim of the hole lift and split. Beyond it, at the height of your ankle, a new street runs straight into the distance, with sunlight on its floor.
