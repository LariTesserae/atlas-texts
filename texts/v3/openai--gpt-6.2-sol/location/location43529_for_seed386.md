At the height where you would expect snow, there is a marsh.

It lies along the back of a mountain, in a saddle wide enough to have its own weather. On either side the ground drops away so steeply that you can stand among reeds and look down on the tops of clouds. The marsh itself is nearly level. A little water passes through it all the time, without seeming to go anywhere. Where the grass parts, the sky shows in long, narrow pieces.

The heat comes up through the soles of your feet. Rain cools the leaves for a few minutes, but it does not cool the mountain. After a shower the black rock dries in patches, from underneath, leaving pale mineral rings around the places where the last drops stood. Water that has passed through the rock comes out hot enough to keep a few channels clear of plants. A few paces farther on, where those channels mix with rainwater, fern roots hang in them in thick brown skeins. Small fish hold in the shade of the roots. If your shadow crosses theirs, they move deeper in without breaking the surface.

From the ridge you can see most of the basin: the open water, the belts of cane, the wooded knolls that rise out of it on skirts of old scree. It looks possible to cross in an afternoon. Down in the cane you can rarely see farther than a few yards. The stems are close enough to touch both shoulders, and now and then a low branch empties its rain down your back. On the knolls, trees grow broad rather than tall, with their weight distributed among several trunks. The wind has been coming over the same notch for so long that even the youngest trees seem to know which way to put out a branch.

There is no road, no wire, no worked piece of stone. But under the western rim the mountain does work on a scale that is hard to think of as merely a spring. Rain goes down through one set of fractures; hot water rises through another. Steam collects under shelves of rock and is released in stages. You can watch the stages travel along the rim by the way the sedges lift and settle, one bed after another, though you hear little beyond a soft knock at the nearest vent. All day the water brings dissolved stone up from below and sorts it into bands: white at the hot mouths, ochre farther out, and a thin black deposit where the flow goes slack. The bands have been forming long enough for trees to grow on some of them. In one place a root has split an old white ledge and a new white lip is growing over the root.

Most of the animals are apparent first by what they disturb. A reed bends against the wind. A set of rings crosses a still pool and meets another set coming back from the bank. In the middle of the day, narrow-winged birds feed over the open water, taking insects almost at the surface. When a cloud rises over the rim, the birds climb out ahead of it and the insects settle under the leaves. For a while the only movement is the rain dimpling water that was already full.

At the far end of the basin, the outflow spreads thinly over a broad sill. The stone there has been worn smooth, with shallow hollows where small ferns can get a hold. There is no great sound at the brink. The water slips over in separate ribbons, and you have to lean out to see them come together far below. On a clear afternoon you can follow the falls almost to their foot. Then a cloud drifts across the lower slope, crosses the path of the falling water, and goes on. Up in the saddle the marsh scarcely changes level. Along its margins, fresh drops are gathering at the tips of the grass.
