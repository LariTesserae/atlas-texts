The room is no bigger than the nave of a church. The city is in its walls.

You come down into it by a stair that spends its last nine steps under the ceiling of the room itself, so that for a little while you are walking out over the dark with nothing visible to either side. The handrail is warm. At the bottom, the floor slopes very gently toward a slot about as wide as a fingernail. All the paving stones have settled into this slope. There was a time when each had a raised rim to give a boot some purchase, but the rims wore away long ago, and now even a shallow puddle can travel the length of the room without breaking up.

It is loud enough that you have to put your mouth against another person’s ear. There is no one here to do that with.

Most of the noise is traffic. Four streets cross the room at different heights, though you could stand at its center and throw a stone clean through all four. The streets have no decks. Their weight is taken sideways into the walls, and the walls hold the shape of whatever is passing through. A freight wagon goes over with a low, sustained bellow. If you happen to be resting your hand on the stone, you can feel its axles individually. Much later come the lighter, irregular impacts of foot traffic. There is sometimes a pause midway across, where the city map shows a bench.

The builders left the interior empty because anything substantial placed in it would have had a share in the load. An early caretaker’s stool appears in the rules, between the prohibition on masonry repairs and the procedure for a stalled tram. It lasted a week. There are handholds, inspection niches, miles of narrow conduits packed into the thick perimeter; there is not so much as a hanging chain in the open space. Even the lamps are sunk into the stone. Most have gone out. One near the foot of the stair comes on for a breath whenever the busiest street changes direction, illuminating six steps, a smear of mineral white, and nothing behind them.

The warmth is steady, the dampness not. Toward evening, when the houses above begin drawing heat, the north wall cools enough to sweat. It takes a long time for the first drop to fall. After that there may be a brief, uneven rain along that side of the room, while the opposite pavement stays dry. The rain has cut small bowls in the old mortar. Some are deep enough for a fingertip. Pale moss grows in two of them within reach of the lamp; farther in, the moisture feeds a thin fungal skin with the color and finish of old paste. Once in a while a cellar cricket ventures out from a joint to graze on it. It moves during the heaviest traffic, when there is no chance of hearing anything that might be following it. What follows it, if anything, is rarer still.

The room has been in service long enough for the wall to have a kind of grain. The original stone is dark and close-textured, but generations of repairs show up under the fingers: a seam slick as a tooth, a gritty patch, a place where the load has drawn a once-straight joint into a shallow arc. None of this is cause for concern. The city long ago stopped trying to bring the walls back into true. New streets are set to the room as it is found.

There is a quiet interval before dawn. You can hear the warm drops striking the paving, and if you wait by the drain, you can hear the water coming toward you. Then the first tram crosses. The drops tremble in their bowls. A little farther down the wall, one that would have fallen draws itself back into the seam.
