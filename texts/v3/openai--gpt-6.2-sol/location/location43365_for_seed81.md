The road comes up through a cut in the basalt and enters the city at roof height. From there you can see three districts, the reservoir between them, and the far escarpment with its old freight incline. What you cannot see is the bottom of the street immediately below you. A thin layer of steam hangs at the level of the second stories. It has a perfectly level upper surface, even when the wind is blowing.

The city occupies a shelf high on the side of a mountain. Beyond the shelf there is a fall of several thousand feet, but the country below is usually hidden, and the height makes itself felt chiefly in the rain. Drops arrive large and widely spaced. They strike warm stone and spread to the size of a hand. Sometimes the pavement dries between drops; sometimes the rain goes on for hours and even the undersides of the bridges begin to run.

Nothing green grows here. The pale fringes on the parapets are salts, soft enough when wet to take a fingerprint. Down at the reservoir, black crusts form over the mouths of the feeder pipes. They grow inward from the rim in little overlapping shelves until the pressure breaks them out. You can watch a whole shelf drift into open water, turn over once, and sink. The pale underside is briefly the brightest thing in the district.

It is hot by day and hotter after dark, when the exposed ridges cool and water rises from the deep workings. The older houses were built for this. Their ground floors are open from end to end, so a rise can pass through without taking a wall with it. Upstairs, the rooms are deep and narrow. Iron beds stand well away from the plaster. There are glazed cupboards for anything that must stay dry, including the switches. In some districts the electric lamps still come on, one per landing, red enough to leave the stair below the landing invisible.

There is a city’s worth of building and almost nothing inside it. Across the reservoir the façades seem close-set; on foot, the distances turn out to be considerable. A single foundry takes up most of an afternoon. You cross its receiving yard, its weighing hall, a roofed space where six cranes hang over six empty pits, and finally a tiled passage with a drinking fountain at either end. Both fountains work. At the center of the foundry the nearest wall is so far away that the rain through a broken skylight seems to be falling outdoors.

The machines that still run are mostly below street level. You notice them in a handrail before you hear them. A pump gives three strokes, rests, then gives a fourth; the iron trembles faintly along an entire block. At the hour when the reservoir is allowed to fall, the submerged stairs come out of the water one at a time, and air enters the drain mouths with a noise like someone shifting in a chair. Then it is quiet enough to hear drops striking the different widths of the gutter.

The gutters are older than the houses. In places a new street has been cut straight through a bend in one, and the bend continues under the buildings, distinguishable by the broad damp stripe it leaves on the cellar ceilings. Everything that has had water running over it for any length of time has settled to a similar finish: basalt, mortar, the heads of great bolts, the fired brick laid around the return pipes. The edges are rounded and cool-looking. They are too hot to lean on.

A few small animals live in the upper works. At the freight incline you may find three-toed prints in the mineral dust, always on the downhill side of the rail. The animal itself is about the length of a finger and nearly transparent except for a dark joint in each leg. It spends the wet hours in the screw holes. When a length of rail dries, it comes out and drinks from the prints it made on its way in. Once in a while something much larger crosses the reservoir underneath the surface, leaving a narrow lane clear of drifting crusts. The lane can take twenty minutes to close.

The water does one thing here that a first-time visitor has trouble believing. A shallow pool reflects whatever stood above it when it filled. In the courts where roofs have fallen, the puddles still show rafters, sometimes with a lamp burning among them. If you disturb the water the image breaks as any reflection would, and when it settles the rafters come back. Fresh rain does not replace the image unless the pool has first gone entirely dry. One of the old municipal jobs was to empty the pools beneath the freight bridges so that after a repair the inspectors could see the new work from below. There are still brass plugs in the paving for this, set flush and worn smooth by the key.

Toward evening the steam in the streets sinks. It leaves the upper windows clear first, then the doorways, then the curb. For a little while you can see all the way down the long avenue from the foundry to the incline. There are cross streets where the lamps are out and others where they have never been installed. Water is coming down the avenue in a sheet scarcely deep enough to cover the cobbles. At each intersection it divides around the corner stones and goes on into streets you cannot see. Far below the city, where the drain discharges from the cliff, the water falls so far that it breaks up completely before it reaches anything. On still nights some of it comes back as rain.
