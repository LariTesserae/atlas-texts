The whole marsh would fit inside a cathedral. From one rim you can see the other, a low sill of stone with a single fern growing over it. There is nowhere in the basin high enough to keep your feet dry. Even the stone sweats in the sun.

It is a warm place, bright enough after rain that the undersides of leaves are lit by their reflections. The plants grow in a few extravagant clumps: broad leaves leaning out over open water, rushes with fat red bases, a little fig whose roots have spread across a slab without finding a crack. Between them lie long reaches of pale mud and water scarcely deeper than the skin of a plum. The basin has been wearing down so long that the harder seams in the stone stand no higher than the whorls of a fingerprint. You can follow a dark mineral band from one end of the marsh to the other without once seeing it cast a shadow.

At first there seem to be only three or four animals. A white wader stands by the fig. Two black insects skate on a puddle. A snail has climbed halfway up a rush and stopped.

Then the wader takes a step, and the floor parts around its foot. What looked like mica is a bed of translucent shrimp, packed shoulder to shoulder. The reddish margin of the pool is made of small frogs with their throats tucked in. In the sun beyond the fern, a haze of wings keeps forming and thinning; when a cloud passes, the whole haze settles on the mud and walks. There are animals in almost every patch of the basin, but they leave the water between the plants so clear that you can stand in the middle and feel alone.

The wader hunts by putting the tip of its bill in the water and waiting. Now and then a shrimp makes the mistake of coming close enough to rest against it. There is a little click. Farther out, a raft of water beetles divides around a submerged stem and joins again on the other side. The loudest thing for most of the afternoon is a drip falling from the fig. It takes a surprisingly long time to gather.

There are two pieces of work here. One is a seat, or something worn to the shape of a seat, set into the rim. The other is a square of dull gray material sunk flush with the floor of the longest pool. It has a thumb-sized chip missing from a corner. Each time the water rises over that corner, the reflection of the sky stops at the break, though the square itself is well beneath the surface. In the little triangle left clear, you can see the underside of the pool from several feet below: roots dangling, shrimp hanging in the light, the dark pads of the wader’s toes. A leaf drifting over the corner appears broadside in the pool and edge-on in the triangle. Its two images reach the far edge together.

Small fish gather there in the heat of the day. They take turns crossing the break and come out with droplets on their backs, though they never leave the water. The wader has learned to wait at the far end of the square. Whatever the thing was made to do, it still does it with a chipped corner and a season’s worth of silt on top.

By late afternoon the open mud is warm enough to hatch the flies that have been working their way up through it all day. For a few minutes the basin is full of them. Frogs rise, the rushes bow under the weight of wings, and the wader walks quickly, taking one long step after another. Then the flies clear the rim. From the seat you can watch them go out over the flat country, where there is no taller ground to catch the last of the light.

The marsh is quiet again before the sun has moved appreciably. On the underside of the fern, a pale bead swells. When it falls, the rings travel almost all the way across the pool.
