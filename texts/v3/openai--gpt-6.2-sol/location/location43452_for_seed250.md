The highest ground is still underwater. It rises in long, blue-black folds, some broad enough to carry an ocean of their own between one crest and the next. At noon, sunlight reaches the upper slopes in moving bands. In the hollows it is still day, but there is nothing down there for the light to fall on.

The rock is new. Along the ridges it has the dull shine of a broken plate, and where a fold has opened you can see pale minerals in the seam, soft enough to come away in the current. There is almost no sand. The water has scarcely had time to make any. A ledge can stand clean-edged through a hundred storms and then go over whole, leaving the same sharp profile a little farther uphill.

The sea is cool enough that the little warmth it takes from the sun matters. Above a broad summit, water warmed through the afternoon gathers in a shallow lens. From below, the lens is visible as a wavering in the light. At dusk it breaks up. Long tongues of warmer water spill over the lip of the summit and are caught, folded, and drawn out to lengths of several miles. Now and then one passes over a patch of vegetation: a few dark green ribbons rooted in a crack, all pressed flat in the same direction. The ribbons have no bite marks. Nothing grazes them or shelters among them. When one comes loose it may travel for years without touching another living thing.

It is loud even in the empty stretches. The sound is mostly too low to tell where it comes from. A fall of rock might be heard on the far side of the plateau, through ground that has not yet learned to break up a vibration. When currents meet above a ridge, their boundary can hold steady for hours, humming on one note, then tear from end to end. Afterward there is often a surprisingly ordinary sound: stones knocking together as they settle.

There are no ruins here, or even stones set one upon another. Nevertheless the sea was made to do something.

You can see it best from a high crest on a calm morning. Far off, a band of water begins to turn. The turn travels toward you, across trenches and through weather, without varying its speed. Behind it, loose material rises. A grain of quartz lifts out of a fissure; a slab the size of a foothill lifts from the foot of a scarp. Both follow the same gentle arc. At the crest they stop. For a moment each hangs clear of its shadow. Then the grain goes into a seam and the slab settles against the mountain with a report that takes half a minute to finish.

Whatever is doing the work does not keep the sea still while it works. Some mornings the new stone is torn away before the turn has reached the horizon. The following morning the turn comes at the same speed, but the arc is different. Over a season a ridge may grow several feet, or lose its whole eastern face and begin again farther west.

There is a place near the top where the current divides around a low shelf. Green ribbons have taken hold on its downstream side. The shelf lies in good light and could be mistaken for an old, settled part of the world. Then a swell passes, and for an instant the surface overhead is smooth enough to show a cloud. The shelf rises almost imperceptibly toward it. In the crack, each ribbon has put out a thin white root into stone that wasn't there yesterday.
