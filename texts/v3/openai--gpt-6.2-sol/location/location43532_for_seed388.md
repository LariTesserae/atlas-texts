The city is six streets wide here, and none of them is quite dry.

It occupies a shallow bowl behind the river wall. From the footbridge you can see the whole of it: the pump house, the backs of the tenements, the long glazed roof of the works. The chimneys look tall only because there is nothing higher behind them. Even the river is out of sight. You know its level by a dark band that climbs the brick in the deepest cellars, a little higher each day for a week after rain upstream.

Underfoot the paving stones have worn into shallow dishes. There is a different sky in each one. Where the alley narrows, warm water comes out beneath a blue door and runs along a channel cut so long ago that the lip has rounded like the edge of a spoon. Nobody has put a grate over it. The little gardens depend on that water. Ferns fill the steps down to the boiler rooms; watercress jams the channel at the corner and has to be lifted out in dripping armfuls. On the old loading platform a fig tree has spread its roots over the grooved iron where the freight doors used to slide. Its leaves lie nearly flat against the windows, making the rooms behind them green.

The works are running. Inside, a flywheel passes again and again through the light of a single yellow lamp. You can hear the broad rush of the belts from every street, and at intervals a blow from the rolling shed comes up through the soles of your feet. At first it seems too loud to live with. After a while you notice that the gutters have a sound of their own, and that one broken pane in the pump house rattles just before the blow, while all the others rattle just after.

Nothing stirs in the warm water. No larvae hang beneath the surface; no flies gather at the fruit that splits on the platform. The fallen figs go white with mold and slump into the cracks. The ivy blooms anyway. On still afternoons its smell thickens under the bridge until you step out into the smell of wet iron.

In the middle of the smallest court is a settling tank with a handrail all the way around it. It takes the overflow from the works. Fine red silt enters at one end and lies down in long ribs on the bottom. The tank is cleaned once a year, though by the next morning the ribs are back, with the same gap in the third one. The habit of the water might account for this. It does not account for the reflection of the footbridge, which crosses the tank from corner to corner. The bridge itself is two streets away. At dusk, when its lamps come on, their reflection comes on here too.

There is scarcely an evening when you can tell that the water has risen. In the morning a brick that was dry is wet. Later in the week a fern frond touches its own reflection, and stays there, making a small crease that the vibration of the works never quite shakes flat.
