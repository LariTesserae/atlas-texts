The city has poured three of the walls. The fourth is still rock.

It is a small room for so much city, thirty paces from the service door to the rock face, but the pipes that come through it are big enough to walk in. They cross high overhead in pairs, with narrower pipes hung beneath them, each going somewhere different. In the amber light the newer concrete looks almost white. You can still see the grain of the boards they used to form it and, near the bottom of one wall, the print of a carpenter’s glove.

The heat is already there when you open the door. It comes principally from a broad insulated main, though the insulation is unfinished at the elbows and you can feel those places on your face as you pass. Water pearls on the cooler pipes that bring in the city supply. There is plenty of dripping and very little fall to the floor. Along one side, a shallow sheet of water has found the level it wants and keeps it. The reflection of the stair comes down through the water more distinctly than the stair itself can be seen through the humid air.

There are cockroaches on the underside of every tread.

You might miss them coming down. There are only one or two out on the open floor. Then the door shuts, disturbing the air, and a hundred feelers move along the lip of the bottom step. The walls are bare at eye level. It is the joints that are occupied: the space behind a length of conduit, the narrow dry shelf under the water main, the bolt holes in a steel plate waiting to be put to use. Some of the roaches are no bigger than grains of rice. Among them are little white ones that have just shed their skins. A house centipede stands with its legs spread across the corner of a junction box, waiting. In the water, below where the pipes drip, mosquito larvae hang head down. When a drop strikes they jerk away, and after a while each comes back to its own small hold on the surface.

The room is loud enough that none of this makes a sound you can pick out. There is the motor on the other side of the door and a great, low passage of water in one of the overhead pipes. They hold nearly the same note. Every so often the motor stops and the other sound goes on, and for a few seconds you can hear the different heights the drops fall from: the close patter under the supply line, the hollow strike of a drop hitting the lid of an empty drum.

At the rock end the floor stops short. The excavation has left a bench of pale stone with half a drill hole still running down it. A little water comes out of a seam there, warm enough that you can scarcely feel it on your hand. It leaves a rough deposit no thicker than paper. A few green threads have grown in the wet beneath the lamp; elsewhere, even where the stone is soaked, there is no green. The only plant with leaves is a seedling in the grit by the door. It has two of them, held level in the draft.

On a clipboard upstairs this is still an unfinished section. There are capped connections for equipment that hasn’t arrived and a rectangle painted on the floor where the equipment will go. The paint goes straight through the shallows. By the time someone comes back to set the machine down, the rectangle will have a rim of sediment on its lower side and the next generation of roaches will be living in the drum. Nothing here is waiting in quite the way the room is. Even the stone has begun to put a little of itself back.
