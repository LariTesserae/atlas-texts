Below the lowest freight landing, a door opens onto a landscape about the size of a dining table. There is room to stand beside it if you keep the door open. Otherwise you have to crouch on a little grated step, with your back against the warm side of the only warm pipe.

The ground is pale ceramic, laid in plates that fit together like pieces of a broken dish. They are not broken. Each was fired to that shape, and each rests on a screw that can raise or lower it by fractions. At the far end, where several screws have already risen, a ridge casts a shadow through the slits in its own unfinished flank. Elsewhere the plates lie nearly flat. You can still read their pencilled bench numbers. The lowest point is a thumbprint in the glaze.

Water arrives from above, though there is no sky. It condenses on a row of black cooling fins and hangs there for much of the day. The air is cold enough to hurt the roots of your teeth, but a thin current from the freight shaft keeps the drops from freezing. Sometimes a drop will reach almost to the ground before it lets go. Most fall into the same shallow runnel. The ceramic there is still as white as everywhere else; there hasn’t been time for water to leave even a stain.

The place is crowded in ways that are hard to see at first. Under every ledge there is a bracket, under every bracket a wire bent to clear a moving part. The gaps between the plates are deep enough to show red insulation and the bright shoulders of gears. There’s no stone behind the wall, either, only more wall: enamel, cork, a pocket of still air, another skin of steel. For all that, the little plain looks exposed. Three tufts of sedge, planted in plugs of grey felt, lean toward the light under the door. A finer green has begun on one of the wet screw heads. Nothing comes to eat it.

Mostly you hear the building farther up: a freight wheel crossing a joint, then the same joint answering under the next wheel. Down here there is a dry click so far apart from the next dry click that you can never quite be sure they belong to the same machine. If you sit through a whole shift, the ridge gets steeper. The runnel loses its slope. Water backs up, covers two bench numbers, and spills sideways into a crease that was dry when you came in. One of the sedges, without moving at all, finds itself on an island.

The odd thing is that the drains were installed first. There are six of them, each guarded by a polished brass grate, and so far the water has found none. You can see one halfway up the new ridge, tipped toward you, immaculate. Below it, in the thumbprint, a pool no wider than a coin is taking on the dim light. Every time the freight lift goes by, its surface trembles. The walls stand still enough that you would never know.
