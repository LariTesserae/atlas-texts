At the lowest point in the city, under a stair with only three steps, is a garden no wider than a potter’s wheel. You get to it by lifting the bottom step. There is a thumb hollow in the stone for this purpose, worn so smooth that your hand slips the first time.

The garden is deafening. With the step down there is only a little tremor in the soles of your feet, easy to mistake for traffic on the paving above. Lift it and you hear a sustained roar with a dry clatter at its edges. The sound comes from the roots of the plants. They grow into hairline cracks in a slab of warm limestone, and as they swell they set the whole slab ringing. One root gives way every so often. In the brief lull you can hear other slabs answering, under other houses, far enough off that you can make out the gaps between them.

Almost all the growing takes place against the walls. Thick fans of yellow-green leaves overlap there, each leaf clean and bright as the inside of a split fruit. The stems are flattened to fit the old mortar joints. They turn out into the light for the last inch or so and divide lavishly. From above, the garden looks crowded; put your head down level with the leaves and you see that there is nothing in the middle. A seed dropped there might have a long way to fall. Nobody has ever heard one land.

There is no water in this part of the city. The beds under the houses are seams of chalk and rock salt, laid down in the age when, according to the masons, stone was soft enough to knead. The plants take up some pale oil from the chalk. You can see it moving in the thinnest stems at midday. A broken leaf smells faintly of roasted nuts and leaves a white dust on the fingers. There are no flies. Once in a while a small blind animal comes up the mortar, eats a scallop out of a leaf, and backs down the way it came. Its footprints shine for a few hours in the dust.

The light comes down through a bare shaft that opens at roof level. Households have built around that shaft for generations. A bread oven bulges into it; the back of somebody’s cupboard forms one bright little ledge. Up near the opening is a row of clay pegs for a sunshade that nobody remembers using. At noon every intrusion casts its own sharp shadow on the opposite wall. All that brick holds the heat late into the night, when the leaves fold edgewise and the noise changes from a roar to something more like a cart going over cobbles without ever getting farther away.

The stair belongs to a passage between two streets. People sit there to patch baskets or knock the salt out of their shoes. Now and then a mason lies on the landing, lifts the step, and runs a finger along the outermost crack. If a root has reached the mark from the previous visit, the mason scratches a new mark farther out. Farther up the passage you can see where this has been going on for a very long time. The oldest marks are halfway up a wall that used to be the floor.
