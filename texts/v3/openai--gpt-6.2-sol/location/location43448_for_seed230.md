The town fits on a shelf of limestone no wider than a field. From below, its retaining wall is the first thing you see: nine courses of stone, the lowest set into a slant in the cliff. The houses scarcely show above it. You climb through a notch just broad enough for a person carrying two buckets, and at the top there is a paved square, unexpectedly level, with a well in one corner and a drain in the other.

There are streets enough to get turned around in, though there are only seventeen roofs. The streets double back to gain a little height, pass under stairs, and come out on the square again. Some doorways open straight onto the paving; others have a single deep step where the builders made use of a ledge in the rock. The houses are gathered near the uphill wall. In the lower half of town, the lanes still have names, but there is little along them except knee-high walls and a stone bench set where the sun reaches it before noon.

Water is seldom visible beyond the town. It seeps from a seam in the uphill rock and collects in a covered basin, cold enough to ache in the teeth. The well in the square is a cistern for roof water. You can tell which supply someone has drawn from by the bucket: the one at the basin has a short wooden handle; the one at the cistern hangs on a rope polished almost black. After rain, the paving dries from the middle out. For another day, sometimes two, the joints stay dark. At the outer wall a stone spout projects over the drop. Most of the time there is a bead on its lip that gets no bigger.

There is good daylight up here. It shows the pale shells in the limestone and the little fans of chisel marks under the eaves, where the rain has never reached. In the open square the light can be warm on the face, but the air stays cool. The walls are thick enough to sit in a doorway without being either in the house or out of it. Wool hangs from pegs in those recesses, airing without much movement.

What grows here grows low. A few tufts of grass have a purchase on the cliff side of the parapet, out of reach of the broom. In a disused yard there is a currant bush with three main stems and a patch of bare ground beneath it where nothing else takes. The lichen is more extensive than the vegetation. It crosses old mortar lines and stops at newer ones, though even the newer mortar is old enough to have lost the marks of the trowel.

A pair of choughs sometimes comes up from the valley. You hear them while they are still below the wall. Their calls rise through the drain opening and seem, for a moment, to come from under the square. There is a goat in one of the upper yards, without a bell. On a quiet afternoon the loudest thing in town may be its hoof against the board of a gate. Then it puts its weight down and the sound is over.

The place is kept much as it is. A split roof peg gets replaced with another of the same wood. If a paving stone rocks, someone lifts it and packs a little grit underneath. The drain doesn’t run directly downhill: it makes a slow turn around a knob of bedrock that would have taken a great deal of hammering to remove. There is a worn hollow on the inside of that turn, shallow enough to cover with a palm. It catches grit before the water reaches the spout. Someone scoops it clean with a wooden spoon kept on a ledge nearby.

From the bench in the lower lane you can see a considerable distance, but not down to the foot of the cliff. The valley begins partway out, with sloping pasture and the far side of a road. Weather passes over it in patches. Here, the bench is dry, the wall behind it is damp, and the empty lane is broad enough for two people to pass without turning their shoulders.
