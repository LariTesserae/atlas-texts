At the bottom of the tram steps there is a square of city that never dries. It is scarcely wider than the shed that roofs it: two loading bays, a pump house, and a strip of water with a yellow rail along one side. The far bank is a tiled wall. You can see the soles of people’s shoes through the grating overhead as they cross from the tram stop to the market.

The city built this pocket below street level because the water was already there. The pumps keep it a thumb’s width below the lowest step. When a tram goes over, the surface shivers and a bright line climbs the tiles, then slips back to the same grout joint. Nothing here has had time to round off. The concrete still shows the grain of the shuttering boards, and the corners of the steps are sharp enough to skin an ankle. Yet everything below knee height looks old in the dimness, darkened by a wetness that never quite becomes a drip.

It is hotter under the shed than in the street. Heat comes off the motor housings and the black pipes going into the wall. A fan takes air from one end of the shed and delivers it, still hot, to the other. There is a pale rectangle under a missing roof panel, but most of the light comes up off the water, where it catches the undersides of pipes and the folded edges of the metal stairs. A little fern grows at the mouth of a drain. It has four fronds, all angled out of the falling water.

The place sounds busier than it looks. The pump motors run at slightly different pitches, so their combined note swells and thins without either motor changing speed. A loose corner of the grating answers every tram. When someone stops at the rail to speak, you can see them decide how loud they’ll have to be.

There are small fish under the lowest step. You don’t see how many until you stand still and they come out over the pale concrete, each with a brief silver seam down its side. Mud wasps water at the fern drain. Pigeons come down one at a time from a beam and walk the rail, looking for somewhere to drink without getting their feet wet. The loading bays stay empty. A hose hangs in a neat coil between them, with its brass end resting just clear of the floor.

The pump attendant has a chair on the dry side of the yellow rail. From there she can see the gauge, the lowest step, and the little run of water from the drain. At the end of her shift she writes the level in a ruled book. The numbers are the same all the way down the page. She checks the gauge before she writes them.
