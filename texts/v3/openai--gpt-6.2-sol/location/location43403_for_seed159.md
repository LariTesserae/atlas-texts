Below the plain there is another plain, warm enough that a hand laid flat on the ground feels no change of temperature. It stretches for miles under a roof of pale stone. At the far end, where the floor and roof ought to meet, there is only a bright haze and the black tips of ridges that have come up since anyone last walked there.

Daylight comes down through crooked shafts. The stone inside them is white and a little translucent, and by the time the light reaches the floor it has lost all direction. You can see the color of your hands. A pebble casts a short shadow. It is easy, after a while, to forget that there is a surface overhead with weather of its own. Then one shaft goes gray, and a few minutes later water spatters onto the warm stone beneath it. The shower may wet an acre and leave the next acre dusty. Elsewhere, drops gather on the roof and fall so seldom that an animal can wait half the day with its mouth open and catch one.

There is nothing green. The yellows that climb the banks are sulfur, and the dark fringes along the pools are the legs of little animals standing shoulder to shoulder. Between the wet places the ground is mostly fresh stone. It has not had time to go round at the edges. A ridge will rise in the course of an afternoon, opening along its crest to show a clean blue interior; by evening the two sides may have settled back together with a row of shed shells pinched between them. There are broad pans of clay smooth enough to reflect the roof, though none stays smooth for long. Sometimes the first mark on a pan is a footprint. Sometimes it is a crack that starts under the footprint and runs on for a quarter of a mile.

The animals are too numerous to take in as separate creatures. At a distance they give the floor a grain, the way wind does to water. Nearer, you can see the long white runners overtaking the blunt red ones, and the little hinged things that travel on the backs of both. There are swimmers in water no deeper than a fingernail, lying on their sides until something puts a foot in. The air is full of broad, slow wings, most of them worn ragged at the trailing edge. When a flock comes down, the animals on the ground go on walking under it. They know how to make room without stopping.

A newly opened seam brings them running. Some lick the warm seepage; others eat the ones that stop to lick. There are pale creatures that arrive late and browse on the empty skins, and after them come heavy black animals whose jaws can take a flake off the stone itself. They leave shallow bites with a gloss at the bottom. The gloss dulls as you watch. An hour later it can be hard to tell where they fed.

For all that movement, you can talk here, if you stand close. The plain has a steady noise like a room full of people sorting crockery. Near the shafts you hear dripping and the slap of wings. A great displacement of stone may announce itself only as a single hard tick underfoot. The animals stop at that sound. For a breath or two you can hear water running deep in a crack, and then the whole plain is in motion again, usually in a different direction.

There are a few shelters on the older shelves. They are built of slabs light enough for two people to lift, pegged at the corners with bone. The doorways have high sills, polished on the inside by bare heels and rough on the outside where animals have tried to get a purchase. In one shelter there are three clay cups and a hammerstone worn to fit a thumb. In another, the floor has tilted enough that a person sleeping there would roll against the wall. Someone has set a flat stone under the low end of the bed. The people who use these places make repairs in small increments.

They mark their paths with low stacks of white chips. A stack on its side means the ground beyond it has moved; a stack standing in a shallow dish means there may be water there when the next rain comes. Some days the only way to cross is to wait for the animals. The light ones will venture onto a new crust almost at once. If the heavy ones follow, there is usually time to get across. If the light ones stop and begin to climb onto one another, it is best to go back, even if the stone looks sound all the way to the far side.

Toward evening the shafts dim in succession, and you can follow the progress of dusk across the roof. The last one lights a reach of floor that has just lifted clear of a pool. Water still drains off it in threads. It is bare for perhaps a minute, a broad shelf of warm cream-colored stone, without a track or a stain. Then a dark animal no bigger than a thumb climbs out of the pool, walks halfway across, and stops. All along the wet edge, others are coming up behind it.
