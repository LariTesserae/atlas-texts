The high country is still high, though there is water above its highest pass. From the pass the ground falls away in long, bare shelves, each with a lip rounded by a current that has taken the same turn for centuries. In the deepest part of the valley there is white silt. You can see it only when something disturbs it. Then it rises in a pale fold and shows how far the bottom lies below you.

The cold is most severe where the water moves. Broad streams slide over the ridges and pour down their far sides, saltier and heavier than the water they pass through. Where two meet, there is a sound like a mill going in an empty house. Elsewhere the valley is quiet enough that the tap of a loose pebble carries from one ledge to the next. There are no mouths here, no gills, no shells under the silt. A new leaf opens entire and stays entire until the current wears a hole in it.

Growth is thick wherever stone gives it a hold. In the clefts stand groves of a black, soft-stemmed plant with leaves longer than a person. Their roots wrap exposed boulders in mats so deep that, when one comes loose, the whole boulder goes down wearing its garden. Between the groves lie acres of clean slate. At intervals the weight of water over the valley changes, and the oldest leaves give off a faint green light along their veins. It is enough to make out the next grove, sometimes the one beyond it. You can watch the light go out along a slope and know which way the cold water has gone.

There are houses, though never two in sight of each other. They sit on the crests with their doors facing uphill. The builders split the roofing slabs by hand and pegged the doorframes with wedges of bone-colored wood. Beneath one lintel hang three fired clay cups on a cord that has gone black but has not parted. Every time the valley fills with the colder water, the cups lift and knock softly together. Inside, the floor is swept clean by the flow. A low bench stays white where the water passing under it is too fast for roots.

At the far end of the valley, the old road enters an open saddle. Here the silt behaves differently. Grains loosen from the stones and fall upward, so slowly that at first they look fixed against the dark. Over the saddle they have made a low ceiling of dust, level from wall to wall. The plants reach it and turn, their leaves spreading flat against its underside, while the road goes on below them. Its paving has worn shallow in the middle. Along either edge, under all that moving water, the small stones set by hand are still standing.
