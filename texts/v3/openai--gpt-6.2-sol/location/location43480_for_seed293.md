The town is only a square and the streets that leave it. You can see all four corners from the middle, provided the rain has not just stopped. For a few minutes after rain, warm vapor comes off the paving so thickly that the far side of the square disappears. Then the air clears and there are the same three houses, the same empty tram shelter, the same heron on the roof of the water office.

Nothing in the square is made of stone. The paving is the upper surface of a single plant, folded into shallow plates with wet seams between them. The houses are plants too, though not the same kind. The water office has a thin, translucent bark through which people on the street can see the shadows of clerks opening drawers. The tram shelter is a leaf held flat by its own tension. At noon it turns enough to spill a little water down the back of anyone who has chosen to stand in its shade.

The land was worn flat long before the town grew here. There is an old survey mark in the square, a brass pin driven into what was then the top of a hill. The hill is gone, but the pin remains at its original height, about a handspan above the pavement. Rain hangs from its point. Children put a thumb beneath it on their way to school. On a clear day, looking out along any street, you can see the marsh go on level with the bottoms of the doors.

The town is spacious in the way a room is spacious after most of the furniture has been taken out. There is a house here, a bench far over there. Green fills every surface, but there is almost nothing at eye level. The gutters are thick with minute ferns. Grass grows sideways from the undersides of the sills. When a door opens, its dark interior smells of cut cucumber and warm laundry.

Underfoot, the water is moving fast. You can hear it even when the square looks dry: a full, uneven rush, with occasional knocks as though someone were moving a table in the floor below. The drains have no fixed direction. They take water wherever there is room for it, including places that are farther away on the return trip than on the outward one. This is the oldest piece of municipal engineering still in use. Nobody knows how to build another. The water office employs two people to listen to it and one to paint the depth marks on the thresholds.

At three in the afternoon the rush cuts out. In the sudden quiet there are frogs. A cloud of little bronze flies lifts from an apparently clean wall, and swallows cross the square so low that their bellies flash in the puddles. The heron comes down from the water office and stands beside a seam in the pavement. The seam widens. Water rises through it, and for half a minute there are fish in the street.

That is when the tram arrives. It comes in on a strip of dry paving that was not there a moment ago, with rain streaming off its windows from weather elsewhere. One person gets out carrying a paper bag under her shirt. She waits for the heron to finish swallowing before she steps across the gutter. By the time she has reached her door, the square has tipped just enough to shed its water, the tram has gone, and a dozen small crabs are working their way back toward the ferns.

From the doorway she could look back and see no sign that anything had happened, except that the brass pin, dry all morning, has a drop of water on it again.
