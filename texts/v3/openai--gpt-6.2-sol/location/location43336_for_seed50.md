There is a room full of water inside the highest tooth of the ridge. It is small enough that a person could brace a foot against either wall, though there is no place to stand and no air to put a head into. From the valley the tooth looks like dry white stone. Its only opening is a slit on the uphill side, ordinarily hidden by the lip of a snowbank.

The room is new. Its walls have the granular sparkle of a broken plate, and the corners are so sharp that the water has not yet learned to turn them without making a noise. What would be the floor in a dry room is a shelf of unjoined crystals. Every few minutes a transparent flake lifts off the shelf, spins in the current, and fastens somewhere else. Over the course of a morning a ledge grows a finger’s breadth into the room. By afternoon there is no ledge, but the opposite wall has acquired a little stair.

Daylight comes through the slit in roughly the amount you might get through a kitchen window. There is nothing blue or abyssal about it. At noon you can see the grain of a wooden peg driven into a seam, and the pale curls left by the knife that shaped it. There are three such pegs, each at a different height, and on one of them hangs a loop of plaited grass. No two are close enough to have held up the same thing. The grass has gone soft but has not come undone.

The noise is worse than a waterfall because it has nowhere to fall away to. A blow lands somewhere behind the northern wall, then the whole room answers at once. Grit jumps. The grass loop draws tight. Between blows there is a grinding roar, loud enough to feel in the roots of the teeth, although the middle of the room is almost perfectly clear. You could look through that clear space and think nothing was moving until you noticed the little stair had lost another step.

On the lee side of the lowest peg is a scrap of green no bigger than a thumbnail. Its leaves lie flat along the wood. A single colorless animal shelters beneath them, jointed like a louse, with two black points where eyes might be. When the room takes a blow, it lets go. It crosses the entire gulf in a straight line and catches the opposite wall. Whatever throws it over is gone by the time the loose grit begins to move.

Water never comes out of the slit. In a hard storm it swells through far enough to show a curved surface in the open air; snow strikes that surface and vanishes into it. Then the swelling draws back, and the snowbank settles over the opening again. A person crossing the ridge hears the wind, the dry squeak of snow underfoot, perhaps a buckle tapping against a strap. A hand laid flat on the white tooth feels nothing. Only if a fingernail slips into the seam beside it does the whole uproar come up the bone.
