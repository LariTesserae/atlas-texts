The plain is only a little higher than the sea that left it. You can walk for half a day without climbing more than the height of your knee, though you spend much of that time stepping down into one shallow runnel and up out of the next. The stone is gray limestone, worn to the shape of old knuckles. In the bottoms of the runnels lies a black grit that clings to wet skin. There is no soil, no root to catch a slipping foot. Rain comes in short spells, and what doesn't sink away gathers under the overhangs, where a pool the size of a palm may last a month.

You hear the place well before you can make out the ground. The nearer sound is dry and hard, like a rain of pebbles on a hollow slab. Behind it is a low, nearly continuous bellow. Neither is an echo of the other. The rattling comes from the little jointed animals that cover the limestone; the bellow comes from larger ones lying out of sight in the runnels. Now and then one of the big animals stops, and through the gap you can hear hundreds of small ones still calling from inside its folds.

On a clear night there is just enough light to distinguish the tops of the stones from their sides. Most of the paler shapes aren't stone. A ledge lifts, slowly enough that you could watch it for some time without being sure it had moved, and sets down again a little farther on. Under it, white legs hurry through a space no deeper than a finger. The animals don't scatter when exposed. They crowd toward the lowering edge, trying to get back beneath it before it touches down.

The plain is open for miles, but almost nothing on it has room to itself. The large animals lie flank to flank in the channels, with smaller animals wedged between them and smaller ones still traveling in the damp pleats of their skin. There are swimmers in the pools that go nowhere. They turn as a body whenever a drop strikes the surface, so that for an instant the whole pool seems to have a white underside. By the next dry spell the swimmers will be gone. Some will have been eaten; others will be folded shut in the grit, indistinguishable from it even in daylight.

Nothing green grows here. On certain nights the wind brings food from the distant water: loose shreds of flesh, eggs, and transparent little creatures that are still contracting when they hit stone. For a while the exposed backs of the ridges are as crowded as the hollows. Afterward, the plain lives on what it has caught and on itself. The white-legged animals graze the soft rims of the bellowing ones. The bellowing ones swallow whatever shelters in the runnel ahead of them. There are shells lodged deep in the limestone, and shells exactly like them being emptied tonight.

When rain starts, you feel it on your face before you hear it. Then the rattling goes out in patches. The animals need to open their joints to drink, and they cannot rattle with their joints open. The quiet advances along the shallow contours, revealing, for a few moments, how far the plain extends: there is always another band of sound beyond it, and another beyond that. Close by, water ticks into a hollow. A heavy body shifts in the dark to put its mouth beneath the drip.
