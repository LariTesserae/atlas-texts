The city is in the thickness of a leaf.

Not between its two faces, exactly, but in a blister at the base of a vein, where the leaf once took a crease and grew around it. From outside there is nothing to distinguish the place from a nick in the plant. Inside, a broad avenue runs from the crease to the opposite wall. It takes seven steps to walk. The cross streets are narrower than a hand, and the houses along them are shallow enough that you can stand in the street and touch their back walls. There are hundreds of houses. Most have nothing in them but the space left for a house.

There is no light. The distinction between the avenue and the houses is a matter of what the air does against your face and how long a scrape of your heel takes to come back. The paving is almost level. Once there were stairs down to the lower wards, but the treads have worn into a slope so slight you would not notice it until you put something round on the ground. It would roll a little way and stop. There are no gutters. Where a street has sunk, the hollow has the soft, even contour of a dish that has spent centuries under a polishing thumb.

Everything you touch is alive. This is not immediately apparent, since nothing is soft enough to bruise and nothing gives off the smell of sap. The paving is a felt of old leaf fibers, cold and close-grained. A house wall is the same material drawn up on edge. The lintels are living ribs that were bent across their openings, held down with wedges of harder rib, and left to take the bend. You can still feel a wedge in some of them, its blunt end rounded nearly flush with the wall. Whoever did the work had a great many wedges and no need of a saw.

The plant has no water to spare for flowing. What it carries from one part of itself to another travels as a fine, dry meal, pushed along by contractions of the veins. At certain corners a little of this meal comes up through the paving. Set your hand there and, given time, you will feel one grain, then another. There are feeder roots at those corners, finer than the fur on a seed. They do not reach out into the street. They lie with their tips at the level of the pavement and take what arrives. It costs them less to wait.

The noise is extraordinary. The whole ceiling is a sounding board, held under tension by the leaf beyond the crease. As those outer tissues shrink in the cold, the city takes their strain. A low note rises until the paving buzzes underfoot. It holds for a long while. Then some old joint gives a fraction, and a report goes down every street at once. The houses answer in different pitches, according to the depth of their empty rooms. At the bottom of the slope is a square where the noise will shake your teeth, though if you stand there through the whole performance you will never feel a breath of wind.

There are no callers in it, no wings batting at the roof. Nothing arrives to pick up the meal that misses a root. At the far end of the avenue you can find the oldest of the feeding places by the smooth mound beneath it. It is only two fingers high. The roots there have been taking less than their share for a very long time.

The city keeps its street plan even where there is scarcely any wall left between one property and the next. Along those worn boundaries, new fibers rise a little differently on either side. Run a fingertip over them and the grain changes direction. That is how a doorway survives after its lintel is gone: the two grains turn toward each other at the place where people used to enter. The plant has been growing that turn for longer than it took to wear the door away.
