Three flights below the street there is another street, eleven doors long. It widens halfway down into a little square with a drinking fountain that has been dry for years. Everything that would be stone aboveground is alive here. The fountain is a sawn root, its bowl kept open by the daily passage of tongues. The paving is a mat of plated leaves so old and so often walked on that their veins show through like the grain of worn stairs. At the edges, where nobody puts a foot, the leaves rise to the knee.

There is room to stand back and see the whole square. Nothing grows across its middle higher than a boot sole. The town’s gardeners once kept it that way with shears; now a herd of pale, six-legged grazers comes through every afternoon and does the work. For the rest of the day you might see only one animal at a time: a long-backed thing climbing a drain, a moth laid flat on a lintel. Then a motor stops and you hear what the motor was covering. Every door has something behind it. The walls are full of chewing. In the warm space beneath the paving, hundreds of small bodies turn over together, and the street lifts just enough to rock a forgotten cup.

At the far end stands the works. Two iron flywheels reach almost to the roof, revolving in opposite directions. Between them a belt of tough green rind carries empty spools up out of sight and brings them down wound with cream-colored fiber. Nobody remembers the last time a spool went upstairs. The night mechanic stacks them in the old ticket office, where beetles unwind the fiber to line their nests. By morning there are empty spools again. The belt slaps at its seam, the bearings knock, and something in the roof calls once at every knock. You can talk over the machinery if you stand close. The trouble is that people here have learned to leave gaps in their sentences for the call, and when the works shuts down they go on leaving them.

Rain arrives late. A shower at street level may take two days to find a seam in the canopy. Then, for ten minutes, water drips from a single spot above the square. The pavement has worn into shallow steps beneath it. Each step holds a little water long enough for a different set of animals to drink. By the time the last step wets, the first is dry. Elsewhere there is only a warm tackiness on the handrails and the faint smell of a green apple cut with a hot knife.

The lamps hang low, mostly to show where the steps begin. Beyond their amber circles the houses withdraw into dark, astonishingly straight walls. They were grown around forms, and here and there a door retains the impression of a carpenter’s thumb where he pressed a corner into place. The oldest door has worn smooth around the latch. On quiet mornings it grows a little farther into its own opening. Whoever comes home first shaves it back with the knife kept on the sill.

Just before closing time at the works, the grazers return by the western stair. They file across the square without looking up, clipping the paving with a sound like scissors through cloth. Behind them comes a smaller animal, never the same size twice, carrying a fresh leaf upright in its mouth. It walks the length of the dry fountain, chooses a place, and sets the leaf down. In the dimness you can see the stem take hold. By morning there will be a little lip on the bowl where there wasn’t one before. In a year, perhaps, it will hold water again.
