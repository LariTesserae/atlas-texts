At noon the plateau is so bright that the animals navigate by shadow. They wait beneath the curled rims of old leaves, looking out across miles of ground the color of bleached straw. When a gust lays the leaves flat, their shadows vanish, and the animals have to cross in full view. From a distance you see a few dozen black bodies spring into motion on an otherwise empty plain.

The plain is a plant. Its surface is a tough, translucent rind, scuffed white where the wind has worried it for centuries. Nothing resembling grass grows on it. Here and there a rib rises out of the rind and stands against the sky, bearing three or four leaves large enough to shade a hillside. You can walk half a day from one rib to the next. The roots are somewhere below the horizon. In the deepest ravines you can see layers of older growth, each following the outline of a plateau that stood a little higher than this one.

There is no water in those layers. The living material carries its sugars in a thin amber oil that runs well in the cold. A fresh split in the rind smells of orange peel and hot rubber, though the air on your face is cool. The oil exposed to daylight hardens almost at once. Even after a storm has torn open a mile of ground, there is nothing wet to step around.

The noise starts far below your feet. In a sheltered fold you can distinguish the strokes of the root pumps, several to a second, and a finer sound from the valves. Out on the open rind the two merge into a roar. Whole districts come under load as the daylight advances across the continent. A line of leaves turns edge-on; its neighbors flatten, flash, and begin to turn in their turn. At the latitude where the plateau is widest, these adjustments run for hours on either side of noon. The mechanism has no straight edges. In a cut bank, a pump looks like a knot in a very old tree.

The plant makes weather out of air and its own castoffs. On a clear morning you can see a storm begin as a little brown shiver at the base of a rib. Two miles downwind it picks up the brittle husks that have been accumulating in the lee of a ridge all season. By the time it gets to you it is high enough to blot out the sun, and the husks are striking one another so hard that some of them come down as powder. The front can cross a valley in seconds. Behind it the rind is bare and brilliant again, except for a few long scratches that the next season’s growth will fill.

Most of the animals live at the edges of things: in the pleats where a rib joins the ground, under a rind that has lifted a finger’s breadth, along the warm seams over the pumps. There are pale grazers with broad, grinding mouths, and little hunters that run upside down on the undersides of leaves. At dusk, when the machinery changes its beat, animals emerge from cracks you would not have thought deep enough to hold them. They can feel a footfall through the rind at a considerable distance. A grazing herd will stop all at once and stand with its feet spread, listening to a narrow strip of ground that looks exactly like every other strip.

From a high rim the country looks settled. The valleys have the broad shoulders of valleys that have been wearing down for a very long time. Far below, a newly broken leaf tumbles end over end through one of them. It is big enough to have animals living on it. Some run along its edge and jump; others disappear into the folds. By evening the leaf has lodged against the opposite slope, where the wind will pound it to fibers. The valley will be a fraction deeper there. In the morning, from the rim, you won’t be able to tell where.
