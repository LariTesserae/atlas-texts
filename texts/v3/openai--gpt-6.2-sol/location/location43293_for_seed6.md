The garden is at the top of the tower, though it is also underwater. From its western window you can see the roofs of the lower towers far below, and, much farther down, the white lines where the city was bolted to its foundation. The surface is another six floors up. On a clear afternoon sunlight reaches the garden in sufficient quantity to read by, though the print on the maintenance cards looks as if it is moving slightly.

The whole place is the size of a little chapel. It has a nave of ribbed steel, two tiers of planting shelves, and a narrow stair at either end. The stairs were made for walking and are now mostly used by roots. Nothing here rests on earth. The beds are glazed trays full of fired ceramic crumbs; the gravel on the floor is chopped porcelain, each piece with one shiny side. Even the boulders that brace the foot of the stair are castings, hollow underneath, with lifting eyes you can put a finger through. The trays still have sharp lips. At the corners of the walls you can count the individual welds. The tower has been flooded long enough to grow a forest and not long enough to lose a single stamped serial number.

There is no open middle to the room. The shelves were planted densely to begin with, and everything that could take a root has taken one. Pale runners go into screw recesses and come out around the heads. Tall ribbon leaves lie against the ceiling, where the gentlest current combs them all in one direction. Lower down are thick leaves like the pads of a rubber stamp, green on top and almost brick-red underneath. In the space left for a person to pass, they overlap by an inch or two. Passing through means putting a hand out and finding somewhere to lay each leaf until you’ve gone by.

It is warmest at the lower rail. Behind the wall there a bank of old motors turns the city’s circulation pumps. You can put your palm on the steel and feel the work, but from a little way off there isn’t much to hear. No bubbling, no patter of little bodies in the foliage. There are no animals, not even the nearly invisible kind that would ordinarily be at work on a dead leaf. The leaf stays where it fell, held against a grate by the current. Over weeks the green goes out of it, then the brown, and finally there is only a clear shape with veins in it. Enough of these have gathered at the eastern stair to show the outline of each tread.

The garden has its own peculiar measure of height. A stem will grow upward through one planting shelf, bend sideways across the aisle, and continue *down* through the next shelf without a kink. It follows the numbers on the original assembly plates. To a plant, apparently, a tray marked 17 is above one marked 16, wherever the fitters happened to put them. There are gaps in the forest where two plates with consecutive numbers face each other across a pane of glass. The leaves crowd the glass on both sides. Some have flattened against it so completely that you can see the green cells with the naked eye.

Once every afternoon the heating circuit closes and the flow through the room slackens. It takes a while to see the change. First the unattached tips of the ceiling leaves begin to lift, one here and one there. Then the long ribbons drift apart and let through an ordinary square of sunlight. It falls on a patch of floor that the plants haven’t reached: pale blue enamel with a yellow rectangle painted on it and, inside the rectangle, the words **KEEP CLEAR**. For perhaps a minute the whole rectangle is visible. The pumps take up their load again, and the leaves come down over it, so slowly that you can’t see which one covers the last letter.
