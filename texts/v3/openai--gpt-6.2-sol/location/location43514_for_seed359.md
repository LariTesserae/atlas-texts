The grove is nine brick courses below the road. You enter by a ramp so shallow that you notice the descent mainly because the road comes level with your eyes, then with the top of your head. From the bottom you can see both end walls. You could cross the grove in forty paces if the trees grew straight.

Everything here is made. The floor is fitted from thumb-length bricks, the walls from longer ones, and the earth in which the trees stand is ground pottery mixed with chaff. The trees were started on spindles of fired clay. You can still see a little of the original spindle at the foot of each trunk, where the bark has drawn back from the maker’s signature. Most of the signatures are fingernail marks.

There has never been water in the grove. The leaves are thick and green on both sides, and if you break one a warm clear oil wells up along the break. It draws itself back inside if you hold the edges together. The trees take in the fine dust that settles on their leaves, working it through the flesh so slowly that a red brick ground to powder in spring will not show as a red vein until the following year. The keepers feed the grove by rubbing worn pots against a stone. There is a low bench for this work, and a hollow worn into the stone by the hands of people who made the rest of the place so recently that the ramp still has sharp corners.

It is warm enough to take off your shoes. Daylight comes in through square openings in the roof, and in the open patches it has the ordinary color of daylight on brick. Most of the grove is crowded shade. There are broad leaves at ankle height and again at the level of the mouth, and a growth of fine yellow stems that combs your sleeves. The paths are swept but narrow. In places the keeper has tied back a branch with a strip of cloth rather than cut it.

There are animals everywhere. Flat pale lizards lie between the bricks with their eyes just above the floor. A kind of moth with folded green wings stands along the leaf edges, one to a tooth, facing out. The little red animals that feed on the moths are hard to see until one moves; then six or seven of what you took for knots in the wood turn their heads. None of this makes much noise. A moth landing on a dry leaf is audible from the next path. From farther off there is the occasional rub of pottery on stone, and between strokes you can hear the cloth on a tied branch tightening as the branch grows.

The grove has a few rules a keeper can show you, though no one has found a satisfactory way to say them. At the west wall, for example, there are two niches. Put a clay cup in either niche and you can reach into the other and touch the back of your own hand. You cannot take the cup out of the other niche. Generations of keepers have tried, and the wall there is shiny with the grease of their knuckles. At midday the square of light nearest the ramp begins to fall on the underside of things. The lizards turn over where they lie and warm their bellies. A person crossing that square casts the shadow of the soles of their feet onto the roof.

These things happen at their own pace. For most of an afternoon nothing will seem to happen at all. Then a leaf you have been looking at for some time will finish passing through the brick that has grown around it. Or you will walk around a tree and see, on its far side, the strip of cloth you untied from it as a child, still with the knot in it. You can untie it again. This does not seem to do the tree any harm.

Along the back wall stands a row of empty clay pots, each with a wooden perch pushed into the rim. The keepers made them for birds. There are no birds in the grove yet, but the perches have begun to sag under a small, evenly distributed weight. Every few days a keeper comes down the ramp with a knife and shaves another sliver from the underside of each perch. This is so that when the birds arrive, their weight will not bend the wood any farther, and they will find it quite natural to stay.
