The plain is still being laid. You can tell by the thumbprints in the clay. Rain has filled them, but none has yet lost its little whorl of ridges.

There is no ground beneath the tiles. At the open seams you can look down through a finger’s width of darkness and see the underside of another stretch of plain, its wet paving receding toward a horizon of its own. Drop a chip through and, after a while, you hear it land ahead of you. The sound is so small that for the next mile you keep expecting to find the chip.

The builders work with wedges, cords, and paddles of fired clay. They are nowhere in sight. Here and there a cord runs taut above the surface, marking a straight line over a distance too great to see. Where it touches a puddle, the water heaps up on the lower side without spilling back. Huge divisions of country are made this way. A low bank no higher than a sleeping mat keeps two weathers apart: on one side drops strike the paving hard enough to rebound; on the other, they hang in the warm air at knee height. Everything beyond the bank looks slightly magnified.

It is hot enough that you soon stop distinguishing the warmth of the air from the warmth of your skin. The water offers no relief. It smells faintly of the inside of a new pot. Along the joints, clear beads swell and run together, making streams just deep enough to cover the nails of your toes. There is nothing green to follow them. White mineral fans spread where they end, delicate as frost. Sometimes a fresh tile is laid over one before it has finished growing, and the pattern continues on the upper face without a break.

For hours there may be nothing upright but a single clay peg. Then a change goes through the plain. You see it first in the reflections: miles of dull water turn bright, in patches, under a sky that has not brightened. The land buckles into enormous shallow folds. One travels toward you, carrying a rain squall along its crest. By the time it reaches your feet it is no taller than a doorstep. There is a soft knock as the tiles settle. Far behind it, basins that held water a moment ago are suddenly overhead, hanging in the dimness with their surfaces perfectly level.

Almost none of this makes much noise. Rain is loud only on the tile you stand on. The squall that crosses the next basin raises little white splashes in utter quiet. Once, in a long interval between changes, you hear the rasp of a paddle being drawn across clay. The sound comes from very close by. A few seconds later, a ridge rises beside your footprint, wet and neatly beveled. It goes on rising until it reaches your ankle, then stops.

At the newest edge, the tiles are still soft enough that you could spoil one with a heel. Beyond them is more heat, more suspended rain, and the dull shapes of other flats not yet joined to this one. They draw nearer without growing larger. A narrow gap closes; water tests it, crosses, and lies still.

By then the footprint beside the ridge has a little inlet of its own. You can see water moving into it in pulses, though the basin it comes from is scarcely wider than your hand. Out on the plain, the same pulse lifts a dark line from one horizon to the other.
