The upland is new enough that you can read the casting numbers on its cliffs. They run upside down beneath the overhangs, black stencils four feet high. At the foot of each cliff lies a little spill of white ceramic chips. This is all the scree the country has made so far.

From the ridge you can see the whole basin: six or seven miles of shallow terraces, a low town of engine houses, and the pale wall of the farther rise. The view is open, though there is hardly an empty yard in it. Stairs pass under stairs. A bank of roof vents fits between the legs of a viaduct; below it, racks of slender counterweights descend into a well. The animal paths are crowded in among the maintenance walks. In places there is just room for a hare to pass between a moving weight and the wall, which it does without hurrying.

There is no soil beneath the country. In the oldest inspection shaft you can look down past nine levels of bracing and see, at the bottom, the heads of the bolts that hold the first floor up. Nothing is visible beyond the bolts. The grasses grow in strips of brown felt clipped to the railings. Mostly they are short and stiff, with cream-colored seeds. The few shrubs have been given deep enamel tubs; on cold mornings you can put a hand to the sunward side of a tub and feel how little heat has reached it.

The light is very strong. Even in the shade you can see the tiny bubbles in a weld. Moisture comes and goes without much weather: a scatter of drops beneath a pipe, a wet seam across an otherwise dusty landing. There are saucers bolted under the joints to catch it. Finches bathe in them one at a time. When a saucer dries out, the birds go on visiting it for several days, peering in over the rolled lip.

All the machinery is running. It takes a while to notice this. The shafts turn in oil baths two floors below the walks, and the engine houses are lined with thick gray felt. You can hear a beetle working its way out of a folded tag on a handrail. Once or twice an afternoon, a relay closes somewhere across the basin, and every pigeon on the roofs lifts its head.

The basin has been getting wider, though no new sections have arrived. The change was first noticed at the footbridge by the warm exhaust grille. In spring it was forty-two paces long. By autumn it was fifty, with the same number of deck plates and the same chipped weld in the middle. The hares still cross it in seven bounds. People who take it every day have learned to stop counting. From the ridge the bridge looks as short as it ever did, and you can watch someone walk out onto it and spend the better part of a minute getting past the middle weld.

Every few winters the weight of the country is transferred from one set of columns to another. The lifting takes weeks. During that time some of the catch saucers stand a finger’s breadth below the drops they are meant to catch. A drop hangs there in the clear air, with the far terraces upside down in it. The finches know about this too. They perch on the rims of the empty saucers and drink by reaching up.
