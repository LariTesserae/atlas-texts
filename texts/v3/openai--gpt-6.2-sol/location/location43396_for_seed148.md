The whole town would fit inside a railway shed. It has six streets, a pumping station, and a square with a drain in the middle. The difference in height between the square and the lowest cellar is about the length of a hand. In a hard rain the inhabitants of the cellar come up through the drain and wait in the square. Most of the time there is no rain, just a cold dampness that gathers on the undersides of things. You can walk a street dry-shod and come home with wet shoulders.

Everything you can touch is a plant. The paving is end grain, still growing, in blocks whose seams open a little at night. The long fronts of the houses are pleated leaves stiffened with silica. They hang from a common stem, so that shutting a door at one end of a row makes the rain gutters twitch at the other. The pump has a black root for a piston and a leather-colored collar that replaces itself from the inside. There is a maintenance hatch beside it. Someone, or something, has been keeping the hatch clear of suckers.

For all that growth, the town is almost entirely empty space. Behind the fronts of the houses lie great single rooms. There are no floors above the ground floor, though the windows go up four stories. You can stand in any of those rooms and feel the cold descend from a height you cannot see. A draft passes from house to house through holes meant for pipes. Occasionally it carries the smell of a bruised pear.

The animals are mostly in the walls. A piece of bark no larger than your palm might shelter a hundred little gray bodies, each in its own slit, with just the tips of its feelers out. In the square, a colony of pale, soft-footed animals takes turns drinking from the one wet screw of the drain cover. They approach it by different streets and never form a crowd. There are hunters, too. One lies flat along the underside of a gutter while the gutter grows around it. When it drops, you hear the prey's feet go on running for a second after the rest of it has stopped.

Nothing here is old enough to be worn. The cobbles have sharp corners that roots are already beginning to round from beneath. At the edge of town there is a cutting into the earth, but the earth has not set: in the dark you can hear the grains arranging themselves. A seam of yellow grit works its way upward through the clay. By morning it will be a little ledge, and by the following night something will have laid its eggs on it. No one has yet found a stone loose enough to pick up.

The station works through the night. There is one click as the valve opens and a faint shiver in the paving as the piston comes down. The mains carry water to the far side of town, where a new street is being laid. Sometimes the pressure falls. The pump strokes faster, and the wet line on the wall beside it rises instead of sinking.

That wall is the first place you notice the difficulty with distance. A bead of water gathers on it at eye level. It starts down, reaches a joint in the leaves, and goes on falling in the cellar across the square. You can put your hand in its way at either place, but not both. The animals know the joints. A small hunter can set its forefeet against a wall and wait for something to come down a stair that has not been grown yet.

At the end of the newest street, the curbs turn inward to make a doorway. Beyond it is a plot as long as a bed, with two survey pegs and a drip of cold water. The pegs stand a pace apart when you look at them from the street. Between them, if you put your ear to the ground, you can hear the pump working very far away.

The town is growing into that interval. Already a fine root has crossed it. On the street side, in the dark, its tip is no thicker than a hair. Farther in, there are animals living under its bark.
