The frost line runs along the roof. Above it there are forty feet of shale and the stubble of a harvested field; below it there is room enough for a town. From the access stair you can see three white lamps at the far end, but not the wall they’re bolted to. Between the stair and the lamps the floor falls away in long green shelves.

The quarry is young. Where the cutters stopped, the stone still has a square lip and a row of pale half-moons, each the width of a thumb. The green is younger. It went in on rolls, with wire pins at the corners, to keep the shale flakes from coming down into the machinery. Now the pins have disappeared and the roots have followed the bedding planes back into the rock. In places a whole shelf is held together by roots. You can put your hand under the overhang and feel the sharp little scales of stone through the mat.

Almost everything you can stand on is alive. Moss takes the level ground; liverwort fills the seams and climbs the lower faces of the benches. Yet there is very little to interrupt the view. A green as low and even as felt runs a quarter mile to a standpipe, then another quarter mile to a single bed of ferns. The ferns grow beneath a strip light whose tube has been replaced often enough that no one remembers what it was put there to illuminate. Their fronds are short, dark, and hard to the touch. Beyond them the floor is bare of anything taller than a boot.

It seldom rains down here. Water condenses on the intake pipes in the mild months and runs along their undersides until it finds a joint. Every joint has a little garden directly below it. In winter those gardens freeze from the outside in. The deepest ones keep a spoonful of liquid under the leaves, and once, on a maintenance round, a man found a small white springtail moving on the surface of one. There are no fish, and nothing flies through the lamps. At the foot of the access stair a spider keeps a short untidy web behind the box that holds the spare ear defenders.

You need the defenders well before you get to the box. The fan house is at the bottom of the quarry, a brick shed scarcely larger than a garage. Its two exhaust fans draw air out of the working pits farther underground. Standing beside it, you can see a person laugh and hear no change in the sound at all. Across the quarry the roar reaches you a second time from the cut faces, so full and continuous that the distance gives no relief. The heater in the shed is too small to warm more than the chair beside it. Someone has put a sheet of plywood over the chair to keep the condensate off.

The fans run at one speed. The lamps go out one at a time and are replaced on a schedule. When a pipe starts to leak, a new green stripe takes most of a year to reach the floor. There is a measuring stake where the moss was last cut back from a drain, with dates pencilled on it in two different hands. Between the oldest date and the newest, the moss has risen about the thickness of the pencil. Farther up the slope, where nobody measures it, a drop of water on the tip of a liverwort holds the reflection of all three distant lamps. It hangs there through the roar.
