The city has a street plan for the whole valley, though in places there is no ground yet. At the eastern end of Foundry Street the pavement stops at a clean edge. Across the gap, fifty yards away, the street resumes with a bus shelter, three shuttered shops, and a steep lane going up behind them. The people who live there come and go by a freight lift. They’ve been promised the rest of the street, and on wet evenings they can sometimes hear tires passing on it.

It is a high valley, higher than the clouds most days, and open enough that from the powerhouse you can count the eight settlements that make up the city. Each has its own water tower, switchyard, and little assembly of brick apartment houses. Between them lie broad shelves of blue-black stone. The shelves look sawn rather than weathered. A boulder still attached to its parent rock by a thin web has hung over one of the tram cuttings for as long as the tram has been running. The track crew paints the year on it every spring. They have to go a little farther out on the stone each time to find a bare patch.

Nothing dries properly here, though there’s seldom enough rain to fill a bucket. Water beads on the underside of the handrails and grows a green fringe along the uphill edges of the gutters. Under the district heating pipes, ferns spread their fronds across the maintenance walkways. There are lush little woods wherever a culvert gives out: shoulder-high angelica, rowans with dark leaves, grass so thick the foxes use the pipes to get over it. Five paces beyond one of these woods you can be standing on cold, bare stone again.

Most of the valley is dim. You can see the sun from the upper cable stations, but from the streets it is easier to tell where it is by looking at the starlings. A flock coming down to roost will turn all silver at once, well after the windows have gone dark. There are thousands of them. Hares feed in the ballast between the tramlines; woolly goats come down the lift ramps in single file and lick the salt off the loading bays. In the cold workshops, moths settle on the indicator lights, so that a foreman making the rounds has to tap each glass with a fingernail to see whether a machine is running.

When the rolling mill takes a slab, the valley is loud enough to interrupt a conversation in a closed kitchen two districts away. The noise travels the length of the rock shelves and comes back through the floor. Then there might be twenty minutes of nothing much: claw tips on a zinc roof, a loose pane ticking in the bus shelter, a drip down a drain that isn’t connected to anything. The animals know the mill’s schedule better than visitors do. A fox asleep on a pipe will lift its head in the instant before the first blow.

The peculiar thing about the place is that distances finish forming at different times. Air is generally first. You can smell bread from a bakery that is still an hour’s walk away, through a pass that on the surveyor’s map has not opened. A view can take much longer. For eleven years the west-facing rooms of one apartment block looked onto an immense blank wall of stone. Then one morning, starting at the ground floor and working upward over the course of breakfast, the windows began to look out over the valley. The wall was still there when the caretaker went outside to check. He could put his hand on it.

Municipal work goes on around these difficulties. The crews leave extra cable in the trenches and fit the long pipes with sliding collars. They pour the footings of public buildings in pairs, sometimes years apart. Nobody minds borrowing an empty lot for a garden, but the older gardeners grow their currants in tubs. Near the upper terminus there is a fine municipal stair, complete with lamps and a handrail, which has been waiting since the city was founded for its last seven steps. Children sit on the top tread to eat their lunches. Their grandparents sat there too.

Below the stair, a long bank of moss runs out toward the edge of the valley. Walk along it in the dusk and you can hear water somewhere underneath. The stream has a name and a bridge, but it has never been seen. In dry years the sound is thin enough that you have to kneel to hear it. This year it is a little louder. The bridge keeper, who oils the hinges on the gates every Thursday, says there are stones turning over down there now.
