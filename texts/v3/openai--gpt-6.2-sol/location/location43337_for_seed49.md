The city is so low that a flight of six steps has a neighborhood name. People say they live Above the Seventh, though there is no seventh step now. The tread wore through a century ago, and at high water the warm drain that runs beneath the stair laps over the sixth.

From there you can see a long way, provided you look along one of the streets. There are no hills; there is scarcely any unoccupied ground. A strip of ordinary blue sky lies between the buildings. Far down the strip, someone is lowering a floor into place, and farther still the day is yellow with rain. The city is wide enough for that rain to take a week to cross it. By the time it arrives, it will have acquired a smell from each of the districts it passed through. People who work outdoors can sometimes tell which way a storm came by what they taste on their lips: lime, hot tin, the faint sweetness of the western shell yards.

Under the city lies a plain of worn black stone. Where the foundations expose it, you can see shallow hollows that once held the same pools for thousands of years. Some are still full. The new floods go roaring through the conduits above them and never touch them. The rim of one such pool serves as the bottom step of a public bath; in another, under a freight station, live pale jointed animals that come up to the surface when a lift goes by. They are after the crumbs the passengers drop. Their antennae break the water long before their bodies do.

Nothing roots in the seams. After rain, the green on the north walls is copper, bright enough in places to stain a sleeve. The soft gray material under the eaves is made by animals no bigger than a thumbnail. They plaster themselves in groups around warm pipes and put out little feathered arms to catch whatever the steam leaves behind. When a pipe goes cold they pack up their arms and walk off. An old wall is pocked with the cups they leave, and under a dripping cornice you can find generations of little cups fitted one inside another.

There is more life at street level than you would guess from the view above it. Mud-colored runners skim from curb to curb on broad hind feet. At the fish exchanges they have learned to follow the folding stalls, and when a stall moves they ride its runners to the new pitch, hanging upside down among the wheels. Glass-backed eels work their way into the shallow gutters after a shower. They can breathe there for several hours. Children carry them back to the deeper channels in the lids of lunch tins, though the bigger eels know when the street is about to drain and go of their own accord.

You feel the noise first in your teeth. There is water falling inside walls, freight going by underfoot, the crack of sheet metal heating in the sun. A market street at noon can be too loud for a dropped cup to make a sound you can pick out. People call to one another by flicking the lights over their doors. At the public counters there are brass plates worn to the shapes of hands; rest your palm on one and the clerk on the other side can speak into your bones. A clerk with a cold comes through particularly well.

Each street has a prescribed quantity of fall. Most of the time there is not enough to go around. A laundress can buy a little extra and tip her end of the block toward the outfall, though if she does it during market hours someone will come round about the eggs. Down in the municipal galleries, where the larger adjustments are made, there are watercourses that run uphill to the eye. A bubble released in one of them goes down with the water. So does a stone. The masons still test a new channel by tossing a stone into it; there is a tray at the far end to catch the old test stones, many of them polished smooth from being sent through again and again.

Around midafternoon, changes in the air pressure begin to shut the freight mouths. A platform that has been serving three buildings draws away from all of them, carrying its porters and their carts. The porters sit on the cart handles and eat while they wait to see where they will be let off. Water stands suddenly ankle-deep in a sunny street. A woman coming home with salt under her arm sees the first eels flicking silver over the paving and goes in by the bathhouse, which has a high door. Ten minutes later the sunny street is dry and crowded again, and the bathhouse has become an island. It happens every few days in that part of the city. The woman who keeps the bathhouse has a tray of clean towels ready for people who had no intention of taking a bath.

Above all the moving decks and flooded passages, the common stone has the rounded shapes of a coast. You can put a hand on a parapet and fit four fingers into hollows worn by other hands. There are names scratched into the stone so shallow that you can read them only when it rains and the cuts fill dark. The buildings change their bearings; the water is lent a new downward course; whole markets go by with frying pans still hot on the stalls. And at the sixth step, under the long blue strip of sky, the drain reaches the same old nick in the stone and spills a thin sheet of warm water over it.
