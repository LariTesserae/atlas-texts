The hollow is about the size of a chapel, though there is nowhere inside it from which you can see both ends. The floor bulges. The ceiling hangs low in wet folds. What look like pillars from the entrance turn out to be the sides of a single immense leaf, pleated to fit a space it has not finished making.

It is bitterly cold. A little water stands in the hollows, black until something moves in it. The rest travels under the skin of the floor. You can follow its progress by the lifting of the veins, one after another, toward a fringe of fine white roots. Where a root puts out a new tip, the water beads on it and freezes. The tip goes on growing inside the bead. In time the ice splits and the two halves remain attached like the husks of a seed.

There is no exposed stone. Even the grit at the threshold is alive: hard, angular grains with a green point at the center of each. They are the first material the hollow makes. Packed together, they knit into a rind; farther in, where the rind has thickened to the width of a hand, its inner face softens and puts out leaves. The place is young enough that there are still seams in the floor with nothing beneath them but more seams, finer and finer, all closing at the rate a fingernail grows. This is not a cave worn out of something older. The hollow and its walls are coming into being together.

The noise makes it hard to believe how little changes. Water strikes a taut leaf somewhere overhead with a report like a dropped board. In the thicker parts of the floor you can hear animals rasping at the pith. They are plentiful, though most are small enough to spend their whole lives between two layers of a leaf. Pale jointed backs crowd the margins of the pools. In the water, wide-bellied creatures hang upside down and comb the surface for spores. Now and then one lets go, and a dozen others rise into the space it leaves. Their mouths make a dry clicking sound that carries better through the plant than through the air. If you rest a hand on the wall, you can feel them feeding on the other side of the room.

The only steady light comes from a narrow strip along the lowest fold. It is barely enough to show the wet backs of the animals. At long intervals, a brighter patch appears deep in a closed bud. There is no opening through which it could have come, yet the patch has the ragged outline of sunlight falling through branches. There are branches far above the hollow, and the plant has a way of keeping the light that falls between them. It holds not just the brightness but the direction it was going. When the bud finally opens, a little rectangle of afternoon falls across the floor at an angle the ceiling should make impossible. The pool in its path stays frozen. The spores there turn toward it all the same.

At the back of the hollow, two thick lips of new growth have almost met. Water hangs between them in a shallow curve. Through it you can see pallid animals moving over fresh green tissue, already grazing on what will become the far wall of the next room. That room is no bigger than the space under a cupped hand. It has its own low, continuous roar. Put your ear close and you can hear the water falling a long way inside it.
