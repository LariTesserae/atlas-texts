The plain is made of clay rolls laid shoulder to shoulder. Each is about as thick as a person’s thigh. From a little way up—say, from the top of one of the low partition walls—you can make out where one crew stopped and another began. One crew laid its rolls in sweeping arcs; another kept to short straight runs and filled the gaps with heelprints. Those prints are the deepest marks in the plain. Water has not yet had time to make any of its own.

There is no soil under the clay. At the unfinished edge, where you might expect to find earth, you find a lower course of fired tiles, and below that a wickerwork of ceramic ribs. All the way across the basin there are things underfoot with room beneath them to move. The building materials arrive from the west in shallow jars: gray paste, pale grit, lumps of unfired red. The jars are broken up and used too. On a dry night you can follow a load by the crunch of its empty containers being trodden into the road behind it.

It is a low country. A mound the height of a seated person can divide one drainage district from the next. You could walk all night without climbing higher than the rim of a well, yet people on opposite sides of the basin cannot see one another’s lamps. Between them are thousands of low walls, baffles, racks of cooling pots, and stacked tubes with their mouths turned down to keep the damp out. Each piece leaves just enough room for a hand or a foot to pass. Once in a while these tight little passages open onto a broad apron where nothing has been set down yet. The dark there is difficult to judge. You can walk halfway across before you realize the click you’ve been hearing is the sound of your own heels coming back from the far side.

Most nights there is no rain. Toward morning, though, beads form beneath the tiles and hang there until something jars them loose. The builders put shallow cups under the longest seams. A full cup tips into a spoon-shaped trough, and the empty cup rocks back upright. One tipping cup is almost inaudible. A whole district going at once sounds like a roomful of people eating. At the eastern end of the basin, where the troughs empty into tall jars, the jars boom. A few have been packed around with wet clay to quiet them. The unpacked ones are useful: from several streets away you can hear whether the water has reached a part of the works you were told would stay dry.

There are animals, although you can spend a week here and see only the evidence of one. A white jointed thing lives between courses and comes out after a fall of water. It walks with its belly lifted clear of the damp and eats the soft rims of cups. An old cup can have its whole lip scalloped away. The larger animals are warm to the touch and almost never seen entire; now and then a lamp catches the blunt end of one disappearing down a sleeve of pipe. They eat the white things. When a larger animal settles beneath a floor, the little cups over it stay dry. People sometimes lay out a row of fresh cups to see where the warmth stops.

Much of the noise comes from the clay itself. The rolls shrink as they set, but their ends are pegged in place. After a cold spell a seam can go on cracking for half a mile, every break a little farther off than the last. Then workers go out with wedges and mallets and listen for which cracks ring hollow. They work without much light. A fresh break has a cool, grainy edge you can recognize with your thumb; if the edges have already softened, that seam is taking water and must be left open until it drains.

There is one peculiarity a new worker has to learn early. Lay two hollow tiles with their mouths facing each other, even a long way apart, and in the coldest part of the night they will slowly draw together. On a crowded floor they push everything between them out of line. So an unused tile is always turned mouth-down. The wide aprons are where the crews deliberately leave pairs facing. By morning a pair may have traveled several yards, plowing up two little ridges of clay as it goes. The ridges are scraped off and rolled into the next strip of plain.

At the far edge of the works, the new floor still gives a little under your weight. If you stand there long enough, the chill comes through your soles. Behind you the finished quarters clatter and knock and spill into one another. Ahead, a worker tests the next roll with the flat of her hand. It is too wet to set down. She turns it over on its board, and in the interval before the next jar booms you can hear it peel away.
