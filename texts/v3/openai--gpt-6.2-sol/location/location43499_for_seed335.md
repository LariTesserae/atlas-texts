The pocket is lower than the marsh. You can stand on its roof without knowing it is there, feeling only that the sedge under one boot gives a little more than the sedge under the other. From the creek, at low water, there is a way in on your elbows. Inside, there is room to sit up.

A slab of river ice opened it last winter. Turning on an ebb tide, the slab caught the bank and peeled away a section of clay without bringing down the mat of roots above it. The break is still sharp enough to show the year's growth in the peat: dark felt, pale runners, then the living green. In the clay below, little shells have been sliced clean through. Their white halves still fit the hollows around them. In another winter the whole lip may come down, but for now the ceiling holds.

There is almost nothing in the middle. A shallow sheet of water lies over hard clay, with one flat pebble projecting from it. The dimness gives that pebble an importance it could never have on the creek bed. Its dry top is the first place a drop hits when the ceiling begins to thaw. The drops darken it from one end to the other, then run down its sides. Even in summer, the walls are cold enough to ache against a bare shoulder. All the green is gathered at the mouth, where watercress crowds into the light and the sedges lean out over the creek. Their tips drip long after a shower has passed.

At first the noise seems wrong for a place this small. There is the close slap of water against the low roof, and underneath it a long, even throb. Down the estuary, beyond two bends and a mile of reeds, the pulp mill is running. On still days you can walk across the marsh and scarcely hear it. Down here the note comes through the bank as much as through the opening. With a palm flat on the clay you can feel it continue between slaps. Nothing made has found its way into the pocket, not so much as a length of fishing line, but the mill's working day is audible in it. When the machinery stops, the water sounds higher and nearer. You can pick out the drops hitting the pebble again.

Most of the animals keep to a handspan of bank just below the roots. Shrimp hang there with their feet busy. Snails graze the clay and leave narrow, clean tracks, which fill with silt and have to be grazed anew. At an incoming tide, sticklebacks enter one by one, each holding briefly in the same notch before darting under the overhang. The heron outside cannot get its head far enough in to follow. It waits where the pocket drains. In the last thin outflow, the fish have to cross a stretch of bare clay no deeper than the width of their bodies. The heron often takes one then, though it has stood still for most of an hour.

Rain makes the ceiling leak before it raises the creek. The tide works the other way: the floor goes dark first, as water presses up through seams too fine to see. For a few minutes there can be rain falling inside and water rising underneath, with the pebble still dry on top. Then a ripple covers it. The sticklebacks spread across the room, and in the last of the light you can see them nosing over the place where, a little earlier, your hand was resting.
