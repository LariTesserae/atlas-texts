The millpond is no bigger than a churchyard. From the road you can see all four corners, though you have to wait for the wind to flatten the rain before you can tell the far wall from its reflection. The pond sits only a little below the road. In wet weather the distinction seems to be maintained by the curbstones.

The mill itself is gone. There is a pump house with a slate roof, a ladder with its bottom rung under water, and a row of iron plates set edgewise into the bank. Nobody has mown here in years, but there is almost nothing to mow. A little salt grass grows where the road drains in. Liverwort covers the mortar beneath the ladder. The rest is stone, iron, and the gray, folded skins of clay that the pond leaves on any surface it touches.

In ordinary daylight the water is a pale brown. You can see a surprising amount through it: the heads of bolts, the round holes in a submerged beam, a white cup lying on its side. Nothing down there is quite still. A curl of silt slips out of the cup and is drawn back in. The shadow of the ladder breaks into bars, packs together, and opens. The deeper currents cross so close to the surface that the whole pond seems crowded, although there is scarcely anything floating on it.

The first time the pump comes on, you notice it in the ladder. A bead of water on the upper rung trembles and falls. Then a clear ridge travels from the pump house to the far wall, where it splits and runs back along both banks. Little pits open in the surface. They fill almost at once, but there are so many that the light wrinkles from end to end. All this happens with less noise than a person walking on the road. Inside the pump house, something gives a dry click. A few seconds later it clicks again.

Each course of stone in the far wall has been worn to a different depth. Some of the blocks have shallow bowls in them, smooth enough to hold the reflection of a passing cloud. Others have worn away beneath their upper edges, leaving lips that look thin enough to break in your hand. They have looked that way for a long time. In the oldest photograph in the pump house, the mill is still standing and the lips are already there.

A gray wagtail sometimes comes down to the iron plates. It takes two or three quick steps, waits for the water to recede, and picks something out of the exposed seam. On days when the pump runs steadily, it can walk nearly the whole length of the bank this way. Today the intervals keep changing. The bird makes it as far as the ladder, then goes back to the road.

After the rain there is a bright spell, with enough warmth to dry the tops of the curbstones. Below them, water still seeps from the bank. The surface settles, and for a moment the submerged beam looks solid enough to step on. Then the pond rises without a ripple. One by one the bowls in the wall fill from underneath, until the last of the reflected sky goes out.
