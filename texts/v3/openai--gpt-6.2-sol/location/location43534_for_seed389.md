At noon, the undersides of the bridges are wet.

The bridges cross a city built in the saddle between two mountains, so high that on a clear day you can see weather moving across the country below without knowing what kind of weather it is. The sun is ordinary here. It whitens the roofs, makes the road markings difficult to read, and picks out the windows of the western workshops one by one. The heat comes from the other direction. Stand over a drain in thin shoes and you can feel it through the soles.

There are no trees, not even dead ones. The things that look like trunks along the avenues are expansion joints. They come up through the paving at intervals, split into two or three arms, and disappear into the buildings. The city is packed close around them: workshops under apartment blocks, stairs in the gaps between boiler housings, little tiled rooms where the lift operators eat their lunches. From inside any of these rooms the city seems to go on forever. From the top of a stair you can see its whole extent, across three miles of saddle to the last row of sheds, where the paving ends in a low curb and the mountain falls away.

Beyond the curb is the new rock. It is black and faintly translucent at the edges. It has not yet had time to become rubble. In places the surveyors have painted arrows on the bare stone only to come back a year later and find that an arrow points into a crack wide enough to put a hand in. Nobody fills these cracks. The city can afford new roads more easily than it can afford to keep the mountain from opening.

It rains perhaps twice a month, always briefly. Most of the water people see has come out of the stone as vapor and collected on the cooler metal overhead. It beads on the bridges, runs down the bolts, and drops onto warm paving, where each spot looks for a little while like a hole. On particularly damp days the people who live under the freight viaduct put out shallow pans. By supper the pans hold enough water to wash a few dishes. By morning there is nothing in them but a line of pale grit.

There is a great deal of machinery, but most of it is holding something still. Down under the eastern street, a bank of hydraulic presses keeps a foundation level as the rock beneath it rises. A dial in the street shows how much work they have done. If you watch it for an afternoon it will not move. Once or twice in a winter a press changes over, and a short iron knock comes up through the floors. People who have lived here a long time sometimes hear it in their sleep and turn over without waking.

Small bone-colored lizards live in the warm seams behind the bathroom tiles. No one knows what they eat. They are so rarely seen out in the open that the same lizard, crossing two or three landings in an afternoon, can start a rumor of a hatch. And there is a bird that comes up from below, though not every year. It lands on the road and walks about with its wings held slightly away from its sides, as if it has mistaken the place for somewhere it can bear to stay.

The oldest peculiarity of the saddle is that a plumb line hangs a little differently on opposite sides of a new crack. The difference is small enough to miss unless you set two lines close together. Children are shown this in school, with brass weights and a pane of glass laid over a fissure in the yard. Later, when they go to work, they learn how much it costs to build a door that will still close in forty years.

Toward evening the roofs cool before the streets do. This is when the bridges drip most steadily. From the high stairs you can hear the drops striking one particular sheet of tin far below, though there are blocks of buildings between you and it. Across the saddle, on a piece of rock that did not exist when the city was founded, a crew has laid out the footings for a new district. The stakes are close together. The ground between them is bare, and from this distance you could take the white survey string for water standing in long, shallow rectangles.
