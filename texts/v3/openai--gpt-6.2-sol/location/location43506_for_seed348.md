The town occupies a shallow bowl in the stone, small enough that from the pump-house steps you can see every street where it ends. There are six streets, none quite parallel, with wide lots between them where nothing has been built. The lots are bare shale. Water darkens it in patches without collecting into puddles, except beside the weighhouse, where a shallow pool fills the impression left by a machine that was taken away before anyone can remember.

It is cool enough to see the breath of some of the animals.

There are more animals than the town appears able to hold. From the steps you might count nine or ten: two broad-backed things under the loading canopy, a pale animal drinking at the weighhouse, several of the small jointed kind negotiating a curb. But go down and put your ear to a drain. There are throats below it at many depths. Each hollow railing is full of little warm bodies. The long brick wall of the turbine hall is an inch out from its foundation, and all along the inch, eyes open and shut. In a good ordinary afternoon light you can see that the apparent blackness under the parked freight cars is made of closely folded legs.

Nothing grows here. The green on the copper is copper. Some of the animals have frills that flatten against the masonry and could be mistaken for liverwort, but when a truck goes by, every frill turns to follow it. There is no soil even in the sheltered corners. What looks like dust there is the fine gray meal worn from the stone by centuries of bellies.

The turbine hall makes a prodigious noise. Outside, you can distinguish the beat of the machines from the iron grilles rattling in their beds; inside they make one sound that is difficult to locate, as if it came from the lower half of your own body. The animals contribute mostly at the high end. On the east street there is a nearly continuous note like a wet finger drawn around the lip of a glass, made by creatures so small that you can stand in the midst of it without seeing one. In the weighhouse, with the door shut, you can hear a drop fall from the ceiling. The intervals are long enough for you to wonder whether there will be another.

There will be. The roof is sound. The water comes out of the steel.

The machinery is old, but old in the way the bowl itself is old. It has found the shape in which it can go on. The places where a belt has polished its guard are bright, but never any brighter. Brick steps are worn into shallow saddles; the saddles do not deepen. A man who used to mind the scales put a pencil mark at the edge of the pool. It is still there, straddling wet and dry. The man has been gone a long while, though his stool is still warm. Something with a soft underside sleeps on it between shifts.

At first it seems that the town never changes because everything repeats. Stay a little longer and you can see that the animals do not repeat. They quarrel, lose interest, find unlikely places to sleep. A black one may spend half the afternoon trying to coax a smaller one out of a pipe and then give up and walk straight into the turbine hall, where it has never been. What does not change is the amount of room they leave. As they gather in one place, they thin elsewhere, without a call or any visible agreement. You can always cross the town without stepping over a body. There will be a narrow way through, perhaps not the one you expected.

Beyond the last street the shale rises by the height of a doorstep. Stand on that lip and the industrial noise drops away so abruptly that you can hear your coat move. The shallow roofs look quite ordinary from there, with their vents and mended flashing. Moisture beads on a disused signal lamp. On the unbuilt lot below, an animal has come up from a culvert into the daylight. It is bigger than the culvert ought to admit. It stands a while on the bare stone, letting the water run out of its ears, and then lies down with its chin on the curb. All the space around it is empty. Beneath the curb, something shifts over to make room.
