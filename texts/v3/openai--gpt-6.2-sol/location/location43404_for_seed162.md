The first rain of the afternoon dries before it reaches the ground. The second falls out of the grass.

High on the plateau, the grass grows blade-down from shelves of black stone. Water collects at the points and hangs there until the whole shelf gives a little shiver. Then the drops fall together. A herd will stop in the lee of a shelf to drink this way, lifting their long gray faces into the brief shower. Afterward they go on chewing, their backs dry.

It is warm at an elevation where breath ought to freeze. Heat comes through the soles of the feet, in patches. A stride can take you from rock as cool as a cellar wall onto stone that makes the tendons in your ankles draw tight. There is almost no soil. Trees grow with their roots exposed, gripping the sides of fissures with pink, muscular folds. Ferns crowd the warm cracks. Between them the stone has the blue sheen of a fresh break, and the ridges are so sharp that fallen leaves sometimes come to rest astride them, neatly divided down the midrib.

At first the plateau seems to consist of a few ridges rising into low cloud. Walk for a day and you find that the cloud is in a valley, and the ridges you took for its far side stand on an entirely different tableland. Between the two lies room for a weather system to spend its whole life. You can watch a storm gather over a pale stand of trees, darken until the trees disappear, and come apart again without ever feeling a breeze from it.

The country has industry but no builders. Under the northern escarpments, seams of stone slide past one another at regular intervals. White clay enters at one end and comes out a mile away as a brittle blue ribbon. In hollows farther south, whole beds of quartz pulse with a weak current. Moths gather there by the thousand, lying flat with their wings open. When the current cuts out, they lift almost at once, and for a moment what looked like a quartz bed is bare black rock. Nothing tends these processes. No one takes the ribbon away. It curls up at the foot of the cliffs and crumbles, and small ground birds bathe in the dust.

There are animals everywhere, though it takes a while to see how many. Clusters of amber eyes turn out to be the underside of a fern. What appeared to be a fern moves off on six legs. Under the overhangs, pale little grazers bite lichen from the stone and catch the crumbs in their forefeet. Herders and grazers alike keep off the broadest flats. There the ground revises itself too quickly. In the time it takes a bird to cross one, a low ridge can turn on its side, exposing a red face that has never been in the air. Its old shadow stays where it was.

That is often the easiest way to tell what has changed. The plateau is dim even at noon, but its shadows are precise. An empty reach of stone may hold the shadows of an entire wood, leaves turning in a wind that has not arrived. Elsewhere a tree puts out roots along the course of a crack that will open tomorrow. The oldest animals know these signs well enough to rest in places that look dangerously exposed. You can see them asleep on bare ground while, a little distance away, the shelter they have chosen comes into being.

Very little of this makes a noise. The clay comes out of the mountain without a scrape. You may see two escarpments close like jaws and hear only the papery click of a seedpod beside your foot. Once in a while, after a ridge has stood motionless for hours, there is a soft report. Every animal within sight raises its head. A few turn to look at the ridge, but most look somewhere else.

Toward evening, moisture beads on the upward faces of the leaves. There is enough to darken the stone, not enough to run. The long gray animals come out along the ledges, with their young walking on the inside. Some ledges will be gone by morning; some are already gone a short way behind the herd. The young put their feet where the adults put theirs. Far below, the last of a storm moves across the valley, and a million wet leaves return what little light is left. For a few breaths there is enough to see how much farther the plateau goes.
