The valley is underground, but it is wide enough to have a far shore. From the pump landing you can see three amber lamps over there, each with a short reflection in the water. Nothing above them is visible. A lamp burns on this side too, lighting a little staircase whose bottom tread is already submerged. Between the two shores lie acres of warm shallows and low banks of gray clay.

The clay has hardly begun to look like land. Its banks are rounded where they swelled out of the floor, not where water wore them down. Break one open and the inside is the same gloss as the outside. Here and there a sheet of pale mineral has grown across the mud, thin enough to buckle under a beetle. Water wells up through the cracks and spreads without making a channel. It can take years for a bank to rise clear of the shallows, and the first thing to happen when it does is that something moves in underneath it.

There is almost nothing green. Under the lamps, a little algae grows in the wet grain of the stair treads. A few sedges have taken hold where ditch spoil was left in a heap. The rest of that packed, uneven country is occupied by animals. Snails rasp the mineral sheets. Pale shrimp mass along the banks with their heads to the seep. In the softer mud, worms have made so many breathing holes that a footprint fills from underneath before the edges slump. Bats roost in the roof fissures, well above the lamplight; their droppings feed much of what lives below. On the clay the beetles walk around one another with the small, repeated inconveniences of pedestrians on a crowded pavement.

You cannot hear any of it. At the head of the valley, water falls out of a fracture and strikes a ledge, then falls again into a pool too deep for the lamps to show the bottom. The roar carries the whole length of the chamber. The pumps add a low beat that you feel in the landing rail before you can pick it out by ear. They are housed in two block sheds on concrete pads, with enough room between the sheds for a cart. A cable runs out along the shore on ceramic brackets. Farther on there is a gauge board, a rack for lifting the intake screens, and a red tool chest that has to be wiped dry before it can be opened without dripping into the drawers.

The air is warm enough that the rail never feels cold. A person coming down from the surface brings a brief coolness in their clothes, then the cloth grows damp and clings. Condensation gathers on the cable brackets and drops onto the clay at intervals. Even beside a lamp, the drops are easier to see falling than to see land: they disappear among all the other little disturbances, the shrimp turning, the snails nosing up through the film, the mud giving way a fraction under its new crust.

The pumps keep the stairs usable. Beyond their intake ditch the water is so slow that last winter’s survey stakes still have clean collars marking where the surface stood then. At the far end, a shoal is forming around a broken bracket someone threw out when the cable was rehung. It has taken six years for the clay to cover the iron except for one corner. That corner is crowded with snails tonight. By next year there may be enough ground there to set another stake.
