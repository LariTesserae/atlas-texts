At noon, the plain folds one leaf and unfolds another. The change in light takes forty minutes to reach the horizon.

There is no ground under the leaves. There are older leaves, pressed edge to edge, their veins aligned in load-bearing courses. Below those are the broad white roots that were laid down when the plain was built. They have the shallow arch of bridgework, though they cross nothing. Their job is to hold the continent a little way above its own underside, where the air is cooler and the spent light goes.

The country is low enough that a rise of six inches has a weather side. On the side facing the light, hard yellow rosettes grow flush with the surface. On the other, thin black stems comb loose grains out of the passing air. The grains are not sand. They are worn corners, shed by the paving as it opens and closes, and the stems fit them into their buds. Weeks later, new paving grows out with the old corners embedded in it. Underfoot you can find a thumb-worn edge in the middle of an otherwise unmarked tile.

Nothing here is thirsty. The long, transparent pipes in the verges carry dry breath under pressure. Where two pipes meet, a cuff of tissue contracts around the joint, and you can put your hand on it and feel the work going on. The factories are the largest of these junctions: miles of pale ribbing through which light passes in bands. They turn out hinges, fan blades, and whole toothed runners that creep away on their own roots. A runner may take a hundred years to reach the part of the plain it was grown to replace. It has to begin the journey while the old part is still good.

From a distance the factories look snow-covered. The whiteness is the exposed inner face of a leaf that has never had to close.

The bright air is cold. There is no dew before dawn, no damp held in the shade. When weather comes, it comes as a long front of shutters turning upright. The plain darkens in strips; white pollen rises in the lee of each strip and is caught by the next. A storm can occupy the whole visible country without making enough noise to interrupt a conversation. Occasionally there is a little tick, as of a cooling lamp. You may turn and discover that an avenue a mile wide has changed its angle.

Some avenues retain their former lengths. This is a nuisance rather than a marvel to the plants that maintain them. Along one such road, the setting-out marks run from one to eight and then from eight to one, while the road itself continues straight. A repair tendril crossing the ninth joint emerges, with undiminished haste, three joints behind itself. For generations the verge has dealt with this by growing the repairs in pairs. One tendril arrives where it is needed. The other is left to flower.

There are animals, though the easiest way to see one is to watch a place where nothing is moving. A small gray creature, flatter than a glove, lives between the closing plates. It can judge by the cold in its soles which plate is about to lift. It eats the soft growth that would otherwise clog the hinges. Sometimes, after a plate has dropped, its tail is visible in the seam for an hour or more. The plate rests lightly until the tail is withdrawn.

Near the end of the bright period, narrow alleys open between structures that appeared to share a wall. Their floors are unworn. Far down them, in light that ought by now to have gone, stand rows of young leaves with folds already fitted to the damage they will inherit. The oldest passageways are more irregular. They have been resurfaced so often that some steps preserve the shape of feet from a time when the factories made something that walked upright.

By evening the visible plain has lost almost no light. It has turned most of its reflective faces toward the distance, so that the horizon is brighter than the paving at your feet. In that glare, movements too slow to notice nearby become legible: a belt of dark growth shifting sideways across a province, an immense rib settling into a socket, a field of fans stopping one row at a time. The cool air barely stirs. Then a single leaf lifts at your side, and from underneath it comes a brief smell of cut wood.
