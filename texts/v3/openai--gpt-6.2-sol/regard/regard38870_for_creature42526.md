The power stayed through a dry week. On the fourth afternoon the water drew back far enough to uncover a little tongue of root below the lip. The water that had gone over it in a film now fell clear of it, drop by drop. Each drop took a little of the brown deposit with it. By evening the bit of wing had tipped over.

The crease went down to the lip several times. There was water below it, in a fold of the root, but the drop between was too far. Each time the crease came back, the youngest stem bent a little toward the bowl.

The power lifted the end of a dead sedge blade out of the mud and laid it across the drop. It took a while for the blade to wet. A bead stood on it without moving until another bead came down and touched it. Then the water followed the blade into the fold.

Toward morning the crease went over. It did not stop in the fold. It went on along the sedge until it reached a place where the blade had split, and there it divided. One arm lay in the water. The other went a little way up the dry half of the blade and stayed there until the light came.

By then the bowl was making its usual noise. The bit of wing had lodged crosswise against the sedge, and a beetle spent much of the morning trying to pass under it. The power could have moved the wing. It watched the beetle back out, turn, and come up on the other side.

Rain lifted the blade two nights later. What lay along it drew in before it floated free. When the water fell again there was a narrow reach of the crease on the underside of the lip, in a place that had always been bare. At the end of it a drop gathered, hung for a time, and ran back along the root.
