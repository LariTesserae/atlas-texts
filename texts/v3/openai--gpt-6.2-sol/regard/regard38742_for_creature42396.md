The first thing the power did was listen from the other end of the white road.

The road had not always gone nowhere. At its foot there was a level place with six holes in the stone where posts had stood. Something still lived below it. When the bronze bell rang, it came out and waited. The power waited with it once, until the boy who had gone too far was back at the gate. Then the thing went in again. It had not been waiting for the boy.

There was no need to ring the bell.

The power came up under the ridge. It could have made itself heard in every room at once, but it had heard what the town did with a sound that went everywhere. Instead it went to the shelf in the bench keeper’s house and considered the white piece in the bowl. The piece was warm on the side that faced the mountain. The old woman had turned it that way without thinking when she put it back.

In the spring, when the measuring rope was moved, the girl found a dark point coming through the white stone beside the well. It was no higher than a joint of her finger. She tried the scraper on it and took the edge off the scraper. Thereafter she worked around it.

That evening her grandmother struck the bench. The girl had already put her tool away. She took it out again, because it was the only hard thing she had with her, and tapped the dark point with the back of it.

Her grandmother knocked a second time.

The girl thought she had not been heard. She struck harder.

“Once is enough,” said her grandmother when she came in.

They went back the next morning with the measuring rod. A knock on the white stone went hardly anywhere. A knock on the dark point could be heard with a cheek against the old paving, though not with a cheek against the new well. The bench keeper fetched two masons and made them try it before she told them where to put their heads. One of them heard nothing. The other heard the girl take the scraper out of her basket.

By the time there were stalls on the square, the point had grown high enough to catch a trailing cord. The council had it marked with a loop of weed so nobody would try to dress it flush. The girl sold shoots from the stall nearest the well. She still went home by way of her grandmother’s house, though she no longer had to go there to find out whether the old woman was ready to eat.

The power did not keep the bench keeper alive. In her last winter she could find the right face of a roof rib by touch, but she could no longer hold the rod far enough from her body to get a good stroke. Her granddaughter brought a paving chip and set it by her hand. It gave a small, dry note. The girl heard it one evening with both arms full of unsold shoots. She put the basket down before she answered.

Years later, when the dark stone had come through in several places, the girl took a pebble and went down the white road. She had a child of her own by then, old enough to mind the stall and young enough to want her back before the light went. At the last of the paving she stopped and knocked. Her child answered from the square. She went a little farther, to where there was no more road to be seen above or below her, and knocked again.

Something ahead of her struck the stone twice. It left so much time between the blows that she had taken the first for a block settling. She held still. The two blows came again, with just that interval.

She tried to give it back exactly.

At the bottom of the valley, the thing came out a little farther than it had when the bell rang. The power, which had been there for some time, moved out of its way.
