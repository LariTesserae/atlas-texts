The grandmother sat down beside the girl. She put out her hand, and the girl laid a finger in it for each little shod as it came into view. At six she took her hand away and waited. The seventh had gone in headfirst. For a while there was only the blunt end of it, with the two hind feet planted wide apart. Then it came out backward, pulling a scrap of old scale.

That was when the roof of the oldest shelter stopped singing.

The grandmother turned. There was nothing to see from the bottom step but the lip of the floor and, below it, the sling in which the boy slept. One end of the sling hung a little lower than the other. The boy had rolled into the low end, as he generally did. He was asleep with one hand open.

Where the two ribs met over the door, there was a space that nobody in the house had ever had reason to put a hand into. The old shod had fitted there when it was small. Later, when it could no longer get its back in, it had lain across the foot of the lower rib, which did as well. All those years the rib had been growing. Now, with nothing across its foot, it was drawing inward, taking the floor with it. The thorn that held the near end of the sling had begun to turn in its hole.

What had been watching the valley put a finger in the space between the ribs.

It had no finger until then. It made one no thicker than it needed to be, with a nail that whitened under the load. The floor stopped moving. The roof did not begin to sing again.

The grandmother went up the stair on her hands and feet. At the door she found the boy’s older brother on his stomach, reaching through the floor. He had got hold of the sling but could not lift it past the edge without tipping the boy out. She lay down beside him and took the other end. Between them they brought the boy in. He woke when his heel caught on the sill and was angry to find that he had missed something.

By then the others had come to the door. One brought a thorn wedge. The grandmother took it and tried it against the joint, not driving it, only seeing where it would bear. The tip would have gone straight into the soft underside of the upper rib. She handed it back.

The girl had come partway up the stair. From there she could see what the others, crowded on the landing, could not: something pale in the joint curling tighter as the rib pressed on it. She went down again for one of the hard halves of her shoots.

It took three trips. The first piece split when they leaned on it. The second slipped out so fast that it struck the boy’s brother on the chin. The third was one side of the arch. It had grown a thick heel where the girl had tied it to its neighbor. The grandmother set that heel against the old shod’s worn place and had the girl hold it there while she cut a seat for the other end.

When the cut was deep enough, the pale thing in the joint eased away. The new brace took the weight with a small tick that the girl felt through both thumbs.

They slept higher up that night, all eleven of them in a shelter built for six. In the morning the brace was still in place. The grandmother marked its ends with the point of her adze, so that anyone looking could tell if it had moved. Then she went along the row of shelters, getting people to show her where their shod slept. In one house they showed her four places. In another they had never kept a shod, and she stayed there a long time looking at the way they had joined the floor.

The girl went down to her patch. The old shod was coming up from the pan alone. At the arch it stopped. The half that was left stood tipped against its tie, with room enough now for the shod to go straight past. Instead it made the old little turn, putting its head down where the top of the arch had been. The girl put her hand on its back as it went by. It climbed one more step and lay down in the light, with all six feet stretched out.
