The first time the power came down to the station, it came at the wrong hour. A fitter was eating an orange on the parapet. He had set a piece of peel on the brick beside him, white side up, and was waiting for the rain to move it. The rain went all around it. When he had finished the orange he picked up the peel and took it downstairs with him.

The power stayed for the changeover.

It could have stopped the pumps. It knew, without having to go and look, how far the water would rise in each of the four streets if it did. In the lowest building there was a bed with a cardboard box beneath it, and in the box were photographs that had already been wet once. The curled corners had been held flat for years under the weight of the photographs on top.

So it let the second motor start. It watched the leaves turn in the channel and the water come down the steps. Then it went up by the second tread, as everyone did. There was a little warmth behind its heel. It had not expected to feel that.

It came back the next day, and for some time did nothing that could have been distinguished from coming back.

In September the crew painted a white cross on the parapet. A crack had opened behind one of the planting beds, and somebody had pushed a folding rule into it and found that the brick had parted all the way through. The bed would have to be lifted out. The man who marked the cross held a taro leaf aside with his wrist while he worked. It emptied down his sleeve.

On the morning they took out the bed, they found, between its end wall and the parapet, an old brick gutter. It had been closed at the street end with half a brick set on edge. One of the fitters got a chisel behind it. The mortar came away in a single piece, with the strokes of the original trowel still showing on the back.

They ran a bucket of water down the gutter to see where it went. It went to the return channel. This saved them putting in a length of pipe, which was what they had come prepared to do. They rebuilt the bed a brick shorter and left the gutter open. At the top they made a little turn in it so that the street water could get past the steps.

There was one joint the power kept the mortar out of. It was no wider than a knife blade and led from the gutter into the hollow on the second tread. There was a slope to it, though either end looked low enough to be the bottom.

At the next changeover the hatches cleared as usual. At the far parapet the water drew back from the holes. A little of it went down the new gutter before the gate opened, and a little stood in the joint. Nothing came down the steps.

The power waited at the foot of them. After the motor had been running for some time, a bead appeared in the hollow. It grew until it held the reflection of the handrail, then went back the way it had come.

The fitter with the orange was the first of the crew to remark on the dry tread. He said it would be a good thing in winter. He had slipped there once carrying a vise, and had had to decide on the way down whether to let go of it. He had let go. There was still a nick in the brick at the foot of the steps.

The power did not fill the nick.

Some evenings now the second tread is wet and some evenings it isn't. Once, long after the changeover, the power came down and stood on the roof, and the dry hollow stayed dry. It went away without putting a foot in it. The next evening there was water there before it came.
