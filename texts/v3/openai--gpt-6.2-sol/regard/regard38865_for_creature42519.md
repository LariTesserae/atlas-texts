The first thing the power did was put a roof over the stool.

It was a small roof, no wider than a dish. The next time it rained, the child on the stool put a hand out to see whether it was raining everywhere else. By the time the hand under the sill let go, there was a dry patch on each of the child’s knees.

The power spent most of that season at the far gate. From there it could see what the people in the square could not: when the woman put her basket down to change hands, she sometimes rested. She sat on the road with her feet in the ditch. Once she took something out of the basket and ate it. Then she got up and tried the gate again.

At the gatepost a root as thick as her arm grew out of the ground, ran a little way along the road, and went back in. The power put two fingers beneath it. The root tightened. All down the short street the water stood still in the grooves.

The power took its fingers away.

After that it brought soil. It brought a little at a time, in the evening, and laid it against the gatepost on the side away from the nest. The town put out fine roots into it. Some mornings the soil had been pushed back onto the road. The power left it there until the rain had washed it away and then brought more. In time a shelf grew out from the foot of the post. It was a poor sort of shelf; it sloped and had a lip on only one side. The woman set her basket on it without bending down.

One day she left the basket there and went a little farther. She came back for it. The next day the shelf was longer.

Her sister noticed it first from inside the gate. It looked like a low windowsill, in a place where there had never been a window. She laid her palm on it. There was nothing underneath. Then her sister’s hand came down on top of hers, hard enough to hurt.

They stood so for a while. The noise was particularly bad that day. A length of bark had come loose under the canopy and was striking the wall of the square. The woman who had been outside said something twice. At last she took her hand away and lifted the cloth from the basket. There were two pears in it, both gone soft on the side they had been lying on. Her sister took one.

By dark there was room for a foot beside the gatepost. In the morning there was still room.

The two grooves in the short street went on carrying water to the sill. For a while nobody sat there. Then the girl who had clicked her loose tooth against the floor came with the stool. Her tooth had fallen out by then. She had wrapped it in a leaf and wanted to see if it would fit into the narrower groove. While she was trying it, something underneath took hold of her finger.

The power, which had been about to take down the little roof, went to find a second stool.
