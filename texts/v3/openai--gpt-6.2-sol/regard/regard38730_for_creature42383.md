The power had been listening for some time. It knew the sound the dog’s collar made when she stopped: one small click as the ring fell back against the buckle. The keeper could not hear that yet. What he could hear was that the steps were becoming harder for her.

There was also a woman on the stairs. She had whistled from habit, at the place where the towpath used to turn in under the bridge. She was carrying a string bag with a bottle in it. For the last few steps she had been trying to get the bag up over her elbow so that she could lift the dog without setting the bottle down.

The power could have brought them both to the top. But the woman had not set out for the bridge, and the dog, though she knew the man leaning over the parapet, was going home with the woman. This was a difficulty of a kind the power took seriously.

It let the next tread be a little deeper.

The dog got all four feet onto it. The woman set the bag down between her shoes and sat. There was room. For the first time the keeper heard the ring on the collar, and called the dog by her name.

She answered with the noise she had made in her sleep on the kitchen mat, a sort of bark with her mouth shut. He laughed. He had not known he remembered it.

“Is that the keeper?” the woman said.

“Yes,” he said, and then, because she might have meant somebody else, he told her his name.

She knew it. There was a little delay before she spoke again, in which he heard her rubbing the dog’s chest. She wanted to know whether the kitchen door still stuck at the bottom. It did, he said, in wet weather. She told him about a screw under the bottom hinge. You could back it out half a turn. She had meant to tell him when she left.

He had stood for nine years on the inside of that door at a quarter to six, at first with the dog and afterward on his own, though in the later years he generally found something to do there. There was a drawer that wanted sorting. The coat hooks were loose. He had thought the woman might have something to tell him, but he had imagined it would be something about the water.

“Will she come up?” he asked.

The woman did not answer for the dog. He heard her take her hand away. For a while there was only the water, and then the collar rang twice and the dog settled with a sigh against the woman’s skirt.

The keeper stayed where he was. After a minute he asked what was in the bottle.

“Milk,” she said. “Unless I’ve broken it.”

They talked until the lamps on the municipal stair came on. The woman had a longish walk ahead of her, by a way that was level once you got past the bridge. She picked up her bag. Before she went she asked him to call once more. He did, and heard the dog’s tail strike the tread several times, hard enough that he would have worried about it if she had been doing it against the kitchen settle.

On Friday there was a patch of bare stone in the moss below the bridge. It lay too low to be of any use to the children coming down from the stair. In the afternoon the keeper went out by the towpath door with a screwdriver and a cushion that had spent nine years on top of the coal box. He backed the screw out half a turn. Then he took the cushion down to the stone and sat on it to drink his tea.

From there, without kneeling, he could hear the stream. There was a fast part and a slow part. The slow part was nearer the house than he’d supposed. He sat a little longer to see if he could tell what lay between them.
