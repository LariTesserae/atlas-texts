The power first noticed the city by the amount of water that never reached the ground. It hung from the belts, collected in the hollow ends of axles, and went away as steam from the jackets of men coming indoors. There was enough of it, if it could be brought together, to fill one of the raw lots to the depth of a man's hand.

It did not bring the water together. The lot it had in mind was full of voles, and in the spring a woman was going to put a dance floor there.

For some time it did nothing.

It watched Ada's granddaughter set the snail on the wall. The snail had drawn itself so far into its shell that the girl blew on it to see if it was alive. Then she went on with the letter, holding her elbow against her coat. Behind her, the gate closed, and a little farther down the wall a snail she had moved on Tuesday lowered itself onto the iron track.

The power could have made the wall more agreeable to snails. It could have put a damp seam in the mortar and kept the blackbirds away. But the track was agreeable to them too, when it rained, and the blackbirds had young in a stack of drainpipes.

That evening it went under the gate and looked at the wheel. The girl had put her whole weight against the gate to keep it from closing while she lifted the snail. She had been able to do it today. As the ground set, the post would lean a little farther into the yard, and by next week the gate would be too heavy for her.

In the morning a fitter found a bright scrape on the lower hinge. He fetched a washer, and while he was fitting it he asked the yardman why the gate had to close by itself. Neither man could remember. They disconnected the weight and left it by the wall. It made a good seat for a child, though it took a long time to warm up.

On Sunday Ada's son brought the photograph with him when he came to sharpen her scissors. He set it on the counter next to the kettle.

“I knew about the bucket,” he said.

“You never said.”

“I thought you didn't know I knew.”

She waited while he tried the smaller pair on the corner of the envelope. The blades met cleanly now, right to the tips.

“Did you know about the truck?” she asked.

“No,” he said. “I thought you'd put it back in the bucket.”

They looked at the photograph again. There was a dark line near the bottom of the basin that might have been a shadow or a stripe in the enamel. Her son turned it over and read what she had written on the back. Then he asked her which yard it was, and she told him how to get there, though neither of them knew what was there now.

The power did not know what had become of the perch. It could have found out, but the two of them had put on their coats and gone to look for the yard, and there was no need to get there ahead of them. It stayed a little longer in the shop. The kettle had just stopped boiling, and on the underside of the zinc counter a drop of water was working its way around the head of a screw. Someone had left a saucer below it. The drop fell just outside, and after a while the woman from the shop next door came in for a button and moved the saucer an inch.
