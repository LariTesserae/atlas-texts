The figure on the far stair was a woman named Senn. She had stopped to change the pot from one hand to the other. It was a good pot, with a neck narrow enough that she could bring it home full, and a handle that had been mended in such a way that there was no comfortable side to it.

Senn lived six treads below the roof from which she could sometimes be seen. In the morning she drew water at a cup near home, if there was water to be had. In the evening she went to the farther cup. She had been making the second trip since the winter her son was born. He was now old enough to carry the pot himself, but he had gone with the sheep, and for much of the year she made it again.

She liked the outward walk. By then there was usually nothing left to do that could not wait until she got back. She did not like the walk home. The full pot knocked her knee on the steeper treads, and by the last rise she had generally thought of something she would have liked to do with the evening.

The power that had been attending to the plateau went with her for a number of days. It went also with the people who drew from the nearer cup. The baker came before light. A child came twice because the jug she could manage was small. An old man was in the habit of saying he wanted very little, though the bowl he brought was quite large. Giving Senn a place ahead of any of them would only have put somebody else on the stair.

There was water under the empty ground on the homeward side of the notch. It went a long way through the rock without coming out, and at last spread so thinly in a bed of rubble that even in a wet year there was nothing to see. Above it was a shelf people used for setting down loads. The shelf was low enough for a child to reach and far enough back from the stair that a sheep could pass a person kneeling there.

In the night the power opened a seam no wider than the white line in a cloven stone. It followed the water to make sure that none of the old cups filled more slowly. Then, where the seam met the air, it took away a little stone dust. That was all it did to the shelf. There was already a hollow.

The first person to find it was the old man. He had set down his bowl to tighten a sandal. He put two fingers into the hollow and went home with them wet, leaving the bowl where it was. By noon he had fetched three people to look. One said it would stop by evening. Another said it would stop when the frost came. Both came back with pots after supper.

It did slow in the frost, but it did not stop. By spring there was an argument about whether sheep ought to drink there. The curd seller fitted a broken roof stone under the lip so that what spilled from the hollow ran down to a place where the sheep could get at it without putting their feet in the cup. Her first stone tipped. The second stayed.

Senn still walked to the notch on some evenings. On the first warm day she went past it, without the pot, to see whether a patch of grass she remembered had come back after a fall of rock. It had not. Farther along, in a place she had never had reason to look, there was a little grass growing under the tilted edge of a slab. She sat there until the sun went off her shoes.

On the roof at home someone gathering wool saw her stop on the stair. There was nothing in her hands, but at that distance there was no telling that. The watcher took in the wool and left the door down against the cold. Senn knew how to lift it.
