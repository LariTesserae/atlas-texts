The power took its hand off the notice.

The threads went on feeling for it for a little while. One touched the enamel and bent back on itself. Then the notice began to tremble again, and the threads straightened in the wash.

It stayed on the stairs until the first crossing. There were eleven people, one of whom had stopped to do up a shoe. The ten set the pocket shivering, but it was the one running to catch up who shook a young thing loose. The fish took it halfway to the lamp. A second came down in the fish’s wake and caught on the underside of a stair tread.

The power had thought, when it saw the fish waiting, that it might put a lip on the landing to keep the young from falling. It had only been there a day.

It stayed through a Sunday, when the first crossing was late, and through a morning when the grates let in no light at all. In the machine shop it saw the long one draw a thread beneath the cart rail and come back with something too small to cast a shadow. It saw the snail interrupt its grazing to go all the way around the gauge. There was a place at the back where the iron had swelled. The snail crossed it with difficulty. It went there every fourth or fifth day and ate something that grew nowhere on the glass.

The knock of the works was not, as it had first supposed, the sound of a part coming to the end of its travel. It was the sound of the flywheel dropping a little in its bearing. There were old drops of metal in the oil, and newer ones, and in the bottom of the housing a shaving so thin it stood up when the oil went by. The power could have put the wheel true without stopping it. The knock would have ceased. It went back to the stairs and put an ear to the rail.

Without the knock, the gauge would still go back and forth. The notice would still flutter. But there were three brackets in the street behind the alterations shop that got no other motion. It had taken the power most of an afternoon to see the last threads open along the third bracket. They were the smallest it had found, except for the young, and one of them opened only on every other knock.

So it held the weight of the wheel in one hand. With the other it let the old bearing rise just far enough to meet it. There was a knock. At the first bracket the threads came out; at the second they came out a little later. The third did nothing. The power had got the sound right but not what came after it. It waited for the wheel to come round again.

For a time, if you listened at the rail, you could hear the works miss. The fish came out once in the middle of the night and went back in. In the alterations shop a grain of lime hung from a screw hole until morning. By the next Sunday the power could feel the small remainder of the knock in its wrist, after all there was to hear had gone. At the third bracket the last threads opened.

It did not leave its hand there forever. There was a spring in the works that had once returned a lever no longer in use. The power brought it down to the bearing and let it take a little of the weight, then a little more. It took three turns of the wheel to find out what the spring would do when warm. On the fourth turn it took its hand away.

On the stairs, before it went, it found the young thing under the tread. It had grown a fringe and found a living in a tremor the power could not at first account for. Then someone came down two steps to get out of the way of a cleaner with a wide broom. The tread gave under the person’s heel. It was a slight movement and occurred on only that stair.

The person went back up. The cleaner swept a ticket down as far as the water, saw where it had gone, and fished it out with the end of the broom. Below them the threads stayed out for a while. There was no telling when someone would stand there again.
