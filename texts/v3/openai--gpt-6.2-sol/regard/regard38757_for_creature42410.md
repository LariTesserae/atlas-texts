The power came at the sound of water and found the trays dry. It waited. When the water came, it waited again, until it had seen what the water left behind.

It did this for nine nights. On the tenth it caught itself waiting for the little click on the stair.

There was a good place for the shrimp farther down the wall. Two lengths of cast tread met there, their ends not quite square, and behind them was a space deep enough to hold water between cycles. The space had one fault: at the height of a fill, water went through it so fast that even a grown shrimp would have come out the other side.

On the sheltered face of a bolt above it hung a bead of water. The power drew the bead a little to one side. The next bead took the same course without help.

All winter the water laid down a thin white edge at the mouth of the space. Iona noticed it one night while looking for a washer. She put a finger in to see how far back the space went. The water inside was warmer than the water in the tray, though not by enough that she would have been willing to say so to anybody.

The big shrimp did not move there. Two of the young did, and for a long time that was all.

In March a fitter came down to replace the bottom tread. He had a new one with a solid foot and no need of a shim. Iona had known the job was coming. She had tried, while the kettle boiled, to work out a way of asking him to leave the stair loose that would not sound like asking him to leave the stair loose.

In the end she asked him to lift the old tread slowly.

He got it up an inch and held it there. The shrimp came out backward. There was no shim to keep hold of now. She stood for a moment on the white rectangle it had left, then crossed the enamel toward the wall. Both of them watched her go. When she reached the white edge she felt along it once with each of her long threads and went in.

“Can you hold it another minute?” Iona said.

The fitter changed hands. Three young came out from under the tread. The last was so small that Iona could not tell whether it had gone anywhere until it stopped going downhill.

Afterward the fitter carried the old tread up to the gallery. Iona made tea. He took his standing up, with one glove tucked under his arm, and asked her how she’d known they were there. She told him about the sorting plate. He listened long enough for her to tell him the part about the drops that went uphill.

The new tread made no sound underfoot. That night, from the fold-down seat, Iona heard the flat knock and the little click as plainly as ever. She went to the rail. At the foot of the stair there was nothing. Farther down the wall, two threads withdrew over the white edge.

The power had wondered whether the sound should be put right. It listened once more, from where the shrimp was. There was time, after the click, to let go of what she was eating and get all her legs inside before the cold came.

It left the sound alone.
