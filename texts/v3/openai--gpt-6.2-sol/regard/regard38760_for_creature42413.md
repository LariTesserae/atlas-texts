The power came to the saddle toward evening and sat on one of the stones that marked a street with no houses on it. From there it could hear the woman under the lean-to tapping a new tread into place. After each blow she put her palm flat on the stone. She was listening with her hand.

The power listened that way too. It could feel water moving under the courts, dividing at this seam and coming together at the next. Under the lowest landing, it was carrying away a little sand. The sand was the same pale color as the stone, and by the time it reached the sedges there was not enough of it in any one place to see.

The landing would hold through the winter. One morning in spring, with somebody standing on it, it would tip into the cistern.

There was a pebble in the seam that the power could have turned. The sand would have stopped going out there and begun going out somewhere else. It followed the other ways the water might take and found that some ended under houses. It left the pebble alone.

That night, after the bowls had been washed, the power let the sound of the sand come up through the landing. It was not a loud sound. It was rather like the sound of a person rubbing a thumb slowly over the unglazed bottom of a jar.

The sisters’ father heard it first. He had come down on his own again and was sitting on the last dry step. By the time he got back to the lean-to, the woman had put her hammer away.

“You’ll have to come down,” he told her. “I can’t do the noise.”

She brought the hammer. He was a little offended by this, until he saw her lay it down without striking anything and put her hand on the landing.

In the morning they lifted two slabs and put a reed down into the space beneath them. The reed went in almost to the woman’s shoulder. She got another reed and tried it a little farther uphill. That one stopped at the length of her thumb.

For a while there was talk of filling the hollow. People brought stones to the edge and stood looking at it. It was the father who asked where the water would go if they did. Nobody answered him at first. Several people had brought quite large stones.

In the end they took up the last of the paving and let the water have that corner. The new steps went down along the side, where the rock was sound. There was room there for only one person at a time. The woman made the treads shallower than the old ones, using a stick on which the father marked the height he could manage without turning his foot sideways. He made her wait while he tried it twice.

The power stayed until the first basket of silt came up the new steps. The girl carrying it met the old man coming down. There was nowhere to pass, so she set the basket on the upper tread and sat beside it. He told her she might as well go first. She said she had just put the basket down.

While he made his way past her, she leaned over and picked a small transparent thing out of the silt. It folded itself around the end of her finger. The old man stopped to look. Neither of them knew what it would become. He held the basket steady while she went down to put it back.
