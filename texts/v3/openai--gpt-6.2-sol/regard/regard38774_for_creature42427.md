The power came on a Thursday and stood for a while in the narrow place between the works and the pump house. A drop fell from the awning onto its wrist. It waited for the next one.

Under the sill, the white animals were drawing a torn root across a gap. They had been at it since the previous evening. Each time the rollers knocked, the root slipped a little in their feet. The power put a finger under it until they had got hold.

That was the first thing it did.

The second had to do with the pump. There was nothing wrong with the pump that day. The man who looked after it had written *running well* in the book, and it was. But the lifting eye on its casing had rusted almost through. When it came time to take the pump out, someone would have to bring a great deal of equipment down the path in a hurry. There was only one place wide enough to stand it, by the sunny gap, and under that place the animals had made something more than a foot deep. It had taken them longer than Ada had lived in the ward.

The power could have kept the pump running. It considered this for most of the afternoon. Then it went to the depot and put a blank sheet of paper between two pages of a woman’s appointment book. When she found it she remembered that she had been meaning to see whether the old hoist in the pump house was still usable.

She came down the following Tuesday. The hoist was sound. The lifting eye was not. She and the pump man stood in the doorway discussing the job until the rollers stopped and they found they had been shouting.

In the end the pump came out in November, on a day chosen for it. The grandson and two others from the works took the door off to give it room. There was a temporary pump on the footbridge. It made an irritating noise, but it was gone by Friday. The man who did the washing got his sheets out on all three days.

Afterward the pump man meant to fill the old bolt holes in the floor. One of them had water in it. He knelt to see where the water was coming from and saw, a little way down, three white feet feeling along the side of the hole. He fetched a washer from his bench and laid it over the opening so that nobody would catch a boot heel in it. Through the middle you could still see the water.

The power stayed until spring. It did not put a stop to the work under the paving. On warm nights the animals went on drawing the ward together, working outward from whatever had settled during the day. But in a hollow beside the upside-down brick, where there had never been anything for them to hold up, the power laid a piece of broken pot across two stones. The space beneath it stayed wet even in August.

Ada found the pot when she was cutting back the vines. She put her shears down and got a hand under one edge. It was an ugly bit of pot, with a streak of yellow glaze on it, and she thought she might turn it over. Then she saw that the roots beneath it hung loose. In among them were a dozen of the little animals. Their feet were folded in.

She took her hand away and finished the vines. That evening, when her grandson came up the path with his sleeves wet, there were two chairs outside her door. She had brought out the good one from the kitchen for him. It rocked a little on the bricks. He was about to move it when she put her foot on the low rung, and they sat like that until it was time to go in.
