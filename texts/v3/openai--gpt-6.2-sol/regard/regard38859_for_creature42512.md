The power came on a Friday, while Ada was tipping the last of the bowl into the fern. It stayed through the dimming of the lamps and the passage of the last tram. When the cobbler fell asleep, it went behind the west wall.

One of the pumps had begun to wear through a pipe. Each time it started, the pipe touched the edge of a bracket. There was a bright mark at the place of contact, not much wider than a hair. By Monday the mark would have a hole in it. The water would come out hot and steady, and the first place it would find its way into the square was through the wall at the head of the cobbler’s bed.

The power held the pipe clear of the bracket. Then it considered how to bring someone down to look at it.

At six the following morning, the pump started and the handle of the baker’s old bread knife, which Ada had left on the shelf above the bowl, knocked three times against the wall. The cobbler came out in his socks. He moved the knife. The next time the pump started, the shelf itself knocked.

He called the number on the cabinet. The man who answered said the pumps had always made some noise.

“Not this noise,” said the cobbler. He put his telephone down on the shelf, and they waited together.

The woman who came to mend the pipe had to move the cobbler’s bed to get at an inspection plate. He offered to do it himself, but she already had one end up. Behind it they found a shoe he had made for a child who had stood on a piece of glass and couldn’t bear a seam across the sole. He’d made two lefts before he got it right. He had always wondered where the first one went.

“Do you sleep here?” the woman asked.

“Yes,” he said, though he had had time to think of another answer.

She fetched two blocks from her van to stand the bed on. The floor, she showed him, was damp along the wall even where he couldn’t see it. Then she went around to the service door and fitted a new length of pipe. The power let go when she had tightened the last clip.

The new pipe did not touch the bracket. It did not quite touch the wall, either. When the pumps started that evening, there was no movement in the key in the bakery lock. The cobbler noticed that first. He went to bed wondering what else would be gone.

The tram still came through the feet of the bed. A little later came a sound he couldn’t place, although it was very near. In the morning he found a dark spot on the inspection plate. A drop from the cold pipe had found its way through the gap where the woman had moved the curtain rail.

He put the little shoe there to catch it. By Tuesday the sole had swelled and the shoe would no longer stand up straight. Ada turned it on its side, with the opening toward the wall. They left it that way. When the lamps went down, a lizard went in as far as the toe, turned with some difficulty, and came out again. The following night it knew to go in backward.
