The power came to the ward on a night when there was more water under the loading yard than the pump could lift. Pell had been out three times since supper. On the third round he took a slate from the fourth threshold, stood with it in his hand for a while, then put it back. The woman with the onions had pushed a towel against the bottom of her door. He could see one white end of it from the street.

There was a place where the water could go. Before the foundry was built, it had gone out under what was now the goods shed and into a hollow at the edge of the field. The hollow was still there. In summer the farmer left the hay in it uncut because the ground would not bear the weight of a loaded cart. The ward had grown across the old outlet so long ago that the roots on either side of it were thicker than the roots that had closed it.

The power could have lifted those roots whole. It could have lowered the yard a little, or stopped the rain over the six streets while letting it fall on the fields. It did none of those things. It went under the shed and held apart two roots that had grown across a piece of broken drain tile.

At first there was only enough room for a thread of water. A little grit came through, then the white shell of a dead snail. The shell caught at the mouth of the tile. The power waited. A second thread went around it, and the two made enough of a current to lift it out. By then the first water had reached the hollow. It lay among the old hay stalks, no deeper than rain ordinarily lay there.

The power stayed until morning. It had not expected the roots to be so strong.

On his first round Pell found the fourth threshold wet only in the middle. He put a finger to the towel end. It was dry. Then he heard running water under the shed, where he had never heard it before.

Bess brought him a heel of bread and held his cup while he got down on his knees. They could see the glint in the tile but not what held the roots apart. Pell fetched a bar. Bess told him not to use it yet: there were snails all down the side he meant to lever against. She went for her apron.

By noon there were seven people at the shed, including the farmer, who wanted to know how much water they meant to send him. Nobody knew. He cut a forked stick and set it in the hollow to mark how high the water had come. The woman with the onions asked whether opening the drain would take away her evening cup. Pell said it might. In the end they fitted a piece of slate into the tile, low enough to let the excess out but high enough to keep the gutters running on an ordinary day. It took three tries to get the height right. The first piece of slate was still in use at somebody’s doorstep, and had to be returned.

When the tile was braced, the power took its hands away. The roots came to rest against the brace. In time they would put pressure on it, and somebody would have to get down there again. Pell went to the clock mender for a bit of chalk and wrote *outlet* on the shed wall, with an arrow that anyone could follow, even when there was no sound of water.

That evening, at the last house, the wren went in under the sill with a piece of dry grass crosswise in its beak. It caught both ends and dropped it. The man’s sister, watching from the kitchen, thought it might try the open door instead. It picked up the grass by one end and went in the way it had meant to.
