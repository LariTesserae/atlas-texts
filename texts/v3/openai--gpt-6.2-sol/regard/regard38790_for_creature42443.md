The power came first to the upper road. From there it could see the woman’s house and the lower terminal and the whole of the ground between them. It went down by the way she went. At the steep part it found a little square of tin let into the bank, just wide enough to set a lunch bag on while you got your feet under you. From the road it had looked like a piece of rubbish.

It spent three evenings at the terminal. On the first, the woman put the broom down and went home. Two darkenings passed up the rock. Much later, when there was no longer enough light to see the shadow, the broom handle made a small sound against the sill.

On the second evening it rained. The three stayed under the machines, where it was dry.

On the third, the woman was late. She had stopped on the slope to put a stone back under the square of tin. By the time she got to the shed, the wet patch had drawn itself out from under the third motor and lay along the inside of the door. She set the broom down without taking off her coat. The patch began to narrow at once. She stood with her hands in her pockets until there was only a shine on the grain of the wood.

The power could have put the old curve back in the shadow. It went up to the white rock and looked at the marks. The one with the round end was nearly covered now. Under the lichen the paint was still red.

A little way below the marks, a root had followed a seam to the edge of the rock and stopped. There was nowhere for it to go except out into the air. The power loosened a grain of stone from the seam. Behind it was a pocket no bigger than the hollow of a spoon. By morning there was water in it.

There were other seams, and the power went along them, uphill as well as down. It did not join them all. In some places it left a dry reach with shade at either end. Where the ground fell away sharply it took more care. At the terminal it let a little water stand in the groove worn in the sill by the feet of the men who had waited for the cars. The groove had been there so long that the woman swept it without looking.

For a while nothing changed. The broom lay across the doorway every night. Then, one afternoon as she ate her lunch, the woman saw the water in the groove gather itself toward the outer end. It went over the edge. A moment later a place darkened on the stone below. The broom was on its nails.

She finished her lunch. The water did not come back, and she did not go out to look for it.

That evening the tally wheel went over one tooth. Something climbed the old shadow as far as the alder. Below it, where the root entered the rock, a little wetness showed and was gone. The wheel did not move for that one.

In the morning the woman found she had left the broom hanging up. She stood in the doorway with her coat still on. There were three wet places on the step. Two were at the outer edge, and the third was in the corner, in the one spot she could never get the broom into. She put her lunch on the bench and went to open the shutters.
