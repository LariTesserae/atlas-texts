There is a drain in the lowest thread of the bolt. It was put there so that a man at the service pane could empty the recess and see whether the washer had cracked. In the time it takes to empty, the service lamp will heat the pane enough to dry it.

On the morning the washer is due to be changed, a woman comes up in the first lift with a new washer in a felt case. She sets the case on the grating and takes the key for the drain out of her pocket.

What has been watching the pond could keep her from turning it. It could keep the old washer whole, too, though the crack now runs from the hole for the leaf almost to the outer edge. It does neither. It holds the late light at the pane for the length of a breath, there in the early morning, while the woman is looking in.

The light goes under the old washer. The fish backs out. For a moment the woman can see both its eyes and the smaller pairs behind it. Then the light goes white again.

She puts the key back in her pocket. It takes her two trips down to get what she needs.

By the time she returns, the fish has eaten a shrimp. The shrimp with the bad antenna is on the ceiling, where it has found a patch of the soft growth with no snails to pick around. The new washer is still in its case.

There is a way to take the load on a collar outside the bolt. The woman has used it before, but never with the recess full. She fits a length of clear hose to the drain and ties its open end to the railing above her head. When she opens the drain, the water climbs the hose and stops. A grain of silt comes up almost to the bend, hangs there, and goes down again.

She unthreads the cracked washer a little at a time. The leaf turns with it. From the pane she can see the stem wind tight, so she goes back and turns the washer the other way. In the end she leaves the old one where it is and seats the new one in the unoccupied thread beside it. This costs her most of the afternoon. She misses the second lift and has to call down for it to come back empty.

When the lift takes her weight, the leaf moves once in the old washer. It doesn’t strike the iron. The little snail puts out its head and goes on eating. In a week the current will have worn a new path between the two washers, and the leaf will strike again, not quite where it did before.

One egg has opened during the work. The parent is still fanning the others. The fish that came out is lying in a letter, with its tail bent up against the edge. When the valve knocks it straightens, rises about the breadth of its own eye, and comes down in the same place. There is room beside it for the silt to settle.
