The visitor stood on the dry patch long enough for the cream to go still again.

The youngest went up to see who it was. By then the visitor had moved to the shelf where the broken things were kept and was turning the three-legged stool over. She had a piece of wood in her coat, the right length for a leg and much too thick.

“Did you bring that for the stool?”

“I brought it in case.”

The youngest took her down to ask. The oldest said yes, she would like the stool mended, if the visitor could make it sit level on a floor that wasn’t. Then she put her sack over her shoulder and went out for the lambs.

The visitor watched her go. From the door you could see the first part of the climb, where the oldest walked upright with the sack brushing the rock above her head. Then came the place where she had to put a hand out. She got her knuckle into the cup and stood for a while with her cheek against the stone. When she came home with lambs in the sack she would have to do it one-handed, with the weight moving.

There were things the visitor could have done from where she sat. She could have made the old joint small again. She could have set the lambs down asleep beside their mothers. Instead, when the oldest came back, she asked her to show her the bad place on the stair.

“It’s a good place,” said the oldest. “It’s my hand that’s bad.”

She went up two steps to show how she had done it when she could still bend her thumb. Coming back down, she laid her palm flat a little to the left of the cup.

“If there was anything there,” she said, “I could use the whole hand.”

By morning there was a lip in the slate where she had laid her palm. It wasn’t much to look at. Water ran over it and fell from both ends. The oldest put her hand there once on the way up and twice on the way down, the second time just to see if she could take hold without looking. Then she sent the youngest up with an empty sack to try it. The girl came back using the cup, as she always had. From her height the new hold was too far over. The oldest was pleased about that, though she couldn’t have said why.

The stool took longer. The visitor cut the leg short on the first try and had to ask for a wedge. The third shepherd, who had said almost nothing since she came, took her out to the walls and showed her which wedges were doing work and which could be pulled. On the way back they stopped to turn a lamb. The shepherd caught it round the middle and the visitor held its wet feet clear of the edge. It was still small enough to be surprised at finding all four of them on the same ground.

That evening the visitor sat on the mended stool and listened to the bells. The oldest had pegged the curtain back. Once, from below the doorstep, there came a single knock, and both women turned their heads. It was a lamb bumping the step. It tried again, got its chin over, and stood there with its forelegs trembling until the oldest bent and lifted it in.

Later the visitor asked if she should let the curtain down.

“Not quite yet,” said the oldest.

So she left it pegged, and stayed until she was asked.
