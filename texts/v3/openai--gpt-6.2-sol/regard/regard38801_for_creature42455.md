The power had been watching the house for some time before it understood that the sweeping was work.

On the far side of the door, the paving went down into water. There was a broom there, and someone who used it. Whenever the door on the dry side was about to open, this one swept the water back. For as long as the door stood open it could keep a little pavement clear. When the door shut, the water came up again.

It used to sweep only as far as the first joint in the paving. That was as far as the girl could step when she first began to walk. Over the years it had cleared a longer and longer strip. There was no need to clear it that morning. The girl meant to go to the city, and the road to the city was dry. But the one with the broom knew she was going somewhere and had swept farther than ever.

The power could have drawn off the water. It looked to see where the water would go. There were houses below, with windows that opened inward. It could have raised the paving, but there were doors along the paving, and some were low already.

It waited until the one with the broom stopped to change hands. Then it let the last stone the broom had passed over keep its dryness.

Water came back over the other stones, and the one with the broom began again at once. It went the length of the strip and turned. On the last stone there was a dry print, the shape of the foot it had lifted a moment earlier. It put the foot down again. After a while it put the broom down too.

Under the bench, the weight in the jar settled.

The girl slipped out of the strap and undid the cloth. She had expected the lid to be tight, but it came away easily. There was water inside, not much. In it she could see the end of a broom laid across a pair of knees. She waited for the person to look up. They did not look up. They were rubbing the place on their palm where the broom handle had worn it.

Her aunt came in and asked if the jar would do.

“I think I’ll take the small one,” the girl said.

She put the lid back, then took it off again. She fetched one of the little stone wedges they used when a pot was too hot to cover, and set it under the rim. There was room now to get a finger in from either side.

By noon she had passed the place where she could hear the wire being drawn. At the other place she heard it stop. She had always stopped there too, and stood until it began again. This time she stood long enough to hear the basin dipper knock once against the stone. Then she went on.

Toward evening the rain reached the house. Her aunt went out to clear the drain and found the long leaves lying crosswise in the mouth of it. She called to her brother to bring the hook. Before he came, someone on the other side called, “Leave those a moment. I’ve just sat down.”

So she left them. There was room in the basin for a little more rain.
