The power came to the edge of the works and waited with the daughter. It had heard the roll come off the board. It did not know, until the mother lowered her hand, how long she meant by longer.

Under the two beds there was a warm animal. It had been there for six nights. On the seventh it would move, not far, but down into a sleeve where the floor was thicker. The power followed the sleeve to its end and found a broken rib. One piece had fallen across the passage. The animal could get past it going in. Coming out, it would catch the loose piece with its shoulder and bring the rest down.

There was time to do something about this. There was also time to see what the animal was going there for.

In a hollow past the break lay three young, each with its blunt head turned into the side of the next. The power had taken them for a single animal at first. Every so often the smallest worked its way out from under the other two and lay with one foot extended into the passage. Then it got cold and worked its way back in.

The power held up the rib.

Toward morning the animal left the beds. A cup beneath the mother began to fill. It was an old cup with a scalloped lip, and when it tipped, some of the water ran backward along its handle. The mother heard the drops hitting a bed peg. She reached down and felt the darkening wood.

“We’ll have to move again,” she said.

Her daughter was trying the roll with her palm. “After this one.”

The mother put her coat under the end of the bed where the drops were coming through. Then she lay down again. From there she could see her daughter’s feet on either side of the board, braced for the lift. When the clay came away it made almost no sound.

Under the floor the animal went in and out three times. The last young one caught its extended foot on the edge of a tile. The animal put it down, took it up from the other side, and went on. By then the daughter had laid the roll and was kneeling to peg its ends.

The power could have fitted the broken rib back together. But there were other ribs from the same firing, and the floor was not yet loaded over most of them. It looked a little farther and found two with cracks that would not be heard from above. Then it looked farther still.

At the change of crews, a worker came along the unfinished edge gathering wedges. She stooped for one and saw a piece of rib hanging loose above a sleeve. The break was dry, and a little pale grit fell out when she touched it.

“Hold off here,” she called.

The daughter pulled out the pegs she had just driven. She got the board under the roll again and called for another pair of hands. Her mother sat up to watch. The two women lifted without letting the middle sag, and set the roll down on the dry side of the beds.

By noon somebody had brought an old pot to sit on and the mother was showing a boy how to tell a bad rib from a good one by tapping it with the back of a wedge. He tapped too hard. She took the wedge and showed him again. On his third try he heard it. He went back to the first rib to see if he could hear it twice.

The power stayed until a prop was in place. When it took its weight off the broken rib, the prop settled a little and held. No one above heard anything. The boy was tapping too far away by then, and the mother had gone with him, carrying the pot by its rim.
