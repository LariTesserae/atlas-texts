The power came at noon and waited until one.

It had meant to ease the old animal out of the bowl. The stone was worn to its shape, and the first leg came free so slowly that it seemed to be caught. Then the power saw the feet underneath. It waited while the old one let the young out, and while the last of them, which had put two feet down on the same enamel chip, got itself off again.

Behind the weighhouse, the screw knocked. The old animal did not move, but along the rim of the bowl the young stopped feeding. They went back to it before the next knock. The power listened through several turns. There was a difference between the sound that came through the rock and the sound the screw made in the air. It was the first that stopped them.

That evening the man who greased the conveyor found a bright place on the casing where there ought not to have been one. At the next stoppage he took up the play in the bearing. The screw still turned, and the casing still held its warmth after a shift. There was no more knock.

For three days the power did nothing else. Without the knock, the little hunters at the mouths of the seams fed better. The young came out more steadily, instead of coming all at once in the quiet after a blow. On the third day there were fewer of them at the far crack.

There was a flake of stone near that crack, broad as a thumbnail and held at one end by dust. The power lifted the loose end just enough that the smallest animals could pass beneath it. The hunters went on waiting at the mouths of the seams. Some of the young still came that way. The ones under the flake came out with dust on their backs.

Late in the summer the old animal could no longer close one of its legs into the groove. A young one, not its own, stood in the space the leg had left. The power stayed through the afternoon. By dark the leg had drawn in a little, though not enough to shut the space. Two more had gone in beside the first.

The next day, when the light came down, there was a clear wing across the old animal’s back. One of the long pale ones put a foot on it and began to feed. Under the wing the old one drew its leg out. The young went past the foot a few at a time, taking the shaded side of the bowl. The last one stopped to eat something on the stone. There was room enough now for it to turn around, and it did.
