The power would come when the pumps were off. In the quiet, the drop striking the sedge leaf would make a sound. There would be time, before the next drop, to hear water going over the lip.

The pumps were off because the tray was to be filled. On the drawing it was the recess at the foot of the old door, and filling it would take less than half a barrow. The woman who had measured it had leaned over the rail and put the end of her tape down through the sedges. She knew there was water in it. She thought it was water that had collected there.

The power would not tell her what the snail was worth. It would let her hear the outlet.

She would go down two more steps and bend to see where the water went. There was a dark line beyond the lip, under the lowest tread, where it ran toward the drain. She wiped the line away with the side of her glove. While she was looking at it, it came back.

She had been going to fill the tray to keep water off the stair. Now she saw that the tray was what kept water off the stair. She went to find the man with the barrow. He had already mixed the concrete, and was annoyed until they found a broken corner of the floor by the loading doors where they could use it.

The power might stay a little longer. In the oldest patch of eggs one of the dark curls had begun to turn. It turned for some time without getting anywhere. At last a snail came out and took hold of the wall. There was nothing remarkable about the place it had taken hold of, except that there was food there, and it began to eat.

When the pumps started again, the tremor returned to the reflection of the tread. The woman put her glove on the warm rail to dry. By the end of the shift it was dry on the palm and still damp along the seams. From where she stood to pick it up she could see the tip of the leaf, but not the place where the drop struck it.
