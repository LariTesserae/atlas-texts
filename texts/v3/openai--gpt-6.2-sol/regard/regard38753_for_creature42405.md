What drew the power’s attention was the spoon.

It fell a long way for a spoon. It struck four steps, missed three, and went under a door. By the time the woman inside had put down her sewing and come out, the child had sat herself on the stair and was waiting. The woman returned the spoon without asking whose child she was. She had been given a spoon herself, though in those days they had been made of bone.

The power stayed to see what else the town had learned to drop.

For most of the winter it did nothing. It watched Sela put a staple into a bowl so close to an earlier staple that the two touched when the bowl was full. It watched Iven’s younger grandson bring him clear water because he had noticed that nobody ever did. Iven set it on the table and waited for it to settle all the same. After a while he drank it.

Under the buried roof there was a room that had been filling since before Sela was born. Water came into it through the old shutter, which had swollen in its frame, and went out through the wall a little at a time. The weight of it was now borne by a single upright. On the other side of that upright was the cellar with the flour bins.

The girl who counted the fish had begun to come down after supper. The missing one was behind the wall. It had found a place where the mortar was soft and had worked enough of it loose to get its head through. The girl could hear it there when she put her ear to the stone. She thought it was eating.

There were several things the power might have done. It could have held up the roof. But the room would still have been full, and another winter would have put more water in it, and by then there might be a bed in the cellar. People put beds wherever they found a little warmth. It could have shown them the upright in the pool, but they would have had to go under the roof to see what they were being shown.

Toward morning, it drew the old bone out from under the slate.

The slate lifted no more than the thickness of a fingernail. Air went in. In Iven’s kitchen, a thread of water came out of a hole where a peg had once held a shelf. His daughter put a bowl under it. When she lifted the bowl to empty it, she found a crescent of yellow paint at the bottom.

Iven rubbed it between his fingers. He asked her to fetch Sela’s mother.

“Was your shutter yellow?” he asked.

“On the inside,” she said. “We ran out.”

She went up to look into the pool. Her sister came with her. They sat on the bench a while, each with her heels in the places other heels had made.

“You could open it from the roof,” her sister said at last.

“Not now.”

“No. I mean we could have.”

They sent for the girl and told her to bring the flour up. Then Iven’s daughter carried his chair to the landing, where he could see the people on the east stair. There was some argument over whether he ought to be out in the cold. He settled it by asking for a blanket.

They worked from the lane below. The man from under the cloud line brought out his last length of wood. He wouldn’t let them cut it until they had measured the opening twice, and when they had cut it he put the offcut in his pocket. By dusk they had a brace in place and a hole no wider than a copper staple. A little water came out. Then a great deal did. They took turns clearing the ice where it ran across the stairs.

The fish came out the next afternoon, after they had widened the hole. It lay on the wet flags with one fin folded under it. The girl brought down the cracked cup, which was much too small. Sela took off her apron and soaked it in the gutter. Between them they got the fish back to the cellar. The other six drew away from it, then came back so close that the girl could no longer tell which one it was.

In spring they opened the buried room from above. There was nothing much in it. A pair of heel marks in the plaster below the window; a little shelf; a darker place where the bed had stood. The sisters disagreed about which end they had put their heads. They sat in the doorway to eat something while the others carried out the fallen stone. After a while the older one moved her knee so the younger could get both feet into the sun.

The power had not meant for the room to be found. It had only meant for the water to come out slowly. When the birds began taking wool from the hem of Sela’s apron, which she had hung over a rail to dry, it watched them for a while. Then it went on.
