The power comes in under the stair, the way the wren does. From there it can see the nail she puts her foot on. The point has come through the old tread and is bright where her toes have rubbed it.

It stays while she makes two journeys. On the first she brings a fly and on the second something green that needs beating against the wood before it will go down. The smallest of the young gets the green thing. It swallows in installments, with its eyes shut.

Above them, the man who buys the clay feet is coming down with a sack of meal. He stops on the new tread to shift the sack from one shoulder to the other. There is a knot at the end of the board, and a split running out of the knot. With his weight off it the split is no wider than a hair. When he sets his heel down it opens far enough to take a fingernail.

The power puts a shoulder under the board.

The man gets the sack settled. A little meal comes out at the stitching and lies white across the split. The power holds the board until he has both feet on the landing. It could close the split then, and take the knot out of the grain. Instead it lets the tread sag. A thin white line goes on lying where there ought not to be a line.

Sella sees it on her way home. She sets down her basket and presses the end of her umbrella into the wood. Then she goes back for the carpenter.

He comes with a saw. She is holding the basket across the stair to make people go the long way round, and while he waits for her to move it the wren comes out between his boots. He crouches. It takes him a while to find where she came from. When he does, he puts the saw down and goes for a length of alder he can lay from one upright to the next, across the whole bad step. That makes the stair awkward. The first person down catches a toe on it and says something to him. He takes the board up again and bevels the edge.

By dusk there is a sound tread and, under it, the two old ones. The new board leaves a little more room at the mouth of the nest. The wren flies straight in, which she has never been able to do before, and overshoots the nail. On her next trip she gets it right.

The power could go then. It stays until dark. There is still a little meal on the landing, and a mouse comes out to eat it, taking the grains one at a time between its front feet. Below, the potter’s husband goes down to listen at the sluice. The old woman has washed her hair. She is sitting by the open shutter with a towel over her shoulders, turning the comb in her hand to find a tooth that isn’t broken.

He has a comb at home with all its teeth. He thinks of it while he stands there. In the morning he brings it down and leaves it on the sill where she can reach it without getting up.
