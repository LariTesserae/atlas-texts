The first thing the power did was wait through a tide when the pump did not start.

A thread had come loose from the float and caught on a burr. Behind the wall the water rose above the usual line and entered a joint that had been dry for years. It went some distance sideways before coming out under a slate near the door. A clear lip formed there, with grains of grit standing in it. When the sea came in, the lip folded over. At the next ebb it lifted again.

The power freed the float. The pump ran for a long time, and the new seep dwindled. By morning the lip was flat and dry. A crab walked over it without pausing.

There were other things to attend to. The eel had begun to scrape its back on a bolt in the drain. The crustaceans came down the pipe whole if the pump had been standing long enough, and in pieces if it had not. In a spring tide the wall took water from both sides and made a sound low down that could not be heard in the hut. The power listened to that sound as well as to the ticks in the mortar. It did not stop the pump.

On the landward side of the wall there was a length of clay drain with a root through it. Its far end lay in wet ground under the roots of a hawthorn. The power eased two of its sections apart, no more than the width of a grain of slate. At the next rise a little seepage went into the clay instead of down toward the intake. The pump took almost exactly as long to empty the sump as it had the day before.

For nine days nothing followed the water into the drain but lime. On the tenth day there was a clear skin inside the first section. It thickened at the broken edge and went no farther. The power left the gap as it was. By winter the skin had gone the length of the clay and into the wet ground. In one place a root dried and it withdrew. In another, beneath the hawthorn, the soil stayed wet even on days when the pump ran at every ebb.

At the seaward face the shrimp went on opening windows. The eel caught them where the slates were bare. One evening it went out through the door and did not come back before the caps showed, or at the next ebb, or at the one after that. There was no way to tell, from the unmarked silt in its hole, whether it had got where it was going.

Late in the winter there was a night of rain when the pump ran almost without stopping. At dawn, on the landward side, a blind crustacean came headfirst out of the clay. It touched a root, turned, and went under it.

In the hut the drop rose in the glass as before. After it vanished the ticks continued, first low in the wall and then farther back than anyone in the hut could have heard. In the lowest pool a shrimp fed at the edge of a window. Behind it, over a stone worn smooth by the old discharge, the window had already begun to close.
