For a while it did nothing. The girl had already called her mother, and her mother had come.

Up by the new bench there was a pool that had not fallen. The surveyor had measured it that afternoon and found it within a finger’s width of yesterday’s mark. But some of the water leaving it had begun to go down a joint in the rock instead of under the footbridge. The joint would take more as the thaw went on. Under the bridge there was a crack full of stonefly young. The water still reached it, though not by much.

It could have closed the joint. It followed it down instead, past the reach of the afternoon heat, to where the water lay still between two faces of stone. There were young there too, fewer and smaller. It left the joint open.

In the morning, when the surveyor made her rounds, the pool was again within a finger’s width of the mark. She wrote the figure down. As she straightened, the power let her hear the water under the bridge. It gave the sound no more weight than the scrape of her buckle against a post. She took two steps before she stopped. There ought to have been more of it.

She went back after breakfast with a cup and her daughter’s watch. By noon she had put one section back half the distance it had risen that winter. She waited at the bridge until water reached the crack, then went uphill to see whether it was still going down the joint. It was. She put both observations in the book. There was no column for the second one, so she wrote it across the bottom of the page.

The power did not bring another pear up from below. It did not turn the fox from the bridge when the thaw came hard, or keep the baker’s willow out of the drain. It stayed long enough to see the baker dig the willow up and the girl ask if she could have it. He fetched her a flour sack to carry it in and told her not to let the roots dry out.

She planted it out beyond the last streetlight, on a piece of ground her mother said might be under water in the spring. The girl asked how far under. Her mother said she didn’t know. They went back for a stake, so that if the water did come up over it they would know where to look when it went down.
