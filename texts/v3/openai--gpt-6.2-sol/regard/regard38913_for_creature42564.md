The next day Iven took the girl out on the tram. Her name was Ada. She had put a spoon in her coat pocket in case there was anything that needed digging up, and the spoon struck the window whenever the tram went round a bend.

At the end of the line Iven showed her how to read the papers. Each had a black mark at the point where it had gone into the seam. You could tell how much the street had opened by how far the mark had traveled. Ada wanted to know whether there was a mark for a person.

“People vary,” Iven said.

Ada took this to mean that she might get in before Iven did.

The power that had taken notice of the place had been there for some time. It knew, for instance, that the street would be wide enough for Ada by Thursday. It also knew that on Friday a leaf would come down across the entrance. After that there would be a long room with a corner in it, and no way to get there. The gray hunter had another way out, through the drains. It was not a bad arrangement except for a person.

The power could have held the leaf up. Instead it went a little farther down the line, where the crane had set out the lamps for the following week. One of them was shining onto a surveyor’s board that had fallen flat. The power stood the board on edge. This threw a narrow shadow across the leaf. In the shadow, a strip no wider than the door to Iven’s flat would grow a little more slowly than the rest.

That was what it did for the street. It did not know yet whether anyone would want a door there.

On the way back Ada asked what the corner was for. Iven said she didn’t think it was for anything. They came home to find her father on the landing in his good shoes. He had set out to meet them and got as far as the first turn of the stairs. In the night the handrail had moved just beyond the place where he reached for it. He said he could still do the stairs if he took them sideways, but he didn’t like to do that with a train coming in. People were in a hurry then.

Iven went down and tried the stairs with her eyes shut. At the turn her hand closed on nothing. She had passed that spot twice a day all week.

There was no stock for a new rail at the station. At supper her father suggested the tall door in the shop. It had a good straight piece on the hinge side. Iven asked him if he was sure.

“No,” he said. “Bring it out where I can look at it.”

They moved the table and lifted the door off its blocks. There was pencil writing along the top that Iven had never seen. Her father had made the door for a woman who wanted to be able to carry a cake from the kitchen without turning it sideways. The measurements of the cake were there beside the measurements of the opening. He couldn’t remember why she hadn’t taken the door.

“Perhaps she tried a smaller cake,” Ada said.

For the first time that evening he laughed. Then he had Iven fetch the saw.

On Friday the leaf came down. Iven took the tram out alone to check the flags and found the dark upright gap at the entrance. She set a paper on either side and went in. The floor had hardened. There were still a few prints in it, though already the dust was softening their edges. Around the corner she found a stretch of level wall at just the height where a person might sit with her back to it. She sat there to enter the figures. Nobody had asked her how much weight the floor would take. There was no box on the sheet for what the room was like with somebody in it.

When she got home her father was halfway down the stairs, running his palm along the new rail. On the underside, where no one would ordinarily see, he had left the penciled dimensions of the cake. He showed her where they were. Then they walked to the station to see whether Ada was at the tram stop.

The power did not keep the gap from growing over. By the time that began to happen, enough people knew about the room to have different opinions about it. One of the freight crews wanted a place to eat out of the draft. Ada wanted it to stay empty so that the hunter would come back. The woman who brought the measuring rods out in the morning liked to go there when she had a letter from home. At the meeting they agreed to leave the far end alone and fit a door at the near one. They asked Iven’s father to come out and show them how much clearance to leave.

He went on a morning when there was no rush. Iven walked with him from the tram. When they came to the opening she put out a hand, then saw that he had already found the edge with his own. She went on ahead to turn on the light.
