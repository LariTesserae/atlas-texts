The power came to the city in the evening and stood in the water while the animals passed. The last of them was the old bull. He shouldered past without looking up. The power had taken on enough of a body to be in his way and had to take a step back.

At the crossing Eda was sitting with her feet on the submerged stone. Beside her was a bowl with a crack in it. She had carried it out to see whether it would still hold water. It would, up to the crack. She was considering what she had that only needed half a bowl.

The power sat down beside her. After a time Eda put a hand on its arm and pointed down. A fish was trying the skin at the edge of its heel. The power had not thought to make any part of itself loose enough to come away. It sat still a little longer, but the fish went back to Eda.

There was a house on the other side of the crossing where a roof stone had begun to knock. Eda had heard it that morning. The woman who lived there had said it could wait until tomorrow; she had a sick child who had only just gone to sleep. Eda did not think the stone would come down. It was a thick stone with a good bearing at the far end. But she had come to sit where she could hear it, and each time it knocked she lifted her head.

The power waited for the next knock. Then it went across and put a hand up under the stone.

Eda came after it with the bowl tucked under her arm. She put her cheek against the wall. Inside, the woman got up to give the child a drink. The stone did not knock.

“Can you stay like that?” Eda asked.

The power could have stayed like that until the warm water stopped coming out of the ground. It said it could stay until morning.

“Not past morning,” Eda said. “My granddaughter needs to hear it.”

Eda went home. For much of the night the power could hear the woman getting up and lying down again. Toward morning there was a long spell in which neither she nor the child moved. One of the little roof creatures came to the power’s wrist, felt along it with its mouth, and went around.

At first light Eda brought the girl. The woman was awake by then and said they could make what noise they needed. The girl put her cheek to the wall, in the place Eda showed her. The power took its hand away. The woman walked from one pier to the next.

“There’s a frog,” the girl said.

“Yes,” said Eda.

The woman walked back. The girl raised a finger and kept it raised until the woman had crossed once more. Then she took her cheek off the wall and went to look through the shims in Eda’s apron.

The power went out by the bare squares. In one of them a calf was feeding in a strip its mother had left. There were stretches where the silt looked clean already, and the calf went over those too. Now and then it lifted its head and swallowed. The power stood well to one side, where it could see what the calf was finding without making it stop.
