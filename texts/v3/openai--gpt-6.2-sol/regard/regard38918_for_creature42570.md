The power came down the stairs and sat on the green step. It had meant to go as far as the basin, but on the landing it saw a heel draw up into the hollow of a leaf. So it sat where it was.

It stayed through a cooling. As the water rose, the animal assembled itself and fed. After a while it laid its ear against the surface. The power listened too. From the step there was only the roar and the cup ticking on the pier. But with its cheek against the stone, the power could hear what came through the water: grass rubbing against grass, and at some distance a gate being shut. The animal’s back jumped at the sound of the gate. Then it put its ear down again.

The power had thought of stopping the roar. It went behind the west wall to see what made it and found that the machinery there kept the rooms above warm. There were people asleep in some of them. In one, a woman had washed a child’s socks and laid them out to dry. The power left the machinery running. It did ease a loose edge of metal back into its seat. After that, when the roar rose, the cup no longer rattled.

It could have filled the basin. But it had seen the cool black soil under the leaves and how abruptly it ended, and had seen the fine roots alive in the joints that a flood would have filled. Instead it went looking for the water the building already let go.

There was a little of it in a blind elbow of the old pipe. Some mornings it ran as far as the east wall and dried there. The power gave the pipe just enough fall to bring that water to the long bend. Now, after the basin emptied, a bead would still travel along the iron and drop onto the pier. The first one ran uselessly off the side of the overturned cup. The second found the fern root. It followed the root into the handle and hung there.

The power put a shallow dish on the shelf in the recess, where a person would have put a second cup. It took some time to lead the water there without moving the old cup or breaking the root. In the end it was the fern that did most of the work. A new root, no thicker than a hair, found the damp line on the brick and went the rest of the way.

The dish held too little for a head. There was enough for an ear on any night of the year.

The first night there was no ear. On the second, a clear rim rose out of the dish and leaned toward the wall. The power had heard the man’s good night in the room, in the way the old places for his feet and hands had kept it. It did not use his voice.

“Good night,” it said from the green step.

It waited. Nothing moved on the shelf that it could see. Then, out under the pipe, two leaves that had never touched bent a little toward one another. Between them a wet line drew taut and held.

The power came back the next evening. Much later, when the animal had got used to it, the power brought a cup of its own. It liked to sit on the worn pier. On the nights when the pipe cooled, the animal ate first. Sometimes it ate until there was scarcely water enough left to listen with. The power never called it away from the grass.
