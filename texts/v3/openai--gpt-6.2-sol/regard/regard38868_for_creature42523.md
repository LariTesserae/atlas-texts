The power that noticed the country waited through one closing of the opening. It wanted to see what Eda would do with the shoe when there was no longer anywhere to set it.

She put it back on the slat. In the following weeks she took it down twice to lend it out. Neither time did it fit.

On the leaf below, the dark speck had been a stool. A man brought it out each morning for his daughter, who liked to sit where she could see the distance between the leaves. She had seen the shoe, though she thought it was a person sitting very still. When the opening closed she continued to ask for the stool. There was a current there that lifted her hair off the back of her neck.

The power could have opened the leaves. It had watched what grew in the cold where they came together, and what fed there; it let them close.

Instead it attended to a thread. The thread had come down from a stain and in the ordinary course of things would have withdrawn without touching either leaf. The power kept it from withdrawing for the length of one cold night. A bead froze at its end. By morning the weight had brought it against the rib below the bench, where it caught.

Eda's brother found it while lifting the ice. He heard a tick after he had set the disk down in the dry cup. He put his hand against the bench leg and heard it again.

He called Eda. She lay down beside him and looked underneath. There was scarcely anything to see. She touched the thread with a dry finger, and somewhere far below a child took her hand off a stool.

For several days this was all that happened. The child would touch the thread where it passed the stool, and Eda's brother, if he was at the bench, would touch it in return. Once he was away looking for a dropped awl, and Eda answered. She touched it twice because she had felt the first touch so poorly. After a while two touches came back.

The thread did not last the season. By then Eda's brother had spent enough afternoons with his cheek against the rib to know there was a place farther along where a sound came through without it. He took Eda there. She brought the shoe, though she could not have said what she expected to do with it.

They lay on their sides with their mouths close to a seam. Eda's brother spoke first. He asked whether anyone down there had lost a slat.

For a long time there was only the sound of his sleeve moving against the leaf. Then a man asked how far apart the holes were.

Eda went back for the slat. The man was still there when she returned. She could hear his daughter asking him to move over.
