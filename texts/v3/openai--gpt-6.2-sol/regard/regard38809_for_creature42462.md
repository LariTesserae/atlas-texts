The bat does not cross the basin. It loses height, catches itself, and loses height again. Its left wing opens all the way, but the right one draws in a little on every stroke. By the time it reaches the shelf its feet are down.

The shelf is too smooth. The bat scrabbles once on the upper face and slips over the edge.

There is a flake on the underside, almost parted from the stone. The power puts no more force into it than the root of the fern will put into the clay seam in a summer. The flake tilts. One of the bat’s feet catches behind it. The other finds a hold a little higher up.

For a while the bat hangs with both wings open. Then it folds the left one and, more slowly, the right. It has torn the thin skin near the tip. The tear will not close tonight.

Nothing else happens for a long time. A drop strikes a cup. The two pale animals have got the fleck loose and are feeding at either end of it. Under the ledge a cricket puts a foot into the pocket where the spider waits. The power does not draw it back.

When the bats begin to leave, some pass near enough to stir the hanging bat’s fur. It opens its mouth at each of the first few and then stops. It stays there through the emptying of the roost and through the colder hours after. It is still there when the first bats come back, flying low, their bellies full of moths.

The power could have put it back among them. Instead it waits to see what the bat does with the foothold.

The bat climbs. It gets one foot onto the top of the shelf and rests with its chin on the stone. The air from the seam has begun to warm. After a while it pulls itself over, goes a little way on its wrists, and stops just short of the dust between the stones. It stretches the hurt wing. This time the wing stays open. It brings the wing in, walks on, and leaves two fine tracks and a shallow sweep where the torn tip drags.

Near the far end of the shelf it finds a crack with room enough to turn in. Before it goes in, it turns its head toward the occupied vault and calls. A bat on one of the low ledges answers, or calls for a reason of its own.

In the morning the loosened flake is still there. There is room behind it for another foot.
