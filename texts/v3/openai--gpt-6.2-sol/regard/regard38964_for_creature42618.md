During the night the old man’s room comes out far enough for him to lie down. He lowers himself a little at a time, with one hand on the sill. The woman stays where she is. Once he is down, he reaches back to tap the floor beside her foot. The answer comes from a house three doors away.

There is something listening under the street. It has been there since before the first foundations rose. Until tonight it has listened chiefly to the animals, which make a noise like a nail drawn very slowly out of wood when they unfold their legs. Now it listens to the man tap again. This time the answer comes from inside his own room. The woman, who cannot hear it, thinks he has fallen asleep.

The thing under the street could hold the two houses where they are. It feels how little force that would take. It feels also, in the next house, a child asleep with both feet against a wall that will have to move before she can straighten her legs.

In the morning the woman finds a little shelf of stone beneath her sill. It has come out of the floor at a slant. She puts her palm on it to get up and feels the old man cough.

She takes her hand away. Then she puts it back and taps.

Across the street there is another shelf. The old man feels for it with his heel. His first answer is much too hard; it stings her palm. After a while they get the measure of it. With her hand elsewhere on the floor, she hears nothing.

The shelves go on working as the street widens. A girl spends part of the afternoon walking back and forth between them, checking. She is disappointed to find there is no ridge in the road connecting the two. When she asks for a shelf of her own, the woman tells her to find someone who wants the other end. The girl goes off and comes back with a boy from the next street. They spend a long time choosing their places. The first place she chooses is inside a cupboard, where he does not want to sit. They settle on two corners where they can go on eating while they talk.

Nothing happens that afternoon. In the evening the boy comes back alone and moves his hand a finger’s width to the left, away from a root with a small animal folded into it. In the morning there is stone enough under his palm to rest it flat.

Not every pair of shelves lasts. A man who moves north leaves his behind. For two days his sister puts her hand on hers at the hours when he used to be home. On the third day she goes north to find him. By then he has found a room with a window low enough to see out of while lying down. He shows her the window first. Then they look about for places to put their hands.

By the end of the week the street at the western gate is wide enough that the old man cannot see the woman’s feet from his bed. He taps once, toward evening. She answers and gets up. Halfway across she has to wait for a many-legged animal to finish crossing a join in the pavement. It is slower on the open ground than it is in the houses. When she reaches the other side he has made a place for her beside him, in the part of the room that was not there yesterday.
