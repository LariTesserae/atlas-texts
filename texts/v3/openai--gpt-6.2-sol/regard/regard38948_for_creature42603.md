The first thing the power did was wait to be mistaken for somebody.

In the market, on the shallow side, the woman put a hand under its elbow. It let her take its weight through the doorway. On the bench inside they sat with their feet in the water, and the woman asked where it meant to go.

“I don’t know yet,” it said.

“Then you can sit here.”

It sat there while she finished the benches. She had found that if she did the undersides last, the dirt came off on her knees when she reached under. Nobody had taught her to do them first. She told the power this because it had watched her begin at the bottom without asking why.

It could have given the town a common water level. It could have made the two streets agree. But it had already seen the girl carry a full jar in six strides, and come home slowly with the empty one. It waited until it knew what the people themselves would leave as it was.

The woman asked for a rail on the deep side of the market doorway. Her wrist had begun to hurt when she took somebody’s weight. She showed the power exactly where the rail should go, low enough for the girl and far enough in that nobody would strike a hip on it coming out. In the morning there was a rail. The woman leaned on it with both hands before she trusted it to a stranger.

The man beside the low wall asked for nothing. The power came to his court in the hour before he slept and asked if it might sit in the empty house.

“Not in there,” he said.

So it sat on his side. After a while he took the bowl off the towel and moved both a little nearer the bed, to make room. He told it that the woman next door used to lay her hand on the wall in the mornings. She got up earlier than he did, but sometimes he woke in time to put his hand where hers had been.

“Was it still warm?” the power asked.

“Sometimes. In summer you couldn’t tell.”

It came back the next evening. He had set out a second bowl, though there was nothing he needed it to hold.

After rain the power went out with the others to the last steps. When the girl lifted her hand from the stone, she caught herself just before she put the other one down.

“You feel it,” she said.

The power laid two fingers in the print.

“You’ve spoiled it now,” she said, and laughed, because she had meant it to.

They tried again the next night. This time the girl made two prints, one with each hand, and felt only one of them. She sat a little way back while the rain moved in toward the market. When she finally reached forward, both places felt warm. She couldn’t decide if one was warmer.

“We should have done it on a colder step,” she said.

There was a colder step on the north side of the cistern. They went to see if it would do. The old man was awake, sitting on his shelf with his shoes beside him. He asked what they were looking for. The girl told him, and he came out barefoot to help. He knew a step the sun never reached, but it was on the far side of the quarter and he hadn’t been there in years.

They took the long way. The girl carried his shoes.
