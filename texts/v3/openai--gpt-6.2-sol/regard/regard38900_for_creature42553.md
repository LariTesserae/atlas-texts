The power came as far as the lip and did not take the hand.

It could have raised a shelf under it. There was stone enough, and the stone was willing. But the root below the ledge was drawn tight, and what looked like a child’s wrist from where the woman lay might have been an arm reaching up from a country in which the ledge was the underside of a roof. A shelf could close the only way out.

The woman said something. She had one palm on the ground and the other lifted to make room, so she could not hear herself say it.

The power quieted the stone between the two shelters. It quieted no more than that. At the western rim a little break went on widening, and in the beds the roots went on drawing through the rock. The woman lifted her head. She heard a rib click against a fingernail.

“Can you let go?” she asked.

The hand took its weight off the rib. Then it put it back.

She had meant, *Are you afraid to?* She saw that the question had been answered all the same.

For a while they lay like that. The woman’s elbow began to hurt. She shifted it, and the hand turned a little, enough that she could see the dirt in its creases. There was no dirt anywhere in the valley like that. She looked at it until she could have told you which crease held the most.

Presently came the drag and knock from under the path. In the quiet it was louder than she had ever heard it. A second knock followed. She rolled toward the sound, as she rolled toward the wall in the shelter, making room without thinking. The small hand slid the rib after her. Its far end scraped stone.

There was ground there. She could not see it, but she knew the scrape. She fetched the hammerstone and came back on her belly. With the handle of the basket she pushed a loose white stone out past the leaf. It came to rest a little lower than she expected. The hand moved aside to let it pass.

She did not cut a cup in it. She had not stood there yet.

By the time the light went she had found a position in which she could lie without her elbow hurting. The hand had gone away once and returned. The second time it went away, it left a little lump of dark earth on the white stone. The woman put a broad leaf over it so it would not blow off, though there was no wind.

The power kept the stone quiet until morning. In that time the woman heard the sleeper beneath the path get up, go some distance, and come back. She had thought, all these winters, that turning over was the most the sleeper could do. Near dawn she heard the basket move at the fissure. A little later she heard it move again.

When the tearing began she went to fetch it. The leaf inside had been eaten only on one side. She sat down and ate the other. Then she took the basket back to the lip, where the new leaf had lifted far enough that she could see, beneath it, the heel of a bare foot.

She set the basket down on its side. There was a little room now, and it was better to have both hands free.
