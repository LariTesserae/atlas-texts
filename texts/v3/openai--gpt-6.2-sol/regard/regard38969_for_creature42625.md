The power noticed that the smaller one could not cross the bridge when the lamps were out.

It could go as far as the place where the first lamp ought to fall. There it waited. The larger one waited with it. On nights when a fuse went, they waited until morning, and in the morning there was no bridge.

The power could have kept the lamps burning. It considered this through a winter in which the man who changed the fuses died and his replacement took a long time to learn which key opened the box. It did not know what the two did on the far side. Once, when they had been gone for several nights, the boy’s horse stood perfectly level on a dry windowsill. When they came back it fell over.

In February the river rose almost to the top of the wall. The town sent a man to look at the pump. He went down three steps, shone his torch on the water, and went back for a longer pair of boots.

While he was gone the power let the elder sister hear a sound she had last heard at seventeen. It was the sound of her mother taking the bottom drawer out of the kitchen dresser. The drawer had stuck in damp weather. Her mother would work it a little to the left and a little to the right and then sit back suddenly with it in her lap.

The sister put on her slippers. In the kitchen the bottom drawer was shut. Water was coming up between the boards.

By daylight the two sisters had got the boy and the woman from the end tenement upstairs. They used the washing pulley to bring up a sack of potatoes, three blankets and, in a basket with a plate over it, a hen. The pump man came back with his boots and found that the water had put out the motor. He stood on the steps for a while holding the end of a cable. Then he went to knock on the doors he had passed on his way in.

The power held the river where it was until the last of those doors had been answered. Upstream, a man watching a mark on a pier thought the rain must have stopped in the hills. He went inside and put the kettle on. By the time he came out with his cup the water was going down.

The town moved the motor to the upper floor. It also proposed to fill the settling tank, which was no longer needed once the works had put in a different kind of drain. The younger sister went to the meeting about it. She brought a jar of the warm water and set it on the table. Nobody understood what the jar was meant to prove, including the sister, who had filled it on her way out because she had not wanted to arrive empty-handed. In the end they left the tank alone. The cost of bringing a lorry into the court was more than anyone had allowed for.

That spring the boy asked the pump man for the broken pane. He carried it home flat between his palms. There was very little of it left in the middle, but one corner was big enough to catch the light from the stairwell. He tried it in several places around the tank.

The power did nothing to the glass. It took him nearly an hour to prop it at the right angle, with a bit of brick at the back and his horse wedged underneath. By then the sisters had called him in twice. He stayed to see a patch of light no bigger than his thumb come to rest on the first rib of silt.

The smaller one came up to it. The boy thought it might cross, but it stayed there, and after a while the larger one came and stayed beside it. He could hear the younger sister on the stairs. He left the horse where it was.

In the morning the glass was still propped against the brick. The horse stood a little way off in a dish of paving stone. The boy bent to pick it up, then saw that the dish had gone dry in the night. He went in for a saucer, set it under the dripping sheets, and waited for enough water to carry back in both hands.
