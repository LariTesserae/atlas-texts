In a year when the sailwings came home white at the mouth, the pool at the shut hut began to go down. At first there was only a dark line on the willow stems to show where the water had been. Then the grazers had to kneel to drink, and the fish took to lying in the prints their knees left in the mud.

A girl came with a cup and found a fish in the last print that still held water. She dipped the cup sideways to take it up. There was nowhere to put it afterward. She sat with the cup between her feet and watched the fish open and shut its mouth.

The power that had been attending to this country could feel where the pool had gone. A willow root had died, and the water had followed it down through the old floor of drowned leaves. It was falling a long way, drop by drop, onto a shelf where nothing grew. The shelf had been wet before. There were the shells of little animals in a crease at the back of it, and the scalloped edges of a pool that had dried before the willow was a tree.

The power went down and stopped the leak. It did not need the girl to find it.

Then it brought rain. Not enough to fill the pool at once: a hard rain on the bare bottom would have swept the mud and everything in it out through the stems. It let the first drops fall far apart, so the dry leaves had time to soften and lie flat. Up on the path the girl put a hand over her cup. She had been taught not to let rain strike straight into a vessel with a fish in it, though she had never been told why. By the time it occurred to her that she was getting wet, there was water enough among the willow roots to tip the cup and let the fish go. It stayed in the cup until she turned it almost upside down. Then it went out with a small kick that struck her thumb.

The rain went on after dark. It fell on the opposite mesa too, where there was nobody to put out a cup. On the walls, the curled leaves took water first. The beetles that had lasted in them came up from under the dry rims, and the ones that had not lasted lay still as the water rose over them.

In the morning an old grazer came to the pool with a young one at her heels. She stood in the shallows a long time. The fish had not yet come out from among the roots, but she stood with her toes spread as she always had, leaving room for them.

The girl returned with her brother. He wanted to see the fish she had put back, and she pointed out three before she admitted she could not tell which one it was. They lay on their bellies at the edge until the old grazer stepped out and sent a little wave over both their sleeves. Then they went home to change, carrying the cup full between them.

Under the old floor, where the dead root had been, the power had put a stone. In time the living roots grew around it. Years later, when one of the willow stems split and had to be laid down, the girl, who by then tended the path with her brother, pulled some of the dead wood clear and found the stone with her fingers. It was cool all the way round. She left it where it was.
