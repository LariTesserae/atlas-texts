The power would wait through a dry week before it did anything. In rain it was hard to tell which way the water wanted to go.

On the sixth day the pump clicked twice and drew the pond down to the bottom rung. The mouths under the far wall closed, beginning at the ladder. Under the road, a little water moved in the old race. It went as far as a place where the loose stone had been packed with mortar to take the footing of a drain. There it stopped. There was a way around the footing, but it lay higher than the water ever got in a dry week.

The power took out a piece of mortar no bigger than the white cup. It took it from low down, where it had been squeezed out between two stones and had nothing resting on it.

For a while the water that came through was white. The first mouth it reached let it go by. The next took some, then shut, and did not open again until evening.

Nothing happened to the pump. It came on the next morning and the wagtail went down to the plates. There were two threads hanging clear of the water. The bird took both. At the far end of the wall, where there had not been a thread in several summers, one was lying almost flat against the stone. The new trickle was not strong enough to lift it. By noon it had caught the soft middle of a seed.

In the autumn a roadman came to see why the verge stayed wet. He found a blocked drain farther up the hill and cleared it. The verge dried. He ate his lunch on the curbstone, with his boots on the road side, and watched the cup appear as the pond went down. He thought of getting it out. Then he saw how much of the ladder was under water and finished his lunch where he was.

Most of the little mouths on the cup dried that year, as they had before. One reached the handle. On a morning when the pond was low enough for the whole rim to show, water came out of that mouth in a drop so slowly that it gathered dust before it fell.

The power left the other mouths alone. It had not come to make the pond a place where nothing died.
