The power came to the low entrance and did not make it higher. It went in bent over, with the back of one hand against the roof.

It stayed a while at the cup where the insect was eating. There was a place on the rim where two old bites had filled in crookedly. The insect went over it twice, turning its head each time, and then went on. Above it, one of the young had got far enough out of the drop to put a foot on the leaf. The power had never seen that done. It waited to see where the next foot would go.

By the clay walls the woman had taken the bowl out of its recess and turned it over on her knees. A crack ran from the lip halfway down the side. She had been keeping that side uppermost when she drank. The boy put a fingernail in the crack. She moved his hand away, then put her own nail in it to see how deep it went.

The power could have closed the crack. It could also see, a little way under the unfinished ridge, a seam of clay that would make a better bowl than this one. Neither thing seemed to be what the woman wanted just then. She was running her thumb across the bottom, where the person who had made the bowl had pared away a little too much clay. It had always sat badly on a flat surface. The split stalk in the wall had been cut to fit it.

The woman set the bowl back in its place.

“Will it last?” the boy asked.

“Not if you keep doing that.”

He took his nail out again.

Beyond the ridge there was another mat, rolled up and tied. The power had noticed it on the way in and had taken it for a spare. When the woman came back she sat down beside it, still holding the bowl. She had carried it all the way home without drinking. After a time she said the name of the person who had made it. She said it at the ordinary pitch at which she would have spoken to someone on the next mat. The stems took the sound. The boy, settling himself on the other side of the hanging leaf, did not hear.

The power did not answer in the dead person's voice.

It sat on the stone some distance away. When the woman saw it she drew the bowl closer, and it stayed where it was. A grazer put its head through the stems, tried a stalk that was too old for it, and withdrew. The woman rubbed the place on her knee where the bowl had left a wet mark.

“Did you hear me?” she asked.

“Yes.”

She looked down at the bowl. “It wasn't for you.”

“I know.”

After a while she slid the bowl across the stone, holding a hand under it until the power had a hand there too. It drank from the good side. When it gave the bowl back she showed it the thin place on the bottom, and told it how much trouble there had been getting the thing to stand up. The person who made it had tried to pare the other side to match. That was what everyone remembered, though it was the first cut that had done the harm.

From behind the leaf the boy said, “Tell the part about the second bowl.”

“There wasn't a second bowl.”

“That’s the part.”

She laughed, and had to set the bowl down.

In the morning she asked whether the power knew how to mend clay. It said it did. She fetched the split stalk from the wall to show how the bowl sat, and asked if it could be mended without making it sit any differently. The power said it thought so, but she would have to hold it while it worked.

The boy came to watch. Halfway through he remembered the four insects and went back for them. There were only three in the folded stalk. He looked in the recess, then along the base of the wall, where the pale root crossed the old thumbprint. On the bare clay just beyond the root he found a line of brown marks, so small he had to put his cheek against the wall to see them. They went up over the lip and down the other side. He followed as far as he could without putting his foot in the stems. Then he went to tell the woman which one had got away.
