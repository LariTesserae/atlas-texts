The power came on an inspection day. The attendant had brought a new book. In the old one the figures were entered to the nearest eighth of an inch; in the new one there were boxes marked NORMAL and ABNORMAL. She stood for some time with the pencil in her hand.

The power could have lowered the water. It looked first at what the water would leave behind. There were snails on the bench legs above the old black line, and in a hollow under the seat a nearly transparent shrimp was working its way out of its old skin. The animal had grown into the higher water. It had also grown too large for the place in which it hid. These were not quite the same trouble.

The attendant turned the page sideways and wrote the depth in the margin.

On her way up she stopped at the pump alcove. One motor was warmer than the other, although it had not run while she was there. She put her hand on it a second time. The power did nothing to the motor, but the warmth stayed in her hand long enough that she turned back from the stair to read the foundry plate.

By the end of the month a fitter had been down. The repair took most of a morning. He wanted to wash the lime out from around the bench feet while he had the basin to himself, and the attendant said no. She had no good explanation ready. She said the bench was not on his sheet, which was true. After he left, she put a broken roofing tile on two bricks below the lowest stair, with the curved side up. There was room beneath it for considerably more than she had seen of the animal. For a week nothing went under it except shrimp.

The power waited by the window.

On the other side it was late afternoon. There was a yard with a plum tree in it, and a chair drawn up to the sill. The chair had once been brought in out of the rain and had never been taken back outside. The lower sash had swollen in its runners. The power eased it up as far as it had gone that summer, and there it stopped. There was no need to open it wider.

For three evenings the animal went the long way round. On the fourth it laid a narrow part of itself across the sill. It stayed there after the branch had ceased to be a shadow and become, pane by pane, a dark branch. A moth came in from the yard and walked a little way along the pale seam. The animal did not stir.

The opening remained. Some nights the animal went to it and some nights it did not. Once a plum lay on the sill until it split of its own weight. The animal was under the tile that night, eating a snail.

At the next inspection the attendant found the shell on the lowest stair. It had a chip of brick in it, though the stair was dry and there was nothing there to make it roll. She left it where it was. A month later, when she came down with a man to show him which pipes were still live, she set her boot sideways on the step to give the shell room. She did this while answering a question about the pumps, and did not look down.
