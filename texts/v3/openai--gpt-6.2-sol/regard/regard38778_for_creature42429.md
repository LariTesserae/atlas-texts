The water went down before the rain stopped. Senn turned the bathhouse sign over and left two towels on the latch. She had been doing this for years, though the towels were generally wetter than the people who took them.

At the surveyors’ office there was a ring on the tiles where the pot had stood. The porter put one foot inside it while he told them what had happened. The elm had split the pot. A woman from the shell yards had taken it away on a handcart, with a wet blanket round the roots.

“Was it alive?” Senn asked.

“It was when it went through the door,” said the porter. He had been asked to sweep up afterward and could answer for very little beyond that.

The power had been in the bathhouse on the day the mason lifted the flat creature. It had wanted to see the small one. The large one had covered it before the power saw anything but a pale edge, puckered like the hem of a sleeve. It could have looked through the large one, or made it move. Instead it waited until the mason put the tile back. It came again after closing, but the small one did not come out that night.

It had been coming back to the city ever since. There were things about the place it could learn no faster than anyone else.

That evening it followed Senn and the freight boy to the shell yards. They found the elm behind a shed, lying on its side in the blanket. The woman who had brought it there had meant to plant it in a disused settling bed. Then the yard foreman had put a pole into the bed and found a live culvert nine inches down. He had chalked the depth on the wall. There was nothing wrong with his measurement.

The boy knelt and put one finger to a bud.

“Is that where it comes out?”

“One of the places,” Senn said.

The woman from the yards came out with a kettle. She poured some water into the blanket and the rest into three cups. She had carried the tree that far and was tired of hearing where it could not go. She asked Senn whether she wanted it.

Senn looked at the elm, which was taller lying down than she had remembered it standing up. There was room in the bathhouse for a pot. In a few years there would be room in the bathhouse for either a tree or a bath.

“No,” she said, and stayed to finish her tea.

Below the settling bed the culvert took the wash from the shell yards. At the end of each shift it ran white and full. The power followed it out, past the last yard, to a place where a wall had fallen into the water. Behind the wall was a shelf of black stone with a shallow lip. On dry days shell dust blew onto it. On wet days the dust washed off. The power put the tip of one broken stone into the gap where the dust escaped. It did not raise the shelf or deepen it. There was a jointed animal living in a hole beneath it, and the water still reached the hole.

The next morning the woman from the yards came down to look for a piece of wire she had dropped. She found the wire and, while she was there, noticed that the shelf held a little heap of wet shell. She put her hand into it. There was less than a thumb’s depth. Still, she went back for the foreman.

They did not plant the elm there that year. The woman found it a packing case, and the foreman brought her a barrow of dirt from under a set of disused scales. For two summers the tree stood by the shed. When the doors were open it had to be wheeled out of the way; there were men who called it the supervisor on that account.

By the time they carried it down to the shelf, there was enough soil behind the lip to bury the foreman’s pole to the first mark. Senn came to help. The boy had a freight run and could not get off, so she took particular notice of what there was to tell him. There were two roots that had gone round the inside of the case and had to be teased apart. There was an earthworm, which the woman from the yards picked out of a split board and put back with the roots. There were seven leaves on the lowest branch, one of them torn.

On her way home Senn stopped Above the Seventh. The water was over the sixth step. A person she did not know stood on the dry side holding a sack of meal and looking down, trying to judge the depth. Senn took the sack while the person crossed, then handed it back.

That night, after she locked the bathhouse, she lifted the lamp from its hook and held it low over the pool. She had not seen the smaller creature since the repairs. There was the powder, and there were the long threads of lint that floated after a busy day. She turned the lamp down a little. After a while she turned it out.

In the dark, something no wider than a fingernail set out across the stone. The power, which had come to see it, waited until it had passed before letting the warm drop fall from the pipe.
