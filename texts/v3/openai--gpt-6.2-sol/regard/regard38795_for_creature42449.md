The power came in a rise year. For a while it stayed below the lowest grating, where the calves were. It had heard the calls from a great distance and had taken the long notes for distress. Some were distress. Some were made by animals who had just woken, or who had lost sight of a flank in the cloudy water. One calf made the note whenever it found a stone it liked. It had found the same stone every morning for six days.

By the time the water reached the second rung of the ladder, the power had come to Ada’s kitchen. She was standing on a chair, trying to see whether the animal under the shelf was still alive.

“Could you knock on the glass?” she said.

The power knocked. A limb came out and felt along the stem. Ada got down.

“I can’t hear it at night anymore,” she said. “I thought it might have died.”

The power could have given her back the hearing she’d had at twenty. It asked her whether she would like that. Ada wanted to know if it would hurt and whether she would have to lie down. It wouldn’t hurt. She could go on slicing the bread.

The next morning she heard the first belt start. She had forgotten the little run of sounds before it settled to its work: a click, two loose slaps, a squeal that somebody had been meaning to see to since she worked there. From the room behind the kitchen she heard her grandson turn over in bed. It was his day off. She put a folded towel under the kettle before she set it down.

When the riverside track closed, Ada went out to watch the herd. Coming home she stopped halfway up the seventy steps. A woman carrying a sleeping child stopped beside her and tried, without putting the child down, to change which arm held its weight.

“There ought to be somewhere to sit,” Ada said.

The power, which had come along to see the animals, asked where.

They went back down three steps. There had once been a door there, before the building behind it became part of the pump works. Ada pointed to the recess. Not all the way back, she said; from all the way back you wouldn’t see anything.

The power sat down. The woman with the child sat beside it, though afterward she would have said she sat on a stone bench. There was room at the end for Ada. From there they could see a patch of canal between two roofs. An adult lay broadside to a landing, and every so often a calf’s head appeared above its back and went down again. The woman got the child’s weight onto her lap. None of them had anywhere they needed to be for the next few minutes.

The bench stayed after the water fell. The pump workers complained that there was nowhere to put their boots when they sat there, so a woman from the repair yard brought a chipped tread and bedded it below the seat. Children ate things on it that left their fingers orange. Farther up, where there was no recess, a shopkeeper began putting out a chair.

The power did other things in the city, not all of them small. Ada never knew what most of them were. At the end of the rise she asked it to supper, and it came. Her grandson was telling them about a fish that had gone through the grading slots backward and come out unhurt. He had told the story before, but this time, at the point where he usually had to stop and do the voice of the man on the next belt, Ada put a finger up.

They waited. The wineglass touched the wall twice. Then her grandson went on, a little more quietly than before.
