The winter the woman hurt her shoulder, the door did not open. She tried it once with her other shoulder and once with her back, her heels braced against the front legs of the stool. The stool went over. She sat down beside it and waited for the pain to let her get up.

Outside, the split-eared female stood over her crack until the ordinary sun reached it. Then she took the herd down to the bowls. There was water in two of them. By afternoon there was water in neither.

What had been watching the plateau could have opened the door. It could have put water in every bowl. It had watched long enough, though, to see the pale grazers drown in a bowl that did not empty after a storm. It had also watched the woman eat her supper standing up on nights when sitting down would mean having to get up again.

On the second morning, a girl from below found a bee on the inside of her window. It was walking up the glass with gray pollen on its legs. She caught it under a cup and took it outside. There it flew a little way up the path and settled, still in reach, on a stone. She thought it was too cold to fly and went after it with the cup.

By the time the bee left her, she was high enough to see that the white door was shut. She had never seen it shut this late. She went up and knocked.

The woman told her to come around to the back.

It took both of them to open the door that day. When the light reached the lower shelf, there were no deer waiting. The woman thought they had come too late. But one of the young ones appeared at the turn of the ledge, moving at a stiff little trot, and behind it came the old female, taking her time. The girl leaned out to see better. The woman touched the back of her sleeve, and together they drew back into the room.

The girl returned the next morning with her brother and a jar of soup. While the women ate, the brother took the latch apart on the floor. There was a mortise in the underside of it, worn smooth inside, though nothing fitted into it now. He asked the woman what had been there. She said she had always meant to ask the woman before her.

He made a wooden lever. It took him three visits to get the length right. On the third visit the woman opened the door with two fingers, and the girl, who had carried the soup all that way again, said she supposed she needn’t come tomorrow.

The woman said there was no need to bring soup.

The girl came without it. The woman had moved the stool nearer the door and brought a chair from the back room. From the stool the girl could see the crack where the old female drank. She had known that animal all her life, she said. From below, she had thought the split ear was two ears laid one against the other, and that the deer could raise either one.

They watched for a while to see if it could.

Down in the gully the bees had gone in for the winter. There had been no need for the one at the window to give up its place among them. At dusk, when the last warmth left the stone, what had sent it up the path came back for it. It found the bee on the sill of the back room, where the girl had put a drop of soup on a saucer. The bee had no use for the soup, but the saucer was still warm. It stayed there a little longer before going home.
