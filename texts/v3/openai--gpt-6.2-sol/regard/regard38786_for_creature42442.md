The power would not stop the pump to see what happened.

It would follow the discharge first. There was a house farther down the road with a spring under the kitchen, and before the pump went in there had been months when the family kept the potatoes on the table because the cellar steps were under water. The family was gone. A woman lived there now who did not know there was a spring. She kept her potatoes on the cellar steps.

The power would leave her potatoes where they were.

In October, when the attendant came to check the bearings, one of the motor bolts turned with his wrench. He had expected to have to cut it off. He took it out and found that the steel beneath the head had worn to a bright crescent. There were bright places on the other bolts, too. He put in for new mounts, and when the county sent the wrong ones he sent them back instead of drilling out the holes to make them fit.

All that winter the pump ran as it had before. The power could have held the motor steady, but there were people who would need to hear it when it began to run rough again.

The new mounts came in March. Once they were in, the sheet of roofing still answered the check valve, and the needle stood just below the red line. The attendant could talk to somebody on the other side of the house if neither of them minded raising his voice.

He came back after lunch for the wrench he had left on the sill. The little leaves at the door were up. He went inside to see why the motor had stopped and found it running.

There was one thing the power did that nobody would have had occasion to do. At the far end of the pipe, it put a stone under the fence stake. It did not lift it much. The stake had sunk far enough into the mud that in a hard rain the cup filled from below, and whatever was in it went down the ditch. Now the cup stood above the flood, and the soft end of the stake rested against the alder.

By August the ditch was dry. The stake had split in the heat and fallen away from the tree. The attendant, clearing bottles out of the culvert mouth, noticed the dark spot on the alder and pressed it with his thumb. It was hard. A little way above it, in the fork of two small roots, there was a wet place he took for sap. He wiped his thumb on his trousers and went on picking up bottles.

The wet place was still there the next morning. It was no bigger than it had been. It did not have to cross anything to get to water.
