The power listened through a stopping of the pump. It had thought the hollow might be waiting for silence. But in the interval a frog called twice, the thrush turned a blade, and the beetle went on climbing. When the pump started again, the power followed the water instead of the sound.

Some of the water went under the road. Some went through a joint in a pipe that had been laid before the oldest alder began to lean. In a kitchen beyond the station, a woman filled a glass and set it beside a bed. The power took its time over the question of the pump.

It did not keep the wren from the beetle. Later, when the wren came for moss, it watched her pull up too much and stand on the trailing end to get what she wanted. The frog sank from the rim of the footprint until only its eyes showed. A little earth fell into the water. By evening it had settled.

There was one thing the power did. The man who cut the grass along the service road had been told to go as far as the alders. From his seat the old sedge looked like grass that had lain down. As he came to it, the power let him notice how far a blade moved when a frog went under it. He put the machine in neutral. With the engine running he couldn't hear a thing, so he got down and stood a while with one boot on the road and one in the wet.

He left a strip that narrowed almost to nothing at the gate. The next time he cut the grass he followed the same line because he could see where he had cut before.

By autumn the toe of the footprint had filled in. The power could have kept it open with less effort than it had taken to turn the man's head. It let the moss grow. The little frog spent the next afternoon in a fold of sedge a few inches uphill, where there was no water to sit in but where, if you touched the underside of the blade, your finger came away wet.
