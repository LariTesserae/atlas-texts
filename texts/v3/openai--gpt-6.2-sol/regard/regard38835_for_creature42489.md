One evening a cup rang in a house with two rooms, and before the woman who had struck it could take her hand away, a cup rang in the next house. Nobody had touched that one. It was hanging on a peg by its handle. The peg was moving out of the wall.

The power put a hand under the shelf.

It did not put the peg back. The woman saw it fall, and saw that the floor had stopped moving, and went into the other room for her father. He had got one foot into a shoe. She brought him out with the other foot bare.

At the narrow place on the way up there was some trouble with a man who wanted to go back. His daughter was ahead of him, but he had counted her among the people behind him. They called her name up the line. By the time her answer came back she was annoyed at having had to give it so often. Her father sat down when he heard it. The people behind him stepped over his legs.

The power kept its hand where it was until the last of them had found a bed. Then it waited a little longer. A pale animal was feeling its way out of the needles at the foot of a pillar. It found a wedge, fed, and went in again. There was no moving the pillar without taking apart the country on either side of it. The power let the shelf go. The sound took a long time to reach the beds, and by then a number of people had already fallen asleep.

In the morning the old man asked whether anyone had brought his other shoe. Nobody had. A woman at the bed took a half-sewn shoe out of the wall and held it up against his foot. It was too small. She put it back.

The power went up to the entrance. On the outside there was a shallow dip in the ground where rain collected and ran away down the hill. It set a broad stone across the lower end and sealed the bottom with clay. It left a way for the water to spill well clear of the slit. Then it widened the entrance on one side. It took out less stone than a person might have expected. The old passage was wide enough almost everywhere; there had only ever been two places where a tin caught, and one where a person had to turn sideways and could not take another person by the arm.

For a while the people still turned sideways there.

The pool filled in the first rain. A child dipped a tin into it and spilled half the water trying to get the tin upright. She went back and tried again. Her grandmother, who had been sitting by the entrance to get her breath, reached down and held one corner steady. They took their time. There was more water than either of them could carry.

Years later a household came up from below with no wish to go down again. They built within sight of the pool. When they marked the heights on the new door, the smallest child objected that hers was wrong. She had been measured barefoot below and had her shoes on now. They measured her again. She had grown anyway.

By then grass had come up around the pool, except on the broad stone, where people sat to take the weight off their feet. On hot days there was always room for one more person if the people already sitting moved a little.
