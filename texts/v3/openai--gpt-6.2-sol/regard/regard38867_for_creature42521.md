The power came to the valley during the argument about the laundry.

The argument had been going on for eleven months. There was no dispute that the wall behind the machines needed opening. What nobody could say was where the water would go while it was open. On one drawing it went into a pipe that had been taken out in 1968. On another it went through Ada’s kitchen. This was not quite what the drawing said, but Ada had put a pin through the place where she sat to do the buttonholes, and no one had been able to show her that the pin was wrong.

The power could see where the water went. It could follow a drop through the brick and out along a strand of old packing, as easily as Len could follow a tram with his finger from the stop to the bend. It could also see the pinhole in the drawing. At three in the morning, when the laundry was empty, it went down to look at the wall.

Behind the last machine there was a narrow space with a sill in it. The sill was part of a window that had looked out on the river before the laundry was a laundry. There, in the dark, was the newt. The caretaker had carried it farther the second time, but the distance she carried it was not the distance it had to come back. There was a way down through the roots of the alders which was scarcely longer than the laundry.

The power stayed there until morning. When the caretaker came in, she found that the last machine, which had been out of use for a year, was standing a hand’s breadth farther from the wall. She went to fetch her broom. In the space behind the machine she found two shirt studs, a bus ticket from a city she had never visited, and a place where the wall was letting out water in short, regular breaths.

She fetched a saucer to see how much there was. Then she went upstairs for the woman who had made the second drawing.

The work took six weeks. There was a day when Ada did have to move her chair. There was also a day when the opening in the wall was so large that people came down from the kitchens to stand in the laundry and look at the river. Most had seen it from the bridge; very few had seen it going away from them at the height of their knees. One of the men on the crew ate his lunch on the old sill. He said it was a pity to lose the window again.

They couldn’t keep the window. They could, it turned out, keep the sill. The caretaker stood over them while they worked out the difference. Under it they left a dark way through, a little wider than two fingers. She had no idea whether the newt would use it. She was glad not to have to catch it to find out.

By then the power had been to the upper tram stop several times. It had meant to give Len a clear day, but each time the weather began to lift he was somewhere else. Once he was walking home with the girl who was learning dentistry. Once he was asleep with his feet out of the blankets. The third time he was at the laundry, holding the end of a tape measure for the caretaker, who had discovered that he could crouch in a place where she could not. The power let the weather go on as it was.

In February Ada took the tram up to buy a zip. Len went with her because she had said she didn’t need him to. Their car was held at the stop while a driver on the down line fetched a broom to clear a drain. They got out to wait. The valley was visible as far as the yellow bridge, with cloud beyond it and a little sun on the wet roofs below.

Ada put the zip in her pocket and pointed out the nursery. Len looked where she was pointing, then considerably lower. He had put the two floors together before, of course, but from here he could see the kitchens tucked under the place where the children played. A woman came out of one of them carrying a pan. For a moment he thought it was Ada. He turned to make sure she was still beside him.

The tram was ready. Ada had a hand on the rail, and Len was still looking down. She took her hand away. There was another car in twelve minutes, and the zip was for her own coat.
