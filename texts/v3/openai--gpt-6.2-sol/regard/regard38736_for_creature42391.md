The power came in the dry season and stayed in a roofless shelter. At first it thought it might quiet the wind. Then it watched a brown bird come up from the river with a fish. The bird rose on a roll of air that would have knocked a standing person down. Below the rim, out of sight, its young began to cry.

For eleven evenings a woman and her son went out to cover the pool nearest the quartz. One corner of the cloth had worn through. The boy had to hold it down with his palm while his mother fetched a flat stone from the far side of the hollow. He liked doing this until the evening a drop got up his sleeve and went all the way to his armpit. After that he liked it even better. His mother let him tell her about it each time as though it had just happened.

On the twelfth evening she came alone. The boy had gone downriver with his uncle, and she was glad he had gone. She put a stone on the worn corner and another on top of it. Before she had reached the path, the cloth came loose.

That night the power followed the water. Some went over the rim and some came down on the bare pavement, where the stone was still warm. A little ran into a joint underneath the pool. It went down past the living roots to a chamber in the rock. There was room in the chamber for more water than a household could use in a year. The power could hear the drops striking the bottom, a long way apart.

It opened a seam no wider than a thread between the chamber and the floor of the shelter. There was an old cup there, so shallow that people sometimes mistook it for a place where a stone had been ground smooth. By morning it was full.

The first person to find it drank and went on. The second fetched her sister. They took a ladleful, waited, and took another. One of them put a dry leaf in the cup to see whether the water came from above or below. The leaf went round twice and came to rest against her thumb.

For most of that season the shelter was crowded at evening. People brought jars that were too heavy to carry home full and had to stand there drinking the difference. Someone set a slab across two stones for a bench. A man who had been meaning to go downriver sat on it for a long time with his sister. When he told her he had decided to stay, she asked him whether he wanted to stay or whether he only wanted not to leave her with the pools. He had thought these were the same thing. They went home and ate, and afterward he asked if they could talk some more.

In time they stopped going to the pools every evening. The cloths were washed and put away, except for the worn one, which the woman cut into strips to tie up her beans. When her son came back he brought a little river mud in a stoppered jar. It had dried to a lump by then. She helped him soften it with water from the shelter, and he pressed his heel into it. The print was smaller than the one he remembered making down below. He tried again, leaning harder.

There was another shelter, far enough away that the new water was no use to anyone caught there in a squall. The power went to look at it before leaving. A length of split wood still held against the roof stones. Beneath it a root hung motionless over a cup. There was one swallow in the cup.

While the power watched, a woman came in out of the wind. She bent over the cup, then straightened and began picking the dead leaves out of it. She was thirsty. The power could tell by the way she moved her tongue before she swallowed. It let a little more water travel down the root. She heard it fall and bent again.

She drank what she needed. There was a swallow left when she went out.
