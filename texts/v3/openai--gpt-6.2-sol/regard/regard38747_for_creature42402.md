The power came in the rain and waited at the step. For most of the afternoon there was nothing to distinguish it from the weather except that one drop, level with the top of the step, did not fall.

It had come because of the bench. Whoever set it there had fastened it to a point that was not in the basin, and had done the work well. The bench would stay where it was when the slab beneath it went over the lip. So would the hanging piece, and the skins pasted to the cool underside. From a little distance it would look as if nothing had happened to the bench at all.

The slab was going to go that evening. A root as thick as a thread had reached all the way through the bright fracture. Its tip was already out in the rain.

The power could have closed the fracture. It followed it down and found the place where the water got in. The water had been going that way for a long time before there was a fern in the basin, or a bench. Closing the fracture would send it somewhere else. It might rise cold, or rise in the wrong seam. The power stayed where it was and watched the animal with the missing toe come out and take its place on the ledge.

There was an old break in the wall, just above the ferns. At the back of it lay a loose flake of stone. The power drew the flake forward the width of a nail. It did not fall. One end rested in the old break, and the other rested on the slab that was going away. It made a low roof over a little stretch of dry stone. There was no way into it from the bench except across the open.

The animal with the notched ear found it first. It went in as far as its shoulders, backed out, and fetched one of the pieces of fern leaflet. This time it did not have to fold it. It pushed the piece against the back of the opening and came out for the other.

The pane rose. The animal on the ledge ate, and went to the bench, and came back to eat again. Under the bench the young one pulled the fragment close. The power could hear its claws on the gray material. It seemed possible that it would stay there through the breaking, and that afterward there would be no stone within reach of its feet.

When the water leaned, the power let the drop at the step fall.

The rush crossed the slab. The notch-eared animal went sideways with it until it caught the edge of the flake. It got under, turned, and put its head out. The other two were already back at the bench, nosing among the skins. The young one came down between them. It sprang once in the usual direction, toward the fern. Then it sprang toward the notch-eared animal, which was worrying the wet end of the leaflet where it stuck out past the roof. The other two followed. The one with the missing toe stopped halfway to eat something. There was time for that.

The slab went over after dark. The flake tipped as the stone went out from under it, and lodged in the old break. There was room behind it for all four animals, though the largest had to back in. The ferns went with the slab. For a while one of them could be seen turning in the rain, its pale roots spread wider than its fronds.

In the morning the step was still there and the bench was still there. Between them was a stretch of stone that had not been warm before. The young one came out, tried it with one forefoot, and put that foot down again. It crossed to the first narrow stripe of sun.

Behind it there were three ticks, and then, a little late, a fourth.
