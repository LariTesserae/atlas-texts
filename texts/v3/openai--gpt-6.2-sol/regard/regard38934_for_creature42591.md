The power came while the shell was falling. There was time enough to put it back. There would have been time enough even after it struck, to lift it out of the silt and fit it to the clay so exactly that the snail, coming round again, would find no break in its track.

It let the shell fall. The shrimp went up the bank backward, feeling ahead of itself with its feet.

There was a night, later in the year, when the tide went out farther than it had since the ice opened the pocket. A wet line still ran down from the root, but it stopped short of the water. Three shrimp hung on it. One worked its way down and came to the dry clay and went back. It did this twice.

The power held a drop on the root a little past the time it would have fallen. It grew until its lower end touched the clay. Then it ran down and joined the wet line to the creek. Two of the shrimp went with it. The third stayed where it was. The next drop fell straight onto the pebble.

In winter the lip came down. It went on an ebb, with the sound of a boot being pulled out of mud. The mat of sedge tipped forward and lay in the creek, its roots in the air. For a while the water ran clear around it. Then the clay began to go, and you could not have seen a hand in the water, even at the surface.

The heron came the next morning and stood where it had stood before. The outflow was gone. It took a few steps along the bank, lifted each foot high over the roots, and caught a fish in a place where there had been no place to stand the day before.

The power stayed through the next flood. There was no longer a roof to bring the sound of the mill down close. You could hear it if you put a hand on the bank, though, and a little way upstream, where the flood had laid the sedges flat, the water was entering under them without bending a single stem. Two shrimp went in. One came out again. The other found a root, turned head outward, and began to fan.
