In the night a little rain falls above the glacier. By the time the water reaches the sill the rain has stopped. It comes down under the skim ice, lifting it in pieces and carrying the pieces edgewise into the shallows. One lodges across the outlet. Two more pile against it. The water begins to rise on the marsh side of the sill.

There is a low place where it can get through. From above it looks like any other gap between stones. Below, it opens into the dry space where the pika has put its hay.

The power has been in the valley since before the geese left. It has had no need to make itself known. Now it follows the water down to the first stems. The pika has already moved to the highest part of the space. It has a stem in its mouth, one end dark where the water touched it. There is room to turn its head but not enough room to lay the stem down clear of the wet.

On the marsh side, a flake of stone stands on edge in a shallow groove. The groove has carried water before; there is a line of black sand in it under the ice. The power tips the flake over.

At first only a little water goes that way. It takes some of the sand with it. Then the passage is wide enough for a chip of ice, which goes through turning slowly and comes out below the sill. The water in the pika’s chamber reaches the lowest bundle and goes no higher. For the rest of the night the groove carries what the outlet cannot.

By morning there is a shallow pool where the groove runs out. A wader tries it, finds nothing, and goes back to the old feeding place. The brown film under the stone at the seep is still wet. At its downstream corner a grain of silt has come to rest in a place that yesterday was bare.

The pika does not come out until well into the morning. It brings the wet stems one at a time and lays them on a slab, where the wind gets at them. One blows away. The pika goes after it, stops, and cuts another.

The power waits until it sees where the new stem is taken. There is a space a little higher up, behind a block that has not moved since the ice left it. The opening is narrow. The pika backs out, turns its head, and gets the stem in.
