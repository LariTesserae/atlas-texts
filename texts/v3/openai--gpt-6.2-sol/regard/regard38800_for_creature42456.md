The other foot held. The young one had put it down in the nest when the resin was soft, and a thread had hardened across the smallest claw. It could have pulled free with both feet under it. Hanging by one, it could only draw the thread tighter.

The power had been watching since before the seam went black in the young valley. It had seen the parent pass over that valley twice without going down. From above, the new fans looked no different from the green left in the dying grove. But that morning a grazer had come out on the lower side of the new growth and turned over a scale. There was enough beneath the fans now that it had not finished eating its way out before noon.

There were many things the power could have done. It could have brought the black mineral to the surface under the old grove. The fans would have gone on opening there, and the hunters would have gone on bringing supple scales to the ledges. But there were seeds already lodged in the grit beneath the brass-colored leaves, some of which would not open until the last of the old roots had come away from the stone. The power had waited a long time to see what would grow there.

It broke the thread.

A little of it went with the claw. The young one dropped close to the cliff, with the outer joint of one wing still folded. The power did not open it. The wing caught air along its ragged edge and opened so suddenly that the young one turned halfway over. For a moment its feet were higher than its head. Then it was flying, though from the ledge it would have looked very much like falling.

Below the first bench the parent turned. There was a route it knew along the face of the cliff, but the young one had drifted too far out to take it. The parent let that route go and crossed the valley. The young one followed, losing height all the way. They came to the new grove below the level of its highest stems, so that they had to fly along the bare slope until the ground fell away enough to let them turn. When they passed the lower margin, a white back moved under the fans.

That night they hung in separate splits no more than a wing’s length apart. The young one opened its foot against the stone and closed it again. By morning the bit of thread was gone.

The power went back to the nest. There were other threads laid across the scales, and when the light came through them it was possible to see how much work the parent had done. At one corner a whole bundle of them held the floor to a projection in the rock. The power left those alone. It took the loose end of the broken one and pressed it down where no foot would catch in it. Then it stayed on the ledge until the sun reached the valley and the two wings, one after the other, came out from the stone.
