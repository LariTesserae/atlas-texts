The power came at noon and spent most of the afternoon under the freight viaduct. It had seen the city from above and mistaken the pans for a way of keeping the drips off the steps.

Sela was washing a cup. She set it down in the pan, lifted it again, and put two fingers into the water. What she brought out looked like a shaving from the rim of the cup. She carried it to the tin, which had been replaced since her daughter lived at home. The new tin had a neater fold. Sela had opened it a little with a spoon.

There was room under it for three or four cuffs. There were nine.

At the new footings, the power found a cuff under a nail head with its first young one already clear of it. The string had been taken in that morning. Without it, very little water reached the nail. The young one went around the head twice and started down the shank. Where the iron entered the wood it stopped.

The power could have made it rain. It looked a long way down the mountain first, at the road where even a brief rain sent stones through the wire baskets set out to catch them. Then it looked closer. A length of the old string lay in the dust beside the stake. The power lifted one end and laid it over the nail. It laid the other end against the underside of a loose flake of stone.

In the evening a drop ran down the string as far as the flake. The young cuff followed it. There was already water under the stone. It went farther in than the power could see without lifting the flake, and the power did not lift it.

The next morning a woman from the crew came to pull the stake. She wound the wet string around two fingers. When the stake came out she saw another young cuff on the nail, and instead of throwing the stake into the barrow, she laid it on its side with the nail under the flake. She had to get down on one knee to do it. From there she could see, along the edge of the new rock, how many of the flakes were wet underneath.

Later, when they paved that part of the district, the crew left a strip of bare stone between the road and the first row of buildings. It was wide enough to put a foot in. People did, getting out of the way of carts, and broke off some of the thinner flakes. Others lifted in the winter. On damp evenings the strip looked dry until the streetlights came on; then, at the level of a person sitting on the curb, there was a little light under nearly every stone.

The power came back once to look for the cuff that had crossed the string. It could have learned which one it was. There were so many by then that it found it did not need to.
