The power came in the rains, when all the crossings were in use and there was nowhere dry to sit. It stayed under the third stair. The young birds were nearly grown. When one of them stretched a wing, the others had to lean.

Late one night, a strip of old floor peeled away upstream. It came down broadside and lodged between the ribs of Cada’s walk. Water backed up behind it. The walk began to turn very slowly, with the stair and the houses hanging from it.

Iren’s cup did not knock. He had fallen asleep with his hand around it.

The power could hold the walk, and did. It held the whole length, as far as the last house, where a woman had left a bowl of dough to rise. Then it moved Iren’s cup a little in his hand. He opened his fingers and heard it strike.

By the time Cada came out with her shell, there were people on the walk in their sleeping clothes. Someone had brought a lamp. It showed the water standing level with the treads on one side and running out in narrow jets on the other. They could have cut the ribs to let it go, but those were the ribs that held the houses. Cada lay down and reached into the water. She found the loose edge of the old floor with her fingers and told the others where to put their hands.

It took eleven people to draw it out. One had to go back for a cord because she could not keep her grip. Nobody had to hurry on the power’s account. When the water went through, it took the old floor with it, and for a long time they heard it strike things below.

The power stayed until the walk had taken its own weight again.

In the morning Iren went down to see about the birds. All but one had left the nest. The last stood on the fork he had tied, opening and closing its feet. Cada came down behind him and sat on the last tread. The terrace smelled so strongly of pear that she could taste it around the hole in her gum.

“Did you knock my cup?” Iren asked her.

“No,” she said. “I heard it from my bed.”

They sat a little longer. The bird went at last. It lost more height than either of them expected, and then flew level under the terrace, where there was nothing in its way.

Later that morning Cada took a good look at the place where they had needed eleven pairs of hands. By the next rains there was a gate there, woven to swing outward. Iren made its latch low enough that he could reach it lying down. His mother tried it from above and from below, with dry hands and with wet ones. Then she called him back to show her how he had done it.
