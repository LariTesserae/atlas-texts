The other person is a girl with a loose front tooth. She kneels where the old visitor kneels and puts her elbow where they put theirs, but the good place in the stone is too far to one side for her. She takes off her cap and folds it under her arm.

“I see the shell,” she says.

The old visitor had been about to tell her where to look.

In the spring the city, which has had two cellars flood in a street long thought too high for flooding, sends a mason to close the old drains. He starts at the bottom because the load of stone is heaviest going down. He has a square plug for the hole and a little board with mortar on it. The plug is well cut. It would go in with two blows.

The power has been there since before the girl took off her cap. It has felt the living root bear down on the dead wood, and the dead wood hold. It has felt the snail with the chipped lip rasp a place clean and go on rasping after there was nothing left to eat. It knows how little water the new fern can do with. It knows how little less would kill it.

It does not touch the fern.

When the mason lifts the plug, the power lets him hear a sound from the wall. It is not a voice or even a particularly strange sound. It is the sound a drop makes falling into water. The basin has been dry for eleven days.

The mason sets the plug on the step. His first thought is that there is a leak he will be blamed for later. He gets down and looks. For a while all he can see is the pale reflection of his own shirt on the sill. He shifts his weight, and the reflection goes away.

His knees are bad. He stays there longer than is comfortable. Then he goes up to the woman who keeps the list of drains and asks her to come down with him. She makes him wait until she has finished her bread. When she comes, he gives her the folded sack he uses to kneel on.

They find that the old watercourse was cut off when the street above was raised. The woman strikes the opening from her list. The mason takes the plug away to use in another wall. He takes the mortar too, except for a little that has dropped on the lowest step. The next rain washes it into a joint.

Nothing drinks the drop the mason heard.

By autumn the little fern has put out a second leaf, and something has eaten one side of it. The girl comes back alone. She looks for a while with her cap still on, then takes it off and settles in. A springtail walks into the bright patch and backs out. She has no name for it. She waits to see whether it will come out again.
