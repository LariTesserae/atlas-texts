The next time the blowers ease, they do not come all the way back.

Behind the wall there is a long sliding sound, and then a knock. The birds leave the rail together. One goes as far as the conduit, where there is nothing to hide behind, and hangs a moment with its wings open before it follows the others into the pipes.

The power keeps the wall warm. This is the part that takes some attention. The little joint above the door has opened and closed with the heat for so many years that if the iron cools all at once, it will close for good. The power holds it at the width it reaches in the afternoon. Vapor comes through. On the burr below the bolt, a drop grows heavy.

It falls while the rail is empty.

For most of the day the garden seems louder than it did before. A beetle gets under a loose piece of porcelain and shifts it. The ferns make a dry sound where their stems cross. Inside the pipe nearest the door the nestling begs, stops, and begs again. It has been doing this all along. The white-toed bird stands at the mouth of the pipe with a fig seed sticking to the side of its beak. It goes in on the second call.

The power leaves the blowers turning slowly for three days. On the third night, when they stop, there is frost on the outside of the service walk, but the bolt over the door is wet. The warmth now comes from a little farther down the wall, where an old return pipe still carries water. Nothing has to turn to bring it there.

In the morning the gecko puts one foot on the iron and leaves it there. A beetle walks past. The gecko takes another step.

By noon the birds are back along the rail. They open their beaks before the drop falls, as they always have. This time there is a thin, uneven whistle. At the first sound all their throats go still. The drop strikes the trough.

The white-toed bird whistles once more, after there is no water left to fall. From the pipe nearest the door, something answers.
