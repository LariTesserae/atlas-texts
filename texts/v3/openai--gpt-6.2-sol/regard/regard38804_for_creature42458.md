The power came in the afternoon, when there was least to see. It stayed until evening and saw the plates come apart in the light.

For a while it watched the fan. The last of the animals to leave the pool was smaller than the others, small enough that a grain which would have served another for a foothold rolled under its feet. It went on trying the grain. Each time it let go, the grain settled a little farther into the wet.

The power could have brought rain. There was rain already falling in the distance, and it would have taken very little to turn it. But the rain was falling on a stand of stems whose lowest chamber had been dry for eleven days. The power let it fall there.

It set the grain against another grain.

The animal took hold. Once it had a wall on either side it went down so quickly that the power, which had been watching its feet, almost missed it.

It followed a little way. Below the fan the opening ran slantwise into the old bed. There was food on one wall, and on the other a pair of marks so fresh that a shred of the clear living edge still hung from the deeper one. The animal passed them and stopped where the walls narrowed. It put its feet out. Nothing about the place would have suggested rest to a creature that slept lying down.

The channel did not hold. At the next hard rain the plate settled and sent the water back along a join. In the morning the fan stood above the wet ground. Its upper grains blew away over the course of the dry season. The underside, which had already begun to harden, stayed where it was. It made a low roof over the opening, and for several years the first water to reach that seam came in from below.

The power returned in the longest dry spell. The stems had folded their leaves so tightly that some of the stands looked dead. Under one of them a root had thickened across a seam. On one side of it the crust was dry; on the other there was enough water, but no opening an animal could pass. The root was alive, too. The power went down along it until it found a place, a little deeper, where an old sheet had broken and one end rested on the other. It lifted the end no more than the sheet had once lifted of itself. Water came along the break, slowly at first. It took most of the night to reach the dry side of the root.

By morning there were animals feeding there. Others stayed where they had been. One went some distance past the new wet wall and laid its eggs in a place that was still dry.

When the rains came back, the power did not keep the old roof from breaking. It did not keep the plates from settling, though by then it knew how much was lost each time one settled. It came back often enough to know other things as well: how far a root would go to get around a stone, how long the clear edge could live without laying down any white, how quite a small animal, coming upon a place where there was no longer room, would back up until there was room to turn. Sometimes it moved a grain. There were whole seasons in which it did nothing anyone could have found afterward, even by splitting the ground along the right layer. It was there for those, too.
