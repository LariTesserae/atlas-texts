The power came down the stair behind a man carrying a bundle of chair legs. On the lowest landing he put down the bundle and counted the legs, because on the way down he had heard one fall. There were twenty-four, as there had been at the top. The sound had been a drop of water striking a step a long way below him.

The power waited while he counted again.

It spent some time in the city before it did anything. It went under the bridges at dusk. It followed a potter on a round of visits and saw her bring home a basket nearly as heavy as the one she had taken out, although she had left a pot at every house. It watched a girl carry a leaf to the roofless district and hold it up in one of the old round holes. Her brother stood in the next room and blew through the hole until the leaf shook. When it tore, they each took a piece home.

For a while the power considered bringing the river back. It could feel where the water had been, including a deep reach under the market that the city had no name for. It went down into that reach. In the dry gravel were thousands of the jointed animals, white even where there was no light. The smallest stood on the backs of the larger ones. From time to time a large one moved and all the little ones stepped down, waited, and climbed up again.

The power left the river where it was.

At the far end of the city there was a house whose door had once opened on a bakery. The bakery was now two miles away. The woman who lived in the house had a good view of the market from her doorstep, but when she went to the market nobody at the stalls could tell her which way to start for home. She had got in the habit of leaving a washing bowl upside down outside the door. As she came down the last avenue she could see its glazed rim.

One night the house moved while she slept. The plate slid into an old branch of the riverbed, and the house went on a little farther than the plate. In the morning she opened her door and found the bowl lying below her, full of water. The doorstep was still in place. She got down on her knees and took it in.

There was a dry way to the house from the other bank, but only if you went far enough upstream to cross and then came back along a ledge behind it. On that side the back wall was blank. A boy on his way to the roofless houses saw the woman at her door and called to her. She called back that she was all right. The boy went on. She had meant that he need not jump.

The power went upstream and walked the ledge. At one place there was room for the ball of a foot but not the heel. It put its hand against the bank and loosened a piece of stone. A little water came out and ran down its wrist. It loosened another piece, then another, until there was room to carry a jar there without turning sideways.

That afternoon the boy returned with his instrument under his arm. From across the channel he saw the fresh white faces where the stone had come away. He followed them upstream and back along the ledge. At the blank wall he hesitated, then knocked on it with his knuckles.

Inside, the woman was setting the doorstep down in front of a cupboard that had always been a little too high for her. She heard him knock and knocked back. It took them some time to work out how he was to get around to the door. By then she had thought of something she wanted from the market, and he had thought of staying long enough to play her what he had been practicing.

The power did not keep the house there. Two winters later it went back into the city and came to rest next to the bakery, with its back wall to the ovens. By then there were thumb marks in the ledge where people took hold of the bank on their way around. The woman went out once more to look at it before the gap was too wide to cross.

In the hollow left by the first stone there was a little water. She had brought a cup, thinking she might be thirsty on the walk back. She dipped it in and tasted the water. It was good. She emptied the cup into the hollow again, because there were grazers coming along the damp wall, and took the long way home.
