The power came during the few days when the upper leaves were pale. On the second day an old sillback went up the ramp with the others. On the way down she stopped at a place where the ramp had worn smooth. She put one forefoot out, drew it back, and stood with her nose almost touching the stone. The others passed her on the outside.

There was a youngster under her. Each time she put the foot out, it put its foot out too.

The power could feel, through the stone, how little was wrong. There was strength in the old animal’s leg. There was even enough roughness on the ramp, except for the width of two feet where generations of sillbacks had come down in the same line. She had crossed that strip the day before. Today she could not persuade herself to put her weight on it.

Above the ramp, a limestone scale had been coming away from the wall for a long time. When it fell it would land on edge, break, and send most of its pieces over the terrace. The power held it by its upper end and lowered it flat. One corner rocked. The power put a little of the grit that lay against the wall under that corner.

The old sillback did not move. She had spent her life knowing which stones were part of the ramp, and this one had not been there when she went up.

A roadkeeper came along the curb carrying the dry socket of a seed. It touched the new edge, went out along it, and tipped the socket into the space between the scale and the wall. Then it came back. By the time the sillback lowered her head to smell the stone, two more had gone that way.

She set her foot on it. The youngster, whose next step would ordinarily have fallen between hers, had to come out a little to the side. For a moment it could see all the way down the ramp. At the bottom, two sillbacks were passing in the oval, one so close behind the other that from where the youngster stood they looked like a single animal with a very long back.

Then the old female moved and the youngster went in under her again.

The power stayed until they were down. It stayed much longer than that. At the roof seam a mother was bringing water to a mouth that kept opening a little to the left of hers. In the basin a white animal had got hold of a wing too wide to pull under. It let go, went around, and began at the other end. The power watched it fold the wing a joint at a time.

The roadkeepers incorporated the scale into the curb. Sillbacks took to putting one foot on it, though there was nothing to make them do so once they had passed the smooth place. In time the outer half wore away. The inner half, protected by the curb, kept its rough surface, and a brown seed lodged there. It did not grow; there was no soft band in the wall at that height. Several seasons later a roadkeeper found it and carried it down.

The old female used the ramp twice more. The following year the youngster came up alone. It tried the leathery leaves first and, finding them as they had always been, stood chewing while the others reached past it for the pale ones. When it came down, it put its foot where its mother had put hers. Its leg was long enough by then to step over the stone. It stepped on it all the same.
