The power came at the hour when Iven usually took off her boots. It had meant to come as a voice, but from the opening it could see how little room there was behind the willows for a person to startle in. So it took the shape of a woman and called from the far side of the basin.

“Have you got a light?”

“I have,” Iven said. “Where are you going?”

“I was hoping no farther tonight.”

Iven came out with the lamp. The woman had got as far as the second stone. She had one foot pointed at the third, and in the light Iven could see the effort she was making not to look down.

“Put your foot in the water,” Iven said. “It’s only deep enough to get you wet.”

The woman did. Iven caught her by the elbow as she came through the willows. Later she would remember that the elbow had been sharp, and that the woman had stood just inside the entrance with her wet foot raised, waiting to be told where she might put it.

They took their tea sitting crosswise on the bed, with their feet toward the lamp. The woman’s sock steamed a little. Iven cut the last of her onion in two and gave her the larger part, which turned out to have gone soft in the middle.

“Here,” Iven said, taking it back. “I can do better than that.”

She trimmed it with the small knife she used for everything. The woman watched the peel come off in one piece and fall into Iven’s lap.

There were things the power could have done from where it sat. It could have put back the whole roof, down to the stalk that had been growing in a crack on the day it fell. It could have set a spring running warm from the wall. It could feel the old goat’s foreleg as distinctly as its own wet foot: the ache was in the joint above the place that looked wrong.

“Do you want a hand with that crossing?” the woman asked.

“I generally manage.”

“I thought you might. I didn’t.”

Iven laughed and had to put her mug down. After a while she said that what she wanted was something to put a hand on. She showed the woman the place on the wall where she reached, every time, for a hold that wasn’t there. There was a good hold higher up, but to get at it she had to lift the kettle first, which was the difficulty to begin with.

They went out to look. The lamp showed their reflections in the little piece of open water. Iven held it close to the wall and put her palm flat on the stone.

“About there,” she said.

Under her hand the power found a pebble, round on one side, caught in the stone when the stone was still sand. It drew the sand away a grain at a time until there was room behind the pebble for the first joints of Iven’s fingers. Then it stopped.

Iven took the lamp away and brought it back. She put her fingers in the hollow. She took them out and looked at them.

“You could have done that before you got your foot wet,” she said.

“I didn’t know where you wanted it.”

They went back to bed. After a little while Iven asked whether she could do anything for the goat.

“Some of it,” the woman said. “Not enough to make the leg young.”

“Would she have to come down?”

“No.”

The lamp burned low. Iven turned it out rather than fill it again, and in the dark she told the woman how the goat came down last, and that it was no good leaving salt for her when the others were already on the floor. She went on talking after she had meant to stop. It was the first time she had told anyone about coming home to the roof gone and the needle still in the blanket. When she got to the part about taking out the stitches, the woman asked how many, and Iven said three. She had not known she remembered.

In the morning the woman’s sock was dry and the woman was gone. Iven carried the kettle out. Her fingers found the hold before she thought to look for it. She stood awhile on the second stone with the full kettle hanging at her side, enjoying not having to hurry.

Up on the ledge the goats began to stir. The old one waited, as she always did, for the others to go ahead. Then she came down the crease, still swinging the stiff leg out a little. On the last ledge she set that foot down and leaned over it to reach a fern she had been leaving alone. Iven waited until she had finished before she broke the ice.
