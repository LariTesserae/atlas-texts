The power would find the third one by the shadow of the drop. It would wait until the animal took its head out. Then it would follow it, if it could, into the crack.

In the first dry year it would be tempted to send rain. But there would be eggs still hanging in the willow court, and a whole afternoon’s heat stored in the upper stone. It would look a little longer. Behind the chip where the young had gathered there was a seam that ran wet all the way down from the willow roots. It passed within the thickness of a leaf of their feet. The stone between was sound except at one end, where a grain of grit had lodged.

The power would loosen the grain.

The wet would come through so slowly that by evening it would still not be possible to see it coming. There would just be a darker place on the underside of the chip. The young would move to it one at a time. One would go past it and have to turn around.

The bird would come in the morning. The power would watch that, too. It would see how far the bird had come on foot, probing the empty joints, and how it swallowed with its head a little to one side. It would not put the bird to flight.

There would be years in which the seam behind the chip went dry. In one of them, long after the willow had died back to a single living stem, a flake would come off the north wall and catch halfway down. The power would let it fall that far. It would leave it there, a little out from the wall, where the sun could reach the face but not the back. There would be no water behind it at first. The first rain would run right through. After a few seasons there would be enough grit at the bottom to hold the wet.

A young salamander would find it on a day when it had gone the wrong way. The others would already be down at the marsh. It would climb into the dark behind the flake and stay there while the rain passed over the far ridges and the crossings closed. It would get out in the next rain. It would be a little bigger by then.
