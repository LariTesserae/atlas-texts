The power came on a day when the wind did not let up at dusk. It watched the long-tailed animal make three trips to the ledge. On the third trip the animal hung below the rim for a long time, its mouth full and its hind feet searching the stone. Twice it got a foot up. Twice the wind took it off again. At the high wall the young went from gap to gap, putting their tongues out into the dust.

There was an old break in the rock just below the animal’s left foot. The power set a flake of stone upright in it. Behind the flake there was room for two toes, out of the wind. The animal found it on the next sweep of its foot and came over the rim so fast that it went a little way on its belly before it stood up.

The power stayed until the young had been fed. Then it went down to see the ledge.

It was wider than it looked from above. At one end lay a heap of husks with their open sides all facing the mountain. A seed-eater was working there. It had found a hollow in the rock and was turning a husk in it, first one way and then the other. The hollow was too shallow. Each time the animal took a foot away to pick up its striking stone, the husk slid out. There were several unopened husks wedged behind it, far enough back that the wind could not get them.

The power could have put the animal in a tree. It could have deepened the hollow. It watched the animal turn the husk over and try the other end.

Before morning it set three more flakes in old breaks below the rim. The lowest was just above the heap of husks. It took some care with that one. From the ledge it looked too far away, but if you stood on the husks the wind would lift you the last little distance. Coming down, you could pass it altogether, as the long-tailed animal did.

For several days the only creature to use the new footholds was a paper-colored runner. It went as far as the second flake and left its skin there. Then, in the middle of the day, the seed-eater came over the rim carrying a husk. It had to set the husk down to get its last foot up. The husk went back over. At the sound of it striking the ledge, the animal turned and looked down.

It went to the low wall and ate there. It slept through the afternoon with all four feet shut. Toward evening it picked up a small striking stone and carried it out to the rim. For a while it lay flat, looking over, with the stone tucked against its belly. Then it put one black foot down behind the first flake and began to feel for the next.
