The power came in the night and found the grain already frozen to the sedge.

It could have put it in the pool. There was still water under the ice, and a seam in the stone where the grain would have lodged. But the cold that held it to the blade was the same cold that kept it from hatching. The power left it there.

It stayed through the first snow. The snow came dry and blew out of the lanes almost as fast as it came down. By morning there was very little of it in the square, but the vole’s turning was filled to the top. The vole came up under the willow where the bank had broken. It went out along the branch that had lost its connection to the old wood. Its feet hardly bent the leaves.

In late winter there was a thaw that lasted two days. Water ran down the uphill lane and cleared the old turning. The stems the vole had cut came out one by one, and most went down the crack in the square. One caught on a shelf below. It was not much of a meal. The little white animals started at the cut end.

Then came the hard weather again. From the valley the pass looked no different on the day the grain froze than it did on the day the ice below it began to go.

That morning, as the sedge came free, the blade lifted a little. The grain was on its upper side. A good shake would have sent it into the wet, but the people who come over the pass in spring sometimes brush against the sedges, and a shake just as good might have sent it into the lane. There would have been other grains. Looking down into the pool, the power could see them in the grit.

It bent the blade anyway.

The grain stuck for a moment to the wet tip. Then it went down through the water, turning slowly enough to show a paler side, and came to rest against a piece of sand. The blade rose. A drop ran back along it as far as the first joint.

By noon the pool was full of sunlight. Nothing had hatched yet. The power went down under the square, where the cut stem had come apart, and stayed a while in the noise of the falling water. Above it the vole crossed the lane in the open. It had begun to do that on fine days.
