The power watched through one of the mountain’s long silences. A drop gathered at the end of a root. On the sheet below, a springtail landed, and the spider ran to it. The drop was still gathering when she went back under the stone.

There was a flake of rock above the shelf, broad at the top and held at the bottom by a grain of quartz. In the afternoon the sun would reach the flake. It would widen a little in the warmth and, after dark, the grain would come free. The flake would take the shelf with it.

The power turned the grain.

After dark the flake came down, but it struck the opposite wall first and broke. Most of it went past the shelf. One piece caught under the stone embedded in the sod and held it a little higher than it had been. The line to the wall went slack.

The spider came out and put a foot on it. She waited. Far down the valley the crushers began, and the loose line trembled against the stone. She reeled it in and went to the wall to fasten it again.

The power did not close the cleft. There were living tips on some of the roots that hung in the air, and nothing could put the walls back together now without crushing them. Nor did it hold the shelf still. The earth in it was softening. A little came away each time the wren visited.

At noon, several weeks later, the white root reached the stone. There was no soil there. It went on growing against the stone until its tip found the thin wet seam behind the fallen piece. In time another root followed it.

By the first snow there was almost nothing left of the shelf at its outer edge. The grass there had fallen away. But what remained was held from underneath as well as from above, and when the wind boomed in the cleft it no longer settled. The line to the wall stayed taut. On a still morning a drop struck it and hung there, too far out for the spider to reach from the sheet. She went along the line to drink.
