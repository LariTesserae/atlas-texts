The power first noticed the valley by the cool air coming out of a hole no wider than a finger. The hole was in a stair wall three levels above the pumps. In summer a dog lay with its nose against it. On the other side, in the dark, a drop of water fell every nine seconds onto a piece of wire left over from the building of the stairs. The wire had been bent double to hang a lantern. The man who bent it had been dead for sixty years.

There was a great deal to learn before doing anything. The power followed the water back as far as the cistern and the dog home as far as the mill. It watched the orchard keeper slide a folded sack under a ladder leg to stop it rocking. It saw the boy wash his grandmother’s shoes, which he did badly, getting the outsides clean and leaving the grit in the toes. In the freight shaft it saw a young bat miss the wall on its return and catch hold of another bat, which bore its weight until it found the stone.

In the autumn the surveyor came for a second reading. This was not his usual practice. He had written a seven in the cabinet at the upper stop and could not remember whether it ought to have been a one. He went down to the saddle after lunch, set up his instrument, and found that it was a seven.

Under the saddle, where the two pours met, a drain had stopped running. A rag had gone into it years before and collected enough fine sand to hold water against the joint. There was no way to see the rag from either end. There was, however, a little clean gravel at the bottom of the stair wall that the surveyor passed on his way back. The power moved one drop of water sideways. It fell on his bare wrist.

He wiped his wrist on his trousers and went up three steps. Then he came back. The wall above him was dry.

It took a week to find the rag and another week to get it out. The woman who did it had to lie on a board laid across a sump. Her daughter brought her lunch on the second day and stayed to hold the lamp. When the drain let go, it ran the color of tea until nearly suppertime. The surveyor put a second sheet in the cabinet because there was no room on the first to write what they had done.

By then the old fig branch was down. The keeper had cut it in lengths, lowering each on a rope so it wouldn’t strike the platform. At the last cut the wood parted before she expected it to. She sat for a while on the ladder with the saw across her knees. From there she could see the driver on the moved bench, peeling a fig with her thumbnail. There was fruit enough on the other branches.

The little hole in the stair wall stopped dripping after the drain was cleared. The dog still came and put its nose there. In the afternoons the miller’s daughter left a bowl of water on the step, though she had to take it away when the flour wagons came through. The dog learned the sound of her bringing it back.

The power stayed through the first cold weather. It had meant to go when the work under the saddle was finished, but one evening the laundress came up without her bag. The boy had gone down on an earlier car to meet her, and they had passed each other at the bend. She stood at the rail in the last of the sun. Below her, a car stopped and started, and its bell sounded twice. She knew he would be on it. She took her hands off the rail and sat down on the bench, which was out of the wind in its new place. By the time he came up she was asleep.

He sat beside her with the bag between his feet. For once he set it down without a sound.
