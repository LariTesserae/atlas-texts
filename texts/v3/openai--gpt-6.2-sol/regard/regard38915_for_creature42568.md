The power came to the bridge while the boy was standing on it. He moved to one side. Afterward he could not have said what he thought was coming the other way.

All winter the bridge had been creeping toward the wall at its sliding end. Mara had taken out the felt and bought it another eighth of an inch. In another week she would have taken a file to the stop. Neither she nor anyone else could see that the bolt behind the stop had begun to pull out of the rock. You would have had to be on the underside of the bridge with a good light, and there was nowhere on the underside to put a foot.

The power put a foot there.

It stayed until the boy had crossed. Then it let the near end of the bridge down onto the stair below, slowly enough that the enamel cup somebody had left on the rail went down with it and did not break.

Mara came out in her slippers. She looked first at the cup, then at the bright length of bolt sticking out of the wall. She went back in for her glasses. By the time she came out again the boy had fetched a lamp, and she had him shine it into the hole. There was a hairline crack all the way across the back of it.

“Further left,” she said.

He moved the lamp.

“No, you were right the first time.”

By breakfast there was a rope across the passage and a sheet of wrapping paper on Mara’s table with the bridge drawn on it. The trouble was not where to put the new bolt. The trouble was that the next sound bit of rock was on the other side of a house. The woman who lived in the house came over and looked at the drawing. She wanted to know how much of her pantry they meant to take.

While they were settling that, the power went down to the original cleft. The moth the boy had brought back was still there. It was dead. Farther in, where a little heat reached the cleft through the floor of the bearing works, there were others of its kind. They had laid their eggs in two shallow pits in the stone. The power left the heat and the pits alone.

Below them, a long way below, there was an old survey road that ended in a fall of rock. Fer had once walked up it from the valley on a day off and sat on the last milestone to eat an apple. He had been able to see the town door from there. To get to it he would have had to go back down to the dairy and take the supply lift, which ran twice a month and did not run at all in a west wind. He had eaten the apple and gone home.

The power cleared the fall. Above it, in the lee of the ridge, it made a footpath. This took longer. The first line it tried was too steep for somebody carrying a child, and the second crossed a ledge the nail-footed birds used when they went out of town. It followed the ridge farther than either of those lines and came up beside the door. There it cut a shallow gutter so that stones coming off the shoulder would pass under the path instead of over it. It walked the whole distance once with its eyes shut, setting down a foot wherever a tired person might. In two places it went back and widened a turn.

Fer was the first to use the path. He came up with a bottle of milk in each coat pocket and arrived during the evening changeover, when there was nobody at the door. His sister found him sitting on a coil of wire, undoing his boots. There was mud in the eyelets. She sat down beside him and put a finger in it.

By then the new bridge went through the pantry. Mara had saved the woman’s bottom shelf and lost her the other two. At the sliding end she had left room enough to put a hand in flat. When Ida’s father brought the baby over, Mara made him wait halfway across while she listened to the knock under his feet.

Ida slept through it. Her father stood where he was a moment longer. From there he could see into his own kitchen, where he had left the window open, and beyond it, through the inspection glass, a short piece of the new path. Fer had said there was a place on the way down where you could hear the dairy water running before you saw the roof. He had offered to show him. It was the first time anybody had made that offer in the evening without meaning some other day.
