The power came on a night when there was no rain. It did not come down through the crowns. It was there in the cleft, where two roots that had once touched had grown thick enough to touch again.

A mother had left a beetle in the hollow of a footprint. She went up the ridge, stopping at the mouths of cracks. At each one she put her chin down and drew a hind toe once across the stone.

Her young were not in the crack she was looking for. In the last rain they had climbed out of it into a pocket under a root. There was room for all three if the smallest lay partly on its side. They had not moved since the water went down. They could hear their mother through the root, but only when she walked. When she stopped to call, they lost her.

The power could have lifted the root. There was a tree on the other end of it, and sleeping birds in the tree, but that was not why it left the root alone. There was a way out already. One of the young had its back to it.

At the foot of the ridge lay a flake that had come away in the rain. The power turned it over. One corner went into the wet clay; the other rested against the ridge. It was a poor fit. A small animal could have dislodged it by stepping on it.

The mother scraped again. This time the flake took up the sound and passed it into the root. The smallest of the young wedged its head between the other two and scraped back. What came out at the foot of the ridge was so faint that the mother went on a little way before she stopped. Then she came down fast enough to skin the soft top of a toe.

She could not get her head into the pocket. She put a forefoot in as far as it would go and waited. After a while something took hold of it. She backed out with the smallest one hanging from her foot, its hind legs still making climbing motions. The next came out by itself. The last had turned the wrong way and had to turn round again. It took a long time. The mother stayed where she was, with her foot in the hole.

By then the beetle had walked out of the footprint. It had got as far as the underside of a leaf, where there was not enough water to swim in but enough to keep its legs wet. The mother found it there. She brought it to the place where the young were waiting and ate a few of the eggs herself before she let them have it.

Toward morning the clay let go of the flake. It fell flat, and nothing said beside it could be heard on the ridge. By then the young had gone to sleep in a different crack. The mother had brought them there one at a time. On the way back for the last she had stopped at a pool and put her lip in, and the beetle had let go.
