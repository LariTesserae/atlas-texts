The first time the power came down into the valley, it came in rain. It passed the bench without stopping. A little way below it, where the paving ended, a jar lid had gone edgewise into the cleft that took the overflow. The water was running over the lid in a smooth white tongue. The power put two fingers in the hole the potter had made for a cord and lifted it out.

The water fell the width of a barleycorn. Under the bench something let go in several places at once, with a sound like a wet sleeve being drawn out of a coat. The power waited to see if it would come any farther. It did not.

There was a shelf above the cleft, dry even in that rain. The power turned the lid over on it, so that whoever came looking would see the potter’s three strokes on the underside. Then it went up the valley, stopping wherever a lid was ticking, to learn what the people did.

It came back in clear weather and sat on the bench. The old woman was there with a cuff to mend. She shifted just enough to make room.

“Put your feet a little wider,” she said. “It comes through there.”

There was no water on the paving. The power put its feet wider. After a time a dry crescent appeared on the uphill side of a flake of slate between them. It stayed there while the old woman drew a long piece of thread through the cloth. When she drew it through again, the crescent was on the other side.

The power had thought of making a second place like the bench, higher up. It had gone so far as to choose the stone. Now it saw that it did not know why the thing came all the way down, or what it was doing when it lay under the seat and moved twice in the course of a morning. It left the stone where it was.

What it did instead was go back to the cleft after the rain had passed. There were old marks on either side, low down, where lids had caught before. With a narrow chisel it took a little stone out of one side, below the marks. It set the lid back in to see. The lid could still go in edgewise, but now there was room under it for a finger of water. From the bench you could not see the alteration. With your ear to the paving you could just hear it.

The next time the valley filled, nobody went down to sit. The old woman had finished the cuff, and the children, who had been down twice with their sleeves rolled up, were asleep. The power went as far as the last stair. The water was white in the court, though the upper trays had almost lost the sun.

It stood there long enough to see a dry line form beneath the seat. At one end the line was as fine as a thread; at the other there was room for the whole dark print of a thumb. The power had never seen it from that far away. It stayed on the stair. By the time it went home there was no light left to see by, and the little passage it had cut was still running.
