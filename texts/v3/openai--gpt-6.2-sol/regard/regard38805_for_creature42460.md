The power came in with the woman on the early shift and let the door shut.

She had come down to see whether opening a valve upstairs had made any difference. It had. The cold-water coupling was wet on the wrong side. She put a finger to it, wiped her finger on her trousers, and stood a while longer in the dark.

There was a springtail at the low end of the spoon. It had got as far as a clean stretch of glaze. The power could have put it across. It could have wet the whole floor, or made the bare tile give up food. But the insect turned, and the power followed it back along the line. There was still something to eat where the line doubled at a flaw in the glaze. The insect stopped and ate it.

Up by the ceiling, the bolt was nearly through.

That was where the power did something. It let a little piece of rust fall. The piece struck the empty housing and broke, and the woman, who was reaching for the latch, heard a sound she did not know.

She came back with a light. Above the bolt was the foot of a hanger that carried a pipe full of water. She looked at it for a long time. Then she went upstairs and shut the water off.

The people who came to replace the hanger found where the rain got in. It entered around a conduit two floors up and ran inside it for part of the way. They sealed the opening. The next time it rained the spoon stayed dry.

For a while the power did nothing. The springtails fed under the collar. The little spider caught one there and carried it back to the bracket. Then the film whitened, and the young went deeper into the join. There was not much farther to go.

On the morning the woman came down to check the new hanger, she brought a cup of tea. She set it on the empty housing and forgot it there. At the end of the shift she came back for the cup. The tea was still a little warm.

There was a sink upstairs. She took the cup to it, rinsed it, and on her way out filled it again. She had no very clear idea what she was doing. She knew that the room had been wet for as long as she had worked there, and that the insects had come out when it was wet. She did not want to fill the spoon. She tipped a little clean water against the drain collar and watched it disappear into the grout.

After that she sometimes brought water and sometimes forgot. On the days she forgot, the power did not remind her. It had found a place, no wider than the cut edge of a tile, where water from the washing of the passage could get under the threshold instead of drying against it. The floor fell almost imperceptibly from there to the drain. Most of the water never showed. It was enough to keep one side of the join dark.

The woman noticed the dark grout and put a scrap of paper against it to see how wet it was. The paper took up a spot the size of a pinhead. She threw it away and made a note to look again next week.

By then the old lines in the spoon had gone dusty. At the collar there was a new line, so close to the metal that she could see it only with her cheek nearly to the floor. She got down that far once. A springtail came out on the glaze, and she held still, thinking it might go farther if she did.

It went a little farther. Then it turned and went back under the lip. The woman put a hand on the empty housing to get up. The iron was warm. She left her hand there a moment before she switched off the light.
