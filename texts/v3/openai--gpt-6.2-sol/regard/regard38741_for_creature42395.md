The power sat on the bench. There was room beside it for someone with a loaf.

It watched Sella clear the drain, then watched the root come back. It could have killed the root without harming the tree. It could have taken the water another way. Before doing either it followed Sella home and saw her set the long lever just inside the door, where she could reach it without putting on her shoes. She rubbed the place on her shoulder where it rode.

The next morning the power went down to the stepping stones. At the last bend it put a finger to the crack and caught a grain of grit as it came out. It set the grain on the bank. By evening an ant had gone around it twice. It was still there.

For a while that was all it did.

One day Iven brought a loaf out to Ressa’s house. She was up on the parapet taking in the sack and saw him lift his hand to knock. He stood a moment with his knuckles to the wood before he struck.

“You can use the spoon,” she said from above.

“I know.”

She came down and let him in. He had cut off neither heel. They ate one each, standing at the kitchen table because there was flour on both chairs. When he left she went as far as the bench with him. There he gave her the rest of the loaf, which she had forgotten she was carrying until he took it from under his arm.

The power, which had been about to mend the spoon, left it alone.

It went instead to Sella’s ward. In the night it held the white root back from the channel. It did not pull on it or tell it to stop growing. It gave it a little earth on the other side of the wall, and the root went there.

In the morning Sella lifted the lid. She poked the channel with the end of her lever, then fetched a candle and looked in. There was nothing to take out. She set the lid down and waited until the children had passed. The smallest was still carrying the board empty. Sella asked if she might put something on it, and laid the candle there, cold now, with a little grit stuck to the bottom.

The child carried it to the ovens. Iven found a saucer for it.

The power did not keep the channel clear after that. The tree might send another root, and in time there would be leaves in it, and silt. But the silt would be silt that had come down in the rain, and when Sella took it out she would be able to put it somewhere.

Toward evening she carried her lever up to the bench. Ressa was there with the sack over her knees. One of the seams she had opened had begun to fray, and she was pulling the loose threads out one at a time.

“Did he come all this way with the bread yesterday?” Sella asked.

“He did.”

“He used to come up here when you were little. To see if you’d got across.”

“I used to wave.”

“Yes,” Sella said. “He’d wave before he could see you. I asked him about it once. He said you might be waving already.”

Ressa laid the sack beside her and looked toward the ovens. Iven had come out with the dog’s pan. He put it down and turned away, as he always did. Then he looked up at the bench. Ressa raised her hand. The dog drank while he was looking, and when he took the pan in there was a little less water in it.
