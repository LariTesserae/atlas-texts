The power came on a rising tide and put a hand over one of the holes.

The note stopped there. A little way off, a pool that had been still bulged in the middle and threw its knees out onto the stone. Air came up through a fish trap and rolled two stones off the wall. The power took its hand away. Before the ebb it had put the stones back, though a woman coming to tend the trap took one out and turned it over. It sat better the other way.

At low water the power followed her. She passed two shelters and stopped at a third, where a man was waiting for his daughter. The woman had seen the daughter that morning. She was mending a basket at her aunt’s and meant to stay another tide. The man asked her to say it again. The holes had begun to blow, and she took his head between her hands and told him. Afterward he moved over to give her room.

The power went down below the plain. It found the warm seep and the crusts that grew in its path. It found the hunters’ young standing in the dark on a shelf, their bills laid along one another’s backs. It followed the tide farther in than the hunters could go and heard the air being driven out ahead of it. There was no way to stop that sound without finding somewhere else for the air to go.

There was, however, a place under the third shelter where two beds of stone nearly met. The power drew them together by the width of a fish spine. At the next flood the little holes still clucked, and the great hole beyond the trap still gave its note. But under the slab the note was no longer in the stone.

The man heard the woman set down the horn cup. He had been sitting beside her for half the tide, thinking she was asleep.

“Did she finish the basket?” he asked.

“No,” the woman said. “She’s waiting for you to bring the needle.”

He reached up. The needle had been over his head all evening. He took it down and put it through the cuff of his sleeve.

The power made the same small change beneath the other shelters, testing each at flood. Under one it had to leave the beds as they were: when it moved them, the drip stopped. It waited out the tide there with a family who had come in wet. At the ebb, after they had gone, it lifted the roof slab and set it down again with a thin stone under one corner. At the next flood there was water in the cup, and a child lying on the floor could hear a fish break the surface beneath it.

On the way out the power passed a place where people often sat to wait for someone. There were two dry routes in sight, and when the light went it was hard to keep both in view. The power gathered enough stone for a shelter and found a slab that two people could have carried between them. It left room above the fingers. It had no cup to put under the drip.

By the next evening there was one. It was a little too tall, and whoever had brought it had dug a hollow for it in the gravel. A woman waiting there for her brother heard him set his basket down outside. She moved her feet so he could get in.
