The order came back with a question written beneath it: where did the ditch end?

Iven wrote *at present* beside the measurement she had given. The clerk sent the form back again. There was no box for that.

The power that had come to the city went out with her the next morning. It had heard the breath from a long way off. From a long way off it had sounded like something asking to be let go.

Iven's son carried the hook as usual. At the end of the ditch he gave it to her and went to see whether the saucer was still there. It was. The animal had moved off it in the night, and a little of the blue pattern showed through the ice.

The power waited beside the pool until the boy straightened up. Then it put its attention down through the seam he had found.

The animal that breathed lay on its side against the shelf. It was not holding the city up. A part of the city, no bigger than the tram stop and the rooms above it, had grown into the soft fold behind one of its forelimbs. When it breathed deeply the stone pinched it. It had learned to breathe a little at a time. What the people heard was the breath it took when it could no longer manage that.

There was room for the animal to move away. The power went around to the other side to see why it had not.

Under the forelimb, on the side away from the stone, was a young one. Its feet were still shut. The water ran fast there, and each time the large animal shifted, the young one slid a little farther down its flank. It would bring the limb in close again, and the young one would stop sliding. That was when the stone pinched.

The power looked for somewhere else to put the young one. There were ledges, but the water swept them bare. There were hollows where the water hardly moved, but the young one would have had to let go of its mother's skin to be put there. It was holding on with its mouth.

Up on the shelf Iven lifted the last grate. The two pale animals folded their legs. One came neatly into the bucket; the other went past it. She walked downstream with the bucket in one hand until the animal caught on a rough place and opened its legs. Then she tipped the first one into the run just above it.

At the bottom of the shelf the power took away a little stone. Not from the part that pinched. It made a shallow place on the other side of the limb, where the current could go around instead of through. Then it waited.

The first time the young one slipped into the shallow place, the mother drew it back. The second time she left it there through one of her small breaths. By the time Iven and her son were taking their boots off upstairs, the mother had let the limb fall open. She breathed. A spoon slid off a saucer in the kitchen, and Iven's father caught it against his knee.

“That one was underneath,” he said.

Iven poured the tea. Her son asked how he knew.

“Because I felt it in my feet,” he said, and put them back on the rung.

The power stayed until the young one's feet opened. It took most of the cold season. Now and then the mother reached into the shallow place and touched it, even after it could hold on. One morning the power came down and found nothing there but a pale animal standing crosswise in the current. It had caught something too large to roll into a pellet and was eating it where it held it.

By then the new grate had come. The clerk had gone out to measure the ditch himself and had found that Iven's figure was short by nearly a yard. He ordered two grates and left her a note asking her to keep the second under cover.

She put it in the pump house on two pieces of wood, so that it would be easy to get the hook under it.
