The next tram is late.

At the end of the line a woman has caught her coat in the door. She is out of the coat before the driver understands what has happened. He opens the door and she puts it back on, one sleeve and then the other, standing on the platform with her bag between her feet. By the time she gets on, the driver has missed his light.

In the north wall the water has begun to go into the mortar. It is going faster on one side of the young animal than on the other. One of its surfaces, which would ordinarily fold in water and unfold again, has laid itself against the stone.

The power notices the woman, too. There is a loose stitch in the lining of her sleeve. For the rest of the day she will catch a finger in it every time she reaches into her pocket. In the evening she will turn the sleeve out and mend it.

There is water on the other side of the stone. It is not far away, but the young animal cannot get to it. Between the two wet places is a little ridge left by the edge of a trowel. The ridge has never carried any weight. Half of it came off in the winter the bench was put in. What remains would probably last another fifty years.

The power takes a grain out of the ridge.

For a while this makes no difference. Then the water on the far side touches the water on the near side. The surface that has laid itself against the stone comes free at one end. When the tram arrives, the young animal turns over.

It does not go through the opening. The opening is too small for that. A thread will go through in time, if the animal lives to put one out. On the far side there is a hollow with a little grey flour in it and room for a sac no bigger than a pinhead. There are also hollows where there is nothing to eat. The first thread may go into one of those.

The grain comes out in the evening rain. It travels farther down the pavement than its size would suggest, stops, and is overtaken by a second run of water. At the slot it catches on a burr. By morning it is gone.

Many years later, when a brood hatches in the far bowl, none of the young will enter by that seam. The bowl will have worn lower by then. The rain will take them across a part of the wall their parent never touched, and one will lodge under a patch put in by a mason who is not yet born. The power leaves that place alone. The mason will need the whole of it to set her repair.

For now the tram passes and the wall settles. The woman sits with her bag on her lap. Under the street, in a place that has been dry between trams, a little water stays.
