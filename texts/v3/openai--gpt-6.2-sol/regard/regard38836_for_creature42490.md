The power could have filled the cup. It looked first at what the keeper did when there was water in it.

They rinsed their spectacles and tipped the rest into a seam at the foot of the wall. On dry mornings they did without the rinse. By evening they were looking through two white crescents, and when the bird came for flies they could turn a page without much danger of seeing it.

There was drinking water in a tank behind the pump house. There were nine days of it left. The keeper had measured it with a stick that morning and written the figure on the back of an envelope. On the front was a letter asking whether they meant to stay another winter. It had come up with the last load of fuel. The keeper had taken it out twice while the driver was there and put it away twice without opening their mouth.

The power went down the road to see what had become of the next load.

A culvert had broken. Water had taken the fill from under the road and left the road itself, a thin shelf with two good wheel tracks down its middle. The driver would come to it before dawn. In daylight there was a small grey crack to show where the shelf began. From the cab, with the lights on, there would be no crack at all.

The power let the shelf fall while there was no one on it. It took a little longer than might be supposed. Grass roots held one end, and a stone the size of a loaf held the other. The power waited until the last of the dust had gone down into the gully. Then it went back up to the keeper.

It could have mended the road. Instead, at the hour when the keeper ordinarily turned on the pump, it let the loose grille give one quite unaccustomed bang. The keeper took a screwdriver outside. From there they could see, far down the slope, two lights standing still where lights did not usually stand. They fetched the field glasses. After a while one of the lights went out and came on again. Then it did it twice.

The keeper had a lamp in the pump house. They stood at the edge of the plateau and answered.

It took the driver most of the morning to come up on foot. She brought two water cans, one in each hand, and had to put them down every hundred steps. When she was close enough for the keeper to see what she was carrying, they went down to meet her. She did not give up both cans. They came the last stretch each carrying one, and set them in the shade with some care, as if there were something breakable inside.

The pump was off. In the quiet the driver heard the bird knocking a beetle against the wall and asked what it was.

The keeper showed her the notch. The bird went away, but not far. The driver sat sideways on the sill, looking out over the pans, and after a time it came back.

“Do you mean to stay another winter?” she asked.

The keeper had an answer. What they had not had, until then, was anyone who could sit long enough to hear all of it. The driver could not take her truck anywhere. She set her gloves on her knees and listened.

That night the power did nothing about the cold. There were eggs under the lip of the dry pool, and the lizards had gone down between the stones. In the pump house the driver slept on the bed and the keeper slept on the floor, where they had often slept in summer when the bolts above the bed were too hot to touch. The two full cans stood just inside the door.

Before lying down, the keeper took the cup from the wall and brought it in for the driver to drink from. In the morning they forgot to put it back. The pale animal crossed the place where it had stood, went down behind the broad face of a stone, and found what the bird had left.
