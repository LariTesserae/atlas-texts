The power came in a week when the rain could be seen every afternoon and did not come nearer. By the fourth day the minnows in the warmest hollow had stopped facing one way. There was no longer any current to face into.

The heron came early. It took three fish from the hollow, then stood a while with its bill open. One of its toes would no longer straighten. On soft ground this made little difference; on the stone the bird sometimes put its foot down twice to find a place for it.

Along the upper edge of the shelf there was a seam the rain had been working at for a very long time. It ran from the hollow toward the sedges and stopped a little short of them. The stone at that end was thin enough to let light through if there had been any way to get underneath and look.

The power took away a piece of stone no bigger than a crab’s door.

Water came out of the sedges. It took some time to get across the dry part of the seam. It went darkly at first, with no shine on it, and twice it seemed to stop. Then a little fragment of a leaf went by, and after that there was water enough to carry the reflection of the sky.

The heron stooped at the mouth of the seam and took another fish. By evening the rest had found their way into the sedges. The bird went there too, but the stems were close and it could not see where to put its bad foot. It went back to the shelf and caught a crab.

In the wet weeks that followed there was no telling that anything had been done. Fish crossed the stone wherever they liked. Later, when the shelf showed again, a rail chick stood in the opening with water running just over its toes. It pecked at something, missed, and put its whole head down to look.

The power had not known about the chick. It stayed to watch.
