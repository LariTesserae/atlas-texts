The power came down on a Sunday and heard the table being moved. It was a table. There was light, too, though it came from a window that had been bricked up when the reservoir was built. The woman with the cough had set a saucer in it with three green onions growing in water. As the light went along the wall she moved the table after it. This was easier with two people. Lately she had been doing it alone.

What the power did first was to leave the light where it was until the woman had finished her dinner.

It went through the ward. At the far fence it found the new stone coming in under the feet of a man who had stopped to tie his shoe. He finished the knot and went on. By evening there would be stone where he had been standing. There would also be a man going home, but not to a house that anyone at the survey office could put a pin in. The power went with him as far as his door. He had the long key with the blue cotton through it. He took it out of his pocket without looking.

The power could have made the two lengths agree. It walked them both before deciding not to. On one there was a little shop that sharpened scissors, and on the other there was a place in the sun where the delivery driver sat to eat his lunch. Neither could have been kept by making an average of the two.

There was, however, a door at the foot of the stairs below the grocer’s. It had a letter slot and a good brass handle. On one side the woman with the cough had hung her apron on it. On the other it was the cupboard where the grocer kept the mop. The power waited until he had taken the mop out on Monday morning. Then it gave the door a landing on both sides.

The grocer came back with the bucket and stood looking in. The woman was sitting at her table, taking the ends off beans. She looked up.

“Is that for here?” she asked.

“No,” he said. “I’ve got the wrong door.”

He shut it. A little later he knocked. He had remembered the basket, and brought it down in case there was anything of hers in it. She tried on the glove. It was much too big. She asked if she might borrow it anyway, to take a pan out of the oven.

That afternoon Ada heard the chair through the socket, then a knock at the kitchen door. Ruth had come up with a piece of toast folded in a clean tea towel. She wanted to know whether Ada could mend an apron if the woman was wearing it, as she hadn’t another one.

Ada said it depended where the tear was. She took her scissors and her tin downstairs. Halfway down she went back for the button.

The power stayed until dark. There were other doors, and some of them, when it listened, had furniture pushed against them from the inside. It left those alone. At the grocer’s it moved the hook for the mop to a place where the wet end would hang clear of the wall. It could hear Ada below, asking the woman to stand a little nearer the window. The light had gone by then. The woman got up and turned on a lamp.
