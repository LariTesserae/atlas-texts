The power stayed beside the print until the heron lifted its foot. The holes its toes had made filled first. Then the whole print shone, though there was not enough water in it to cover the calf’s smallest toenail.

It followed the mechanical note away from the plain. Beyond the trees a pump stood on a concrete slab. In the room beside it a man slept on a narrow bed with his work trousers folded under his head. The pump supplied a tank for the houses farther up the road. When the tank was full, a valve was supposed to close. It had not closed in months. The overflow ran down the back of the tank and into a ditch, where a pale crust marked how far it got before the ground took it.

The power could have stopped the pump. Instead it waited for the man to wake, and when he went out with his cup in the morning, it let him hear the water falling behind the tank. He had been hearing it for months. This time he set the cup down and went around to look.

The repair took him most of the morning. He came back twice for a different wrench. At noon he carried a ladder out to the tank and called to a woman at the nearest house to turn on her tap. She called back that it was running. He stayed on the ladder a little longer, watching the float rise. When it reached the top, there was a click. It was the first time he had heard the machine stop by itself.

Nothing changed on the plain that day. The calf put its foot in the drinking hole again. The mongoose caught a frog, and one of her young tried to take it from her before she had killed it. She put a paw on its head and went on eating.

Weeks later the river rose in country too far away for the rain to be heard. The power went ahead of the water along the gray roots. At the mongoose’s den it paused. The hole went farther back than the entrance suggested, but not uphill. In a good flood it would fill to the roof.

There was a loose piece of bark on the bank above it. The power turned it over.

The mongoose came out to the sound and found a beetle running for the grass. She caught it, then searched under the bark and found two more. Her young followed her up. The one that liked to dig at the bank stayed behind until she came back to see what it was doing. By then there was water across the mouth of the den. She took it by the scruff and carried it up past the bark to a place among the thorn roots. In the morning she went down again to look for the other beetles.

The flood was not deep everywhere. In some places it did little more than darken the clay. The fallen tree held on the wet side until evening, then lifted at one end and settled a little way downstream. A strip of sound wood broke off. The gecko rode it as far as a fig and went up the trunk without stopping.

After dark the spadefoot came out. It sat for a while beside the plug it had pushed up, with clay on its back. Then it went down into water that would last long enough. The power heard it call and, though it knew where the toad was, could not have told by the sound alone. From the next pool another answered. There was a long interval before either called again.
