The power came in winter, when the black water was low enough to show where the red roots went into the rock. It sat on the rim with its feet in the ferns. Before long its trousers were wet to the knee.

It could have looked through the bottom of the pool. Instead it watched what came up.

The little creatures among the roots fed even in winter. There were fewer eggs to be had, and sometimes one of them would spend a long while cleaning a frond on which there was nothing at all. The oldest, the one with the loose skin at the throat, had taken to going to the pointed shelf. It would put its mouth into the fall and drink. Seen from the far bank, it lay on the rock opening and closing its mouth in the dry air.

One evening it came to the shelf and did not drink. It edged forward until its head was clear of the stone. Then it drew back. It did this three times.

There was a drop of less than a hand’s breadth from the shelf to the first of the loose slabs. In summer the creature had crossed it. You could tell by the small dark grains it had left on a stone farther up, where it had lain in the sun. Now when it tried to lower itself, its foreparts trembled.

The power took one of the fallen pieces from the foot of the wall. It was a thin piece, with a white corner that fitted a white notch overhead. The power laid it between the shelf and the slab, rough side up, and went back to the rim.

For two days nothing used it but a shrew. On the third day the old creature came out and lay on it with its throat in the water and the rest of its body dry. It stayed that way through the knocking and the hiss. When the spots opened along its sides, the one nearest its head opened last.

It did not go up to the sunning stone. It could have, from there. It went back among the roots.

That night a wren came to the low branch. It called during the turning, too early for an answer, and then again into the hiss. There was ice on the outer branches and the little branch dipped each time the bird shifted its feet.

The power knew where the other wren was. It was down among the junipers, prying at a fold of bark. At the first call it had stopped to listen. At the second it had gone on prying. There was something in the fold it wanted.

So the power waited with the bird on the branch. After a while the turning slowed, and from somewhere among the roots came the little sound of a note begun and left unfinished. The wren bent forward. A moment later there was a stir in the ferns behind it, and it moved along the branch to make room.

By morning the thin stone had settled a little under its own weight. The power put two fingers to it and felt that it would hold. Then it went away.

In the spring a fern grew up through the gap underneath. By midsummer its fronds covered the stone so completely that you could stand by the pool all afternoon and never see what the old creature was crossing on. You could see it pause halfway, though, where the sun came through. Sometimes a smaller one had to go around it.
