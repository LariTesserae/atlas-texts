The power first took notice of them on an evening when the young one went down ahead of its mother. It got as far as the pale nick and waited there, looking back. The old female was still at the gutter. She had begun to take longer over a drink.

There was nothing the matter with the nick. It would be there long after both of them were gone. But the tuft below it, which had held through all the evenings of the young one's education, had come loose at the root. The old female would have known by the way it lay against the rock. From where the young one stood it looked just as it had the night before.

The power could have fastened the tuft back. Instead it let a little of the seep run behind it. The tuft tipped, hung by a thread of root, and fell. The young one watched it go. Then it put its nose to the wet place and found, a little to the left, a seam the old female had never used.

By the time she came to the lip it was feeding below. She went down the old way as far as the nick, smelled the raw earth where the tuft had been, and stood for a while with her belly against the rock. From the grass below came the small noise the young one used to make when it could not follow her. It made it twice. Then she took the seam.

In the autumn the old female stopped going down. The young one, which no longer walked under her and could no longer have fitted there, went on using the seam. Sometimes on its way back it found her just outside the shelter and lay beside her until the water came up over the lip. Neither of them liked being wet when there was no need for it. They would go in then, the younger one first, and take up nearly all the underside of the slab.

On the last night of the old female's life the temperature dropped far enough to freeze the seep. The power did not keep the frost off the meadow. It kept a bead of water liquid at the end of a root that hung down beside her head. When she woke she put out her yellow tongue and took it. A little later there was another bead. She took that one too.

In the morning the younger animal came out late. It stood at the gutter and put one foot in, as she had done, but there was ice under the foot and it drew it back. It drank farther up, where the water was still moving. Then it went back under the slab. For three days it slept there with her. On the fourth it slept at the other end, where the fallen stones let the sun in for an hour before noon.

By spring there were two places to get down from the nick. In the old place a saxifrage had taken hold in the earth the tuft left behind. The younger animal left the flowers alone that year. It had acquired a taste for the stalks, but only after the flowers were gone.
