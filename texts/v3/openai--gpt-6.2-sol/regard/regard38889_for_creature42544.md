The power came before sunrise and stood where the girl had taken her hands off the bowl. It put one hand on either side of the fissure and felt how the shelf was held.

There was still a great deal of stone under the seven houses. Most of it was no longer bearing their weight. What held them was a ridge deep in the mountain, narrower at one end than the stair on which the woman cooked. At that end it was beginning to crumble.

The power could have closed the mountain. It would have had to close the black-feet’s winter chambers with it, and the spaces in which the old roots were putting down their fine new hairs. Instead it went down to the ridge and held it through the morning.

Just after breakfast, the threshold cutter heard a bowl scrape. He looked beside him, thinking his granddaughter had come back for hers. The bowl was where she had left it, on the half of the step that belonged to his house. As he watched, it moved the width of a grain of meal.

He lifted it. Then he called across the lane for someone to help him up.

By noon the people from the seven houses had brought out their bedding and their sealed jars. Someone fetched the woman’s stove, which had to be passed up through the leaves the same way she passed supper. The wall sleepers were harder. The woman stood beside the public stair and showed people where to put their hands. They lifted each occupied stone with the stones above and below it, so that what slept in the space between would not have the roof taken off its bed.

They could not get them all. Behind the stove there was a face the woman had known since she was a girl. When they took away the first course of stone, the eyes opened and the face drew back. She waited on the stair with her hands dusty to the wrists. Her daughter came down for her twice. The second time she went.

In the cool hour the black-feet came up out of the sunken square. Some took the lane and some went by the roots. The girl lay on her stomach on the roof and held a leaf aside until the last one had found the crossing. Then she went to look for her grandfather. He was asleep on somebody else’s bedding, with his feet level.

Under the shelf the power eased its hand away from the narrow end of the ridge. It let that end settle first, then took the weight farther in. The seven houses went down together. Those watching from the rim saw the roofs dip, and then saw a wall from the inside that they had only ever seen from the lane. Below the summit the shelf came to rest against the mountain. The sound reached the plain after the dust did.

For a while people said there had been no wind that day. Later they remembered the dust in their teeth and the leaves standing straight out from the ledges.

The next morning the woman went to the rim with a cup. She could see the wall behind her stove, tilted far enough back that rain, if there had been rain, would have fallen on the thick ends of the stones. A long white belly passed over one of them and stopped. The animal put out its head. There was a moth under the lip of the old stair, and beside it, just touching, another.

The woman stayed to see which one it would take. By the time she went home the water in her cup was warm.
