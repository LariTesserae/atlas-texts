The first thing the power did was wait through a change of shift. It had taken the attendance of the woman for one of the conditions of the place, like the heat or the height of the water. Then the Sunday woman came down with her stool, and the other woman went up the steps carrying an empty lunch tin. At the grating she stopped to let a man with a sack of onions go first. The power had not known there was anyone waiting for her at home.

It watched the fish with the torn tail. It could have made the tail whole. There would have been no scar and no interval of healing. The fish would have gone to stop in the run from the drain and traveled half an inch farther than it meant to.

The power watched it miss three things and catch a fourth. When the big fish came, it went into the shade. Later, when the big fish was gone, it stayed there for some time. There was food in that part of the water too, though less of it came by.

At the back of the pump house, where nobody stood unless they were taking the cover off a motor, an old vent had been painted shut. The power lifted one flake of paint. The next time a tram passed, the lower edge of the vent came free. It opened no wider than the thickness of the attendant’s book, but now the fan had somewhere to send the air.

For several days the attendant thought one of the motors was running cooler. She put her hand on both housings and found them as hot as ever. On Sunday her relief brought down two plums in a paper bag. They ate them by the rail before the attendant went home. Neither had to raise her voice quite as much.

The power did one other thing. In the tiled wall, just above the water, it loosened a grain of sand. The fall of the grain made no sound the woman could hear. Water from the drain began to follow a second course along the grout. Most of it still fell where it had before, but a little collected in the hollow left by the grain. A wasp could stand on the tile and drink there. The first wasp to find it went to the root of the fern twice more that morning.

Toward the end of summer the attendant saw two fish make the extra stroke. They were under the step together, and one was so small she couldn’t see what was wrong with its tail, or whether anything was. She leaned forward until the chair creaked and both fish went out of sight.

At the end of the shift she checked the gauge and wrote down the usual number. She had a little space left before the bottom of the page. She thought of putting something there about the two fish, but she would have had to say what she had seen, and she wasn’t sure yet. She left the space for tomorrow’s reading.
