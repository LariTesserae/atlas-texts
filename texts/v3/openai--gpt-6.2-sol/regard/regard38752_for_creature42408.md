The power had come prepared to mend the wing. It had not understood about the table.

It stayed through supper. Twice someone caught a knee on the corner. The second time, a child started to push the table back and was stopped by a hand laid flat on the boards. Nobody said anything. Overhead, the latch went past with its uneven step, and a little red powder fell into a bowl that had already been emptied.

The wing hurt. Not all the time, and not where you would expect. The turned joint itself had gone nearly numb. It was the small muscles along the breast that hurt, especially after the latch had crossed a room in the air. On those nights it hung awake long after the others had tucked their heads away.

The power took away the pain that night. In the morning the latch stretched the wing, then folded it. A little later it stretched it again. There was no one in the passage to see.

On the flats a hunter lay with one ear flat and the other half raised. Beneath a leaf, a grazer no longer than a finger was eating a crescent out of the violet edge. The power could feel the hunger in both of them. It stayed until the mother came and put her foot down. At the deepest part of the afternoon note the hunter ran, and the power did not turn it aside. It had no wish to make the hunter's young wait at the mouth of their run for a soft sound that would not come.

There was a seam under the old quarter which the repairers did not know about. It ran beneath the shared passage and ended in a place where the wood had folded back on itself. The two sides were still alive, but the fold held them just too far apart. Every year the contraction drew them a little farther. In time the passage would have pulled crooked, and the people would have shaved the doors to fit until there was nothing left to shave.

The power went down into the fold. There was no room there for stools, and nothing to brace against except the two sides of the break. It drew them together and held them.

Toward morning Eda woke and put her hand against the wall. Somewhere along the rib her brother was up early, dragging a stool across his floor. Then she felt a small warmth pass under her palm. She kept her hand there until it was gone.

That evening the young latch dropped from the rib and missed its hold. It went on falling for almost the height of the stairs before its wings caught the air. It came down badly on the back of a chair and sat there, gripping the wood, while the people at the table made an effort not to look at it.

The latch with the turned wing went out to its place on the wall. The nearest flower did not open. One much farther off did, low on a shelf where the last of the dust was moving away. The latch leaned out to watch it. When it came back in, it stopped at the narrowest part of the passage and stretched its wing into the room. The child at the corner drew in both knees to let it finish.
