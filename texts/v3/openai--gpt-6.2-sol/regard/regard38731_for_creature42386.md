The power came while the mark was still wet. It had heard the middle grub chewing. From farther off it had mistaken the sound for something being mended.

It stayed through a wash day. It learned that the stair came down hardest not under the woman with the two full pails, but under a boy who carried nothing and liked to take the last three steps at once. It learned that the spider could go hungry for a long time, and had. It followed the centipede to the back of its slit, where it found the empty skin the animal had taken most of a night to get out of. The skin had split behind the head. Even the last pair of legs had come away whole.

At dusk the keeper came out with a little water left in her pail. Her right hand hurt. She set the pail down to change hands, and the power held the stair up while she did it.

She noticed. She had been setting pails on that tread for eleven years. It had given a little even before it split. She picked the pail up and set it down again. The handle fell against the rim. Beneath it there was no answering creak.

Then the power let the stair go. The keeper saw where it moved against the one above it. She went back for her lamp.

In the morning she brought a fork of willow from the heap by the well. One arm of the fork was too long. She pared it sitting on the step above, catching the shavings in her apron because they were good for starting the washhouse fire. Twice she tried the fork against the underside of the tread. The first time she had it over the split and would have closed the room altogether. But the ends rocked there. She turned it in her hand and set it farther out, where one arm could rest against the old sill and the other take the tread. She struck it home with the heel of her hand.

Her hand hurt after that, too. She dipped it in the warm pail and held it there until somebody called for her.

The room still took water. On the next wash day a sheet of it crossed the floor, but there was space above it, and the long springtail stood on the sedge until it had gone by. The grub went on eating under the boy’s feet. There was no softening of the chip in its jaws; it had to take the chip from the other end.

The root reached the silk four nights later. By then there were young in the check. Three went down the new way before morning. One went back up. The centipede found the root that evening and climbed it as far as the wet mark, which had widened in the meantime and was no longer distinguishable from the damp of the wood. It caught one of the young there. The others stayed in the check until air coming up from the lane drew their lines out past the root. Not all the lines caught.

By spring the fork had put out a white root of its own. It entered the room from the open end. The keeper would have to cut it back in a year or two if she wanted to get the fork out. For now she could set down a full pail without leaning her knee against it.

The power had meant to leave when the stair was braced. It stayed until the first hard rain. Water filled the lower lane and rose through the slit where the centipede lived. The animal came out backward, its last legs sweeping the floor until they met the new willow. It went up the fork and waited on the underside of the tread. In the old room there would not have been height enough. Its feet were in the water, but the long openings along its sides were clear.

Above it, in the dark washhouse, the keeper had left both pails upside down to dry.
