The power came on a Tuesday, while Pell was taking pins out of a hem. It had to knock twice. The first time a tram went through.

Pell’s daughter got up to answer. There was a woman on the landing holding a door. It was an ordinary door with a brass kick plate, considerably wider than the landing. The woman had it balanced on one foot.

“Is that for us?” the girl asked.

“It needn’t be,” said the woman. “Would you help me put it down?”

They rested it against the wall. The girl took her hand off first. Nothing happened to the door, which was a relief; she had been holding it by the knob and it had felt at one point as if there were weather on the other side.

The woman went away without it. By supper somebody had put a notice on it about keeping the landing clear.

In the morning the woman took the door to the housing office, where she asked for the plan of the end block. The clerk showed her the basement, the lift shaft, and the void behind the stairs.

“What’s the void for?” she asked.

“It’s what we measure the rest from.”

“Can you get into it?”

“If you could get into it, it would be on the other sheet.”

The woman asked to borrow a pencil. In the void she drew a room. It ran off the edge of the plan, over the clerk’s blotter and down onto her own cuff. Where it ran off her cuff was her business. It was her own room she was giving, not the void. When she gave the pencil back there was still a void on the plan, exactly the size it had been before.

The clerk got out the book of allocations. He’d lived for three years with his refrigerator in his bedroom. He could think of things to do with a room before he’d seen it.

“Who’s it to go to?”

“Put down a key at this desk,” the woman said. “Put down another at the school. If you run out of keys, have some cut.”

He wanted to know who would pay for the light, which was how the conversation got to something he could do that morning.

They hung the door under the stairs. On the other side was a long room with a washbasin and a window so high up that nobody would have to agree on what to put in front of it. There was a cupboard with a few mismatched cups. At one end you could hear the transit loop. At the other you could hear the tap if it hadn’t been turned all the way off.

The first week there was an argument about whether children could be in there without an adult. The second week there was an argument about the adult who was always in there. He liked to read the paper and had got into the habit of telling people not to scrape the chairs. Somebody brought felt for the chair legs. Somebody else told him to go home once in a while.

Pell went down to see it with some work in a bag. She stood in the doorway a little while, then went back upstairs and finished the work in her front room. Later she came down again without the bag. Her daughter was there, lying on the floor with one heel propped on the washbasin pipe. Pell nudged the heel with her shoe. The girl moved over.

After a while the bicycle man came in pushing a bicycle. He’d made it out of parts he’d been keeping in his shallow drawer. The back wheel went round without a sound, but something in the front wheel clicked at every turn. He asked the girl if she’d ride it slowly past him so he could hear where the click came from.

She went down to the far end and turned. On the way back he bent his head, listening. The click had stopped. She went past him anyway, a little faster this time, and he let her go.
