The pup calls again. This time the old seal answers. He has been looking down the valley, where the lamps are coming on, and at the sound he turns nearly all the way around.

By the time he reaches her, the warm water has carried her a little farther south. He puts his nose against her side. She lets him do it for the length of one breath and then dives. He dives after her.

The power that has noticed them stays through the night. It follows the two seals for a while, then goes back to the high ground where the fish have hatched. It follows those fish, too. Some are eaten before midnight. Some pass over the first crest and the second. Just before dawn a gate opens on a valley that has been filling slowly all night, and the fish go through a grate into a pipe. On the far side of the pumps there are scales in the water, though the fish were scarcely old enough to have scales.

At the next opening the power holds a little water back. It does this so gently that the number on the gauge at the station takes another six seconds to reach the number it was going to reach. Along the foot of the ridge the current lifts clear of the floor. For those six seconds there is a strip of still water under it. The small fish that reach the strip stay there after the rest have gone.

There is a woman at the station who has been trying to get a look at that gate while it opens. The inspection camera shakes as soon as the flow starts. She has asked for a bracket and been given, twice, a new camera. In the morning she finds six seconds of steady picture. She watches it over her coffee. Then she goes back and watches it again, not looking at the gate this time.

In winter, when a reach is drained to replace a seal on a shaft, the crew leaves a low curb along one foot of the ridge. It costs them half a day. One of the men smooths the top with the back of his shovel because he will have to come down here again, and does not want to catch his boot on it. When the water comes back, a little sand settles behind the curb. By spring there is enough to take the root of a piece of kelp that would otherwise have gone through the pumps.

The old seal finds the place before the kelp does. She comes down along the concrete as she has always done, and the fish she has been following slips over the curb and is gone. She doubles back and puts her nose into the slack water. On the next tide she takes a fish there. On the tide after that she goes hungry.

Her pup is no longer with her. He has gone north to the old shore, where at low water there are stones with dry tops and wet sides. He sleeps badly on them at first. Each time he wakes he raises his head as if the stone might have carried him somewhere.

By the end of the second summer the curb has gathered sand deep enough for a crab to sit in with just its eyes showing. The lamps still come on toward evening. The pumps can still be heard from the old shore if the wind drops. After a gate has opened, when the whole valley seems to be going one way, a loose blade of kelp behind the curb lifts and points the other.
