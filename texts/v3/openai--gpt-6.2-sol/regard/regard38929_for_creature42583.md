The power would come on a day when the marsh was low and look at the town from the bank. It would go down among the houses, where it could no longer see the whole of the place at once. It would take some time there. The sound under the bench, for instance, would concern it until it saw the mason’s mother put both hands on the seat and rock a little, to make the stone knock before she got up.

It would leave the bench alone.

There would be a night later in the year when rain fell on dry ground a long way off. None would fall on the town. Sella would leave the pot out, and her brother, who had come to stay two nights, would go to sleep with his shirt hanging from a peg low enough to reach from his bed.

By then the power would know what happened to water between that dry ground and the marsh. There was a place where it spread out among the roots of dead reeds and went on slowly, and another place where it came all together again. It could not turn the water away without sending it somewhere else. It would follow it as far as the dead reeds, then go back to Sella’s door.

In the dark it would put a little water against her heel.

She would draw her foot in under the blanket. The power would wait. When she put it out again, it would wet her heel a second time.

Sella would get up crossly and find the stars out. She would stand in the doorway for a while, testing the stone with her bare foot, and then go down to the outlet, where the boy had left a stick pushed into the mud to mark how far out he had seen the eel. The stick would be gone. She would fetch her brother. He would bring the lamp, and by the time they came down together there would be water coming through the hole steadily enough to bend the flame.

They would wake the houses nearest the outlet first. The young man with the quiet threshold would go back twice for things, the second time for his neighbor’s shoes, which she had not thought to ask him for. The mason and his mother would have a quarrel about whether she could walk to the bank without him. She could, but in the end she would let him carry one end of her bench. By daylight there would be five people sitting on it.

Water would get into the walls. The girl would find her blue wool in a clot of rushes halfway to the landing, and the spider would spend the morning on the underside of a bowl. There would be a great deal of work once the marsh went down.

Sella’s brother would stay to do some of it. When he came to the groove at her door, he would kneel with his chisel across his thighs and ask her, as he always did, whether she wanted it put right.

She would ask him to show her where he had gone too deep. He would lay a finger in the join, and she would lay hers beside it. Then she would bring out the pot and pour a little water for him, so that he could show her what he had meant it to do.
