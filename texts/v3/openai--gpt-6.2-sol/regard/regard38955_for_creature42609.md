The power did not stop the water to make itself heard. There were things in the valley that had never lived in still water.

While Eda was out with the boat, it went into her room. It did not mind the damp. There was a cup on the table with a second cup upside down over it, and a book propped open on a block of wood to keep its pages off the table. The book was a record of the water. The early entries were in several hands. In Eda’s hand there were seven years of figures, with such notes as *stake loose* or *read from boat*. Once, three winters ago, she had written *did not go out*. The next day’s reading was in its proper place. Nobody had asked about the missing one.

The power could have dried the room. Instead it noticed that when Eda sat with her back against the wall to take off her boots, a drop fell from the ceiling onto the back of her neck. There was a little run of mineral on the ceiling, ending directly over that spot. It lengthened the run by the width of a fingernail. The drop gathered, traveled the extra distance, and fell behind the bed.

Then it waited for her at the landing.

Eda tied up, took the spanner out of her coat pocket, and remembered that she had meant to tighten the hinge on the lamp box before coming home. She set the spanner on the step. The power kept it from rolling into the water. It could have done that without her knowing, but when she reached for it again she felt, quite distinctly, that someone had put a finger on the other end.

“Hold that a minute,” she said.

She went up for the lamp box key. When she came back the spanner was where she had left it. She sat on the step and tightened the hinge.

It had been a long time since anyone had come across without needing a reading or a tool. She stayed on the step afterward. She did not explain the valley. There was not much to see just then except a snail going over the knot in the boat rope. She moved the lamp a little so they could both see it.

The power stayed until the snail was over. Then it stayed a while longer.

In the morning Eda took a stake out to the bracket. She laid it across the boat and sounded the bank with the oar. There was firm clay enough to take it on the near side, but she would have had to drive it through the passage underneath. She went a little farther and found another place. On the board that night she entered the reading and added, because whoever came after her would want to know, *Stake set one boat length east of iron. Water runs under bank.*

On the pump side somebody read the note and drew a short blue line on the survey. It was the first mark on the survey for water that did not need to be taken away.
