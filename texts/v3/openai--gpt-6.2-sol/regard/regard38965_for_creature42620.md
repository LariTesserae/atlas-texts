The power came to the plateau in the dry hour before noon, when the shallower pans were already warm and the rain was still a long way off. It went first to the rim, where there was nothing to prevent it from seeing all the way down. Then it went under the return bridge and stayed through two changes of the pumps.

It could have kept the western dishes full. It watched one empty and saw the little animals come out of the joints to eat what had been left on the stone. It watched a four-footed fisher take one of them. Farther along, in water no deeper than a wet footprint, a shell animal stopped and let its young down. The power let the dish empty.

At the pumping works, Iven’s nephew was lying on his side with his ear against a pipe. There was a sound he had been trying to hear again since Tuesday. Every time he asked someone to listen, it stopped. He was beginning to think it was the sound of his own sleeve against the pipe.

The power waited until he got up to go. Then it held back one drop at the lip of the gallery rail.

In the little interval where the drop should have fallen, he heard the knock. It came twice, and the second time he had his hand on the pipe and could feel it. He fetched the woman who had taught him to renew the pads. She put down her tea and came without asking whether he was sure.

They found a footing that had gone soft at one corner. By the time the western pans began to drain they had the housing up on jacks. The other pump took the load, and under the bridge there was a longer wait than usual for the cool water. Some of the animals let go before it came. The apprentice, sent out to see that the return was clear, watched them catch hold again farther down. He stayed until the pulse went through. Then he went back in and drank the tea his teacher had forgotten. It was still warm.

That evening Iven had to read the cabinet at the end of the laundry gallery. She ran the spoon under the door and brought out an old animal that stood so high on its legs she could see the smaller one beneath it. She held the door open with her hip and waited. The small one went as far as the toe of her shoe, tried the leather, and went back.

The power had watched Iven do this work all afternoon. It knew how her hand hurt on the last turn of the key. It knew, too, that she sometimes sat in her father’s chair after supper simply because she had not yet decided to get up. It did not mistake that for loneliness. But there were evenings when she heard her sister set the second chair out and wished, without quite being willing to go around and say so, that it was meant for her.

When Iven shut the cabinet, there was a scrape on the gallery above. Her sister leaned over the rail.

“Have you eaten?”

Iven had. She went up anyway.

The power stayed by the cabinet a little longer. The old animal had found a thread of water coming out beside a bolt. It stood over it while the small one drank, or fed, or did some other thing for which there was no word in the town. Upstairs, Iven laughed with her mouth full and her sister told her to mind the mending.

The held drop fell. By then there was rain on the rail a mile to the west, and in a few minutes there would be rain here.
