The first thing the power did was keep the far fountain running until morning.

There was a washer in it that had worn through. Ordinarily the water would have gone on running into the wall, which was warm enough that nobody would have noticed the damp for several days. The power held the washer together until the man from across the reservoir came for his drink. When he turned the handle, a little piece of leather came out of the spout and landed in his glass.

He took it to Esta. She knew where there were more washers. What she did not know was how to get the cover off the fountain without cracking it. The man said there was a woman in his district who had done that work. He went back for her, and the two of them returned after dark.

Esta carried her lamp down to the passage. It was the first time in years she had seen the bed from that end of the hall without a light on it. She had expected it to disappear altogether. Instead she could see the white edge of the sheet, which she had tucked under the mattress that morning.

The woman got the cover off. Underneath it, scratched into the iron, were the sizes of the washers, and beside the sizes was a name. The man knew the name. It belonged to somebody who had lived in his house before him. There was a drawer in the kitchen that would not open all the way, and now he wondered whether there might be something behind it.

There was. There were three washers, a bent spoon, and a photograph so badly stuck to the bottom of the drawer that he left it there.

After the fountain was mended, the woman asked Esta to bring the lamp outside. There was a place at the turn where a lamp had once hung. The hook was still good. If you hung a lamp there, you could see it from the receiving yard and from both fountains, though not from the scale.

Esta tried it for a week. She found she did not mind coming back to a dark bed, provided she could hear the scale. The man began bringing his glasses in a little sack so that he had a hand free for the lamp on the nights she wanted it brought back.

The power did not lower the reservoir or cool the city. It went down once to where the discharge broke apart in the air and watched the drops rise past one another. It could have made the country below visible. There were orchards down there, and long sheds roofed in tin, and a station where people stood with their coats over their arms waiting for the evening train. Most of the people in the city had seen those things. Some had come up the incline to get away from them.

In the inspector’s court, the pool grew shallow. The boy’s grandmother had died that winter, and he had stopped hanging the shirt out. He still came to look at the groove in the rafter. As the water withdrew, the groove was the last part of the roof he could see.

The power could have kept it there. It waited beside him through a long afternoon, while the water shrank to a patch no larger than the palm of his hand. Toward evening he went indoors. He came back with a ruler and knelt down, but by then there was nothing to measure. He sat for a while with the ruler across his knees.

Rain came that night. In the morning the pool showed the open sky and, along one edge, the boy’s face. He moved back and forth until he found the place where the rafter had been. Then he put the ruler down on the paving and made two little scratches in the salt, one at either end of it. The scratches were too close together. He knew that, but he left them.

Years later, when the scale gave its usual tap, Esta woke and found that she could not get her shoes on. One foot had swollen in the night. She sat on the edge of the bed with the shoes in her lap, thinking how far it was to the passage and how foolish it would be to start out barefoot.

At the turn, the man set his two glasses on the tiles. He filled one and put it down inside the other. Then he looked up at the lamp. Esta had trimmed the wick the night before, but one corner of it was longer than the other, and the flame leaned out beyond the glass.

He went to tell her about it. When she heard his steps between the pits, she called to him to bring a chair. He brought the chair and went back for the water. By the time he returned she had managed to get one shoe on.

He set the glasses beside her on the platform. The beam lifted and touched its stop. Neither of them moved to take the glasses off.
