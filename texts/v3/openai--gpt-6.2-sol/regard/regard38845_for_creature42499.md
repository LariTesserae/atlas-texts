The power came in August, while the keeper was raising the path. It watched him move a block a hand’s breadth from the place where the old one had been. There was nothing under the old one that day. He moved it all the same.

It stayed through the winter. Under the ice, the pool was smaller than it had looked in summer. What had seemed to be its bottom was mostly the stems of other years, with water between them. In places there was just enough room for a newt to turn. The power could have lifted the whole mat and laid it down somewhere else. But there were newts in the mat, too, and things smaller than newts that would spend their whole lives there.

East of the pool, where the ground rose a little, two seams in the rock met under a patch of moss. Water entered one seam and came out of the other. The power followed it down. There was a shallow basin under the moss, with a crack in its lower lip no wider than the edge of a leaf.

During the thaw it set a blue flake of stone into that crack.

For most of the spring there was nothing to see. Water went on entering the upper seam. Some escaped around the flake, but not so fast as it came in. When the moss lifted, it did not break all at once. A piece the size of a glove came free on one side and floated there, still attached on the other. On a clear day you could see blue rock through the opening.

The first summer there was nothing in the new water for a female to fold. In the second, a few soft leaves came out from under the floating edge. A female spent part of an afternoon there. Twice she took hold of a leaf that would not bend far enough and let it go. The third leaf was so small that when she had finished with it there was scarcely any green showing outside the fold.

The old pool went on filling. In time the pipits could walk across a place where the keeper remembered seeing open water. Below their feet, the stems were wet all year. The newts still went in.

The keeper noticed the new pool one August when a cloud took the glare off it. He stood on the grating with a block under his arm and watched a larva rise from the blue bottom. It hung in the water a moment, working its gills, then went back down. He had crossed there in both directions all morning. Once the sun came out he could not find the place again.
