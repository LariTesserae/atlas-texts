The youngest felt the curb move after the vibration from the shutter had passed. It put up a second foot. The two feet told it different things, and it was too young to know which to believe.

What had drawn near the plain did not at first know either. It followed the weight of the curb down through the paving. Three layers below the hollow, a brown strand crossed the corner of an old square. The weight bore on the strand just where it was beginning to twist. If the curb were lifted, the twist would slip back. If the curb settled another grain’s breadth, the twist would hold, but there would no longer be room for the mother to turn around.

There was a small square of pavement farther in, scarcely wider than her mouth. It had once been the top of a step. Nothing rested on it now. The power let the square sink the depth of the step it had been.

The youngest took both feet down. Its mother woke when it crawled out from under her chest. She put a foot into the new space, then another, and turned. It was the first time that season she had been able to face the opening without going out into the cold.

By noon the curb had settled. Far below it, the soft part of the twist began its slow passage toward a shutter on the far side of the ridge. The power followed it for a little while. There were two living strands at the shutter, and it could not yet tell which would get there first. It left them to arrive.

The new step caught dust. For several mornings the mother pushed the dust out as she had always done. Then one morning she left it. The youngest had found that it could lie on the little bank with its mouth clear of the floor and all six feet down. From there it could hear the leaves turning even when its mother was scraping at the entrance. On the day it first went out alone, it waited for the twelfth click. There were only eleven: the shortest stalk had grown enough for its leaf to turn without touching the curb.

It went out a little before noon, on the strength of what it could hear.
