The power came down with the man who had the plans. He had come back for a folding rule he’d left on the bench. The lift was going up empty and he put his hand out to catch the gate.

The power gave him time to miss it.

He stood with the rule in his hand and watched the underside of the lift go up past the first landing. Then he heard the drop. He went over and looked into the cable sleeve, as if he might see the other end of it. When he straightened up he put his heel on the tile.

“Don’t move,” Ada said.

She came down the stair and showed him which way to take his foot off. The tile settled on its washer. Under the edge was a line of green no thicker than a thread.

“Is that what you wanted the drain for?” he asked.

“I want the drain so nobody slips with a box of fish.”

He opened his plans on the bench. The paper rolled itself up twice before Ada fetched her cup to hold down one corner. He showed her where the landing would join the old floor. She showed him the pale rim in the dust where the water went when there was more of it than usual. From the drawing he had thought the gutter ran downhill the other way.

The power stayed while they went to find a level. It could have told them which way the gutter ran, and how much water would come down the sleeve in the wettest week of the year. But Ada had been wanting a level for some time. There were two other things she meant to check with it.

In the end the landing was two inches higher than it had been on the drawing. A mason made a shallow channel at its foot and left a little pocket of the old floor against the wall. He wanted to bed the tile properly. Ada showed him the frond and he cut a notch in the underside instead. It took him three tries. The first notch was too shallow and the second was in the wrong place. By then he had missed his lift too, though the power had nothing to do with that.

When the stair opened, the street was colder. Ada could eat a pear on the bench again, but now there was generally somebody sitting on it. The fish porters used it to change their boots. They brought a smell with them that stayed after they’d gone, not quite the smell Ada had been looking forward to, though she liked it well enough.

A little light came down the stair in the morning. It reached the channel but not the ferns. For a while the power considered what to do about that. Then one of the porters, who couldn’t see to tie his boots, asked why the lamp at the foot of the stair was always off. The market paid to have it put on a separate switch.

On the first morning the lamp was left burning, Ada went back down to turn it off. She had her hand on the switch when she heard someone coming down the new stair, slowly, feeling for each tread. She took her hand away. By the time the woman reached the bottom Ada had gone up in the lift, with her cup wrapped in a clean handkerchief. She’d decided she wanted it at home for a day.

The woman sat on the bench to do up a buckle. In the quiet after she’d finished she heard water strike the floor. She looked down, waited, and saw where it fell. Then she picked up her bag and went to work.
