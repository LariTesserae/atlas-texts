The first thing the power did was take its weight off the mountain. It had come to the summit in the ordinary way, putting a foot down wherever there was room. At the seam it felt a root below the drum begin to thicken for it. It took its foot away. The thickening stopped halfway around the root and remained there, a little ridge no wider than a fingerprint.

It could have turned the wheel. There was nothing wrong with the bearings that it could not have put right. It could even have made the air come the way it used to, striking the rough faces of the leaves first. But under the ledge the silver edges were trying an unusually long rest. The power waited through it. The coarse sound resumed a stroke at a time, farther back in the stone than before.

Down on the plain there was a road that had once led to the mountain. It was hard to pick out now, though in two places the salt lay paler over the ruts. The power followed it to a house with a short flight of steps and no roof. Beneath the bottom step lay the thing the man had set against the rail. It was a tin cup with a flattened handle. He had put it there to catch water from a crack in the step, in the years when water still came down it.

The power left the cup where it was. There was a beetle under it.

It went back in the cold part of the night. The broken shelf was drawing in. At the outermost pair of roots, the powder had packed hard, and one blunt end was taking weight before the end beside it could let go. There was a small living crease in the root where this had happened on other nights. The power took out a grain. It waited, then took out another. The root eased against its neighbor. For the rest of the night the power did nothing. Once it thought a third grain would have to come out, but toward morning the root turned a fraction on its own and the grain fell.

At the seam the power paused. A flake of stone had come loose at breast height. It would fall soon, and when it fell it would lodge crosswise in the narrowest part of the entrance. The power lifted it out and laid it flat on the summit, where the wind could not get under it.

The next visitor still had to take off her pack. She put it down on the bare crescent and knelt beside it, with one knee on the webbing of a strap. The upper note went out. After a little while she moved her knee, pulled the strap free, and sat back on her heels.

Beneath the ledge a leaf had begun to turn. It finished turning after she was gone. There was no weight left on the crescent by then, but the leaf stayed as it was until the stone cooled. In the morning there was room beside it for a tip that had been rolled tight for a very long time. It opened partway. The rest took another night.
