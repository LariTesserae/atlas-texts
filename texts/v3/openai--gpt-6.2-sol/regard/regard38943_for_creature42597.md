The next year the pumps were to be stopped overnight. There was a coupling to replace, and the new one had come in a crate with one corner dark from rain. The man doing the work set the crate on the drain cover while he went back for his tools.

The power had been there through several of the shorter stoppages. It knew how long the lowest seam would hold, and that the larva could live in its tube after the surface of the mud had lost its shine. Overnight was another matter. The clay was already warm to a depth the marsh would not cool by morning.

It did not keep the pumps running. The man had to put his hand between the two halves of the coupling.

At the marshward end, where the oldest rush had split, there was a dead blade caught in the bend of the youngest stem. The power drew the blade down until one end lay in the marsh and the other rested against the lowest shelf. There was a crease along the blade. Water moved up the crease, darkening it by degrees. It took most of the afternoon to reach the clay.

The upper pools dried as they usually did. The springtails came down to the last seam, where there was room for them along the bank but not much room to pass. The larva worked in its tube shortly after dark. A cloud came out under the bridge and went nowhere for a long time. At the foot of the shelf the end of the blade made a wet mark no wider than the blade itself.

In the morning the man turned each half of the coupling by hand before he put the guard back on. When the pumps started, the first trembling of the drain cover was from one pump alone. Then the other came up to speed. Water rose over the dead blade and lifted it out of the rush. By noon it was lying flat in the marsh.

The lowest shelf had a white rim like the others, except for a short break at the marshward end. It could be seen from the drain cover for about a week. Then the clear skin grew over it, and a bubble formed in the break and stayed there until late in the afternoon.
