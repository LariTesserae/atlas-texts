The power came to the south stair while the woman was pressing the clay into place. It watched her leave the tip of the shoot uncovered. It had been going to join the two stairways.

There was no great difficulty in joining them. The lower flight had a sound root, and the upper one could be made to put out a stem as thick as a person’s leg. But the power went down to look before it did anything. At the foot of the older stairs an eel had made its bed in the turn of the rail. A little farther in, a shelf projected from a wall that had grown across its own doorway. There were eggs on the underside of the shelf. On top of it, in a shallow hollow, lay a white stone no bigger than a tooth.

The power went back up without touching anything.

In the morning the woman found that the shoot had leaned out over the stair. It would crowd the tread if she let it go on. She put her thumb against it to see how readily it would bend. Then she noticed that she could see the lower flight.

It was the wrong time of year. There had been a storm two days before, and the surf was still loud enough to feel through the tread. All along the south face the water was full of grit. Only here, in a strip scarcely wider than the stairs, could she see down.

She went for her grandson. He had just set out his clay and wanted to know if there was time to cover it. She said she thought so. She waited while he fitted the lid to the bowl and put a pebble on it to keep it from lifting.

At the last step he took his usual place and offered her his shoulder.

“No,” she said. “Come beside me. There’s something I want to show you.”

She pointed out the shelf. He looked first at a projecting root, then at a pale place on the wall. She had him put his head close to hers so she could sight along his arm. Even then it took him a while.

“That little thing?”

“Yes,” she said. In her memory it had been wide enough to set a supper on.

She told him her mother used to keep salt there. She told him that when she was small she took the smallest stone and licked it whenever she passed, and always put it back with the wet side down. Her mother had known. The woman had not understood this until she had a child of her own.

The boy asked if she wanted him to go down and get the stone.

She thought about it. From where she stood she could see that the shelf was thicker than it had been, and that it had something underneath it that stirred all at once, then was still.

“No,” she said. “But I’d like to sit a little.”

They sat with their feet in the two hollows of the last tread. The water stayed clear until the boy saw the eggs for himself. Then a little grit came through, and the shelf was the first thing they lost.

On the way up the woman stopped at the shoot. She asked the boy to hold it gently toward the wall while she worked the clay in behind it. He held it too far at first. She put her hand over his and brought it back a little.

They left enough room on the step for two feet side by side.
