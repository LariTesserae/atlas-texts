On the tenth morning the landing arrived at the bakery. It came in a little crooked. The woman who ran the bakery put a heel of bread in the green basket and let it down anyway. The boy who opened the night baker’s shutters had been taking his breakfast that way, and she hadn’t told him about the landing.

At the other end of the city, something had gone wrong beneath the building where the man with the photograph lived. A lower story had opened as far as it could and stopped against a large root. All night the building had been settling on it. In the morning there was a fine gray dust on the pavement, and the man’s door would not open.

The power that had been watching the city went down into the excavation. It could have moved the tree. There was room for it on the other side of the avenue, and the earth there was deep. But the tree had already grown around a broken drain, two buried bricks, and the bones of a small animal. There was no way of moving it that would take everything.

So the power took the weight of the building for a while.

Upstairs the man heard his door come loose. He came out carrying both lamps, though it was morning by then. At the bottom he found a workwoman on her knees at the edge of the excavation. He set one of the lamps beside her. She leaned over and held it down by its handle. In the light she could see the root and the gray wall above it, with enough space between them to put her hand.

“Keep it there,” she said.

The man thought she meant the lamp.

By afternoon the crew had fitted a squat pier on either side of the root. The workwoman stayed below while the others cleared their tools. She watched the little gap as the weight came back onto the walls. When nothing in it moved, she reached in and brushed some grit off the bark.

Sela came by in the evening. The man was sitting on the stair again, peeling an orange. He told her she could come up now if she wanted to see the room. She said she would another day. She had been walking since before the bakery opened, and if she sat down she wasn’t sure she’d get up.

He moved over anyway. She sat.

After a while he asked whether her daughter still had the photograph. Sela said she did. It was on the bedroom sill under a cup, because the corners had started to curl.

“Tell her she can turn it over,” he said. “I only put it that way so she’d see where to find me.”

That night the power went east, past the cistern, to the old foundations. The sister had finished sweeping. She and her brother were eating at the low wall, with their bowls set between them. There was no door, so the power knocked on the end brick.

The sister put down her spoon. She looked first toward the path, where people usually appeared, then toward the end of the wall.

“Just a minute,” she said.

She went to fetch another bowl. The power waited where it was.
