For a whole shift the power does nothing. It has mistaken the brass grates for the important parts, and it needs the shift to find out what they are for. They are for a great deal of water, should there ever be a great deal of water. The animal and its young live on what the grates would not notice.

Just before the freight lift starts up again, the last young one takes a step along the wire. It has grown since the others were carried. One of its feet goes around the insulation; another comes down on the first foot and pries it loose. In this way it gets as far as the bracket and can go no farther. The adult is on the other side of the bracket. There is room for either of them to turn, but not both.

The power could lift the young one. Instead it waits to see what the adult, which has carried all the others past this bracket, will do. After some time the adult backs out onto the plate. It comes down from the other side and takes the young one by the rear feet. The blunt head slips neatly through the bend in the wire. They come up backward into the light.

Above the new nest, a cooling fin has a burr on it. Water gathers there, but before it gets heavy enough to drop it runs along the black edge to a lower corner. The power bends the burr down. There is no sound to it. Though the change is smaller than one of the animal’s feet, the next drop hangs in a different place.

It takes nearly an hour to fall.

By then the last young one is in the felt with the others. The adult is lying on the plate over the old runnel. When the fin gives, the animal lifts its belly and faces the thumbprint. There is no splash there. There is a little sound from uphill, not quite a splash either: water on the brass, and then water passing through it.

The animal goes under the ridge. It pushes in among the young and drinks from the felt without coming out again.

On the next shift the ridge rises another fraction. The grate tilts far enough that its bars are no longer in the path of the drops. Now they fall through the opening without touching brass. The power has allowed for this, but not for what the animal does the following night. It brings up a loose corner of the felt and tucks it into the grate. The thick end still hangs down where the young can graze it. The thin end lies on the white plate, in the cold air, and stays wet.

After a few days there is a green line along that end. At the end of the line, just outside the grate, the adult has begun leaving the pieces too large to take below. There is a blue thread among them. One morning a drop catches on it and hangs there even after the lift has gone by. One of the young comes up, sets two feet on the brass and four on the felt, and drinks it before it falls.
