The first thing the power did was put a hand on the back of a child.

She was on the old crossing in the north, walking with a cup in each hand. Behind her, the end of the slab had lifted. It did this sometimes when somebody stepped off the near bank, but this time it stayed up. A little fan of grit ran out from under it.

The child stopped. There was nobody close enough to have touched her. She stood quite still for a moment, a cup in each hand, and then, because whoever wanted her to stop had not taken their hand away, she backed up. The slab turned over without breaking and went down edge first.

What she said afterward was that she had nearly dropped the cups. One was hers and the other belonged to a boy who had gone ahead to look at a room. He came back along the bank and was cross with her for having seen the slab fall when he had only heard it.

The people came up to set the spare. They found the far ledge whole, but the near one had split, and the old crossing stood on edge in the fissure, wedged tight. A man went down on his elbows to see whether they could get a rope around it. He came up with his sleeves wet to the shoulder and said there were animals down there. The fallen slab had closed the hole they breathed through. Already there were little lifts in the water on the wrong side of the stone.

The power could have lifted the slab. There was no one there strong enough to do it, and it would have been a kindness. But the fall had also opened a space deeper down, where the stone had been fitted close against itself for longer than there had been houses. The water had got in. It would be a good place to go in the hot part of the year, when the upper pools grew close and the small animals came up more often than they ought to.

So the power held the old slab where it was and eased it outward by the width of a finger.

On the bank the man heard it move. He put his face down again. Presently he asked for a chip, a thick one, and someone brought him three. He tried them in the gap until he found one that would go in broad end first. The child with the cups asked how he knew it would hold.

“I don’t,” he said. “Give me the other thick one.”

They set the spare a little farther down, where they had to cut a step on either side. For years afterward the old crossing could be seen from the new one. When the water was low, a person lying flat with one cheek against the stone could see the two chips and, just beyond them, a silver patch where the young went up to breathe. People brought their children to look. The children generally saw it before the people who had brought them.

The power stayed in the north until the work was done. That evening it went into a house whose door had been left open and sat by the pool. There was a cup on the shelf. It drank and set the cup upside down.

Under the bed, drops were gathering at the edge. The woman who slept there had gone to help with the crossing and would be late coming home. The power ran a finger along the underside, as she would have done. It had meant only to give the drops somewhere to go, but they ran ahead of its finger and fell into the trough one after another. From the next room a sleepy child called out that somebody was floating chips without him.

The power took its hand away. The woman was coming down the passage. She said no, nobody was, but he could come and help her if he wanted. There was a thump as he got out of bed. Then a long time in which neither of them spoke.
