The power came in the thaw, when there was nothing left to save the animal from except what it had already done.

It watched the animal eat. The first root went down whole. The second caught across its mouth, and it backed against a stem to get the root straight. There was no need to help with that. The power waited until the animal had finished.

It had meant to make the winters milder. There were pools where every bubble went flat before the young had feet enough to hold them at the surface. It had seen one of those pools from below: the seeds all sprouted, the roots hanging down in good order, and nothing moving between them. But it had also seen what happened in a winter without a lid. The seeds washed to the deep end and lay there. The pool animals went on feeding late into the year, and in spring there were fewer of them than there had been in autumn.

So it left the winter alone that year and stayed beside the animal.

After eating, the animal caught a blade of grass in its mouth and pulled. The blade bent, drawing it up until two of its worn feet touched the surface. Every time it let go to breathe, the blade straightened and dropped it. On the third try the power held the blade down.

The animal breathed. Then it bit through the blade.

It sank with the piece in its mouth and took it to a place where the water had eaten underneath the bank. There it wedged the blade crosswise. In the small slack it made, scraps of root began to collect. The animal ate a little more and tucked itself under the bank.

The power kept the place in mind.

In the following winter, at a pool a long way east, it saw an animal come to the stem it would have to pass through. There was already a thread on the farther side. On the near side the animal had one foot braced against the stem, and as the bubble pulled it the foot grew thin. The power did not move the bubble. It bent the stem, only as much as a bird standing on it would have done.

The animal came through with all its feet. Three of the young slipped out of the roots in the commotion. It went back for them in an order the power could not account for: first the one farthest off, then the one that was swimming back on its own, then the one that had caught a root and could have waited. By the time it was done, the stem had straightened.

There were many pools. The power learned to tell the strain of a foot from the strain of a root, even in a storm when the ground itself was coming loose. It did not always arrive in time. Once it bent a stem too far and the little ceiling cracked. It spent the rest of that night holding the two edges together while the animal worked a thread into the crack. In the morning it eased its hold. The thread held.

At the first pool the old animal came out from under the bank. It had grown a little skin over the ends of its feet. The skin was still too soft for walking, but it could spread its toes, and it did so whenever the current lifted it. One afternoon it climbed onto the piece of grass it had wedged beneath the bank. There was room on it to lie straight.

The power had never seen it do that. It stayed until the animal curled up again.
