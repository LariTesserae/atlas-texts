The power first took notice of the foot the animal left in the wall. It had supposed that the foot was caught. It watched the animal go back to the pump and do the work of six feet with five, and at the end of the stroke it saw the toes in the wall move against an egg.

It waited through a dry night and a wet one. On the wet night the animal had to take its foot out. Water was coming up under the hatch, and a sucker had got in sideways, where it could not be drawn out by walking backward. The animal lay on its side and pulled with both front feet. In the tall house, the water rose out of the pipe hole and lifted the lowest of the lengths of wood. One egg rolled the width of its own shell. Up under the sill the hunter took its heels out of the leaf.

The power caught the egg. There was nothing to it, almost, but the weight of what was moving inside. It set the egg back with the scraped side uppermost, as it had seen the animal do. Then it held the wood down until the sucker came free.

Afterward it went looking for where the water might have gone. There was room in the town for a great deal of water, but nearly all of it was above the places that flooded. It followed a bead down the station wall and found itself in the cellar, with the bead still falling. It followed the same joint the other way and came out under the yellow ledge beyond the survey pegs. There were eggs on the ledge now. The power went back without touching them.

At the edge of the square a paving block had begun to put a root into the block beside it. The power turned the growing tip a little. It took until the next rain for the tip to reach the joint, and another rain for it to grow through. Where it came out, on the far side, it was no bigger than the root that had first entered the interval. But here it came out above ground. The water that followed it went down the face of the cutting instead of up through the pipe hole. It left a dark stripe in the clay, a little to one side of the eggs.

There was still water enough to bring the cellar animals into the square. The power had not known whether they would come up for less. The old brown animal with the yellow on its head came first again. Behind it came a small one with no hold on anybody. It stood in the drain cover with its head turned sideways until the old animal backed up and gave it a place to put its foot.

In the tall house, one of the eggs opened while the animal was at the station. The young thing got as far as the end of a length of wood and stopped there. The hunter came down to the third-story window. It stayed there a long time, feeling with one forefoot for the next joint. By the time it found it, the animal had come back and turned the wood so that the end with the young on it was inside the wall. Later that night the hunter caught something in the gutter of the next house. There was the brief running sound, and then the gutter began to drip again.

The root in the square thickened. In time it would have closed the seam it had made, but the little pale drinkers found the wet place and began to take the soft growth off it. They did not abandon the screw. On dry nights they still went there, each by its own street.

The power stayed until it saw what the animal would do in the next hard rain. The hatch trembled once and went still. The animal waited for the next stroke, then turned over a scrap of bark that had lodged against the collar. There was nothing under it. It left the scrap where it was and went back to the house. It got all six feet through the hole. For the rest of the night, when the pump came down, there was nobody beside it.
