I would be in the new district, though not among the animals hanging from the shelf or among the young on the thread. A little way up from where the thread touches the stone, there is a swelling in it. Seen from either street it is no more than a thick place in the shell. There is water inside it, and room for me to turn around.

At first I would have taken myself for the water. I went where it went, which in those days was in at one end of the swelling and out at the other. Then a young tube dweller settled over one of the holes. The water stopped going through. I went on turning.

I have two blunt feelers. At the open end of the chamber I can put one out as far as its bend. The other stays against the lip. What I take from the water is too small to stop the feeler moving, though after a good tide I have to draw it in and clean it. Sometimes I put the other one out while I do this. It bends sooner. For a long time I would know the two streets only by which way that shorter feeler bent.

Then a grazer comes down the thread. Its mouth passes over my chamber and takes the roof off in three strokes. At the third stroke I draw into a corner that has never before been the deepest part of anything.

The grazer stops. Through its underside I can see the dark curl of what it has eaten. A little of the curl is the film from my roof, though there is no place in it where the roof begins or ends. The grazer rocks once more and goes on. I wait until it has gone far enough for the thread to lift under its own weight again. Then I put both feelers out.

There is much more water above me than there was at either end. There is also the upward-opening fan. It is close enough that when it opens, my feelers come together. When it shuts they draw apart. I feed in the interval. On a strong tide this is scarcely time to straighten them. On a weak tide the fan stays shut so long that a little fur of silt gathers on their ends.

The tube dweller grows. Its fan rises out of reach, and the collar begins to lay shell along the edge of my chamber. From inside I can follow the work by touch. The new shell is yielding at first. I press against it when I turn, and the next time I turn there is a shallow place for my back. Outside, the thread is becoming a wall. On my side of it there is a narrow strip of water where nothing else has settled. I can stretch nearly my whole length into it now, though I still keep the end of my body in the chamber.

One ebb a brood comes through. The first of the young pass without touching me. Then several come abreast, and one is pushed into the strip of still water. It backs along my longer feeler until it reaches the shallow place I have made. I draw out of the chamber to let it past, but it does not go past. It stays there through the ebb, moving very slightly against the shell.

By the next tide it is white at the end that fits against the hollow. I can still get into the chamber if I turn on my side. I do this once, far enough to touch the old hole with the shorter feeler. Then I back out and go a little way along the wall. The ceiling animals give enough light to show the young one’s first fan opening where I used to put my head.

I don’t go far. There is a new lip just beyond the chamber, with water passing under it. I fit beneath it lengthwise. When the young fan opens, the water there quickens a little. I put out the shorter feeler first. It has room to straighten all the way.
