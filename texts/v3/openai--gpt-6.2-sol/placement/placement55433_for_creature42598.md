I’d live on the old mesa, near the willow that grew shut. Not in the hut. I’d keep a little way back from it, where the animals coming to water have worn two paths around a patch of ground that looks no different from the rest.

I think at first I’d be one of the people who comes with a cup. I’d have a place where I usually knelt, and I’d be annoyed, in a small way I wouldn’t care to explain, when a grazer stood there. Then one afternoon I’d come earlier than usual and find a young one asleep with its chin in the very print my knee had left. After that I’d look before I put my knee down. We could go on for years like that, the grazer and I, without either of us getting much better acquainted.

But when I try to imagine staying through the part of the day when nobody comes, I keep finding myself lower down. A little below the pool, where the water that went out under the willow has reached the rim. It doesn’t fall clear. It follows a root back under the ledge and wets the stone there. In the dry weeks there’s no visible water at all, only a patch the color of the inside of a broken cup.

I’d be a ledge bird, I think, but not one of the ones in the sailwing ferns. I’d have a nest in a place where a root is slowly lifting a flake of stone. The opening would be just wide enough to get through with my wings shut. Every year it would be a little wider. For a long time this would make it a better place to live.

From there I could see the feet of anyone who came to the pool by the lower path. I’d know one person by the end of a cord that always trailed from their bundle. Once it would catch in the leaves and they’d go on without it. I’d spend the better part of a morning trying to pull it under the ledge. It would be too long. By noon I’d have got enough of it inside to make the nest awkward to turn around in, and there’d still be a length out in the sun. When the person came back for it, the whole thing would go out through the opening in little jerks. I’d stand well back. A bit of last year’s nest would go with it.

Most days there’d be less to do. In the hour before the rain I’d stand by the wet root and watch the little dry mouths of the burrows. Once, when the earth spilled from one, nothing would follow. I’d wait until the rain reached the ledge, then go over and look. A young grazer on the other side of the turf would have put a toe through. It would draw the toe back very slowly, leaving a hole through which, for a moment, I could see the pale hair on its belly.

I’d know the young ones first by those blundering feet and later by their calls. There would be one that took a long time to come down. Its mother would call from below my ledge, and the young one would answer from the top, going farther away each time as it looked for a place. I’d hear this on several mornings. Then one day the two calls would come from below me. I wouldn’t have seen it happen. I’d go back to watching the burrow mouths, with the whole top of the mesa suddenly quiet.
