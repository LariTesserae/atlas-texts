I’d be in one of the chambers where the back wall has worn through. From the lane you could see straight through to the pale curb on the other side, provided neither sillback was home. You wouldn’t see me. I’d be in a shallow fan above the opening, where a larger animal than either of the present tenants used to work its jaw.

For much of the day I’d look like the darker end of that fan. I’d be flat enough to turn around in it without showing an edge. When the sillbacks came in, I’d put out my two front feet. They’d be thin enough at the tips to feel the first dampness settle on the stone. Most of my water would come from the smaller sillback. She slept with her nose tilted up, and after a while there’d be a wet patch just above it that I could walk across with my mouth to the wall. I’d get a little farther each time before the stone went dry. Then I’d go back and wait for her to breathe on it again.

I’d eat in the lane. Not in the middle, where the roadkeepers work, but on the outside of the curb, where they put what they don’t want. I could make a meal of a strip of skin too stiff for a roadkeeper to fold into its scoop. A milk tooth would be no use to me, but I’d go back to one every night for a week because scraps caught beneath its roots. From there I could put my forefeet on the curb and lean out. I’d know a sillback was coming by the grit jumping against the undersides of my feet. The wall’s note would tell me nothing about that. I’d have heard it since I was small enough to stand in the hollow of the tooth.

Then for several nights the smaller sillback wouldn’t come in. I’d go down to the wet patch at the usual time and find it dry. I’d try lower, between the places where her nose and her chin had been. On the other side of the opening the big sillback would be asleep as usual, but his breath would come out along the floor. I’d never been all the way down there while he was in the chamber. His nearest foot would turn out to be a poor wall: warm, easy to climb, liable to move.

I think that’s where I’d find myself: on the far side of that foot, in a room I’d been able to look into all my life. There’d be an old jaw fan close to the floor, nearly hidden by the big animal’s chin. I’d take to it. When the smaller sillback came back from the high thicket, with a shred of pale leaf caught in the split of her lip, I’d cross over to eat the shred. Then I’d go back to the low fan.

From there, with both animals home, I could reach either lane. I’d still favor the one with the tooth in it. One night a roadkeeper would come out through a break in the curb and tip a load over the tooth, and I’d have to learn the new edge by walking it. By morning I’d know where to put my feet. The sillbacks would have slept through the whole change.
