I thought I’d be on the dry shelf. It’s the place the basin seems to have left for somebody: a seat out from under the roosts, with a view of the pale bank and room to put both hands down.

But I keep coming back to the clay under the fern. The little holes there go in at an angle. You can tell which way the feet were going, if you get low enough.

I think I’d be one of the crickets that goes only that far. The others come back with grit caught in the joints of their legs, and sometimes they bring in a smell I can follow for a little way after they’ve passed. I don’t know what makes it. I know the fern by the hard underside of its stem and by the dirt that gives a little where the stem goes in. That’s where I eat.

On the way back there’s a pocket in the ledge. I used to put a foot on its lip. Then one morning something on the lip put a foot on mine. I go over it now in one jump. On the other side there are so many of us that sometimes I hang with a foot on another cricket instead of on the stone. If it moves, I have to find a place of my own.

Once I don’t go back with them. I stop beneath a low flake, not far from the shelf, and hook my feet into the underside. The skin along my back opens. Getting my hind legs out takes a long time. For a while I hang by the new feet, white even at the joints, while my old feet keep their hold on the stone. I can’t make the jump over the pocket like this. I can scarcely take one foot off the flake without pulling the others after it.

A bat goes by. The empty skin stirs against my back. One of its long feelers touches me, and I draw mine in.

By the time I leave, the others are already up in the clay. If you turned the flake over the next morning, you might think you’d found me. Even the smallest hooks would still be there. You’d have to touch a leg to find out how little was holding it down. By then I’d be back under the ledge with a foot on somebody who was just about to move.
