I’d come down with an arrow wrapped in brown paper. It would be a good arrow, black on cream enamel, with four holes and a little padding at the corners so it wouldn’t chip in the lift. The paper would say LOWER MARKET in my handwriting. I’d have copied those words from a work order without wondering why a market needed a sign at eleven at night.

At the end of the street I’d unwrap it and hold it against the rock. I’d been given a height above the floor, and I’d go so far as to measure it.

“Is that for now?” Ada would ask.

I’d look at the bench, then at the arrow. “Apparently not.”

I think I’d be the city’s sign painter, though painting would be less than half the work. Mostly I’d go out with a folding rule and find that a door opened the other way, or that someone had put a bell exactly where a name ought to go. I’d be used to making small decisions nobody had thought to leave to me. Down here, for once, I’d have to take the whole decision back upstairs.

While I waited for the lift, Ada would show me the cable sleeve. I’d look up into it instead of down at the dust and nearly get a drop in the eye. She’d hand me a rag from her pocket. I’d keep it by mistake.

So I’d come down again before there was any news about the stair. I’d bring the rag, washed and stiff from drying on a radiator. Emil would be doing long division on a step. I’d sit two steps below him and find, when he asked me to check a remainder, that I could no longer do it without writing the whole sum out. He’d give me the back of a spoiled page. We’d get different answers and both be wrong.

Once the stair opened, I’d have a reason to pass through without a work order. I’d buy fish toward the end of the market day, when the fishmonger was willing to sell me one mackerel instead of two. Coming down with the parcel under my arm, I’d be able to see my arrow from the landing. From there it would point back the way I’d just come. I expect I’d still take a little pleasure in how square it sat on the wall.

But where I’d find myself, if Ada’s door was open, would be just inside it. I’d have learned not to put my parcel on the ledge with the pear. I’d hold it while she finished her round, shifting it from arm to arm as the paper grew warm. Sometimes a buyer from upstairs would stop on the landing, having heard the drop hit the new drain. They’d peer down through the grate for a second and go on. From where I stood I’d be able to see the underside of the landing lamp, which stayed on now whether anybody was coming or not, and one fern frond laid out in its light.
