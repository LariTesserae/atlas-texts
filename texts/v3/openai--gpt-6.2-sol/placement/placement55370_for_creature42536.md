I think I would be on the other side of the roof. Not out on the hillside, where there would be grass and weather, but in the seam between the chalk and the first packed layer of soil. There’s room in the seam if you have six feet and can put them down without lifting your body very far.

From below, my shadow would be the largest thing about me. Up there I would have dirt in the joints of my legs and a white stripe down my belly from going the same way every day. I’d know where the chalk would take my weight and where a foot put down carelessly would go in up to the first joint. At one place I would always put a foot down carelessly. There would be something there to feel for.

It’s the root, though I would know only the part of it that is thick and brown. It goes into the chalk at a slant. I can put a foot alongside it, but only one, and only far enough to feel it move. Most days I do this on my way past. The root gives a little under my foot and comes back when I take it away. I don’t wait to feel it come back. I know how a root behaves.

Then one evening it pulls away from me.

I set my other feet wide. There is a slow pull, then a brief slackening, then a pull so steady I begin to walk along the seam looking for what has caught it. I can go a little way in either direction before the earth presses me flat. There is nothing to see on either side. When I return, the root is still bent. I put my foot into the space it has left.

That is where I’d be during the long dry spell, when the three of them are on the root. I wouldn’t know when the young one lifted its foot or where it put it down. But there would be a moment when the weight shifted without growing less, and my foot would slip a little farther into the chalk. I would stay because taking it out would mean putting my weight back on the root.

By morning I’d be gone. There is a place farther along the seam where I can get my head under a dead leaf and eat the soft parts. I would go there and find it dry and eat it anyway.

The next afternoon, on my way past, I’d put my foot down and find nothing. Then I’d reach a little lower, past where the root had always been, and there it would be. For a while I’d keep both positions in my body: the old one, which my foot would go to first, and the new one, which I would have to reach for. And after enough afternoons I’d go straight to the lower place. All I would know of the hollow would be that one of my steps had got a little longer.
