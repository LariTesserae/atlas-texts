I’d be at the edge with a broken jar across my knees, trying to get one more piece out of it. There’s a turn in the shoulder of a jar that makes a good scoop if you can break it off whole. I’d have got two already. The third would be the one I spoiled by wanting it too much.

I used to fill gaps with my heels. There’s a strip in the old quarters where you can see the print of my toes too. I put my whole foot down once because the shoulder I was leaning on moved. The woman whose shoulder it was said she was sorry, which made me feel worse. For a long time I went around that strip. Then somebody set a rack of pots over it, and now I go that way whenever it’s quicker.

At the new edge I do whatever can be done while the clay is too wet. Most of it is carrying. The rest is taking things out of the way of the people who carry. I know which boards you can lift by one end and which ones will let go of their load so quietly you’ll take another step before you notice.

The mother asks me for three pieces of jar. I offer her one of my scoops, but she wants the flat pieces from the bottom. She knocks two together and gives them back. The third she keeps.

“Put one where the wall runs out,” she says.

I set it down at the end of the partition. From her bed she tells me to turn it over. On its back the shard rocks; when I step on it, it gives two little knocks, one under my heel and one as I lift my foot. She has me put the second at the turn toward the old quarters. Then she gets up and tries the way herself. At the first shard she stops and asks for my arm. I give it to her too high. She takes me by the wrist and brings it down to where she wants it.

In the morning the shard at the turn has gone. A pair of tiles crossed there before dawn, and I find it edgewise in one of their ridges. I dig it out and move it a foot nearer the wall. The next night she tells me a foot is too far. We settle on the width of her hand.

After a while people bring me pieces that rock. They leave them beside my boards, sometimes while I’m asleep. Not all of them are for paths. A man wants to hear when his sister has brought the bed boards back, so he can stop keeping supper warm. Two children want to know if anyone goes out onto their apron before daylight. I give them a piece that makes a fine loud clack, and the next morning they bring it back. They’ve been up all night hearing it in every other sound.

I still carry rolls when there’s a roll to carry. But on cold nights, before the work starts, I make the rounds of the pieces I’ve set down. Some have sunk and need a little grit under them. Some are gone because the person who needed them has moved. At the turn by the old quarters there are two shards now, a pace apart. The mother put the second one there herself. She says the first tells her where to turn and the second tells her she’s turned enough. When I come home late I step over both. Her bed is close by, and there’s no need to tell her where I am.
