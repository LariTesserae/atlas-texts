I think I’d be the third stepping stone. Not at first. At first I’d want to be the loose half of the shell, for the pleasure of being lifted once and fitting. But I’d rather be where things happen often enough for me to get them wrong and learn the difference.

I’d know Iven first by the foot she puts on me when the kettle is full. It comes down hard at the near edge, then rolls inward as she gets her weight over it. On her way out she sometimes skips me altogether. For a long time I’d take that for a sign that the kettle was empty. Then one morning she’d skip me with a full one and get a bootful of water. She’d stand on the far side and laugh, once, quite sharply. From where I lay there would have been no way to tell she was in a hurry.

In summer the birds would come up out of the gap and stand on me to dry. I’d get warm enough for their feet before I got warm enough for mine, if I had any. They’d leave little wet prints that went away from the toes inward. In winter I’d learn the weight of a bird that had meant to bathe and found ice instead. It would stand longer than a dry bird had any reason to, looking down. Sometimes it would peck.

I don’t think I’d be very old as a stepping stone. As rock, of course, I’d have no idea. One end of me would still have a roughness that came from being pried out of somewhere, but I wouldn’t remember being pried. The first thing I’d remember would be rocking under a heel. Whoever put me there had to lift me again and slip a chip underneath. It’s the chip I’d be afraid of losing. Every spring there’d be a day when water ran fast enough under me to make me think it was gone.

One year Iven would come out without the kettle. She’d squat on the second stone, put both hands under my edge, and lift just enough to let the water through cloudy. I’d feel the chip go. She’d set me down a little nearer and try me with one foot. I’d rock. She’d try the other corner. After a while she’d put me back, not quite where I’d been, and wedge me with a piece of the ice she’d broken from the basin. It would hold long enough for her to fetch a proper chip. In the meantime she’d go inside and put the kettle on.

I’d spend that little while held up by something that was already giving way. The light would be good. A bird would come down to the gap, which would still be wide enough, and lower itself into the water without testing anything first.
