At first I’d put myself on the upper gallery. I’d have a room a few doors down from Iven, and when the water took the street I’d be the person who could tell you how to get to the far side of town without going through anybody’s kitchen.

But I think I’d spend most of my time underneath the stairs.

I’d be the person they sent for when a step began to talk. A loose step makes quite different noises going up and coming down. People would tell me it was the third from the bottom, and I’d ask which way they’d been going. Usually they’d have to walk it again. I’d wait below with my palm flat against the ceramic until I felt the little knock.

I’d carry wedges wrapped in a piece of old sleeve so they wouldn’t knock against one another. The thin ones would be easy enough to make. The good thick ones would come out of steps laid before my time, and I’d go to some trouble to get them back whole.

Under the laundry stair there’d be a step that knocked only in the late afternoon. I’d go at noon and find it sound, and go back after supper and find it sound again. Eventually I’d bring my bowl down with me and eat on the paving, waiting. The first shell animal out of the western pans would walk right past my bare heel. The second would stop under the step and lower itself. Then another would come, and another. There’d be barely enough clearance for the old ones. Each time one raised itself to go on, its shell would lift the loose end of the tread. The knock I’d been sent to fix was the tread coming down behind it.

I’d still fix the step. It would be no good leaving it to come down on a leg. But instead of driving a wedge under the low end, I’d take the tread up and bed it a little higher at both ends. For a week afterward Iven would catch her toe on it coming home. The second time she did it with a full bowl, she’d set the bowl down and ask me how much room I thought I needed.

I wouldn’t know. I’d made room for the largest animal I’d seen, with a bit to spare. So the next evening she’d come down with her spoon. We’d sit on the paving and wait for one of the old ones to pass. When it did, she’d slip the spoon handle into the space above its shell, first flat and then on edge. Flat it went through. On edge it didn’t.

“You can have half of that back,” she’d say.

I think that’s where you’d find me: not on the gallery giving directions, but below it the next morning, shaving a little off each bed while Iven went about her round. I’d leave the tread dry-laid until evening. Then I’d sit far enough away for the animals to come through and near enough to hear if I’d got it wrong.
