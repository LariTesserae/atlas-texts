I’d find myself one flight below the meeting house, where the water crosses the landing and goes in under Iven’s door. I’d be the person his daughter calls when it starts doing that. Not because I know what to do, particularly. I live across the landing, and I have a spoon thin enough to get under the gutter stones.

The spoon was a good spoon once. Sela says she remembers eating with it. This is what she says whenever I bring her something I’ve broken in the course of fixing something else.

I earn my supper carrying things between people who can manage perfectly well without me on a dry day. Bowls mostly, and once a live goat in a flour sack, with its head out. I also carry messages. These are harder. A woman will ask me to tell her sister the soup is ready, and by the time I get there I find the sister has been sitting with her coat on for an hour, waiting to be asked to supper. I used to repeat what I was told, word for word. Now I sometimes say, “She’s put your bowl by the fire.” Then I have to go down ahead and make sure she has.

I’d spend enough time with Iven to learn the yellow sand, though not enough to tell it from the yellow sand he says is entirely different. Some afternoons he asks for water from a gutter that has given nobody any trouble. We let it settle. He’s content to wait without talking; I’m the one who keeps getting up to see if it’s done. Once, when I brought him water from the landing, he put his finger in it and said, “That’s warm.”

It was no warmer than usual. I said so.

“On this side of the door,” he said.

We sat with that a while. Then I took the spoon out and lifted the stone I generally lifted, and for the first time put my hand in *upstream*. There was a hollow back there large enough to take my arm to the elbow. The water came past it in little pushes. Something brushed my knuckle. I took my hand out so fast I skinned it, and Iven, who could hear me but couldn’t see me, called to ask whether I’d found the trouble.

I said I’d found somewhere for the water to go.

That wasn’t true yet. I spent the rest of the afternoon on my knees making it true, with his younger grandson holding the lamp too low because he wanted to see what had touched me. We found a piece of blue crockery, a tooth neither of us cared to claim, and a small gray bird that came out dry and furious from a hole well above the water. By supper the landing was clear. In the morning it was wet again, but on my side this time. Iven’s daughter brought over a flat stone to put my shoes on. She didn’t say anything about the direction of the water. She’d had a whole night without getting up to move her father’s bed.

I don’t think I’d go often to the pool. I’d be glad other people did. I’d want to hear about the unfamiliar roof while it was still unfamiliar, before somebody’s mother remembered the bone and everybody went home. But I’d want to hear the rest, too: who went to look, and what they moved to get in, and whether they put it back. If Sela told me about her mother and her aunt, I’d ask how her mother got in after the shutter was closed.

“Through the door,” she’d say. “There was a door.”

And the next time I passed that stretch of lane I’d have to stop myself looking for it at the height of my shoulder, where a door ought to be. I’d stand there with somebody’s soup cooling in my hands, trying to see a girl climb in under my feet.
