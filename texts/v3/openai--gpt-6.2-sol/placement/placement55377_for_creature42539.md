I’d be the carpenter who came up too early.

I would bring one door. I’d made it at home on the kitchen floor, where I could tell it was square by the joints in the tiles. It would have a latch you could lift with the side of your hand if you were carrying something. On the way up I’d keep shifting it from one shoulder to the other. By the time I got to the square I’d have a very clear idea of what I wanted to do with it, which was put it down.

The masons would be kind about the door. They’d show me the step they thought might suit it and help me stand it up. The bank behind it would be no higher than my knee. I’d lift the latch and swing the door toward me, and we’d all look at the little bed of mint it opened onto. One of the masons would put his foot on the step to see how it felt to come home that way. Then he’d take it off because there was a spider.

I’d spend the afternoon taking a little wood off the bottom edge, though there was no floor for it to catch on. When the masons left, I’d stay to finish. I could see them stopping to clear the gutters as they went, each man bending and straightening in turn. I’d mean to follow while there was still enough light to carry the door.

Instead I’d lay it across the stone bench and the bank, with my coat under it to keep the latch off the stone. I’d sit on the turf and eat what I’d brought. From there I could hear a hen working at a root under the bench. Now and then she’d stop, and I’d think she’d got it, but then there would be the small sound of her bill again.

After dark it would get cold enough that I’d have to reach under the door for my coat. Something would be standing on it. I’d feel the cloth gather between my fingers and then go taut, and I’d let go. There’d be room for me under there too, if I went in feet first, though I wouldn’t quite dare. I’d lie along the bank with my hands in my armpits. In the night I’d wake once to the latch lifting and falling, a sound I knew from having tried it so many times on the kitchen floor.

In the morning there would be two wet heel marks on the upper face of the door. A little farther along, where the wood had dried, I’d find a third by putting my cheek down close to the grain. I wouldn’t know whether the hen had come up to see where the noise was coming from or whether she’d simply found a shorter way onto the bank.

I’d leave the door where it was and go down for another coat. The next evening I’d bring up a blanket as well, and a short length of wood to keep the door from rocking when anything walked across it. I’d still take off my boots before lying down. It would be some time before I stopped setting them side by side at the doorstep.
