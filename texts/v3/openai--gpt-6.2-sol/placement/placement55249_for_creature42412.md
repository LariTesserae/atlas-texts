I’d be in the last bend of the old trail, just below the saddle, where people stop to take a stone out of a boot. There’s a good seat there. If you sit on it, you can see one white post but not the other two. You have to get back on your feet to find out whether the way is open.

I think at first I’d be the person who put the seat there. I’d have a short iron bar and a bag that left a square of dust on my back. My work would be to take apart the old trail as the new one came in. There’d be nothing wrong with most of the steps. I’d get the bar under them and lift them whole, and underneath would be the white stems of plants that hadn’t seen light since the trail was laid.

I’d be slow about the seat. People would still use it after they stopped needing the bend. The first few mornings they’d come by out of habit; later they’d come because it was the one place on the pass where you could take off a boot without getting your sock wet.

But I don’t think I’d have put the seat there. I think I’d have been under it when it was put there.

The white stems would be what I ate. When the iron came under the stone, I’d go down as far as I could, and the person with the bar would see only what looked like a loose root pulling back into the soil. That evening I’d come out and try the new saddle. It would be warm all the way across. I’d get farther than I meant to, because without a joint or a curled leaf to go under, there’d be nowhere to stop.

At the far shoulder I’d find the bronze leaves. I wouldn’t go behind them. I’d find a wet line on the wall below them and put my mouth to that. It would take me a while to learn that the water was coming up. The first time I got it right I’d get so much that I’d have to let go of the wall.

Then I’d go back. There would still be the narrow space where the seat had been, with a roof of packed dirt held up by all those roots. At first light a walker would come around the bend and sit down without looking. I’d hear the small surprise of their heels hitting the trail. Then they’d laugh, and stay where they were to shake out the boot.

One of the stones they shook out would lodge in the mouth of my hole. I’d spend the warm part of the morning drawing it in, a little at a time. It would be too round to make a good door. I’d have to dig a place for it to sit.
