I’d start by looking for myself at the tide gauge. Not in the box bolted to the rock, but at the little bright place inside the fairlead where the cable has rubbed the steel smooth. For an hour there’s a sound there that carries to both sides of the ridge. I like the thought of having that much reach and then none at all. But at slack water, when the cable hangs loose, I think I’d want to be doing something.

Farther down, at the rim of the south bowl, there’s a flat stone with one end sunk in the silt. I’d be a brittle star underneath it. The stone would be small enough for the octopus to take away if she ever came down that far. From above, all you’d see of me would be three arms, each coming out at a different angle, far enough apart that you might take them for separate animals. The rest of me would fit under a mussel shell.

One arm would get out into the running water and curl around whatever it caught. Another would go along the bottom, trying the underside of a scrap of kelp. A third would be shorter than the others, with a pale new length at the end. Whatever took the old length took it so quickly that the rest of me was back under the stone before the silt came down.

Late in the winter, the empty skate case would fetch up against my stone. One horn would go into the gap I use to reach the open water. For several tides I’d work an arm past it and have to draw the arm back whenever the case shifted. Then I’d start going out through a gap on the other side. There’d be less to eat there, but I could stay out longer.

I don’t think I’d see the skate. It might settle a foot away, in water running clean over its back and bringing nothing of it to me. What I’d know of its arrival would be the case: how easily the tide turned it now, how the split end opened and shut. One night it would go. In the morning there’d be a little groove where the horn had scraped the silt away, and I’d put the short arm out through the old gap. It wouldn’t reach as far as the groove. For a while I’d keep putting it there anyway.
