I’d start under the northern cliff, where the blue ribbon comes out. I’d want to see what happens to its end when it reaches the ground. From above it looks as though it curls of its own accord, but down at the foot of the cliff there is a little brown bird walking backward, tugging at it. The bird wants the dust underneath. Each time the ribbon breaks it has to come forward and take hold again.

I could be that bird. There would be a great deal to do within the length of a single curl. But I think I’d stay after the birds had gone, when their bath hollows were still distinct in the powder and the cliff was beginning to warm the air an inch above the ground.

There’s a seam there I could get into if I had a flatter head.

I’d be an animal about as long as a fallen flower, with four feet and a belly that could tell the temperature of stone without touching it. In the morning I’d go into the seam headfirst. By afternoon, when the inner walls grew hot, I’d come out tail first, feeling around behind me for a foothold. My tail would be white at the tip. A bird would have tried it once. I’d have a new tip by the time it tried again.

For a while I’d live on the little things that turn up underneath the ribbon: shed skins, moth eggs, a kind of mite that goes still when its feet leave the stone. Then the seam would close. I’d be out when it happened. There would be no report, just a pinch of blue dust coming up through the place where I’d meant to put my head.

I’d spend that first night in a hoofprint. It would have a raised lip on one side, enough to get my feet under. In the morning there would be six more prints ahead of it, all with the same ragged place where the animal’s hoof had split. I’d follow them until they stopped at a wall.

The wall would be cool. The last print, right up against it, would still be warm.

That would keep me there long enough to see a moth come down the wall without ever landing on it. Its wings would go on opening and closing, very slowly, as it descended. At the bottom it would disappear into what looked like a stain. I’d put a foot in after it and find a space between two stones. From the other side the opening would be quite plain.

I think that’s where I’d live: in a passage the herds walk over, that the moths know from above and I know from underneath. In dry weather it would take most of the night to get from one end to the other. After rain I could do it in a few minutes. There would be no advantage in being able to do it quickly except that sometimes I’d come out in time to see the rain still falling off the grass.

Once a calf would lower its head to the opening. I’d know it was young by the way its nostrils kept touching the stone. It would breathe in, and the loose skin along my back would lift. I’d go farther in. A little later a crushed flower would come through, pushed by the calf’s lip, and stop just short of my feet.

I wouldn’t eat the flower. I’d wait beside it. All night, as the stone cooled, small things would come out of it on legs.
