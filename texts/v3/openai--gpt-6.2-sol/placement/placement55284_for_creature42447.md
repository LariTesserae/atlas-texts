I think I would come into the square from the laundries. There’s a door in that pale wall, but the bottom has swollen and we don’t use it. To get outside I’d have to go past the extractors, through the dispatch bay, and all the way round the end of the building. By the time I got to the platform I’d have used up a fair bit of my break.

I’d be the one who mends the laundry bags. The big ones come down with sheets still caught in the corners. You have to turn them all the way out before you put them under the needle. Things come out of the seams: collar stays, a key with a blue cap, once a little silver fish with a hole through its tail. I keep the hard things in a saucer beside the machine. Anything soft goes in the bin.

For a while I’d go out there to eat. Then there would be a wet week when I ate inside, and I’d find myself going out after I’d finished. That’s when I’d start to learn what could be heard from where. On the platform you hear the extractors through the soles of your shoes. On the grass by the alders you can hear them coming through the wall. If you rest an elbow on the pump house pipe, you can hear a drop well before it falls, though I don’t know whether that’s the drop or something going on farther back in the pipe.

I don’t think I’d see the animal until I’d already put my lunch down on one of its bricks. I’d move the lunch to see why the bottom of the tin was warm. By then there would be nothing to see except the edge of a long mouth going in behind the clay. I’d go back to work with a yellow mark on the tin and spend the afternoon trying to remember whether I’d seen an eye.

The first time I heard the second tap clearly, I’d be out there with the blue key. It had been in the saucer long enough that the dust underneath it was the shape of a key. I’d brought it out to try on the loading office door, which would turn out not even to have a lock. On the way back I’d drop it on the curb.

There’d be the little report from the bank, and then that other sound, so much like a sound from work that I’d turn toward the laundries. When I looked back, the clay would be stirring at the bottom. I’d leave the key where it was while the animal came down. It would put its mouth to the curb on either side of the blue cap, then go on to a brick. I’d wait until it had turned the corner before I picked the key up. I’d still have it in my hand when I sat down at the machine.

In autumn I’d miss the day the clay fell. I’d see the yellow patch from the tram and get off with everyone else, carrying my lunch past the dispatch bay. At noon I’d go and look at the shallow step the animals had left. From out in the square the hole had looked scarcely big enough for one of them. Up close there would be a second hollow just inside it, lower down, where the little ones must have lain.

I’d sit on the curb after that, rather than on the platform. Near my heel there’d be a cobble that moved when I put my weight on it. I’d get in the habit of setting both feet a little farther out. Even in winter, when there was no good reason to think anything was under that particular stone, I’d sit that way, with my feet out in the gravel and the tin balanced on my knees.
