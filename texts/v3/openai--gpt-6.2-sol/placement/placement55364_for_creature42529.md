I would be in the little dry place under the falling water. At first I thought I’d be the dry place itself, but a shrew sleeps there with its feet slack and its mouth half open. I wouldn’t want it to have to sleep inside me.

I think I’d be the bit of stone it puts its head on. Not a piece of the wall that could be fitted back. A little loose flake, with one end nearly thin enough to see through. When the shrew turns in its sleep, its whiskers touch the underside of the shelf. The first time this happens I take the movement for a waking. After a few afternoons I know better. The feet are what you watch.

For a long time I would believe I could hear the turning better than anyone else, being stone and in contact with so much of it. Then one day a vole would put a foot on my thin end and I’d tip, and the turning would go on exactly as before. What I’d been hearing best was a little grain of sand under me, working back and forth. When I settled again it would be on a different corner. The shrew would come and try the old place, push at my edge with its nose, and go to sleep with its head on the bare floor.

I’d spend a winter like that, tilted just enough to see out. I could see the low branch but not the roots it grew over. A bird would come and sit there, and from as low down as I was its feet would seem to be holding the branch up. When it called, I’d hear the call twice: once from the bird and once from the roof of my little shelter. The second sound would be so close I might mistake it for an answer. The bird never would.

In spring the young vole would come down farther than it had before. It would put a forefoot on me and rock me back into my old bed, not because it wanted to move me but because it was reaching for a bit of fern. I’d lose my view of the branch. In exchange I’d have the weight of that foot to think about. It was heavier than the last time I’d seen the animal, and for a moment, before the other foot came down, it was the whole weight.

Later there would be a thin drag across my edge. The vole would be going uphill with the old blue cotton, which had come loose in the wet. The knot would catch on me. I could keep it there for perhaps the length of one hiss. Then the vole would come back down, take the cotton nearer the knot, and pull from the side. It would go free without moving me at all. I’d have the blue for that long, and afterward, when the shrew came back, I’d have a warm place no larger than the underside of its jaw.
