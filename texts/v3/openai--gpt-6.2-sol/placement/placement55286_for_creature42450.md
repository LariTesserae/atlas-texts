I would be the root that has grown through the split where the copper fish sleeps. Not all of the root. A hand’s breadth of it, where it leaves one face of the stone and takes hold of the other. The rest could go on down the ridge without me. There would be no clean place to make the cut.

For a long time I would know the fish by the part of it that didn’t fit. Toward evening the lower edge of its tail would come to rest against me. It would be easy to take that touch for the whole animal. In the morning it would draw away, and the water in the split would warm by a little.

I would grow across the opening. There is no reason a root should leave a door. The smallest of the six fish would be the last to have any trouble getting through, though it would be the one I felt most often. It would stop halfway, its fins working against both sides of the crack, and pick at the soft red point of me. I would put out another point a little farther in. It would find that one too. By the time it was too broad to pass, I would have grown flat against the stone. A root can be kept to a shape for a long while by something that has no use for that shape except in passing.

Or I might be farther up. I keep coming back to the two short feet of the grazer. On the wall it has to feel out a place for each of them, but among the roots one can come down almost anywhere. I would be in the shallow fold where the shorter of the two comes down. The first few times it stands there, it loosens me. A little red root hair goes drifting out over the scarp. After that there is a hollow just deep enough to hold the foot without its having to curl.

From there I could not see the leaf in the air. At noon there would be a dark place on the stone, thin at first, then broad enough to cover the fold. The grazer would come up and stand with its short foot in me, and some days, when the water lifted, it would take the foot away. I would feel the weight go through the other roots as it reached. Then the foot would come back, not quite where it had been, and feel along the edge until it found the hollow.

I think I would be there after the grazer stopped coming, too. The hollow would fill from one side. The first leaf to open in it would lie almost flat, with room for another beside it. From the valley there would be nothing to distinguish that bit of green from the rest of the crest. Up close, for a season or two, you could still put the tip of a finger where the foot had been.
