I think I’d come in by the west street carrying a stool. I wouldn’t have brought it for the town. I’d have had it a long while and known how to set it down on uneven ground without trying all four legs. At the market I’d put it down on the shallow side to free a hand for the woman who took my elbow. Then I’d step through and find that what I needed was not a hand but somewhere to put my foot. She’d let go as soon as I found it. By the time I thought of the stool again she would be washing it, starting underneath.

For a while I’d sleep in the market, on the bench she finished first. I’d get up when she came to wash it, which isn’t the same as getting up at a particular hour. Once, finding me still asleep, she’d wash as far as my knee and go on to the next bench. I’d wake with one warm leg and one cold one and have to put a hand down to find out why.

What I’d want, after a while, would be a door I could leave ajar. This would take some doing. There are plenty of doorways, but the only door I’d find would be lying flat in a court. I’d spend a day getting it upright. It would be heavier at one end, and I’d assume that end had taken up more water, until I put my fingers into the hollow along the bottom and found it packed with little shells. I’d clear enough out to lift it and leave the rest. Hung on a loop of cord, it would make a small sound when it moved.

I’d end up in the house with the gutter, above the old man’s cistern. We’d come to know each other by the noises we could safely sleep through. He’d know my door. I’d know the sound of his shoes being set on the shelf before rain. The stool would be for the landing, where people could put down a full jar and straighten up before taking the stairs. No one would sit on it much. It’s hard to sit on a stool with a jar in your lap and get up still holding the jar. I’d learn this by trying to help.

The girl would sometimes come home with the jar held away from her body because she’d put something new in it and wanted to hear what it did against the tiles. One evening she’d ask me to keep it while she went back for something she’d seen by the garden wall. I’d set it on the stool. Much later she’d come through from the west, empty-handed. She’d tried the short way around, she’d tell me, and of course the wall hadn’t been there. I’d offer to go with her in the morning. But she’d have had enough of the thing by then. What she’d want was for me to listen to the jar. I’d tip it too far and the tooth would slide into my palm. She’d take it back without looking, finding it with the pad of one finger.

I’d still think of myself as someone staying a while. Then one night I’d come home and find the stool gone. I’d put my hand down twice in the place where it ought to be. From below, the old man would tell me he’d lent it to somebody.

“Did they say when they’d bring it back?”

“I told them where you live.”

I’d leave the door ajar so they could hear that I was in. After a while I’d remember that it was the door they could hear, not me, and move my foot so it could swing.
