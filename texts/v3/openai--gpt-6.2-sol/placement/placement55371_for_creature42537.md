I think I would live far enough from the beach to get the weather wrong. From the old school you can see a piece of sea between the houses, but not the rocks below the bank. I’d look up from the sink and think there was plenty of time to go down, and by the time I got there the water would be over the place I’d meant to stand.

I’d be the woman with the school key. There are six keys on the ring, though only two still have locks. One opens the school and one opens my back door. I know them by touch. Every so often I take the others off and put them in the kitchen drawer, and then for a week I keep thinking I’ve left the keys somewhere because my pocket doesn’t hang right.

The nailbrush was mine. I put it out after I saw her trying to get something out from under her thumbnail with the corner of a leaf. I was inside the school at the time, taking the paper off a shelf. She didn’t see me. For a while afterward I was careful not to be there at the hour she came, which meant I had to admit I knew the hour.

In February the pipe froze. It always froze in the bend where it came out of the wall, but that year it split on the inside, where you couldn’t see. When it thawed there was a sound in the infants’ room like somebody frying an egg. I shut off the water and put a note on the tap.

I wrote USE THE ONE AT MY HOUSE and then took the note down. She knew where I lived, but there was no way of leaving that note without saying I knew who would read it. In the end I carried a basin of warm water over from my kitchen. By the time I got there it was barely warm. I put it on the step and went round to the front of the school, where the bottom of the door needed planing.

She came while I had the door propped open with my foot.

“Is that water doing anything?” she asked.

“No,” I said.

She went round the back. I took another shaving off the door, though it was already clearing the floor. After a while she came back with the basin and tipped it out against the wall. Then she crouched to pick up the shavings that had blown across the step. One had curled small enough to go round her finger. She put it on and held up her hand, and I held up the plane so she could see the shaving still coming out of it. We stayed like that for a second, showing each other the two ends of a very ordinary thing.

I might never go into the hollow. I’d know the bank, and perhaps once, in winter, I’d notice the peg. I’d go on past it with the rest of the beach to walk. At the school, when the tap was fixed, I’d forget for several days to put the brush back. Then I’d find it by my own sink and remember how good it had been to hear her come round the corner and ask me for something.
