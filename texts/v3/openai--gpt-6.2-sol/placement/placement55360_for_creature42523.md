I would live on the lower leaf, a little to one side of the place Eda can see. There is a shelter there, but the dark speck isn’t the shelter. It’s a mat I put out to dry. The shelter is under the mat.

For a while I think I would make my living catching things from above. In fact, there wouldn’t be enough to catch. In a good year I might get a length of fibre with a knot worth undoing, or a chip of worked stone worn smooth on one side. Mostly I would go out to see what had struck the leaf in the night and find a star of white splinters where a bead of ice had landed. The sound it made coming down would stay with me longer than the splinters.

I’d make mats. Ours have to be stiff enough to lie across a seam and loose enough to roll in a hurry. The trick is to leave the two outside fibres unpaired. It makes for an untidy edge, and people who learned from somebody else are always tucking the ends in when they come to visit. Once, while I was off getting water, a man did the whole length of one side. I thanked him and waited until he’d gone to pull them out again.

It would be while doing that, sitting out with the mat across my knees, that I first saw the shoe. I wouldn’t know it was a shoe. I’d think there was a nick in the upper leaf and wonder why no light came through it.

The thing about looking up from underneath is that you can’t see the person standing back from the edge. I could watch for most of an afternoon and never see Eda. She could be eating, or mending something, or sitting close enough to the lip to hear the pebble shift inside the shoe. All I would have to go on was the dark mark, and whether it was there the next time I looked.

I would try moving the mat. It’s a large mat. I’d drag it out past the shelter, stand on it to flatten the corners, then go back to my work and look up so often I’d lose my place in the weave. The mark would go away at the usual time. The next day it would come back. I’d move the mat again, though by then I’d have to admit that I didn’t know how much of our leaf could be seen from up there. For all I knew I was carrying it in and out of sight with every fold.

After a while I’d put it back where it dries best.

I think I’d be the person people bring a mat to when it has begun to curl. They’ll tell me it was flat yesterday. I’ll ask them to show me where they slept. Sometimes the mat is fine and what they want is for somebody else to stand where they stood and feel how much the floor has moved.

On those walks I’d get to know the opening from places where I’d never thought to look. There would be a good view of it from halfway down a stair I use every day. Two steps lower, nothing. I’d start leaving the water jar on that step while I rested my arms. Some days there would be a mark up there, and some days I’d watch a long time before I could be sure there wasn’t.

Eventually a child would ask me what I was looking at. I’d make room on the step. From where the child stood it would be all white, so I’d take the jar in my lap and let them stand a little higher.
