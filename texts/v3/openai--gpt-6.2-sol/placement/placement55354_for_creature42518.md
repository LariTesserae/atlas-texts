I’d be in the avenue, a little below the place where a grain stops rolling. I’d have come farther than the grain, though it would have taken me longer.

I’d been a wedge in a doorway. For a long time I thought the doorway was getting narrower. What was happening was that the rib had learned its bend and no longer bore down on me. The wall went on growing behind my thin end. After each winter there was a little more of my blunt end in the street. People wore it smooth passing me. Then there stopped being people who passed at that height.

The report that got me out wasn’t an especially loud one. I struck the paving twice, once on my end and once flat, and lay with the face that had been against the rib turned upward. It was rough. I hadn’t known that about myself.

I went down the slope a little at a time. I could move when the paving shook, but most winters the shaking only turned me crosswise, so that the next winter had to turn me back. A grain caught beneath me once. For as long as it took the floor to take it in, I could tip from one edge to the other. I did it whenever there was enough of a tremor. It was the first motion I’d had that didn’t get me anywhere.

By the time I reached the turn in the grain where the two young ones put their feet, I’d grown a fine nap on my underside. It caught on the fibers and kept me there. One of them lay with her heel on my thick end. This lifted the thin end against the side of the other’s foot. She moved her heel and I lay flat again. A little later she did it on purpose.

They tried to talk that way for part of a winter. It was an awkward way to talk. One could only press and the other could only wait for the pressure to go off. Mostly they went back to putting their feet together. But sometimes one arrived before the other and set a heel on me without pressing. I could tell which one it was by where the weight came down. Neither of them knew this. Each thought she was the one who always got there first.

The nap on my underside has grown into the paving now, except at the thin end. After the report, when they lift their feet to let the new rib finish striking the floor, I lift a little too. There’s room under me for a grain. So far nothing has come. The floor there is growing more slowly than the floor around it, and sometimes, between their visits, I can tip.
