At first I thought I would be the shim Ada’s grandson cut for her. I liked the idea of coming into the ward in his shirt pocket, dry and exact, with a pencilled line still across one face. But he would have taken me away again. There is no reason to leave a good piece of wood under a chair that doesn’t rock.

I think I would be the brick on which he rested his heel while he tipped the chair back. Not the one Ada turned over. Mine would have its name underneath, if it had one at all, and a corner gone where the water from the awning fell before somebody bent the sheet metal.

I would know Ada mostly by the order in which she put her feet down. Coming out with tea, she set both feet on me before going on to the chair. Coming out empty-handed, she stepped over me. For a while I would have thought the tea was heavier than it was.

On Thursdays her grandson would sometimes stay until the pump came on. I wouldn’t hear what they said through the kitchen wall. I’d get the scrape of his chair, then the little pause in which he waited to see whether Ada wanted him to take her cup as well. On his way out he’d put his heel on my missing corner. It was a convenient place to stop and pull the back of his shoe up with a finger. Each time he did it I tipped a little toward the house.

There would be a week in spring when I didn’t tip. I wouldn’t know what had happened until the first warm rain, when water came over my edge and, instead of running down the old way, stood in a thin line against the sole of his shoe. He’d move his foot. The line would hold for a moment after he’d gone.

The next afternoon Ada would come out with her tea and stop with both feet on me. Then she’d go back inside. I’d think she had forgotten something. She would return with a saucer and set it down on my chipped corner, where she could reach it from the chair.

The cup would stand level. The saucer would not. All afternoon a little tea would keep to one side of it, and when Ada went in she would tip that last cold mouthful into the vines. She would take the cup and leave the saucer. In the morning there would be a snail on the underside, just inside the ring that kept it off the brick.
