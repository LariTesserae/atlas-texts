I would be the tooth the wheel was trying to get over that evening.

There is a bright spot on my sloping face where the pawl has struck me twice. The woman could find it if she took the cover off, but she has no reason to. When the men come down from the winding house, one of them puts a thumb on the rim and turns the wheel a little to see whether it’s free. He always turns it forward. I don’t know whether he was told to do that or whether it’s simply easier from where he stands.

I used to come round several times in a shift. There was time between cars for the oil to run down to the root of the tooth below me, but not for dust to settle on it. I knew the empty cars by how little of the shed shook. A man waiting to go up would sometimes rest his lunch tin on the cover. You could hear the spoon inside it when the car came in.

Now it takes me most of a winter to come round. For much of that time I face the wall. I know where the sunlight is by which part of the cover ticks as it cools. I know when the woman has brought an apple because she sets it on the workbench before she takes off her coat, and it rolls as far as the little hollow where the vise used to be.

That evening she stayed after the light went. From inside the cover I heard one end of the broom touch down, then the other. I had expected her steps to go away at once. Instead she stood in the open doorway for a while, holding the door by its handle. The hinges didn’t make a sound. She had oiled them the week before.

I couldn’t see what crossed the broom. In the morning I heard her sanding it, and when she tried the handle with her palm she found a place she’d missed and sanded that too.

The next time the wheel moved, it was still light. The pawl came up my face slowly enough that I could feel it catch in one of the two little dents. Then it passed over. Immediately below me, another tooth took the weight.

The woman was eating her lunch. At the sound she turned her head toward the window, but she didn’t get up. She had a boiled egg in one hand and was taking the shell off in small pieces. By the time she finished, there would have been nothing to see on the white rock. She folded the pieces into the paper the egg had come in and put them back in her bag to take home.
