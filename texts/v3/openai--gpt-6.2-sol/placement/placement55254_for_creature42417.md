I think I would start by trying to get above the valley. At the fork I’d take the smaller root uphill, on the mistaken understanding that a high place would give me a view of the whole. I’d get as far as the turn and sit there with one leg on either side of the root, trying to work out how the grazers did it. They would come up behind me and go round. Their feet know the sides of the root as well as the top. Mine would keep looking for the top long after there wasn’t one.

I’d come down with a crease across the inside of each knee and a broad leaf I’d pulled loose to see whether it shone on both sides. It wouldn’t. I’d find that out by setting it over my face at the foot of the fork. Someone coming along the lane would ask if I was all right, and I’d say yes from under the leaf. I might stay there a little longer than I needed to.

So I think I’d be a person who makes the shades people hang beside their mats. I’d sleep beyond the ridge with the others, but I’d work on the near side of it, where there was room to lay a leaf flat. The trick would be to fold it while the middle rib was still supple. Too early and the halves would ease apart as soon as I let go; too late and the rib would split into shining little windows. I’d carry a finished shade into the stems and put my head inside it to check the fold. From the lane it would look as though I’d been swallowed up to the shoulders.

The boy would come to find me one day with the wet stalk in both hands. He’d seen me make a patch of dark and would want one small enough to set over an insect. I’d cut him a piece and prop it up on two bits of stem. We’d wait. The insect would go along the wet fold until it came to the shade, and then it would turn and go back.

I’d think we had made it too dark. The boy would move one prop so the leaf came down and touched the wet stalk. The next time the insect came that way, it would put a foot on the underside of the shade and then another. We’d have to get our cheeks almost against the clay to see it walking there. It would be the one with the brown feet. He’d know before I did.

After that I’d make his sleeping shade with one corner left long so he could lift it from the mat. Sometimes, passing on my way to lie down, I’d see his fingers come out and feel along the edge for the corner. I’d put it into his hand if he couldn’t find it. From where he lay he could lift it just enough to see the low gap in the stems, where a grazer’s foot might appear. I’d get down beside him now and then. Most nights there’d be nothing there. We’d hold the corner up awhile anyway, taking turns when an arm got tired.
