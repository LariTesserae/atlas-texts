At first I put myself on the underside of the loose brick. There’s a hollow there that a woodlouse has worn to the shape of its back. When it goes out in the evening, the hollow is empty, but not for long enough to live in.

I think I’m farther in, at the foot of the clean cut in the clay. I’m a crumb of brick there, small enough to go unnoticed in the black earth, except that one face of me is red. When it rains hard, I turn that face up.

One night a drop comes off the dock and knocks me clear of the earth. I go surprisingly far. The next drop does nothing to me. By morning I’m lodged at the square corner, in the little space behind the leaf the snail didn’t eat.

From here the leaf looks badly made. Its underside is ridged and the ridges are hairy. When water comes between it and the clay, the leaf moves out a little, then settles against me. For most of the morning I’m under water. A bubble works its way along a ridge and gets past me, though there hardly seems to be room.

The snail comes back while I’m there. It goes over the top of the leaf this time without eating it. The leaf presses me into the wet clay. I think I’ll stay there, with the red face showing, but as the leaf lifts it takes me with it. When the sun reaches the upper bricks, I’m hanging a little way off the ground.

There isn’t much to see even from that height. I can see the cut end of the other leaf’s stalk, which has gone brown. An ant comes up it, feels about at the end, and backs down. Up near the missing tile a gnat keeps passing through the same patch of light. I can’t tell whether it’s the same gnat.

Toward evening the leaf dries enough to let me go. I fall into the crack the root found. There’s already some earth down here, and a white thread so fine it moves when I land. I settle against it. The next rain doesn’t reach us at once. First the clay goes dark above me, then the thread straightens, and then, quite late, the water comes.
