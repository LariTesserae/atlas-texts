I’d be halfway up the far bank, where the stair to the mountain trail turns and a level lane goes off toward the market. I think at first I’d be the person sitting at that turn, mending a sleeve. It’s a good place to sit. There’s enough light to thread a needle, and if somebody stops to talk, you can tell from the way they’re breathing which way they’ve come.

But the person mending the sleeve has only sat down because there’s a chair. When she’s done, she puts the chair back against the wall and goes uphill, biting off the end of the thread as she walks. I find I’m still there.

I’m the old kitchen the lane goes through.

The front door was always there. The door at the other end used to be a window. For a summer people went through by putting one foot on the flour chest, and then somebody took out the sill. There’s still a shelf at the height the sill was, just to one side. A person coming up with a load can rest the bottom of a basket on it without taking the straps off.

I have a patch of blue limewash where the stove stood. In wet weather it darkens in the shape of the missing stovepipe. I have a nail bent upward to hold a lid, though the lid is in another kitchen now and nobody who goes past the nail knows why it’s bent. For a while someone hung onions from it. At present it holds a mitten. The mitten has been there through two changes in the weather. A woman takes it down on her way to market, turns it over, and puts it back with the thumb pointing the other way.

Nobody lives in me, which isn’t quite the same thing as being empty. There’s a stretch around noon when the light comes in through both doors and warms two separate lengths of floor. The smaller of the duck boys knows this and eats here when he has something he doesn’t want to share. He sits in the uphill patch and leaves the downhill one for whichever duck has followed him. Today none has, but he keeps his feet out of it all the same.

At night I’m often the only dry part of somebody’s walk home. I can tell when they reach me because for four steps their shoes stop making that wet sound. Sometimes there are only three steps. Sometimes there are four, and then a long while before the fifth.

I wouldn’t put the sill back. I’d like to keep the chair.
