I would be the lamp at the bend, the one lying on its side with a stone caught under its rim. Before the ground moved, I lit the way to the pump. Now I light the underside of the road. There are pebbles in it the road crew must have rolled in on a hot day. When the pounding stops I can hear them dropping out, one at a time.

There is a crack in my glass. A root has got through it and swells a little every time I warm up. I used to think it would break the glass. Instead it has grown around the filament, leaving a space no wider than a thread. In the middle of my light there is a fine dark line. It isn’t much use for seeing by, but you can tell which way I used to stand from the way it falls.

The broad one passes close enough to rock me on my stone. I see its underside, which is paler than I expected. There are small round marks between its feet where the young have held on. One of them has let go. It comes backward through the light, walking as hard as it can on nothing, and catches its foot in the root sticking out of my glass.

For a while I have the weight of it. Not much, until the water changes direction.

The front comes down the road and I go out. My beam goes on shining under the pavement. I can see it from here, which is how I find out that seeing was never what I was doing with the filament. The little one pulls its foot free and tucks itself against my housing. The metal is still warm. Every so often it puts its square mouth to a screw head. There is nothing there for it, but it tries all four.

When the broad one comes back it comes low, with its forefeet dragging. It puts one down on either side of me. The little one climbs up; the root comes away with it. Then the broad one lifts a foot and I roll off the stone. I don’t go far. My cable has only an inch or two to give.

Under the road the old beam still holds every pebble in place. When the contact closes again, I find I’m pointing straight into the concrete post, too close to light anything but the socket. The little one there has its feet braced against the sides. It draws the chip toward itself, then pushes it out a little. In that much light I can see what I couldn’t from the bend: there is a shallow hollow in the back of the chip. It has been trying to get a foot into it.

I stay on. On the far side of the post, where my light doesn’t reach, the road goes on to the pump.
