I’d be on the bench. I’d have been down in the room once, though, when I was new to the city and took the warm handrail for a sign that the stairs led somewhere open to the public. I went all the way down. The noise was such that when I called up to ask if anybody was there, I felt my voice in my throat and heard nothing. On the way back I counted the last nine steps twice. I was sure I’d missed one in the dark.

Years later I’d take the bench most mornings on my way home from cleaning the swimming baths. My hair would be dry but the backs of my trouser legs would be wet. There is a trick to hosing down the undersides of the benches in a changing room without getting yourself wet, and every few weeks I’d think I’d got it.

I’d sit at the end nearest the crossing, with my feet out. There is a woman who comes along early with a pram and a boy old enough to walk but not always willing to. I’d draw my feet in for them. Sometimes the boy would sit beside me quite suddenly, as though the decision had been made some distance back and he’d only just reached the place where he could carry it out. His mother would go on a few paces before she noticed. Neither of us would say anything. He’d have his feet out too, though his didn’t reach the ground.

I think I’d know the bench had a loose end. Not loose enough to report. If I put both hands beside my hips and lifted myself a little, I could feel it settle again when I sat down. In winter I’d do that to get my coat out from under me. That would be the whole of what I knew about it.

On the morning the young one got into the wall, I’d still be at the baths when the first tram went over. Somebody would have left a hair elastic around the cold tap, and I’d be trying to roll it off without breaking it. Later, when I got to the bench, the boy would already be there. He’d move over, though there was plenty of room.

I’d sit where he’d been sitting. Through the damp cloth I’d feel, for a little while, how warm he’d left the seat.
