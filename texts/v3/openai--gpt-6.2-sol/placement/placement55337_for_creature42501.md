I would start in the shelter, with one wet sleeve. I think I would stay there long enough to mistake the bird’s waiting for company. Then it would take something from the green an inch from my leg and swallow it, and I would see how little it had needed me to sit still.

The place I would end up is the narrow passage. Not in it, but just to one side, where there’s room to sit with a knee drawn up. From there I could see the mother coming for a long way. On the smooth stone she walks with her head turned a little to keep the young one’s tail from dragging. I would want to help the first time the feet braced against the uprights. It would take seeing her do it twice to notice that she waits after the first foot lets go. If she pulls then, it goes back up. She waits for the second.

I don’t think I would have built either shelter. I would have hands, though. I’d know because of what happened when I put one on the ground. On an ordinary stretch of slate it would go cold from the heel outward. Beside the passage the fingertips would go cold first, and if I left them there they would feel, from underneath, the little blunt pressures of feet. Not the feet of the animal going through. Those I could see. These would go on after she’d picked up her young and walked away: four tentative pressures, then three, then two together so close I couldn’t tell which had come first.

For a while I would think this was something left over from the crossing. I’d come back to check when there hadn’t been a crossing, and find it happening then, too. It would happen even when my hand was stiff with cold and I had to lift it by the wrist with the other one.

What I would be, I think, is an animal that spends most of its time on the underside of the valley. I would have no view from there. There would be a great deal of work in getting a hand up through the stone, though no hole to show for it. Sitting by the passage with a knee drawn up would be as much of myself as I could get into the open at once. The pressures under my fingers would be my own feet, still trying for a purchase.

I would keep coming up beside the passage, even after I knew that much. One day the mother would set a young one down with its head toward me while she got a better grip. It would put a foot on the back of my hand. I wouldn’t feel the foot at all. I would see the toes spread, though, and for that little while I’d know exactly how much of my hand was out in the light.
