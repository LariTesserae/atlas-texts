I would live on the lower shelf, close enough to the north gate to hear a button come back.

I think I would have come there as a road-maker’s apprentice. My first job would have been to set a cup on the sill and watch which way the powder in it crept. I was good at that job and less good at the ones that followed. I could hold a measurement in my head all the way up the long road, but I had a habit of making both sides of a gate agree when one of them needed to be left a little wrong. In the end my teacher gave me the shelf to look after. There wasn’t much to do besides scrape the white growth off the lintel and keep people from bringing carts through. I stayed after she stopped paying me.

By then I had a room under the road. When someone took the bend above my bed I could hear their steps spread out, then draw together. For years I thought this was something people did at bends. Then a worn patch in my ceiling opened and I saw the black seed cases running straight across it. The old road was still there, taking the weight for a few paces. On the surface you would have sworn nobody had ever gone that way.

I kept a chair outside for people who came through the gate and found the shelf colder than they’d dressed for. People usually sat just long enough to decide they hadn’t needed to. Once a woman arrived with one slipper on. She’d put her foot through what she took to be the door of her pantry. We waited for the house to find its pantry again. She told me what she’d gone in for, and we talked about what she could use instead. When the way opened she went back in such a hurry that I had to call after her to give her the slipper. She had tucked it into the side of the chair to keep it from going over the edge.

In the evenings, when the knocking started, I carried the chair inside. One night there was a latch on it. I knew the turned wing from the way it stood a little off the body, like the lid of a box with something caught under it. I don’t know how it got down. The gate had been open, but there were ledges all the way from the rim, and it might have spent the afternoon walking.

I went to lift the chair. It put a wrist around the back rung. So I left it there and sat on the sill myself.

A flower opened well below us. From town you would have seen its red mouth. From where we sat there was only a thin red edge, which widened when the stem moved. The latch leaned so far forward that one of the chair legs came up. I put my foot on the rung. It settled back without looking at me.

After that I kept the chair turned outward. It made a poor seat for travelers: they had to look over their shoulders to talk to me. But on some mornings there was red powder under it. I used to think the latch had brought the powder down from town. Then I found it on a night when no latch came, in a neat little heap on the seat. I held a hand above it. A grain struck the underside of my finger.

I still scrape the lintel. I leave the chair alone. Once in a while someone brings a second chair down from town, thinking I must want one for myself, and we sit in it by turns while we wait to see which way the dust will fall.
