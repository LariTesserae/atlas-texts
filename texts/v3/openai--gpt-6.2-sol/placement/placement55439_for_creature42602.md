I think I’d be up at the lip at first. I’d like to be able to put one foot down and hear the town, then take it back and hear the lining of my coat slip against my sleeve. I’d do that more times than there was any need to.

But I don’t think I have a coat. I think the sound is the dry edge of my belly passing over the shale.

I live in the signal lamp. There’s a screw missing from the bottom of its housing, and if I get my head in the hole I can work the rest of myself through. In the afternoon the red glass warms up well before the iron does. I lie with my belly against it and my feet on the rim, where it’s cooler. Once a driver stopped and wiped the glass with his cuff. I took my belly away. He wiped it again, waited a little, and went back to his truck.

There’s room in the lamp for two of me, though I’ve never met another. The other half is taken up by a loose washer. In a high wind it travels from one side of the housing to the other, and I have to lift my feet to let it by. I could push it out through the screw hole. I have put it there more than once, edge down, and then fetched it back.

Toward evening I come out. I don’t go far down into the bowl. There’s a place on the slope where the grilles begin to rattle before the rest of the noise comes in. I like it there, especially when a truck has just gone by. The tires leave two bands in the stone that are warm enough for me to tell which way it went, though by the time I reach the second band I’ve usually forgotten to care.

The evening the large animal comes out, I go farther down. From the lamp I can see something white on the shale next to its head. By the time I get there the mother has eaten it. I nose the place anyway. Then the large animal breathes out, and the little plates along my back lift one after the other. I move a little to the left. The breath reaches me there too, but only as far as my shoulders. I spend some time getting this right.

Later the animal raises its chin. The young one is asleep in the crease of its jaw and goes up with it. I can see all its feet, even the ones it had tucked out of sight. The big animal looks toward the lip. The lamp is dark by then. It’s looking a good way past it.

When it lowers its chin again, it leaves a little more space than before. I settle there, on the side away from the young one. All night, whenever the animal swallows, I have to get up and find the place again. By morning there’s quite a wide patch of stone I know in the dark.
