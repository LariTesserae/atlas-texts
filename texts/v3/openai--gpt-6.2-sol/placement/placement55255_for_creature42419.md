I think I would live a little below that house, on the side where the water had been held back. Not under another tree. I’d have a sleeping place there, and a pot to catch the one dependable drop, but the place I’d think of as mine would be a stretch of the old path that came out crooked every year.

It bent around a bed of short, dark leaves. You could save perhaps twelve steps by going straight. People did, especially when they could see the doorway of the house they were going to. Most got across. One year a man went in to his thigh and handed me his basket with great composure, as if this were the reason he had brought it. There were six eggs inside and a little cake with a thumbprint in it. While he worked his leg free I could hear a child on the far side asking if the cake was all right.

I would be the person people asked about the path. I wouldn’t have made it, or even know why it took the turn it did. I’d have time to sit at the bend, which is how a great deal of knowledge would come to me. At first people would ask how deep the water was. Later they’d ask whether somebody had gone by. That question would take longer to answer. I might have seen bare feet with a cut on the heel, or heard a basket set down twice. If someone sat with me a while, we could generally work out who it had been.

After the ridge gave, that part of the path would dry sooner than anyone expected. The first person to notice would be a child sent to fetch me. She’d come straight across the dark leaves. I’d tell her not to, too late, and she’d stand on the other side with both feet quite dry. Then I’d go across myself, slowly enough to annoy her. In the middle there would be a firmness I’d never felt there, even in the driest weeks. I’d have to put my hand down to find out what I was standing on.

A stone, as it turned out. Not a big one. Pale at one end where my toe had caught it. I’d spend a little while clearing the stems to see if there was another beside it, and the child would finally tell me what she’d come for: her grandmother had caught a fish and wanted to know if I had a pot wide enough.

I’d leave the stone where it was. On the way back with the pot, I’d take the bend, out of habit and because I’d filled the pot with water. The child would go straight, then wait for me without saying anything. It would be one of the small pleasures of growing old there, I think, to have known a place long enough to be wrong about it in ways a child could show you.
