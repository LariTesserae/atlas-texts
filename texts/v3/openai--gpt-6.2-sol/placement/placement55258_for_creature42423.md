At first I thought I’d be up on the ridge, where you can see a person on the bridge without having to decide whether they’re taking a long time. But there’s nowhere to sit up there except on the rail, and by noon the rail would be too hot. I think I’d end up down among the engine houses, in a room with a low window, turning out the pockets of somebody’s overalls.

I’d mend the work clothes. There would be a treadle machine and a pipe I could dry my socks on, except on Thursdays, when for reasons nobody had explained to me the pipe ran cold. The fitters would leave their clothes in a bin without saying what was wrong with them. It was usually the knees. I’d know who had been working in a tight place by the wear on the backs of the shoulders.

The person who could stand still with a young hem on his leg would be one of my regulars. His cuffs would come in with little snags on the inside. I’d turn them under by less than I ought to, so there was still something for the next one to get hold of.

During a lifting, I’d take my supper out to the lower walk. Not at first to look at anything. The lifting would put the machine out of level just enough for the drawer to creep open against my knee, and by evening I’d have had enough of shutting it. From the lower walk I could watch people crossing home. I’d get to know a woman who stopped at the middle weld to change her lunch tin from one hand to the other. In the weeks when the bridge was longest she’d change hands twice, once before the weld, once some time after it. I don’t think she knew anyone could see her from below.

One evening there’d be a young hem on the rail beside me. I’d know it was young because of how much of it there was to see. It would put a foot on my sleeve while I was eating. I’d stop with the spoon halfway to my mouth, which is a much less comfortable position than it sounds. After a while I’d put the spoon down anyway. The hem would take its foot off me. Then it would put it back.

I wouldn’t become especially good with them. I’d get bitten once, trying to get one out of the bin, and after that I’d tip the overalls onto the floor and let whatever was in them find its own way out. But I think I’d start taking supper down there even when the machine was level. I’d bring my mending if there was light enough. Above me the woman would set her lunch tin down on the bridge to work some feeling back into her fingers. From underneath I’d hear the small hollow sound of the tin touching the plate. Then, for a while, neither of us would be on our way home.
