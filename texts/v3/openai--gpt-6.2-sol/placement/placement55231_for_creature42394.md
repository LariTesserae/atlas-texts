At first I think I’d be down in the room, on the last stair where there’s just enough air to stand and breathe. But I don’t think I’d go down often. I’d be the one in the yard with a hand on the gate, waiting for the woman at the last tub to tell me she had enough.

She always said enough before she had enough. If I let the gate fall at the first word, she’d put her head out of the washhouse and ask for another dipper. I learned to wait for the sound of the dipper laid flat on the boards. Then I could let the gate down. The trough would give one last knock, and I could hear her wringing out the cloth she wiped the tubs with.

I’d know something about the room from having held a lamp while it was built. The bricklayer sent me up twice to trim the wick. I thought he was particular about seeing his work, but the second time I came back he asked me to sit on a different step. It was my knee that had been in his light. I can still put my hand on the place where I sat, without looking.

Once, after the slots were cleared, I’d lift the tile to see whether the wire had been left below. I’d find the sand spread halfway across the floor. At the time I’d be pleased: I’d been told to keep an eye on the little heap beneath the feeder, and here it had got smaller without my having to do anything. It would take me a day or two to understand what I’d seen.

By then I’d have got into the habit of looking. One evening I’d see a pink grain travel along the bottom stair. There’d be no current to take it, and it would go the wrong way besides, toward the wall. I’d lean farther over and lose it in the reflection of my own sleeve. I’d roll the sleeve up, though it wasn’t the sleeve that was in the way.

What I’d want, after that, would be to see the grain move again. I’d lift the tile before lowering the gate, so as not to waste the bit of light that remained in the jars. Most evenings there’d be nothing I could swear to. Once I’d watch the same grain for so long that the woman would come out and ask whether I’d dropped something.

“Not yet,” I’d say. By then my wrists would be aching from holding up the tile. She’d take the other side, and together we’d lower it into its square, slowly, so as not to catch either of our fingers.
