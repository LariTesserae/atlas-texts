I think I would live behind the canteen, in the wall made of the ends of firings. Not in the oldest part. The older pieces have settled against one another so closely that there is scarcely room for dust. I would be in a joint low down, where a comparatively new piece had been put in with its writing sideways.

At first I thought I might be the person who reads the names for people who can no longer get down on one knee. There must be an afternoon now and then when somebody comes looking for a name and finds that the knee will go down perfectly well but has its own ideas about coming up. But I don’t think I would be much good at finding names. I would know the pieces by which ones stayed warm after the shift changed, and which ones gave a little click just before dawn. I would be one of the flat animals. I would have six feet and a chipped place on my back that made me look like a piece of something a person had already tried to sweep up.

The wall would be a good place to live except at lunchtime. The shells would come over the edge and get everything worth having. Even a crumb small enough for me to carry would be worth stopping for, to a shell. For a while I’d go out at the bell anyway. Then I’d learn to wait for the scrape of the bench. The workers pushed it back with their heels when they stood up, uncovering a strip of pavement the shells hadn’t been able to get at. One worker ate an especially brittle sort of biscuit. I might know her by the way she sat down, long before I knew that she was the person who brought it.

One day she’d bring a child to the wall. I would be out in the strip behind the bench, and the child would see me take hold of a crumb too large to get through my joint. He’d watch for a while, then break it with his thumbnail. He would do this so slowly and carefully that I’d have time to retreat all the way into the wall. By the time I came out again there would be four pieces, any one of which I could manage.

I don’t think I would remember him the next time. I would remember that food sometimes broke apart if I left it alone.

In the cold part of the year I would go farther inward, behind the canteen stove. There would be a gap there I could enter only by turning on my side. On the warmest days I’d come back to the wall and lie on the sideways writing. Someone would put a finger down beside me to show where their name began, and I’d move just far enough to let the sun reach the place they’d covered.

From that low down I could see the basin only through the gap beneath the bench. Mostly what I’d see would be shoes. But after the last person left, if I stayed out long enough, the first amber light would come on in that gap. I wouldn’t know there was a person beside it. In any case the stone would be cooling, and I would usually go in before the second light. Once or twice in summer I would stay. I imagine the two lights would look, from where I lay, like something small trying twice to get through a wall.
