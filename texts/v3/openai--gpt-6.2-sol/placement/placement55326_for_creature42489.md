I think I would be the person at the bottom of the cord.

The person who goes back for the heights has a steadier foot than I do. I go a little way with her, as far as the bed where the lintel is chipped on the left. She leaves her full tin with me and takes the empty one. On the way home, she says, she’ll be glad of the weight. I don’t understand this until I have waited long enough to start imagining the little sounds an empty tin makes against somebody’s back.

There is a shoe in the wall of the bed. I take it out and work three stitches, using the holes already made. The fourth hole has torn through to the edge, and I have no awl. I put the shoe back. Three stitches are not much, but the next person can start at the trouble.

By the time she comes back I have eaten the salt I brought for both of us, a little at a time off the bend of my finger. She has tied the cord around her wrist so the knots won’t drag. I ask how the cup sounded.

“It didn’t,” she says, and sits down.

At home I kneel to hold the end against the floor. The new jamb has a little foot that sticks out, so the cord wants to slant. The tallest child says this will make everybody taller. His sister says shorter. We try it both ways against the tallest child and find that neither of them can bear to be wrong about it. In the end we move to the other side of the door.

Some of the knots still have names. At one, the woman who went back puts her nail to the stone and says, “That was me.” It is barely above my shoulder as I kneel. I try to picture her standing there and all I can picture is her as she is now, crouching.

The last knot is high enough that she has to get a stool. We don’t know whose it was. The tallest child wants to stand on the stool too, to see what the house looks like from up there. He climbs up as soon as she gets down. He looks over our heads for a while and then says he can see where we keep the good wick.

That night I sleep on the raised stone nearest the door. I’ve been telling people that I sleep lightly here because the house is new, but what I mean is that when I wake I reach for a wall on the wrong side. This time my hand goes over the edge. There is a wedge under it, and another just beyond. I leave my finger between them until one lifts its pads and moves on.

In the morning the smallest child stands at the jamb. She was carried out of the old house asleep and wants to know whether she was little there, too. I put my finger on her old mark. She backs up to it and finds it with the crown of her head. Then she bends her knees, just a little, and asks me to make another.

I make it. I know who will have to carry that knot.
