I would be in the second pumping shed, in a shallow cupboard on the wall opposite the belt. The cupboard has a glass front. From outside, when the light is on, you can see a pale roll of paper in it and a pen resting against the paper. The pen looks too heavy for the line it makes.

I think at first I would be the pen. There would be some satisfaction in that: the long stroke lifting me, the interval letting me down. But the pen can be taken out of its socket. Someone did that once and put it back a little crooked, and for three days the whole plain, insofar as it existed on paper, stood an inch higher than it had any business to. I would be the little chamber behind the cupboard, where the pressure comes up a copper tube and presses on a diaphragm. The pen would be the part of me somebody could take away to wash.

For a long time there would have been a person who came in just before the light went out. They kept the cupboard key in a fold of their sleeve. They would hold the paper flat with two fingers and draw a short upright line at the point where they had arrived. I could not have told from the pressure that anyone was in the shed. The upright line was how I knew.

There is paper still. The roll turns, though the drive slips and sometimes a night goes down in the space meant for an hour. The traces bunch together until the paper is almost worn through. Then the drive catches and there is room again for the little irregularities: a valve late to close, a pipe taking a breath it usually takes on the next stroke. These are the things I would know best. I would know the plain rather badly. The tube enters the floor on the wrong side of the shed for the stair. Between me and the stair there is a broad passage under the slabs, and the water can take either of two routes through it. Much of what happens there never reaches me.

Once, when they cleared the passage, there was a change so slight that the person with the key took the pen out and bent its arm. They thought it was dragging. The old animal had drawn itself into the angles while the water was off; when the water came back, it opened a lobe into a slot that had been full of scale. On the paper there was a small shoulder where the line had always sloped cleanly down. Bending the arm made the shoulder fainter. It did not take it away.

I wouldn't know about the young one. Its fall doesn't move enough water, and the tick goes into the stone. For a few years it will be on the other side of everything I can feel. Then, if it lives, some part of it may reach the open joint and lie there on a long stroke. I may get a little less water in the chamber than I have been getting. Or I may get it a little later. The two look almost the same when the paper is moving slowly.

I would like to be there long enough for that. Not to find out which it was. Just to have a little room left on the roll when it happened.
