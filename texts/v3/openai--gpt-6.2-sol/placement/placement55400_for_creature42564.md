I think I’d carry the post. I’d have a narrow sorting room under the stairs at the station three stops back from the end of the line. In the morning, going up to the platform, people would pass close enough to my window to tell me where they’d be that afternoon. This would save me a good deal of walking, though not as much as they thought.

I’d know Iven’s father before I knew Iven. He’d be one of the people who still got letters addressed to the ground floor. For a while I’d correct them. Then one day he’d show me a letter from a man he used to hang doors with, and I’d see that the address was in a different hand from the letter. The man had got somebody to do the envelope for him. After that I’d leave it alone. There would be a card above my sorting bench to remind the substitute: GROUND FLOOR, TWO FLIGHTS UP. It would be useful for years.

At the far end of my round I’d deliver to the lamp crew. Their shelter would travel on a flat wagon, and the men who wrote to them from other districts would put things like *past the third crane* on the envelopes. The crew would seldom be past the third crane by the time I got there. I’d learn to look for a kettle on a shelf outside the wagon, where it could cool without warming the room. Once I’d take a letter out to the newest lamps, find the shelter gone, and come back with the letter and the kettle. They’d send someone for the kettle before they sent anyone for the letter.

I’d be no good at telling where a street was going. I’d ask Iven, as her father did, but I’d want to know for the sake of my feet. There’d be a short stretch of pavement behind the dentist’s that saved me six minutes each morning. One week it would get shorter at both ends. I’d keep using it for a couple of days after it ceased to save me any time, because a woman at an upstairs window had got used to lowering a bag for her post. Then one morning I’d find the bag resting on the pavement. She’d be standing in the window in her slippers, trying the new height with one foot.

The girl next door would ask me whether she could send a letter to the new street. I’d say yes before thinking to ask who it was for. It would be for herself, naturally. She’d write her name and *the new street*, and bring it down with a stamp stuck over the fold at the back. I’d put it in my bag with the lamp crew’s mail.

I’d carry it out twice without delivering it. The first time there wouldn’t be room to stand. The second time there would be room, but only at the mouth of the seam, and she’d be quite particular afterward that standing at the mouth was not the same thing as being in the street.

The third time she’d come with Iven. I’d see the two coats from the tram, the small one tucked under Iven’s arm while the girl went sideways between the leaves. I’d wait until she was far enough in that I couldn’t hand her the letter without going in myself.

“Here?” I’d ask.

“A bit further,” she’d say, and I’d hear her take another step.
