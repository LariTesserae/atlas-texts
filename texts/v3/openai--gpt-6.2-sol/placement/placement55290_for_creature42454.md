I’d be one of the grey animals under the floor, the one that learns to get into the room above.

For most of my first rains there would be no room above. There would be the underside of the floor, which felt warm against my back, and the line of air in which my mother’s whiskers moved. There would be the ledge where she left us. When the water got high enough to lift me off it, I’d swim the length of the wall and come back pleased with myself, only to be picked up and put down in exactly the same place.

I’d learn to climb at the corner where the wall had split. The bottom of the split would be easy to reach in the rains and too high in the dry months. I’d know how high the water was by how much of my tail I had to pull out of it before my front feet found purchase.

The first time I got into the room, a dauber would be at work in the recess. I’d stand dripping beneath her. Each time she went away, I’d get up on my hind feet and smell the fresh mud. Each time she came back, I’d drop down again. This could go on for much of an afternoon. I would have eaten already.

Later I’d bring food up there. Not often: getting a snail through the split meant climbing with my mouth full, and the little ones were hardly worth the trip. But there was a place on the floor where a shell, once set down, did not roll. Down below I had to hold a snail with both front feet to keep from losing it. Up here I could put it down and scratch.

I think that is what would make it my room, though the daubers would go on using it and a grazer would occasionally put its muzzle through the doorway. I wouldn’t sleep there. Even on a still night the air touched too much of me at once.

In the dry months I’d make the climb from a strip of bark fetched up against the wall. It would tip as I pushed off, and sometimes I’d go into the water backwards. Then one evening I’d come back and find that the water had risen in my absence. I could reach the split without the bark. I’d go up and eat my snail. Coming down, I’d lower myself to the old ledge and find two of the new young floating just above it, their little feet working. I’d put one back. By the time I caught the other, the first would be out again.
