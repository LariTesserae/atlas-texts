I’d be on the root where she puts the young while the water goes down. Not in the hollow with them. I’d be farther along, on the underside, where the root hangs clear of the stone.

At first I thought I’d be the other face of the break. There would be something to do there, even lying still: take the little scrape and give it back where it was needed. But I keep coming back to the root. There’s a place on it the mother has to cross on her way to the hollow, where the bark is worn through to the wood. I’d know her by the order in which her feet came down there. With a beetle on her lip she puts both front feet down before she brings either of the back ones up.

I think I’d have come out of the clay the root lifted when the ridge rose. A pale thing with too many legs to place them all separately. By day I’d fold myself into a seam in the bark and eat the soft wood at the back of it. At night I’d come out as far as the worn place. There’s not much to eat there, but after rain a drop hangs beneath it, and I can drink without letting go.

When the young are first laid in the hollow they move around enough that I go back inside. Then they settle. I can feel one of them swallowing. Once, when the mother is a long time gone, a young one puts a foot over the side and touches my back. I draw in all my legs. It leaves its foot there. After a while I put mine out again, one at a time, and go on drinking.

I wouldn’t know how many she’d had to begin with. I’d know how many were on the root because their weight would come off it in little jerks when she returned. Then there’d be nothing on the upper side, and I could go up and eat where their wet bellies had softened the bark.

Some mornings she comes back along the root after they’ve gone. At the worn place she stops and wipes her mouth on the wood. If she’s eaten, there might be half an egg left in the corner of it. She’s never yet waited to see what comes out after she lifts her foot.
