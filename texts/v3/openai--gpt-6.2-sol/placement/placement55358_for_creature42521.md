I think I would be the person they sent for when water appeared somewhere it wasn’t supposed to. Not a burst main. There were people for that, with a van. I’d get the saucer on top of the refrigerator, the dark patch that dried out on wash days, the drop that fell from the nursery ceiling every time someone opened the gate to the wet meadow.

I would have a room between the laundry and the fernery. It would be warmer than either, owing to a valve nobody wanted to move. On the back of the door I’d keep a coat for going out in and a coat for going farther in. The second one would have no buttons below the waist. I’d have taken them off after leaving one, on a Saturday when nobody knew where I was, in a wire ladder two floors below the kitchens.

People would tell me what they’d already tried. They’d show me the bucket they’d moved six inches to the left. I would learn to stand where they stood when they first noticed the trouble. This would take longer than it sounds. The woman with the saucer would have noticed the drip while taking her shoes off after work, and from the height at which I usually went into a room I’d have missed the fresh wet line running along the underside of the cupboard. She’d wait until I’d crouched down to say, “There, you see.”

I’d be wrong often enough that some people would wait for me to be wrong before offering their own account. Ada would not wait. When I told her the water behind her mirror was from the nursery, she would ask why it was worse on Sundays. I’d go upstairs anyway. It would be very pleasant up there on a Sunday, all the little coats gone, the long blocks in a box on wheels. I’d spend half an hour looking at a perfectly dry sink before coming down to ask if she did anything different on Sundays.

“I cook,” she’d say.

She’d let me have a bowl of what she cooked. We’d eat on either side of the folded mirror. I wouldn’t have an answer by the time the bowls were empty, but I’d know that the wall was warm at the bottom and cold at the top, and that the mirror, tilted as it was, gave her a view of the landing while she worked. When I came back with two little wooden spacers, she’d make me hold the mirror in place while she sat at the machine.

“A little to your left,” she’d say. Then, “No, my left.”

From her chair, after we got it right, she could see who was coming down the stairs as far as their knees. She knew nearly everybody by then.

There would be whole districts in which I knew only one room. In the payroll office I’d know the cupboard where they kept the spare carpet tiles and not the name of the woman who had given me the key. Under the yellow bridge I’d know a door that opened inward and could only be shut by someone standing outside. Len would know a way around that door. For a while he wouldn’t tell me, because it was the way he went fishing. When he did tell me, I’d see that I’d passed the opening a hundred times. I’d taken it for a place to put your bag down while you got your keys out. It was that, too.

Late one evening, coming back from a call, I’d find the man from the laundry at the upper end of the reopened passage. He’d have the washing with him. I’d say, thinking I was saving him a walk, that he could get through now.

“I know,” he’d say.

Then I’d hear the kitchen woman on the stairs behind us, stopping at each landing to settle something in the bottom of her bag. I’d go on through by myself. The new lights would be on, and in the middle of the passage there’d be a single drop of water hanging off the bottom of a pipe. I’d put my finger to it as I passed. Cold. A little farther on there’d be another.

I’d have a pencil in my pocket, but no paper except a receipt for two eggs. I’d write down which pipe it was, and the time, and leave enough room on the back to write down what happened tomorrow.
