I think I would come into it as a drip, though not the part that falls.

I'd be the half that stays on the sharp edge after the other half has gone. For a while there would be enough of me to hold the square of daylight upside down. Then more water would come, and I'd go sideways along the stone, picking up a little of the white at the end of an old route. I'd leave most of it farther down. Over a winter I might move it the width of one of the insect's feet.

But that gives me too much of a journey. I keep coming back to the crease.

I'd be on the inside of the fold, just past where the two faces of the metal stop touching. Not an egg. The space at the blunt end of an egg, perhaps, which is still there when the egg has been pushed in as far as it will go. In summer the insect's head would come down and cut off the light. Then it would go up, and there would be a new surface beside me, duller than metal, with a shallow buckle where she'd pressed too hard. For days nothing would change except that the buckle would slowly come out.

From there I wouldn't know the mountain had broken. I'd know the street by the small give of the metal when two people met on the landing and one stepped aside. I'd know the kitchens by the hour at which the egg's surface lost its shine. The sound of the ice coming off would reach me through the fold before it reached anyone on the stairs.

I'd have been full of water in the bad winter. No room then, even at the blunt ends. In spring the water would go away, and the eggs would stay a little longer. One might come loose on a day when nobody crossed the landing at all.

What settles it for me is the female coming back in winter. She puts her foot down and waits. The gutter is still moving, though there isn't enough movement left to see from the keyhole. She waits a little longer, then lowers her head to the opening. Nothing comes in after it. When she lifts it out again, I'd be just the amount of room she found.
