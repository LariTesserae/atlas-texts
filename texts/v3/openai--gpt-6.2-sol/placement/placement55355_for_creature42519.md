At first I thought I’d be the thing that eats the fruit. I liked the idea of being expected at a house without having to come in. But I don’t think I’d have the nerve to cross the street at that height, with the two walls sounding off and nowhere to put a foot except the sill of a woman who has gone to a great deal of trouble not to watch me.

I’d be one of the children, a little too old to catch lizards for the pleasure of it, still young enough to lie down beside a smaller child and tell him when to put his hand out. I’d sleep in a wall on the western street. There would be a hard place in the bark just under my hip. Every night I’d get into bed a little farther up, and every morning I’d wake with my hip on it. I’d have a knife I wasn’t allowed to take out of the house, which meant I’d have to bring things home to cut them.

The stool at the sill would be one of those things. One of its feet would sit in a groove and the others wouldn’t, so that whenever I shifted my weight the stool would knock. I’d take it home and pare the foot down and bring it back, only to discover I’d pared the wrong one. After that I’d set it down sideways and sit with my knees against the wall. It would be a good way to sit. From there I could see the woman on the road put her basket down without having to look straight at her.

The first time she took my finger I’d give it a little tug, the way you do when you think someone wants help getting up. She’d let go at once. I’d sit there with my hand flat until my legs went dead, but she wouldn’t take it again that day. I don’t think I’d tell her sister about that. I’d say I’d come too late.

After a while I’d know the hand well enough to notice when it had been doing work. There’d be a split beside the thumbnail, or a ridge across the palm from the basket handle. Once there’d be something under a nail that scratched me, and I’d spend the afternoon wondering what there was to dig in on that road. I could see most of it from the square. It was a bare road. But I’d never actually been on it.

I’d have friends who said they were going to get her out. I’d go with them as far as the bend, mostly because I wanted to see what they’d brought. One would have a length of cord tied round his waist. One would have a heel of bread. We’d get hungry waiting for noon and eat the bread, and the boy with the cord would have to go home when his mother saw what he’d used for cord. Years later he’d ask whether I remembered that we once tried to get her out. I’d remember the bread particularly well. There was salt on the bottom of it.

I think I’d grow up to be someone who went out the near gate fairly often. I’d be good at buying things. I’d know which handles could be soaked back onto their blades and which ones a seller had soaked that morning. I’d bring the woman with the dish fruit when there was fruit to be had, and when she said she hadn’t asked for any, I’d take some back out of her basket. We’d get along.

And some days, coming home late, I’d find a child on the stool with one arm stretched out and the other folded under her head. She’d be trying to sleep. I’d sit on the curb and put my hand on the sill beside hers, and when the woman let her go, the child would withdraw her finger very slowly, so the woman could find mine. Then the child would go off with the stool. She’d be using it for something else. I’d hear it knock against her shins all the way down the street, in the little gaps where you could hear a thing that small.
