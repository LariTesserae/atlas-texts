I think I’d be on the stairs when Sela went past. Not at either lamp, but halfway up, where I could still see to pick a splinter out of my thumb.

I’d have come to put in a handrail. The man in the upper room hadn’t asked for one. I’d seen him coming down that morning with a basin of wash water, taking each step sideways so as not to spill it, and gone home for a length of wood. By the time I came back he’d emptied the basin and was sitting on the top stair eating a pear. He let me hold the rail up against the wall. Then he tried coming down with the basin again and showed me that he needed both hands for it.

What he could use, he said, was somewhere to set it when he got down.

So I’d make him a low shelf. He’d hold the wood while I cut it. We’d get it wrong the first time, putting it so close to the stairs that he could only set the basin down by standing on the very step he was trying to get off. By the time we got it right it would be too dark to see whether it was level. He’d fetch the lamp and pour a little water into the basin. We’d both get down on our knees to look.

I don’t think I’d be a carpenter, exactly. I’d own a saw and be a little too ready to go home for it. On the fourth day I’d have made a wedge for a woman who kept her window open with a spoon, only to learn that she liked the spoon: when the wind changed, it fell into the sink and woke her in time to bring in her washing. I’d keep the wedge in my pocket after that. It would come in handy under one leg of the shelf.

I’d be living on the other side of the river, in a room with two doors. One opened onto the street; the other opened onto a room I hadn’t been into because somebody had left a pair of slippers just inside. I’d heard nothing from that room for nine days. Still, when I swept, I’d stop my broom at the seam.

That night, after we’d tested the shelf, the man would take the basin upstairs and come back with the other half of the pear on a saucer. I’d eat it where I was, on the stair. He’d go in again, and I’d hear him moving about overhead. I’d stay long enough for a woman walking home to ask if this was my building.

“No,” I’d say, and lift the lamp so she could see the number.

The next evening I’d come back without the saw. I’d bring two pears. I’d get as far as the shelf before it occurred to me that I didn’t know whether the man was home, or whether he’d meant me to come again. There’d be a little water in the basin. I’d set the pears beside it and go up to knock.
