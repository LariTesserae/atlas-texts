I think I would live one walk below Cada’s, under the place where visitors stop to take off their good shoes. They tuck the shoes into their belts and go on barefoot. Every so often somebody tucks a shoe in badly.

I would have a long shallow basket for those. It would spend most of the year pushed up against the ribs of my ceiling, where its only use was to make a dry shelf for the little white-faced animal. When I took it down I would find all the heels licked clean and one or two with the stitching worried out. I’d learn to hang it by a single smooth cord. The animal would learn to come down the cord.

People would say I kept what fell, but there’s no living in that. Most things go straight through. I’d make my living sitting on the lower side of the jump with my feet in a pair of loops, taking things people wanted to have both hands free of. A covered bowl. A sleeping child. A pot with something in it that had taken all morning to get to the right temperature. You can tell people to throw a pot and they will do it; tell them to throw you a child and they will lie down at full length and lower it tenderly into your hands, at which point there is nothing left for them to do but slide after it. A great many people who say they can’t jump have crossed that way.

I’d know Iren by the things he left with me while he went back for something else. Once it would be the forked shoot, which I’d keep out of the sun under my shirt. Its wet end would be cold against my belly. He’d take it back without noticing what I’d done, and I’d be glad he hadn’t noticed. A person his age doesn’t want every little thing he does to have two people in it.

My loops would be on the side where the terrace flowers. That’s where the work is, since from above you can put a bowl down on the last tread and have a good look at the person you mean to give it to. From below the lip hides their hands. People coming home after dark would call to me to ask if I was ready, and I’d have to ask what they had. “Just me” was the answer that made me put both feet in the loops.

Once, when I was new at it, I’d sit there through a whole meal with my hand out for a woman who had called down that she was bringing soup. I could hear her spoon against the lid as she made her way along the upper walk. Then nothing. It would get cold enough for me to put my hand inside my sleeve. By the time she appeared on the last tread she would be carrying a shallow dish of cut fruit. She’d seen me waiting from her kitchen, back when the terrace was in flower, and assumed I’d come up for some.

I would not get good at making sense of that. I’d get good at putting a lid on whatever somebody handed me and setting it where it wouldn’t get kicked.

In the wettest part of the year there’d be very little traffic. Then I’d take the loops down and rub them dry with a handful of combed rush. I’d sit far enough back from the edge to watch the birds without making them wait for me to leave. The one with the nest in Iren’s fork would sometimes come down with a strip of bast and step on the loose end by mistake. It would pull and pull, looking up each time to see what had hold of it. Once it got loose it never lost a moment to wondering what had happened.

At night I’d sleep with one of the loops hanging below my bed. Not a cup; I’d have spent enough of the day listening for crockery. The loop would be stiff where people’s heels had dried it, soft at the bottom where my own foot went. If the walk leaned just a little, the loop would brush the weave beside my ear. I’d know that sound from the jump: somebody on the last tread, drawing one foot back to try again. Before I was properly awake I’d have shifted over to make room.
