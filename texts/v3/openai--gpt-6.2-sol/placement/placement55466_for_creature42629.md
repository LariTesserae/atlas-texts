I’d work in the office that tells people where their street has gone.

Most of the work would be ordinary. A woman would come in with the number of a house she’d lived in as a child, and I’d tell her that the house was still where she remembered it, but the three steps to its door were now part of a retaining wall. Sometimes people would ask for the steps back. There was a form for that. In all my years at the office I’d see it work once, for a man whose dog wouldn’t go into the house without them.

I’d have drawn the picture the water office sent to the shopkeeper. I’d have put a little cross on the pavement to show where the warm water came from. When she came in to ask if she could see it, I’d have said there wasn’t much to see.

“I know,” she’d say. “I used to spend all day there.”

I’d take her down on a maintenance day. She’d bring a bag with two pears in it, not as an offering but because she sells fruit herself and that’s what she has to eat. I’d bring her the long way, which has fewer ladders, and she’d tell me halfway down that she’d have been fine with ladders.

In the room she’d put her thumb in the worn track and move it back and forth a little.

“He could have fixed that wheel,” she’d say.

I’d wait for the rest of the story, but there wouldn’t be any. She’d sit beside the warm square and eat a pear. I’d eat the other one standing up, because I’d been down there often enough to have acquired rules about it that nobody had actually given me.

Then the first note would sound.

I’d be about to tell her it wasn’t the rail when she put her palm flat on the pavement. I’d keep quiet. In a while the second note would come, and she’d move her hand a little, the way you move your hand on a door when somebody on the other side has begun to open it.

On the way back she’d ask for another copy of the drawing. At the office I’d pull out the original and ask her what I’d got wrong. She’d turn it around on the desk. I’d drawn the room from the threshold, which was the only way I knew to draw a room, but from where she used to sit, waiting for her father to finish, the gutter ran the other way.

I think I’d spend a good part of my life making that second set of drawings. I’d still have an office upstairs and a drawer full of forms for getting your steps back. But people would start bringing me things I hadn’t known to ask for: the side of a fountain you stood on if you wanted the sun, the bit of curb you could sit on without getting your coat wet. I’d go out and find those places. Sometimes the side with the sun would be facing into the mountain, and that would be that. Sometimes there’d be room to put a hand there.

On the drawing of the lowest room, I’d leave the cross where it was. Beside it I’d draw a little circle for where the pear seller’s daughter used to sit. She’d look over my shoulder and move my pencil a thumb’s width nearer the wheel.

“Otherwise he couldn’t see me,” she’d say.
