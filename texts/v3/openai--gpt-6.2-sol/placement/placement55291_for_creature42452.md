I’d come to the room to take the machine out.

By then there would be a name for the room, though on my work order it would be the wrong name. The right one would be written on the back of the door in pencil, with an arrow pointing down. I’d prop the door with my bag and go down sideways, carrying a wrench too long to let swing.

I’d know the machine. Not this one, but enough of them to put my hand out for the lifting eye before I had a good look at it. Someone would have painted the eye yellow and someone else would have painted over the yellow. Under a loose curl of the second paint there would still be a bit bright enough to get on my thumb.

The bolts would be worse on the water side. I’d start with those. There’s a pleasure in finding out early how bad a job is going to be, especially if you’re being paid by the hour.

I don’t think I’d see much of the room that first morning. I’d see where to put the jack. I’d see that the empty drum was in the way, and when I moved it I’d see how much darker the concrete was underneath. Then the motor beyond the door would stop and I’d hear a drop land inside the drum in its new place. I’d move it another inch. It would take a while for the next drop to tell me whether I’d moved it far enough.

By lunch I’d have three of the feet free. At the fourth I’d lie down with my cheek close to the floor and find a line of white paint going in under the foot and coming out the other side. It would be sharp and clean in there. A long time ago somebody drew a rectangle, and for all the years the machine stood in it, that inch of paint never got walked on. I’d have to scrape some of it off to get a wedge in.

When the machine came up, the shims would stay on the floor. I’d pick them up one at a time: two steel, one brass, and a little square cut from the lid of a tin. Someone had needed just that much more. I’d keep the square. Not as a keepsake. I have a compartment for pieces like that.

I’d be halfway up the stair before remembering the wrench. Going back for it, I’d come down without the bag, without anything to set down or fit into place. My eyes would be used to the light by then. From the bottom tread I’d be able to see all four pale squares where the feet had been. Water would already be crossing one of them. It would get as far as a grain of grit and go around it on both sides.

I’d wait long enough to see the two sides meet. Then I’d take my wrench and go.
