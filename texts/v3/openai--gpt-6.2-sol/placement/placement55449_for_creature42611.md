I’d be the foot under Sella’s brother’s bench. The left one, made of alder, with a split across the heel. He drives it into a shoe to hold the leather steady while he works. There’s a right foot, too, but it’s a little bigger. We weren’t made as a pair.

For most of the day I’d know the town from the inside of its shoes. The woman who keeps to her side of the public stair wears a ridge into her left insole where she braces on the turns. Pell comes in with fern spores in his socks and insists he hasn’t been through the ferns. The shoemaker tips a little pile of brown dust out of his shoe and says nothing. That’s one of the things I like about working for him. He doesn’t require a shoe to tell him everything it knows.

An old shoe is easier to get into, but it takes me longer to get out. For a while after he pulls me free I still have the feeling of a person about to put their weight down. If he’s mending both shoes, I can sometimes cross the shop in those few seconds. My right step makes no sound, and my left makes the sound you’d expect from a block of wood dropped on a floor. He thinks it’s the hearth crab getting its brick.

I wouldn’t be the one who goes out to see where the drinkers go. I’d get as far as the door most nights and stand there until the feeling wore off. But one evening the shoemaker would put me outside to dry. He’d washed glue off my heel, and the crack had taken water. He’d set me down pointing into the shop. By the time he came to fetch me I’d have turned around.

Across the road, on the near side of the market, there’d be a wet print with a long smear behind it. I’d seen that mark before on a shoe brought in with the heel worn right through. The man it belonged to went down the stair sideways now. The mark in the square faced uphill.

The next evening I’d get a little farther. I’d bring a scrap of leather stuck to the peg in my heel. It would take three evenings to get the scrap across the road. On the fourth, I’d wait beside it while the animals came out of the wall. By then the leather would be damp and curled, not much good for a repair.

Nothing would step on it. I’d be thinking about how to get it home when the paving took a footstep beside me. Another landed a little ahead. Then the first foot dragged forward, slowly enough that I could hear the skin catch on the stone.

In the shop there was a narrow stool for people to sit on while they tried their shoes. I couldn’t bring the stool. I could turn sidewise, though, and put my heel in the shallow joint between two stones, where it wouldn’t slip.

A weight came down on me. Not much, and not for long. I could feel the toes spread.

In the morning the shoemaker found me there. He brought me in and put me on the bench to see whether the wet had warped me. I had a shaving of pale stone jammed in my heel. He worked it out with the point of his awl, then reached under the bench for the other foot. He stood us side by side, looking down at us.

All those years he’d kept us in the same box. I don’t think he’d ever tried to make us stand.
