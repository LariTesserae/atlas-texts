At first I thought I would sit on the middle stone. I’d put one finger in the cup and keep the other hand underneath, trying to catch the water in the act of coming through. I could spend a long time on that. But the closer I get to the stone, the less I want to be on top of it.

I think I’m under the rim, in the little space a hand leaves when it curls over an edge. I know the hand is there because the stone warms unevenly. I move toward the warm part and somebody putting a finger into the cup wets the ends of my mouth hairs. I back down over two of my brothers. One of them hangs on to my hind foot in what I take, at the time, for a personal grievance.

When the cups empty, I don’t drop. Not at first. The water has always been close enough to touch by lifting my head. Now I lift it and touch the far side of the cup. I go a little way around the rim to see if the water has gone there.

By the time I land, the first great splash is over. I find a warm slope and walk up it. It is a person’s foot, though I won’t ever know this. I will know that it has a fine, loose skin that gives when I push it, and that it smells faintly of the fern bed at the far end, where the person must have stepped getting to the stone. I stand on it long enough for the person to want to move and decide not to.

Then a leaf tips. I go.

For several days I live under a leaf I can’t walk across. It buckles at the first push and springs back at the second. I keep trying from different sides. Eventually I eat the grub that has been living in its stem, and the leaf settles flat. For a while afterward I expect this to work with other difficulties.

When I’m grown, I’ll sleep at the end where the ferns are low enough to pull down without standing on my rear feet. I’ll have a frog that jumps a little late each time I get up. And when the cups empty, I’ll go out with my mouth to the floor and wait for the small feet. I won’t be particularly good at gathering them. The first one I catch will get a foot into my nostril and I’ll stop dead, afraid of sneezing it out.

There will be one still in the middle after I’ve taken the others in. I’ll hear it testing something that gives a little but does not move away. I could go and fetch it. Instead, I’ll put one foot on the stalk of a full leaf and push. The water will come down all at once, just in front of me. Then I’ll have to wait, with my mouth open, to find out what I sounded like from there.
