I would come in on a beetle’s foot and get off by accident.

At first I’d take myself for a root. I’d be white, with a fork at one end, and I’d spend the better part of a day under the washer. But a root would have something to do down there. It would put out a finer root or grow around the worm. I’d just get wet and turn clear. At the end of the day, when the washer let go of me, I’d still be the same length.

A thread, then. Not even a whole thread, but one of the fibers that had been twisted to make it. There might be more of me on the cuff of somebody who unloaded a truck in the yard. Nobody would have to come looking.

The river would move me a little at a time. I’d go lengthwise through spaces the springtails had to go around, then catch crosswise in a space they could walk through without lowering their heads. Once, when I was stretched between two grains, the animal from under the paint would take hold of one of them. I’d feel the pull before I saw her. She’d turn the grain over, and I’d go slack. She’d eat from the underside and move on. For the rest of the afternoon I’d lie in the shallow place she’d made, with one end sticking up.

I think that’s where I’d be when the drop struck. Not under the roof, but just beyond its low edge, where one of the two little runs of water would flatten me against the sand. The water would be gone almost at once. I’d keep a little of it. Sand would stick to me, first at the fork and then all the way along, until from above I looked like a crooked row of grains.

Later, when she came out, one of her feet would land on the covered part of me. The other end would rise. She’d stop. I’d stay up as long as she kept her weight there, and then she’d take the next step and I’d lie down again.
