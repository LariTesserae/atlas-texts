I think I would be on the bright rock just past the shadow, where the little one put its foot and took it back.

At first I would be hard to distinguish from damage. The foot has left no print, but one of the pale grains nearby leans on its stalk. At noon its shadow touches the shadow of the next grain. There is no wind up there, and yet by the following noon the two grains are a little farther apart.

I would spend a long time getting from one to the other. From below there would be nothing to see. Inside the cleft, though, one of the new leaves would turn a little before the click. The other three would turn at the sound as usual. Far down, the sleeper would open the groove along one rib, then close it again. I would take that for permission. There wouldn’t be much else to take it for.

When the little one puts its foot out again, there is room for it. The leaf has opened. All five small joints fit in the shade. But it puts its foot where it put it the first time, a little beyond the edge, as if checking whether the bright rock is still hot.

This time something lifts with the foot: a flake so thin the sun comes through it. On the rock it had looked no bigger than a grain. Held up sideways, it is long enough to reach from one joint to the next.

I think that would be my first body.

For a while I would go wherever the foot went. I would see the basin sideways, in glimpses, as the little one shifted in its sleep. Once, when it drew both feet up, I would see the white rib through the skin far below, and the four wires coming off it at slightly different angles. From there I could see something the noon light never showed from above: the wires did not lie quite at the bottoms of their grooves. Under each one was a little dark.

Eventually the flake would work loose. The little one would set its foot down and I would stay where I was, half in the shade. By evening there would be a hair’s breadth between me and the rock. A pale grain would come to the edge of it and stop.

I wouldn’t know what to do for the grain. I would remember the flat toe at the old leaf, how long it could stay in one position, and how little it lifted. I would try that. It might take me the better part of a night to lift one edge.

In the morning the grain would be on the other side. The little one, putting its foot down in the same place, would find the flake warm on top and cool underneath. It would put a joint under the edge I had raised. I would have to learn to lift a little farther.
