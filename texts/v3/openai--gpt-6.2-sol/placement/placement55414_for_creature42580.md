I think I would live under the loading ledge at the freight sheds. At first I thought I’d be the person who put the crust in the paper, but whoever did that could go home afterward. I’d be there when the bird came back the next morning and tried the same piece of paper again.

There’s a joint in the ledge where a little water gets through even when the top is dry. I’d be the lacing in that water, such a small piece of what lives out in the pools that I couldn’t raise the water as far as the head of a nail. I could keep it against the underside of the ledge, though. In the afternoon, when the iron on the freight bed was warm, the drop would lengthen until it touched a blade of grass. I’d have a little more room then. By morning the grass would have drawn away.

I’d know the older bird by its landings. It comes down hard on the good foot and sets the other one down afterward, with hardly any weight on it. The younger one walks right out to the edge and pulls at whatever it has found. Once it pulled a strip of wrapping down far enough to touch my drop. The wet went up the paper in a crooked line. I drew in, as I do when something touches the water, and for a little while the bird and I held opposite ends of it.

There was nothing in the wrapping that time. The bird went off between the sheds. A corner of the paper stayed under the ledge, and in the next dry spell it was the only part that didn’t blow away. I lived in its folds for three days. When the cloud came down again, I let go of the stone and went with the water as far as the first sleeper.

That might be where I’d spend most of my life. I wouldn’t know the town by its roofs or even know there was a rim to it. I’d know that underneath the sleeper there was a root, and that if I held the water against the root overnight, by morning I could reach the next one. Once, a wagon would go over while I was stretched between the two. I’d come loose at both ends and lie still in the dark water. Then, quite a while after the wagon had passed, the sleeper would settle a little farther into the peat, and the two roots would be nearer than they’d been before.
