I think I’d come down from the rim to fix a lid that didn’t fit.

I’d bring a square of sacking to kneel on and a little tin of red chalk. You rub the chalk on the mouth of the jar, turn the lid once, and lift it. The places that touch come away red. On this lid there would be three of them, well apart. I’d be pleased at first. Three high places are an easy morning’s work.

Before I got the file out, a girl coming down the steps with her shoes in her hands would ask me what I was doing. I’d show her how the lid rocked. She’d put a finger on the far edge and press, and the near edge would rise just enough for me to get a nail under it.

“That’s where you open it,” she’d say.

She’d go on down the steps. I’d have to wash the chalk off with a corner of the sacking. I wouldn’t get all of it, and for the next two or three rains there would be a pink line on the jar where she put her finger.

I don’t think I’d stop fixing things after that. A cracked dish is still a cracked dish. I’d get to know which ones could be lifted with the water in them and which had to be emptied first, a cup at a time, because the weight of the water would finish the break. I’d have a workroom partway down the valley, where a lane broadens enough for two people to pass if one turns sideways. In the doorway there’d be a good flat stone to sit on. In wet weather people would leave their shoes there before going down to the jars. I’d know whose were whose by what they’d worn through.

The first time the water reached the bench, I’d go down with the others. I’d want to feel the two movements. I’d sit a long time with my palm flat, trying not to make anything of the small motions in my own hand. Eventually somebody would come to the top of the steps and call that they’d put my supper by. I’d take my hand away. The stone underneath it would be warm, which would surprise me after all that cold water.

Later I think I’d be more often at the workroom than at the bench. There’s a trick to getting a wet shoe onto a cold foot without folding the heel in. You put two fingers down the back and leave them there until the foot is all the way home. I’d be the person a child could lean on to do that. Some would tell me what came out of the jar. Some would be working too hard at getting their shoes on to tell me anything.

And there’d still be mornings when nobody came by and I could carry a broken dish up into the light. I’d turn the pieces until I found the little ridge where the potter had stopped smoothing the clay. It would run across the break. That’s how I’d know I had the pieces right.
