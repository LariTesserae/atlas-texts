I’d be a little way down the mountain, at the first place you can sit with both feet flat. There’s no grass there either, but the slope eases and there’s a stone you can lean against without having to hold yourself up. I think I’d have left my pack there. Going up to the pocket, I’d have taken the cup out of it and put the cup inside my shirt.

I wouldn’t have made the cup. I’d have come from a house where we used it to catch the little bit of oil that ran down the outside of the jug. The uneven rim was useful for that. You could get it quite close under the jug’s lip. I’d have washed it before bringing it up, though in warm water it would still have smelled faintly of oil.

What I wanted was to find out whether the stone moved. I had been up once before, and on the way out I’d caught my heel where I didn’t remember catching it on the way in. At home there was a door that did that in the wet. You could shut it in the evening and in the morning have to lift it by the latch. I thought if I set the cup level and came back in a week I might find it tipped a little.

It took me much longer to set it level than I’d reckoned. In the dark I couldn’t tell how wide a chip was without putting the cup down first. One chip was exactly the thickness I wanted but broke when I pressed it. When I found two that would do, I waited before letting go of the cup. I don’t know what I was waiting for. By then my hands had got warm enough that I could no longer tell, by touching the clay, whether there was water in it.

I meant to come back in a week. It was longer. The jug at home left a ring on the board, and somebody folded a rag and put it underneath.

When I did come back, I got as far as the place where I’d left my pack. I sat down to do up a boot that had been loose since the last grass. As I pulled the lace, I felt the sole come away a little under my heel. There it was: the thing that had caught. I could put my finger into the gap.

I’d still have gone up, I think, if I’d been able to see the entrance from where I sat. But there was a stretch of climbing first, and then the business of finding the slant and lying down to get my feet into it. I took the lace out of the top two holes and passed it round the whole boot. That held well enough to walk on. I went home that way.

So I’d be the person it would know by the pause, who doesn’t know there was anyone there to notice. For a while, whenever I lifted the oil jug, I’d think of the cup. I’d picture it as I’d last held it, empty, with my fingers still taking some of its weight.
