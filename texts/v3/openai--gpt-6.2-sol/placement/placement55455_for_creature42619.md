I think I would live in the last house before the squares, though not in the room with the view. From the room I’d sleep in, you could see the ankles of anyone who stopped at the door. I would get to know people by how long they stood in the water before taking the step.

I’d make bowls. Not the good ones you eat from, but the broad, shallow ones people carry silt in. There would be a place beside my door where you could leave a cracked one without coming in. I’d know which bowls were mine by a little flat place on the rim where I rested my thumb. It would take me years to notice that people had begun to set them down with that place toward the wall, so there was room to get a finger under the other side.

I would want Eda to teach me what she hears. I’d put my cheek where she put hers and feel, mostly, the warmth she had left. If she needed someone to walk across a room while she listened, though, I could do that. I could go back and do it again without trying to make the second step sound like the first.

Her granddaughter would bring back the bowl she’d taken out to make the wall. There’d be a little clay left in it, too dry to use without wetting.

“He ate it,” she’d say.

“The wall?”

She’d put her chin out and move it from side to side to show me. I’d give her the bowl again and ask her to show me where.

Out on the square I’d have trouble seeing the place. She would point to two prints with a stretch of plain clay between them. I’d be looking for a smear, or some little ridge his jaw had missed. What she wanted me to see was how far he’d had to turn his head.

I don’t think I’d make anything of that until later. I’d go home with her and put the bowl upside down by the door.

After a while I might become the person you fetched when something had fallen into a crossing and moving your feet to look for it only made the water cloudier. I’d bring a bowl and sit on the step. Sometimes the thing would be almost at once visible. Sometimes we’d sit so long that the person who’d dropped it would go home and come back with something for us to eat. I’d like the moment when one of us caught sight of it and put a hand on the other’s knee, not wanting either of us to get up yet and spoil the view.

There would be one bowl I never brought inside. It would stand on the dry end of my threshold, catching the water that came down the wall. A shell in the clay just above it would send the drops a little out from the wall, and now and then I’d move the bowl to catch them better. In the evening, if I sat on the step with my feet in the street, the bowl would be close enough to my ear that I could hear a drop land in it over all the frogs.

I’d leave it there even on nights when I could have slept.
