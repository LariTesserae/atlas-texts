I thought I would be on the landing, where a person coming down the stairs might stop and ask me which of the three lamps was Eda’s. But there isn’t much need for me there. Anybody who has come down to see her already knows which lamp, and anybody who hasn’t will have to wait until she comes across.

I would be farther along, past the rack for the screens, inside a pipe with a slot at the bottom. The pipe would be bolted to the gauge board. There would be just enough room inside it for me to go up and down.

I would be a float. A hollow copper one, with a soldered seam and a thin rod coming out of my head. The water in the pipe would be the same water as the water outside, but it would have to come in through the slot. A boat could go by without lifting me. A pump could stop and for a little while I would know nothing about it. Then water would begin to come in, and the rod would rise past a little brass finger that Eda had set at the last reading.

In the first year I would be quite good at this.

Then a snail would lay its eggs inside the pipe. Most would hatch below me and go out through the slot. One would settle above the seam, where a shallow dent held a little water even when the level fell. For days it would weigh almost nothing. By the time it was heavy enough to matter, it would have grown used to traveling up and down with me. It would feed on the pipe wall at heights it could not otherwise have reached.

Eda would notice that the rod was low. She would wipe it with her thumb to see if a grain of sand had caught in the guide. Then she would get down on the bottom stair and put her hand under the pipe. Water would be coming through all right. She would go away and read two stakes and come back with the short spanner.

I’d spend perhaps a minute out of the water. That would be my one good look at the valley: the three lamps, the carpet in the boat with one corner turned over, the narrow place on the stair where Eda had set the nuts in order. She would turn me upside down to empty me and find there was nothing inside to come out. Then she’d see the snail. She’d lift it off with a fingernail and put it on the wet tread, out of the way of her heel.

After she put me back she wouldn’t read me straightaway. The pipe would have drained while she worked, and she knew how long it took to fill. She’d sit on the stair with the spanner across her knees. Down by her boot the snail would put out one feeler, then the other. I wouldn’t see it take hold. I’d be back inside the pipe by then, coming up so slowly that even Eda, looking right at the rod, would have to look away and back to see that I had moved.
