I think I’d come up on the last car with the laundress, though I wouldn’t get off with her. I’d go back down in the car with the driver. There must be a stretch after the upper stop where the valley disappears and the window gives you back the inside of the car: the empty seats, a thumbprint on the glass, whatever the last passenger has left behind. I’d be the person who picks that up.

Not the cleaner exactly. I’d have a broom, but my work would be at the depot, in the room where things left on the tram are kept. At first I imagined shelves full of objects with long histories. In practice there would be a great many lunch tins, two sizes of work glove, and things that couldn’t be kept overnight. Someone would leave a dish of taro with a saucer over it. I’d put it somewhere cool. In the morning the owner would come for the saucer.

I’d get to know people by what they said when asked to describe a thing without seeing it. A man who could tell me nothing about his hat would know exactly how much of the hatband had come unstitched. The laundress’s grandson would come in for a blue pencil and, on being shown three, admit he didn’t know whether his had an eraser. He’d have to go back up and ask one of the other children. By the time he came down again he’d have a pencil behind his ear.

There would be a high window in my room, at the height of the path behind the depot. From my stool I’d see feet pass and, across the path, the bottom of the wall where the shutoff leaks. I’d have learned to leave the window open a finger’s width in summer. If I opened it farther, the draught would lift the corners of the claim slips; if I closed it, I’d miss the sound of the laundress coming down in the morning. She wouldn’t stop to talk on her way in, but sometimes she’d set a fig on the sill. I’d know which ones she meant me to eat right away because she’d put those down split side up.

After the bench was moved, I’d find a washer in the last car, lodged where the seat leg met the floor. I’d put it on the sill. The boy would say it wasn’t one of theirs: he’d counted the washers into the shoe bag and counted them out again. The driver would say the bench didn’t have washers. Then she’d take it up anyway, because one end of the bench rocked a little in the new place.

I’d go up on my day off to see if it had worked. It wouldn’t have. The driver would have folded a milk-carton flap under the foot, and the washer would be sitting on the rail, too hot to pick up. I’d sit at the other end of the bench until the shade reached it. From there, for the first time, I’d see the depot roof from above and discover that the little window I’d been looking out of for years was directly under a place where people stopped to shake the dust out of their shoes.
