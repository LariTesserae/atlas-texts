I’d start looking on the white ridge. It’s the one place where I could put a hand down and be fairly sure of finding the same thing there when I lifted it. I’d put a finger into one of the old tunnels. The rain can get into those now. I wonder how far it gets before the passage turns uphill.

But I don’t think I’d be the stone. I think I’d be farther down, in a root that grew through the roof of a gallery and stopped.

There would be five or six of us hanging there. I’d be the short one. The end of me would have gone yellow and blunt, and whatever I did up in the leaf bed—taking up water, dividing around a stem—I’d keep that one end still. An animal would pass beneath it often enough that I’d know the small upward tug it made on the roof. I’d have no way of knowing that it was the same animal getting larger. For a year or two I might take the increasing strength of the tug for a change in the weather.

Then the hillside lifts. The other roots touch down first. It’s a poor sort of landing: their tips buckle, then work sideways through the old mesh. Mine is still hanging free. In the night the animal comes backward along the gallery, and for the first time its back brushes me. I bend. After it has gone by, I straighten a little, but not all the way.

I thought I might be the root that keeps the passage open. I’d like to be that useful. But by morning I’m growing into the space the animal has just left, and whatever passes that way next will have to go around me.

It takes a long time to reach the floor. When I do, it’s with the side of my tip, at the bend the animal put in me. I send a fine root out from there. Then another. They’re the first parts of me to grow in this country that have never hung in the air. They go down readily, with none of my hesitation, into leaves that were still green when I began waiting.
