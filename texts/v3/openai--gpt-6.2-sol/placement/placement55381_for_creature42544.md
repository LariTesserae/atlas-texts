I’d live on the public stair, in the house you pass through without knowing you’ve entered it. The first two steps are outdoors. The third is where I sit to put on my shoes. If you come down while I’m doing that, I put one foot inside the door and let you by. Sometimes you stop and tell me what you were about to go upstairs to tell somebody else.

In the evenings I take the bowls home. This isn’t work anyone gave me. I ate for a while at the sill of the third house, and when I got up I took my bowl back, and then it was silly not to take the one beside it. Now people leave them upside down along the upper lane. I know the bowls better from below: the belly of one has a ridge that fits between two fingers; another has been scraped flat on one side and rocks if you set it down the wrong way. The old threshold cutter’s bowl is unmarked except for a little crescent where he rests his thumb. The crescent is clean even when the rest needs scraping.

For a while I think I’m the person who knows which things belong where. Then the woman at the sill comes out looking for a bowl I’ve just returned to her neighbor. She’s lent it to him for so long that he’s forgotten it’s hers, and she’s forgotten it too until tonight, when she wants to beat something in it. I take her to his house. He brings it out with both hands and says, “I wondered why I had two.”

One evening the cutter’s granddaughter is sitting with her heels in the lane and her legs stretched flat on the roof. She has the empty bowl in her lap. There’s wax on the front of her shirt where she’s come back through the leaves.

“Would you hold this?” she says.

I reach for the bowl, but she means the leaf. It’s the long one that lies across the fissure. I lift it by the blunt end. Underneath, the older of the two roots is drawn straight. The other still has a little bend in it, enough that a black-foot could hang there with its belly against the warm stone. The girl looks for a while. Then she takes the bowl home by the lower lane, the long way round. I let the leaf down. From where I’m standing you could put a foot on it and never know.

The next day I bring my own supper down the lower lane. There’s a step beneath the cutter’s window where the rock stays warm after the roofs have gone cold. I sit there and he asks what I’ve got in my bowl. I tell him. He says his is the same, only his has too much salt. We trade a spoonful apiece. They’re exactly the same.

His granddaughter comes down after us with her meal. She sits on the next step and stretches her legs until her feet touch mine. The cutter says that if we’re going to eat down there he’d like to come down too. He makes us wait while he finds the shoes he hasn’t worn since the last time he went out by that door. One has a black-foot in it. We wait for that as well.

I think that’s where you’d find me, after a while: not always on my stair. There’d be evenings when I’d take the long way with an empty bowl under my arm. I’d set it on the warm step beside me, right side up so it wouldn’t look like something I’d come to collect. Someone coming down later could put a little of what they’d brought in it. Or they could move it over and sit there.
