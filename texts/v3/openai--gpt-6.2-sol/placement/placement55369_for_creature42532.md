I would come up the mountain with the relief keeper’s keys. The regular keeper can tell them apart through her mittens. I have to take mine off and try the keys one by one. By the time I get the door open I have a little burn on the pad of my thumb, which in the warmth of the station turns out to be a shallow cut.

For my first few shifts I would think of the rivet as the one place I didn’t have work to do. There is plenty to wipe down. Frost gets under the hinged covers of the gauges and makes the needles look wider than they are. The rag freezes in the time it takes to walk from one end of the seam to the other. I learn to fold it with the wet part in, so there is still a corner I can use on the door latch.

Then one night there is a bead of ice under the rivet. It is attached by a clear neck. I get close enough to see the upside-down handrail in it, and farther back, where my face ought to be, the yellow square of the open door. I bring my ear up to the wall. The noise is so much worse than I expected that I jerk away and knock my elbow on the rail.

I don’t get a look at the shore until the following afternoon. Even then I mistake the white backs for a single thing. On the next shift I look for that thing, can’t find it, and decide it has died. Then a scrap comes down the bank and the animal I thought was dead folds itself out of a dozen gray ones. By the time I know to look at the lip, the bead outside has gone opaque. I can see the bodies lean, but I can’t tell which one is late. I’m not sure I could tell even if my eye were better. The one that takes longest to come upright might be doing most of the work.

I’d still be the relief keeper. I wouldn’t come to know the rivet the way I know the pumps, by what I can hear from the room where I eat. But on a night when the pressure runs high I’d take my tea out onto the walk, set it down, and put my bare hand against the wall a little below the seam. The steel would be cold at first. Then I’d find the warm place where one of the copper fingers passes behind it. It wouldn’t be where I’d guessed. I’d move my hand half an inch uphill and keep it there until the pump came round again.
