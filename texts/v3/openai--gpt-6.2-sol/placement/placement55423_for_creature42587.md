At first I put myself on a roof. I liked the thought of being able to see a long way without being obliged to go anywhere. But by the time the far stairs began to show pale, my feet would be cold, and I’d have gone down to see who was cooking.

I think you’d find me one flight below the ovens, on a landing just wide enough to lay a door flat. I’d mend the hide doors people brought me. The frames would be the part I worried about. A tear in a hide could be made sound again, though not handsome; a split in a piece of good wood might run another finger’s width while you stood there deciding what to do.

People would tell me what was wrong with a door by showing me how they opened it. They wouldn’t mean to. They’d say it stuck only in wet weather, then lift it with a knee and a little sideways turn they’d been doing since childhood. I’d ask them to do it again. Sometimes I’d follow them home and find there was nothing wrong with the door. The step had tipped, or somebody had put down a new mat and was unwilling to give it up.

My own room would be behind the landing. A tread of the stair above would come through the wall at shoulder height, just far enough to hold a cup. On the day the spring party came through to reset the stairs, I’d forget to move the cup. Somebody would strike the other end of the tread and I’d catch it on the second tap, pleased with myself until the spoon came down separately.

For a while I’d think of myself as somebody who stayed put. Then a woman from the next district would bring me a frame too large to carry back alone. I’d take one end and go with her. We’d have to turn it upright to pass the sheep, and we’d stand on either side of it in the notch, unable to see what was holding up the flock. After a while she’d ask if I was still there. I’d put my fingers through the place where I hadn’t yet laced the hide, and she’d put hers through from the other side.

Her doorway would be lower than mine and open onto weather I couldn’t see from my landing. I’d hang the door, take it down, and hang it again. While I worked she’d go out twice to look at a dark patch on the stairs. The second time she’d bring in a stool she’d left there to dry. I’d stay the night. In the morning I’d lift the door I’d just mended and find that the patch of weather had gone no farther than her house.

After that I’d take longer jobs now and then. I wouldn’t become one of those people who knew every district. There’d be a door I’d worked on three times whose house I could never find from above. But on my way home I’d come to know, by a warm place where I put my hand to turn a corner, whether the ovens had been lit. I’d know it before I could see my door. And if somebody had set a frame down on the landing while I was away, I’d leave it there a little longer, lift my own door by its good edge, and go in and eat.
