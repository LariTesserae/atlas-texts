I would look for myself first at one of the three notches. I like the idea of being a place where you can see a long way. But I don’t think I’d be out in that light. I think I’d be under a loose plate, a little inland from the rim, and I’d be the small stone that makes the plate rock.

There would be another stone taking most of the weight. You could stand on that end and never know I was there. Farther along, your boot would go down a little and the water would come up through the seam, cold even through the stitching. You’d take your foot away. The plate wouldn’t come quite all the way back.

From underneath, the pale things would be easier to tell apart. There’d be one with a nick in its edge that went over me so often I’d know it by the little wedge of grit it always missed. Another would start across the underside of the plate in the morning and be back where it started by dark, having cleaned both sides of a line I couldn’t see. I wouldn’t know what lay beyond the seam except by what came through it. Once, a feather would come in quill first and get stuck. For days the current would work its free end up and down. By the time it came loose there’d be hardly anything left to go.

I’d have thought the day I moved would be a day of unusually heavy water. But it would be late in the dry season. One of the deeper passages would have gone quiet, and the big ones would be coming up. The bird would step on the plate, hardly any weight at all, but on the end nobody steps on. The plate would tip. I’d roll once and catch at the mouth of the joint.

For a while I’d have the view I wanted. Not the blue distances: a shallow pool seen from just below its surface, with the bird’s toes at the far end. It would be pulling at something I couldn’t see. Each time it shifted its grip, one toe would lift clear of the water and go down again in exactly the same place.

Then I’d go through.

I wouldn’t get far. There’s a place a little way down where two faces of rock nearly meet, and I’d lodge there with one corner in the current. The fall would turn me over, so that the face I’d always rested on would be the one the water washed. It would take a long time to lose the dark mark where I’d sat against the bed. Before it was gone, something pale would come down the wall as far as my upper edge. It would stop there for most of a day. Then it would put a little of itself out across the gap and touch me.

Up on the shelf, the plate would now lie flat at one end. At the other there would be room for something wider than a thumbnail to get underneath without folding itself. I’d never see that. For a long time I’d go on expecting the weight to come down from above.
