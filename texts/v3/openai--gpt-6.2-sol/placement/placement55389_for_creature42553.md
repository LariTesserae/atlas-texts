I would be in the nearer shelter. There is a place just inside the door where a person coming in on hands and knees might look up and expect to see a face. I would be a little lower than that.

At first I think I would be the one who kept this shelter. I would know which ribs in the roof had gone brittle, and turn them over before they split. I would have a place for things left by people who meant to come back for them. But there is nothing in the nearer shelter that needs keeping, and I don't think I would have got there by walking.

I would be a stone in the wall, with one face dressed and one left rough. When the shelter was built, a splint was wedged under me to stop me rocking. The splint is long gone. I still rock, though only if you put your weight on the corner, and no one does that by accident.

The woman did, once, before she knew me. She was coming back from the farther shelter with someone behind her. They were carrying the basket between them, each with a hand under the bottom, which had just begun to give. She put her elbow on my corner to let the other person get past. They stood like that for some time without getting past at all. Something in the basket had made them laugh and they were trying not to spill it. I don't know what it was. I know the quick changes in the weight on her elbow.

Now, going home, she sometimes stops and puts a hand on me. She does not press the corner. With her other hand on the floor she can make the tearing cease, and for a while there is the small sound of the roof taking her weight where her shoulder rests against it. Then she goes on. I used to think she was listening for something in the wall. I think now she likes to take her hands off the ground one at a time, to have that instant in which the noise has come back but she hasn't yet had to stand.

I have a rough face because there was no reason to dress it. For a long time it looked into the space between two stones. Lately, on that side, there has been a fine cold pressure. It comes and goes. Once it went away so completely that I rocked without anyone touching me, and a little flour ran out of the joint and down the wall. I had supposed the wall was older than whatever might happen to it.

On her way back from the lip, she comes into the shelter with her wrist wrapping undone. She sits and winds it again. There is a little crease in the strip where she always pulls it tight with her teeth. This time she stops with it in her mouth and looks at her hand. There is nothing in it. She turns it over anyway.

I would like to be something she could tell. But a stone is a poor thing to tell if what you need is for the telling to be over. Years from now I would still have the weight of the elbow and not know what was in the basket.

She finishes the wrapping. Before she leaves she presses my corner, lightly and then a little harder. I move. She takes her hand away and I go back almost to where I was. Then, from the rough side, with her hand already on the threshold, something sets me straight.
