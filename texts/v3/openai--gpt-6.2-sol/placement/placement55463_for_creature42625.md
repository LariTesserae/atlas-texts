I’d come down from the dry part of town on a bicycle with a pane of glass strapped to my back. At the bottom of the footbridge I’d get off and walk. There’s a place where the front wheel goes into a dish in the paving and the back wheel slips sideways, and I’d have learned it the expensive way.

I’d be the glazier. The sisters would know me because I’d put a piece of bathroom glass in their stairwell, and afterward the younger one had made me come back and turn it round. She wanted the smooth side in, where she could wipe it. I’d said it made no difference and she’d handed me the rag.

The works would send for me about the broken pane in the pump house. I’d get there during the dinner stoppage and do a good job. I’d pick the old putty out of the corners, cut the new pane an eighth short, and get the points in without cracking it. The old glass would come away in one piece, with a forked crack and a little triangular hole near the bottom. I’d wrap it in newspaper and put it in my saddlebag.

That evening the elder sister would catch me at the bridge.

“You’ve stopped my window,” she’d say.

I’d think she meant the stairwell.

No: she meant the pump house. When she was carrying a full basin across the scullery, the rattle gave her time to set it down before the blow. It was just enough time, she said, if she didn’t try to get it all the way to the table. She could put it on the step.

I’d tell her the putty would harden in a week. I don’t know why I’d tell her that. I’d know perfectly well there was nothing she wanted it to do in a week.

So I’d bring the old pane back. There’d be no putting it in its frame again without the foreman noticing, and anyway it seemed a bad thing to ask a woman to depend on a piece of glass that might come down any night. I’d take it into the little court and stand it on edge against the rail while I thought what to do with it.

The bridge lamps would be on by then. I’d see them in the tank, and then, quite ordinarily, I’d see them again in my pane of glass. Only there’d be a bit more of the bridge in the glass than there was in the water. At the near end, where the tank cut the reflection off, three steps would go down to a landing. A pail stood on the landing. Somebody had put a brick in it to keep it from blowing away.

I’d move my head to see what was beyond the pail and get my own sleeve.

The boy would help me after that. I’d hold the pane and he’d go round the tank telling me when he could see the steps. We’d quarrel over what he meant by *left*. Eventually we’d get it right. He’d ask if I could cut away the broken corner. I’d say I could, but we’d already tried turning the pane around, and from the other side there was no landing. Neither of us would want to find out how much of the glass it took.

I’d make a wooden frame with a foot that sat flat on the coping, and a little catch to hold it against the rail. Setting it out would be a job for dusk. In the morning I’d bring it in so nobody knocked it into the tank. The works would go on sending me regular work. There’d be a drawer for my tools in the sisters’ scullery, under the one with the spoons. I’d get in the habit of stopping there before I started back up the hill.

I don’t think I’d see anyone on the steps for a long while. Then one evening the pail would be on the second step instead of the landing, and the brick would be beside it, dark at one end. I’d call the boy over. He’d look for a bit and say the pail had been there yesterday. He’d thought I knew.

By winter I’d be living in the six streets. I’d take the room above the scullery, low enough to hear the elder sister moving about. The new pane at the pump house would still be good. I’d listen for the little slackening of the belts before the blow and, once I was sure I had it, go downstairs and ask her if she could hear it too. She’d say of course she could, standing here doing nothing. Let’s see if you can hear it with a basin in your hands.

So that’s where I’d be, some evenings: halfway across the scullery, holding the basin while she worked the catch on the back door. After supper I’d take the glass out. There’s a place at the rail where I can hold it by the bottom of the frame and not put my face in the way. Most nights there’s only the pail. I’d stay until my hands got cold; a little longer if someone had left it on a step.
