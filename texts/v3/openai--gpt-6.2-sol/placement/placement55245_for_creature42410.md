I think I would be the sound of the step, though for a while I would mistake myself for the step. I would know the weight of a boot in the middle of the tread, the ease with which it went down, the little catch on the way back up. It would take Iona coming down close to the wall for me to learn the difference. There would be her boot, and there would be no sound. Then, after she had passed, there would be me.

From the gallery I’d be a knock and a click. Under the shim I’d be two pressures with a space between them. I don’t know which of those the shrimp would attend to. The first winter, before Iona put the wrench to the other bolts, there must have been nights when the tread knocked and didn’t click. A boot could stay on it while someone leaned over to see how much water was left. The shrimp would have had to wait for the boot to lift. I would never have learned how to hold a weight that long. All I could give her would be the foot coming down and the foot going away.

I’d be wrong often enough to be of very little use. Once I’d come while she was already under the stair, and she would go on combing something off a leg. Once I’d come while the tray was still full, and the return chain would make so much noise that Iona wouldn’t hear me at all. But there would be a night when the shrimp had gone farther out than usual, after something caught in the white edge of the deposit. At the knock she’d stop feeding. At the click she’d turn. She’d have to walk back, not just fold herself in. Iona, standing with the basket against her knee, would get to see how fast she could walk.

Perhaps Iona would try the bolt again in spring. She’d bring a thin washer down in her pocket and sit on the bottom step with one foot on the enamel, waiting for the water to go. The washer would be bright enough to see by the light from the next basket. I’d sound while she was sitting there. She’d feel nothing move under her. She might put the washer back in her pocket then, or she might fit it anyway. It’s a bad step to leave loose for someone who doesn’t know to take the wall side.

After that there would be no way for her to make my sound. I think she’d miss being able to. On a quiet night she might put her heel in the old place and give it a little of her weight, not enough to test the work, just enough to ask. I wouldn’t answer then. I’d come later, while she was at the sorting plate, with both cuffs in the water. She’d lift her head. Under the stair the shrimp would draw in one thread, then the other. And for the few seconds before the cold reached either of them, there would be nothing coming down.
