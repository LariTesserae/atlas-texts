I would be under one of the mite’s feet. I would not know which foot until it lifted.

While the mite is tucked into the pit, there is no telling me from the rest of the dimness on the raw side of the glaze. The pit was a little darker than the rest of the flake before the mite found it. Some of that darkness belongs to the hollow itself. Some belongs to the cup. I would have to wait for the mite to move to find out how much was mine.

After rain, when the chalk at the back has gone dark, the mite puts a foot out of the pit. There is no direct light to make a good sharp likeness of it. I would be a grey thickening on the clay, scarcely longer than the foot, and joined to it at the tip. Then the mite lifts the foot again. For an instant there is a strip of lit clay between us. That is where I would find myself: not in the dark under the handle, as I’d first supposed, but a little way out on the clay, with light all around me.

I would go with it as far as the dead tuft. While it fed, there would be six places I might be, and no reason to choose among them. The mite would take its time. At the ragged edge, one foot would come down over an empty chamber, and I would fall across the opening without going in.

By the time the chalk was white again I would be back in the pit. The foot would fold in against the belly. The whistle would be as clear as it had been all day. Nothing would mark the place on the clay where, for a moment, the foot and I had been apart.
