I’d be in the cut bank, a little below the place where you’d put your hand to steady yourself on the way down.

From the opposite slope I’d look like a white ring. Only the lower half of it would move. The upper half would have dried when the bank broke open, leaving me with a mouth against a valve and a breathing rim that opened sideways onto the ravine. For most of my life the two had been several feet apart, with a good weight of rind between them. Now, when the valve closed, my whole body moved in the light.

At first there would be too much to listen to. The bank would return the beat of the pumps a fraction late. Husks would strike my rim and make a noise I’d keep mistaking for something trying to get out of me. At noon the wall would warm and bend just enough to lift my mouth off the valve. I’d go hungry in the brightest part of the day, with the oil passing so close I could feel it move.

There would be other rings in the bank. For a while I’d take them for neighbors. A loose collar would rattle in the wind, and I’d draw in my rim as I used to when something nearby drew in its own. After a season of this, a piece of the wall would come away and I’d feel how little it weighed when it hit the slope. Nearly all the white rings would be empty. The plant had gone on making the bank around them long after there was anything inside to press back.

Then a hunter would come along the face of the cut with a young one hooked under its belly. It would put one foot on the dry half of my rim. I wouldn’t know its tread. I’d close, and it would go on.

It would come back after dark. The second time it would set the young one down inside the ring before I could close. The young one would hook into me. A small pain, very near the air. By morning there would be four of them, spaced along the dry lip, and I’d find that I could still breathe if I let the lower half hang a little farther out.

I don’t think I’d get used to the hunter. It would leave by a different route each night, and sometimes return with enough weight to make the dry lip creak. But I’d get to know the young by the way they took their feet off me. One would lift all but one foot long before it was big enough to hunt. It would stand like that through the noon roar, feeling for something on the other side of me.

There’d be nothing there but the valve. Still, each time it shut, the little foot would lift and come down a little nearer my mouth. By the time the young one left, there would be a row of pale dents in the soft part of the ring, stopping where the rind began.
