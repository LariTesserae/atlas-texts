I would be under the last tree, in the part of the gap you can reach without lying all the way down.

At first I thought I would be the root. It gets to cross. But the root has work enough at night, and there is a little space beside it where the stone on either side still fits. A knob on the plateau points into a shallow cup on the wedge. On the lower lip of that cup I would be a scrap of the pale tissue, no larger than a thumbnail. My other end would be caught behind the knob. If you put your hand in, you would go right past me. Your knuckles would touch the root.

For a long time I would have no salt. Then one evening a crystal would come down edge first and stop in a slit in me. In the morning it would look as though I had grown a tooth. I would thicken around it, leaving one corner bare. On cold nights, when the wedge drew away, that corner would lift. For a while this would be the only way to tell how far the wedge had gone. By daylight the corner would lie flat again, and you could go on thinking nothing here had moved.

I don't think I would know about the crowns. I would know the hour when something far inboard took its weight off the stone. The knob would give a small twist; after a long enough interval for the twist to seem finished, the cup would answer. Once, in the middle of the day, the answer would come first. That might be you, standing on a spindle with your mouth shut, trying to decide whether the second beat was in the rock or in a tooth.

The night the green tip came down as far as the cup, it would miss. The wedge would already be too far out. In the morning the cup would come back under it, but by then the root would have lifted. This could go on for years. From above there would be nothing to see but a slight swelling, and if you came back after a long absence you might put your hand beneath it in exactly the old way.

Meanwhile the tooth would turn in me. Not far: just enough that its bare corner came to rest against the rim of the cup. The next time the wedge moved, the crystal would score a short line in the stone. After that it would run in the line. I would wear through at the knob before I wore through at the cup, and hang down the wedge with one end loose. I think I would be that too. There is no wind in the gap strong enough to carry off a thing that thin.

Then on an ordinary morning, with the root going slack and the spindle settling back against its ridge, the green tip would come down on the salt. It would stay there while the wedge traveled the last little distance inward. By the time the stone was warm, there would be a green hook over the lip of the cup.

You could still get your hand in. It would be easier from the other side.
