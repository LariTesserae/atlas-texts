I think I would be the person who brings the western keeper a dry heel seam. Not a shoe; she has hide enough and likes to cut her own soles. It is the sewing she puts off. She can go a whole round with the heel flapping and come home with a fern growing between the hide and her foot.

I would live inland, a little way up from the settling pools, where the houses still have shelves for the young that drop out of the towers. I would have a narrow shelf. There is a place in the wall where a root has pushed the boards apart, and whatever lands there has something to climb by. I would know this from lying awake listening to the claws. For a long time I would think I could tell how many had made it back up. Then one night I would put out a hand and find a single youngster going up and down the root, practicing the climb as diligently as it practiced folding its wing.

I would sew at the pool end of the house, where the boards are warmest. Nets mostly, and the soft shoes for children going out on the rounds. People would bring me both shoes, though there would generally be nothing wrong with the hard one. They would set them down together. The soft one would be worn through at the toe or torn at the heel, and the hard one would have a little basalt grit pressed into it at an angle. I would ask where the child had slipped. The child would say nowhere. Then, while I was working, I would get the whole account.

I would not be good at the rim. The first time a spillway cleared under me I would take hold of a brass ring with both hands, and afterward the keeper would have to ask me to move so she could get past. I would learn which patches of grating stayed hot, and where there was a root to put a foot against on the way down to the ditch. I would never learn to hear the early river as she heard it. Once, when she said it was coming, I would hear the gravel quite plainly. It would be hard to say whether I had heard it before she spoke.

There would be mornings when I went all that way and found her shoe sound. On one of them she would move over on the plank, and I would sit beside her with the useless seam rolled up in my pocket.

The sows would come down. The old one would drink and lift her head, and the last youngster would stretch its neck and draw back, stretch and draw back. I would put out a hand to steady it. The keeper wouldn’t stop me. The youngster would smell the hide in my pocket and come to investigate, and by the time it remembered the water the others would be starting up the path. It would go after them without drinking.

The next time I came I would sit farther down the plank. From there I could feel, through the wood, the small rearrangements of the beetles underneath. The youngster would get one foot into the cup after all. It would stand there with that foot in the water and drink.
