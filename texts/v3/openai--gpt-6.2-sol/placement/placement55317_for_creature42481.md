I would be somewhere under the loosened curb. From above there would be nothing to show for me except that one end of the curb had stopped settling.

At first I think I would be the thing that stopped it: a flake of old paving, pushed in edgewise by the nursing female as she cleared the floor. Most of what she scraped out went ahead of her into the open. This piece she caught with the side of a foot and drove in farther. It was too big to carry out with the dust and too small to be worth going back for. The next time the curb settled, it came down on the flake.

But a flake would have had a life before that. It would have lain flat in one of the old squares, with a top and a bottom, perhaps with a little traffic across it. I don’t think I’d remember any of that. I think the first thing I’d know would be the curb coming down.

There is a sound a loaded stone makes that an unloaded stone does not. You could tap the flake before it went in and tap it afterward and get two quite different notes, although there was no more or less flake. I might be that difference. In the afternoon, when the curb warmed and lengthened, I would change a little. At night I would change back. When the mother came in, she would put a foot on the low end of the curb before ducking under it, and for that moment I would be different again.

The youngest would know me at an hour when almost nothing happened. The last shutter had turned. Its littermate was asleep. It had one foot resting overhead, and after the vibration passed there was still something to feel. Not another vibration arriving from farther away. This one went away when it took its foot off and came back when it put it on. It tried a little harder. The note went thin. It eased off. The note came back.

For a while I thought I might be teaching it something. But the youngster was warm and had just fed, and may simply have liked doing it. It kept at it until the leg grew tired, then let the foot fold down over its nose.

Later in the season the mother will clear the floor again and the flake will come out. The curb will drop the width of a foil-leaf, no more. By then the young will be sleeping somewhere else. One evening, at a joint scarcely wider than a claw, the younger one will get five feet across and hold the sixth in the air. The joint will be making the sound of closing. It will also be making the sound of having closed long ago.

I won’t be either sound. I’ll be there when it sets the sixth foot down on its own side of the joint, very lightly, to hear what changes.
