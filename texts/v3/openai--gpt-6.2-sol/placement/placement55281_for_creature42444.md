I’d be the person on the other side of the seam, holding a pack that wasn’t mine.

The woman who gave it to me would say, “There’s a handle,” after I’d already taken it by a strap. Then she’d turn sideways and be gone. I’d have my own pack between my boots and hers against my shins. The two of us would have come up to look at the drum. She’d brought a socket wrench. I’d brought lunch and a sheet of paper large enough to take a rubbing of the plate.

I would get my turn. She’d come out with the wrench still wrapped in its cloth and hold both packs while I went in. I’d kneel and put the paper down and find that I could hear the pencil. Until then I wouldn’t have realized how much noise a pencil makes. It would pick up a screw head beautifully. I’d get all four screw heads, round and black, and between them a gray field with a few marks that might belong to letters or might be places where I’d leaned harder.

Outside, she’d put one finger on a mark and say, “That’s an R.”

I would be able to see it until she took her finger away.

We’d eat on the summit. She’d ask whether I’d heard the knocking. I’d say no, and she’d knock twice on the heel of her boot, wait, and knock once more. I’d go back in for that. There’d be no knocking. When I came out she’d have her pack on and be standing at the place where the descent begins, looking down with the particular concentration of somebody who has found that the way up and the way down are not quite the same path.

For a while I’d think I was the one who hadn’t heard it.

I’d come back alone. Not the next day; there would be a winter in between, and other things to do. I’d bring a shorter pencil, though I’d leave it in the pack. What I’d want to try would require both hands. Just inside the seam, before the leaves, there is a place where a person can stand with one palm against either wall. I’d stand there and call her name.

The first time I’d do it softly. Then I’d call as I would if I had lost sight of her on the descent. The middle of her name would go into the room. The last part would come back past my ear quite plainly, in my own voice. I’d try it a few more times, shifting my hands. Nothing would answer. A little powder would come off on the heel of my left hand, and I’d wipe it on my trousers.

On the way down I’d find a glove of hers wedged where the path turns under an overhang. I’d know it by the repair at the thumb, which she’d made with blue thread on the way up while we waited for the salt to go out of our water. It would be stiff on the side that faced the sky. The other side would still fold.

That’s where I’d be, most likely: below the garden by then, on a part of the mountain you can’t see from the summit, working the glove free a finger at a time so the stitching doesn’t catch. I’d put it in my pocket. At the bottom I’d forget it was there. Then, some ordinary morning, reaching for my keys, I’d find I had hold of somebody’s thumb.
