For a while I thought I would be the person who put the rail beside the stairs to the unfinished cellar. I liked the idea of putting something there for a hand before there was anything for a foot. But I would have gone down to see how the cellar was coming along, and after the second or third visit I would have wanted to put a notice up. The bicycle has got along perfectly well without one.

I think I would work in the room at the back of the tram shelter, where things left on the trams are kept. There is just enough heat from the driver's cubby to thaw them. In the morning the gloves are stiff enough to stand on their cuffs. By ten they have fallen over. I put a tray under the umbrellas.

The room is a little too far out for the job. People who lose things in the center of town have to ride to the end of the line to ask for them, and by the time they get there they have generally thought of somewhere else the thing might be. I get to hear about the hook behind the pantry door and the sister who borrows things without asking. Sometimes a person comes all that way, looks at a scarf hanging from my shelf, and says, “No, mine was longer,” with such relief that I'm glad I didn't take it down.

We have a book. Under *Description* I write what I can see, and under *Returned to* I write what I'm told. Once a man came for a tin of buttons and identified it by a dent in the lid. He put his thumb into the dent without looking. The tin held picture hooks. He took his thumb out and asked if he might stay a minute in the warm.

On Thursdays I fetch the tea from Ada's shop. The first time I went to the new one my feet took me to the old door, which was lying flat on a stack of boards. There were three screws in a saucer on it. I stood there longer than I needed to. Upstairs Ada gave me a cup with a button in it to hold down the end of the tea string. I drank the tea there and brought the button back the following Thursday. She had not expected it back, but she tipped it into the right drawer without looking at it.

What I like best about the shelter is the window at the back. The bottom of it is level with the plain. I can see a survey flag from my chair, but not the next one, so I have no idea what is being measured. One morning a fox stopped there and lifted a front foot. I thought it had heard me turn a page. Then it set the foot down in the same place and scratched its ear with a hind leg. When it went on, there was a small untidy patch beside the flag. By the time I went out with my tea, the ground was hard enough that I could put my own foot beside it and leave nothing.

In the bottom drawer I have a mitten that fits me. It came in before I took the job. There is no entry for it, only a tram ticket tucked into the thumb, so faded that I can't make out where anyone meant to get off. On cold afternoons I wear it on the hand that doesn't have to write. If somebody comes to the window, I've got into the habit of taking it off before I ask what they've lost. I don't know why I do that. It would be easier for them to recognize it on a hand.
