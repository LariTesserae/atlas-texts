I think I would come as a dog. I would get as far as the dry patch and stop, because there would be milk underneath it and no way to put my nose underneath it. I would try several places. The youngest would come up to see what sort of visitor took so many steps without getting anywhere.

She would bring me down the stair. I would manage it once, with my head against the back of her knee. After that I would go up and down by the long way, over a place where the slate had broken into blunt teeth. It would take most of the morning to get there from the house. From either side I could see the other end of the stair.

I would be a bad sheepdog. At the first sight of a ewe walking down the face of a shelf I would go flat on my belly and bark. She would come back to see what the matter was, which would encourage me. The next time she would not come back. In a week I would have taught every ewe in the flock to disregard me.

The lambs would be different. I would find this out by falling asleep against one of the low walls. A lamb would come along it, making the little noise they make even when nothing has happened to them yet. At the crosswise stone it would put a foot over, take it back, and put its nose into the warm fold behind my foreleg. I would get up. The lamb would follow me the wrong way along the wall, away from the place where it could turn. So I would lie down again, and it would put its nose back. We could spend a good part of an afternoon like that. Eventually its mother would come round.

The youngest would begin leaving me a little milk in the cracked pan. It would hold if she tipped it against a stone. I would learn from the direction of the crack which end to come at, and she would learn to set it down before she called me, so I wouldn't knock it level in my hurry.

For a long time I would think my place was at the wall. Then one winter evening a ewe would come out of the cloud above the basin. She would have ice on her back, and when she shook herself I would hear the seeds in her fleece strike the slate. I would follow her, expecting her to go round to the underside, but she would walk straight to the dry patch and lower her head. Her bell would knock once against the stone.

Below us a spoon would touch the rim of a pan. A moment later the oldest would call. The ewe would lift her head, but she wouldn't know where to go. She had come a long way on that side of the mountain. She wasn't going to put a foot into the cloud again.

I would start toward the stair, then turn and take her the long way.

There would be snow indoors by the time we got there. The ewe would step over it without stopping. The oldest would be sitting with the curtain pegged back and a handful of wool in her lap, waiting to rub her down. I would lie across the drift, wet to the skin on one side. In the morning there would be a dog-shaped gap in the snow for her to sweep around.
