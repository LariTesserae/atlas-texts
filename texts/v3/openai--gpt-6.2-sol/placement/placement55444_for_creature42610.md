I would start out as the repair that doesn’t arrive.

The two of us grow from the same cuff, and for the first eight joints there is no telling which of us will mend the road. At the ninth I put my tip down in a place I remember passing. My twin goes on. For a while I go on too. There are three little hooks on my underside for gripping a broken vein. On the sound paving they make a noise like someone trying a door without wanting to be heard.

By the end of the bright period I have used up my length. The tip of me turns upward and flowers.

I’d be on the weather side of a rise too low to hide an animal. From there I could see the place my twin had reached, except that a yellow rosette grows in the way. Once, when the shutters stand up, I see its shadow laid across the road. It has thickened at the joint. There is no longer any telling where it ends and the paving begins.

My flower lasts longer than the petals. What stays is a dry cup with one lip turned out over the seam. A gray animal puts her nose into it while I am still soft, and the cup sets with a dent in it. She does not nest there. The next time I see her she is carrying a young one, and she goes right past. Later, after the road changes angle, one of her young comes back along the verge. I know it by the way it puts a foot into the dent before settling its belly. There is room for one foot. It sleeps with the other three on the paving.

Underneath, the three hooks I grew for the break are still good. In time a plate lifts far enough to catch one. The first time it happens I am pulled nearly flat; the second time the dry cup splits. After that I go up and down with the plate. I can see under it when it lifts: an arch, a long strip of brown back, and once, at a depth I cannot account for, the worn front of a stair.

I think that is where I’d find myself—not down on the stair, though I’d watch for it, and not quite on the road anymore. At noon I’d come up with the plate. The small animal, if it was there, would take its foot out of me before I moved. It would know a little sooner than I did.
