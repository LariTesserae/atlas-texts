I’d be under the brass grate, in the length of red wire somebody left slack.

Not the red covering but one of the copper strands inside it. When the ridge was flat you could have pulled me straight with a finger. Now I make a little hanging turn clear of the gear. The person who bent the wire put a thumbnail into the insulation to hold it while they worked. The mark is still there, on the side nobody can see.

The young stand on me. There are easier places to stand, but here they can get their mouths against the felt without taking their feet off the wire. As it dries it draws up between them, and they have to stand a little taller each time. I know them mostly by this: when the lift passes they all straighten, and afterward one of them is slower than the others to put its weight back down.

I thought at first I’d be the warm pipe. There’s something to be said for being the thing a visitor can lean against. But I don’t think I’d be that far from the work, or that constant. As the screws go up, my hanging turn gets shallower. What reaches me from the fins changes. A drop can let go now and I get almost nothing of it, though I still get the two wheels at the joint and, now and then, the adult coming along a bracket with something it can’t quite get around the corner.

It brings the last one in sideways. One of the little feet catches in the thumbnail mark. For a while neither moves. Then the adult backs down the bracket, just enough to take the weight off that foot, and the foot comes free.

Below us the pool has widened past the thumbprint. It hasn’t reached the drain. It has reached the gap beside it, where a drop is gathering on the underside of a plate. There may be enough of it by morning to fall. For now it touches the loose end of the felt, and the felt darkens a little way up. The young don’t move toward it. They go on eating where they are, and the wet comes to them.
