I would be under the gravel where the young one stays. Not directly under its mouth, where the stones are being turned, but a little to one side, where the doe’s scraping has taken away just enough weight for me to move.

I don’t think I would be one animal at first. There would be several of us in the wet grit, each with a clear rim and a darker middle. When we met, our rims would join. There would be no struggle over which way to go. The edge on the wetter side would go on growing, and the other edges would stop. In a good rain I might become broad enough to cover the underside of a stone. In the time between rains I would draw down into its pits, and what had been one animal would be several again. A stone could be carried a long way with some of me awake on it and some of me not.

I would eat the pale threads that grow between stones after rain. They would be best just before they could hold the grit together. Sometimes I would get there too late and have to go around a tuft, leaving a clean curve on either side. An animal looking for water might turn up the stone and find those curves, but there would be no reason for it to look twice.

The doe has uncovered a stone I’ve been living under for a long time. It has a shallow pit on one face, deep enough to keep a little water after the rest of the gravel has warmed. The young one turns it over and gets the water. Then it puts a foot on the stone. The pressure is uneven: a little on one side, a little on the other. I wouldn’t know it for a foot. I would know that a passage which has been open all night is closing, and that another has opened beside it.

Part of me would go through. The rest would stay where it was, in a hollow with enough moisture to last until the gravel settled. That would usually be the extent of my travels. But there is a grain caught at the edge of one of the young animal’s pads, and the part that goes through would spread onto it. When the foot lifted, the clear rim would stretch once and part.

I would go a surprising distance before falling. The young one would stop twice, and the second time the doe would stop too. Then they would go on. The grain would come loose on a shelf that hadn’t had rain in years. It would roll into a hairline crack and lodge above a much older grain. There would be no food there and, for a long time, no water. I would shrink until I could lie wholly in the small angle where the grains touched.

Back in the gravel, I would finish eating the threads under the stone. I would reach the place where the foot had been, find it dry, and turn back. I wouldn’t wait for the other part to return. But years later, when vapor came up through that shelf and a drop hung in the crack, there would be a little more of me on the lower grain than there had been the night before.
