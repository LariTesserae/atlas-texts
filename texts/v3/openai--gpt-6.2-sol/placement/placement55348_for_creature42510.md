I think I would come up to take her place on the fifth morning.

We would pass in the cleft, where there is only room for one of us with a bag. She would go back a few steps to let me through. By the time I had put my bag down she would remember something that hadn’t gone into the book: the kettle was full, or the upper bolt had turned with its nut. For the first few months I would know her mainly by these afterthoughts. We’d each spend more nights in the other’s company than we ever spent face to face. A cup turned upside down to keep the grit out, a folded rag under the short leg of the bed.

I would be good at the machinery. The meter would click in the night and I would know whether I had to get up. That would come sooner than knowing how to walk out onto the plank without making the water jump on either side. I’d keep thinking I’d seen one of the animals, and it would keep being a drowned leaf with the stem still on. When I finally saw one, it would be in a place I’d already looked. I’d have taken my hand off the sill and found it standing in the shallow water where the shadow of my hand had been. By the time I thought to count the toes it would be gone.

There is a step just below the warm pipe where I would end up eating most of my meals. From there I could see the pump-house roof but not the peaks. Anyone coming up from below would have their head in the sky long before they saw me. I would like that step before I liked the marsh. It takes a while to get used to a place where the farthest things are easier to see than the nearest.

The first year she’d stay up with me for the shutdown. We’d find out that neither of us slept much with the machine off. I’d hear her come out in her socks and stop on the step, and after a while I’d bring the blankets out. Without the vibration there would still be a little tick in the pipe as it cooled. She’d put her heel against it to show me which sound she meant. We would sit there till we got cold enough to go in.

In the morning she’d show me the young behind the leaves. I’d have brought a pencil, thinking I might write down how many there were. Some would have their heads tucked out of sight in the narrowest part of the seep. We’d count different numbers and change places and count different numbers again. In the book I’d put the time the machine stopped and the time we brought it back up. In the margin, where we generally put things that didn’t need doing, I’d write *young in the seep*.

Years later I’d be the one going down on the fifth morning. I’d get as far as the cleft and then go back, not because I’d forgotten to tell the new person anything, but because from there I could see them standing on the plank in their good dry boots, looking straight down into the water. I’d go back softly enough not to make them turn. I know now how long you can stand there seeing nothing, and how much it matters not to have somebody beside you saying where to look.
