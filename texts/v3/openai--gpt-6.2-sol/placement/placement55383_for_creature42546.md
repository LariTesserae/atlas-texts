I would be on the underside of a stone that had already fallen.

For a long time I would have the advantage of thinking this was where the stone belonged. It had come to rest on three points, with enough room beneath it for water to pass. I would live in that room, taking up what the water carried dissolved and growing a thin collar around whatever it left against me. Most of the grains would be pale. One would be black and flat and would take me years to grow around, because it rocked a little whenever water came in from the east.

The turn would come through every morning. I would know it by a brief reversal in the taste of the water. Then there would be a silence in which I could feel all three points of the stone resting on the bottom. I would have no reason to distinguish that silence from the stone itself.

One morning there would be only two points.

The third would knock once, a little way off. A fish might have known what to do with the sudden acreage of water under it. I would draw myself tight around the black grain. It would rock the way it always had, though there was no longer an east in any place I could find it.

On the ridge I would come to rest wrong side up. The three points I had known would be out in the water, touching nothing. For the first time something would pass over my skin at a speed I could follow. It would take most of the afternoon to cross me. The next day it would come again, and after several days I would begin to open a little ahead of it. Only when a ribbon came loose from the shelf above and lay across me would I find out that what I had been feeling was warmth. Under the ribbon it passed on either side and did not reach me.

The ribbon would not stay. A little piece of its root would. It would catch beneath the collar of the black grain, and each evening, when the water spilled over the shelf, it would tug there. I would grow more collar on that side. This would make no difference to the root, which was broken and would not grow, but in time the black grain would stop rocking.

I think that is where I would find myself: not in the hollow that had suited me, or even on the ridge where I could begin to expect the sun, but just at the place where the stone was being considered again. The soft margin beneath the shelf would have felt it arrive. Each morning the turn would run through us, and some mornings the stone would lift a fraction before settling back. There would be no way for me to tell whether it was being fitted into the ridge or tried against it and found wanting.

In the meantime, a living ribbon would send a root down from the shelf. It would follow the split between my stone and the next until it reached the black grain. There it would have to go around. By the time it came out on the other side it would be as thick as the grain itself. I would feel the small, continuous pressure of it long after the evening water had gone by.
