I think I’d be the cupboard in the water office. Not one of the knocks. I’d have to sit through all seven of them without being any the wiser.

There would be a shallow drawer in me for things brought in from the square. A key with a green thread through its eye, a tram ticket swollen to twice its thickness, a buckle with a little of the shoe still attached. The younger brother would put each thing away with a slip of paper saying where it had been found. He’d write “north gutter” even when he’d picked it up himself and could still see the spot from his chair.

Iva would keep her paint in my bottom drawer. She wouldn’t have to open it all the way. I’d know her by the way she reached in without looking and set the tin down in exactly the ring it had left. In summer the ring would go soft. Once, when she was away for a week, I’d grow a fine root up around the tin, and she’d have to cut it with the edge of her ruler. For the rest of that morning I’d smell of cucumber.

The boy would come in one day looking for a fish. He’d have lost it somewhere between his kitchen and the far gutter, and it would seem reasonable to him that a person finding a fish should bring it to the water office. The younger brother would open the shallow drawer to show him what they had. He’d let him look all the way to the back.

What I’d like about being the cupboard is that I wouldn’t face either the street or the floor. I’d get the little noises people made when they were waiting to hear something else: Iva working a dry flake off the lid of her paint tin, the elder brother easing his good ear out of his collar. At three the younger brother would crack his egg against my side. He’d always do it in the same place, and after a while there’d be a pale hollow there that his thumb fitted into.

One afternoon the elder brother would get up in time. He’d lay his hand flat on my door. The younger would stay in his chair, holding his egg, and neither would say a word until the water started up again.

“Well?” the younger would ask.

“Your egg’s getting cold,” the elder would say. But he’d leave his hand there a little longer.
