I’d be under the second tread from the bottom, in the angle where the tread meets the stringer. There’s room there for a spider to flatten herself. A person coming down the stair could put a boot an inch from my legs, and afterward I could come out and walk across the warm place the boot had left.

It would take me a few tries to get the web right. The first time I’d run a line down to the sedge. From the steel it would look like a good fastening: the leaf was broad and went on being there. But the first drop to hit it would pull the whole web sideways. For a while I’d keep going down to take up the slack, only to have the leaf rise and pull the line tight behind me. By morning I’d have cut it. There is a rough place on the underside of the tread where the paint has lifted. A much shorter line would hold there.

I’d know the pumps as a shake in whatever I stood on. On the steel, the shake would come up all my legs at once. Out in the web there would be places where I could scarcely feel it. I’d spend part of the day in one of those places, with two hind feet still on the stair.

Below me, on a stem which has never held one of my lines, a fly would be getting its wings open. I wouldn’t know about the work it had to do there. I’d know it all at once, when it struck the web: a small hard weight, then a beating that came down one line and stopped. When I got there I’d find nothing but a fine leg. The fly itself would be on the wall, a little above the old water stain. It would stay there long enough for me to go back under the tread. The next time it flew it would go the other way.

Most of what caught in my web would be water. Some mornings I’d take down a line that had bowed almost to the next tread, and the beads would run together as I reeled it in. Once, the snail would climb a leaf high enough to touch the hanging water. From above I’d see the blunt end of a feeler come up, the bead leave the thread, and the thread spring back. I’d go down to see what had struck it. By then the snail would be feeling along the other edge of the leaf. There would be nothing for me to do, but I’d have come far enough down to see the middle of the pool through the gap beside its shell.

From there I could see the track over the black pebble. It would be broad enough, at that distance, to have two distinct edges. I’d stay until the next drop fell. Then I’d climb to the dry side of the stair, where I could keep one foot on the line without putting my whole weight on it.
