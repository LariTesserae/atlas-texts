I think I’d be at the far end of the locked storeroom. Not all the way at the back, where there’s nothing to do but touch the wall and turn around. About twenty minutes in, on a chair with my coat still on.

I’d have put the chair there myself. It’s a bad chair, one leg shorter than the others, and for the first week I’d go in with a different thickness of scrap under my arm, trying to get it to sit right. Then one day I’d move it six inches and find a place where it didn’t rock. I’d mark the place with chalk. The next time I went in, I’d find the mark well uphill of the chair.

That would be why the works kept sending me back. They wouldn’t call me a surveyor. A surveyor would be wanted if the discrepancy got big enough to affect the step between the landings. My job would be to see that nobody stored anything in there.

People would find this unreasonable. It would be the only municipal storeroom with room in it. A woman from the tram office would come down with twelve folded blankets and ask how much harm they could possibly do. I’d say she could leave them by the door while we found out. After a week we’d still be able to see them from the threshold, but it would take both of us the better part of a morning to bring them out. She’d take six, and I’d go back for the other six, and on the second trip I’d begin to understand the appeal of a job where, if you cleared a drain, there was at least some water that went away.

I’d get into the habit of having my tea on the upper landing. People would come up the stair, take the step, and set off down the passage without breaking off what they were saying. You could learn a little about almost everybody that way. Most of what I’d learn would be the ends of things. *Until your aunt gets here.* Or *No, with the spoon still in it.* Once a man would step across, stop, and step back. He’d take another run at it, holding a bowl in both hands. He’d been told the shortcut would save him spilling the soup. It did; he just hadn’t expected it to.

Then one morning there would be wet footprints coming out of the locked door.

They’d be a child’s, but not a small child’s. Whoever had made them had stood for a while just outside the room. There’d be two prints dark enough to show the missing corner of a heel, then a turn toward the lower landing and the usual scuffs where people came in off the street.

I’d take a lamp and go almost to the back wall. The prints would be coming toward me all the way. I’d have to sit down once. Coming back, I’d pick up my chair and bring it out.

It would be Iven’s son, though I wouldn’t know him yet. They’d pulled the last grate up to measure for the new one, and he’d seen somewhere he could put a foot. Then somewhere he could put the other foot. He’d tell me this later as if the two facts went some way toward explaining what he’d done. He hadn’t seen a door. He’d seen a thin line of light, and when he put his fingers into it the door had opened.

Iven would come to look. She’d want to know precisely where the water stopped. We’d walk in together with her lamp and mine. Her stride was longer than mine; after a while she’d notice and slow down. We’d find a little grit, a crushed pellet, and nothing that looked like a way through. On the way back she’d ask if I’d ever been out to the ditch. I’d say no. She’d say to come before they put the grate on.

So I think in the end you’d find me at the north tram stop, with the chair. I’d have carried it out for Iven’s father, who would refuse to sit until somebody else did. We’d take turns on it while she worked. Her son would show me the place he’d stepped, now scarcely wider than the hook, and be a little put out when I believed him without trying it.

When the new grate came, I’d take one end. We’d lower it a little crooked and have to lift it again. From where I was kneeling I could see a pale animal in the run, folding and unfolding one leg. I’d ask Iven if we ought to wait. She’d lean over to look, then ease her end sideways half an inch. Water would come through under my boot. The animal would set its leg down.

The chair would stand level out there. I’d leave it by the pump house. The next time I came it would have been turned to face the pools, which was a better way to have it, though it hadn’t occurred to me.
