I’d be the chair Len takes out to the fountain. Not the good chair with the arms that take a board. I’d be the kitchen chair he brings out because it’s light enough to carry with a cup in the other hand. One of my legs is short. On the paving that hardly matters. If he sits long enough, the other three sink.

I came from upstairs. For years I had a piece of folded newspaper under that leg. I don’t know what was printed on it, but I know the fold: when the floor was washed, the paper swelled along it first. Down here Len tried folding a leaf. By breakfast it had opened flat and walked me almost to the stove.

There’s a little wood left in the shaving Ada takes off the door that is pale all the way through. Len saves those pieces for my leg. They work for a while. Then one morning he tips me over and finds that the shaving has grown a row of white hairs and got a grip on both the floor and me. He cuts it loose with the bread knife. I’m still dead wood. It takes him a moment to find the join.

Most evenings at the fountain I face the works. Len likes to see Ada coming and pretend he hasn’t been waiting for her. He’ll ask her to hold his tea while he gets up, as though she’s happened along at exactly the right moment. Sometimes she holds it. Sometimes she drinks it.

I thought at first that the animal wanted him to move because of his boots. Then he came out in his stocking feet and tucked them under me, and still it stood there with the leaf in its mouth. When he picked me up, it set one foot in the little dent my short leg had made. It stopped there to turn the leaf around. I felt the stem brush my rung.

After that Len began putting me a little farther back from the bowl. From there he couldn’t see into it without standing. I could see underneath. A thread had come out of the root, no thicker than one of the hairs Len leaves knotted on the washstand. It went down into the dent. For three evenings it did nothing I could feel. On the fourth, when Len came to take me in, that corner of me held for half a second.

He put me down again. Then he went home and brought out the good chair. The door was wide enough that day; he didn’t even have to turn it sideways.

I still go in when the grazers come. They like the salt where Len puts his hands. But on the nights he forgets me, the short leg settles a little farther into the paving. Whatever is coming up to meet it is taking its time. For years I thought what I wanted was to stand level. Lately, when Len gets up to go in, I find I like the small drop after his weight leaves me: the moment when I should rock back, and don’t.
