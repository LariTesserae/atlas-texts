I’d be in the back of Mr. Ist’s cupboard. Not in the cupboard: in the back of it. There’s a difference of about the thickness of a fingernail, which is all I’d have to begin with.

I’d have come in with a drawing of a hall. A boy had drawn his father coming home, and because his father always came home with two bags of groceries, he’d drawn him twice as wide as usual. The hall didn’t fit. Mr. Ist showed the boy how to turn his father sideways. The drawing went in then, except for the bit of hall the boy had left for his father to put the bags down. When Mr. Ist shut the door, that bit slipped behind the backboard.

For a while I’d think I was a mouse. I’d know how to get from the cupboard to the skirting without crossing the floor, and on the other side of the wall there’d be a biscuit tin. But I wouldn’t want the biscuits. I’d want the room the lid needed to come off. It was a good tin. You could get the lid off with both hands and not hit anything.

By the end of term I’d have reached the school door. The caretaker would notice that he could set his bucket down before he got his keys out. He’d assume he’d finally worn the step level.

I’d go home with him, probably. He had a landing with a hook for his coat and nowhere to stand while he took it off. I could do something about that. But the first time I tried, the woman across the landing opened her door and found her shopping pressed up against it. That’s how I’d learn I wasn’t an allotment. I couldn’t bring any more room in. I could only move about in what there was, and when I moved, somebody’s things moved too.

Pell would catch me at it. She’d be down on one knee looking for a pin and see the floor under her table get a little longer. She’d put a finger down where the pin was, wait for me to move past it, then pick it up.

“Not there,” she’d say, the next time. “I’m using that.”

I think I’d stay with her after that. There was work I could do under the table: hold a fold open while she got a hand inside it, give her somewhere to set the scissors without putting them down on the cloth. At first I’d do these things whenever they seemed useful. Once I gave her a great deal of elbow room just as she was leaning on her elbow, and she put a stitch through her skirt. After that she’d tap twice where she wanted me.

Her daughter would be harder to learn. Some evenings she liked to sit in the doorway with her foot against the opposite jamb. If I widened it, she’d put her foot down and go somewhere else. Once she came in with a neighbor’s casserole, hot enough that she had the tea towel wrapped twice around the handles. I made straight for the table. She said, “Chair,” through her teeth. Of course. The table was covered in work. She needed room to pull the chair out and put the dish on the seat.

So that’s where I’d find myself, most likely: in the front room when supper was nearly ready, under a chair that couldn’t be pulled out all the way. Pell would be trying to finish something before she ate. Her daughter would come in with the plates and stand there until Pell looked up. Then Pell would lift the cloth, still threaded to the needle in her hand, and I’d go where the first plate was about to come down.
