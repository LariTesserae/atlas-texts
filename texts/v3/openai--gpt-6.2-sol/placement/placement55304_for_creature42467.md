I’d start on the saddle, where I could go down to either river. In the evening I’d watch the cloud come up on both sides and try to tell which would reach me first. I think I’d spend quite a long time up there before admitting that I wanted to know what the deer were doing with their mouths.

From the ridge it looks as though they’re drinking. Down close, you can hear the little tearing noise. A doe with orange feet has got hold of the end of a strip of moss. She walks backward, and the strip comes out of the stone much longer than it looked when she took hold of it. At the far end there’s a white root laid crosswise. She pulls once more. The root holds. The moss parts.

I think I’d be the bit that stayed under the root.

For the rest of that afternoon there’d be light in a place that had been dark for years. The water in the joint would be quite still. I’d have no way of seeing it, but the doe would lower her mouth into it, and for the length of a drink her nose would be the roof. Then she’d go off with a green thread hanging from her lip. The strip she’d pulled out would lie on its side, with a snail making its way along what until then had been the bottom.

I wouldn’t fill the opening quickly. Grit would come down into it. A grass seed would lodge there and swell, then get washed a little farther in. In the first dry week my exposed edge would curl away from the stone. That would be nearly all I did that week.

But I keep thinking about the two kinds of deer. The ones that know the pool could cross over me all their lives, putting their feet in much the same places, without ever putting their mouths down. Then an animal born in the marsh would come over the saddle, thirsty, and stop at this undistinguished line in the turf. I’d be part of what it recognized. Not the water, exactly. The little green end you take hold of to find out if there’s water.

Years later, when the opening had almost closed, I’d have grown over the crosswise root. There’d be no outward sign of where the old strip ended and I began. A deer pulling from the other direction might get the whole thing. Or the root might hold again. From the saddle you wouldn’t see even the deer’s head move.
