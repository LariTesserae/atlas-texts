I’d be on the far side of the road, in the old fence stake. I’d be the staple that used to hold the bottom wire.

There would still be a bright place inside my bend where the wire rubbed. In winter, when it shortened, I could feel a pull from three posts away. A cow putting her head through gave a different pull: two or three short ones, then a long, absentminded weight. When the fence came down, whoever took it used the claw of a hammer on the upper staples. Mine was too near the ground. They cut the wire and drew it through me, and for a while I held the shape of that pull with nothing in it.

The animal would come up the ditch side of the stake, where the wood was softest. Its cup would take one of my legs before it reached the other. All summer I’d be able to move a little without falling. A truck passing on the road could make me knock once against the wood. The pump could not. Over here the pump was something you knew by the water coming out of the pipe, and even when the water stopped there was a little more of it to come, draining from the lip.

I’d fall in the first hard rain. Not far. One leg would catch in the stake and the other would come to rest against the alder, just below the dark place. In that position I’d collect a drop. It would run down one leg and around the bend and hang there, with the alder upside down in it. There would be a long time between the drop getting heavy enough to touch the bark and its getting heavy enough to fall.

That would be when the animal crossed. I’d know it first by the next drop, which wouldn’t fall. It would get smaller from the alder end. By morning there would be a bit of ground-up wood caught on my burr, and nothing on the bark that couldn’t have been there the day before.

I don’t think I’d be much use after that. Once the ditch rose high enough, the stake and the tree would meet above me. The alder might take my point into its bark, though I’d more likely go down with the last of the stake. I’d lie in the ditch with my legs up. Grit from the road would settle in the bright place the wire had made, and in August, when the rest of the ditch went dry, there would still be enough damp under my bend to darken a grain of sand. I’d be there the first summer the attendant looked under his stone and found nothing, while on the alder behind him a flake of bark that had lain flat all season began, at one corner, to lift.
