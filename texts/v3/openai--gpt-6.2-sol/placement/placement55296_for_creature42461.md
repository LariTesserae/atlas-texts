I’d start under the shelf in the smallest house. There’s room there if you don’t mind keeping your head down. I’d put my hand up to the wood the way you put a hand to the ceiling over an unfamiliar stair, and find the place where the carpenter took off one shaving more. For a while I’d think I was the person who did that. It’s a tempting mistake, finding a bit of work you understand and calling it yours.

But I think I’d be down in the kitchen, in the space the animals go around. Not the door. The little bit of play in the bottom hinge that let you lift the door by its handle when it stuck.

It must have been a nuisance when both hands were full. Somebody in the house knew how to do it with a knee. I wouldn’t remember their face or what they carried. I’d know the lift, then the push, and how much easier the push was if you got those two things in the right order. Now and then one of the little animals would come down the wall on the wrong side of the opening and I’d feel it pause. There’s nothing for it to wait for. It waits anyway.

I don’t think I’d stay in the house all the time. I’d find that out the night the thing from the gap went around the tram loop and stopped where you have to raise your foot too high. It would have no foot to raise, but the fold coming up behind the first would catch on something. I’d feel the catch from the kitchen. Then I’d be there, under it, giving it just enough lift to go on.

After that I’d try the public stair. There’d be very little for me to do on the steep part. Whoever cut those treads had already thought about the weight someone would bring home. I’d go as far as the top, though, and feel how near the last tread came to the mountain.

I could fit there. I’d be careful not to. It’s one thing to help a door open and another to take up the space it opens into.
