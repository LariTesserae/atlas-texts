I think I would be the room in the bowl, though for a while I would take myself for the visitor. I’d know the dust in the bootlaces and the small embarrassment of looking into somebody’s bowls before they’d asked me in. Then the visitor would take a step back from the shelf, and I wouldn’t go with them. I’d still have the sill, the awl, and the little pull in the door where a stitch had been drawn too tight.

Through the larger chip I could see the visitor’s boots. Through the smaller one I could see a strip of the keeper’s left shoulder as it went by. I would learn to tell whether it was coming in or going out by the way the hairs lay down against the wall.

The keeper would use me now and then for a job it couldn’t finish in one breath. Once it brought in the animal with the split hoof. The animal stood with its good foot on the sill and the sore one in the keeper’s hand. The keeper took the old binding off a strand at a time. I had thought all the hair in the binding was the same color, but laid out on the sill there was a white length and a brown length, and one so fine I could keep sight of it only while it was moving.

When the breath ended, the animal was out among the others again. The keeper was still sitting with its hand cupped. It stayed like that until the next breath, when the hoof came down into its palm with a little more weight than before.

I wouldn’t be good at keeping everything. Dust would come back to the sill where the keeper had wiped it. A fold in the hide would work itself flat. But I could keep the shallow dents, and after a while I would know why there were two. The keeper didn’t lie down as soon as it came in. It stood there first, sometimes for a long time, letting its arms hang. One foot took more of its weight. That dent was the deeper one.

The visitor would come back and take off their boots. I don’t know whether I’d recognize them by their face. I’d know them when they put their feet down. They would stand evenly at first, and then one knee would ease, and the weight would go into the deeper dent. Outside, an animal would close its teeth on a glass cup. The visitor would look down, thinking the sound had come from under the floor.

By then I think I would know better than to try to show them anything else. I’d let the warmth last long enough for them to decide whether to sit.
