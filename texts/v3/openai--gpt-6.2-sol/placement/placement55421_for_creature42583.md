I’d be just outside the bank at first, turning the bundles of kindling. There’s a place where the crown of the bank is broad enough to lay them two abreast, provided you leave room for a foot between them. The children leave room for one foot. Anybody carrying something has to call down for me to come and shift a bundle.

I bring the wood from dry ground in a flat boat. On the way home I can see my roof for a long time without seeming to get any nearer to it. I know it by one white stone, which I put up there myself. It was too round to be much good at holding the roof down. The mason told me so, and I put it in a hollow where it couldn’t roll. He was right about the roof: it lifts a little at that corner in a wind. But I can pick out my house even on a day when the roofs and the water are much the same color.

Once I’m over the bank I lose sight of it. I go down sideways with a bundle under each arm. There’s a turn where somebody has to back up if we meet, and because I’m carrying wood people generally do it for me. Sella doesn’t. She takes one bundle from me and waits for me to turn, then gives it back. It took me a while to realize that from where she stands she’d have to back up two steps, and from where I stand I only have to turn.

My door is below hers. The room has a high shelf for wood and a low shelf for everything else. At the back of the high shelf there’s one bundle I don’t sell. It came from a pear tree on the dry side, the year it split under its fruit. The woman who let me cut it up asked me to save her a little for the first fire in her new house. She ended up living with her daughter, who already had a fire. Twice she’s told me I may as well burn it. I’ve burned some. The bundle is much the same size because I keep putting other sticks in it.

Before rain I have to get the wood off the bank. I can’t see Sella’s heel from up there, and by the time the first drops show on the mud I’ve usually left myself one trip too many. So I’ve taken to asking the eel boy to tell me when the bubble comes out of the hole. He won’t leave the hole to do it. He tells whoever’s nearest, who tells whoever’s nearest to them. By the time it gets to me the message is just *wood*, called by somebody who may not know why.

On a day when I’ve brought back more than usual, people take the bundles under their own roofs until I can come for them. I know roughly where the wood is, but not always. Weeks later I’ll go to somebody’s door and find a little stack they’ve been stepping around, dry to the middle. They’re glad to see me. They’ve been wanting that bit of floor back.
