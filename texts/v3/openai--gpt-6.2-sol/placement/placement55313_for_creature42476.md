I think I would come in with a new length of glass wrapped in newspaper. Someone would have noticed the drop going up and put in a report about the gauge. By the time I got there the tide would be out, the glass empty. I’d follow the pipe with my finger and find where it ended. There would be nothing to test.

I would have twenty minutes before I could get back around the foot of the wall. I’d unwrap my sandwich on the sill and move the cup under the drip. The first drop to hit tin would make me look up. The next one would make me look up too.

That would be my first visit, anyway. On the second I’d bring a short pencil and put a mark on the glass where I thought I had seen the drop stop. Then I’d have to rub the mark off, because the drop hadn’t stopped. It was my eye that had stopped at a flaw in the glass. I’d get interested in that distinction. I’d arrange to be there at the next flood, and the next. At some point there would be no report left to make. I suppose that’s when I’d begin to belong to the yard.

I wouldn’t learn to read it the way the eel does. I’d have to put a finger in a seam to find which way the water was moving. Some days I’d be sure for a moment and then feel the grit pass the other way. I’d get to know the eel mostly by the shrimp it didn’t take. There would be one out on the fan of slate, in water hardly deep enough to cover its back, and I would be waiting for the strike when I noticed the eel had already gone. What looked to me like open ground was the last of a road that had closed behind it.

I’d keep shifting the cup. The drip would move a little when the motor ran, and eventually I’d find a place where it hit the rim and sent half the water inside. There would still be a wet spot on the sill. I’d leave it at that.

One morning I’d find a blind thing in the cup. I’d carry it as far as the doorway, thinking to tip it into the lowest pool, and stand there longer than I meant to. There would be the pool, and the pipe it had come down, and the wet brick it was trying to climb. I wouldn’t know which of those counted as putting it back. In the end I’d lower the cup into the shallow water at the foot of the wall and let it fill from the edge. For a while I’d be able to see the little animal through the clean water in the cup. Then the grit would come over the rim and I’d lose it.

So I’d be the person who came to change the glass and never did. The new tube would stay in its newspaper on the high shelf until the paper went soft. If someone came down at the turn of the tide, I’d make room for them in the doorway. I’d show them where to put an ear, but I wouldn’t tell them how many ticks to expect. I used to count them, and once, just after I’d said there were seven, another came from low down, almost under my heel.
