I think I’d be at the other end of the tram line, under the part of the roof the car never reaches. There’s enough room there to lay a door flat and walk all the way around it. I’d have a bench, a kettle, and a drawer full of screws taken out of things that were thrown away. People would come to me for a screw and bring the whole thing wrapped in a tea towel because they couldn’t say where the screw went.

But I don’t think I’d be much good at fixing clocks or locks. I’d be the person the city sends when something rocks. A bench, a basin, one of the little bridges. The work order would say *loose*, which is often wrong. A surprising number of things rock because somebody has wedged them too firmly in the wrong place.

At the washhouse I’d be sent for the bridge. The new man would come out in his apron and step on the near corner to show me. I’d put two fingers on the far corner and ask him to do it again. Then I’d ask him to go inside and walk past the basins. He’d look at me, but he’d do it. I’d feel the stone go down a little before his foot came through the doorway. The bridge would be sound. The sill would be moving.

That’s where I’d spend most of the morning: not under the bridge but sitting on it, with my tools still rolled up, trying to decide how much sill I could take away without taking away the place where the door came to rest. The new man would bring me tea. I’d move my foot so he could set it down. That would be when I saw the sole, in the space my boot had been shading. I’d mistake its head for the pale edge of a chip and reach for it. It would draw back, and I’d sit there with my hand open over the water, feeling foolish.

I wouldn’t know it had been there eleven years. I might go on thinking I’d seen a young one.

By closing I’d have the sill packed and the door swinging clear, though it would catch a little in wet weather until I came back with a sharper plane. The new man would ask if I wanted him to step on the bridge again. I’d say yes, and he’d put his whole weight on the corner, harder than anyone would coming in with a bundle of washing. Nothing would move. He’d go in for his coat.

I’d wait to see the animal again and miss my tram. Then I’d have half an hour to wait anyway. I’d take my tea to the parapet, where it would be just possible to see the lit windows of the tram passing below, before the roofs cut it off. When I went back for my tools, the new man would be standing with a foot on either side of the channel, holding the door open with his hip. He’d ask if I’d dropped something.

I’d say no, and leave the tools where they were for another minute.
