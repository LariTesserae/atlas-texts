I think I would be the fourth mark in the gutter, the one nobody made.

For a long time there would be only the three peck marks and, just downhill of them, a grain of something softer in the stone. Water would pass over it all summer. In winter the seep would freeze from the edges inward, leaving a thread that still moved in the dark. A little of the grain would go each year. Then one afternoon a husk would catch where nothing had ever caught before, and the water would stand a hair higher on one side of it.

From there I would know the old female by her foot. She puts it down upstream of me, in almost exactly the same place each evening. The first time the young one tries it, it puts both forefeet in and has to drink with its hindquarters in the air. A little mud comes down. After they leave, a hair from one of them lies across me until the water works it loose. I don't get to know whose it was.

I wouldn't see the lower meadow. For a while I thought I'd rather be down there, perhaps one of the saxifrages the young one has yet to eat. But I like the small mistake of being uphill, where their return is something I can hear without knowing how it was managed. The old one crosses the gutter in a single stride. The young one, even on nights when it follows her down without a sound, still puts a foot in on the way home.

In time I'd be deep enough to keep a little water after the seep dwindled. A bee might come down to drink, though there are better places. Grit would settle in me. On a cold morning the first ice would take my shape, and when the sun reached the gutter it would lift whole and travel a few inches before breaking.

Someone sitting in the shelter to get a stone out of a boot might run a thumb over all four marks and take them for the work of the same hand. I would like to be there for that: the boot on its side under the slab, the thumb coming back once to feel how shallow I am.
