I’d be the girl they send to hold the step up while the mason makes his mark. I’d do it with the heel of my hand, having slipped often enough with my thumb. When he says *a little more*, I know by now that he means the light, not the step. I move my head.

I keep his old chisel. There’s nothing wrong with it except that it’s worn too short for him to strike without catching a knuckle. I use it to get the salt out of my shoes. I’ve asked him about the print with the missing toe often enough that he’s begun to say yes, it was the same animal, though the first time I asked he said he didn’t know. I wish I’d left him with his first answer. It was a better thing to have two streets over.

At home I sleep on a shelf above the bread oven. There’s a place where the plaster has come away by my pillow, and with an ear against the brick I can hear the garden even after everyone downstairs has stopped moving. One night the cart stops. A little farther down, a single stone gives a low, quite ordinary knock. I wait for another. There isn’t one.

The next morning I take the chisel down to the garden. The mason isn’t there. I lift the step and, where the passage wall meets the landing, scratch a short line level with my knee. It’s a poor mark, white at one end and scarcely there at the other. I brush the grit into my palm.

Years later, when they raise the passage, that little line is the only one I ask them to leave. By then I’m the mason, and the boy who holds the step for me wants to know what I measured there.

“My knee,” I tell him.

He puts his knee to the wall. It comes a little above the mark. For the rest of the morning he holds the step higher than he needs to, and I let him.
