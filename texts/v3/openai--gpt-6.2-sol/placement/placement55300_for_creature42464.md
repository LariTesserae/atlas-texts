I would be just above the place where the mouse stands to drink. Not directly above it: the bowl would put her too far out from the wall. I’d be where she could get to me without having to jump.

At first I thought I’d be something she passed over, a flake of limestone that rocked under her foot and settled again. But I keep wanting to know when she’s coming. The stone would know only when she was already there.

So I think I’d be the second young swift, the one that answers. The first has a good place under a lip of rock. I have a good place for one foot. With the other I can feel along the ledge and find a shallow pit that will take two claws if I put them in sideways. I do this most of the night. Whenever I get both claws in, I want to move the first foot.

I know the mouse first by her tail. It passes over my toes without anything to stand on following it. The first time, I open my foot. For a moment I hang by the two claws I’ve been trying all night to get into the pit. After that I can find it without feeling along the ledge.

When the old birds come in, I open my mouth before I know which one is mine. Sometimes I keep it open after they’ve all gone past. The other young bird does this too. We can go on calling with our mouths pointed into the same inch of dark, though neither of us is what the other is waiting for.

Toward morning there’s a little tearing sound below us. It stops, starts, stops for so long I tuck my head in. When I look again, half a moth wing is traveling across the floor. A beetle has got beneath it. At a ridge in the stone the beetle goes on and the wing stays behind. The draft lifts it, turns it over, and sets it down a little nearer the sill. For a long time that’s all the farther it gets.

I don’t think I’d fly that morning. I’d move out one foot’s worth, to where I could see the wing and, beyond it, a piece of the snow on the next mountain. Then one of the old birds would come in low, fold herself almost to nothing, and go over my head. The air off her wings would lift the loose ends of my feathers. I’d open my bad foot and put it down a little farther out.
