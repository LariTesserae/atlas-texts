I would be under the broad chip on the dry side of the cup. Dry is a word for the top of it. Underneath, there is a rim of damp that advances in the night and draws back in the day, and I would spend much of the summer moving the width of a grain along that rim.

I think I would take the rim for a feature of the mountain. It would have a bay where the stone was nicked and a long straight shore where the chip rested on a flake. Then one morning a dark animal would pick up the chip and the whole coast would turn over.

I would be a larva in a coat of wool. Not much wool: a few fibers bitten out of a tuft that had come down from the sheep fields, with a bird’s gray down caught crosswise in the seam. The fibers would have to be short or they would snag. I would find this out with the first coat, which I would spend half a day trying to back out of a crack before cutting myself free of it. The next one would have a rough little collar where I kept adding to the end I ate from.

When the chip went into the water, I’d draw my head into the coat. The animal would drink. Its chin would touch the surface between swallows and send a small wave under the ice it had lifted. Some of that water would come back with us to the dry patch. I’d stay shut up until it had run off. Then I’d put out my head, and the bay and the long shore would be there again, but the chip would be down at a different angle, with a way out where there had been none before.

I’d take it. Not far. As far as the pointed chip’s old place, where there would still be a slender space between two flakes. The young animals looking for the chip would pass their feet over me. One would catch the loose down in my coat and pull me halfway out. For the rest of that winter I’d have a bare spot there, which I would keep turned to the stone.

I might end up one of the moths in the lower cracks. I don’t think I’d remember the shape of the shore once I had wings. I’d still have an inclination to get under things. On the day the light came down, while the birds were lifting their bodies from their hollows, I’d go farther in.

A young bird would call into the crack. I’d open my wings to get over a grain of stone and there would be that little click. The bird would come a foot closer. Its bill wouldn’t fit, but the fine feathers at the base of it would stir against the opening while it waited.

I’d be on the other side of the grain by then, folding my wings so I could pass.
