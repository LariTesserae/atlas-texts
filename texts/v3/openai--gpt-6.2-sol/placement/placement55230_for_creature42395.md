I think I would be the person people ask to read an inscription when they have already tried wetting it.

There would not be much call for me. Most of the marks in the town are doing something more useful than saying something. The notches under the oven shelves tell Iven where the boards will sit without rocking. A cross on a drain lid gives Sella a place to put the end of her lever. I would have a room in the passage between the two rows of houses, with a table I could drag out to whichever end had light. Mostly I would mend baskets. I would be better at that than at inscriptions, though it would take people longer to find out.

The child who brings pebbles to the mounting block would come to get me one day. On the underside of one of the pot rims there is a mark like a letter with a leg missing. He wants me to see it without taking the rim all the way out. We lie down in the street, our heads together, and he holds it up with his thumbnail.

I can think of three letters it might be. I can also see how the potter might have made it by catching the clay with a nail. I tell him this. He asks me to show him the three letters anyway. I draw them in the dust in the gutter. He looks at them and puts the rim back, then draws the mark beside them, with its short leg just as short as it is on the pot. His is the one that looks most like writing.

In the evening Ressa brings me a basket with a split in the side. There is flour in the split, and when I press it open a little falls onto my knee. She asks if I can leave the handle as it is. Her father made it, and it is too thick to get your hand comfortably around, but he takes hold of it whenever she brings the basket to the ovens. I say I can leave it.

I would walk it back to her when it was done. At the bench I would set it down and rest my hand on the handle. From there I would see something white at her house, lower than the parapet. I would wait for it to move. After a while it would, and I would lift my hand, though I would still not be sure it was Ressa, or that she could see me.

Then I would pick up the basket and go the rest of the way. I would knock with my knuckle, because a spoon that thin ought to be left for people she doesn’t know yet.
