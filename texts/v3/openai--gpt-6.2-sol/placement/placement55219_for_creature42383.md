I think I would live across the gap on Foundry Street. I’d be the person who came up on the freight lift to mend the bus shelter. There wouldn’t be much to mend. The pane with the loose bead would be the same pane every time. I’d have cut a wedge for it out of a wooden clothes peg, and by the time I got there the rain would have swollen the bead and the pane would be quiet. I’d leave the wedge on the bench. Sometimes the mill worker would use it to scrape the mud from the welt of his boots.

No, I think I’d be there even when there was nothing to mend. I’d be there after the maintenance man went down on the lift.

I’d be the bell on the bus.

There’s a bus somewhere. The people on Foundry Street can hear it in wet weather, though they take it for a lorry because of the way the engine labors. It has to come up a fairly stiff grade before it gets to them. There’s a turn at the top where the driver puts his hand out to steady his tea. By then the water that’s got in around the back window has run all the way to the front, and the first passenger off has to step over it.

I don’t suppose it’s always the same bus. The floor was linoleum once, with a strip of brass down the aisle. It’s rubber now. For a while I was a round bell in a little dome over the driver’s head, and now I make a sound from a box no bigger than a matchbox. The driver can tell me from the other noises the box makes. He rests two fingers on the brake when he hears me.

Mostly I know the valley by when people press. There’s somebody who does it almost as soon as she gets on. She has a long way to go, but her hands are free then and may not be later. There’s somebody who waits until he can see his gate and has to walk back from the next stop. On the steep part a shopping bag will sometimes do it. The driver knows about the shopping bag. He looks in the mirror and goes on.

Then one wet evening I’d ring and he’d put his tea down. The back doors would fold apart. On the pavement a man with a lunch tin would finish doing up his coat. He’d be early for his shift; he’d allowed time for the lift. He’d look behind him at the shuttered shoe shop, then down at the step, which was a little lower than he’d expected.

I’d like to be there for the next morning, too. He’d be asleep by then. His mother would come up with a loaf in a bag, and when the shelter came into view she’d press with her elbow rather than set the loaf down. By the end of the week she’d know exactly when to do it: just as the front wheels came off the old pavement, at the spot where, if you hadn’t got a hand on the rail, you took one small step forward.
