I’d go to the sill first. From there I could see where the water went, which is something I’d have wanted to know after an afternoon in the cane. But I don’t think I’d stay. The place I keep coming back to is the low side of one of the wooded knolls, where a tree has put out a root just above the water. From the ridge it would look like a place to sit. Down among the stems you’d find that the root was too slick to sit on and that there was nowhere to put your feet.

I think I’d be a piece of the bank there. A piece about as wide as a mouse is long, with two living sedges and the dead roots of several more. Water would have been going under me for some time. On the tree side I’d still be held by one tough root, and on the water side I’d rise a little whenever the animal came out from under the ferns. It wouldn’t touch me. It would just move enough water to lift me and set me down.

For a long time I’d take that lifting and settling for the extent of what could happen.

Then one night the last root would let go, and I wouldn’t get far at all. The sedges would catch in the cane. By morning there’d be a finger’s width of water between me and the bank, not enough to see unless you were down at the height of a mouse. A mouse taking its usual way to the snails would put a foot on me. I’d dip. It would take the foot back and go around by the tree.

That would be my first day away.

I’d have some of the bank’s inhabitants with me and lose them at different rates. The small things that needed running water would do well at first, now that there was water on all sides. A snail that had spent its life on the shaded underside of a root would find the shade gone and make its way down through me. One of my sedges would yellow. The other, relieved of the weight of the bank, would stand a little straighter.

I don’t think I’d go over the falls. There are so many chances to stop before the falls. In the first hard rain I’d come free of the cane and cross a channel barely wider than myself. There I’d catch, upside down, against the roots of a fern. The next evening the broad animal would come to feed. Its toes would pass through the fern roots, close, and draw away. On the third or fourth stroke it would get some of me. The fish would take what came loose.

What stayed would be the living sedge, caught by its leaves, its white roots trailing uselessly in the current. It might hang that way for weeks. Then a stretch of quiet water, a little silt among the roots. In time there’d be enough ground there for a snail to come out of the water and go on eating without noticing it had come ashore.
