I think I’d be the person people send for when they can’t tell whether a mark is theirs.

Not the one who lays out the rooms. I’d have a room at the western end, with a good deep shelf and a door that has to be lifted a little to shut. I’d keep meaning to take the door off and see to it. Most mornings I’d be out before there was enough light in the room to see what was wrong with it.

At first I’d go east with the man who cuts stairs. He wouldn’t ask me to come. There would be a basket he couldn’t carry along with his tools, and I’d carry it, and for a long time that would be how we arranged to meet. I’d learn that when he set his tools down and went back up three steps, he was looking at the stairs, not waiting for me to say anything. I’d also learn that he left the heel of his bread in the basket until he was quite sure he didn’t want it.

I’d get to know the town by what people put against its walls. A broom laid crosswise meant a floor was still wet. A bowl turned down over a chalk mark usually meant someone wanted the mark kept, though once it meant that there was a moth under the bowl, and I let it out to the great annoyance of a boy who had gone to fetch his sister.

There would be mornings when several people wanted me at once. The baker would ask if I could see whether the black near her wood was soot or damp. The widower would want a second pair of eyes on the drain. I’d go to the baker first because she’d give me something hot to hold, and then I’d have to explain to the widower why I couldn’t put both hands down in the water.

I wouldn’t always know. In the new quarter especially, a mark could be where a man meant to stop cutting or where he meant to start. You could ask him and find he’d gone up to the fields for the day. I’d get in the habit of putting a stool in front of anything I wasn’t sure about. This would work until the baker’s admirer discovered she could sit on it. After that, wherever I put the stool, there she’d be, with her heels knocking softly against the stone.

One evening I’d come upon the children holding the cup against the wall. I’d suppose at first that they’d broken it. Their mother would be across the yard, talking about a door, and the boy would ask me to take the cup before his sister let go again.

I’d take it. There’d be a little circle in the chalk where the base had rubbed. The girl would want to know if the men would cut the shelf there now. I’d say they could, if that was where the bed was going. She’d say the bed was going wherever it had to for her to reach the shelf.

I’d ask her to sit on the floor and show me how high the bed was. She’d put one hand up to her knee, change her mind, and raise it a little. Her brother would say that was too high. They’d go on like that awhile. I’d hold the cup.

In the morning, going east with the basket, I’d see that their mother had drawn the shelf again. She’d left the little circle where it was. The line passed just under it.
