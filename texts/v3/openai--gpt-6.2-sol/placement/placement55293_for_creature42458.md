I’d be near the coarse end of the fan, though I don’t think I’d have come down with the rest of the grit. I’d have been there before the channel, a flake worked loose from the lip of a plate and left leaning against it. On dry days there would be no way to tell me from the edge I came off. Rain would darken the little space behind me first.

The first water down the diagonal would turn me over. Not carry me far: I’d go the length of a hand and catch with one corner under a grain heavier than I was. What had been my underside would spend an afternoon in the sun. There would be two little hollows in it, side by side, with the crispness of the rest of the surface gone from their deeper ends. They’d be old feeding marks. I’d have come loose along a layer that covered them, and for that afternoon they’d hold water again.

I think I’d have expected to be one of the animals. There’s something appealing in the way a foot reaches out for a wall that ought to be there. But I’d be the thing it finds. As the pool drew back, an animal turning over in the water would put one foot against my thin edge and another against the grain above it. For a little while I’d feel the pull from both sides. Then it would go past me into the fan.

I wouldn’t be much use at keeping the opening clear. Fine grit would come in with the next rain, and some of it would lie where the animal had passed. The crust would grow up around my caught corner. It might join me to the larger grain before the next dry spell, leaving the other end free. At noon that end would be hot and the caught end still wet. The seam between them, if you could get your eye down to it, would be only as deep as I was thick.

Later the plate might settle and send the water off some other way. The fan would dry from the top. You could brush it with the side of your hand and the loose grains would skitter off, but there would be a rough piece at the bottom that came up all together. I’d be in it, a little crooked, with the two hollows on the side you wouldn’t think to look at. And underneath my free end, in the last place the air could get to, there might be a fresh pair.
