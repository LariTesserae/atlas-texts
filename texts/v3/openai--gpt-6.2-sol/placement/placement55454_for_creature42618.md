I would stay near the western gate for a while. I would think at first that I was the walker, because the woman had asked me to walk and because I could. But once I stopped, there would be those little sounds behind me, and one of them would come from a place I hadn’t put my foot. I think that would be where I was.

Not in the sound exactly. In the bit of floor that had kept it.

At first I’d have very little to do with what came through me. A heel would strike six houses away and I’d give it back under an empty cupboard. Someone turning in his sleep would dislodge a tap I’d been holding all afternoon. The woman would put her ear down and hear two people where there was only one. I wouldn’t know which of these things she wanted. I might learn by the length of time she kept her ear there after I’d gone quiet.

I’d be a poor floor to live on. But there would be a child who liked me. She’d tap beside her bare toes, then run to the next doorway to see if she could get there ahead of the answer. Sometimes she could. Once she’d wait there until the light went down a floor in all the houses, and I’d return her tap at the place she’d started from. She’d miss it. Someone asleep with his cheek against the floor would hear it very clearly.

When the street widened I would go on being floor. That seems important. The woman and the old man would have to cross me, and at first she’d take the whole distance in one step, setting her foot on his threshold as if the pavement between them were still too new to trust. Later there’d be room for three steps. Later still, on a day when his feet hurt, they’d each come partway and sit down in the street.

I’d know them best getting up again: her palm flat, his knuckles first and then the heel of his hand. For a little while after they went indoors, the places where they’d sat would be warmer than the rest of me. A small animal could cross from one to the other without waiting for anything to move.
