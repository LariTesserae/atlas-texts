I would have a belly by evening. At noon I would still be making do with four wet feet.

There would be six of us in the drop, and I would know the others chiefly by what they did when the water moved. One put all its feet out. One tucked them in. The smallest opened its mouth so wide that sometimes I could put my whole head inside without either of us leaving the water. I did this often enough that it learned to close its mouth when it saw me coming.

We had a roof. I knew that much about where we lived. A rib crossed overhead, and when it let go of a drop the adults' faces grew large around us. I thought this was how faces grew.

The day the roof came down, I was out of the water. I had got a foot onto the leaf and was trying to put a second foot where the first had been. The leaf was dry and held the first foot fast. I pulled. The foot came away so easily that I went on pulling for a little while after there was nothing to pull against.

Then a mouth took me up. On the way out I saw the others still under the rib. On the way in I saw a gray animal with its back end in the sun and its face under a leaf. I could not understand why we had come to this animal. There was no room for it in the mouth with me.

It turned out there was room for me in the mouth with it.

I don't know how long I lay under that leaf. The animal's chin was pressed flat and the water was shallow on one side. I kept trying to get my missing foot into the deeper part. At intervals another mouth fitted over ours. Each time I expected to be taken out, and each time something else came in: a little water, another of the young, a shred of pale skin that one of us ate. The animal holding us did not move even when I put a foot up its nostril. Until then I had never known an adult to let me do anything twice.

By the time we came out, the leaf had grown a welt around its shoulders. It left most of its belly hair in that welt. We ate on a shoot that evening, the big animal at the top scraping down and the rest of us below it pulling our dinner out of the air. I remember looking up between bites and seeing the blue underside of its body. I had thought blue was the color of a place where there wasn't an animal.

That is where I would find myself first. Later I would be one of the old ones that travel in the open. Not from courage. The tight places would hurt where my nap had gone, and I would have learned that a long fall can be shorter than the time it takes a new leaf to reach you. I would walk where I could see what was coming up.

Except on dry afternoons. Then I would go down under the ribs with the others. There would be one young one that always got a foot over my lip when I lifted my chin. I would put it back with my tongue. It would get the foot out again. The rib would be dry, and the street above us would begin to turn, and because I could not close my mouth I would have no good way of telling it to stop.

I think I would lower my head just enough to let it feel the leaf while I still had hold of it. Not the dry part. I would find the little dark place where a drop had landed before us, and let it stand there a moment on all four feet.
