I’d be down one level from the willow court, at the place where you have to turn sideways to keep your shoulder dry. You could put a hand on me going through. I’d feel like part of the wall.

For a long time I would have been part of it. Then one winter, ice would have opened the seam behind me and I’d have come away at the top, leaving my lower edge wedged in place. Not much of a fall. A little grit coming down in the dark. From then on there would be an inside to me.

It would take a few rains to find out what sort of inside. Water could get in at the top, but most of it would cling to the wall behind me and come out cold at my foot. A strand of dead sedge would wash in and stay all summer, too long to make either of the turns that would let it out. Along one edge there would be room for a spider. Along the other, for nothing bigger than the spider’s thread.

I think I’d first know the salamander by the way it moved the sedge. It would come up from below, push the strand crosswise, and spend half the day with it across its back. When it went out, the strand would stay where it was. After a number of visits there would be a clean place on my inside face where the animal’s head fitted. The rest of me would go on collecting the fine brown powder the water left.

In a hard rain I’d be a poor place to stop. Water would come in faster than it could get out, and the sedge would stand up and beat against the roof of the crack. I’d have this in common with the upper courts: the thing that made me habitable could make me briefly uninhabitable without any change in my shape.

There would be a morning, after such a rain, when you took hold of me. The passage you meant to use would still be full, and the stone below your boot would have a skin of clay on it. I’d move a little. You’d take your hand away and try the other wall.

Afterward, the sedge would slide out. A grain caught under my lower edge would have turned, and the entrance from below would be narrower by the width of that grain. The salamander would come back at the usual hour and put its nose in, then one foot. It would back out. For a while there would be nothing touching me but the slow water from the wall.

Then, higher up than it had ever come in before, I’d feel it try again. There was a way in there too. Until then it had always been the way out for the water.
