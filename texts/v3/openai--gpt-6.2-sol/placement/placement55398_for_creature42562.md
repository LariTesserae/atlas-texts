I’d be on the western shelves, under a lip of shale low enough that you’d have to put your cheek to the ground to see me. Even then I don’t think you’d see an animal. You might see two damp spots a finger’s width apart and suppose the stone was slow to dry there.

At first I thought I’d be in the cup. There’s water there, and a little of the sky even after the sky itself has gone dark. But the cup is busy. A grazer puts its head in it; grit slides down its sides; on some mornings the whole surface lifts and comes away on the animal’s chin. I’d be a little farther back, where water has to travel along the underside of the shelf before it can fall.

The two spots would be the ends of me. One would be in a shallow pit that stayed wet most of the afternoon. The other would be farther out on the lip, where the wind could get at it. Between them I’d be thin enough to dry hard without breaking. In the dry part of the day I don’t think I’d know I had two ends. Then a bead would swell out of the pit, and one end would soften and draw the rest after it. For a little while I could reach into the track the water took. I’d feed there on the fine stuff a bead carries before it grows heavy enough to run.

The grazer below would be known to me mostly by what happened to the water. On nights it came early, the drop wouldn’t strike the cup. There’d be a small scrape as the animal lowered its head, and then the bead would pull away almost before it had formed. Some nights a second, lighter weight would come down beside it. Once, that smaller animal would stand high enough that a rough piece of its birth shell brushed the lip. The outer end of me would come away on it. I’d close over at the torn place. By the next afternoon there’d be only one damp spot.

I don’t know that I’d feel the loss for long. The pit would still fill. The adult would still come and drink. But a little way downslope, on an overhang too low for the adult to get its head under, the young one would back in at dawn. It would do that for several weeks, until it grew broad enough to rest out on the open shale. Each morning the rough patch on its back would touch nearly the same place. One morning there’d be enough water on the stone that what it carried could stay behind.

That second place has no cup. Water runs to the edge and falls into gravel, and even after a good afternoon there’s nothing there for a grazer to drink. I think that’s where I’d find myself, toward the end of the summer: not in the old track but just short of a new one, spread out along a grain in the stone. The little animal has stopped coming. It’s warm enough now for it to sleep in the open. In the afternoon a bead starts well to one side of me, and there’s a long time when it looks as if it will fall from there. Then its lower edge touches the grain. The whole weight of it turns.
