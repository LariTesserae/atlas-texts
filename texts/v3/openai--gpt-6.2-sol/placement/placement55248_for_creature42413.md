I think I’d be the person who brings the stoneworker her supper. I’d come from one of the houses on the far side of the saddle, close enough to see her lean-to from our door and far enough that, with a hot bowl, I’d have to go most of the way around the upper square. In the morning I’d cut straight across to fetch the bowl. For a while she’d know me chiefly by how much sooner I arrived when I wanted something back.

I’d be old enough to carry supper and not yet old enough to be much use with a hammer. When she let me chalk the numbers on the fallen pieces, I’d make them large enough to read from the street. She’d have me rub them out and do them smaller, near the edges where they wouldn’t be dressed away. Once I’d put a number on the fern block. She’d leave it there, and for a few days I’d think that meant she intended to use it after all.

I’d be the one who put the cup beside it. Not because I thought the fern needed watering, exactly. It had been my job to bring water when she was working, and that was the only stone there she’d stopped working on before it was finished. I wouldn’t have known what to do with it. A cup was something I could put down.

Later, when she sent me out to look for a tread on my own, I’d come back with one from the empty street above the gardens. She’d ask which end I’d taken it from. I’d say neither end; it was just lying in the middle. We’d go.look, and she’d show me the gap in the row of pale stones. I’d have to drag it back uphill. The next time somebody called that street empty, I’d know what they meant, but I’d look down the length of it anyway.

I don’t think I’d end up dressing the stair blocks. I’d get good at moving them. I’d know which landings would take a loaded sledge across the corner and which ones you had to go around, even if it meant unhitching the rope and coming at it from the other side. I’d have opinions about where people left their stools. The sisters’ father and I would sometimes move the same one in opposite directions without discussing it.

And I’d still bring her supper. By then I’d have a house nearer the cistern, and the quickest way would be down the broad stair. I’d go round the square instead. Coming that way, I could see under the lean-to before she saw me. If she was still working, I’d set the bowl on the wall and wait for her to finish the face she was on. On a warm evening I could sit there quite a while with the bowl between my hands, listening to how seldom she had to strike.
