I think I would come up from the clay country with a jar, and for the first few days I would mistake the country for a place where you could set a thing down.

I’d set the jar beside my bed and wake to find it tilted, its round foot half swallowed by the leaf joints. I’d move it onto a patch of good firm gray, only to see the gray rise and fall a little with the breathing of something underneath. In the end I’d carry it even on short errands, my hand inside its mouth and my fingers hooked into the shoulder. I’d learn to do most things with the other hand.

But I don’t think I’d be much of a traveler. I’d stop at a breach on the lee slope, not the one with the jars but a smaller one a little downhill, where a grazer had stepped through and left a strip of root felt hanging. On my first evening there I’d hear a caller in the gray wood. I’d put my ear to the wall and hear almost nothing. From my bed, six paces back, I’d hear it plainly. I’d spend an unreasonable part of the next evening moving my bed a handspan at a time.

That would be my occupation, if it became one: finding the places to listen from. People would ask me where the sound came from and I’d have to say I didn’t know. I could show them where to kneel, though, and which way to turn their heads. There’d be a spot where you could hear the small hard click of the front nails of a grazer, though the passage it was walking in hadn’t opened in anybody’s lifetime. On some nights you’d hear the click come back from ahead of the animal, and it would be hard not to think there was another file down there coming the other way. I’d think so myself until I heard one animal stumble and the echo stumble with it.

I would know the woman and her brother just well enough to recognize them by the sounds they made coming down to the jars. He would set his heel down first. She would stop a little uphill of the gutter, where the bench was solid, and call to me if it was light enough to see my bedding. Once, when she found me lying with my cheek flat to the slope, she’d ask if I was listening for water. I’d say yes, though I’d been listening to the faint dry scrabble of a silverfish. She’d wait beside me for a while. I’d be glad I couldn’t hear anything either.

Years in, I think the jar would crack. I’d plug the crack badly, then better, and finally set the jar on its side in the breach, with its mouth a little below the strip of felt. Something would take up residence. I’d hear it in there at dawn, turning around and trying to get comfortable; the clay would give its small movements a consequence they didn’t have in the wood. I’d be tempted to reach in. I’d know exactly how far my hand could go before I had to turn it, and how cool the shoulder would feel against my knuckles.

I’d leave it alone. But on mornings when there was enough seep to wash my eyes, I’d sit beside it until the leaves began to turn, and sometimes from inside the jar there’d be the little intake of breath of a sleeper about to wake. I’d hold still long enough to hear what it did next.
