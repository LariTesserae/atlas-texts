I’d start on the river side of the alder. There’s a place where you can get a knee onto the trunk without sinking the bark, and from there you can reach a dish on the other side. I’d put a finger in it. I’d be listening for the place that went quiet, and at first I’d miss the place that didn’t.

It would be very close. A little fall of grit under my knee, too soft to feel through the bark.

I don’t think I’d stay the person kneeling there. I’d be down in the clay at the end of one of the threads, though even that isn’t quite where I’d begin. For a few weeks I’d be a whole animal under a leaf. All night I’d take in the soft silt, and by daylight there would be a bright crescent of mica around me. Then the dish would spill. I’d join another animal at the notch, and after that I’d go on eating but never again make the blow that brought the food down. I’d be a mouth in a reach that extended past the alder. In time the alder would fall across it.

The fall itself might not do much to me. I could feed under a great weight of wood. What would matter would be the next rain, and the one after that. Leaves would lodge against the trunk; the leaves would catch clay. On the river side, the food would begin going past too fast. On my side, it would come to rest. There would still be one blow, but by then nearly everything it fed would be on one side of the tree.

In a dry year I’d draw down into the clay. A snail would take its time over the place where I’d been. Above me, a root growing out of the fallen wood would find the old groove and follow it. The root would be alive when rain came again.

I’d like to be there for the long wait after the first blow. Some of the water would have reached me by then, but not enough to move the silt. The root would be in the way. I could let it through, as I’d let others through; there’s room just below it where the clay is soft. Instead I’d close on the very end. By morning there would be a new mouth there.

That would be the one I’d find myself in. The old mouth would feed when the blow came from under the tree. The new one, a root hair’s width away, would feed when I struck. For a while the two would take turns clearing the same little patch.

A person with a finger in the dish could stop one of us. With a knee on the trunk they might hear the other, and shift their weight, thinking it was the wood giving a little under them.
