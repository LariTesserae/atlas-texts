I’d start by looking for a place on the rim where the water below was louder. I think I’d keep trying even after I knew better. I’d go out to one of the blunt points and turn my head, first one way and then the other, as if there were some position in which the sound would open and let me hear what was making it.

But the place I’d live is a few hundred paces back, under a slab that looks too low to get under. The entrance is at the uphill end. You have to go in headfirst and let your hind feet come off the ground before there’s anywhere to put them. Inside, there is room to turn around. There is even a place where, if you lie on your side, the stone touches neither ear.

I think I’d be one of the long animals. A young one at first, old enough to go out alone but still inclined to bring things back that have no use. Once, a whole stem with the leaves still fitted around it. I’d carry it crosswise and lose most of the leaves getting it through the entrance. In the morning I’d nose through what was left and find the little many-legged animal I’d been after, alive in the only pair of leaves that hadn’t come apart.

For a while I’d mistake anything overhead for a bird. A shadow passing across the mouth of the crack would send me flat. Then one afternoon a grazer would stand on the roof. I’d know where each foot was. She’d shift her weight and I’d feel the little slip of one hoof before it held. After a while something would brush the stone very lightly, several times over. When I went out, there would be a strip of her old coat caught on the uphill corner, still attached at one end. She’d be feeding a little farther up, with another strip hanging loose over her flank.

I’d get to know that female by the way she stood to drink. There’s a spot below her end of the pan where a fold in the stone lets me get close without coming into view. From there I can see the underneath of her jaw, which has a patch where the hair grows backward. On the last day she comes, I wait for her throat to move. It doesn’t. After she goes I climb up and put my tongue where hers has been. The rock is warmer there than it is on either side. I try both sides to make sure.

Late in the season I venture into one of the cracks that leads out below the rim. It takes a long time to get there. At one turn I have to back up and go through with my head on its side. I expect a nest, but the ledge I come out on is empty except for a dropping with a beetle wing in it. I can see a bird across the gap, under the next overhang. It’s feeding its young. Its tail sticks out and jerks a little each time it reaches in. I stay until it leaves. I’d thought that if I ever got down here I’d spend all my time looking down, but there’s no way to look down without taking my forefeet off the ledge. What I can see very well is the underside of the shelf. There are roots in it, finer than the hairs I pull out of the beds. One has a bead on the end.

That is where I’d go in the driest part of the year. Not often enough to wear a path. I’d get quicker at the turn in the crack, though, and learn to come out with my head already tipped back. Most days the root would be dry. On the good days I’d have to touch it with my tongue to get the water off, and afterward it would go on swinging for a long time in the air under the stone.
