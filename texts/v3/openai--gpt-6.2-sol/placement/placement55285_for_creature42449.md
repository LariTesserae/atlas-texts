I think I’d work for the tramway, in the room where people went for things they’d left on a car. It would be halfway down Ada’s stair, though to get to it from the platform you’d have to go down one flight and up three steps. People would arrive annoyed at having passed so close to it on the way down.

Most mornings I’d have six umbrellas to dry and nowhere to open the sixth. There’d be lunch tins, a surprising number of single shoes, and parcels that the conductors had wrapped a second time because the first paper had gone soft. We weren’t supposed to unwrap food. In warm weather we made exceptions.

The questions would be good ones. What did the handle feel like? Could you lift it with one hand? People could seldom tell me the color of a thing they’d carried every day for twenty years. Then I’d bring out two nearly identical bags and they’d put a finger straight through the broken place in the weave of the right one.

Ada would come in once for a wineglass. She’d left it on the tram coming back from her sister’s. I’d be about to ask how she meant to distinguish it from any other wineglass when she said there was a little lump on the stem, just where your finger went. We’d have it. It would stand crooked on the counter.

“Will that do?” I’d ask.

“It doesn’t have to do anything,” she’d say, and take it home.

I’d like to have been there long enough to remember the last rise, but not long enough to remember it well. I’d give the wrong year for the water coming over the sill, and someone waiting in the doorway would correct me. At the next rise, when the riverside track closed, I’d take the unclaimed umbrellas upstairs. The outgoing conductor and I would carry them in armfuls, catching the hooked handles on each other’s sleeves. On our last trip there’d be a calf with its chin on the landing, and we’d have to stop. From where we stood we wouldn’t be able to see the adult it was climbing onto. We’d just see the calf get a little taller, pause, and get taller again.

I’d live one step farther down, in a flat with a dry patch on the kitchen floor where a chair had stood since before I moved in. Quite often I’d get home with somebody else’s umbrella. I’d know it as soon as I went to hang it up. Mine had a straight handle. I’d stand there with my finger in the curve of the other one, trying to remember at which door I’d picked it up.
