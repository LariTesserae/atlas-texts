I’d be up at the door at first, in the little play between the latch and its catch. The baker’s mother shuts the door with her elbow because she has the broom in one hand and the tin in the other. The latch goes in, but the door isn’t quite done moving. It leans back against the catch. That’s my knock.

From up there I’d know the room mostly by what reached the stair. Water sounds much the same whether it’s coming toward you or going away, but a sparrow coming up the stairs is quite different from a sparrow going down. Coming up, she flies. Going down, she likes to hop from tread to tread. Sometimes the old hen would get as far as the third tread just as the baker’s mother put her foot on the first. The woman would stop. There would be a scuffle below her, then nothing, and she would put her foot down where she’d meant to.

One morning the baker would have a sack to bring through and the latch would catch her sleeve. She’d get a hammer and bend the catch inward. She’d try the door a few times and be pleased with it. It would shut with one good sound, the kind that tells you there is no need to turn around and try it with your hand.

For a few mornings the old hen would wait in her hole. Not all morning. A crumb would fall or another sparrow would get to the pan first, and out she’d come, tugging her white nail free. From upstairs you wouldn’t have known there was any change at all.

Then one day the baker’s mother would leave the tin on the third tread while she went back for the broom. She’d shut the door out of habit. A little later the tin, which she’d set with one corner over the worn part of the tread, would tip and strike the brick below.

That’s where I think I’d find myself: not in the tin exactly, and no longer in the door. Down one tread from where the woman usually stops, with a little dust sliding over my edge.

The hen would come out backward and drop. By the time the woman opened the door again, she’d have one wing in the pan. The woman would stand on the third tread and hear a sound she knew from a yard that no longer had a tap or a dish. Then she’d go down one more step to get her tin, and from there she’d see what was making it.
