I would start at the freight incline. The road comes into the city so high up that I think I would spend the first few days trying to stay at the height I’d arrived at. At the incline there would be oil cans to unwrap and a place to sit out of the rain. I could make myself useful without yet having to learn which doors opened on rooms and which opened on the tops of stairs.

But I think I would end up under one of the freight bridges, in the shortest of the inspection bays. And I don’t think I would be the inspector.

There is a pool there that shows a man holding a lamp. He is standing on a plank laid across the bay, with his free hand up against a joint in the ironwork. The plank is gone. If you look straight down from the paving you can see the sole of one of his boots. A nail has come partway out of the heel.

I would be the man holding the lamp, but not the man who went home in those boots. He must have shifted his weight a moment after the pool filled. He must have set the lamp down somewhere, and perhaps at the end of the shift he pulled the nail with a pair of pliers. I would know about the nail only because people kept bending over to look at it. From where I stood I couldn’t see my own heel.

For a while I would think the work was to hold the lamp steady so that someone could see the joint. Certainly I would take an interest in anyone who came with a key. There is a brass plug within reach of the paving, and I would see the key going down toward it and wonder whether, when the water returned, I might be standing a little farther along the plank. It would take me a long time to understand that there was no farther along for me. The new water would show the underside of the bridge, with nobody under it.

Esta would come once in a while with the newspaper. Not to read by my lamp; it gives no light above the water. The paving under the bridge is dry for a good stretch on either side of the bay, and she could put the sheets down flat there. When she leaned over to take them up, the paper would show white beside my hand. I could read a few words backward. The first whole thing I learned to read would be the price of eggs, from a column she weighted with the brass key.

One evening she would bring the man from the unlit district. He would put his two glasses down by the pool and separate them. She would point out the nail in my boot. He would look at it, then move one of the glasses a little nearer the edge.

There would be two lamps in the water then: the one in my hand and a little curved one in the side of his glass. He would turn the glass until the second lamp came clear. I would have spent years with my hand against that joint, and it would never have occurred to me that there was enough light to spare.

After they went away I would still be curious about the key. I don’t think I would ever see it come down without wanting to know what happened next. But on evenings when someone stopped beneath the bridge to set down what they were carrying, I would look first at what they had brought.
