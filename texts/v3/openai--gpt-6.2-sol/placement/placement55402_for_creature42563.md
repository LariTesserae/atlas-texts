I think I would live at the last door before the old crossing. Not the farthest north anyone had ever lived. There would be a door a little farther on that my aunt could still get through if she took off her coat and went down feet first. She said this to anybody who called the room abandoned. She hadn’t been down herself in years.

I would be the person she sent for the cup.

I’d expected a good cup, to account for the sending. This one would have a handle too small to put a finger through and a place on the rim that caught at my lip. Coming back up I’d have to set it on a ledge above my head while I got my shoulders through. I’d put it down out of sight and for a moment I’d be sure it had gone over. Then my fingers would find the handle. That would be the part I told my aunt about. I wouldn’t tell her I’d drunk from it in the room.

“Was it upside down?” she’d ask.

I’d say yes.

“Well, there you are,” she’d say, though I wouldn’t know where.

For a while I’d make an occupation out of going into rooms people no longer went into. A man wanted the stone his sister had used to hold her skirt down while she nursed. Someone asked me to see whether the pool in his old house was still warm. I brought back a bottle of the water, which by then was the temperature of my hand. We passed it between us without opening it. He was perfectly satisfied, or had the courtesy to look it.

There were errands of my own. What I liked best in an empty house was to find something I could put right without having to bring it away. I tipped water off beds. Once I spent most of an afternoon trying to wedge a shelf level before it occurred to me that it had been set at a slant to drain. I took the wedge out and stayed to watch the water find its old way off.

The children would know where I went, because I’d leave my coat outside. They’d start leaving things in the pockets. A stone that fit a thumb. A shrimp carapace, complete down to the whiskers when it went in, dust by the time I reached for it. One day there’d be a cup. It would be a perfectly good cup from somebody’s kitchen, and I’d carry it around half the morning asking whose it was before a child told me where it was supposed to go.

She took me to the old crossing and pointed down. She’d picked out a shelf in the room they were always finding under there. I could see the shelf, though there wasn’t a dry way to it and there wasn’t a room.

“Not yet,” I said.

She took the cup from me. I thought she meant to drop it through, but she lay on her stomach and worked it down onto a little step just below the bank. You could reach it from the crossing if somebody held the back of your coat. She had me do that. When she got up her sleeve was wet clear to the shoulder.

On my way home that evening I stopped and had a drink there. I had to lie down too. The water was close enough that I could see it rise against the stone when I put my elbow in. I drank, set the cup upside down, and got up with some difficulty, there being nobody to hold my coat.

After that I’d go by that way even when I had no errand north. I’d keep a chip in my pocket to steady the cup when the little step began to tilt. I’d have to replace the chip now and then. The children, who could have found another shelf in the time it took me to do that, would let me.
