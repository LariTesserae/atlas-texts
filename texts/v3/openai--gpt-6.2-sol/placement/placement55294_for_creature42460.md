I’d come down with a tape measure to see whether the empty housing could go out through the door.

It could go through the door. It couldn’t make the turn before it. This took me most of a morning to establish, because the tape would go anywhere I asked it to. I could bend it around the shutoff wheel, draw it out above the low return, and get a perfectly good number between two points that no piece of iron could ever occupy at the same time.

After lunch I brought down a piece of cardboard cut to the size of the housing’s base. The woman from the early shift took one end. In the narrow part we had to hold it edgewise just to get ourselves into position. Then we lowered it, and my end caught the wheel while hers was still well short of the door.

“Now make it three feet high,” she said.

We put the cardboard down. The current had gone off while we were working, and I rested my hand on the housing. I’d been calling it the dead one all morning. It was warmer than my hand.

I think I’d be the person who left the cardboard there, propped against the wall with one corner down in the spoon. Not to see what would happen. I’d have intended to take it upstairs and put it in the bin, and then needed both hands for something else.

A week later I’d come back for the tape measure. The cardboard would have a dark tide mark halfway to the first fold. When I drew it away from the wall, a springtail would be standing on the underside. I’d turn the sheet a little to get it into the light, and the springtail would disappear. I wouldn’t know whether it had jumped or simply gone around the edge.

By then the removal sheet upstairs would have **NO ROUTE** written across it in my block capitals. I’d leave that alone. But I’d lower the cardboard again, slowly this time, until the wet corner touched the tile. I’d hold it there a while before taking it away.
