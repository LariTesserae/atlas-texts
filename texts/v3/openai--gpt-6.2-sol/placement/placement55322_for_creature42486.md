I’d be the person who comes down to read the electricity meter.

The meter is in the pump house, behind a door that opens only as far as the chair. The first time I come, the attendant gets up and moves the chair for me. After that she moves it when she hears my case knock against the yellow rail. We have never arranged a day. I come on the last working Thursday of the month, unless there’s a holiday, in which case I come on a day she has no way of predicting.

I have to put one foot inside the pump house and keep the other outside to read the dial. There’s a pipe at the height of my forehead. In winter I take my hat off so I can see it.

For a while I think the attendant watches me because she’s not supposed to leave me alone with the equipment. Then one day I shut the door and she says, “Move your foot a little.”

My foot is in the pale patch from the roof. I move it. Under the step I see the fish.

“Which one?” I ask.

She hasn’t said anything about one.

“The one coming back,” she says.

I see it stop, or almost stop, and do something small with its tail. On the way up I decide the tail was torn. By the time I reach the market I can see the tear quite clearly, which is how I know I didn’t see it.

The meter is replaced in autumn. The new one can be read from an office on the other side of the city. I’m there when the electrician takes out the old one, and he lets me hold it while he puts his gloves away. It’s heavier on one side. Through the glass I can see the last number I wrote down. I tip the meter a little, though there’s no reason the number should move.

The next month I go down without my case. The chair is still in front of the door. I tell the attendant I was passing, which isn’t quite true: at the tram stop I let my tram go.

She puts a finger between the pages of her book and comes to the rail. We wait a while. A pigeon walks the length of the rail toward us, then walks back. When the next tram goes over, she takes her finger out of the book and lets it close.

“Sometimes it’s over by the pipes,” she says.

From the chair she can’t see the water directly beneath them. From where I’m standing I can, but only if I lean far enough for the rail to press into my ribs. There are two fish there. One is scarcely longer than the brass end of the hose. The other keeps turning just before I can see its tail.

For a while after that I come on Thursdays. Not the last working Thursday, just Thursdays when I have time. I bring a folding stool once, but there’s nowhere to open it that doesn’t make the attendant move her chair, so I take it home again.

I stand by the end of the rail, where I can see under the pipes. When there’s a fish there I say where it is, if she asks. Mostly she doesn’t. Sometimes, when the motors are in that part of their note where it’s easy to hear, she tells me something that happened on a day I wasn’t there.
