I’d be at the foot of the upright brick, on the side you can’t see from the street.

At first I thought I’d be the collector’s hand. There’s a good place for the thumb, low down, where it could bear against the edge without scraping the jamb. I can imagine getting the brick open with one hand and keeping a little cup level in the other. But the hand would go away again. What I keep coming back to is the bit of the turn that’s still possible after all this time.

There is very little of it. In the afternoon, if you put your cheek to the street, you might see the top of the brick lean out. You would have to pick a grain on the jamb to sight against, and even then you might blame your eye. Down at the foot, one corner takes the weight and the other comes free. That much is left of the old opening. I think that’s where I’d fit.

The first afternoon after the leaf appears, the brick pinches it. When the brick lets go, the leaf doesn’t move. A spot of oil has stuck its point to the jamb. All night the leaf is held there, folded except for a little belly in the middle.

The next day a grain comes along the worn curve and stops under the corner that lifts. It’s too large to pass under the threshold and too small to see from the street. When the brick cools, it comes down on the grain. There’s a small dry click. The brick is no easier to open than it was, but it rests at a slightly different angle.

That would be my whole event. I wouldn’t know about the leaf until the next afternoon, when its point, dried fast to the jamb, breaks off. The rest of it opens on the inside. It’s a crooked little leaf now, short on one side, and when the hot air comes down it keeps brushing the brick with the broken end.

Years ago a hand could have turned the brick far enough to let a person through. I’d still have room for that turn. But the leaf has grown across the place where the thumb would go, and I’m not sure I’d want the hand back.
