There is something in the bowl besides the animals you can see. It is easiest to find when a snail puts its mouth to one of the stems. Before the snail takes its first bite, a narrow crease climbs the stem from below. It stops just short of the mouth. The snail eats down to it and goes no farther.

On the ragged stem the crease has gone crooked. If you watch for a while you can see it trying the same short passage over and over, drawing back at a split in the skin. It has no color of its own. Where it passes over a green part it is green, and where it passes over the pale sheath you can lose it altogether. For most of an afternoon you might take it for a worm.

Then a beetle comes up through the slit with its silver burden and strikes the underside of the root. The crease lets go. At the same instant the moss draws in a little around the two frogs, and three of the bladders under the pennywort flatten out of order. The bowl goes quiet. It lasts only long enough for the beetle to turn itself over. You can hear, in the interval, the snail eating.

Whatever drew back does not come forward all at once. First the water goes over the lip again. Then a bubble gets as far as a folded leaf and waits there, making the leaf look as though it has a clear bead sewn into it. The churn starts low down. The crease returns on a different stem.

It isn't the root, though there are parts of the root it has never been seen to leave. In the wet months a hair-fine extension of it sometimes goes out into a pennywort runner. You can follow its progress by the little checks in a bubble traveling the other way. It has been as far as a leaf that lay flat on the open marsh, well beyond the room. A moorhen stepped on that leaf one morning. The whole runner sank and came up shining, and for the rest of the day the bowl let its water go in small, uneven spills.

It lives on what settles. There is a soft brown deposit behind the lip that looks the same from one afternoon to the next, although a grain of it, marked by a white fleck or a bit of wing, will travel the width of the bowl by evening. Sometime in the night the grain comes apart. The water takes the white fleck. The bit of wing is still there in the morning, upright and astonishingly clean.

The frogs seem to know nothing about it. The beetles may know a little. When the current fails, their feet go on working under the floor, but after a time they all turn, and the feet you see through the slit are pointed the other way. One of the small crustaceans has found a place where the crease passes out of a stem and across a bare patch of root. It backs into that place every afternoon. The crease lifts it just enough to wash the mud out from under its belly. Neither appears to be waiting for the other.

In a dry week, the outermost folds empty first. You can see old reaches of the thing then: shallow ridges, no wetter than the root around them, with a husk or a snail dropping lodged against each one. Farther in, the active part has gathered close around the five stems. When the youngest stem breaks through its sheath, it comes up so fast that a transparent strand stretches between the two. All afternoon the strand gets thinner. At dusk it parts, and the end on the old sheath curls up and is carried over the lip. The other end is already hard to see. It lies flat against the new green, a little below where the snail will put its mouth.
