For most of the year the animals in Allowance are flat. One could lie under a washer without lifting it. They are the color of the cut ends of the bricks, except along the belly, where there is a faint rust stain from what they eat. The maintenance crew found the first one by taking a photograph of a plant and then taking another, nine months later, from the same position. A blemish on the brick had gone around a corner.

The rust-colored leaves have a seam where they double back. A powder collects there as the leaf ages, and the animals work it out with the narrow end of the head. It takes a long time to tell which end that is. They’ll go backward rather than turn, even on an open face of brick. At the lip of the ditch, though, they turn and go down headfirst. They never cross the ditch. There’s nothing on the other side they couldn’t eat, and the drop is less than the length of a finger. If you lift one across, it goes about its business there.

The young are easier to see. They have six white feet apiece and hold their bodies clear of the masonry. They come out from under the leaves at widely separated intervals, usually one or two at a time, but once all the plants gave up their young in the same week. The crew came in to find them strung out along the cut edges of the crest. They were taking little bites of something too fine to see. When a glove brushed the brick, every foot on that length of crest let go. The young fell into the keyed grit and spent the next several days coming back up.

Once, during a shutdown, an electrician ate his lunch on the threshold with the door propped open. In the quiet he heard a dry tapping and took it for a relay. It was one of the older animals walking up the shallow ditch. Like the young, it had six feet. It put them down hard and well away from its body, in the manner of someone finding steps in the dark. Two more came out while he watched. They walked past the nearest plant without stopping and went on to the saddle.

There they got under the washer. The electrician could see the rim rise and settle as each one worked its way in. He stayed after his break to see what they’d do when the compressors came back. At the first low note, the washer came down flat.

He marked the spot on the brick with a pencil so he could check it at the next shutdown. Later somebody rubbed out the mark. It was on the yellow square, and the people who went there at shift change had got into the habit of setting the washer just outside the paint, where they wouldn’t step on it. Nobody had told them to do this. It was a small space to stand in, and moving the washer was the first thing you did when you got there.
