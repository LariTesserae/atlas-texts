The animals on the white ridge are called shod, whether they have managed to get a scale onto their backs or not. The young ones are the size of a hand and quite bare. You can see the short ribs working under their skins as they run. They catch scales by going underneath them and rising on all six legs at once. Then they have to stand still while the scale contracts. An old shod can tell by the first pinch whether to keep a piece or duck out from under it. The young sometimes get their legs folded up. You find them rocking on the pan, furious, with their feet an inch off the ground.

This is one way the people in the shelters get shod. They pry a small one loose and carry it home under an arm. It bites all the way up the stair. For the first few days it eats the wedges out of anything that has been repaired recently. Then it finds a place where two walls are drawing together and goes to sleep with its back between them. A good house shod will move from joint to joint for years. When it outgrows the house, you have to follow it around for a morning and see what it was holding up.

There are eleven people in the oldest shelter. You would count nine at first. One of the children sleeps in a sling beneath the floor, where the roof cannot wake him, and his grandmother spends much of the day on the outside wall. She is the one who cuts the steps. She lost two toes to a step she had cut herself: she rested her foot in the notch while she sharpened the adze, and by the time she wanted it back the notch had narrowed. Her new steps are broad and shallow. Everybody complains about them except the children, who can climb to the door without using their hands.

They talk with their hands, but not much at a distance. A long account is told with the listener’s wrist lying across the speaker’s palm. The children make a game of telling two people different things at once. At supper, someone who wants a dish puts a finger on the back of the hand that has it. Someone who wants the last piece puts down a whole hand, and is likely to get a whole hand laid firmly on top.

They eat the soft side of the shoots, too. They have a broad little knife for taking it off in one piece, leaving the glazed side upright. Below the shelters there is a patch of these half shoots, each with its fur turned to a scar. In a few days the scar thickens enough to cut again. The youngest child was given a patch of her own and cut both sides, including the hard part, which she likes to chew. Her shoots have all curled over. She has been tying them to one another in pairs, trying to get them to stand. So far she has made a low arch that the house shod goes out of its way to walk through.

Of the things that travel with the groves, the easiest to keep is a leaf animal with a blunt brown head. At rest it closes two green flaps over its head and looks like a damaged leaf. Several spend the day under the eaves. They leave one at a time and come back all together, crowding into the same fork and shoving with their elbows. Nobody has seen them feed. The grandmother once sat beside the fork for the better part of a day to find out, and caught herself picking burs off one. It had been leaning against her knee for some time. Now when she comes in from cutting steps, they walk down her back and pick the adze chips out of her collar.

The house shod has been sleeping by the door lately, with its head out over the stair. At the first lean of the new ridge it gets up. It backs down the shallow steps, feeling for each one with a hind foot. The girl follows it as far as her patch. She expects it to stop there as usual, but it goes straight through her arch and down to the pan.

It is too old to run with the others. At the edge of the new green it lowers itself until its belly touches, and waits. Presently a little bare one comes out between its front legs. Then another comes out. The girl has polished that broad back with her sleeve, has picked the crumbs from under its scales; she has never looked underneath it. She sits down on the last step to count. By the time her grandmother comes down to fetch her, she has counted seven, including one that keeps going back in.
