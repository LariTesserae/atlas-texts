The seed-eaters have a way of carrying things with their feet that makes it difficult to count them. What looks like a pair clinging belly to belly under a branch may be one animal and a husk. They sleep with all four feet shut. To get a sleeper off a branch, its young climb onto its back and pick at the toes one at a time. The last toe holds on after the animal is awake and looking around.

At the low wall they use the hammerstone without lifting it. They fit a husk into the cup, turn it until it stops rocking, and strike it with a smaller stone held between the forefeet. A good husk opens with very little noise. The work that has worn the cup so deep is the turning: they will sit there a long time with their cheeks laid against the shell, rolling it a fraction one way and then back. The young strike whatever side is uppermost. They eat a great deal of shell.

There are two sorts of the paper-colored runners, though you can spend a day among the trees without seeing the difference. One has a dark line down its back. The other has a slit there, and at the end of the day the slit opens and a dark-lined runner comes out. What remains goes on holding the branch. In some of the less windy forks these empty skins have accumulated until there is scarcely bark enough for a living foot. The seed-eaters pull them loose to wrap the kernels they don't eat at the wall. A wrapped kernel can be wedged into a tuft of little bronze leaves and left there for days. Often it is still there when the leaves have grown around it.

The grazers begin life inside the cracks. You can tell where there are young by the old ones that stand with their heads turned sideways, taking the new growth from only one lip. For a while the young eat the other lip from underneath. Then one evening a little face appears in the crack and bites in the wrong direction, out into the air. The old grazer backs away a step. It does not help the youngster out, and sometimes the little face is gone again by morning. Those that come out keep the flat shape of the crack for several days. They can get under the lowest course of the wall without disturbing the dust.

Nothing in the trees is quite at home on the open lanes. Even the runners, which can cross a lane in the time it takes a bell to swing back, stop at the far side with their bodies held off the ground, as if the heat had followed them under the leaves. The long-tailed animal on the rim is different. It lies out on stone through the middle of the day. Its belly is pale and tough, with a seam down it where the hair changes direction. Now and then it gets up, leaving a patch of rock cool enough that the runners collect there.

It is not waiting on the rim for something to come up. It is waiting for something to go over. There is a ledge below, invisible from the grove, where the rising wind presses loose husks and leaf scales flat against the mountain. Small animals land there too, and some of them spend the rest of their lives on that shelf. The long-tailed animal goes down after dark. On its way back it pauses with its forefeet hooked over the rim and its mouth full, the loose tail winding and unwinding below it. Whatever it has brought up, it carries to the highest of the three walls.

There are young in that wall. They are old enough to come out but not yet heavy enough to stay out. During a lull they put their noses through the gaps and lick the warm stone. When the wind comes back they withdraw so quickly that, if you are watching from the wrong side, all you see is a row of small tongues disappearing. Later they will learn to lean into the wind. For now the parent feeds them through the gaps, going along the wall and stopping wherever it feels a mouth take hold.
