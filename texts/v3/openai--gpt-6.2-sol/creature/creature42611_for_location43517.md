There are seventeen people in the eleven houses, though if you ask how many live here, someone will usually ask you what time of day you mean.

The woman who fetches the water is called Sella. She shares her house with her brother and a crab that fits exactly into the space beneath the hearthstone. The crab came with the stone. For years they thought the hearth gave a little sigh when the fire went out; then one winter evening the whole thing stood up and walked into the pantry. Sella’s brother put a cold brick in its place while it was gone. It has since learned to bring the brick back when it wants the fire put out.

Her brother mends shoes. Most of his work is on the insides, where a person’s toes curl to hold on during the descent. He can tell by the wear who has been taking the public stair and who has been cutting through the ferns. He doesn’t tell Sella, who disapproves of the shortcut. He takes it himself when the ferns are dry.

The youngest person in town is a boy named Pell. He has never seen the market in use and thinks a stall is something you make to keep a table from blowing away. For a while he tried to give each of the wall animals a name. It turned out that what he’d taken for one animal with a torn ear was three animals that followed the same route, one feeling for the ledge where the last had felt. Now he names the routes. There is Milk Door, which passes a door that hasn’t opened in his lifetime, and Long Pocket, where the animals disappear into a fold in the rock and come out much lower, with pale dust on their backs. Pell has put his arm into Long Pocket up to the shoulder. Even in summer it is warm inside.

The black newts live on mites and the eggs of the gutter flies. Early in the year they are so thin that they can turn around in the joints of the paving. Later they have to go on to the next crossing. The moths have a taste for salt. Leave a shirt on one of the market tables and in the morning the collar will have the clean, faintly prickled feel of something that has been licked. People used to lay damp shirts there on purpose, until Sella found Pell asleep on a table with moths settled all along his eyelashes. He said he had been awake the whole time. It felt, he said, as if somebody were counting him.

The others are harder to learn about. They don’t invariably come between the same two animals, or leave prints of the same depth. One drags a foot. One goes so lightly that its passage is visible only when it steps in a print already filling with water and the water trembles. And one, not much higher than the bowl, always comes back a little way after drinking. You can follow the small wet tracks to the middle of the square, where they stop. A woman who lived opposite used to wait there for it. If she held out the hem of her apron, the cloth would draw tight between her fingers. She did this for years. Nobody now can agree whether she was offering to lead it somewhere or letting it keep her where she was.

The bowl is an ordinary one. When it cracked, Sella replaced it with a soup plate for three nights, and the plate was emptied too. What matters, apparently, is that there be enough water for the last arrival. In a dry week that can be a difficulty. Sella has carried her pot down full while her brother stood at their door with flour on his hands, waiting to make the evening bread. He has also, without being asked, taken the pot from her and carried it the rest of the way.

There was a time when people tried to see where the drinkers went. They dusted the far edge of the square with flour, which told them nothing they hadn’t known already and brought the newts out white for a week. Pell found a better place to watch, sitting on an alder root above the uphill wall. From there he could see the paving take the first footstep. It landed some distance out in the square, as if whoever made it had already been walking for a while. He watched until his legs went to sleep. When he climbed down, he saw that someone had swept his own tracks away, except for the two beside the root. Those had been swept around.

In the mornings, the person who sweeps is usually the first to find whatever the rock has done in the night. A curb may be a finger higher at one end. A step may have come loose without making a sound. People get dressed and come out to see whether the change is worth keeping. They have acquired some good steps this way, and lost a doorstep Sella particularly liked. The white root that nosed into the road last night has lifted a stone just far enough to let daylight under it. Pell lies down to look and finds two newts inside, each facing a different way. He fetches a chip from the shoemaker’s bench and wedges it under the stone, so that when the root grows on there will still be room for both of them.
