The largest animal in the marsh can go all summer without showing more than the top of its head. It is a broad, mud-colored thing with four short legs and a tail flattened from side to side. When the water is low you can sometimes see its back under the ferns and mistake the pale seam down the middle for a root. The seam is where its skin folds when it bends. It can turn around in a hole scarcely longer than its head.

It eats the little fish, though not many of them. Mostly it eats the larvae that live in the brown skeins of fern root. At dusk it puts a forefoot into the skein and combs steadily, drawing its toes together at the end of each stroke. Then it brings the foot to its mouth. It will do this for an hour in the same place. The fish get used to the foot and come out to feed on what it shakes loose.

There are fewer of these animals than there are warm pools. Some pools are warm in the wrong way: a seep enters beneath a bank and makes a pocket of almost hot water with no easy way out. The young are small enough to get into such pockets. The old ones keep to the mixing places, where they can move a little toward the rain or a little toward the mountain without leaving cover. In the coldest part of the year they lie in the channels under the cane. A stem may have ice on the side the wind strikes and, lower down, a soft deposit of eggs on the side that stands in water.

On the wooded knolls live mice with coarse, nearly waterproof coats. Their paths run under the spreading trunks and out along branches that touch the sedges. They eat cane seed when there is cane seed, and for much of the year they eat snails. At the foot of a tree there will be a collection of little broken shells, some bleached and some still green. A mouse brings a snail to the same fork in the roots because there is room to brace its hind feet there. In a wet year the heap grows high enough to be found by a mouse born the following spring. It need not have seen another mouse eating there. The fork is still a good fit.

The birds that skim the open water do not sleep in the basin. Toward evening they go out through the notch, sometimes so low that their wings pass between the last stalks of cane. A smaller bird stays. It is dun except for a patch of white on each heel, visible when it hitches itself up a stem. It nests in the hollow left when a tuft of sedge is lifted by steam and settles crookedly on its bed. It brings grass to the hollow but does not line the bottom. Its chicks sit on the bare mineral shelf with their toes spread. When rain falls for several days they are dry underneath before they are dry on top.

At the outflow the inhabitants are small enough that a hand laid on the sill covers several kinds. There are flat grubs that hold to the stone in the thinnest water, and black snails no bigger than the seeds of the grass above them. Just short of the brink a fly lays its eggs on the underside of fern leaves. The larvae drop into the current when they hatch. Most are carried over. When the flow slackens, a few lodge in the hollows and grow there, eating the soft green film on the stone. Their empty cases are still attached when the next rain comes. Water divides around each one and joins again on the other side.

The warmest mouths have a population of their own. It shows mostly as color, but not quite the same colors as the stone. A pale shelf that appears bare in the morning may have a shine to it by noon, like the wet inside of a peeled reed. At its edge, where the water cools enough for other things to feed, there are abrupt vacancies in the shine. By the following day some of them have filled in. The new lip growing over the tree root has a living skin on the side toward the vent. On the side toward the tree, a little brown snail has been eating its way along the join. The trail it leaves is narrower than the root's finest crack.

When the sedges lift along the western rim, almost nothing moves away. The mice know the tremor of a shelf and the different tremor of a foot on it. The little bird turns its eggs. The fish shift their heads into the changed current. Beneath them, in water the color of the bank, the broad animal opens one eye. It has a fern root across its nostrils. After a while it raises its head just far enough to let the root float clear, takes a breath, and lowers itself again. From above, there is only a little bright water where the root has moved.
