There is a vole with a white toe on its left hind foot. You can follow it by that toe for most of an afternoon. It cuts a stem of sedge, sits back to eat the tender part, and leaves the rest lying across its lap. When it goes down to the water it takes the stem with it. There is another stem lying on the stone where it sat, cut at the same slant. If you wait for the vole to come back and eat that one, you will waste the afternoon. It has had enough.

The animals get full. That is one of the first things you learn if you stay longer than it takes to notice that the food does not run out. The wrens stop hunting and go into the trees to put their bills under their wings. The newts come out of the water with bellies that barely clear the rock. A shrew that has eaten will stretch on its side in a dry place no wider than two fingers. The dry place is under the dripping shelf. Its fur stirs a little at every fall of water.

The white-toed vole has a nest under the middle tree on the rim. From below you can see the ends of the grass it has brought in, dangling out among the roots. One end is not grass but a length of blue sewing cotton. Except for the orange stain, it is the only thing in the hollow that is that color. The vole draws it in when it works on the nest. Later you see it hanging down again, a little dirtier, with the same knot at the end.

In the nest are three young. Two are sleek and busy and one still has the blunt, unfinished look of something that ought to be under its mother. It is this one that comes farthest out. It gets as far as the first fern and stands there with its forefeet together, nosing at the beads along a frond. The mother fetches it back by the skin. There is no second young vole left standing at the fern.

You begin to look for what there are not two of.

There is no second white toe. There is only one wren with a bare patch at the angle of its jaw, where, when it sings, a bit of yellow skin shows. One of the newts has lost the end of its tail. The stump has healed smooth. It favors the palm-sized stone, and the other newts climb over it so freely that for a while you think there must be several with that little blunt tail. Then they go into the water, one after another, and there it is, last to move, lifting each foot with care as it gets its belly clear.

The moth with the crooked wing is harder to account for. On a still morning it goes over the rim. You can see it from above for a long time, flying out over the slope with the crooked wing giving its flight a small hitch. It gets down among the junipers. It is not coming back. Under the fern, meanwhile, a moth works one leg over its head and catches it briefly on the crooked edge of its wing. When you lift the frond it flies. It goes straight to the stone where the blunt-tailed newt lies, and the newt takes it. The next moth stays under the frond all day. Towards evening it lays six eggs, widely spaced, along the midrib.

There is something that gathers the eggs.

You see its work first where a fern grows out over the water. The frond is too far out for a vole to reach without bending it, and it does not bend. One by one the little cream dots go dark. Then the nearest one is gone. When your eyes adjust to the reflection you can see a pale mouth against the underside of the leaf. The mouth opens sideways, and there is a row of tiny moving parts inside it, finer than the teeth of a comb. It eats the eggs at the pace of an animal that has plenty of time and would rather not swallow fern.

From the far bank you can see that the mouth belongs to a creature lying in the pool. From where you stand beside the fern you can see that the mouth is a foot higher than the water. You can walk back and forth as often as you like. The creature goes on feeding.

In the shallows it is the length of your hand and has a soft tapering body, with a line of dark spots low on either side. After it feeds, it settles among the red roots. The roots are full of them. Some are scarcely thicker than the fern stems; one has skin loose enough to fold at the throat. When the deep turning slows, those folds draw together. The creature presses its belly down. All the others do the same. You can see the spots on their sides open, separately, like the holes in a flute under fingers that are finding their places. Then comes the brisk knocking and the hiss. At the very end, one of them makes a sound almost exactly like a wren starting a note and thinking better of it.

That is when the wrens turn their heads.

One winter a wren came to the low branch of the tree in the pool and waited through seven or eight of those calls. Its feathers were puffed so far out that you could hardly see its feet. Each time the sound came, it bent forward. Finally it answered. The answer went out over the rim, quite thin in the open air. A second wren flew in from somewhere down the slope and landed beside it. They sat without touching for a while. Then the second bird began to pick something out of the feathers behind the first bird’s eye.

There are nests in all four trees, but that one is the only tree the wrens go to when they have lost sight of one another. They do not wait at the rim to see what will come in. They sit out over the black water, where the branch moves with their weight, and call down. Sometimes there is no answer after the hiss, and they have to call again. Sometimes the bird they are waiting for comes through the ferns behind them, with wet feet and something in its bill, and by the time it reaches the branch they have already turned to make room.
