The first person to live on the street is a woman named Ada Venn, though she has a room aboveground and goes there every morning. If you ask where she lives, she gives the address of the room. If you ask where she keeps her good cup, she points to Number Two.

She minds the salt. Most nights this means reading six instruments and writing down six numbers, five of which are the same as they were an hour ago. At first she was afraid of copying the old numbers by mistake. Now she can tell from the stair whether the fourth one will have changed. There’s a loose tread halfway up that stops rattling when the return pipe warms. She told a fitter about it and he offered to tighten the tread. She said she’d let him know.

Her cup stands inside the inspection door, on a ledge that is exactly wrong for keeping tea and exactly right for keeping a pear. She brings a hard pear down on Mondays and eats it on Thursdays. There is a knife for this, and a saucer with a hairline crack that has gone brown from the pears. She eats them standing in the cool strip with her elbows tucked in. She used to eat them on the bench.

The tile beside the ferns was her doing. The day shift has since improved on it by painting the toe end white. Every so often somebody brings a bottle down to water the ferns, and Ada has to explain that they have water. What they could use is a little more light, but the lamp they could use is the one she switches off when she goes up to read the instruments. She has tried leaving it on. From the stair she can see the empty street lit all the way to the bench, and after an hour or so she comes down and turns it off.

On some nights there’s a boy at the other address. His name is Emil and his job is to listen to the bearings. He has permission to bring his schoolwork, provided he keeps the pages out of the fan intake. He works his sums on top of the motor casing until the paper gets too warm for his hand, then moves to a step. At two he comes over to Ada’s door to ask if she wants anything from upstairs. She always says no. He always comes back with two cold apples, one in either pocket of his coat. By the time he reaches her, the apples have sweated through the cloth. She saves his for him on the sill where he’ll see it on his next round, though she knows perfectly well he meant her to have both.

There is one other thing alive on the street, not counting what lives under a fingernail or in the water before it falls. In the damp oval, one of the ferns has put up a frond on the far side of the tile. To do this it has grown along the tile’s underside, in the thin space left by a lump of old mortar. Ada found it when she took the tile up to wash off the white paint somebody had spilled over the edge. The new stalk lay flat for the length of her thumb and then turned up so sharply that at first she thought she’d broken it. She fetched a washer from Number One and fitted it under the tile to give the stalk a little more room. The tile rocks now if you touch it. Everyone who works there knows not to touch it.

Ada has seen the plans for the stair. There will be a landing right where she eats her pears, and people will come down from the market carrying fish and bunches of wet onions. She’s looking forward to the fish. She has asked about a drain for the landing and whether anyone has worked out where the water from the cable sleeve will go. The man with the plans looked up at the sleeve and said he hadn’t known it was there.

She waits for a drop to fall whenever she brings somebody to see it. This takes longer than she expects, even though she’s there every night. Most people look away too soon. Emil will wait. Sometimes when they’re both standing there a valve opens and the chain comes up tight, and neither of them can hear whether the drop has fallen. Then they have to wait for the next one.
