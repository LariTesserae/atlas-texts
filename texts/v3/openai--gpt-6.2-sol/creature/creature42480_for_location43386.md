The husk under the paint belongs to the animal that laid the eggs. The legs are still on it, and so are the fine hairs along the back. The washer has a sharper edge than it looks from outside. She caught the old skin there and walked out of it, leaving even the skin of her feet behind. For most of that morning she lay on her side. A mite went over her twice. By evening she had hardened enough to carry the eggs, one at a time, from the washer to the paint.

She is the length of a grass seed and about the same color, except just behind the head, where a meal shows dark through her. What she eats is the soft growth on the undersides of grains. She turns a grain with her forelegs, feeds, and lets it fall. Much of the ridge under the paint is made of these rejected grains. Some still have food on them. The smallest springtails come up behind her and eat what she has missed.

The river brings in others. They arrive with the water and are left in pockets of air as it recedes. One of the clear worms has lived for some days in the damp under the washer. When it extends itself, the dark matter inside it lags a little, then catches up. The grass roots have grown around that pocket. There is a pale new root there tonight, with the worm wrapped loosely about it. Neither is quite still.

The animal under the paint goes out when the door opens. In the bright strip her shadow is difficult to tell from that of a flake of brick, until it stops and raises a foreleg. Sometimes a beetle’s shadow passes over hers and for a little while there is only the beetle’s. When she comes out on the other side she has sand on her back, as if she has been somewhere with a low ceiling. Once she came back without it, carrying a bead of water between her forelegs. She drank some herself and set the rest beside the eggs. It stayed round until a springtail touched it.

The eggs are not all at the same stage. Through the nearest shell you can see a row of small joints. In another there is only a cloudy patch, which has not changed since yesterday. She moves that one with the rest when the damp comes up. She takes hold of each egg at its blunt end and walks backward. The empty skin is light enough to move too, but she leaves it where it is.

Toward evening the drop on the pipe lengthens. Its reflection takes in the underside of the slab and the little blue roof, but not the animal beneath it, who is busy at the riverward edge. A grain there gives when she puts her weight on it. She turns it, eats a little, and lets it go. The paint settles at once. One of the springtails gets out; another waits in the space left by the husk.

When the drop falls it strikes the paint and runs off the low edge in two directions. There is room now for less than there was in the morning. The eggs fit. She backs in after them, folding her legs in order from the rear, and the last thing visible in the gap is one forefoot, feeling along the wet edge for a place to rest.
