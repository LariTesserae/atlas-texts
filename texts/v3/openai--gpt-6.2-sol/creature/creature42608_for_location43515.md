The lizard on the rain spot is an old female. In the tree you would know her by the two toes she lacks on her left forefoot. On the slope you would know her by the way she walks: six or seven quick steps and then a pause with her chin on the stone. The others rarely come far enough out to have to walk. They sit at the lip with their front feet in the sun and their hind feet somewhere they can get a purchase.

She has a good deal more color outside. There is a yellow seam along her mouth, and the loose skin at her elbows is the brown of an apple seed. She doesn’t lick the rain spot. She lies on it until the stone around her is dry, then gets up and leaves a wet likeness of herself, complete down to the notch in one heel. Sometimes she turns around and drinks from that.

The smallest lizards live above the entrance. Coming in, you put your hand just below them; going out, you put the back of your head against their floor. There is room in the shelf for an egg to hatch but not for a grown lizard to turn around. The young come out tail first and hang for a while by their front feet. The first thing most of them eat is a mite. They have to learn to let the red ones go by. A red mite tastes bad enough that a hatchling will put its tongue out and wipe it on the ceiling.

The old female was one of those hatchlings. She is not the mother of all the lizards, though in the few minutes after the water comes she is liable to have most of them walk over her. On the branches they keep a little distance from one another. At the roots they fit their heads together like the fingers of two hands. The barking starts before they jump because a lizard with its mouth in the water cannot bark, and the ones still on the wall keep calling long after there’s room for them below.

The crickets are crowded for a different reason. Only a few leaves are any good to them. On the others, a lizard can feel the rocking through the stem and come right up underneath. On the good leaves the stem is so thick that the leaf moves without telling the branch. These are the leaves that catch on your ears. The crickets there can go on singing while a lizard lies a thumb’s width away.

There is one place under the tree where no lizard waits for a drop. It is the dry side of the second peg. The young cut across it from time to time, and if you watch one do it you can see each foot hesitate in turn, as if the floor were a little farther down than it looked. The old female goes around. She goes around even with a cricket in her mouth, even with another lizard coming the opposite way. Where they pass, one has to climb onto the other’s back. The stone is shinier there than anywhere else in the hollow.

Late in the day she brings a cricket up to the lip and eats it facing out. She always leaves the long hind legs. A mite can get along one of those legs without ever touching the stone, and sometimes several make the trip before the wind takes it. Then she backs in until only her yellow mouth shows. For a while the whole slope below her is in shadow and the rock under her chin is still giving off the day’s heat. This is when the smallest lizards come down. They come over her head one at a time and stand on the stone she has already tested, lifting and setting down their feet.
