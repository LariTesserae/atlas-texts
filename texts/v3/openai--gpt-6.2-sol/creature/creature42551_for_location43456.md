At the bend in Number Two Lane, where you can hear the drops strike the leaves, there is a hook at the height of a child’s shoulder. This is where the people who work below the yard leave messages for one another. The messages go in a lidded tin. The lid is important: a drop lands on it about once a minute, an inch in from the hinge. Under the lid there is usually a pencil, a pump reading, and some news that would have been easier to tell in person if there were anywhere else to stand and talk.

Nell lives behind the switch room. Her door is on the dry side of a little vestibule, up two steps that used to hold a spare motor. She has a narrow bed, a kettle, and a window the width of her hand. The window looks into the switch room. When she turns out her light, the green indicator on the panel puts a small square on her ceiling. She can shut it out with a piece of cardboard, but she generally doesn’t.

She was hired to stay nights while a new pump was on order. That pump came and was found to be the wrong size, and by the time the right one came there was some question about whether the old ones ought to be replaced at all. Nell stayed. She has an allotment above ground, two stops down the railway, where she grows onions in a place so dry she carries water to it in milk bottles. There is a photograph of the allotment in her room. The onions are not much to look at. What she likes about the photograph is the length of their shadows.

In the daytime there are usually three people downstairs. Bassi does the electrical work. June tends the beds and is the person most likely to bring a handcart down. Nell does a bit of both. They have lunch at different times because there is only one dry chair. On Thursdays June’s grandson comes after school and sits on a turned-over bucket. He is the one who put up the message hook. He was shorter then. Nobody has moved it.

June can put her arm into a bed almost to the elbow and tell, by the direction the roots pull against her cuff, which end the water has been entering from. Beds that look identical under the lamps have different bottoms. In the second bed on Number One there is a ridge of old brick that the roots straddle. In the next there is a loose tile, and the roots have gone under it and lifted it by the thickness of a coin. June pushes it down each spring. Each spring it comes up a little less readily. She has stopped promising to get it out.

Bassi once left a mug on the ledge behind the bigger pump. By the time he went looking for it, a fern had grown a frond through the handle. He brought the others to see. Then he broke off the frond, washed the mug, and had his tea. There are things they leave alone down there, but none of them could have said why the mug should be one.

In the quiet bend, Nell’s hearing is not especially good. Bassi’s isn’t either. They used to come to the bend and say things twice. Now if it is a reading or something to do with the gate, they write it down. If it is something else, they sometimes go all the way up the stairs. Nell knows Bassi’s daughter is getting married because one morning he asked her to come up with him, and they stood beside the railings while he told her. It was raining. Neither had brought a coat.

Late at night, when the yard is empty, the water keeps coming in warm. Nell makes a last round. She puts a hand to each pump casing, checks the lower tanks, and stops at the tin even when she knows she emptied it herself on the previous round. Sometimes there is a message she has put there for the morning, and she takes it out and adds something. The other night she wrote *water down half an inch at six*, then went back and drew a line under *down*. Above her, a train that had been standing in the yard since before supper began to move. She waited for it to pass before she closed the lid, though there was no one there to hear it close.
