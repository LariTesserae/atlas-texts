The woman who sweeps the lower terminal lives on the far side of the valley, in the house with the roof patched in two colors of tin. From the upper road it looks as if she could walk to work in ten minutes. It takes her an hour. She comes down with her lunch in a net bag so that she can use both hands on the steep part, and in winter she puts the bag down first and lets it slide.

She has swept the terminal since there were cars on the line. She used to sweep around the boots of men waiting to go up, and they would lift their feet for her without interrupting what they were saying. Now she sweeps around a wet patch that moves. On Monday it may be under the third motor. By Thursday it will have crossed the aisle. It leaves nothing behind it, not even the rim of dirt a puddle leaves when it dries.

There are three of the things that make the patch. She knows this because one afternoon she shut the door on her way out and heard three separate weights come against it, low down, one after another. She opened it again. Nothing went past her that she could see, but out in the sun the iron step darkened in three places. One of the marks was no bigger than the heel of her hand.

In cold weather they spend most of the day under the old machine beds. They draw the water out of the bolt holes without disturbing the little collars of moss. Once, when she set a saucer under a leak in the roof, she saw the water in it rise at one edge and go over in a clear fold. She could see the pink flowers on the saucer through the fold, all pulled long. It took the smallest one most of a morning to get over the lip.

What they do with the freight line is harder to see. At the hour when the cable shadows reach the white rock, the wet places in the shed begin to shrink. The brass wheel gives a little knock, not always enough to move it. On days when a tooth goes over, there is a darkening in the sag of the old shadow, about the length of a car and not much wider than a hand. It travels uphill. The woman can watch it as far as the first alder, where the shadow passes off the rock and there is nothing pale enough behind it to show what happens next.

One of the men who greases the bearings remembers riding the line before it was tightened. Coming down empty, he says, you could put a cup on the floor for most of the journey, but there was a place near the bottom where you had to pick it up. He came once with his daughter to make the red mark. She stood back with the paint tin while he dabbed at the rock, then asked him why he had made it so short. He said that was all the brush would do. After he went down she put a little more paint on it. That is the only mark with a round end.

Last autumn, on the first afternoon cold enough for gloves, the woman heard the wheel knock twice and fail to turn. There was water under the third motor, though the rest of the floor had gone dry. She waited with her coat on. The strip of light went down the green wall and out. Then she took the broom off its nails and laid it across the doorway, with the bristles outside.

In the morning the broom lay where she had left it. The handle was dry except for a band about a foot from the end, where the wood had swollen enough to raise the grain. She sanded it smooth with a scrap from the workbench. That evening she put the broom down again, and has done so every evening since. She still hangs it up while she eats her lunch. There is no reason, in the middle of the day, to let the bristles get damp.
