There are things living in the banks that cannot be found by opening a bank. Open one and there is the pale wall, a little clear water, and on the cut face a row of chambers no larger than the pits in a thimble. By the time the cut has closed, whatever was there is farther along.

It goes by leaving a little water behind. A chamber narrows, but not quite to its usual width; its neighbor receives a little less than it should. On the next stroke the first chamber empties properly and the second holds back. This can go on through a great many chambers without changing the beat that a hand laid on the root can feel. There is a difference between a bank at work and a bank with something going along it, but it is a difference most easily noticed when the thing comes back.

They have routes. One goes round the same three banks for most of a year, except when a new layer of tissue closes the little cross-passage it uses. Then for several weeks it comes up to the closure and backs away. There is another way round, through the fine roots of a leaf, and others of its kind use it. This one does not. It waits for the wall to grow thin on the other side, and goes through when the chambers there begin to take water again. By then the crossing is a finger’s breadth from where it was. The next year it will be farther still. The creature may be very old; the crossing it keeps trying to make is no longer anywhere.

They collect where air is taken up. Under a pocket of air the chambers can remain partly empty for several strokes, and there is room for one of the creatures to double back without meeting itself. Sometimes two arrive and go round the pocket in opposite directions. Each yields a little at the place where they meet. After a long time a third short circuit begins between them and continues after both have gone. It is not yet much of a creature. It may go round three times and be lost. If it lasts until the air parts, it takes the smaller pocket down into a root where the chambers are close together. That little tearing sound is often the only part of its beginning that reaches the leaves.

Not everything in the lowland lives this way. In the hanging cups there is a nearly transparent growth that stays where it first took hold. Its underside fits the cup; its upper surface is flat, even when the leaf bends. It feeds at the join between water and leaf. An old one puts out a narrow rim, and the water going back into the leaf wears the rim away. The leaf supplies enough for it to put the rim out again. They are common under the places where the damp air descends, though you can pass beneath a hundred of them and see only water. A cup with nothing in it has a brighter edge.

A hand pressing into the mat makes a poor crossing. Some chambers flatten and some remain too full. The older travelers turn aside well before they reach it. A young one may spend a long while at the heel of the hand, trying one chamber after another, taking the next small yielding for a way through. If the hand stays, it will eventually find the route around the fingers. When the hand lifts, that route is easier than it has ever been. For a while the creature keeps going around it, making the long turn at each knuckle with nothing there to impede it.
