The little pale animals are waiting for their legs to harden. The ones nearest the light have gone the color of old teeth at the joints; higher up, where they are still white all over, you can see the dark of a leaf through the flat of a thigh. They have got themselves into positions they will be able to get out of later. A foot set across a vein is a foot that can be lifted without catching. There are no spare footholds on some of the smaller leaves.

Under the root is their mother, though she has never had their shape. She fits the hollow so exactly that if she were to come out, the first thing you would notice would be how much of the root was missing. The rings you can see are the only parts of her that grow. Once in a long while the last ring opens along a fine seam and a pale animal backs out of it. For a little while it has no notion of where to stop. It will put one soft foot down on another animal's back and leave it there until the animal underneath has hardened enough to object.

The clatter comes from the clear places because that is where the others live. There are two of them between the three thickets. They have no color of their own, but sometimes, from the stool, you can see a furrow in the salt shorten at both ends without anything passing over it. One of them has turned. They spend their lives passing grains back and forth, a great many at a time, and the noise is the grains striking one another. Nothing falls. If you hold a hand out in the gap you feel nothing until you take it away, and then there may be a grain in the crease of a knuckle, warm enough to feel after you have brushed it off.

The stool was made by someone who used to come down with sore feet. They stayed long enough for the warmth to reach the bones, and then went back up to work. At first they brought a saucer for the grains they shook out of their socks. Later they made the dish. No one comes to empty it. Every so often one of the pale animals is ready to cross the floor, and on its way it climbs the dish and eats a grain. They have to stand on the rim to do it. The grain there now was brought up by an animal that found, on getting it into the light, that it was a piece of mica.

The folded leaf is the first thing any of the newly hardened animals will walk toward. It isn't food yet, or shelter. What it has is a long bare stalk with nothing on it to tell a foot where to go. By the time an animal reaches the fold it has learned to lift each of its feet without having to lift the one beside it. It goes in at the tip and works its way down. From the stool you can see the leaf move a little as the animal turns inside. Then, all at once, the copper sound, and a strip of red where there was none before. For the rest of that visit you keep looking at the empty bit of air just above it, where the tip used to be.
