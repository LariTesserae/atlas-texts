There are people in the green patch. You may have been looking at it for most of the afternoon before one of them stands up.

Their houses are under the trees. Where a trunk crosses a hollow, they wall up the spaces beneath it and lay split wood over the branches for a roof. From out on the pavement you can see the leaves tossing and, occasionally, a pale square of cloth drawn in through a door. Inside, there is room to sit upright. The tallest person in one household is usually the last to get up in the morning, not from any indulgence but because everyone else has to get out of the way first.

They grow beans beneath the ferns and a red, oniony thing in pockets of black soil. They are particular about where they put a foot in a hollow. A fern bent back over a path may be keeping the sun off somebody’s supper. The pale snails are a nuisance in the bean beds, and children carry them away in handfuls to the far end of the grove. The snails come back. Nobody has worked out whether they are the same snails.

Some of the roofless shelters were built by people whose names are still remembered. Others have always been roofless, as far as anyone knows. The people here stop at them even when they are in no need of rest. They clear the cups of dead leaves. They turn a slab smooth side up if it has fallen. In the shelter with water in it, the cup is set at just the right distance from the wall to catch a drip off the hanging root. In a good week a person can get two swallows there. If there is only one swallow, they generally leave it.

They do not raise their voices on the pavement. To get someone’s attention they throw the shadow of a hand across the stone in front of them, if the sun is out, or touch the back of a sleeve. When they come indoors they go on touching one another for a little while, unnecessarily. Talk begins with the small news: a bean in flower, a ladle gone missing. Later somebody will say that a person has died downriver, or that they themselves are thinking of going down and not coming back. There is no difficulty hearing that part. The hard thing is to know when the other person has finished.

The brown birds nest below the rim. Near the end of the dry season they bring up fish from the river, and scales turn up in hollows miles from the edge. A child can know the river first by these scraps: a scale that dries clear as a fingernail, a little comb of bones with one shred of meat still on it. The first time such a child goes down, the thing that surprises them is not the water but the mud. They have never walked on ground that keeps the shape of a foot.

In the evenings there is work to do at the pools. The people who live nearest the rim lay cloths low over the water, weighted at the corners. Without a cloth a shallow pool can go away in an hour. Even with one, the smaller drops get out. By the time the work is done, water beads in their hair and on the undersides of their chins. They come home wet in places rain would not have reached.

There are nights when a lane of rain passes over the same house again and again. The family moves its bedding by degrees until everyone is lying in the one dry corner, with the youngest against the wall. Under the floor, in a joint too narrow to get a finger into, a tree root takes up the water. The tree was there when the oldest person in the house was born. She remembers when that root was thin enough to pull aside to retrieve a dropped spoon. The spoon she uses now has a short handle. She could not tell you when she stopped expecting to get the other one back.
