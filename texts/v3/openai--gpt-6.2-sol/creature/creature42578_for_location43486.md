The gray animal has young. You see one first as an extra forefoot under its chest, set down a little too late. The adult stops. A small head comes into view in the angle between its legs, though there is no room there for the rest of the youngster. Then the adult shifts its weight and the young one comes out entire, gray down to the pads of its feet, with a strip of clear skin along its side where the flap will grow.

There are three of them. They follow the adult through the canes, taking care to put their feet where it puts its feet. This is not always where a foot would seem to go. One lifts a paw over a stem and sets it down in a patch of sunlight that stood, a moment before, well beyond the stem. Another keeps trying to go around. Each time it does, it ends up on the same side of the same leaf. It sits down and makes a noise so slight you might take it for a tooth touching a tooth. The adult puts its head back through the leaf fork. The youngster gets up and tries again.

They eat sitting very straight, with a blade held crosswise in their forefeet. The adult works a single plate loose from the blade and draws it into its mouth. It leaves all the other plates in place. Pale green moves down the leaf to the spot where the plate was, and stops there. The young ones bite right across, making a great dry business of it. The adult takes the spoiled pieces from them and packs them into the hollow of a jointed cane. It has done this before. Looking into the hollow from above, you can see the pieces stacked below the cane, on a stretch of mineral where the stalk itself casts no shadow.

There is a small white creature at work on the stack. It is too flat to see from the side. It comes into view as it goes over the end of a fragment and turns to work on the underside. Behind it the fragment is neither eaten nor discolored, but the copper face has lost its shine. The white creature goes on until you cannot distinguish it from the crust. Then another fragment tips up at one end.

The gray animals spend much of their time apart without ever being very far from one another. The adult can lie under cover while a youngster stands out in the bare, and turn an ear toward it through the fork of a cane. When two of the young discover the same broad leaf, they play at coming through its fork. They see each other long before they meet. One flattens itself and waits, hindquarters twitching, while the other comes nearer and nearer along what looks like the same four inches of ground. They collide at last and roll out on the crust in a place neither was looking at.

There is something that hunts them. You know it first by the adult’s stillness. It lowers its chin until the skin under its jaw touches the mineral. The young go flat. On the far side of the opening, a brown head rises no higher than the nearest ridge. Its face is broad and its eyes are set well out to either side. It looks into one gap after another without bringing its body out of the growth. Once it puts a forefoot between two narrow leaves, and on your side of the opening the gray adult lifts its hind foot out of reach.

The brown animal stays there a long while. Its foot does not stir. Eventually a twig ticks well behind you, and it takes its foot back and goes toward the sound. You see its ribs pass through a strip of light. It is smaller than the head led you to expect, and thin. When it has gone, the adult begins on a blade it dropped, starting at the torn end.

The raised flaps are for resting. You can see this when the young settle down, each with its bare strip of skin turned toward the light. The adult lies beside them and opens its flap all the way. Beneath it the skin is lined with little moving points of brightness. As the animal’s breathing slows, the points draw together into a narrow band. One youngster noses under the flap. The adult lets it stay there until it begins to wriggle, then folds the flap shut and moves an inch away. The youngster, without opening its eyes, moves the same inch.

On your way out you stoop to look at the chip once more. A third print has appeared beside the two you saw before. It is much smaller. You turn the chip over in your palm, thinking to set it down with the bright face uppermost, and the little patch goes dark. There is a scrambling among the leaves some distance off. You turn it back.

For a time there is nothing in the patch but the three prints. Then a small forefoot comes down beside the last of them. The youngster leans forward until its nose is almost at the edge of the chip. It sniffs twice at the warmth coming through. Behind it you can see the flank of the resting adult, rising and falling, and one of the other young asleep with a piece of leaf still caught between its toes.
