A spider lives under the seat. The slab does not quite meet its uphill support, and in the space between them she has laid down a white pad about the size of a thumbnail. It is the only white thing in the hollow that bends when the wind gets at it.

She is brown, with two pale marks at the back of her body which do not match. When the sun reaches the slab she comes out onto one of the wedges and stands in a strike mark, her forelegs on either side of the groove. From there she can reach the underside of the stone without turning. Once a loose flake tapped three times in the wind and she ran out at each tap, a little less far each time. At the fourth she stayed where she was.

There are days when nothing crosses the floor that she could eat. Then there will be a morning with flies all over the wall. They come out of the cloud or come up ahead of it; by the time there is light enough to see them, they are standing on the white faces of the stones, working one foot over another. Some are gone before the first drop falls. Others walk down into the cracks and stay there until the rock warms. The spider takes them on the way out. She leaves the wings attached, and in the back of her shelter there is a little heap of them at angles to one another, like the stones on the floor.

A gray bird visits in unsettled weather. It lands on the lip, drops out of sight, and turns up a moment later beside the cup. It has a thin bill and a dark line through the eye. It drinks from the part of the ring nearest the hard knot, where a person trying to scoop up the water would find there was hardly any. Sometimes it finds a fly. It has never sat on the seat. For the bird the whole hollow is a place to stand out of the wind, and there are better perches than the slab.

Under the floor, in the wet seams, there are springtails so small that a dozen could feed on the darkened track of a single grain. They graze on a film that leaves no green on the stone. When the water withdraws they follow it, down past the chips into gaps too close for the spider to enter. Some go down farther in an afternoon than they will come up in a week. The drops do not always open the same ways for them. A grain settles across a passage; a bubble holds in a neck of water until evening. There are small pockets that stay wet for days after the cup has frozen.

No one has used the seat for years. The spider's shelter is on the uphill side, but on the other side there is room for a hand, and if someone sat down and put a hand there in the afternoon they would find the slab warm underneath. It keeps its warmth well after the sun has gone off it. The spider is usually still out then, on the wedge, with her feet in the old cut.
