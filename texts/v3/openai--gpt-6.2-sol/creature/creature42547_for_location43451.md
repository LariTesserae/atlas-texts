The first thing to come to the footprint is a mouth. It comes up through the inlet sideways, working one lip and then the other past a grain of clay. Behind it is a length of clear flesh no thicker than the water it displaces. You can follow it back to the joint by the little things going through it: a grain of white salt, a bubble drawn out to the shape of a seed. When it finds the heel of the print, it fastens there and begins to drink.

There are others at the thumbprints. A whorl with a tenant in it dries from the middle outward, leaving a bright ring of water in the outermost groove. If you put a finger in that ring, the tenant lets go. It does not go far. In a little while you feel it taking hold under your fingernail, where the warmth is steadier. It seems to like the salt there. After you have walked on, a white fan grows in the next print you leave.

These are the young of something that cannot be seen entire. The dark line that rises across the plain is one of its movements, though not a movement of the whole animal. A portion of it, drawing water, has made room for another portion to fill. Close by, this lifts a lip of clay. Far off, under the weight of a basin, it may lift half an inch of country. The mouths feed whenever the water comes in and close whenever it is taken away. In a good place a mouth can go on doing this long after the flesh that joined it to the rest has dried and cracked. In a bad place the flesh stays wet and the mouth never opens. These shut mouths turn opaque and have the dull white of a tooth in the clay.

At the low banks, where the rain hangs in the air, there are animals that stand on it. Their feet are fine black hooks. A foot takes a drop by its skin and gives a little when the drop gives. You might see one of them hanging with its belly nearly on the paving, picking up a foot and waiting, as if the placing of that foot required a great deal of thought. Then another drop drifts within reach and up it goes. They have flat heads and broad, bare backs. Mouths settle on those backs and drink the rain off them. When a walker grows too heavy with passengers it sinks, a drop at a time, until one foot touches the bank. It stays there until it can climb again. The bank is pitted where generations of them have waited with one foot down.

A laid tile is not immediately abandoned by the thing that laid it. For a while there is a pressure beneath it, shifting from corner to corner. This is when the little mouths withdraw from the joints. Up on the tile the water may not stir at all. Underneath, something is feeling for a place to let go.

Once, at a seam wide enough to admit a wrist, you can see one of the builders doing this. What you see first is the paddle, held flat against the underside of the clay. Then there is the thick pad in which the handle has sunk. It takes the weight off the paddle a little at a time, drawing itself away at one end and bearing down at the other. It has no fingers on this side. When it turns, you see the inner face of it, soft and deeply ridged. The ridges are full of water. Several mouths are feeding there, as intently as they fed in the print of your heel.

The builder rests with its pad turned up until they finish. Then it pulls the paddle free. A little chip comes down through the seam and catches on the pad, and the builder carries it off. Later, well ahead of you, there is a small sound on the paving.

By then the mouth in your footprint has taken in enough water to show what it was doing there. The heel is going shallow. A little ridge pushes out from the inlet, and the water slips around its ends, leaving a dry place in the middle. On that dry place lies a black hook, still folded against the clear flesh that grew it. Another comes free beside it. The animal lifts one and holds it out over the water. The next pulse comes in from so far away that there is time to watch the hook tremble before a drop stands up to meet it.
