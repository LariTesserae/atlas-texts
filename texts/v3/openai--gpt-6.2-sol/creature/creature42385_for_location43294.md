The oldest inhabitant is the little evergreen, though its oldest part is not the trunk you can see. One of the four turns is a branch that took root where it lay against the wall. The wood below that turn has been dead for years. It still carries the weight of the living part, and on its underside there is a strip of bark loose enough for a moth to put its head under. The moths go in headfirst and come out backward, leaving a little of their gray on the wood.

The snails are easiest to find when the basin is empty. Each has a place on the underside of a ledge, sealed to it by a film that looks like the skin on boiled milk. The places are not quite interchangeable. A snail that has grown too broad for its recess will spend two or three wet nights trying it before settling somewhere else. There is one with a chipped lip that has taken to sleeping in the old hinge hole. When it draws in, the shell fits so close against the root that it looks like a knot in the wood.

The white-legged creature is a springtail. There are dozens of them behind the loosened scale of stone, among the threads of a fungus that never puts up a cap. The one that comes out into the light is not always the same one. Seen from the sill, they are distinguishable only by what they do at the edge of the patch: most turn, a few back away, and occasionally one vanishes so abruptly that you go on looking for it in the direction it was walking.

A spider lives nearest the street. It has no proper web, only a few lines from the underside of the sill into a crack. The line nearest the opening gets broken by sleeves. The spider always replaces it a little farther in, until a wet winter drives it out again. It can go a long time without catching anything. The smallest moths can walk between its lines, and the snails go through them. Once a snail carried a line all the way down to the basin and the spider followed as far as the rim. It waited there with its front feet on the silk until the snail went under a leaf. Then it cut the line and went home.

At the bottom of the wall, a fern has come up in a shell that lies mouth upward. It is too small yet to make a frond like the ones above it. Its only leaf is divided in two, and even that division is shallow. The shell is wedged in the moss with its broken side toward the basin. When the water rises, that is where it gets in.

The old visitor sees the leaf on the second or third visit. At first they take it for the green fork they remember, and then they see the large fern behind it, reaching around the stone. They put a finger through the opening to show someone the difference. The finger makes a shadow over both plants. They pull it back and wait for the other person’s eyes to adjust.
