The people who live under the nursery know when the children have been given the long wooden blocks. The blocks come down flat, one at a time, as if somebody upstairs were laying a very slow floor. The machinery never does that. On Sundays the rooms are quiet enough for the people in them to hear their own refrigerators.

In the end kitchen lives a woman named Ada who alters clothes. She has a fitting platform made out of a drawer turned upside down, and a long mirror she keeps tilted forward because the wall behind it sweats. A surprising number of people bring her only one trouser leg to mend. It is always the leg on the downhill side of the bicycle. She asks them to bring the whole garment next time. They promise. They are the same people who, when she goes out in her good shoes, call to her from halfway up the stairs to tell her which gutter has overflowed.

Her grandson Len has never seen the valley from the upper tram stop. He’s been there, but both times the view was gone, and he has formed the opinion that there’s nothing much to see. He knows the river from underneath the yellow bridge, where an older girl taught him to fish. They caught nothing that day. What he remembers is her taking off his right glove and holding the back of his hand against the line so he could feel the rubbish coming downstream: the little taps, then the slow pull of something that wouldn’t come through the arch. She has since gone away to learn dentistry. When she comes home she is appalled to find him taller than she is. He lets her be appalled all through supper before he shows her the boots.

There are people here who work all day in the dry. The payroll office has carpet, and the woman who locks it up at night goes down seven flights to an allotment where the dirt comes over the tops of her shoes. She grows flowers nobody can eat. For a while there was a dispute about whether these belonged on a deck built for food production. This ended when the man most exercised about it needed flowers in February. He came himself, in the rain, with a blue bowl to put them in. She still asks after the bowl. It was his wife’s, and he has started using it for his breakfast.

In the spaces behind the houses live things that do not need much room. The fernery has a pale slug that can get from one bench to another along the underside of a watering pipe. In the inspection cupboards, spiders put their webs across the backs of the doors, where an open door will do them no harm. A caretaker once found a newt in the laundry, flat against the glass of an idle machine. She carried it up to the wet meadow in a soap dish. Two mornings later there was a newt in the laundry. She couldn’t swear it was the same one. She did, however, take it farther the second time.

In the evenings, when the shifts overlap, there are people eating breakfast on either side of a wall from people washing up after supper. You can buy a hot egg from the kiosk under the bridge until the last tram, though the proprietor will have put the salt away and you’ll have to ask. At the laundry, a man who has finished work sits with a bag of somebody else’s washing between his feet. He is waiting for a woman who works in the kitchens. They’ve been walking home together for three weeks, ever since the lower passage closed. It opened again on Monday. Each is waiting for the other to mention it.

By then Ada has folded the mirror in against the wall. The nursery is empty. Len comes in with a paper bag tucked under his coat and puts it on the table. There are two eggs in it, warm through the paper, and at the bottom a twist of salt. He goes to take his boots off. Before he’s back she’s rolled both eggs under her palm, cracking the shells all over the way he likes, so they’ll come off in one piece.
