The woman who rolls the cart out of the freight exchange is bringing supper to the pump crew. She sets the brake with her heel and waits. The men and women come up one at a time because there must always be somebody below. Their sleeves are wet to different heights. They eat standing at the cart, with their bowls on the upper shelf and their gloves on the lower one. By the time the last man comes up, the woman has put his bowl back in the pot to keep it warm. He fishes it out with the long tongs she uses for the eggs.

She lives above the exchange in what used to be the drivers’ dormitory. There are seven households along the corridor, though only six doors: the sisters in the end room put up a partition when one of them had a baby, and now you have to go through the other sister’s kitchen to get to the baby. The old room numbers are painted on the fanlights. At night the numbers show on the corridor floor, each in a different color according to what somebody has hung over the glass.

At the seaward end of the corridor a little boy lies on his stomach tallying the fish trucks by the headlights they send across the ceiling. He can tell the empty ones from the full ones by the rattle of the tailgates. Sometimes he gets up and checks, but there is hardly any need. The drivers have been taking the same turn since before he was born. He has never seen most of their faces.

Below him, in the joints of the apron, a crab is backing out of its shell. The split has opened across the rear of its body. It draws the legs out slowly, doubling each at the joint to get it through, and when it is free it scarcely moves. The old shell keeps its stance. In the morning a gull will turn it over and find the underside as perfectly made as the top and nothing in either. The crab itself will still be down there, pale enough that you could see it in the dark if you knew where to put your eye.

In a flooded cable box two lanes away, there is an eel. It came in when it was thin enough to pass through a broken conduit and spent a summer eating whatever washed down to it. The electricians found it the following winter, when they lifted the lid. One of them offered to fetch a bucket, but by the time he said it the eel had gone down among the cables. They shut the lid. Now, before they lower a hand into the box, they tap twice on the rim with a wrench. The eel does not always move. On cold mornings there it is, laid over the bundled cables like another length of the same black rubber, except for the small movement at its throat.

The woman brings the cart back in just before midnight. She rinses the bowls at a sink big enough to have washed a truck axle in, then takes the lift upstairs. It was built for loaded pallets and there is room in it for the whole dormitory to ride at once. Tonight it carries only her and, in a covered bowl, what is left of the rice.

The boy has lost count. He has gone to sleep on the corridor tiles, where the floor is coolest. She steps over him to put the rice away, then comes back and sits beside him to unlace his shoes. Under the floor the pump starts up again. A spoon somebody left on a saucer begins to tick against the china. She reaches up to the windowsill and moves it, without turning on the light.
