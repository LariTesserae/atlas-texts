The first person you see is sitting on one of the thresholds the wall has just uncovered. You take her knees for two chips in the stone. Then she rubs one with the heel of her hand.

She is about the size of a child, though the skin at her elbows hangs in the fine folds of old age. Her back is broad and her chest is scarcely deeper than the width of two fingers. Sitting sideways, she leaves room for you to pass. For a while she looks at your shoes. Then she puts her bare feet flat on the pavement, as if to show you that hers have soles too.

There are others behind the doors. They come out as the space in front of them widens, first turning their faces into it, then bringing out a shoulder and a hip. One has been asleep. The lines of the floor are still pressed into his cheek. Two younger ones stoop to look down the street, each with a hand on the other's back. They can see the plain at both ends. One of them has never seen it before. He keeps changing ends, wanting to know whether the pale strip at one end will still be there when he turns to look at the other.

They have been living in the rooms that are not quite rooms yet. In a house that looks empty, there may be a whole family in the thickness behind a cupboard. The floor comes out from beneath the wall a little at a time, and they sleep with their feet tucked up until there is somewhere to put them. They like the new cold. The faces of the children who come out last are flushed dark at the temples.

They eat the clear ribs of the plants, but only the short ones that grow back toward the wall. To get at them they lie on their sides and work a finger in behind the root. This is one of the few things the adults do less well than the children. A child will chew off the soft end and put a hard piece into an old person's palm without either of them interrupting what they are doing. They leave the long ribs that span the alleys. When a building draws away from its neighbor, the little animals go on using those bridges until the last root gives.

The people can make themselves heard across a house by tapping the floor and waiting. A good floor gives a tap back in much the same place. An old floor may give it back in another room entirely. The woman on the threshold listens to the ticks that follow your steps and moves her hand twice, not quite tapping. Then she asks you to walk as far as the gate and stop. You do. She goes to a door halfway down the street and puts her ear to the sill.

Someone inside has been tapping since the day before. Nobody could tell from which side.

When the door opens there is an old man behind it, standing in a room so shallow that he has to keep his head turned. The woman goes up to him and puts her forehead against his. They stay like that for some time. The others go on looking out at the plain. One of the young ones crouches and picks at a white mark on his ankle.

By morning there will be room for the old man to lie down. By the next evening, if the street keeps widening, he and the woman will have to step off their thresholds to touch. They have lived through this before. At the south edge there are pairs of houses whose occupants can see each other only by lying flat and looking under the partitions. When somebody moves north to take up a colder room, it can be a long time before the person across from them finds out.

For now the woman sits down again. The old man gets one foot out and sets it beside hers. His other foot is still inside the door. Neither of them tries to bring it through.
