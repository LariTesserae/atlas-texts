The animal beneath the bridge is the only one in the room with a soft back. The weave has sunk into it, and through the weave you can see the flesh come up in little squares. When the animal at the wet end steps onto the bridge, the squares rise one after the other, just ahead of its feet. It goes across without getting its feet wet. It is carrying a scrap of frond much wider than its head.

The animal underneath eats the part that hangs over.

On the ceiling, the frond with its tip in the drop has a tenant. At first it looks as if the frond has put out a second, blacker root. Then the root unhooks a leg and reaches out along the green. It goes no farther than the margin of the drop. It wets the leg to the first joint, draws it back, and passes it between two small plates under its mouth. After a while it does this again. On the far side of the same drop there is a smaller animal, facing the other way, washing a foot. Neither can see the other. When one pulls too sharply at the water, the other has to spread its legs.

There is an old animal in the outcrop of backs. You can find it by a pale spot where its chin has worn through the color on the shell beneath it. Nothing else about it is conspicuously old. Its legs fold away as neatly as the others’, and when it eats, it eats quickly. It is the one that gets up. First it takes its chin out of the hollow; then, by degrees, it draws its back out from under the chin above. The two exposed places are so bright that several animals turn their heads. The old one walks to the edge of the outcrop, picks up a little powder, and returns. It has worn a path to the powder no deeper than the track a dry brush leaves on a table. In all that noise, the animal whose chin it carries knows when to lift its head.

The transparent animal on the floor is not quite colorless. There is a brown fleck inside it that goes slowly around its body, taking most of a day to pass the six tucked under its rim. At each one the fleck stops. For a little while the animal underneath has a roof that casts a shadow. One of them always unfolds a leg in that interval. It is a long leg, with a joint still doubled from having grown in too small a space. When the fleck moves on, the leg folds up again, though each time there is a little more of it to get back under.

At the shelf there are no animals. There are several facing it, close enough for the light around the grain to show through the thinner parts of their shells. The nearest is missing the very end of an antenna. Now and then it reaches forward with the good one, not toward the grain but into the little space below it. It has room to do this without touching anything. The antenna comes back clean.

The animal at the uphill end is a young one. The flange over its eyes is still narrow, and when it wants to look at its foot it has to turn almost on its side. It does this twice. The second time, it lifts the foot a little way out of the new hollow. There is a pinch of powder in the heel of it. The animal works that powder loose with another foot and eats it. Then it sets the foot down again, a little farther in. On the wall behind it are four shallow places, just the size of its feet when they were smaller. In the smallest, which it can no longer get even a toe into, something with a white head has tucked itself in for the day.
