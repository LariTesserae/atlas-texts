The animals that look like chips in the paint are the mothers. Seen from beneath, one of them is scarcely wider than the seam she came through. It is the spread of the wings that makes her look substantial. Each wing is cream at the root and clear at the edge, with a brown nick that looks like dirt caught under paint. The two nicks are never quite opposite. On the wall this is the easiest way to tell one mother from another.

She has come back from the spoil with her abdomen full. She walks the underside of the conveyor casing, stopping at the heads of the rivets, which hold their warmth longer than the sheet around them. At the right seam she backs in and lays a row of eggs. They adhere by their narrow ends. For a little while she stands over them with her wings folded so tightly that from the side she looks like another row of eggs. Then she goes out. The bodies being taken apart below are often hers.

The young are nearly transparent when they first come down. What gives a seam its color is the food in them, a short dark thread that lengthens as they feed. They can live for days where two faces of stone are close enough to touch their backs and bellies at once. A hunter will wait at the mouth of such a seam, with its front legs laid flat against the rock. It may catch the first young one out and the second. While it is feeding on the second, a hundred go past.

The long pale animals are not hunters. They eat what is left on a wing after everything that can be pulled off it has been pulled off. One will stand over a patch of moving ground until a discarded wing comes by, then put a foot on it. The little scavengers go on tugging. Under that foot the wing slowly turns, as a scrap of paper turns under a finger. When the long animal has finished, there is still a wing, but it has lost the dusty surface that gave it color. The clear ones work down into the bowls and lie there among the enamel chips.

What looks like a bolt in the noon bowl is the oldest of the pale ones. It is wider than the others, and its legs fit into grooves beneath its body. For most of the day there is no telling it from a piece of worn iron. When the light reaches it, the small animals withdraw, but it stays. At the very edge of the light it draws one leg out of its groove. There is a line of young underneath, standing so close that their legs cross. It lets them out on the shaded side, a few at a time. They walk stiffly at first, putting their feet down wherever there is room. Their parent settles back into the bowl. By the time the light has gone, there are young feeding all around it, and nowhere to see what it was that opened.
