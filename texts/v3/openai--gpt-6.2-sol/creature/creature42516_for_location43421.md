The six-footed animals have a different skin on each pair of soles. The forefeet are soft enough to close over a leaf without bruising it. The middle feet are hard and black, worn to a shine by the warm stone. On the hind feet the skin comes off in translucent cups. You find these cups all along the lips of the shelves, some no bigger than the bowl of a pin. In a good year a female will raise three young, and for some weeks after they leave her there are little cups inside the large ones.

They eat the pale growth under the mats. To get at it they go in backward, feeling with their tails for a stem that has not yet toughened. A feeding animal looks like an animal being swallowed: first the hindquarters disappear, then the middle feet, and all that is left is a blunt head resting on the leaves. Its eyes close. Farther in, out of sight, its tail is doing work as delicate as a tongue.

The warm crossings are where they sleep, but they do not all sleep at once. An animal coming off the stone touches its nose to the flank of the next one in line. In this way a place passes from one body to another through the long dark. A youngster sometimes forgets to get up, and the one behind it will wait with its nose pressed into the fur until there is a hollow there.

The gliders spend most of their lives as grubs. You can see their roofs if you lift the edge of a mat: thin white chambers with roots growing through them, each roof kept clear of dirt from below. They have a small black jaw for this. When they come out, the jaw stays behind, hooked into the roof, and the opening of a hundred wings leaves a hundred black hooks in the ground.

A glider does not eat after it opens. What looks like a broad gray wing from above is two layers of skin with a scattering of eggs between them. On the far shelf it works the edges of the wing into the leaves and lets the rest of its body dry. On a shelf where they have landed year after year, the old wings lie in overlapping patches. A very young fern can grow its first curl between the two skins of a wing and never touch the open air.

The hunters get some of them before that. A hunter has no wings of its own. It goes down on a thread that pays out from the underside of its body, and if it catches a glider it comes back up with the glider folded lengthwise in its mouth. The climb is slow. At the rim it has to reach over its head for the stone with both forelimbs, and for a moment its belly and the soft place under its jaw are exposed. The six-footed animals, which it could eat and sometimes does, sit quite still while it makes this last effort. Then they go about their feeding. The hunter leaves the wings wherever it finishes its meal. More than one shelf has been settled this way.

There is also something alive in the seams. The black mineral is black whether it is there or not; only on a fresh break can you see the narrow clear margin it has laid down. The living part is thinner than that margin. It advances into the wet rock and leaves behind it a mineral wall, working around a dry patch by either side and joining again beyond it. In a piece small enough to lift, the wall looks like a set of faint tide marks. In the mountain it runs from one seam to the next, sometimes across a break so old that the rock on one side has no name in common with the rock on the other.

Where the living margin reaches the underside of a shelf, its discharges make the leaves turn. For the first few breaths after the silver has passed, the little damp rooms under the mat are all open to the drop. Most nights there is nothing out there for them. But when vapor comes over a saddle, it can get in among the stems before the leaves settle, and the mat closes over it with almost none left on top.

No animal down there has seen a cloud. The six-footed ones know water chiefly as a thing that must be lifted. A mother whose young are still in their cups combs the underside of the leaves with the fur of her throat, then lies down among them. They pull at the darkened fur with their forefeet. By the time they are large enough to follow her to the warm stone, they have learned to do it for themselves. In the morning, where the fern is growing toward its little piece of ice, there may be a track of dry leaves leading away from it, each one wiped clean on the side you cannot see.
