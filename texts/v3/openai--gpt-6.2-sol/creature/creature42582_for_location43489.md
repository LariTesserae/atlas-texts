The birds come down for the water in the hollows. They land on the stone between the animals and lean over to drink, though it would be easier to land on a back. The old backs are rubbed smooth around the hollows. In a dry year the birds wear a little farther into the skin, trying to get their bills under the rim of the ice.

There is something in the water besides the birds’ reflections. A white animal, no longer than a barley grain, goes around the edge of each pool with its mouth against the skin. It feeds on what collects at the waterline. When a bird drinks, the little animal folds itself into a crease and waits out the sudden lowering of the water. Sometimes there are two in a hollow. They go in opposite directions, and when they meet, one walks under the other without either taking its mouth off the skin.

By morning the pools are solid. The white animals have gone down between the shoulders, into a pocket that stays soft even when the rest of the back is hard with cold. You can see them in there if you get close enough: small mouthparts still going, occasionally catching on one another. The animal underneath gives no sign of feeling them. It is difficult to tell when one of these broad, flat animals feels anything. Even when it gets up, it raises itself by degrees, with long rests at heights that would seem to cost more effort than standing.

In its first summer it has legs long enough to get tangled in the groves. It goes about with old leaves stuck to its knees. There is no hollow in its back then, only a strip of tender skin that the walking flies find as soon as it stops. Whatever relief it gets from lying down among the others must be considerable. A young one will try a seam too narrow for it again and again, putting in a foot, withdrawing it, and putting in the same foot a little higher up. The adults do not make room. The young one has to wait for a departure, or for a death to work its way to the edge.

A death can take most of a season to show. The back still holds water. Birds continue to drink there. The difference begins underneath, where the feet lose their purchase and the weight settles onto the turf. Small grazers come in along the underside and eat the leaves the animal has been lying over. They leave the roots. By the time its neighbors have moved far enough apart to expose it, the dead animal can be lifted from a bed of bare woody stems, each bent flat in the direction it grew when there was light.

The little white animals leave before that. On a mild evening they come out of the shoulder pocket and stand at the lip of the hollow. They have grown a pair of dark hooks at the ends of their front legs. When the bird leans to drink, they catch in the short feathers under its bill. Most are rubbed off on the first branch the bird uses to wipe its face. Some crawl down the branch and go on feeding there, at the line left by a bead of mist.

A few reach another back. The bird dips its bill and they let go. In a hollow that has never held anything but water, they begin to make the round, passing over the place where the shoulder pocket will open. For the first few nights there is nowhere to go when the pool freezes. They stay at the rim. At noon, when the bird comes back, the ice is still white in the middle, but all around the edge there is a thin, clear ring where their mouths have been.
