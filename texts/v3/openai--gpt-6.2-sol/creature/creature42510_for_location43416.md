The track is made by an animal with four feet. You can see the prints where it has had to climb over a sunken stem, though in the open silt its belly wipes them out. The hind feet have three toes apiece. The front feet are so small that the marks they leave could be the pricks of a sedge seed.

It is about as long as the first two joints of your finger. Underwater it is the color of the silt. Out of water the skin darkens as it dries, starting at the shoulders, and you can see a row of brown spots along its back. It has eyes, but when it comes out from under the roots it goes along with one side of its head against the bank. If a root ends, the animal stops and puts out a foot before it moves its head. You can watch it take several minutes to get around the head of an old bolt.

There are five or six of them in the marsh. In the warmest pool they will pass one another without touching. In the colder water they lie together in a thumb-sized hollow with their heads facing out. They eat the little white worms that gather in the husks of drowned seeds. A husk will sometimes move across the bottom in starts, although there is no current there. Each time it stops, a little more of the husk is gone.

The woman who greases the vise used to think there was only one animal. She would see a track in the morning and another in the afternoon and suppose it had gone somewhere while she was at work. Then one spring she lifted the plank and found six of them underneath, each in the shallow print of a knot in the wood. She had already carried the drill up from below. She went back down for a second plank and laid it higher up, leaving the old one in the water until it softened enough to come apart in her hands.

She spends four nights at a time in a room down past the warm pipe. From her bed she cannot hear the overflow. She can hear water going through the wall behind the bed, and once or twice a night a dry click from the meter by the door. When she comes up in the morning she puts her kettle on the workbench and looks into the shallows while it cools enough to pour. There is usually nothing to see. The tracks show best in the afternoon, when the light gets under the leaves.

Once a year the machine is stopped. The water clears first around the pipes, where the fine grains that have been stirring in place all year settle onto the collars of the roots. By the second day the animals have left the pools. They climb behind the curtain of round leaves and creep a little way into the highest seep, where the water is warm whether the machine runs or not. Their young are there already. They hang head down in the thin water, with their feet spread against the rock. The young have dark eyes and transparent bellies. Now and then one lets go, drops the length of its body, and catches hold again.

The machine starts slowly. The woman puts her hand on the workbench until the vise begins to tremble. Behind the leaves the young stay where they are. One of the old ones comes down, feeling its way along the wet stems, and pauses where a leaf rests on the surface of the pool. It puts a foot into the water, then lifts it and waits a little longer.
