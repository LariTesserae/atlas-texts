One of the inhabitants sleeps in the narrow house at the end of the avenue. It is taller than the doorway when it stands, though it seldom stands. It goes about on its knuckles and the outer edges of its feet, with its long head held sideways. The lower of its two eyes watches the ground. The upper one is small and cloudy and is of use chiefly at dusk, when it can distinguish a lit room from a piece of sky.

In the back room it lies along the wall with its feet turned up. There are two young in the folds behind its knees. They are pale enough that you can see which of them has eaten: a dark thread runs from the mouth down into the belly. The adult combs the corners of the room with three fingers and draws out little ropes of living dust. It passes them back without looking. The young that has already eaten will take the next rope too, if it can. The adult sometimes has to push it away with a heel.

The gray hunters come indoors for these ropes. A hunter will wait flat against a wall for half the day, looking like a place where the stone has been rubbed smooth. The adult in the narrow house has lost the end of one finger to a hunter. Now it blows into a corner before reaching in. Its breath is scarcely enough to move the dust, but the hunter blinks, and the adult can see where it is.

There are others of the long kind on all three ridges. You can walk the length of a street at midday and find none of them. The giveaway is on the stairs. Put your palm against the side of a flight and you may feel a succession of small knocks, several steps apart. These are not footsteps. An animal going down knocks with one knuckle, waits, then knocks again. If there is an answer it sits where it is. There is generally room to pass, but when two of them have tried to pass on a lengthening stair, they have been known to spend the rest of the day on opposite sides of the same tread, each waiting for the other to finish coming past.

A young one is light enough to go down a flight without finding all of the steps. The adult goes ahead and puts its weight on each in turn. The young one follows with its chin almost against the adult’s wrist. On the public steps, where a new tread may be wider than the length of its body, it sometimes gets down onto the new stone and immediately climbs back. The adult lets it. They can take all afternoon over the flight from the square. By the time they reach the landing, there may be no square in sight.

The landing is where the grown ones wash. They scoop feldspar from a shallow pocket below the parapet and rub it into the skin of their arms. The powder comes away in rolls with the old skin. They do each other’s shoulders and the backs of each other’s heads, places they cannot reach with any comfort. They are not particularly gentle about it. From well up the steps you can see a head jerk and a free hand come down hard on the paving, though there is nothing to hear. When one finishes with another it often stays sitting behind it, its clean fingers laid loosely on the clean neck.

The one in the narrow house has a pale patch between its shoulders that has not been rubbed for some time. At dusk, when the far square appears through the back room, it brings the young to the threshold. They do not go out. For the first few evenings the young look only at the adult’s hand, which is spread on the sill. Then one evening the larger of the two looks up.

There is an animal on the far steps. It is coming down slowly, touching each tread before it trusts itself to it. It has nearly reached the square when the light starts to go. The adult in the house bends and puts its knuckle to the sill. There is a knock under the larger youngster’s feet. After a while there is another, so faint that the youngster lifts one foot and sets it down again, as if the fault were in the foot.

The room is dark by then. The adult waits a little longer at the sill. Behind it, the smaller young one has found the corner where the ropes are and is trying to do the work with its whole hand.
