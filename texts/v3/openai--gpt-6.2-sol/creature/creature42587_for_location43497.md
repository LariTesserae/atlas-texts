The people who live on the plateau are not particularly good at telling you how high up they live. They can tell you how much flour a sheep will carry from the last place where flour is cheap, or which of two roads will get you home first after snow. Ask how far it is down and they generally ask where you mean to stop.

There are households that have not moved in two hundred years and households that move twice a year without anybody thinking of them as unsettled. The latter keep sheep. An old woman may stay behind in the city with a grandson who is not yet much use on the slopes. They tease wool together, badly at first on his part, and she sends him out to see whether anyone has come in from the summer country. She wants news of the sheep, but also of particular patches of grass. A place where the flock fed for three days when she was a girl may now be bare, or it may have grass again because a fall of rock has kept the sheep out of it.

You can recognize people at a distance by where they set down a load. One man goes a little past his door to a height of wall that takes the basket without his having to stoop. A woman whose shoulder has long since healed still turns to let her daughter take the weight from the other side. Children know these habits well enough to meet an adult with a stool, though they may be nowhere to be found when called to bring one.

Most children start their errands with water. A cup in the rock is not a well: it cannot be drawn down and left to fill in the ordinary way. Some cups must be dipped from with a spoon, and some will take the mouth of a jug if you know how to turn it. A child old enough to go alone is old enough to be told who was there first, even if what is there first is only a pebble set on the rim. Children occasionally move the pebbles. The resulting disputes can be surprisingly hard to settle, since two reasonable people may disagree about whether a pebble is a claim or merely a pebble. At the better-used cups there is generally somebody within earshot who saw what happened and has been waiting to be asked.

There is a good deal of this kind of watching. It is not possible to keep the street out of the house entirely, and people do not try. In winter an elderly person can spend most of the daylight hours just inside a hide door, with a spindle or a bowl to mend. Such a person knows that the baker has had a delivery by the pace of the feet overhead, and that the weather has cleared on the far ridge because a girl has come through carrying her coat. Being watched is an annoyance chiefly to those who have something ordinary they wish to do privately. A boy going to see a girl can contrive a reason to make the trip. It is the third trip in the same afternoon that gives him trouble.

Not all the houses are warm, even among people who can afford fuel. In some of the deepest rooms the stone gives back the summer late into autumn; in others a bed put against the rear wall will go damp through the mattress. New spouses sometimes spend their first winter shifting things that have stood in the same places for years. A good rearrangement can earn the approval of a person who opposed the marriage. So can a good loaf, particularly in a house that has been eating the bread of one competent but indifferent baker for forty years.

The sheep have an opinion of the city as a place to spend the night and a much lower opinion of it as a place to spend the day. A flock brought in early will nose each of the little green pockets on its way to the pen. People defend valued tussocks with stones set on end. This works until a ewe discovers she can put her chin between two stones and pull. Some ewes discover it repeatedly, having forgotten the particular tussock but not the useful shape of the opening. The shepherds know these animals by face. They also know which ewe will wait at the turn of a stair if the rest have gone on without her lamb. She can be relied on to wait, but not to make room.

The cliff birds nest in places the cats can see and cannot get to. In spring, little white chips of shell turn up on the roofs. A child who finds one may search the wall above until a parent points out how far a bird can carry a thing before dropping it. Under the fallen rock there are other nests no one gets to see. When a pika vanishes with a long stem held crosswise, the stem sometimes catches at the entrance and stays quivering there after the animal is out of sight. Then it turns a little and goes in. In the first thin snow the block fields show how much traffic there has been between openings that look no larger than cracks. A fox will spend an hour listening at one of them, step away, and listen at another. Sometimes all it gets for the hour is snow on its back.

The people who mend the long stairs are mostly people you would see doing something else if you met them at home. A woman who sells curds may have a good eye for a tread that is rocking on one corner. A stoneworker may have a bad knee and come only as far as the first notch, carrying the food. Children go when they can keep up. On the outward walk they are eager to be given a hammer; on the way back they are glad to be trusted with something lighter. There are men and women who met on those walks and who, years after they have stopped going, can still disagree on precisely where the party stopped for dinner that day.

A few people cross between districts often enough to know houses by their smells from the road. They carry wool, news, a borrowed tool being returned after an embarrassingly long time. They carry invitations that have been made broad enough to allow for the weather. The person coming to stay for a birth may arrive in time to help with the last of the heavy work or find the baby already named. Either way there is something to do. There are always wet cloths to take out into whatever sun there is.

On a clear evening you can sometimes see a figure stop on one of the far stairs. It is hard not to think the person has stopped to look back at the city. More often a sandal has worked loose, or the load needs settling, or there is a companion a few treads below who has asked for a moment. Nearer home, somebody comes out to bring in the wool and stays at the edge of the roof after gathering it. There is time for the figure to move again before the wool has to be taken inside. There is no way yet to tell whose door will lift.
