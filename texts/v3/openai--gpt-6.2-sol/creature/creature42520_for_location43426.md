The animal that breathes out in the open water spends most of the day with its feet in the clay. It has four of them, each with three long toes that close together when it pulls them free. Its belly is pale and soft enough to take an impression of whatever it rests on. An old one coming up through the sedge may have the pattern of roots printed all along one side.

It has a blunt head, with the eyes well forward and a pair of nostrils farther back, on a little rise between the shoulders. When it comes up for air it puts that rise through the surface first. The loose skin there folds back with a wet sound. Then the whole length of the animal settles a little as it breathes out. By the time the rings have spread far enough to rock a leaf, it has its feet under it again. It can cross a wide reach this way without showing so much as an eye. In the shallows it has to go on its belly, and this is where the wading birds see it. They step over the old ones. A young one is small enough to take, though it is hard to get down: it spreads its toes in the bird’s mouth and hangs on.

At the edge of the sedge the old ones feed with their heads tucked up under the roots. They eat snails, drawing them out through the mouth of the shell. It takes a long time. The snail can hold on for most of an afternoon, and the animal, which has nothing else to do with its mouth, can wait. Sometimes it falls asleep that way and wakes to find the shell empty. Under a good feeding place there is a little drift of shells, all with their lips intact. Shrimp go into them backward. The smallest shrimp can turn around inside.

The females carry their eggs in a fold low on the belly. In deeper water the fold hangs open and the eggs trail like a bunch of hard green fruit. When a female comes into the shallows she draws the fold shut, so that the eggs don’t brush against the clay. She leaves them one at a time, in places where a stem has bent low enough to touch the water. The egg sticks just above the point of contact. As the young one grows it gets heavy enough to pull the stem down a little farther. Its first breath is taken inside the egg. Its second is usually taken with the broken skin still over its head.

For a while there are young ones all through the flooded grass. You can see their feet from above, pink against the black bottom. They have to come up often at that age, and will put their nostrils into the drop at the end of a leaf before they learn that it isn’t air. If the drop holds, they back away from it; if it breaks, they startle so hard they lose their footing.

Out on the bare humps there are no shells yet, and nothing tall enough to hang an egg from. The first animals to get that far go there in the hottest part of the day to lie with their bellies in the new clay. They leave when the water rises over their backs. In one of the hollows a little fish can stay behind after the rest of the shelf has drained. It lies on its side at first. Then, from below, water begins to come up through the holes the toes made, and the fish rights itself.
