The woman whose roof came off is called Siv. She was inside when it happened and came out still holding the two halves of a seed she had been prying apart. Now she has them tucked into the fold under her chin, where people here keep small things when they need both hands. The fold is deep enough for a meal, though not if the meal has legs. Children are always trying it with things that have legs.

Siv is old enough to remember when the plant over her house came up through the road. The road went around it for a while, and then the stem leaned and people went under it. Now it is overhead. She can put a finger through the old root hole from her bed and feel the feet of people going by. When she was ill last winter, the millers used to stop there on their way home and put a little flour into her hand. She knew which of them it was by how long they waited for her fingers to close.

Most people in the town are broad across the back and go a little bent, even at the bright edge where there is room to straighten. Their eyes are set far enough apart that a person coming up beside them in a lane is seldom a surprise. What is harder to see is someone directly ahead with no light behind them. In the dark, people announce themselves by dragging a thumbnail along the wall. There are a few who tap instead. One of the millers, who has no nails left on his right hand, makes a sound with his tongue that is very like a seed cracking. In a house where someone is preparing supper, he sometimes has to do it twice.

The children sleep in the warmest rooms until their backs harden. Before that, you can see the work of breathing through the skin between their shoulders. They are forever getting out of their beds and settling in places that feel better to them: the lee of a jar, the underside of a bridge, the palm of a gatekeeper’s hand. A keeper with a sleeping child in one hand can still work a gate and eat supper. The trouble comes when the keeper needs to get up. There is a whole manner of passing a child from one hand to another without waking it, which people who have never done it suppose is a matter of being slow. It is a matter of keeping the back warm.

Not everything in a house is there by invitation. Siv has a white animal under her largest jar. She knows it by the two clean arcs it leaves in the dust when it turns around. It eats the husks of the things that would otherwise eat her flour. When the jar is empty she puts a flake of stone in the bottom so it will still be too heavy for a child to lift. Next door there was an animal of the same kind which took to eating flour itself. For some time the two households disagreed about what sort of animal it was.

The neighbors get the roof settled at last. One of them is a young man who lives two streets away and has never been inside Siv’s house. Seeing how little room there is beneath the new roof, he offers to cut the leaf that hangs down over her bed. She gets him to lie where she lies and look up. From there he can see a small dark seam running the length of the leaf. In the morning, when the stone begins to warm, a row of pale animals comes out of that seam, one after another, and stands with its front feet on the edge. Some days they fly. Some days they stand there until the leaf is in shadow again and then back in the way they came. Siv doesn’t know what makes the difference. The young man stays on the bed a little longer, getting the angle right so he’ll know where to look if he comes back.
