The fish has a dark seam down each side where its ribs end. At rest the seams are the easiest way to find it. The rest of the fish is mostly a slight displacement of the lettering behind it, as if two of the letters had been struck a little out of line.

It doesn’t always take a shrimp. A large one came across once with a white egg caught in the comb of its hind legs. The fish turned to follow it, and the shrimp stopped. It hung there working at the egg until it came loose. The egg went one way, the shrimp another. The fish took neither. For the rest of the afternoon the egg went round and round in a little turn of water at the foot of the pane. By morning one of the snails had reached it.

The snails are pale under their shells, and their shells are pale where they are new. The oldest have dark tips made before the feed pipe began to sweat cold. Some of those tips are worn open. The animal walls off the hole from within and goes on adding shell at the other end. From below, in the late sun, the old ones look as if they are carrying a grain of iron apiece.

There are things smaller than the snails in the stamp. In the last letter, where a stroke doubles back nearly to meet itself, a colony of little stalked mouths grows on a single flake of casting scale. The mouths open like bells. When the valve knocks, they draw down all at once, though the flake doesn’t move. They open again at different speeds. It is possible to watch this happen for an hour and still be surprised that so much can draw itself out of so little.

One of the glass shrimp has lost the use of its long antenna on the left. It comes away from the pipe more often than the others and keeps to the iron. When it reaches a letter, it steps down into it and disappears except for the sound antenna, which goes on feeling along the old edge. The fish waits for this shrimp, too, but the shrimp seldom gives it clear water. It has been up as far as the ceiling mat. There it found the underside of a leaf crowded with newborn snails, not yet dark enough to cast shadows even in the sun. It picked its way among them without eating any. What it wanted was the soft stuff growing on their shells.

The leaf that goes through the washer is split at the tip. Each time the lift comes up, the two halves strike the iron a fraction apart. A snail lives between them. It is too small to close the split with its shell, but when the leaf folds in the current, the edges meet over it. On the way down the leaf twists the other way and the snail is exposed. It keeps its foot down through both passages. Afterward it goes a little farther toward the tip, eating the fine brown line that grows along the torn edge.

Nothing here has to wait for the sun, but there is something that happens only while the sun is in the pane. The fish’s shadow reaches the ceiling. It passes over the snails without making any of them draw in. The shrimp, on the other hand, will let go of a leaf when that shadow reaches them, even if the fish itself is below them and looking the other way. For those few minutes there are shrimp dropping out of the upper leaves and climbing back by the wall. Then the angle changes, the shadow falls short, and the fish goes back to being a mistake in the lettering.

It has laid eggs in the recess behind the washer. There is scarcely room there for its head. It goes in sideways and comes out backward, drawing a little silt after it. The eggs are clear at first. Later, if the lamp is on and the water has settled, there are pairs of black points deep in the gap. When the warm seam opens, the parent leaves its place between the pipe and the gasket and hangs by the washer, moving its fins. The loose grains ride over its back. On the far side of that thin body, in water the lift can barely stir, the eggs rock.
