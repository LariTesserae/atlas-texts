The wet prints on the cistern belong to a hen. She has no feathers below the breast, and her feet are soft and gray, with a little round heel that comes down last. She comes up through the overflow and spends the first part of the morning standing on the rim. In the cold her toes need some time to straighten. If a mason comes for water before they have, she lets him dip his bucket between her feet.

There used to be a name for these hens. One of the masons remembers his grandmother using it, but only in the plural, and he isn’t sure whether she meant the birds or the sound they made. They had a loft full of them when he was small. These birds make almost no sound aboveground. Down in the drains, when the water slackens, you can hear one call and another answer, so far apart that it seems unlikely they are speaking to each other. Leave a little flour at both drain mouths, though, and the same bird will come out at each, the flour still on her bill when she puts her head up at the second.

There are eleven this summer. They sleep upright in the covered passages, wherever they can get their backs against something dry. That is why the passages are so high. The masons widened them after finding the first brood asleep in a row under a loose paving stone. Even now, when a stone has to come up, someone kneels at the opening with a coat stretched between their hands. The birds in the dark will step onto the coat before they’ll step into daylight.

The masons go away in the late afternoon. Before they go they walk the gutters, clearing fern stems and the little mats of mint roots that break loose from the banks. Anyone watching would think they were fussy about the work, and they are, but they also want to know which of the birds are home. The old mason does the count by putting his hand into the inspection holes. A hen he knows will lean against his knuckles. A strange one moves just out of reach and stays there. He counts those too.

One of the younger birds has taken to the space under the lifted end of the bench. At first there was only room to get her bill into it. Now, if she lies on her side and works a shoulder in, she can reach the damp black seam behind it. She brings out white roots with grains of earth still hanging on them. The masons had meant to reset the bench. They still check the marks, but no one has fetched the wedges.

What the hens do with the roots was a mystery until a child stayed late on the bottom stair, waiting for the cloud to touch her ankles. She saw a hen come down carrying one, stop beside the cup, and let it trail over the edge. From the cloud below, something took the other end. The hen braced herself on both feet. There was a long interval in which neither end moved. Then the root went down, and after a while the hen went back up.

The child has been leaving roots beside the cup ever since. Most mornings they are still there. On those mornings the hen drinks, stepping all the way into the cup to do it, and walks back up through the town, leaving a print on every tread. By the time the masons arrive, only the round marks of her heels are wet.
