One of the birds has a white toe. It is easiest to pick out at noon, when the light reaches the rail and the toe shows through the curl of its claws. It was hatched in the pipe nearest the door. Its nest is still there, pushed halfway out of the bend by the nest that came after it. Among the rope hairs and dry fig stems is a red filament that once marked the end of a length of wire. The bird pulls on it sometimes. The whole old nest moves when it pulls, so it lets go.

It gets most of its water from a bolt above the door. The bolt has a burr on its lower edge, and a drop hangs there long enough for the bird to take it without touching the steel. When the bird was young it pecked the bright head of the bolt until a place at the tip of its beak wore smooth. Now it waits. The others wait farther down, where the water occasionally reaches the drain. They open their beaks even for a drop that ends in the rope. The white-toed bird does too, though it has already drunk.

Behind the ceramic spacers lives a gecko with a tail the color of new putty. The old tail is gone. For two days after losing it the gecko stayed flat against the back of a spacer, with only the tips of its front feet showing. A beetle climbed over one of those feet and found nothing there it could eat. Now the gecko comes out in the afternoon to hunt along the trough. It will let a beetle go by on the iron and take it a moment later on the porcelain. On the iron everything seems to be moving under its feet. On a loose shard it can feel the beetle walk.

There are things that live in the shard the gecko stands on. Along its broken edge the unglazed clay is dark with a growth fine enough to be mistaken for a wetting of the surface. It dries almost to nothing in the morning. By late afternoon it has softened again, and a creature smaller than the dirt in the thumbprint makes its way out of a pore and feeds. There is a whole afternoon's grazing for it between the place where the glaze ends and the place where a fig root has closed over the edge.

One of the figs has put a root through the bottom of its trough. The root found the floor, ran sideways for a little while, and came to a square opening. Its tip dried there. Behind the dead tip two finer roots turned back into the trough, and one has since gone all the way around the collar of the tree. In time it may pinch the tree that made it. For now the tree is putting out a leaf. The leaf is still folded down the middle and has a damp, pale seam that a bird could pull apart, though none has tried.

The youngest birds have never heard one another in the ordinary way. In the pipe they knew a parent had come by the little push of air ahead of its breast, and by the jolt when it landed. Out on the rail they watch throats and feet. A bird that has found a split fig makes a movement with its neck so slight that it could be swallowing. The others notice. By the time it goes back to the fig there is usually another bird beside it.

When the blowers ease, the white-toed bird shuts its beak a little after the others. Then there is the tick against the trough. From inside the pipe nearest the door comes a small scraping: a nestling, hearing something it can answer, getting its feet under itself. It calls once before the noise returns. The white-toed bird turns all the way around. It has been bringing food to that pipe for days, but this is the first time it has heard what is in it.
