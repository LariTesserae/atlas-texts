The blunt-headed animals are called sillbacks. A full-grown one stands about as high as the curb of an upper terrace, though it is long enough that a youngster can walk under its belly for several steps without coming out the other side. Its upper lip is divided into two hard pads. It closes them on a spray of leaves and draws its head sideways, leaving the twigs combed in the direction it was going. The oldest thickets are full of these little indications of traffic.

A sillback can go down a grade that it cannot come back up. It goes down hind feet first, with its chin almost on the stone. At the bottom it turns and eats whatever it came for, then follows the base of the ledge, sometimes for half the width of the city, to a ramp. The young learn these circuits by walking under their mothers. For their first season they seldom have an unobstructed view of where they are going. What they know of the city is a succession of slopes and turns, the swing of a belly above them, and the places where they must stop because the feet on either side have stopped.

They sleep with one flank against the wall and all four feet pointing toward the lane. A sleeping sillback works its jaw, slowly and with considerable force. Over a lifetime the movement of its head polishes a shallow fan in the stone. The fans of former occupants often show above the head of the present one. In a chamber where the back has worn through, two animals may sleep on opposite sides of the opening, near enough that the larger one’s breath lifts the hair on the smaller one’s neck. Neither need have come in by the same door.

The little roadkeepers live on what falls. They are six-legged animals with a pale, jointed shell and a broad scoop beneath the mouth. With it they pick through powdered stone for leaf scales, cast skins, and the minute green seeds that pass whole through a sillback. Most of what they pick up they carry out again. The far side of a curb is accordingly rich in things they will not eat. You can find a sillback’s milk tooth there, or a whole wing from one of the roof animals, pegged down at the corners by centuries of grit.

The roadkeepers usually walk beside the curb rather than on it. An animal coming the other way touches them once with a feeler and goes around. But if the curb is broken, they walk out through the break, tip their loads, and return by the new edge. A single passing heel can divert them for months. There are lanes in the lower city where the original doorways have disappeared and the roadkeepers still make a neat turn around the place where a threshold used to be.

What looks like a folded wing on a ledge is often two wings with a head tucked between them. The ledge sleepers have loose black fur and short, naked thumbs. Their hind claws are good for hanging but poor for walking; on level ground they pull themselves along on their thumbs, with the folded wings rising behind them like the ends of a coat. They drink at the roof seam. Water soaks into the rock on the far side, and at certain hours there is a bead to be licked from the underside of a projecting scale. The youngest have to be carried to it. A mother hangs with the young one clasped against her breast and puts her own wet muzzle down to its mouth. From the floor you could watch her there for an hour and see nothing but a dark thread against a lighter patch of stone.

They make most of the noise, though not always for the reasons you would guess. A flying animal calls sharply and is answered sharply. The long sound that seems to fill the whole roof is made chiefly by animals that have not moved. A youngster wakes and calls into the fur around it. Its neighbors stir and make the same small sound, and somewhere over the basin another part of the colony stirs. By the time the first youngster has gone back to sleep, the sound has reached roosts it will never visit.

There is a white animal that lives in the gypsum at the bottom. It has eyes, but they are under the skin. You can see them on a dead one, two dark points like seeds beneath the surface of a grape. A living one seldom shows you more than its back. It works forward just under the powder, lifting a low ridge that slumps behind it, and comes up beneath whatever has landed on the floor. A husk it takes down at once. A dead roof sleeper may take it most of a year. The two wings remain on the surface after the body is gone. At first they still lie as they fell; later they draw together at the shoulders and sink a little. Sillbacks step over them long after there is anything left to smell.

The thickets have inhabitants that never use the roads. In the forks of the oldest stems there are flat brown animals no bigger than a thumbnail. They feed on the loose outer skin of the wood. A mother lays her eggs under a flake she has loosened but not detached, and when the young hatch they eat their way out along the grain. The white marks on the stems, which look like cracks made by dryness, are their first meals. Where a branch extends over bad footing and the sillbacks never reach it, several generations can live on the same spray. Where the leaves are regularly browsed, the little brown animals are stouter and stay much closer to the root.

For most of the year an upper ramp is no busier than any other. Then the grazers start to go up it. They can tell the new leaves are there before the light reaches far enough to show green from below. An old female will make the climb, put her nose into the thicket, and come down without taking a bite. The next day there will be three or four behind her. A youngster may try the leaves on the dark side and get a mouthful of the ordinary leathery growth. It chews while it waits.

When the pale tips are gone, the animals take their usual ways down. The roadkeepers come out to collect the few tender pieces that fell between the big feet. Under the thicket, on the side no grazer can stand on, there are still some new leaves. They harden in the returning shade. Months later, one of them shakes loose and drops across the width of a terrace. A roof sleeper turning in the air goes past it. It lands flat on the basin floor, where it lies until a white back rises under it and carries it down.
