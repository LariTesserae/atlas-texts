The small ones have mouths, though not at the end you would expect. Turn one over and there is a slit between the white pads. The lips of the slit are black and very fine. When the animal settles on a blade, the pads spread and the lips part, and a shaving of stone comes up between them. It is still attached to the blade at one end. The animal takes a long time over that end. When it moves away there is another silver mark, and nothing falls.

They do not all eat. Some spend the whole day with their forelegs tucked under them, letting the stone move their bodies. At a particular stroke of the motor you can see the legs of a hundred of them unfold a little and fold again. The big ones in the roof do not stir at that stroke. They stir at the slower one. Where two bellies touch, each has a little bald place worn into it by the other.

The cups are tougher than they look. A small animal can stand in one without making it nod. It does so with its feet drawn close and its pads lifted clear of the paper. There are grains inside, the same pale violet as the light on the insulator, though out of the light they look brown. The animal takes one grain. On the way down it wipes its mouth on the stalk. That is where the buds come from. They start as a rough place on just one side, at the height of the mouth.

The crossing at the stool is not a circuit. The animal that goes under the fourth bar stays there. The one that comes out from behind the foot has been waiting with a grain in its mouth, sometimes for hours. There is a narrow shelf below the bar, invisible from above, where it puts the grain down. The shelf is crowded with little white things, each no bigger than a pad. What they grow first is a second pad. For a while there is nothing between the two but a clear thread that goes slack and tight with the thudding.

Once in a while a roof animal comes down. It is a slow business. There are places where it has to take its weight on the hinge of a leg while it finds room for the next one. Its pads have gone yellow at the edges, and where they peel away from the stone they make a noise like a person lifting a bare foot from a warm floor. It does not eat on the way. It goes to the switch and draws its legs in under the little shelter. There is just room, behind the rolled lip, for the soft part of its back.

Someone used to sit on the stool. You can tell where the glaze is dull on the front edge, and where two heels have polished the sides of the same stone blade. The smaller animals use those places readily enough. They do not use the handhold. The shallowest part of it would take both pads, but one of the pads would be on the wrong side of the tremor.

At the threshold a young one will sometimes reach out a leg. The dust takes the mark of its foot. It draws the leg in and works each joint against its mouth until it is clean. Then it tries the same place again. By morning there can be a whole row of those little punctures beside a shoe print. None is any farther out than the first.
