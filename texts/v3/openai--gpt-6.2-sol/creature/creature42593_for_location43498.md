The white bird doesn’t live in the marsh. Toward evening it wipes its bill on the fig root, first one side and then the other, and goes. Its footprints fill before it has cleared the rim. The snail, which has spent the better part of the day on one rush, comes down in the dark and crosses to another. By morning it will be halfway up that one.

The frogs spend the night out on the open mud. They are smaller there than they looked in the water, where each had a reflection to add to its bulk. A few call. The rest sit with their forefeet planted wide, catching the flies that failed to clear the rim. These are mostly flies with something wrong with a wing. They make good time on foot. Now and then one walks right over a frog that has eaten enough.

Something has made a nest under the chipped corner of the square. You can see it best after the bird has gone, when there are no toes in the way. It looks like a scrap of rush pith caught among the roots, except that the roots move when a beetle goes over and the scrap doesn’t. Its white threads are tied to little stones. You can see the bottoms of the stones and the knots that hold them, and, at the mouth of the nest, a pair of hands no wider than a shrimp’s eye.

The animal backs out to work. It has a blunt head, a long soft belly, and six legs, though only the front two look much like legs. The others are folded tight and end in hooks. All day it has been close enough to the crossing fish to touch them. Now it goes a little way out into the pool, paying out a thread from its belly. It passes behind a root and disappears. On the near side of the root a shrimp stops feeding. It picks up one foot, then another, and steps over a thread you can’t see.

There are three more nests under the square, beyond the part you can look into. You can find them by watching the shrimp. In certain places they go high on their legs for a few steps, even when the mud under them is perfectly smooth.

Late in the dry season, when the pool has shrunk to a narrow run between the fig and the square, the nest at the corner opens. The first young one out catches a hook in the pith and hangs there for much of the morning. Two others climb past it. They travel up a thread to the break, where they become visible from above for the first time: pale things with their legs drawn in, each enclosed in a bead of water. A fish noses the first bead and takes it away on its brow. The young one inside opens its legs. By the time the fish reaches the rushes, it is gone.

That year one of them makes its nest on the underside of the fern. It has only a snail shell to tie to, and the shell is empty. For days the weight of the nest pulls it a little farther down the frond. Then rain fills the basin, the tip of the fern goes under, and the animal comes out and ties its loose end to the stone.
