The six-footed grazers do not go straight to the plants when they get up. They spend the first part of the night walking the edge of the ground they fed on the night before. At intervals they set the blunt end of the head down and wait. There is a slit below each eye that opens against the stone. A plant can be dry at its tips and still give off enough moisture at the root for an animal to find it through a seam. The grazer eats two or three leaves and goes on. A good plant is visited from several directions in a season. When one of the grazers dies, the leaves on its side of the seam grow out farther than the others.

The young have no dark skin to speak of. For their first summer they go about with pieces of their birth shell still fixed to them, light on the inside and gritty on the outside. They get under the adults at dawn, not always under the one they came from. You can tell which of the resting animals have young by the small stones that roll out from beneath them at evening and then stop short, each on its own six feet. A young one may take most of a summer to get as far as the next adult.

There is a hunter on the shale that takes them at that age. It is about as long as a hand and so flat that its back bends when it goes over the rounded lip of a shelf. It hunts underneath the overhangs in the afternoon, when the grazers are still folded up. The adults are too heavy for it to turn. A young animal it can lift just enough to get a foot in under the rim. Often there is nothing in a cup below an overhang but water and a thin piece of shell, still curved to fit the back that wore it.

The hollows where the great animals winter are used even in years when the herd does not come. In the deepest ones you can put a hand into the old bedding and feel the different winters: a loose black layer, a layer felted tight with salt, another loose layer. The hair-tenders go down into it when the herd rises in spring. Most climb back aboard that day, while the adults are still standing with their legs apart and the calves are finding their feet. The rest stay in the bedding. Those eat loose hair all summer and come to the surface at the first hard frost. A herd passing through a hollow it has never used will sometimes come out with a different sort of pad beginning on its flanks. The animals there pack the hair with the cut ends outward, and for the first few weeks the wind runs over the herd in little white streaks.

The calves spend more time on their feet than the adults. On cold afternoons they walk from one body to the next, nosing into the space behind a foreleg. Several of the winter lodgers are thin enough to live there without being felt. They eat on the outside, where grit and dead skin collect, and bear their young farther in. One kind has lost the use of its hind feet. When it has to cross from an animal that has risen to one that is still lying down, it goes on its forefeet, dragging the rest of itself through the old hair. That is the only time it is ordinarily seen whole.

There are small inhabitants of the brine troughs that never put on iron. They live on the joints of the animals that do, where the plates have to remain bare at their edges. The blue light makes them plain for a moment as each plate brightens. They draw back from it, so that the first movement you see in a trough going into production is not the passage of the light downhill but a fine retreat uphill, from joint to joint. When the ice goes out, they come up to the surface and stretch themselves flat. Some are carried into the next trough on the feet of the animals that come for salt. The rest are left in the dark water. By midsummer there may be nothing to mark a mile of worked brine except a red line on the undersides of the stones, a little above the new water level.

On the pale gravel, a grazer sometimes lifts itself and then stands for a long while without taking a step. Others do the same farther out. From a rise it is possible to see the standing begin in a band and pass across two or three miles of ground, though there is nothing above it you can see moving. Down among the animals you can feel what has reached them. The air near the ground has stopped taking moisture from the stone. A turned pebble, bright side up all day, is cool enough now to hold a little damp against your thumb.

The shower is still a long way off. At the first overhang a drop has gathered on the lip of the shale, a little to one side of the cup. As it swells it touches the shallow track worn by the other drops and runs back along it. Below, a grazer has put its head into the cup. The drop lands on the closed lid of its eye and runs down into the seam where the plant is. The animal keeps its head there after it has finished drinking. In the morning it will fold itself beside the cup, on the side that leaves the track from the lip clear.
