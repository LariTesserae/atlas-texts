The pale animal at the crack is not waiting for something to come out. It is drinking. If you put a finger in the water beside it, you can feel the little inward tug at each swallow. It has no teeth, but inside its mouth are two rough pads with which it wipes the lips of the crack as they pass. It lives on what the stone has kept from the rain: a little salt, the soft brown matter that grows in the dark, sometimes the root of a rush.

It turns so slowly because there are young under its back. The skin there is nearly transparent. You can see their mouths opening and closing long before there is any sign of a foot. They take water from the same swallow as their mother. When they are ready to leave her, they go down through the crack. For a time there may be six or seven in the dark beneath her, each with its mouth to the underside of the stone. A storm can pass over all of them without any of them letting go.

The hard wings belong to a much smaller creature than the noise suggests. You could close your hand over one. It has three wings, one of which is short and almost square. On landing it strikes the long wings together and puts the short one out into the rain. The rain drives it along the surface on its folded legs. Only when it reaches rushes does it stand up. It goes from stem to stem, feeling for the hollow ones, and lays a single egg in each. The egg takes most of a year to swell enough to split the stem. You can tell a rush that has hatched by the way it bends: not at the root but at a little pale break halfway up.

Most of what lives here can be taken for dirty water. The amber film has a feeding edge so fine that a drop dents it without breaking it. After the drop has fallen, the edge draws in again, bringing with it whatever the drop shook loose. Where the water stands warm for several days, the film doubles over on itself. Minute grazers get between the folds. They eat their way outward and emerge with amber on their backs; from above, it looks as though bits of the film had learned to go against the water.

The walls are tended. This is easiest to see by putting a stone back badly. Leave it rocking on a point and return after rain: it will be lying flat. It may have been moved no more than the width of your thumbnail, but underneath it there will be a fresh print in the dust.

Those who move the stones come out on the bare margin at dusk. Seen from a little way off, they look like wet rushes laid lengthwise on the ground. They walk on the knuckles of four long fingers, keeping the softer parts of their hands folded in. They have eyes low on either side of the head. When one stops at a wall, it puts its chin down to look along the bottom course, not over the top. The young are carried crosswise under the belly. They trail their fingers in the water until they are old enough to keep them tucked in.

The walls are not meant to keep water out. The little straight ones are places to set a load, or put down a child while both hands are needed. In the loops they keep things that must be found again. The dust matters particularly. A creature coming off the margin puts one wet finger in it and draws that finger across the stone. The next to arrive lays a dry finger alongside the mark. By the time the marks have dried they are scarcely visible, but the wall-tenders read them with the soft inside of the wrist. At some of the loops even the smallest young will reach down as they are carried past.

They eat the amber folds, rolled around the tender bases of rushes. At certain walls there are piles of the tough outer leaves, each split neatly with a thumbnail. There are games, too. The young have a way of lying flat on their backs just inside a loop and seeing how long they can keep their feet out in the weather. When one pulls its feet in, the others do not make a sound. They kick the wall, all together, and the grown ones a little distance away lift their heads.

You could follow the bare strip all evening and see none of them. They do not keep to its middle as a walker does. They pass in and out of the rain, stopping where the water is deep enough to gather food or where a wall needs a hand. But at the rush with the broken stem, there is sometimes one waiting. It has come to see whether the egg has opened. It holds the stem down with the back of a finger and looks into the split. If it finds the husk empty, it lets the rush rise. Then it goes on, and for a little while the stem stands straight between the two rains.
