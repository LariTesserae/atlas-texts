The green in the bolt hole is not the same green as the stain under the rushes. Under the rushes it comes away in a slick if you touch it. In the bolt hole it stands up. There are six or seven little shoots of moss in there, with room between them for the square corners to show. The one nearest the lip is longer than the others. A drop coming down the siding sometimes catches on it and goes into the hole instead of falling.

The rushes belong to a single root that came in sideways from the marsh. The stems are three different ages. The oldest has a split down one side, and on a hot afternoon you can see the white pith through it. The youngest is still coming up. For a while its tip rested against the underside of the concrete. It has since found the edge and bent around it, though it keeps the flat place where it pressed.

There are animals in the pocket, but nothing that has to set a foot on the mud to get where it’s going. In the seam below the middle shelf, little round-bodied springtails walk on the water. They favor the line where it meets the clay. If a drop lands close to one, it is suddenly somewhere else; you never quite see the jump. Most of the time they are still enough to pass for the pale grit that has washed out of the shelf. The difference is that the grit stays put when the light goes off it.

Below the clay bridge there is a larva in a soft tube made of the same black mud as the bank. It looks red when it comes far enough out. It bends its head down, takes something too small to see, and draws back in. The tube has two mouths. Every so often the larva works its whole body back and forth inside it, and a little cloud comes out of one mouth. The cloud travels along the underside of the bridge and settles. There are other tubes farther in, but from the drain cover you can only see this one.

Most of what lives there would not look alive even if you brought it up into the light. The washer has a clear skin over the part that lies in water. In the afternoon it gathers bubbles, very small at first, then big enough to move when the pumps come into step. One will slip loose and go up, leaving a round bare-looking place that isn’t bare at all. On the underside of the washer, where there is no light, the skin is thicker and the yellow paint is easier to lift than to scrape.

When the pumps stop, the springtails go down with the water. They end up crowded for a while at the foot of the lowest shelf. The larva stays in its tube. The moss in the bolt hole goes dull at the tips, but if there is a drop already on its way down the wall, it still gets that drop.

The white rims outlast some of these lives. A new skin grows across them when the pools come back, clear enough at first that the old level is easy to see. By the end of summer there may be a leaf of moss lying flat in the water below the bolt hole. The springtails walk out on it as far as the tip. The leaf is barely longer than one of them. From above, what gives them away is how often they go out there and turn around.
