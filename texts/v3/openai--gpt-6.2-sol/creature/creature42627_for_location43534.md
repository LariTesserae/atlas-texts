The bridge painters call them cuffs. On a dry morning they are hard to tell from the collars of old paint around the bolts. By noon their edges have loosened. A drop comes down the shank and stops under the lip of the cuff, where it gets smaller without falling.

Take one off and there is another underneath it, nearly transparent, with a brown thread running through it. The painters used to think the outer one was dead. If you leave it on the back of your hand for a while, though, it will draw itself together so slowly that you notice only when it pinches a hair.

They do poorly on the newer bridges. It was thought for a time that there was something in the primer, but there are cuffs on the little service bridge behind the laundry, which has the same primer and a slight sag. On that bridge you can see how they feed. The water stays in each low place long enough to grow a skin. After dark the cuffs leave their bolts and move along the skin, taking it in at the front and leaving clear water behind. In the morning there are thin tracks in the dried grit. They go around the places where the metal has been touched with a greasy hand.

Under the freight viaduct lives a woman named Sela who can tell when a pan has a cuff in it by the sound the first dish makes. The creature gets between the dish and the bottom of the pan and there is no clink. Her husband used to throw them out with the wash water. Sela began putting them on the tin above the back step, mostly to see if they would get back up to the bridge. Some did. Others stayed under the folded edge of the tin. It was warmer there, and the drips off the viaduct struck hard enough to send water in under the fold.

For years the tin was the only place anyone had seen young ones. They are not particularly small, but they have a straight edge, like something cut off a sheet. They come out from under the old cuff one at a time. The old one gets narrower with each departure and is left at last looking like a short white bristle. The young ones spend their first wet evening feeling along the bottom of whatever they are on. Most come back to the fold. One will get as far as the corner, curl over it, and keep going on the other side.

Sela’s daughter remembers this as something they were made to watch, like the plumb lines at school. Her mother remembers it differently. She says there was nothing to watch for most of the evening and the children got cold and went in. By the time she brought in the pans, there would be one fewer on the tin.

A cuff can spend its whole life on the underside of a bridge. Those are thick and quite pale, and a painter can lift one with the corner of a scraper and put it back after the paint has set. The ones that leave the bridges turn up in less promising places: inside the metal caps on the stair rails, behind the enamel numbers on doors. One was found in the surveyors’ yard on a pane of glass that had been left over a crack. It had settled at the point where the glass touched the rock. The surveyor who found it moved the pane to finish taking a reading. The next time he went out, he found the cuff on the rock, a little way inside the crack. Beneath it the stone was wet. He put his fingers in as far as he could and felt neither the bottom nor the wet.

Out at the footings, the crew has started finding them on the stakes. Not on the wood, which dries too fast, but under the flat heads of the nails that hold the string. There is usually only one to a nail. The crew leaves them alone unless a line needs moving. Then someone walks the string in to the next stake and pulls it taut, and for a moment all the drops along it run in the same direction.
