The first tenant was a woman named Pell who took in alterations. The housing office had put her in a flat with a front room barely deep enough for its door to open. She put her table in the corridor and gave the neighbors a little bell to ring when they wanted to get past. After a week they stopped ringing and began bringing her things.

What she altered was the room people had already paid for. A man from the end block brought her the space between his bed and his window, folded twice and tied with thread. He wanted enough of it let out to get his feet onto the floor in the morning. Pell ran a thumb along the fold and asked if he ever opened the window. He said no. She took a little from the swing of the sash. The man went home and sat on the edge of his bed for the first time since moving in. In summer he came back about the window.

The people here are solid enough to wear out a stair. They have heels, and small hard teeth that show when they laugh. They don’t breathe the water. Most of the time they don’t notice it, except when they go to use their hands. You can tell someone is angry by how slowly they take a cup down from a shelf. If they snatch at it they’ll have to wait for it to stop going round before they can drink.

At the school the youngest children are learning to draw a house with the rooms in the right order. They’re good at the front door, less good at putting a kitchen somewhere a person could actually get to it. Their teacher, Mr. Ist, keeps an empty cupboard at the back of the classroom. If a child brings him a drawing with a particularly promising room in it, he opens the cupboard and they see whether the room will fit. Once a girl drew a kitchen with room for her mother to sit down while the oven was open. It was too wide for the cupboard. Mr. Ist let her leave it on his desk overnight rather than make her rub any of it out.

Her mother works for the transit authority. She goes down before the first allotment and walks the platform with a stick, tapping the edge to make sure it’s all there. The trams don’t stop, but she has to stand back as they pass. There’s a moment when the windows go by and she can see, not seats, but whatever kind of distance the car is carrying: the length of a landing, the clearance for a person to pass behind a chair. That morning there was a whole straight run, long enough to get up some speed on a bicycle. She watched it go into the tunnel. By the time she came up for her break it had become service access on either side of a boiler.

Nobody has a bicycle yet. There is a man who repairs them. He has a stand and a shallow drawer of tools, each tied by the handle so it won’t wander off when the trams go by. On fine days he brings his stool out to the park. He was the first to notice that one of the glass trees had put a root through its square of soil. He lay down to look. The root had met the poured glass underneath and turned sideways. A week later it reached the next square, where the tree hadn’t done anything at all. The man put his stool over the place where the root crossed so nobody would step on it.

Pell has a daughter who is old enough to be embarrassed by the worktable in the corridor. When she comes home from school she squeezes past without touching it, even when there’s nobody there to see. She does her homework in the front room with her legs up the wall. Recently she began to go out after supper. Pell supposed there was someone she liked, which was true, although the person was herself and the place she went was the staircase behind the end block, up near the loose tile.

The maintenance department had chalked a circle around the tile so they could find it again. The girl found that if she put her thumb on one corner she could stop the ticking. Then she could hear people through the wall: somebody washing up, somebody asking where a towel had got to. One night she heard a person practicing what to say to someone. They got as far as “When you came in,” and then began again, more quietly. She stood there holding the tile until her wrist hurt. When she took her thumb away she couldn’t hear them anymore.

On the morning the maintenance crew came, they found a wedge under the corner. It was a scrap of distance, nothing the authority would have missed. The girl had taken it from behind her mother’s worktable, where there was just enough give in the corridor to turn sideways with a schoolbag on. The crew pressed the tile. It held. They washed off the chalk and went on to the next job.

That evening Pell rang the little bell herself. Her daughter came out of the flat and found her standing in the corridor, holding the worktable by one end. Together they tipped it up, got it through the door, and set it down in the front room. There wasn’t space for both chairs, so the girl went back for one and sat in the doorway while her mother finished a hem. When a neighbor came by with something wrapped in a tea towel, the girl drew in her feet and let her knock.
