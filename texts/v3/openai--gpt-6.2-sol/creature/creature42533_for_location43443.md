The long-legged animals are called stitch deer by the people below, who see them mostly from across the valley. Up close they have very little of deer about them. Their eyes are low on their heads, almost level with the corners of their mouths, and when they drink they can watch the surface of the water from underneath their brows. The young are born with feet too broad for the rest of them. For their first few days they sleep standing, with their feet on top of their mothers’ feet.

The deer go back in their own prints only at water. The crust around a bowl is thin enough to break under a misplaced foot, and a broken rim can lose a morning’s seepage before anyone else gets to it. A youngster that breaks one will sometimes stand there licking the damp underneath until the bowl is empty. The adults do not drive it off. They go on to the next bowl.

The pale things at the rims are soft-bodied grazers with one hard tooth apiece. You can find the teeth in the dry bowls in the afternoon, mixed with the grit. The grazers go down into the stone as it warms, through pores into which the point of a grass blade won’t fit. Farther in, where the pores join, they gather in such numbers that the rock has a faint pulse under a laid palm. The gliders can feel it through their feet. A glider that has missed its strike will sometimes walk over to a bowl and stand there a while, listening to its supper go deeper.

There is a woman in the white building. She sleeps in the back room, which smells of boiled grass roots, and keeps the front room empty except for a stool. When she opens the door she stands behind it, out of the early light. In winter she has to put a shoulder to it. In summer the latch lifts a little of its own accord, and the old females on the lower shelf are already on their feet by the time she takes hold.

She knows some of them by their ears. One with a split left ear used to bring her young to the straight edge of the light and wash them there, taking a mouthful from a crack and letting it run down their backs. This year the old female has no young, but she still comes to the same crack. The woman can see her from the stool if she sits well back.

The work in the gully has bees in it. Their entrance is below the present floor, though the days they fly out the gully is briefly deep enough to hide them. They bring home a gray pollen from something that used to grow in the valley. At the loop, the thorn has begun putting out leaves unlike the ones it grows on this side: larger, with little points for the rain to run off. A small brown bird has nested in the fork. It goes around the loop to get to its nest, even when that means flying into the wind.

No animal sleeps on the crossing. The herd will stop there to scratch or let a youngster catch up, but toward evening it beds down on stone. The mothers lie on the outside of the group and the young settle wherever the rock is still warm. After dark, something much smaller comes across the other way. Its tracks begin at the shallow ramp and end under the thorn, where it digs for roots with both forefeet. The woman has never seen it whole. Once, looking out before she opened the door, she saw its tail sweep the dust off the last foot of the ledge, and then sweep a little farther, where there was no dust and, to her eye, nothing to stand on.
