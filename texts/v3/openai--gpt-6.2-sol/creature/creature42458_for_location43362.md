There is an animal in the seams, though you can spend a morning looking straight at it. It lies lengthwise in the wet line with its feet braced against either wall. Its back is the brown of the seam, and the water passes over it without much disturbance. The place to look is just below a bead that has stopped. If the bead swells and then goes on all at once, something may have drawn in its feet.

Lift a loose flake from the edge and you can sometimes see one from the side. It is not much longer than a grain of the coarse grit at the mouth of the pool. It has a blunt head and a soft belly, white except for a dark thread of food. On a flat surface it makes poor progress. Its feet reach outward, not down, and it keeps trying to take hold of a second wall. If you set the flake back a little crooked, leaving a narrow gap, it will get between the two surfaces and go quite fast.

It eats the soft outer part of the crust. The marks it leaves are paired, shallow at one end and deep at the other, where it has pulled away a mouthful. Most of its feeding is done past the bend in a seam, where the light stops but the water still moves. There are old marks in the hard material, too. The crust has sheeted over them without quite filling them in. Split a piece along the right layer and you can follow a meal the length of your hand.

The crust itself is seldom the same white all the way through. At a growing edge it is nearly clear. A little behind the edge, where it has begun to lay down mineral, it is the color of watered milk. The dense white part that rings is mostly what it has left behind. When a plate settles, some of the living margin dies under it. The rest grows into whatever space is left. In the recent slump there were thin living edges well below the surface, wherever a fold in the old material had kept from closing. They went dry in the exposed face within a day. Farther back, the folds were still wet.

The seam animals lay their eggs on the dry side of the bend. This looks like the wrong place until a heavy shower: the water there rises slowly from below, after the first rush has passed. The eggs take up enough water to loosen from the wall. You can find the young the next morning in seams where there were none the day before. Here and there, on top of a plate, a few have been carried beyond the next opening and stranded. They dry to little brown commas. The wind moves them no sooner than it moves the grit.

Under a stand of stems the seam smells faintly of cut grass, even at the depth where there are no green parts. The roots follow the opening for a while and then cross it, making a step that catches fragments of dead crust. Older stands have several such steps. Between them are chambers just large enough to keep their shape when the dust drains. The animals go there in the hottest part of the day. You can tell how far down they have gone by the freshness of the feeding marks. In a long dry spell the upper walls grow smooth again, while below the lowest roots the paired marks are close enough to run into one another.

A few have been washed into the new pool. In open water they turn slowly on their backs until a foot touches grit. At the coarse end of the fan they can get down between the grains; at the smooth end there is nothing to take hold of. The first day the underside of the fan is bare. By the time the pool has drawn back a finger’s width, there is a clear skin on some of the larger grains. The animals begin feeding there before the skin has any white in it.

If the channel holds, the fan may harden from underneath. For a while it will be possible to lift it whole and see the separate grains still loose on top. There will be a new edge then, in a place that had none before. But the near side of the pool is already shallower than it was this morning. As it dries, the animals are leaving it by a hairline opening at the foot of the fan. They go in one at a time. The opening is too small to show a shadow, and from above there is only the white grit and a narrowing line of brown.
