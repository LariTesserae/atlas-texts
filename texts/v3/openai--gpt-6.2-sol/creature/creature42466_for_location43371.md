The white springtail was not lost. There were others under the leaves, where the ice had lifted them a little from the stone. If you took the leaves away you would see nothing at first. Then what looked like a scratch in the wet black underside of one of them would shorten, turn, and go in at the stem.

They live on the softening parts of the leaves. A good leaf lasts them most of a winter. The top can still be green when the underside is full of holes, and sometimes a leaf comes away in a single piece that falls to powder as soon as it dries. In the deepest garden there are springtails of several sizes. The smallest can feed in a leaf without ever coming out on either face. When one of the larger ones jumps, it is as likely to hit the underside of the ice as to get anywhere.

The gardens are a long way apart for them. Two may be close enough that a man with a rag can put a hand in each, and one will have springtails and the other none. The empty one may look the better of the two. There is no quick way to tell why it is empty. A joint can go three weeks without dripping and still have a dark patch below it when someone comes round with a lamp.

There is a woman who comes down on Tuesdays to read the gauges. She has learnt to do the ones in the fan house first, while her glasses are still clear. She takes her tea standing up, with the flask cup on the edge of the heater. The chair under the plywood is hers in the sense that she brought it down, but she has not sat in it since a drop struck the plywood, broke, and went neatly down the back of her collar. On the wall behind it she keeps a calendar from a year when the dates fell on the same days. She has crossed out the year but not the picture, which is of a lamb with its head through a gate.

At the standpipe she washes the green off her boots. There is a small stiff brush on a string for this. It reaches the soles but not the place behind the heel where the rubber is beginning to part from the leather. Twice a week those boots go into the cupboard at the top of the stair. Once, when the woman came down in a different pair, a man tidied the cupboard and left the old ones beside the fan house. After a month there was a line of new moss along one of the welts. She wore them for the rest of the shift before she noticed it.

Most things brought down the stair do not find a place to live. The seeds that stick to a wet coat open at the foot of the stair and nowhere else. A mouse came as far as the first bend one autumn, leaving its droppings on three consecutive treads. The spider does better. It gets almost nothing in winter, but the box of defenders is just far enough from the door that the web does not have to be made again every time the door is shut.

Under the shelves the roots have reached places that were never green. A flake loosens and drops a fraction of an inch, and the roots hold it there. Water enters the space above it. The woman knows a few of these places because they are good for setting a pencil down while she writes. She used the same ledge for years, until one Tuesday she put the pencil down and it rolled in instead of out. She fetched another from the shed. The first is still there, caught crosswise a little way inside the rock. Around the wet end the wood has gone soft enough to eat.
