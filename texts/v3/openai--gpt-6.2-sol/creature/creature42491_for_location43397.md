A snail lives at the blind end of the gap, where the old wall goes into the new clay. There is a space under the last brick just deep enough for its shell. On dry days it draws up into that space and closes the mouth of its shell with a film. After rain it comes out with a grain of yellow clay stuck to its side. The shell is the color of the wet bricks except at the lip, where it is nearly white.

The snail eats the green film on the lower courses. It leaves little overlapping strokes, narrow enough that you have to put your face quite close to the wall to see them. Two courses up, the film grows in a band beneath a joint that seeps long after the rain has stopped. The snail sometimes spends most of a night there. In the morning its track shines across the bare brick, then stops shining from the top down.

Under the loose brick there are woodlice of several sizes. The smallest are pale and quick and can go some way into the old mortar without shifting a grain. The larger ones have worn shallow hollows in the black earth where they lie. A wet leaf brought in whole in the autumn is still there, though by spring there is little left of it but the ribs. When a woodlouse walks over it, the ribs move separately.

The spider is a little farther out than she was when the ground first opened. She has laid three lines from the buried course to the underside of the dock, and a few shorter ones between them. They catch mostly things that don't move: a fleck of mortar, the empty skin of an aphid, a seed hair with no seed on it. Once a gnat comes down low enough to catch a leg. The spider crosses the whole distance in a hurry. By the following evening she has taken down the line that held it. The fleck of mortar remains, going up and down when the dock leaf moves.

There is life on the clay too, though for a while it looks like nothing more than the clay getting dirty. In the snail's resting print, water stands a little longer than it does on the flat. A green dust gathers along one edge of the print. It disappears in the dry weather and comes back in the same place. The seed on the square corner swells, splits, and puts out a white root that follows the edge for a short way before it finds a crack. Its first two leaves are so small that an ant can pass beneath them without lowering its load.

By the time the snail comes that way again, the leaves have opened flat. One fits against the wall; the other projects over the drop into the black earth. The snail eats the projecting leaf, all but a small piece at the stem. The leaf against the wall stays green. As it grows, it lifts a little from the clay, and a drop of water lies behind it after the rest of the corner has dried.
