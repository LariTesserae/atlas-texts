One of the pieces of brick at the outlet has a head. It puts it out when the current eases, then three pairs of legs. The legs are a clear brown, darker at the joints. They get a purchase on the sill and draw the brick after them.

There are a dozen grains in the case, the largest no bigger than a poppy seed. At the mouth the grains are small and carefully fitted, so the animal can pull its head in without catching. Toward the narrow end it has used whatever came to hand. One has a bit of white glaze that shows every time it rolls over. The larva spends a good part of the evening on its back, trying to right itself. It can reach the clay with one foot but cannot quite bring the case round. A shrimp walks over it. A second larva comes up behind and climbs partway onto it, and the weight is enough. Both go on.

They feed on the soft coat that grows over the bricks. Where they have been feeding for several nights the floor looks newly laid. In the old channel they leave the chisel nicks green at the bottom, where their mouths cannot reach. A little way up the wall there are empty cases with the grains coming loose. These are easier to see than the occupied ones. A live case is always somewhere you would expect a bit of brick to be.

When one is ready to change, it goes down under the sill and fastens its case there. It closes the mouth with a mesh too fine to see unless a bubble catches on it. Water goes through the mesh for days. A snail passes over it, then passes over it again on the way back, leaving a clean strip across the case.

The fly that comes out has to swim before it can fly. It kicks up through the water and catches hold of a sedge blade, with its wings doubled against its back. The blade bends until the tips of the wings touch water. For a while nothing improves. Then the fly climbs the last little distance to the tip, and the blade lifts under its diminished weight.

By morning there are several flies on the upper wall. They are the color of the clay when it dries. From below, their folded wings look too long for them; at rest each fly keeps its antennae straight out in front, as if feeling for the next surface. They leave through the join in the roof, which is wide enough, though from the threshold it never looks wide enough for anything to get out.

Some nights later a female comes in low over the water and lands on the lip of a submerged pot. She walks down the inside. Air caught in the hairs of her wings makes her bright for a moment, even at that depth. Under the lip she leaves a patch of eggs in clear jelly. The pot is one of the oldest in the room. Its handle has worn away, and the frogs have never been able to sit in it, but there is still a little ridge inside where the potter drew a finger round the clay. The eggs are laid along it.
