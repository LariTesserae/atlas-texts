The brown bird has white feet. This is hard to see unless it lands above you and has to catch itself on a steep face of leaves. The toes spread so far that the foot looks for a moment like a chip of the white root showing through. It can hold to the leaves while they turn, but not while they are flat. On a flat stretch it runs with its wings a little out.

What it puts its head down for is a small gray animal that lives in the cups. There may be a dozen of them in a cup you have already looked into. They lie along the hairs at the bottom, with the larger end toward the joint. When the cup tips, they draw themselves into the wet space under the lip and wait for it to fill again. The bird knows the cups that are about to tip. It presses one with its breast, takes what comes out, and moves on. In cold weather it has to go a long way for a meal. Now and then one ventures up to the pass and tries the little pools in the heel marks. It always tries a fresh one, though there is seldom anything in it.

The birds nest on the crests, but not in the thickest part of the roots. They use the place just below, where a tough old plate rests on younger growth. In winter the opening is no more than a slit. As the young leaves lengthen, they lift the plate until there is room for a bird to go in without closing its wings. The nest itself is a pad of dry leaf ends laid on the plate underneath. There are usually two eggs. On some slopes the plate comes down again before the young can fly. You can tell where this has happened in other years by the little notches in the lip. A parent bird will work at the same notch all day, carrying away single strands. Some of the notches have been used so often that the roots turn aside as they grow and leave a permanent door.

There are animals farther in. If you ease back the turned lip of a hollow and keep it open with your hand, the black layer will presently begin to show small holes. A cream-colored head comes as far as the mouth of one, then another. They are soft animals, about as long as the last joint of a finger, with a dark line in them where the old leaf goes in. They eat their way along the wet seams. Where two plates press together they stop, sometimes for a season, and thin white roots run across their backs. When the plates draw apart, an animal may go on feeding with a root still trailing from it. The galleries it leaves fill with water. A hillside can drain through galleries so fine that none of them, opened alone, would wet your hand.

Most of what lives here can be found within the depth of a boot. Below that, a leaf keeps its shape for a very long time. In the deeper cracks there are pale films that grow over the cut ends of roots and nowhere else. A root may go through three layers of old leaves before it branches. Its tips are blunt and almost clear. In an opening that lasts long enough, you can watch them darken, though you are unlikely to see them get any longer.

There is one other place to look for the birds. Late in a dry afternoon, watch the lee of a crest just before the old growth is drawn under. The young ones come out onto the loose roll. They have the white feet already, and for a few days their heads are a good deal darker than their backs. The roll carries them uphill. One will put a foot into the standing growth and draw it back, then try again a little higher. When it finally steps off, the others follow at once. For a moment you can count the whole brood. Then the roll goes under and the ridge stands empty, though the birds are still there, picking their way between the stems.
