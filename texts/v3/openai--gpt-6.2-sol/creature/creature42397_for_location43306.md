The seed that takes the curve stops under the threshold. In the morning the wind brings it back. By noon it has been out and back twice, and a whitish patch has appeared on one side. It can go on like this for years. The seed has enough oil in it to make its first leaves, but no way to tell how much stone those leaves will have to cross. Most open in the wrong place. Their little pale forks lie among the grit, each still holding its two halves of shell.

There are three plants in the seam. The one in the middle makes nearly all the seed. The one on the left has never made any; it puts out immense leaves that sag over the dish and have to be shouldered aside by the growth behind them. The third is almost gone. It began at the exposed end of the seam, where the vapor was plentiful while the mortarless wall was settling. A brick shifted. Now it lives on what leaks past the other two. It makes one leaf at a time, no longer than a fingernail. Seen from below, those leaves look like the new growth of the big plant. They are in fact the oldest living thing in the room.

Nothing in the floor can take root. The polish where a hand used to rest is particularly bad for seedlings. The first fork skates on it; the second lifts the seed bodily, and the next wind lays it on its back. Along the wall the grit is coarse enough to hold a fork down, but there the vapor comes and goes with the wind. A seedling can grow to the size of a spread hand before a run of cool afternoons starves it. Its leaves go clear from the tips inward. For a while, through each of them, you can see the grain of the brick.

The little plant at the end of the seam has lately reached the back of the brick that closes the entrance. This is not far, but to reach it the plant has had to grow down. Its fringes have entered the narrow space where the brick stands against the jamb. The space widens towards the street. Every afternoon the outer face gets hot before the inner face does, and the brick moves just enough to pinch the fringes. At night they thicken again. There is a fine black line now on the street side, low enough that it would be missed by anyone looking for a door.

The collector used to turn that brick rather than lift it. Near its foot, on the side that faces inward now, there is a step worn by the turning. Nothing has used the step since. Grit settles on it, and once in a while a seed comes to rest there. The soil, if it can be called that, is no deeper than the white patch the floor rubs on a traveling seed.

One has opened there. The fork nearest the street is dead. The other has met the black line coming down from the seam and spread its fringe across it. A little oil passed between them before the heat went out of the street. By the next afternoon the seedling had put out a leaf. It is still folded, and so nearly the color of the mineral that from outside it looks like a flake about to come off the brick.
