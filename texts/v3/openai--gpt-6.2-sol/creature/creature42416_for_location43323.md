The animal that crosses beneath the drop is a little longer than a grain of sand. It has six feet and a blunt head that it can tuck so far under its body that it seems to have a blunt head at either end. When it stops, its belly goes on moving for a while. This is easiest to see after it has eaten.

It eats the fungus that grows on the root. It takes a thread in its mouth, backs away until the thread breaks, and then draws it in by little jerks. Sometimes it gets hold of a live root hair instead. It will pull at one of these for most of a morning. By the time it lets go, the hair has bent far enough to brush its back. It goes away in a hurry, then comes back to the same hair.

There are two of the animals now. The smaller one stays near the seedling and eats the skin that peels from the stem as it thickens. The larger one comes down to it after feeding. It puts its mouth to the smaller one's mouth and gives it something that takes a long time to pass between them. While they do this their feet are so thoroughly mixed that it is hard to tell which animal is holding the other up.

The husk under the sand is the smaller one's. Before it shed, it used to sleep against the root with its head tucked in. The larger animal covered it with grains there, but the root grew and pushed the grains apart. The next time it carried them to the seedling. The fourth grain was too broad to get its mouth around. It moved that one by walking backward with the grain balanced on its head. It took the better part of a wet morning, and twice on the way it stopped to feed the animal underneath. By then there was nothing underneath that could swallow. The smaller one was out on a leaf, waiting for its new feet to harden.

On the crossing the larger animal walks with the same short, rather fussy steps it uses on clay. Halfway over, it always lifts one foot higher than the others. There will be a knob there when the tube is older. On mornings when the tube is older, the animal has to go around the knob, and arrives at the floor with a little white dust on its side.

It has not gone into the blister. It goes as far as the rim and puts its head down, the way it does when the smaller one is hungry. A mite came out past it once, carrying a scrap of the seedling's skin. The larger animal took the scrap and ate it. Then it went back to the seedling, where the smaller one was still picking the scrap loose. It waited beside it until the work was done.
