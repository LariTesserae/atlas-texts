The people at the rim keep two sorts of shoes. One has a sole hard enough to stand on a grating hot from a discharge. The other is no more than a piece of hide with a seam up the heel. A child learning the rounds wears one of each. Where the path crosses a buried pipe, the soft shoe tells which way the water is going. Where it crosses a root, the hard shoe keeps the child from curling the toes and being carried off balance. By the time both shoes are worn out, the child usually knows the rounds.

They live a little back from the maintenance huts, in houses with floors that can be lifted a board at a time. The space underneath belongs to the hens. These are heavy birds with no habit of flying and a fine sense of where a drip is about to fall. A hen will lean out of the way of a drop before it has left the ceiling. People sometimes bring one into a hut where they suspect a leak. A hen that sits down is not evidence of anything. A hen that stays standing and takes one deliberate step sideways is worth watching.

The broad animals on the paths are called sows, though the keepers have never found a satisfactory name for the males. The name matters less than knowing which animal goes last. Usually it is an old female, not always the largest, with the whiskers worn short where they pass through the crotches of roots. A youngster that loses the herd lies down. The old female comes back and lies down touching it. They may stay that way through a whole gate cycle. Then the old one gets up and walks off slowly enough that the youngster can keep its nose against her flank.

Sows eat the mats of fern root that peel off the trees. In districts where there are no sows, the fallen mats lie several deep and a person can walk across one without discovering there is a stream underneath. Where the herds pass regularly, there are bare places in the streambed where the frogs come down out of the trees to breed. A frog carries its young back up one at a time, pressed wet against its back. It may pass a dozen pockets of water to get to one it has used before. If the tree has fallen in the meantime, it follows the trunk along the ground.

There is something living in the warm drains that the keepers took for a kind of packing until a piece of it grew across the joint it was supposed to seal. It is tough and pale and comes away in strips with knots at intervals. One keeper put a strip in a bowl with water from the drinking jar. It spent the night drawing itself into the shape of a cup, with the rim just above the surface. By the next evening there were eggs in it. Nobody saw what laid them. In an old, disused drain you can find hundreds of the cups, some no bigger than the hollow in a knuckle, holding water long after the pipe around them has emptied.

Inland, the people who live by the settling pools do not think of themselves as keepers. They mend nets, trade smoked fish for salt, and sometimes clear a conduit because they remember swimming in it as children. Their houses have wide shelves at the level of the windows. At the season when the young leave the intake towers, the shelves fill with damp, bad-tempered creatures about the weight of a hand. Their wings are dry even then. The noise comes from a row of hard slips along the trailing edge, which strike one another as the wing folds. A youngster may spend all night opening and closing one wing, bothering everybody in the house. In the morning it drops off the shelf. A good many have to climb back up and try again.

The pool fish know the sound of those first flights. They gather under the shelves with their backs out of the water, breathing and waiting. Children go out with baskets to lift the fallen young before the fish get them. They do it for two or three mornings, until the novelty wears off or they get bitten. In a good year for the fish, the adults in the tower call for several days after there is nothing left to answer.

There are fewer households near the rim than there used to be. A gate can tend itself for a lifetime, and there is no good reason for six families to live within reach of one when there are fish inland and ground where a cooking fire will draw. Still, somebody makes the rounds. The gates cannot hear the early river. They open for the weight of water when it comes, which is soon enough for the gates.

One woman on the western rounds has been setting out the same plank since she was small enough to drag it by a rope. She says the beetles used to cross it. Now most of them get only as far as the middle. They tuck themselves into the checks in the wood and ride out the rise there. At low water she turns the plank and taps its underside before putting it away. Out come the beetles, a few threadlike worms, sometimes a frog so small that the place where it sat stays wet longer than it takes the frog to disappear.

On mornings when the river has given no warning, she goes down anyway. There is a cup in the basalt beside the ditch that never quite empties. A sow and her young drink there, and the old animal has worn a smooth place with her jaw. The woman sits on the plank while they drink. From there she can hear the gate and the frogs and, in the intervals, the small sound the last youngster makes trying to get its mouth down to the water without putting a foot in.
