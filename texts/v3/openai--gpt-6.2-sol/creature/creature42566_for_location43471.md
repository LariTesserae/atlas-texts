Something lives under plate 17. Its door is a piece of the grey felt the sedges came in, drawn across the gap from below. In the cold part of the day the felt sticks out a little. You can see three white hairs on it. Only two belong to the felt.

It comes out when a drop is about to fall. It comes out for the drops that don’t fall, too, and those are the occasions when you can get a look at it. The body is the color of the ceramic where the glaze has missed. It has six feet, and holds the hindmost pair so far forward under itself that at rest it appears to have four. The other two are long enough to reach down either side of a plate and find the brackets. It is much quicker underneath the plain than on top of it. On top it goes a little way, stops, and puts its belly down.

For a while you might take this for caution. Then a drop lets go while it is lying there and you see the belly lift before the drop strikes. It has been listening to the plate all along. The fins give a little when they lose the weight of a drop. That much goes through the wall, the brackets, the screws. By the time there is a splash, the animal is already facing it.

It drinks at the edge. Then it walks around the edge picking up things you need the light from the open door to see: a curl of blue cloth, a grain of something yellow from a freight wheel, a flake of skin. These it rolls together with its front feet. The ball is quite loose. If it loses a piece on the way home it goes back, even if the piece is smaller than the dirt that sticks to its feet.

There are young in the gap under 17. They don’t look much like it yet. They are blunt at both ends and stand about on the red wire with their mouths open, though nothing has ever been put directly into their mouths. The adult brings the ball down and presses it into the felt. During the day moisture works through it. At night the young graze the soft underside, leaving little troughs that fill again the next time it rains. This is why the felt has to be drawn farther out every few days. It is being eaten from the back.

When the screws began to rise, the animal went on coming to the old runnel. It would put its feet on either bank and wait there, with its belly over the dry channel. Once it waited through three falls of water, all of them close enough to wet the plate it stood on. It found the pool in the thumbprint by following one of its own lost balls. The ball had come apart, but the blue thread in it had caught across the new crease, and one end was floating.

Since then it has been moving the young. There is a place under the raised ridge where the felt will hang clear of the gears even when the screws have gone all the way up. It carries the young there one at a time. They are stiff little things out of water and awkward to get around a bracket. On the open plate the animal stops often and puts its belly down, and the one it is carrying puts its belly down too.

The last of them is still on the wire. From the step you can see it if you lean almost to the pipe. Each time the lift passes, it opens its mouth. Farther up, under the immaculate brass grate, the others are doing the same. There is no water near them yet. They have their feet in the felt, which is still wet from the old address.
