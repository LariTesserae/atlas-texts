The pale things are easiest to see when they cross a grain of sand. The grain goes dark, then bright again, and nothing appears to have passed over it. Watch the next grain. Under the right light you can just make out a soft rim advancing over the stone. The larger ones would cover the nail of your thumb. They have no thicker part that you could call a head.

They spend most of their time on the undersides of loose plates. A plate that gives under your boot may have hundreds beneath it. If you turn it over, they draw in their rims and look like little flaws in the stone. Put it back wrong side down and by the following morning they will have gone around the edge. There will be a clean band where they passed. They eat what settles out of the water before there is enough of it to see: the first haze on a grain of quartz, the softening edge of a drowned feather. There is a great deal to eat on the plateau, but it never lies there long.

When one grows too wide to fit beneath a plate, it makes for a joint. You would expect the water to take it. Instead it stretches a little way into the opening, adheres, and draws the rest of itself after. It may take a day to go a handspan. Farther down, where the rock is always wet but the current seldom touches it, the walls are lined with them. They grow thicker there, and darker. At the outer rim something the color of old ivory sometimes shows behind a thread of falling water. That is the exposed edge of one that has spent years in the cliff.

The young pull themselves flat at a footfall. Under a steady pressure they soon spread again, even if the pressure is considerable. The adults are less tolerant of any change. The long note of the buried water passes through them without disturbing their hold, but if a stone shifts in the bed, they lift their edges. This begins over the place where the stone moved and goes outward through the joints. It is possible to see a pool change in this way, though nothing happens to its surface. The bottom freckles, holds for a moment, then goes pale again.

In the dry part of the year a branch of the buried water will sometimes fail. The silence it leaves is too small to hear from above. The adults on those walls begin to move. You can find them at the surface then, crossing a shallow pool in close procession, their rims turned up so that you can see the faint rose of the underside. They are much larger than the pool dwellers. Some have grown to the shape of a crack and keep that shape for a little while after leaving it, going laboriously over the open stone with long lobes that have never had room to lie flat.

It is these the grey bird comes for. It walks along the fins, putting one foot directly in front of the other, and stops wherever the stone trembles under its toes in a way it knows. Most times it goes away hungry. Now and then it braces itself and draws a pale strip out of a joint, shifting its grip as the strip comes free. There is often much more of it than the bird can carry. A piece breaks off and falls into the water.

Below the rim, on ledges the bird never visits, pieces dropped in this way settle into cracks of their own. For a season there may be nothing to mark them. Then, if water finds the ledge, the first thing they do is clear a little space around themselves. From a distance the whole cliff looks wet and dark. Up close, at the very end of one of those white threads, there is a patch of stone clean enough to see what color it is.
