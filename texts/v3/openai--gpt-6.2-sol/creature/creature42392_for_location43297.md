The animal under the ice is about as long as a blade of grass, though it seldom lies straight. It has a blunt head and six soft feet. The front two are almost always in its mouth. It draws them out one at a time with a thread of clear jelly between the toes and wipes the thread on the underside of the ice. That is what keeps the bubble from slipping away. As the ice thickens, the animal lays down more jelly at the uphill end of the bubble and eats it away at the other. By morning it may have moved the bubble the width of its head.

There are dozens of these animals in a pool before it freezes. You can see them in daylight if the water is moving slowly enough. They go headfirst into the spaces between the grass stems and come out backward with dead mites caught in the folds of their necks. They eat the soft parts and leave the silver backs. A little way downstream the grass is hung with them, all turned the same way.

After the first hard freeze there is generally only one animal left in a pool. The rest have gone down into the wet sod. The one that stays is not the largest. Sometimes it is so small that the bubble lifts it clear of the bottom and it has to finish its work with its hind feet kicking in the water. Once it has a good hold, it brings up a mouthful of pale seeds. It sticks them one by one into the dry underside of the ice.

The seeds are from the tall grass that grows in the coats of the white grazers. On the ground that grass scarcely ever gets high enough to flower. On a standing animal it flowers at the shoulder, where it is out of the water and where the animal cannot reach it with its teeth. In late summer the old grazers leave narrow shorn tracks across the plateau, with seed falling on either side. The little pool animals come out as far as the ground stays wet to gather it. They can carry a surprising quantity in the throat. A bird that catches one at that time of year will often put it down twice before swallowing it, to get a better grip.

Under the ice the seeds swell. A root comes out of each and hangs into the water. The animal parts the roots with its head when it goes out to feed. By midwinter there is a curtain of them about the bubble, and inside the curtain are the young. They are white at first, with feet too weak to pull free of the surface. They lie on their backs in the water and take air through their bellies. When one drifts out among the roots, the parent fetches it by a foot. It is not gentle about this. The foot can stretch to twice its length, and the young animal often comes back with a mite still in its mouth.

Now and then a pool freezes all the way down on one side. The animal begins to move its bubble while there is still water enough to get beneath it. The young go along easily; the roots have to be bitten through. In the morning you can see where it has been by the rows of seeds left in the ice, with their roots all ending at the same depth. Sometimes the track comes out on the far side of a grass stem without going around it. The animal does not always get through cleanly. You find a foot on the near side, still holding the thread.

In the thaw the ceiling goes before the floor. The young spill out into open water and spend the first day running along the surface, getting used to a world in which air isn’t held anywhere. The adult stays below. It has not breathed from the bubble since the seeds put out their first roots. Its feet have worn down to pale knobs, and it moves by catching a stem in its mouth and pulling the rest of itself after it. There are mites down there again, but it lets them pass. What it eats first are the roots it cut in winter. They have lain on the bottom all this time, green at the tips.
