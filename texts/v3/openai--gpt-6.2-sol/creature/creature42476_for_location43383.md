Something lives behind the wall. Most of the time it is no thicker than the water between two bricks. It lies in the joints with the lime and the fine roots of things that grow on the other side. When the pump starts, it comes apart. Some of it travels down the pipe; some stays behind. There is no particular piece of it that has to stay alive for it to be there the next day.

In the lowest pool it has a skin. The shrimp make holes in this skin, and on mornings when the pump has run for a long time you can see the holes lengthen in the direction of the outlet. They close from the upstream edge. A shrimp will sometimes go on feeding in a hole until there is scarcely room to back out. What it takes away is gray. What grows back is clear at first, and holds grains of slate on end.

There is an eel that has spent three summers in the yard. The first summer it could turn around in any of the drains. Now there is only one hole in which it can turn, and at the bottom of that hole its tail lies against the wet back of the wall. When the pump runs, the eel backs away from the vibration. It does this even when the pump is dry and there is nothing coming down the pipe. The shrimp, which have no such memory, come out as soon as the flow stops. The eel has learned to wait for them.

There are things in the wall that have never been in the sea. A little blind crustacean sometimes comes through the pipe and swims hard for the brickwork, although the brickwork on this side is of no use to it. One was found alive in the tin cup. There was a thumb’s depth of rainwater in the cup then. It went round the bottom, climbing each time it came to the seam in the tin, and each time it climbed it slipped back. By the next ebb the cup was empty. The crustacean may have gone over the rim in the flood. There is no reason it should have known which way to go from there.

The thing in the wall does not come out all at once. The rising drop in the gauge is the nearest it comes to showing how much room it takes up. If you put your ear against the bricks as the drop rises, you hear a faint succession of ticks, some near the floor, some well above your head. The glass isn't connected to the pipe, but the bottom fitting is still bedded in mortar. It is through the mortar that the drop comes. After it disappears, the ticks go on for a little while.

At full tide the eel goes out through the door. It passes close to the sill, where the cup has left a rust ring, and turns along the wall toward the open shore. It could keep going. There is nothing at the mouth of the yard to turn it back. But before the iron caps show again, it is usually inside, nosing along the foot of the wall. Water has begun to come out of the joints by then. The eel tastes each little stream until it finds the one that leads to its hole.
