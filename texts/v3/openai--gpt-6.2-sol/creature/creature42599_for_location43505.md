Ada Venn lives over the grocer with her son and her son’s daughter. She does alterations at the kitchen window. The girl, Ruth, wears the cut-off ends of trouser legs on her arms when she goes out to play. In the old city this made the other children laugh. Here they ask Ada for some too, especially the heavy gray cloth that keeps its crease. There is a game in which you have to lay your arm along a rail and stand very still while someone goes by looking for you.

Ada’s son works on the trams. On his way home he passes their kitchen window twice, once on either side of the ward. Ruth used to wave both times. Now she waves the first time and puts the kettle on the second. She knows which car he is on by the interior lights: one tube over the rear doors takes almost the whole length of the ward to come on.

Someone lives under their kitchen. For a long time Ada took the sound for the grocer getting ready to open. It was a chair being pulled out, a little work with a knife on a board, and then the chair again. When the grocer went away for his sister’s wedding the sounds went on. They came up through the socket behind the stove. Ada took the plug out of it so she could hear better.

There are two people, or there were at first. The one who does the cutting has a cough and knows how to stop it with a swallow of water. The other goes out early in hard shoes and seldom comes back before Ruth is asleep. On Sundays they move something heavy a short distance and are quiet for most of the afternoon. Ada thinks it is a table and that they move it into the light. Her son points out that there can’t be light down there. He has never been home on a Sunday when they do it.

Once a small white button rolled out from under the stove. It was warm, which didn’t signify much, coming from under a stove. Ada put it on the sill. She has six more like it in her tin, but this one is worn thin on one side where somebody has worried it with a thumbnail. When she has a job that calls for those buttons she can tell it from the others by touch and doesn’t use it.

At the shop, the grocer keeps a shallow basket of things people have left behind. There is a glove broad enough for Ada’s son, a purse with a tram ticket in it, and a long key with a twist of blue cotton through the bow. In the wet weeks the basket fills up. A woman from outside the ward came for the glove, put her hand in it, and said no. She bought an onion so as not to have come down for nothing. The grocer doesn’t put the basket away at night. He leaves it inside whichever door he won’t be opening in the morning.

Ruth has started doing her homework on the landing below the shop. There is a warm tread where she can sit in her shirtsleeves, though the wall against her back is cold. Last week she stayed there after she had finished, rubbing out the date and writing it again. From somewhere beneath her came the sound of a person taking the stairs slowly, pausing at each turn. She put her feet to one side to let them pass. By the time Ada called her in, she could hear them quite close. She left her things on the tread so they would know she was coming back.

In the morning the exercise book was still there. Someone had moved it to the dry end of the step and put the pencil across it to hold it open. Down the margin, beside Ruth’s long division, was a faint row of crumbs. She brushed them into her hand and ate one before she thought to wonder. It tasted of toast.
