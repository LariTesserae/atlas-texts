The people who live there call the huts beds. A bed belongs to whoever has to stop in it. Even a child knows not to leave anything on the raised stone, though there are often things tucked into the wall: a twist of lamp wick, a shoe with the sole half sewn on, a little packet of salt brought down from outside. If you take the salt you leave something in the fold of paper so the next person won't have to open it to find out it's empty.

They live in small households on the backs of the shelves, where the rock turns inward and a lamp can't be seen from across the cavern. There are cooking pots and arguments and babies who will go to sleep only while somebody is walking with them. A person walking a baby uses up a surprising amount of lamp oil. Some households keep a cord stretched at waist height across the room so the walker can put the lamp out and go on walking.

They bring their water through the slit in flat tins. Coming down with the tins full is easier than going up with them empty, except for the first few steps with the weight on your back. A child making the trip for the first time is apt to ask, on the way down, who will carry the water when everybody is old. The person ahead generally says, “You will,” which is not at all what the child wanted to know.

The wedges sometimes come into a house. They don't go to the hearth or the salt. They go to the foot of a bed with somebody sleeping on it. In the morning you can tell which end the sleeper had their head at by the close black stitching along the bottom of the stone. As soon as the sleeper gets up, the animals loosen their pads and begin to leave. People who sleep badly sometimes put a hand over the edge to feel whether any have come. It's a comfort, on a night when you think you haven't slept at all, to find them packed in tight.

There is a pale animal that lives among the white needles. You seldom see more than the end of it, pinkish and blunt, feeling along the glass. It eats the flour off the wedges without hurting them. When the dark band on a seam moves on, the pale ones are left behind. For a few days they'll take flour from a finger that's been rubbed on the underside of a wedge. Then they stop coming out. Nobody knows how long they wait in there. One woman who came back to a shelf she'd lived on as a girl put her finger into the fur of a pillar and drew it out clean. It was the first thing she told her sister when she got home.

The broad animals in the basins come up under the houses in winter, though there is no winter down there except in the things people carry in. A tin of snow set on a shelf will keep its shape for half a day if one of the animals settles beneath it. People used to bring snow down for this, before they understood that the animal was being made to go without a meal. Now and then somebody still brings a little, usually for a child born below who has never had anything melt in their hand.

A household may keep the same shelf for three generations or leave it between one meal and the next. Often all that happens is that somebody taps the cup and hears, for the first time in their life, the full sound of that particular piece of copper. It's a lovely sound. People mention that afterward, even when they've lost a great deal. They wait, and when they can go, they take the tins and the sleeping children and whatever is already in their pockets. The cooking pot may be warm when they leave it. Nobody goes back for a pot.

What they do go back for, if the cups permit it, is the height of a door. At every place they have lived, a child has stood against the jamb while somebody made a mark. In a new house they can't copy the marks from memory; they have tried, and quarreled over the width of a finger. So a person will take a cord to the old shelf and knot it at each height, starting at the floor. Sometimes there are marks for people no one living remembers. They knot those heights too. Then they come home, and somebody holds the cord straight while somebody else puts the children back on the wall.
