The snail on the gauge is not the only one that has found a living in the movement of the needle. There is something under the rim that feeds when the needle goes back. It lets out a fringe of white threads, and the little wash of water across the glass lays them all in one direction. By the time the needle moves forward again the threads are gone. The snail has never eaten them. It has eaten right up to them on both sides.

There are others like it in the district, though none of the others is quite the same shape. The one in the machine shop is as long as a forearm and thin enough to live between a cart rail and its bed of mortar. In the alterations shop there are six, one in each of the cutting table's screw holes. You can watch those for a long time without seeing anything but a little hollow fill and empty. What gives them away is a thread occasionally drawn in with a grain of lime caught halfway down it. The grain stops at the mouth of the hole. After a while the thread comes out again and pushes it off.

They don't seem to mind the dark or the light. What matters is that the water should move one way and then, however slightly, the other. A steady leak will leave a length of pipe as bare of them as it leaves it of moss. But where a leaking pipe runs through a bracket that brings the knock of the works down from the wall, there may be a whole row. Each opens a little later than the one before it. If you put your hand on the bracket you can feel why. The iron rings for longer than you can hear it.

The clear water beneath the landing is where the young gather. They are small enough to come up in the warm beads from the rail, one or two folded inside each bead. In the pocket they unfold. They don't have threads yet, only a pair of soft rims with which they take hold of the stone. On most of the underside of the landing there's nothing to distinguish one patch from another. Near the edge, where the water from the rail arrives, there are so many of them that an old mason's nick looks freshly cut. They have kept it clean to the bottom.

For a few minutes each day, when people cross the station to the first platform, the pocket shivers. The young nearest its edge let go. Most catch hold again a little farther along. One may slip out into the street water and go a long way down before it finds anything it can take hold of. That is when the fish comes out of the drain. It crosses under the landing, turns on its side, and takes what it can. It has learned to come a little before the first crossing. On days when no one comes, it waits so still that the ribbing of the nearest lamp shows through its flank.

There is one under the loose end of the old fare notice now. It has been there long enough for the moss to grow over the lower screws. It feeds on a much quicker movement than the knock of the works: the small, uneven tremor of the enamel. If you steady the notice to read it, the tremor stops. The threads stay out for a moment in the still water. Then, very slowly, they begin to feel their way around the edge toward your fingers.
