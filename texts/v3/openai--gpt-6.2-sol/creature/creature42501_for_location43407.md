The pale bird lives on the woolly growth. It pulls up a mouthful and shakes it until the slate dust comes out, then eats the orange tips, which are well below the surface and would be hard to find without the bird. It sleeps in the lee of the boulder, standing. The droppings under the ledge belong to something else. They are blunt at both ends and have little bright scales in them, though there are no fish in the pools.

At the pool with the single blade, there is a small animal in the water. You can look straight at it for a long while. What gives it away is a root that turns aside without bending any of the roots next to it. Then you see the mouth, working slowly, and the little dark patches behind the mouth that might be eyes. The animal is long enough to lie across a palm. It has feet, but in the water it keeps them folded so neatly against its sides that they look like seams.

It raises its head clear of the pool. In another pool twenty yards off, a second head comes up. The first goes down; the second stays up a moment longer. This is one animal. When it comes out on land it is no longer than it was in the first pool, and the trouble it has getting its hind feet over the rim is considerable.

There are larger ones. You hear them best in the shelter because they come there to shed. A skin caught in the green on the sill is almost impossible to distinguish from the wet until it dries, when it has the yellow of an old fingernail. The feet come out last. That close, patient scratching is the animal trying to get a toe out without tearing the skin. The bird eats the skins if it finds them. Sometimes it waits on the sill, with a slight forward set to its body, for a long time before there is anything there.

The three walls were put up by somebody with hands. The animals make no use of the walls except that on the sheltered side they can lie out of the water without drying too fast. Once in a while a large one will come up beside you. Its skin is not pale like the bird's but clear brown, and where the foreleg joins the shoulder you can see the blood moving. It will settle into the green a little at a time. They are not warm animals. Even so, the place where one has lain feels warm to the back of a finger.

They get their young across the valley before the young can manage the pools. The mother goes overland. She holds each one crosswise in her mouth, taking care on the smooth strips, where there is very little for her feet. At the two upright stones she has to put the young one down and take it up lengthwise. There is generally some fumbling here. The young one, which has been limp and good for half a mile, discovers that it can wedge all four feet against the passage. From the shelter you can hear her trying to get it through. You can hear the small feet letting go, one after another. You cannot hear her when she calls to it.

Across the valley the other sill is just low enough for her to get her chin over. She puts the young one down in the green and goes back for the next. By the time she returns, the first may have crawled the length of the sill. It has left no mark she can follow. She goes along with her mouth open a little, touching the stone with the soft part underneath her jaw. When she finds it she lies down beside it. If it moves again, she puts a foot on it, very lightly, without opening her eyes.
