The white stair has a household at either end. The people at the bottom keep shoes for the people at the top, who grow plums and lower them in the shoe basket. They are not related. When the stair was first opened, a woman came down it barefoot and asked to borrow something she could walk home in. She has been dead for thirty years. On her birthday the two households eat on the steps, leaving a tread clear for anyone coming through.

Most people live very close to a garden. A room on the open stone is cheap to rent and expensive to sleep in. There is nothing to take the upper notes out of the foundation noise. People who have lived in such rooms for a long time can tell, by putting a spoon between their teeth, which district is being drawn tight. They tend to keep this to themselves. It is the sort of thing a visitor asks you to do twice.

In the gutter blocks, children are sent out at dusk with cups. They are supposed to catch the rail water from the spouts, but some like to lie on the platform and put a finger to the underside of a rail. The whole hanging line comes toward the finger. There is a small, nearly transparent animal that comes with it. In a cup it looks like a shred of the white lining of an orange. Given a choice between two wet surfaces, it takes the colder one. Children race them on the backs of spoons, warming one spoon in the hand to make the other win. Nobody has found them in rainwater.

There are people who go looking for what a street has acquired overnight. Not everything the foundations draw in is drawn in evenly. A flight of steps may come back with a shallow extra tread, hardly enough to set a foot on, but on a long street there can be room for a bench that wasn't there at supper. Usually it is only room: the bench is something you have to bring yourself. There is a woman who does. She has a light folding bench and a bag of mending, and when the first trams come out she is sometimes sitting well beyond the last red lamp, patching a knee by the light clipped to her collar. She says the extra distance is quiet. Her sister says she goes out there because if you can see where she is, you will bring her something else to mend.

The pale grazers have begun to feed in one of those intervals. It is on a crossing between the green terraces and the district with the egglike windows. There is a low patch of herbage midway, which from either curb looks like a tuft caught in the paving. When the interval opens it takes the herd most of a night to eat its way across. The cows go down on their front knees to get at the short growth. Their calves sleep with their chins on the cows’ hocks and wake when the hocks move. For several weeks the morning tram drivers saw the same calf still out there after the survey lamps went off. It had discovered that it could stand in the road and drink from its mother on the far side of the curb without moving its feet.

A driver began leaving a shallow basin of water in the shelter. The calf never touched it. But the basin drew the underside hunters out of the garden, at first one at a time, then a whole litter, thin enough to get under the shelter door without flattening their ears. They drink with their forefeet in the water. The driver comes early now to wash the grit out. On the days she is off, someone else does it. There is no agreement about whose job it is, and the basin has not been dry since.
