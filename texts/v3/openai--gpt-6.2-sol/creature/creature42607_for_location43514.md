The animal at the back wall lives in the spaces between the pots. There are eleven pots and ten spaces. It can lie in all ten at once, but if a keeper moves a pot so that two of the spaces become one, the animal has to draw a hind foot out of the way.

The foot is the part most often seen. It is white, with four soft toes and a fifth that lies folded along the ankle. The print it leaves is no bigger than the end of a little finger. In the morning there will be a row of these prints on the dusty side of a pot, going upward. There are none on the inside. A keeper once dusted the inside specially to see, and by morning the animal had come down the outside and brushed away the little spill she had left on the rim.

It eats the skins the lizards leave between the bricks. It draws them out by the tail, taking a long time over the heads, which tend to catch. It will also eat a shaving of wood if there is a knot in it. The keeper who trims the perches sorts the shavings on his knee. Most go into his pocket. One or two he puts down beside the pots, and while he works they turn slowly on the floor, as if somebody were trying them first one way and then the other between his teeth.

In the middle of the day the animal comes as far as the first tree. It does not go into the square of light, but its shadow does. On the underside of a low leaf you can see the head, blunt and a little too large for the rest of it, and the line of the back, which trembles when the animal breathes. The ears are short. There is always a bit of lizard skin stuck somewhere about the mouth. The shadow will let a moth walk all the way across it without moving anything but one ear.

For years the keepers thought there was only one of these animals. Then the pot at the end of the row cracked, and when they lifted it they found two feet laid one over the other in the narrow space behind it. The top foot was very small. They set the pot down again. For the rest of that summer, whenever the keeper put out a shaving, it was pulled first toward one pot and then toward the next. Sometimes it traveled the length of the row before anyone got a bite.

The cracked pot has been replaced with a slightly smaller one. At night the young animal comes out into the extra room. It has learned to climb the pot but not to get down. You can hear its toes going around the rim, past the perch and past it again. Eventually the old animal puts up a foot. It does not reach very high. The young one has to lower itself quite a long way before there is anything to stand on.
