The smallest animals on the plateau are white underneath. You find this out late in the day, when they come to the hot stones and turn on their backs to eat. Each has two mouths, one at either end, and a row of short legs that keep walking while it feeds. The legs are no use to it in this position. They go on feeling for the stone. When there are several animals feeding together, their legs catch in one another and the whole company rolls over. They lie still for a little while, then turn themselves right side up.

The long-legged animals can stand among them for days without coming down on one. A foot will descend almost all the way and wait while a white belly works its way out from under. Sometimes there is no animal under the foot that you can see. You can put your face close to the stone and see the tooth marks and the grit moving through them, and still the foot waits.

There is another kind that takes the round stones out of the hollows. It has a soft-looking collar the color of the dust inside a watch. It puts this collar down around the stone and draws itself upright. The stone stays where it is. The hollow, with its white sides and its exact round bottom, comes away on the animal’s head.

These animals travel mostly along the north slope. They take the hollows down one at a time and come back without them. For a while it looks as though they must be making somewhere to live down there. But at the foot of the slope you can see the hollows set out singly, some close at hand, some well out in the basin, all perfectly level. In each sits a young animal with its legs folded against its sides. The older animals go from hollow to hollow and put their heads down inside. If you are near enough you can hear them bringing up grit. A young one will take a grain at a time, close its mouth over it, and hold it there until the grain has warmed.

The young have no finish on them yet. Their plates are clear enough to show the next plate underneath. In the evening they begin to unfold. They always put out one leg more than there is room for. That leg searches the rim of the hollow, reaches past it, and comes down somewhere a long way off. The rest of the animal goes after it, not all at once. For much of the evening there may be nothing in the hollow but a pair of hind feet, set neatly together.

An old one sometimes comes back to a hollow after the young has gone. It lowers its head and waits there with a mouthful of grit. In time it spits the grit out and goes uphill for another hollow. Some of the bowls in the basin have a little pile in the bottom, no more than you could pinch up between two fingers.

On the plateau the round stones are still where they always were. After dark they are easy to stumble over. There is one near the western rim that the small animals have been climbing all afternoon. They walk up one side and down the other, leaving two fine rows of marks. By morning there will be a shallow groove around the stone. The wind will put a little grit in it. The stone will begin, very slowly, to turn.
