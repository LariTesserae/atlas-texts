The people who keep the crossings live underneath them. You can pass directly over a house and see nothing but a place where someone has wedged a chip under the end of a slab to stop it rocking. The door is several yards away, down a crack that looks too narrow to admit a person. It is too narrow at shoulder height. At the bottom you turn sideways and step into a room with a dry shelf and a dark pool. There is usually a cup set out on the shelf. If nobody is home, you can drink and leave the cup upside down.

Dry is a relative thing down there. A bed is dry if the water gathers on its underside. Before lying down, you run a finger along the edge to give the drops somewhere to go. In some houses there is a little trough at the foot of each bed, and in the morning the children float chips of stone in it. The gray chips sink at once. A fresh green one, if it is thin enough and laid down very carefully, will stay on the water until somebody speaks too close to it.

The people are not particularly quiet. They quarrel over cups, sing to babies, and tell long stories whose endings are known to everyone in the room. It is out on the flats that they seldom speak. If two people are walking together and one wants the other to stop, they put a hand on the other’s back. This is such an ordinary courtesy that a child encountering a stranger will sometimes do it without thinking, then stand in considerable embarrassment with no idea how to take the hand away.

They eat mostly from the deep water. A basket lowered through a hole in the floor comes back with blind white shrimp clinging to its outside. The basket itself may be empty; the knack is to turn it over the bowl without first setting it down. The flat animals in the shallow pools are seldom eaten. There is too little to them, people say, though they will spend half a morning helping one that has got itself pinned under a shifting stone.

The little pair of tracks is made by the animal’s two forefeet. At the end of the tracks it folds them against its throat and pushes off on the air it has brought from under the shelf. Once out of sight it turns over. The bubble spreads along its belly almost to the edge, and the animal hangs there under the stone, pale side up, turning its eyes separately toward whatever goes by. The young are scarcely wider than a thumbnail. They have to come up to breathe much more often, and sometimes six or seven of them will use the same narrow hole. From above you see the water lift, subside, and lift again. On a still day this is the only way to tell that a house has a nursery under it.

The low enclosure is where people go to hear something they cannot bring below. A woman once carried an egg there in the front of her shirt because she thought it had begun to hatch. It was a stone egg, rounded in a pothole; she knew that. What she had heard was her own fingernail clicking against it as she walked. She sat for a while anyway. Later she brought her sister, and they took turns holding the egg and making the sound. It is still on the wall, in a notch where the rain does not quite reach it.

Farther north there are fewer doors. A house is no good until the stone over it has finished moving enough that the way in stays the way in. The children go north to look for rooms all the same. They lie on the old crossing and peer under it into the daylight, arguing about which ledge will be a bed and where a cup might stand. The spare slab is in their way. Every few years they manage to lever up one end of it, hoping there will be something underneath. There is never anything but water, and when they let it down, one of them always keeps a hand against it until it stops ringing.
