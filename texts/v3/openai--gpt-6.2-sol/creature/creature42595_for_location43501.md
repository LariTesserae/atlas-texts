Something lives in the well, though the crew generally say it lives on the roof. It is easier to see on the roof.

When the pumps draw the water down, the wet mark on the upper gauge falls with it. For a little while there is a bead at the bottom of the mark, fat enough to have a reflection in it. The bead does not drop. It travels down the enamel, slowly over the numbers and rather quickly over the blank porcelain between them. On a dry day it can go all the way to the bottom and hang there, with nothing on the bricks beneath.

There used to be a third board, a spare, kept in a rack in the station office. One of the fitters brought it up to check the graduations and left it propped against the parapet while he ate his lunch. When he came back it showed a depth of seven feet. Held upright it showed six feet nine, which was what the other two showed. He put it away. For a week afterward the bottom of the rack was damp.

It does not seem to need the numbers. The ladder is another of its places. When a rung comes clear, the green on top stays pressed down long after it ought to have lifted in the little wash from the pumps. If the next rung comes clear, the first one lets go. During a low summer the crew had occasion to go to the bottom. The man who went first said that each rung felt warm through his glove until he put his weight on it. Then it felt like iron.

What it does in the ordinary course of a day is small enough to miss. It keeps the underside of a taro leaf wet while the top dries. It moves a piece of grit out of the path of the drip, then, over several days, moves it back. Once a taro stalk broke and lay with its leaf flat on the paving. A shallow pool gathered around it in the shape the leaf would have had if it had gone on growing. By the time somebody cleared the stalk away, there was a thin green edge to the pool, well out past the torn margins.

The motor changeover is the only time it comes the whole way out. In that quiet, the lower gauge goes blank first. Not dry: blank. The enamel is still wet but there is no line on it. Above, the hatches clear from underneath, one after the next, and the water at the far parapet draws back from the holes in the brick. It crosses the roof so thinly that you can see the dry sand where it is about to be. You can stand in its way. It will go around the low side of your shoes, and for a moment afterward the roof will be dry except for two narrow places, warm and wet, just behind your heels.

Then the gate opens. Down in the well the leaves turn over and go home with their pale sides showing. On the roof, water comes down the steps. There is never any on the pavement at the top. It starts on the second tread, at the little hollow worn by people coming down with their tools. If you have stayed to watch, that is where you have to put your foot to go back up.
