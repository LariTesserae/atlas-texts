Something lives between the leaves and the stone. If you put your finger in behind the newest leaf, it will move out of the way. You feel only the cool rock and, a moment later, the leaf settling against your knuckle. Take your hand away and wait. A little clear foot comes out from under the rib. It tries the place your finger warmed, then draws back.

The rest of the animal is broad and so thin that the markings on its back look like stains on the leaf. In the open it would fit in a teaspoon, though it could not keep its shape there. It needs a surface above it as well as one below. To turn around, it folds one edge of itself over and follows the fold. This is slow work. When the leaf is young, turning leaves a crease that remains after the leaf has hardened.

There are others down there. Most are small enough to live in the space made by a single grain of mortar. They eat the pale growth that forms where a wet leaf touches stone. The oldest leaves have been eaten clean except around the veins. A few of those veins still carry water. You can see it if you lift a dead leaf: the fine branching lines go dark, all the way to their tips. Set it back quickly. The animals under it have no way of closing themselves against the air.

Before the floor broke, there was a colony in the joint around the pump. Some of it is still there. At night, as the copper leaves turn, the young travel down the wet stalks and onto the root that skirts the housing. The root ends at the break now. For several nights after the slab lifted, they kept going to its torn end. In the morning there were little dry commas in the white dust.

The broad one behind the new leaf came up with the rock. It had lived deeper than the others, under a leaf whose stalk had long ago been pinched off. There was enough seepage to keep it alive, but almost nothing left to eat. When it first reached the new leaf it fed along the edge, leaving a row of shallow scallops. Since then it has moved toward the middle, where the leaf bends a little with each change in the pressure of the water behind the wall.

In the coolest part of the night it lays its young there. They are flat even before they hatch. There is a space between each egg and the next, and none lies across a vein. All morning, while the big leaves lose their water, the little leaf at the end of the room stays wet. By noon its rim has dried fast to the rock. Behind it, where the broad one has left off feeding, there is just enough water for the young to turn in their eggs.
