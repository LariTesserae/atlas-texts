There is a woman called Eda who can tell which part of a house has shifted by putting her cheek against the wall. She asks whoever is inside to go on with what they were doing. A man sets down a bowl; a child walks from one pier to the next. When a roof stone has come to rest on only one corner of its shim, she feels the small knock it makes. She carries new shims in the fold of her apron. The old ones go back in the same fold, and when she gets home she sorts them by what can still be made of them. A piece too thin for a roof may have years left in it as a spoon.

Her granddaughter has been trying to learn this work. So far she can tell whether there is a frog on the other side of a wall. Often there is. The frogs put out their forefeet when there is clay being pressed into a joint and brace themselves against it. If the girl is patient she can ease one out whole, with its feet still spread. She sets it in the water facing away from the house. By the time she has wiped her hands it is usually back.

Most people have a room they sleep in and another place where they go when they cannot sleep. Eda goes to a street crossing where a broad paving stone is submerged to the depth of a finger. She sits with her feet on it. The small fish take the loose skin from between her toes. They do not do this for her granddaughter, who cannot keep from looking down each time they touch her.

On the roofs there is a creature the size of a thumb joint that very few people have seen from above. Its shell is pale and nearly square. What makes the clatter is not the shell but a pair of hard paddles folded beneath it. A hatchling uses them to work its way along the ceiling until it finds a seam where it can put down its soft mouth. In a room where a child has been born, somebody usually takes a little clay and fills the seam directly above the sleeping place. The creatures pass over it and settle on either side. In this way a baby may lie in a narrow quiet with the rest of the roof going on as before.

The great animals on the flats are not quiet, though from the city it is easy to think they are. Close by, you can hear them drawing the film off the bottom. They lay one side of the mouth flat against the silt and walk forward a few paces. When they lift their heads, little air bubbles come out of the grooves at the corners of their mouths. Their calves have to go nearly under to do the same thing. For the first few weeks a calf feeds where its mother has already passed. What she leaves behind is not much, but it comes up more easily.

In the evening Eda’s granddaughter goes out to watch them cross the bare squares. She has seen a cow stop with one foot raised while a calf nosed at the back of her knee. The calf went on nosing after the foot came down. As the herd drew away, the girl stepped into the print the cow had left. It took both her feet and there was room in it yet. She stood there while the water came in through the places the cow’s toes had made.

Once she brought a bowl of clay out to a square and began laying a little wall. She had put it well to one side of the tracks. An old bull came through after the others, stopped at the wall, and rubbed the underside of his jaw against it until there was nothing left above the water. He stood there chewing for some time. The girl had been sure he would step over it, and the next evening she came without the bowl to see where he would put his feet. He came the same way. At the place where the wall had been he lowered his head, but only far enough to drink.
