The pale insect is not looking for food. She has food enough in her to last until she gets back to the paving joint, though there have been mornings when she has spent nearly all of it on the return. What she is testing with her foreleg is whether the gutter has finished moving.

There is a crease on its dry side, left where the metal was folded before it was brought up the stairs. Along most of its length the two surfaces touch. At one end they part just enough to admit an egg. In summer she puts her eggs there, one at a time, and presses each one in with the flat of her head. In winter she comes down to the same place and finds it empty. There is no difference, from above, between the days on which she comes to lay and the days on which she comes for nothing.

The young are darker than she is. They hatch with a little of the egg still fastened to their backs and spend their first hours working it off against the metal. Then they walk to the end of the crease. Some go over. A few find the underside of the support and follow it to the stone, where, in the lee of a pale grain, there is a thin brown stain. It looks like something left by a careless thumb. There is enough to eat in it that one of the young can stay there for a week and leave a mark you could cover with the point of a pencil.

Nearly everything that lives here is in that stain. More of it lives on the dry side than in the water. The wet side is stripped too often; even the smallest inhabitants need time to fasten themselves down. On the dry side there are hours when the cloud comes up and lays water on them without carrying any of them away. When the kitchens begin drawing, the stain dries from its edges. Under a lens those edges are ragged. The young insects have been eating their way in.

There was a winter, before the gutter was made to tip, when the ice grew tall enough to touch the lower support. Water climbed the contact and wetted the crease. None of the eggs that year hatched. The next summer a female came in from the street and laid there again. She was not descended from any insect that had lived behind the tread. What brought her down was the band of cold air coming out of the joint at noon; what kept her there was a place where she could get her head in and the rest of her would not follow.

The little drift sometimes lasts three days. On the second day there may be an insect under it, standing in the hollow with her legs drawn close, not feeding or laying. From the keyhole you will not see her. You might see, just before noon, a grain roll down the steep face and another settle into the space it left. Then the light reaches the back of the pocket, the hollow shows through blue, and there is nothing in it but the six shallow dents where her feet were.
