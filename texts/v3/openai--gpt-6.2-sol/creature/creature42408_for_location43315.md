The thing that leaves the roof is called a latch. It is a poor name for it in the air. On a wall, with its wings folded one over the other and its head tucked into the bend of a wrist, it looks like something you could lift to let yourself in.

There are eleven latches that sleep in the old quarter, although only nine of them hunt or visit flowers. One is too young to go out. The other has a wing that healed with the outer joint turned in. It can get from one side of a room to the other if there is nothing in the way. In the house where it sleeps, the people have moved the tall cupboard.

By day you can walk right under one without knowing it. The children know where to look because of the fine red powder on the floor. They draw a line through it with a finger to see how much has fallen by the time they come back from school. In a good flowering week the line will have filled in. In winter a latch may hang for six days over a line as sharp as the day it was drawn. It does not sleep all that time. Now and then it opens an eye, and if you have been standing very still beneath it you get the impression that it has been waiting for *you* to move.

The people of the old quarter have the habit of putting a hand out when they pass through a doorway. A stranger takes it for a way of feeling for the jamb, but they do it in wide doors too, and in doors they went through an hour ago. They are feeling for what the house is doing. In some streets you can tell that a neighbor has begun to plane a table before you hear the plane. A child learning to walk at one end of a rib will sometimes have an audience at the other. In the new houses you have to go and knock to find out whether anyone is home. Some people move out there for precisely that reason.

Most of the repairers are unremarkable to look at. Their left and right sleeves wear out at the same rate, which is not true of other trades. They often work with somebody they don't particularly like. A woman called Eda has sat across breaks from her brother for twenty years, though he has not been inside her house since their father died. He brings the low stools; she brings a square of felt to lay under each of his heels. His heels hurt in the cold. There are mornings when the color takes so long to come back that they have said everything they meant not to say to one another and still have an hour to sit.

Out on the flats the six-legged grazers have their young under leaves too narrow to hide an adult. A newborn can go a long way in the space between two laminations. For the first few days its back is not slate but almost the same violet as the light at the edge of a lifted leaf. If it keeps still, the only way to find it is by the little clean crescent it has eaten around its mouth. Its mother comes back in the afternoon and stamps once. In a patch of ground no wider than a doorstep, three or four young that are not hers may stamp back. She goes to each in turn. What she is listening for isn't a difference in the sound. She puts a foot on the leaf above each one and waits until it puts a foot up on the other side.

A latch feeds at a flower with its wings spread and the tips resting on the stem. Up close it makes a good deal of noise. Its tongue goes in and out with a sound like a seam being picked out of a coat. When it pulls free there is pollen on the bare skin beneath its jaw, where it can't reach to groom. Some of that pollen goes into the next flower. Some goes home. No flowering stem has ever come up in the old quarter, but if you lift a floor where the red powder has been falling for years, you sometimes find a blunt red bud pressed flat against the underside. People fit the boards back loosely over these. It costs nothing to leave a little room.

The young latch in the old quarter is learning to let go. It has been doing this for several evenings in a passage that runs through the backs of three houses. It drops the length of a hand and catches itself, climbs back to the rib, and drops again. At the first knock from the flats it stops. The older ones turn their heads toward the opening over the stairs. The one with the turned wing goes on down the passage on foot, rolling slightly from side to side. It knows a place on the outer wall where it can get its head far enough out to see a flower open.

Downstairs, someone has set the table a little crooked to leave that passage clear. Nobody straightens it when they sit down.
