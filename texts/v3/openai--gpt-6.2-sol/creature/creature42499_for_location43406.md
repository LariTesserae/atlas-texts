In the deepest pool there are newts. Most of the summer you could kneel on the grating and look straight at one without seeing it. The glare is part of the trouble, but even under a cloud its back is the brown of a sedge stem that has been dead all winter. Wait for it to turn. There is a little yellow on the underside, not enough to light up the water, only enough to show that what you took for a stem has a belly.

The females lay their eggs one at a time. A female finds a leaf soft enough to bend and folds it between her hind feet. She holds it shut for a while, then lets go. The fold stays. Toward the end of spring there are dozens of these little creases in the drowned leaves along the margin. A good leaf is harder to find than it might seem in a pool so full of vegetation. The old sedge splits instead of folding. In a cold year some of the young are still carrying their feathery gills when the first ice forms between the stems.

There are newts under the path, too. The keeper used to put each new block in the dent left by the old one. Then one August he lifted a block and found three newts lying in its imprint, their feet tucked close, with the grain of the wood impressed on the wet moss around them. Two slid into a joint in the rock. The third stayed where it was while he held the panel up with his shoulder. He shifted the new block a hand’s breadth. He does this now even when there is nothing under the old one. A row of bare oblongs beside the grating shows where he has been, though by the time he comes back most of them have greened over.

A dipper lives below the generating house. It nests on a shelf that stays dry behind a subsidiary fall, close enough to the lip that the keeper sometimes sees it go over. From above it looks like a bird that has failed to catch itself. Then it comes back up with something pinched crosswise in its bill. It does not feed in the plateau pools. In a hard frost it works the water below the rim, where the current keeps a few stones open. There is white on its breast and, by February, ice on the ledge outside its nest. The nest itself is lined with dry grass.

The pipits have a better summer. They find crane flies down between the sedge stems, where the long legs give the flies more trouble than help. A bird with young goes back and forth over the same few yards until it seems to have taken everything alive out of them. Its nest is under a tuft leaning east, the entrance just above the water. If you stop on the path nearby, the bird waits on a post with its bill full. There is no point in trying to locate the nest by the sound of the young. The thing to watch is the bird’s feet: when you have gone far enough, it lets go.

In the shallower pools the newts have already ceased to breed. They still go into them after rain, and the small ones hunt among the stems where there is scarcely enough water to cover their backs. One of the old pools can be found by the way the sedge lies down in winter. It falls inward from all sides, and at the center, beneath the bleached leaves, there is a palm’s breadth of water. A newt can get into that water without coming to the surface anywhere you could see it. There are passages under the mat, some no wider than a finger, and chambers where a root has died and washed away.

Late in the season the young leave the deep pool. They do not all go in the same direction. Some get no farther than the new row of sedge before slipping down into the roots. Others come up through the squares of the grating on wet nights. By morning they are underneath it again, all but the occasional one caught out by a clearance in the cloud. The keeper once found one on a dry bar of steel. He wet a finger and put it down in front of the animal. It stepped across his nail and stood with its front feet on the skin. He had time to notice how far apart the toes were before it went on into the moss.
