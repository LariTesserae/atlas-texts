The crabs have doors. When the water goes off a bank, they come out of holes no wider than the end of a pencil and begin sorting the mud. They lift it to their mouths and put it down again in little balls. By afternoon a busy bank looks as if it has been sown with a round gray seed. At the first touch of returning water the crabs back into their holes, scooping mud up under themselves as they go. The balls dissolve before the water is deep enough to cover the bank.

The heron eats the crabs, though it has to catch them some distance from their holes. It has better luck on the stone, where there is nowhere for a crab to go but under the heron. Sometimes the bird stands with one foot lifted for so long that the foot begins to look like something caught on its leg. Then it sets it down a little to one side. A minnow comes into the shade the leg makes.

In the warm pools there are animals that spend their whole lives upside down. The backswimmers hang from the surface with their long legs out, each holding a bright scrap of air against its belly. From above they look like bits of husk. A rain shower knocks them down into the pool, and they row back up through the bubbles. When a pool gets too shallow even for that, they climb out onto the stone, untuck their wings, and fly. They do this mostly after dark. They sometimes come down on wet rock and have to get up and try again.

The frogs are small enough to sit in the angle where a sedge leaf leaves its stem. A calling male swells until his throat is wider than his head. There will be another answering him from a foot away, and another well out in the flooded grass. What sounds from the bare stone like a single note is a great many frogs leaving just enough room for one another. Walk into the sedges and the note develops a hole around you. Stand still and it closes, usually beginning behind your heels.

There are rails in the darker strip. The chicks are black, with an unlikely amount of bare pink at the corners of their mouths. For their first few days they ride out rain under a parent whose feathers hang nearly to the water. When the family moves, the grown bird takes long steps between the stems and the chicks paddle across the spaces it has stepped over. On a falling day the mud will show where they came out to feed: two lines of long toes and between them a mass of little marks. By the time the mud is dry enough to hold a print, they are feeding somewhere else.

The plain has plants that are easier to see from down among the chicks. In a runnel there are bladderworts, loose green strands with tiny traps along them. They take things too small for the minnows to bother with. In a good wet week, yellow flowers appear above the runnel, each on a stalk fine enough to disappear against the water. When the water pulls away, the stalks lean over. The stone is left with a few short green threads laid flat on it, and the next rain lifts them.

Others make nothing one would recognize as a flower. The blue-green mats grow out over the silt grain by grain, binding it to themselves. A dry edge will curl up far enough for a mite to get underneath. There are pale worms in that little space, and clear grains that turn out, under a lens, to have legs. A crab can scrape it all off in one mouthful. A little farther along the shelf, where the crab has not been, new mat is already growing over a sedge seed.

By the end of a dry spell there may be fish in only three or four hollows on a shelf. The heron knows those hollows and walks directly between them. But there are also fish in the sedges, in water you would take for a sheen on the mud. They lie close to the stems until the rain gets in. Then the first thing to move out across the shelf is often a minnow, going over the old line of wings without disturbing it. The water does not yet cover its back. For a few inches you can follow it by the little upright fin, and then there is enough water for the fin to go under.
