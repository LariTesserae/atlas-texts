The dark animal is soft when it comes out of the pool. Its back is folded down the middle, and where the two halves meet there is a line the color of milk. It stops because it has walked as far as it can on legs that have not yet hardened. Behind it the others stop at different distances. For a little while they look like stones cast up by the water, graded by the reach of a wave.

By morning they will be able to dig. The upper jaws are small and nearly useless; the lower jaw is a shovel with two teeth on it. They work it under the skin of new stone and lift. What they eat is the slick underneath, which has a smell like a cup that has held broth. The black animals with the big jaws eat the same slick, but can get it out of old stone, where it survives in pockets. Often a black animal opens a pocket and finds a dozen of the little diggers already inside. It eats them too. It takes a surprisingly long time over a mouthful. The little ones fold flat and are difficult to crush.

The white runners are not always white. They take on the color of whatever they have been eating, faintly at first, in two stripes along the belly. After a rain there will be a few with purple bellies, from a soft deposit that forms where shaft water meets the water of the plain. A runner can live on this deposit for days, but its young need meat. She catches the blunt red animals by getting ahead of them and lying down. They will go around a standing runner. Lying down, she apparently looks like something they can walk over.

The hinged things on their backs have no such preferences. They will ride a runner, a red animal, a person, a cup being carried to a pool. They open when the air cools against the inner faces of their shells. This makes them good at getting off at water and very bad at getting off a person who has stopped to drink. The people find them later, by their little dry clicks in the folds of a sleeping cloth. Children keep two or three in a cup with a wet chip at the bottom. In the morning there are generally two or three in the cup, not necessarily the same ones.

There is a bird, or something the people call a bird, that spends most of its life walking on the roof. Seen from below it looks too small to account for the wings it lets down when it loses its footing. It walks to the places where drops are forming and puts its bill against the stone a little way uphill of each drop. In a good place it can drink for hours without moving its feet. The broad-winged flocks feed on its young. The young have broad wings themselves and join the flocks when they fall, which makes the feeding hard to see from the floor. A flock may go round and round under a wet patch until it has eaten all but one of the young birds. That one will spend the rest of the day trying to land on the undersides of things.

The people do not live all the time in the shelters. There are weeks when a person comes back to the same bed each evening and weeks when nobody sees a bed at all. They carry a rolled cloth and a cup, and sleep on shelves that have been up long enough to lose their first sharp edges. An older person feels for those edges before unrolling the cloth. A child has to be told. The warmth of a newly lifted shelf can be very persuasive when you are tired, though it is no warmer than anything else.

At one of the shelters a woman has been coaxing the door slab back toward its old position. Every few days she takes out a peg, shaves off a little bone, and puts it back. Her brother says she could have built a new shelter by now. He sleeps in this one whenever he comes through. His daughter is the one who set the stone under the bed. She tried three stones before she found one that did not click when her uncle turned over.

They eat the red animals, which have a white joint at the base of each leg that comes cleanly out of the shell if it is twisted rather than pulled. They eat the eggs of the diggers, when they can find a pocket before something else does. There is other food in the shelters, brought down from above, and people are not always glad to see it. A man who has been away a long time may bring a sack of apples, expecting everyone to want one. The children like the first bite and leave the rest lying cut-side down on the sill. By dark the apples are covered with small animals. In the morning the skins are still there, neat as the bowls from a set of spoons.

A path is rarely named for where it goes. It is named for something that happened on it, even if that part of the path no longer exists. There is a crossing called the Three Cups, though only one cup was ever lost there. The other two were set down while people looked for it. There is a place called Lie Still where a man lay for an afternoon with his ear to the floor, thinking he could hear someone on the other side. It turned out to be a pool draining. He still stops there to listen. The sound returns in wet weather.

People who have spent their lives below are not especially quick to move at the hard tick. They stop with the animals, and for the same reason: what matters is the sound that comes next. Sometimes there is no sound. A runner starts off, another crosses its track, and the people pick up their cups. Sometimes the water underfoot runs faster and then seems, quite distinctly, to fall away. On those days the people go first. They leave whatever they have set down. If there is time they turn the nearest stack of chips on its side, but people have learned not to count on there being time.

On the new shelf, the first little animal gets its jaw under a flake. The flake is wider than its body and will not lift. Another animal comes up against its side and begins to pry at the same spot. Neither moves aside for the other. Presently the flake goes over, and the two of them put their heads down so close together that from a few steps away they look like one animal. Farther up the shelf, where the water has already dried, there is nothing for them to eat. They leave that part alone. In the morning a woman crossing with a cup in either hand will find a foot’s width of clean stone between the two dark bands of feeding animals. She will take it without thinking, and by the time she comes back it will be a path.
