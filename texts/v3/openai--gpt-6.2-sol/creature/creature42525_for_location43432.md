The heavy grazers have six feet. The front pair are small and deft, with nails worn to wedges from lifting the lips of leaves. The other four do the walking. A whole file will stop while the first animal works a nail into a seam, and when it has lifted enough of the seam to get its mouth in, the one behind puts its chin on its rump and waits. They have no arrangement for passing. An old female at the head of a file may know a way through a bench that has not opened to the sky in eighty years.

They eat the pale joints, not the leaves. A joint is sweet for a little while after sundown, and a grazer will take it so neatly that the leaf goes on lying flat until the next wind. Then it goes sailing. There are nights when whole hillsides come loose in this fashion, and the hunters overhead have to fly with their eyes half shut.

The thin-winged hunters are after the grazers’ passengers. By day these little things sit in the folds behind a grazer’s knees and take what comes to a knee in a close, damp gallery: flakes of softened wood, mites, the occasional bead of seep. At dusk they stand up and spread their wings. Most do not go far. A hunter can follow a file for half the night, picking off the passengers as they rise, and never once touch a grazer. The grazers dislike the shadow all the same. One will duck, and the next will duck, and for a long way down the slope there is a bowing of backs under nothing.

In the roof of a good hollow there are animals no bigger than a barleycorn that spend their lives hanging by their hind ends. They are nearly transparent except for the grit in their bellies. When a drop gathers beside one, it reaches out and holds it. It can hold several times its own weight this way, with its forelegs spread and the drop bulging between them. Now and then it puts its head in. The young live inside these drops until they are large enough to hang on their own. If you turn a ledge over in daylight, what looks like a sudden fit of rain may be a whole nursery letting go.

A breach made by a grazer is usually occupied before its edges stop crumbling. First come the dry things, which want the fresh powder: squat beetles and something like a silverfish that goes down a wall headfirst in short, perilous runs. The damp inhabitants follow the roots. There is a blind white animal that cannot dig in wood at all but can wedge its shoulder into root felt and work it back and forth until it has made a bed. It lives on the silverfish. In a dry year it may have to wait three days between runs. It lies with its head out of the felt and its mouth open, and silverfish will sometimes run across its face while it sleeps.

The shelters belong to people who travel in pairs or in small households. They leave a hoop standing whenever it has begun to take the green weave, even if they know they will not sleep there again. Something will use it. They are less generous with jars. A good jar has to be carried up from country where there is clay, and the people mend them with plugs of green wood that swell in the damp. At the row on the lee slope, the jar that still catches water has three such plugs. You can tell which one went in last because it still has its bark.

A woman and her brother come to that jar twice in most years. Neither made the gutter. Their mother did, when she was small enough to lie straight out on the bench and put her arm into the seam up to the shoulder. The brother wants to cut it open again, but the woman has found a nursery under the lip, and for now they take what comes. It is enough to wet a cloth and wash their eyes. They leave the rest under its split leaf. Beneath the jar, where the green is deepest, a white animal has made its bed. On warm mornings they can see the point of its nose.

The loudest callers are seldom the largest. In the gray wood there is a creature that draws one stiff wing case across the other, stops, and listens. In a narrow burrow the stroke is hardly louder than a fingernail catching on cloth. Given a chamber of the right shape it can be heard across a ravine. The creature walks a little way between strokes, trying the wood. Sometimes two get into the same chamber and one has to leave; neither can hear the answer it is waiting for.

In the hour before sunrise the grazers lie down where the galleries widen. The hunters fold their wings along the lee sides of the spires, each with its feet on the wood and the rest of it hanging free. The water holders are still awake. Under the lee ledge one of them lets a drop pass from its forelegs to the root below it, and a young one goes with it. It catches in a fork a little farther down. By the time the first patch of leaves turns in the sun, it has found a place to hang.
