A copper back is about as long as the last joint of your thumb. Seen from below, the animal is all foot and mouth. It has eyes, but you have to wait for it to turn sideways to see one: a wet black comma in the crease of the neck. It can close the eye from either end. When it drinks, it puts both forefeet around the twig and walks its mouth up the underside, taking the oil before the drop has grown heavy enough to fall.

The young are white all over. Their first copper comes in a spot between the shoulders, and for a while you can tell how old they are by how much of the spot the next animal in the sleeping row covers with its chin. They are forever getting out of order. One wakes hungry and climbs over the rest; another follows it and forgets why. An adult can restore a whole row by backing into it. There is a little squeaking, inaudible unless you put your cheek to the wall, and then each animal has its face in the warm hollow behind the one ahead.

They have a use for the folded things that come back along the ground. A shred of paper will do, though what they ordinarily collect is leaf skin, peeled from the tawny underside and worked small in the channels. They carry these packets into the lip. In a recess there, if your eyes have had time to adjust, you can see dozens wedged edgewise between the stone and the sleeping animals. Each holds a little oil. A youngster that wakes when the branches are out of reach can nose a packet open and feed without leaving the row.

There is one animal that no longer goes up into the crowns. You can recognize it by a hind foot gone yellow and hard, like the foot of a very old bird. It goes about the lip nosing the packets, passing over full ones and pulling out the empties. These it takes to the corner bush. It has a difficult time getting them into the joint. The good hind foot braces on one block, the bad one slides down the other, and the packet often drops. It goes down after it. The bush has grown a skirt of fine roots in the oil that seeps out of the old folds. At the very back of the joint the roots are bound around a brass button, all that is left in the garden of somebody’s coat.

The first crossing is made without an adult. A youngster comes out along a leaf rib until all four feet are on the blade. It is still so light that the leaf scarcely tips. Several times it backs up, mouth open, and takes hold of the rib again. What finally sends it off is a small downward movement of the stone beneath the tree. The branch rises into the space the stone has left and the youngster lets go.

It does not get to the far side. Halfway there it gathers its feet in, loses its spread, and drops onto a lower branch. The next thing it does is eat. From where you sit it seems to have forgotten the crossing entirely. But on the leaf it left there is another white animal, and below it, coming up one at a time out of the shadow of the bench, there are four more. The branch that caught the first is still low enough to reach. For a short while the whole litter feeds there, on a part of the tree that will be twenty feet above them by dark.
