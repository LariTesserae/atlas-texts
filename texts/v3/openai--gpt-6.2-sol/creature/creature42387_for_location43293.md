The first gardener to live in the tower after the flood was a cutting left in a measuring jug. Its roots came out through a crack at the one-litre mark. By the time the handle broke off, they had found the underside of tray 6 and grown around the two nuts that held it to the wall. The jug is still there. If you turn it, the whole lower shelf trembles.

Not everything in the garden comes from that cutting. The ribbon plants were there before it, and there is a small, stiff kind with leaves in pairs that has never managed to get farther than the western window. It grows a new pair whenever its shadow touches the sill. On the long bright days it can make three pairs; in winter it will sometimes spend a month making none. Seen from the roofs below, it would look as if someone had stood a row of little green ladders against the glass.

The gardener is the one with the red undersides. There is no particular stem you could point to and say *there it is*. For a long time the shelf by the window was connected to the shelf by the door. Then a stem parted where it had swollen around a sharp edge, and the two went on growing separately. They still put out their soft new leaves on the same days, but now one has begun growing into an empty bolt hole that the other, given a hole just like it, has left alone.

It does not cultivate the garden as a person would. It has never picked up a dead leaf. But a ribbon plant that gets loose from its tray often drifts no farther than the next stout leaf, and there, over the course of a season, grows roots through it. The gardener tolerates this until the ribbon gets enough purchase to stand up in the current. Then the pierced leaf yellows and lets go. You can find old ones caught under the rail, each with a neat fringe of somebody else’s roots.

There was a mistake in the numbering at the eastern stair. Two brackets were stamped 24. One holds a tray; the other holds nothing and never has. The gardener sends as many shoots out over the empty bracket as it sends into the tray. In the tray the shoots thicken and make leaves. At the bracket they go on reaching, thin and close together, supporting one another for longer than any one of them could have held itself straight. The oldest have reached the wall and turned back. There is room to put your hand in among them, though not to take it out again without bringing a few away.

Only at the yellow rectangle does the gardener grow in a shape that can’t be accounted for by the fittings. There are runners in the blue enamel on three sides. One has crossed a patch where the paint came off in a flake, and another has gone clean over a sunk screw. At the yellow line each turns and grows along it. At the corners the turns are as square as the brushwork.

The maintenance card for that part of the floor is still clipped to the lower rail. It shows a man kneeling inside the rectangle with the pump cover on his lap. He has set the screws in a little dish beside his knee. There is a thumbprint in the grease on the card, over the dish, where somebody used to hold it to read.

When the current slackens, the gardener’s newest shoots lift off the floor. Through the window you can see their pale tips, all along the line. In the minute of sunlight they stand quite still. Then the water takes them and lays them down again, pointing into the space where the man would have knelt.
