The shrimp are not all small. The ones you see are the ones that can afford to be caught out on the enamel. A large one lives under the shim at the foot of the south stair, in a space you would have to unbolt the stair to see into. She comes out backward. First there are two jointed threads in the water, sweeping it on either side, and then the blunt end of her tail. She can get nearly her whole body out while keeping a grip on the shim.

For three weeks in the first winter she carried eggs. You could see them through her, a close bunch the color of the oil in the gallery windows. She kept them moving even when there was no water over her back. When the tray filled she unfolded her legs and rose just enough to let the current through. The men coming down the stair would have put a boot on her if she had not folded up again at the first touch of a hand on the rail.

A woman named Iona found the empty skin. She had come down with a socket wrench to take the play out of the bottom step, and there was the shrimp, upside down on the thin white line where the last fill had ended. Iona touched it with the end of a cable tie. Both eyes came away with it. When she looked under the step there were two more eyes, set a little farther apart, and the animal behind them was so clear that she could see the burr on the shim through its head.

She tightened the other three bolts.

Iona works the interval between the last regular lift and the first one in the morning. There’s a fold-down seat in the gallery for her, and a cupboard with a kettle and two cups in it, though the last person she shared a cup with down there was the electrician who put the socket in. She has a room aboveground. In winter she does most of her sleeping in it after the buses start, with the sash up an inch. A bus idling outside the window used to keep her awake. Now she has more trouble on Sundays, when there’s no bus.

During the long part of the night she clears the baskets. There is a procedure for anything that comes up in one: spread it on the sorting plate, take out anything that can damage a pump, and rinse the rest into the waste bucket. After the eggs disappeared she began finding young shrimp on the plate. Alive, they were difficult to tell from drops of water. She learned to tilt the plate and wait. Water ran downhill. Here and there a drop held its place, or went a little way up.

She began doing the sorting with the bottom of the plate still in the tray. The change added a minute or two to each basket. It also gave her colder hands. When the day crew asked why she was drying her cuffs on the kettle she told them the basket handles were too short, which they were. A requisition went in. The new handles are long enough to work the baskets from the seam, but she still steps down into the trays.

Some of the young reached the unfinished end. The pools there have very little in them, and by the end of a long dry interval you can see the shrimp picking at the paper on the pipe caps. They start at a corner and work under it, pulling themselves almost entirely out of the water to get at the paste. On the lowest cap they have eaten around the printed number, leaving it on a narrow tongue. The tongue stiffens between cycles. In a good light you can read it from the gallery.

There is something else at that end, though there’s no agreement about how much of it is there. The first sound at the south gate has been there since the beginning. The sound of a foot on the bottom stair is newer. It comes in the dry interval. Iona heard it several nights running before she went down to look, because it was the sound the step had made before she tightened the three bolts: a flat knock and then a little click as the tread settled on the loose one. She put her weight in the middle of the step and got both sounds. From underneath it the shrimp backed out at once.

That was the part she had not expected. The shrimp always went in when someone came down.

She sat two steps up through the next cycle. It took a long while. She could hear the kettle switch itself off in the gallery. When the first roar came, the shrimp stayed in the open, working over a patch of enamel with the fine parts of its mouth. Then came the click of the step, and the shrimp went under. A bead on the handrail shook loose. Nothing came down the stair. A few seconds later the air went cold and the water entered at the north end.

On her rounds now, Iona goes down the stair close to the wall, where the tread doesn’t give. Sometimes she finds that the shrimp has come out very far, beyond the reach of the shim, and is standing in the last water with all its legs spread. As long as Iona takes the wall side it will let her pass. By the time she comes back with the basket, the little click has usually sounded. There is never anything left to see under the stair but a pair of threads moving in the dark.
