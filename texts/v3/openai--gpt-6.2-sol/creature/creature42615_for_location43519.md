A spider lives under the shelf of sod. She came down with it. Her first web is still in a fold of the turf, though the fold has opened and the web hangs in two pieces. For a while after the fall she went back to the wider piece and sat on it. It was where she had always sat, with the grass roots just above her back. Now the roots were beside her, and the torn edge of the web moved in the wind.

The new web is lower, in the lee of a stone embedded in the sod. It is a shallow sheet with a disorder of lines above it. The spider hangs underneath. Almost all the lines go back to the turf or to the stone, but one goes out to the wall of the cleft. When the sod settles, this is the line that parts. Twice she has put it back.

There are springtails in the sod, white ones that never came to the surface even when the surface was the floor of the forest. They go on eating through the soft parts of the old needles. At the wet end of the shelf, where the soil meets the rock, some of them come out and walk on the stone. A falling drop can scatter them. They spring in every direction, so abruptly that it is hard to see where they have gone. One lands on the underside of a root and walks on. One goes into the web. The spider comes across the sheet so fast that the rest of it scarcely stirs.

She does not come out for the crushers. Their beat reaches the stone where she has fixed her line, and the line moves, but nothing moves differently from anything else. When a fir spray falls and lodges across a corner of the sheet, she comes partway out. She stays there a long time with two feet resting on the silk. By morning she has cut the spray loose. It does not fall far. The tips of its needles stand in the little pool.

Higher up, a wren sometimes works along the exposed roots. It can go a surprising way down, bracing against the tangle, probing into the pockets that held stones before the stones fell out. When it leaves, it flies almost straight up. A little earth comes after it. The spider takes no notice of the bird, but one of the crumbs lands on the sheet and she goes to see about it.

The grass on the tipped shelf has grown enough to shade a narrow strip of wall at noon. Under it, where the spider used to sit, the old web has collected a little soil. A new root has come through the soil and grown down past the torn silk. Its tip is white and blunt. It has not yet reached the stone.
