The first occupant of a bank is usually an eel. Nothing of it shows above the clay. It comes up through the flooded seam underneath, while there is still water enough to swim between the bank and the floor. As the clay settles it keeps a passage open by going back and forth. The passage has two mouths, one at the seep and one in the still water. A patient observer can tell which mouth the eel is in by the shrimp. At one end they stand with their heads to the current; at the other they keep backing out of a patch of perfectly good water.

On the older banks there are beetles that have never been wet. They hatch in pockets under the mineral crust, where bat dung and the shucks of other beetles lodge in the cracks. Their wings are full-sized but stuck together. If the crust breaks under one, it runs around the hole until it finds a place where the clay has folded back on itself and lets it down gently. There are mites on those beetles, particularly under the rim behind the head. The mites get off at a meal and climb onto whichever beetle leaves first.

The snails at the broken bracket are not all feeding. Some are feeding on the iron-colored film, but most have come to lay their eggs in the shelter of the corner. The eggs look like drops that have failed to run off. When the clay covers the iron altogether they will lay on one another’s shells. There are snails on the far shore carrying two generations of small snails, the newest no bigger than the grains the old one rasps loose. A snail can cross a bank in a night and leave its young a considerable way from where they began.

A woman named Eda lives behind the middle lamp. There is a room there, cut into a ledge above the old high-water mark, with a door that opens inward because of the bats. They hang in the little angle over the lintel when the roof fissures get too warm. She could brush them off with the broom, but an inward-opening door costs her nothing now that she has one. She sleeps on a narrow bed with all four legs standing in saucers of water. This keeps the beetles off the blankets, though from time to time she finds a snail halfway up a leg.

Eda keeps the far lamps and reads the stakes. She has a rowboat with a flat bottom and a square of carpet where she kneels to clear the propeller of the little electric motor. For most of the crossing she uses an oar. She knows the banks by what the blade brings up: gray paste from a young one, beige flakes from a crust, empty snail shells from places that were dry before the pumps went in. There is one reach she poles across very slowly. An eel has taken to lying along the edge of the boat’s shadow, even while the shadow moves. She has twice caught it between the pole and the clay and felt it turn, hard as a wrist, before it got away.

On the pump side the men who change the screens come and go. Eda knows who has been down by how the tools are put away. One of them leaves the chest open to dry, which does not dry it. Another has begun bringing orange peels for the beetles. They have no interest in orange peel, but a pale, jointed animal comes out of the cable hole after he leaves and eats away the white inside. In the morning the peel still looks whole until someone picks it up.

Above all this are the bats. In the hours when they are away, the place where they hang stays warm. When they return, the young call from cracks the adults can find at speed. None of it reaches the landing over the water. Sometimes, though, a bat flies low enough to touch the pool, and the landing lamp shows a line opening behind it. The line runs into the ordinary dimpling of the shallows and is lost.

Tonight Eda has come over to borrow the short spanner. On her way back she stops at the broken bracket and holds the boat off with one hand in the water. She wants to know if a stake will stand there yet. She pushes two fingers into the clay beside the iron. The crust gives at once, but underneath there is a firmer layer, and under that her fingers go suddenly into open water. She keeps them still. A little current goes between them. Something on the other side of the bank has kept its passage clear. She takes her hand out and rows home. By the time she reaches her lamp, the hole she made is full of shrimp.
