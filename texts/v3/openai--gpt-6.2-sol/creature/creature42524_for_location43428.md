At the back of the grove, where the wall looks solid, a woman has a bed. You get to it by putting a foot on the second stepping stone and going sideways between the willows. There is a dry space behind them, high enough to sit in if you remember where the ceiling comes down. She keeps her boots on their sides so the mice won’t fill them with husks. In the morning she puts them on before she puts her feet out.

Her name is Iven. She is forty or thereabouts, with a pale patch under her chin where the sun never reaches. Three days out of ten she goes down to the village for flour and lamp oil. She always means to bring something back to read. What she generally brings is an onion, which she sets on the ledge by her bed and cuts a little of every evening until there is nothing left but the hard white part the roots grow from. In spring she puts that part in the moss. Once it grew.

Before the roof fell there was light enough for willows at the mouth, but none to spare at the back. Iven used to know the hour by a little oval of sun that came through a hole no bigger than her wrist. In summer it travelled down the wall and disappeared behind the cup. Now at noon the whole wall shines. For the first few weeks she caught herself looking for the oval in all that light.

The fall happened while she was down buying salt. She came back to find the birds flying in and out of a part of the sky they had never used before. Nothing had fallen on her bed. A needle she’d left threaded was still there, poked into the blanket. She sat down and finished the repair. It was a heel she was mending, and she remembers having to take out the first few stitches because she’d pulled them too tight.

The goats don’t belong to her. She knows which kid was born to which mother and which of the mothers will let a kid that isn’t hers steal milk. But she can be gone six days and come back to find them in much the same places. The small one on the highest ledge is the oldest. When the others start down she waits until they’re all past, then comes after them with her bad foreleg held out stiff. Iven occasionally leaves a twist of salt for her in the dark cup. The old goat is responsible for the polish on the rim. Iven drinks out of a tin mug with a wire handle that catches in her hair when she tips it too far.

The strip of rawhide was put there to hold the willows clear of her face. That was her doing. The stepping stones were there when she came. The third is too far from the second for her to cross easily with a full kettle, and every winter she thinks she’ll move it once the basin thaws. But the birds bathe in the gap. They go in up to their bellies and come out looking smaller, and sometimes, instead of flying away, they sit on the third stone with their feathers sticking up and wait for the sun to do what it can.

She did try the two pieces of shell together. The loose block was heavier than it looked, and to get it up to the wall she had to cradle it against her belly. From there she couldn’t see the join. She fitted it by touch and felt the little ridges run unbroken under her thumb. Then she had to put it down. Some nights a mouse comes from under that block and takes the crumbs she shakes out of her lap. There may be several mice, but there is one with an ear folded over that has learned not to go away when she reaches for the lamp.

This evening she’s late getting in. The basin has already skinned over, and she breaks the ice with the bottom of the kettle. At the sound the old goat rises on her ledge. No salt tonight. Iven shows her an empty hand, though at that distance the goat can hardly see it. Then she takes up the kettle and makes the awkward step. Behind her, where the new ice has broken, one loose piece turns slowly in the water. The goat puts her head down again. From behind the willows comes the scratch of a match, and a little light picks out the stems that have grown over the rawhide.
