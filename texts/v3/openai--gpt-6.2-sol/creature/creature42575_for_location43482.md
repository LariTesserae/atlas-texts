The groove along the western edge is occupied even when there are no fish in it. At slack water, what looks like a loose edge of paving lifts a little and lets the grit run underneath.

The animals there are long enough to lie across several joints. They have a dark seam down the back and a row of soft feet on either side, tucked so far under that a crab can walk over one without finding anything to pinch. The young are almost transparent. In the old ones the skin holds a good deal of stone dust, and where they have gone a long time without moving it is possible to follow a pale streak in the pavement straight across an animal’s back.

They feed on what is left when the water changes its mind. A scrap of flesh, the empty skin of a drifter, a fish egg with grit stuck to it: anything that takes a little longer to settle than the powdered stone is drawn under the lifted edge. Most of the grit comes out again. Some stays, packed into the little hollows between the feet. Crossing a clean sill, a large one leaves two dotted lines with an unmarked strip between them. The lines last until the next turn of the water.

At the doorway with the socket there are two old ones which seldom leave. One lies on the basin side, the other on the flats. For much of the day neither has any use for the opening. The flow is too fast, and the white sheet goes through without touching them. But when it thins, the one on the receiving side eases forward and puts the end of its body into the socket. Later, when the water comes back, the other does the same. There is just room for both ends; now and then they touch. Each curls away a little, and neither yields the hole.

Their young spend the first part of their lives underneath the pavement. They can pass through a joint that will not admit the tip of a crab’s leg. How far down they go is difficult to tell. A block sometimes settles after a long spell of quiet, and a string of them comes up out of a seam, pale and folded, to be taken by the fish. Those that reach a groove get flat very quickly. Within a day there is nothing to distinguish them from the little flakes that come off the stone.

Elsewhere the crabs have worn shallow pits under the stairs, and the silver fish have rubbed the sills bright with their sides. The long animals make less obvious changes. A groove they have used for years may be a little wider at one end, or a joint may have lost its sharpness where they pass over it. They do not always follow the grooves. One has made a bed across the top step of the great stair, where the water seldom leaves enough behind to feed it. On most days it lies with its whole length exposed. There is a shallow nick under its forward end, no bigger than the print of a thumb. That is enough. As the last of the silt goes over the step, a few grains turn in the nick, and the animal raises its edge.
