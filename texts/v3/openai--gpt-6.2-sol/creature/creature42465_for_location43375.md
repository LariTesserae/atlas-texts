The animal that closes the ring is not quite as broad as it appears at dusk. Much of its width is a fold of skin that hangs almost to the ground. On a cold morning it draws the fold up and walks out on six narrow legs. It has to turn a little sideways to get through the gap. Once it is clear of the stones, you can see the smaller animals it has been sheltering: some are still fastened by their mouths to the underside of the fold, their hind feet trailing. It takes them a while to wake up and let go.

It spends the day along the red seams. It does not eat the grit, though its lips are red with it. There are little white bulbs down in the seam, with roots like hooks. It draws them out whole and works them in its mouth until the roots hang from either side. Where it has fed you can put a finger a surprising distance into the hill.

In the late afternoon the pale-winged animals begin to settle on it. They do not land on its back, which is dry and rather dusty, but on the hanging skin, and only on the side turned out of the wind. The first few have to keep shifting their feet as it walks. By the time it reaches the ring there are enough of them to weigh the fold down. When it lies in the gap they open their wings a little, side by side, and the last light makes a second, lower wall inside the stones.

The ring is seldom empty even in the middle of the day. Lift one of the stones and there will be a thin gray animal on its underside, flat enough that the depressions in its back take the little points of the rock. It stays there when the stone is turned over. After a while one end lifts, and from under it comes a much smaller copy, wet and yellowish. There may be four or five of these. They go off into the ring by different routes. On the open ground they travel only in the shade of grit, from grain to grain, and some of them spend the afternoon getting the length of a hand away from the stone.

There is an old ring above the middle basin whose gap has filled up. Nothing broad has slept in it for a long time. The little gray animals still come out there, but at evening, when the stones begin to cool, they go downhill. It is possible to follow them because they take the shortest way, straight over the ridges in the rock that the grazers walk around.

At the pool they climb the legs of the waders.

A wader out of water has a fine dry leg, jointed well above its foot. The gray creatures go up to the joint and wait there, sometimes a dozen to a leg. When rain comes and the feet open, they climb down again. They are among the first things in the pool. They spread over the submerged stone and feed, leaving little clear ovals in the brown film. The waders pick at the newly exposed specks with their narrow mouths. From the bank it looks as though they are feeding the stone to one another.

In a dry year a wader may carry its load for weeks. It still goes out each morning to where the pool was deepest and lowers its head. Sometimes one of the gray creatures starts down a leg before there is water. It reaches the closed foot and feels along it for a place to put its own feet. The wader stands still while it does this. You can watch for a long time before the little thing turns and makes its way back up.
