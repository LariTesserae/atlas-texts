There is a fish with a piece missing from its tail. The attendant can pick it out as far away as the tiled wall, not by the gap itself but by the extra stroke it takes to stop. It is one of the smaller fish. For most of the morning it holds the best place under the step, where the run from the fern brings down things too small for her to see. It makes a dart at each one, backs into place, and sometimes darts again at nothing.

The biggest fish stays out in the warm water by the pipes. When it comes under the step, the small one gives way at once. It goes no farther than the next patch of shade. There is room for both fish. There has always been room for both fish.

A mud wasp comes down to the drain several times an hour. It sets its front feet on a root of the fern and lowers its head. The root is about as thick as one of the hairs on the attendant’s wrist. Some days the falling water knocks the wasp off it. The wasp goes round the pump house and comes back to the same root. Where it goes with its water the attendant doesn’t know. She has looked for a nest under the stairs and found two old nests, both with their mouths broken open.

At noon a pigeon drops a length of market string into the water. The fish come out from under the step and follow it, keeping a little distance from its trailing end. There is no particular leader. One after another they go as far as the wall, where the string catches on a rough tile. The interest goes out of it as soon as it stops moving. By the time the attendant gets up with the hook she uses for rubbish, the fish are back under the step. She lifts out the string with a little weed on it. The pigeon has gone down to the lower rail, from which it still can’t reach the water.

On Sundays the woman who relieves her brings a folding stool. She likes to sit in the pale patch under the missing panel, though by the time she has opened the stool the light is usually somewhere else. Once she asked whether the fish were put there to eat mosquitoes. The attendant said she didn’t think so. The first morning she’d worked there she’d thought there were only three, and for a while she’d put down a tally in the margin of the book. Then there were nine, then four again. One afternoon a tram went over just as the whole shoal turned, and she saw several fish no longer than the nails on her smallest fingers. She’d been counting only the ones she could see from the chair.

There are weeks when she doesn’t see the fish with the torn tail. Each time she decides it has been eaten, some fish comes out into the run from the drain and takes that little second stroke. She has never been close enough to tell whether the gap is the same.
